## 18. Blank Pages

They took my name at the door of the cell, and I want to be clear: that is not a metaphor. I heard the sound of it leaving.

It came out of me the way a splinter comes out — a small, wet resistance, then nothing where something used to be. A bailiff with a face like a folded writ held a ledger open under my chin, and my name slid off my tongue and onto the page in ink the color of old bruises. After that, when I tried to say *Beatrix*, my mouth produced only the shape of it, like a glove with no hand inside.

They took my maps next. All of them, even the ones I'd sewn into my coat lining, even the one I'd drawn on my own forearm in ferry-ink back when the hinterland was still trying to eat us. The bailiffs peeled them away with the tenderness of people handling money that belongs to someone else. My instruments went last — compasses, calipers, the good pen Grandmother Brightwater left me, which was not actually from Grandmother Brightwater but from a flea market in Millbrook, though I had always lied about that to give it gravitas.

Then they put me in a room with no edges worth measuring.

That's what they thought, anyway. That's what they'd designed it to be, I imagine, some centuries ago, when the court beneath the mountain was younger and crueler in ways that required architecture. A cell for cartographers. No corners — the walls curved into floor and ceiling without decision or seam. No fixed light; a grey glow that came from everywhere and therefore could be said to come from nowhere. No window, no door I could find, no variation in temperature. Nothing to triangulate against.

A prison built on the principle that a mapmaker without reference points is just a woman in the dark.

I sat down. I will admit that for a while — an hour, a day, I genuinely couldn't say, because they had also confiscated time's usual furniture — I did exactly what the cell wanted. I sat, and I shook, and I replayed the moment everything collapsed: the thorn-cage flaring open under my compass needle, Ivo's hands closing around the lantern-soul, the half-second of impossible triumph before the Magistrate of Debts unfolded out of the air like a contract being read aloud by someone who enjoyed it. *The prophecy required a thief,* he'd said, in a voice like wax hardening. *It never required a success.* And then the bailiffs had taken him, taken the soul, taken the book, taken me, and the whole beautiful stupid gamble had folded shut like a trapdoor.

Ivo. They had him somewhere deeper in this mountain, and his soul was back in its cage, and I was in a room shaped like a shrug.

Good. Excellent. Very restful.

I gave myself permission to despair for as long as it took to exhaust itself, which, if you've never tried it, takes considerably less time than you'd think once you're angry underneath. Despair is heavy but shallow. Anger goes all the way down. By the time I'd catalogued every specific thing the court had taken — name, maps, instruments, freedom, Ivo, the prophecy book, my mother's face, my own name off every map I'd ever drawn so that even my life's work no longer pointed at me — I had arrived somewhere cold and quiet and useful.

Because here is what the court did not understand, and I mean that sincerely, not as bravado. I lay back against the seamless wall and I made myself think it through like a surveyor, because thinking like a surveyor is the only way I know how to hold onto myself.

They had taken every map I owned.

But they had not taken the cartographer.

* * *

Let me tell you something about maps that most people never learn, because most people only meet maps as objects — folded things in gloveboxes, framed things in libraries. A map is not paper. Paper is just where the map was staying that day. A map is a claim about reality: *this is here, that is there, and between them runs this distance, and you can cross it.* A map is an argument, and reality — real reality, deep reality, the kind this realm was built out of — can be argued with.

The hinterland taught me that. The Country of Almost rearranged itself whenever nobody watched, because it was made of forgotten things, and forgotten things have no spine. But when I drew it — when I committed lines to paper and named the distances — it held. It obeyed. I had crossed an entire shifting nation on the strength of my ink.

And this court? This court was not made of stone pretending to be bureaucracy. It was bureaucracy pretending to be stone. Every noble a personified clause, every bailiff a writ with legs, every corridor paperwork grown vast and architectural. The whole mountain ran on ledgers, contracts, margins, seals. It ran on *documentation.*

And documentation, my friends, is just cartography that got ambitious.

So: they had stripped me of every external record of myself. Fine. There is one map no bailiff can confiscate, no ledger can index, no clause can seize. It isn't written anywhere. It has no original, no copy, no location in any archive. It cannot be bought, stolen, burned, or filed. It exists in exactly one place, held by exactly one person, owned by no one — because the moment you own it, it stops being true.

A map drawn from memory.

I closed my eyes, which changed nothing, since the light came from nowhere anyway, and I began.

Not with the underworld. Start small. Start with what you know better than your own pulse. I drew Thornvale first — the valley, my valley, the one that refused magic at its borders and called it safety and paid for it, though none of us knew the currency until now. I drew it the way I'd drawn it a hundred times: the river's lazy S through the middle, the orchard terraces, the mill with its crooked chimney, the inn at the valley's edge where a man had once dropped his coat because brambles walked in on root-legs wearing his dead grandmother's voice.

The inn. The doorway. The coat.

I held it all in my head, line by line, and here is the thing I need you to believe, because I barely believed it myself: it *held*. In a cell engineered to have no geography, I built geography. The walls didn't move — couldn't, somehow, in the space I was making, because I had declared them fixed. I set a scale. I set a north. North was toward Ivo. Cartographically improper, sentimentally impeccable.

Then, very carefully, I added the first feature that did not belong to my cell: a corridor. Grey stone, candle-sconces burning salt-flame, the faint smell of wax and old vellum that pervades this entire mountain like the smell of a bank vault. I knew the corridor existed. I had walked parts of it disguised as an archivist of debts, ink-stained and terrified and taking notes with my eyes. Memory is a survey instrument too. Nobody ever tells you that.

I opened my eyes.

The wall in front of me — the seamless, cornerless, reference-free wall — had a seam in it.

Faint. Hairline. Like a crack in porcelain, running floor to ceiling, roughly where my drawn corridor's entrance should be. The grey glow bent slightly around it, the way water bends around a submerged stone.

I laughed. It came out ragged and slightly unhinged, which felt appropriate. Somewhere in this mountain, I suspected, a ledger had just developed an anomaly it couldn't explain, and some clerk-noble was going to spend a very frustrating century trying to file it.

The realm obeys cartography. And I am, whatever else they've confiscated, still a cartographer.

* * *

Here is the discipline of it, because it is not easy, and I would rather you understand the cost than think I simply wished hard enough.

Drawing a map in your head is like holding a hundred notes of a song at once. Every line wants to drift. Every distance wants to renegotiate. Fear gets into the measurements — I'd draw a corridor forty paces long and it would come out sixty, because sixty felt safer, and a dishonest map is worse than no map, because reality takes you at your word. So I learned, over hours or days, to draw clean. To draw what *is*, not what I hoped. Honesty as a technical skill. My mother would have been proud, if I could still remember her face — which I can't, which I traded for passage across lost roads, and which I refuse to regret on schedule; grief keeps its own calendar.

I rebuilt the underworld in my mind, section by section, the way you'd reconstruct a city you'd only ever seen from its gutters.

The debt-vaults, where I'd found Ivo among the shelved obligations, where we'd decoded the margin-note in mapmaker's notation — *stolen before new moon, void* — three days that had seemed like such a generous allowance at the time. The corridors of writ, where bailiffs hunted us, their bodies literally contracts: parchment skin, wax-seal hearts, clauses for teeth. The heart-chamber, which I had mapped in my head while pretending to consider the court's deal — the thorn-caged lantern at its center, the concentric rings of defense, each ring a clause, each clause a noble, the geometry of greed rendered as fortification.

And the cell block. Because I was in it, and a cartographer surveys her own position first. That's not poetry; that's procedure. Fix your own coordinates before anything else, or every measurement downstream is garbage.

My coordinates were these: a cell with no edges, somewhere below the debt-vaults, near — I was nearly certain — the archive annexes, because prisons are always built near archives. Jails and libraries are the same building with different lighting. I'd bet my confiscated pen on it.

I drew the annexes from memory. I drew the reading rooms where I'd found Ivo's contract, the margin-scrawl in notation only a mapmaker would write — and I still didn't know whose hand that was, and I intended to find out, because whoever wrote an escape clause into a soul-contract in *my* professional shorthand was either an ancestor, an ally, or a ghost with excellent penmanship.

And then, on the third iteration — third full reconstruction, each one faster than the last, the way a route grows familiar — I noticed something that stopped my breath.

My mental map had a blank region. A large one, west of the heart-chamber, south of the vaults. On paper, a blank region means *unsurveyed.* But this wasn't unsurveyed. This was *redacted* — and redaction, in my experience, is just information wearing a locked door. More than that: the blanks weren't empty. When I held the map steady and looked at the shape of the absence, the absence had structure. Negative space has edges. Edges can be surveyed.

There was something beneath the court. Something the court had drawn a box around and labeled, in effect, DO NOT SURVEY.

Which, historically, is how you tell a cartographer where to go.

I sat in my edgeless cell, in front of my hairline seam, and felt the strangest sensation of my life: the mountain leaning on me. Not attacking — I don't think it knew how to classify me anymore. I had become, from the ledger's perspective, a rounding error with intent. But the realm obeys cartography, and I was carrying a country of it in my skull, growing more detailed by the hour, and the realm could *feel* it, the way a floor feels footsteps.

Somewhere above me, I'd have sworn, a bailiff paused mid-stride and could not remember why.

* * *

I worked until I couldn't tell whether I was hungry, which in a cell with no meals was either encouraging or alarming. I reconstructed the seam-corridor, then the stair it implied, then the junction, then the long gallery of unclaimed debts where I'd once hidden behind a filing cabinet the size of a modest barn. Each feature confirmed the others. Each confirmation tightened the map. That's the secret joy of surveying, the thing I never told anyone because it sounds deranged: it's a conversation. You ask the land a question, the land answers, and if you listen carefully, the answers start arriving before you finish asking.

This land was starting to do that.

When I drew a door in my mental map of the corridor beyond my seam — just a door, plain, oak, iron-banded, entirely fictional — I felt a faint, distant, structural *interest*, like the mountain clearing its throat. When I gave the door a lock and then, deliberately, drew the keyhole's mechanism in cross-section, the interest sharpened into something almost like attention.

Understand what I'm saying. I wasn't just mapping the underworld anymore.

I was proposing amendments.

The court ran on documents, and I had discovered the one document they could never seize, audit, burn, or buy: the one in my head, unwitnessed, unwritten, owned by no one. Their entire power was the power of the recorded claim. Mine was the power of the unrecordable truth. And truth, given enough precision, beats paperwork every time, because paperwork is only truth's nervous, bureaucratic cousin.

I thought about Ivo. I let myself, briefly, properly — not as motivation, not as fuel, but as a person. The way he'd looked at me in the stacks, fierce and interrupted. The way fragments of his stolen soul had been leaking back into him on our midnight run — the taste of first snow, his grandmother's apology, a boy who'd never learned to be afraid — and the terrible softness in his voice when he'd asked me which self I'd fallen for. Both, I should have told him. All of them. Any version of you that lets me argue with you about maps.

He was somewhere below the heart-chamber. I was nearly certain of that too, now. The court wouldn't keep him far from his forfeit soul; a pledge stays near its collateral. Which meant the redacted region — the blank with edges — was either where they kept him or where they kept the thing that mattered most.

Either way: that was my next survey.

I settled against the seamless wall, closed my eyes, and went back into the map. Deeper this time. Past the galleries, past the vaults, right up to the border of the redaction, where I laid my imaginary instruments down on the imaginary ground and began, patiently, professionally, to survey the shape of what the court refused to show.

The negative space pushed back. Of course it did. Redactions are maintained by effort, and effort has a texture, and texture can be measured. I pressed my attention along its edges the way you'd run a thumb along a wall looking for a hidden latch, and slowly, slowly, the blank began to confess. Not its contents — its *contours*. The shape of the censorship. And the shape of the censorship, if you must know, looked remarkably like a second court. Older. Smaller. Sealed off beneath the current one like a cellar beneath a bank.

Something down there predated the ledgers. Something the Magistrate had drawn a box around and hoped no one with a steady hand would ever notice.

The seam in my cell wall had widened while I worked. Barely — a finger's width of darkness, breathing faintly with salt-and-wax air from a corridor that did not exist until I insisted otherwise.

I stood up. I had no name, no maps, no instruments, no face for my mother, and no idea what waited in a sealed court older than the law above it.

What I had was the whole underworld in my head, and a finger-width of door.

"Right," I said to the cell, to the mountain, to whichever clerk-noble was currently failing to file me. "Let's talk amendments."

I stepped through the seam — into a corridor I had invented, which stepped politely aside to let me pass, because I was its author, and it knew it.

Behind me, far above, I heard the first alarm bell of the court begin to ring — and then stop, mid-peal, confused, as if it had checked its own ledger and found no entry authorizing the ringing.

Good. Neither had I.
