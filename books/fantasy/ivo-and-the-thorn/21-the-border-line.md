## 21. The Border Line

The underworld did not end so much as lose interest.

Behind us the mountain was folding itself shut like a ledger after a bad year — corridors rolling up into scrolls, bailiffs sloughing into unsigned paper that scattered on winds that had no business existing underground. The Magistrate's scream had followed us for the first mile. Then it had become a groan. Then it had become the particular silence of an institution realizing it has been dissolved.

I did not look back. Ivo could not stop looking back, which worried me more than anything else, because looking back is a luxury and he was spending it like a man who suspected his account had been frozen.

"Beatrix." His voice came out wrong — layered, faintly doubled, as though two people were saying it a half-beat apart and only one of them had learned to be polite about it. "The seam. How far?"

"Half a day," I said. "If the hinterland cooperates."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we negotiate. I've gotten rather good at negotiating with geography."

He laughed, and the laugh had shadows in it. Not metaphorical ones. Actual shadows, thin as spilled ink, moving under his skin when the light hit at the wrong angle. I had watched them arrive in the heart-chamber, watched him press the lantern-soul into his own chest and stand there while something enormous poured into a vessel built for one life instead of two. Half of him now belonged to a country that no longer existed. The other half belonged to Thornvale, where magic went to die.

I did not say out loud what both of us were doing the arithmetic on. We walked instead.

* * *

The hinterland was dying with its court, and it died the way forgotten things die: reluctantly, and in reverse. Roads we had crossed on the way in were un-crossing themselves. A bridge we'd paid a memory to cross was reassembling itself plank by plank, eager to be whole again, to be *owed* again. Somewhere behind us a river ran uphill toward its source, unspilling.

Ivo stumbled twice in the first hour. By the third hour he was stumbling because his feet kept forgetting which direction down was.

"Tell me what it feels like," I said. "Not for the map. For me."

He sat down on a stone that might have been a milestone in some dead traveler's memory, and pressed his palms flat against his knees like a man steadying a table.

"Like being a house," he said, "where someone left a door open all winter. Half of me is warm. Half of me is snow. And the warm half keeps trying to close the door and the cold half keeps insisting the door was always open, that it never knew any other way to be." He looked up at me, and I saw that his eyes had changed too — the brown of them shot through now with a grey like slate seen underwater. "Which half do you suppose fell in love with you?"

"The one that's still talking," I said. "The other one strikes me as a sulker."

He smiled. Under his skin, the shadows moved like something turning over in its sleep.

We kept walking.

* * *

By dusk the Country of Almost was almost nothing. The shifting hinterland that had rearranged itself whenever we blinked was now rearranging itself even while we stared, unable to hold a shape long enough to be crossed. Hills dissolved into fields mid-stride. A forest became a memory of a forest became a suggestion of a forest became, briefly and insultingly, a very nice sitting room with a fire lit, before collapsing back into bracken.

I unrolled my maps on the ground and weighted the corners with stones, and they held — my ink, my lines, the only fixed points left between the mountain and home. But every time I looked away and looked back, the margins had crept inward. The paper was forgetting itself from the edges in.

"We're losing the road," Ivo said quietly.

"No," I said. "The road is losing us. There's a difference, and the difference matters."

"How can you possibly know that?"

"Because I'm a cartographer, and cartographers are professionally optimistic liars." I rolled the maps up and stood. "Come on. The seam should be over the next ridge."

It wasn't. It was over the three ridges after that, and by then the moon was rising — the new moon, the deadline moon, the moon whose arrival had once meant our souls forfeit and now meant nothing at all except that it was very dark, and getting darker, and Ivo was getting colder.

I felt the change in him before I saw it. The warmth of his hand going strange against mine, not cold exactly but *thin*, the way a candle flame goes thin just before the draught takes it. He was closer to Thornvale now than he had been since childhood. And Thornvale does not abide magic. That was the whole point of the valley; that was the bargain his grandmother had struck with his soul as the coin. The border line would not welcome the half of him made of underworld. The border line would treat it as an infection, and burn it out, and take whatever of Ivo happened to be attached.

"You've figured it out," he said. We had stopped walking without either of us deciding to. Ahead of us, maybe fifty yards, the air had a seam in it — a hairline brightness, the place where my world began and this one gave up pretending to be anywhere. Home. Fifty yards of dying hinterland between us and it, and I could already feel the valley pulling at him like a tide, greedy and clean and merciless.

"Yes," I said.

"So have I." He said it calmly. That was the worst part. He said it the way he ordered breakfast. "You should go through. You can draw the border closed behind you. Keep everything out. Keep yourself safe. That was always your gift — you can make a line mean something."

"That was always my curse," I said, "and I am not leaving you fifty yards short of a life."

"Beatrix—"

"Ivo." I took off my satchel and set it on the ground, and I knelt beside it, and I did the thing I should have done hours ago, the thing I had been circling since the heart-chamber like a moth that hadn't admitted it was a moth. "Sit down. I need flat ground and I need you not to argue with me for approximately ten minutes. Can you manage ten minutes of not arguing? For me? Think of it as a date."

"It's midnight," he said. But he sat.

"I've had worse anniversaries."

* * *

Here is what I knew, kneeling in the ruins of a country that had fed on forgotten things until it forgot itself: magic cannot cross the Thornvale border. Everyone knows this. Every compass spins, every spell dies, every charm gutters out within a mile of the line. The valley is magic-dead. It has been magic-dead since 1897. It has been magic-dead because a woman named Ilsa sold her grandson's soul to buy that death, and I had spent three weeks learning exactly what such a bargain cost and how such a bargain was written.

And here is the thing they do not put in the histories, because the histories are written by people who think maps record the world instead of the other way around:

A border is not a wall. A border is a *line*, and a line is only ever a decision that someone drew once and everyone else agreed to keep agreeing to. I had spent my whole life mapping the unmapped from inside a valley that refused magic, and I had never once asked myself the obvious question — *refused how? Who taught the border to refuse? What hand drew the line that spells die on?*

Someone's. Someone drew it. Which meant someone could redraw it.

The court beneath the mountain had understood this better than my valley ever had. That was why it feared cartographers. That was why the prophecy book's blank pages were worth a war to it. Paper obeys whoever holds the pen — I had proven that in the heart-chamber, redrawing their corridors out from under their feet while their bailiffs came apart into unsigned paper around me. The realm obeyed cartography. All realms do. They simply don't advertise it.

So I took out a fresh sheet, and my pen, and the last of my ink — the good ink, the ink that bled sideways at the border and opened doors, the ink that had gotten me into this and was now, if I had anything to say about it, going to get us both out.

"What are you drawing?" Ivo asked.

"A country," I said. "Small one. Population two."

"Beatrix, the border won't bend for—"

"It bent for a grandmother's grief," I said. "In 1897 a woman wanted her valley safe more than she wanted her grandson whole, and reality filed the paperwork. Grief moved the line once. Don't tell me love can't move it again. Love has better references."

I began to draw.

I will not pretend it was easy. Ink that isn't backed by a court, by a prophecy, by a century of accumulated agreement, has to be carried entirely on the hand that draws it, and my hand shook, and the shaking got into the lines, and for a while my small country had borders like a seismograph reading. But I have been drawing maps since I was nine years old. I know what a coastline looks like when it means it. And I was not drawing terrain. Terrain is easy. I was drawing terms.

A small country. Two citizens. One border, drawn not around the valley but around *us* — a circle of perhaps twenty feet, here, in the dying hinterland, fifty yards from the great refusing line of Thornvale. And at the point where my little border met the valley's great one, I did not break my line or theirs. I drew a junction. A confluence. The place where two rivers agree, for a while, to be one water.

Because the valley's border refused magic — fine. Let it refuse. My country was not offering magic. My country was offering *citizenship*. And citizenship is not magic. Citizenship is paperwork. And I had just spent three weeks in a realm made entirely of paperwork, and I had learned its grammar the way you learn a language when failing to learn it kills you.

*Let them in,* I wrote along the junction line, in notation no court had invented and therefore no court had forbidden — mapmaker's shorthand, margin-language, the secret script of people who describe the world instead of commanding it. *Two souls, traveling together. One native, returning. One naturalized, by marriage of borders. Their debts are discharged. Their claim is settled. Let them in.*

I signed it. My name had been taken by a ferryman, stripped from every map I'd ever made, and I had thought I would spend the rest of my life as an unplaceable woman. But a signature doesn't have to be a name. A signature is proof of intent, and I intended, oh, I intended with my whole borrowed, bartered, half-erased self.

"There," I said. My voice cracked on the second syllable and I pretended it hadn't. "One country, ready for habitation. It's terribly small. The scenery leaves something to be desired. The government is a dictatorship, I'm afraid, and the dictator is exhausted."

Ivo was staring at the page. The shadows under his skin had gone still, listening.

"It's a map," he said.

"It's a treaty," I said. "Maps are just treaties the land hasn't read yet. Come on. We're presenting ourselves at the border."

* * *

Fifty yards. I had thought it would be a march. It was a crawl, in the end — the hinterland made its last arguments on the way, hills softening underfoot like old bread, distances lying, a wind full of whispers offering us rest, offering us forgetting, offering to make us part of itself, two more forgotten things in a country of them. Ivo went down twice and I hauled him up twice, and the third time I didn't haul, I lay down beside him in the grey grass and put his cold hand against my sternum and said, "Feel that? That's a heartbeat. It's keeping time for both of us until further notice. Get up."

He got up.

And then the seam was in front of us, the hairline brightness, the edge of everywhere I had ever known — and I felt the valley notice us. Felt it the way you feel a landlord's eye through a keyhole. The great refusing line woke along its whole invisible length, and turned its attention to the thing approaching wearing half a soul of the underworld, and began, gently, terribly, the work of refusal.

Ivo gasped. Went to one knee. The shadows rose in him like silt stirred in dark water, and beyond them I saw the light of him guttering — the warm half of the house trying to close the door on the cold half, and the cold half beginning, horribly, to agree that maybe the door should close, maybe the cold half should go, should stay here in the dark between countries where at least it was allowed to exist—

"No," I said, and I spread my map on the threshold of the seam, my ridiculous twenty-foot nation, and I knelt on it with both knees, and I took his hand and put it on the page between the two names I had written there, side by side, in the same ink, at the same altitude, equidistant from every border. "Read your address, sir. You live here now. Both halves of you. It's in the deed."

The valley pushed. My little country held.

I felt it happen — felt the junction take the weight, the two lines meeting and arguing and, slowly, the way rivers argue, agreeing. The border of Thornvale did not break. I want to be clear about that; I did not break it, I would not have broken it, that line had kept my people safe for a hundred years and breaking it was not mine to do. It *bent*. It leaned, the way a tree leans toward light it has been denied, and at the single point where my small country touched it, the great refusing line curved inward, and made a room, and the room was the size of two people, and it let us in.

Warmth. Real warmth, valley-warmth, the smell of thorn and loam and the far-off mineral breath of the river. The seam closed behind us like a book whose ending had finally been approved. And Ivo collapsed onto actual grass, in actual night air, under an actual moon — the new moon, three days late and forgiven — and the shadows under his skin did not burn away, and did not spread. They stayed. Settled. Became, I think, the same kind of resident the rest of him was: present, permanent, belonging by treaty rather than birth.

He laughed. It was a terrible, wet, wonderful laugh, and it had only one voice in it now, and the voice was entirely his.

"You drew me into existence," he said.

"I redrew you," I corrected, lying down beside him on the grass of the only country I would ever need. "There's a difference, and the difference matters. Besides, you'd have done it for me."

"I'd have done it worse. My handwriting is atrocious."

Somewhere across the valley, in the archive where a certain book had waited a hundred years for its final page to be read, I would later swear I felt a pen move. But that is tomorrow's business. Tonight there was only grass, and starlight, and the sound of the border line holding — bent, but holding — and Ivo's hand finding mine in the dark with the unerring accuracy of a man navigating by a map he has finally been allowed to read.

"Beatrix," he said. "What happens to the valley now? If the contract is struck, if the mountain forgets — what comes in?"

I looked up at the stars, which seemed, for the first time in a hundred years, to be leaning slightly closer.

"Honestly?" I said. "I think we're about to find out."

Far off across the dark fields, where the border line ran invisible through the thorns, something glimmered — faint, green-gold, patient as ink drying — as if the valley's long refusal had finally, softly, begun to answer.
