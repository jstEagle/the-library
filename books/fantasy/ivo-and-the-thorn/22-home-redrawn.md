## 22. Home, Redrawn

The morning Thornvale woke up magic, I was asleep on Ivo's kitchen floor with a compass in my hand and ink on my cheek, which is not the position I would have chosen for the most important cartographic event in a century, but the underworld does not consult your preferences. Neither, apparently, does dawn.

I woke because the light was wrong.

Not bad-wrong. Good-wrong. The kind of wrong that makes you sit up too fast and bang your head on a table you've known your whole life. The light coming through Ivo's window had color in it — a faint gold-green shimmer, like sunlight through bottle glass, like something alive had gotten into the air supply. I lay there for a moment, blinking, and watched a mote of dust drift past the window and *glow*.

"Get up," I said to Ivo, who was asleep on the bench by the fire, half in his coat, shadows pooling under his skin like tea leaves settling in a cup. "Ivo. Get up. Your valley is doing something."

He surfaced slowly, the way he did everything those first weeks — as though he wasn't entirely sure the world would still be there when he arrived. He'd been back four days. Four days of eating everything Mrs. Pell brought to the door, sleeping twelve hours at a stretch, and flinching whenever anyone said his grandmother's name, which in Thornvale was often, because grandmothers are civic infrastructure here.

"What is it?" he mumbled.

"Come see."

He came to the window and stood beside me, and we watched together as the border line — my border line, the one I'd spent eleven years mapping, the one where compasses spun and spells died — simply stopped being a wall and became a *sieve*. Magic came through it the way water comes through cloth: gently, everywhere at once, without force. A bramble at the edge of his garden put out one exploratory tendril and bloomed out of season, purple flowers opening like small astonished mouths.

"It's seeping," I said. "It's just — seeping in."

Ivo was quiet a long moment. His hand found mine without either of us arranging it; we'd gotten efficient about that. Under his skin, faint as watermark, the shadows moved. Half-claimed, the ferryman had called him. Half-claimed and wholly himself, I maintained, and I was the cartographer, so my word was law in at least one profession.

"Do you think it will stop?" he asked.

"I think," I said, "that borders only hold if both sides agree to them. And I don't think the other side agrees anymore."

* * *

By noon, half the valley had turned out at the edge of the green, wearing the particular expression of people who have lived their whole lives inside a locked room and have just noticed the door standing open. Nobody panicked. Thornvale doesn't panic; Thornvale *gossips*, which is slower and more thorough.

Old Marta Hesse stood at the boundary stone where her family had kept bees for six generations, holding one hand out into the shimmer. A moth landed on her finger and glowed faintly blue, and she looked at it the way you look at a letter from someone long dead.

"My mother told me the valley was cursed," she said. "Safe, but cursed. She said we were lucky."

"We were," Ivo said quietly. "That's the worst part."

And it was. That was the thing I couldn't stop turning over, the way you turn over a stone to see what's underneath even when you already know: the valley's peace had been real. Children here had grown up without fear of the thorn-crowned court. Farmers' fields hadn't withered under fae bargains. Every good ordinary day anyone in Thornvale had ever had was built on top of a boy's stolen soul, paid out in installments across twenty-three years, and no amount of justice could make that not true. You cannot un-eat a meal. You can only decide what to cook next.

Mrs. Pell, who had fed Ivo through every winter of his childhood and never once asked why he sometimes went quiet as a held breath, pushed through the crowd and took his face in her hands.

"You look terrible," she announced, which was her highest register of affection. "You look like you've been somewhere."

"He has," I said.

She looked at me over Ivo's shoulder. "You brought him back."

"I drew us back. There's a difference."

"There is," she agreed, to my surprise, and kissed his forehead, and then mine, and then went off to tell everyone that Brightwater's girl had walked into hell with nothing but paper and come out with a person, which was true, and also somehow made it sound like I'd done it for a bet.

* * *

The maps changed first. Or rather — they changed *back*, or forward, or sideways; the vocabulary for this hasn't been invented yet, and I intend to invent it, so consider this a placeholder.

Before, my maps showed Thornvale perfectly and everything beyond it as blank paper, because the valley refused to be seen past and I refused to lie about it. Now, when I unrolled the old surveys on my worktable, the margins had begun to fill in. Not with anything dramatic — a hint of road here, a suggestion of hills there, drawn in a hand that was almost mine but softer, more tentative, like a stranger introducing themselves carefully. Places, showing themselves to me a little at a time. Trusting me.

I sat with the map of the hinterland — the Country of Almost, the shifting gray country between the veil and the mountain — and watched it render itself onto my oldest sheet, the one I'd drawn at nineteen, full of errors and bravado. It corrected itself gently, the way a friend corrects your spelling rather than your character.

"They're showing you where things really are," Ivo said from the doorway. He'd taken to watching me work with an expression I couldn't classify, somewhere between wonder and homesickness, which made sense, because part of him was still homesick for a place that had tried to eat him.

"Places trust me now," I said. "Which is flattering and slightly alarming. What did I do to earn it?"

"You redrew their country," he said. "You erased a court. If I were a place, I'd trust you too."

I laughed, and then, because the light was right and the moment was there, I got up and crossed the room and kissed him, which we had both become much better at since the underworld, since everything now carried the flavor of *could have been otherwise*. He tasted like tea and faintly, always, of cold mountain air. I've decided not to mind. Some souvenirs you keep.

* * *

The shadows under his skin didn't fade. We checked. Dr. Ellery, who had delivered Ivo and buried his grandmother and was therefore entitled to opinions about both, examined him by lamplight and pronounced him "the healthiest impossible man I've ever seen." The darkness moved when he moved, settled when he slept, and on the new moon — our new moon now, the one that had nearly been his deadline — it rose close to the surface and glimmered like fish under ice.

"It's not a wound," I told him, the night he finally asked the question out loud. "Wounds want to heal. This isn't asking for anything."

"Then what is it?"

"A citizenship," I said. "Dual. You spent three days down there and a hundred years of yourself before that. Of course some of the dark stayed. It's yours. You bought it fair."

He was quiet, turning his hands over, watching the shadow shift in the creases of his palms like ink in water. "Sometimes I remember things," he said. "From the soul. From before. Grandmother crying in the kitchen at night, thinking I was asleep. The taste of snow. A version of me who wasn't afraid of anything, and I keep waiting to feel jealous of him, and I don't. I just miss him. Like missing a brother."

"You're allowed to miss him," I said. "You're also allowed to be the one who survived. Those aren't competing jobs."

"And which one did you fall for?"

I considered lying, briefly, for comedy. "The one who walked into a court of monsters and told the truth about his grandmother until the bailiffs couldn't bear to listen. Fear and all. The fearless one sounds exhausting."

He smiled — the whole smile, the one that reached whatever was living under his skin, because it all reached, everything reached, that was the point of him. Then he said, "Beatrix. What happens to the valley now?"

"Now? Now it chooses. That's the whole amendment. I didn't break the contract, Ivo. I struck the fraud from it, and what's left is a valley that can be warded or opened, sealed or shared, decided by the people who live here instead of by a bargain struck in a kitchen a century ago. Old Marta's bees will decide. Mrs. Pell will decide. Probably by committee. It'll take years and involve shouting."

"That sounds like home," he said.

"It does," I agreed. "God help us all."

* * *

We went to the border seam on the last evening of autumn — the place where I'd first seen my own ink bleed sideways, the door only a cartographer would see. It was still there, but changed: no longer a wound in the air, just a thinness, a place where the world spoke slightly more quietly. We brought bread and cheese and a flask of cider, because if you're going to sit at the edge of reality, you should do it properly provisioned.

The seam breathed. In, out. On the far side, the Country of Almost lay quiet, its gray softened to silver, its forgotten things resting easier now that no court was harvesting them. Somewhere out there, the mountain was forgetting itself, corridor by corridor, clause by clause, dissolving into geology. I liked to imagine the Magistrate of Debts unraveling into unsigned paper, blowing away across a landscape with no ledger left to hold it. I liked imagining it very much. I did it daily.

"Will you go back?" Ivo asked. "To map it properly?"

"Eventually. When it's finished becoming whatever it's becoming. You can't survey a country that's still deciding what shape it is." I paused. "Will you come?"

He looked at the seam for a long time. The shadows under his skin stirred, attentive, like a dog hearing its name from another room.

"Yes," he said finally. "But I want it on record that I'll complain the entire time."

"Noted for the record," I said, and wrote it down, because I write everything down.

* * *

Here is the last thing, the thing I almost didn't believe until I saw it with my own eyes, and I see things with my own eyes professionally.

The prophecy book sat on my shelf for a month, blank again, its 1897 pages gone white as fresh snow once the contract was struck from existence. Blank pages, the court had wanted them so badly — pages to write new debts on. But the court was gone, and the book remained, and a book is a habit before it is an object.

On the first of winter, I found ink on the final page.

Not wet this time. Settled. Certain. Written in a hand that was mine and also older than mine, the same hand that had filled the margins of Ivo's contract with escape in mapmaker's notation — the hand, I suspect, of every cartographer who ever loved someone enough to redraw the world around them:

*The thief returned, and kept what he stole.*

Below it, smaller, fresher, as if the book had thought of one more thing:

*The valley chose to open. The valley chose to stay. Both choices were true.*

And below that, in a third hand — uneven, unfamiliar, learning itself as it went, and I would know that handwriting anywhere because I have spent four days teaching it to hold a pen:

*Ivo was here. So was Beatrix. We live at the edge of the map, and the edge is where everything begins.*

I closed the book. Outside, the first snow of the season was falling through a light that glowed faintly gold-green, and down in the valley Mrs. Pell was lighting lamps, and the border line ran soft and shining around us like a drawn breath — not a wall, not a cage, just a line, and lines, I have always believed, are only promises about where one thing ends and another begins.

Somewhere beyond it, places were waiting to be named.

I sharpened my pencil, and I began.
