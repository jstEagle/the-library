## 2. A Proclamation Nailed to Every Door

The proclamation arrived on a Tuesday, which Mirabel Quintana considered a personal insult. Tuesdays were ledger days—clean, orderly days when the worst thing that could happen was an arithmetic error or a client who insisted on paying for a bottled thunderstorm in installments. Tuesdays were not, as a rule, the day the world ended.

He heard it first as a sound: the particular clatter of Pellam Wicks, the guild's youngest clerk and its fastest runner, taking the front stairs three at a time in direct violation of a standing rule Corvane had posted for eleven years. Then Pellam burst into the counting room with his cap askew and his face the color of good parchment left out in weather.

"Master Quintana," he gasped, "there's a man nailing things to doors."

"Nailing things to doors is generally what men do," Mirabel said, dipping his pen. "It's why we invented hinges."

"These are *proclamations*, sir." Pellam lowered his voice as though the word itself might be listening. "Crown proclamations. He's nailed one to the baker's, one to the chandler's, one to the *guild's own door*, and he's got a whole satchel more."

Mirabel set down his pen with great care, the way one sets down something that might otherwise be thrown.

The Guild of Impossible Things occupied a narrow building wedged between a wig-maker's and a shop that sold, exclusively, keys that opened nothing—a fact Mirabel had always found either very funny or very ominous, depending on the week. The guild traded in impossibilities of the small and merciful kind: last words bottled for grieving daughters, a jar of someone's childhood summer, maps to places that had stopped existing. It was dusty, it was underfunded, and for nine years it had been the safest place Mirabel knew.

He followed Pellam down to the street.

The man with the satchel was already moving on to the next door, but the proclamation on the guild's threshold fluttered in the morning wind, and Mirabel read it twice because the first time his eyes refused to cooperate.

*By order of His Majesty's Crown,* it began, in the crisp bureaucratic hand that always made cruelty look tidy, *let it be known throughout the realm that a great sickness of sight has taken root among the populace. There are those who can perceive what ought not be perceived—who chart the unmapped, who see through the Veil that mercifully divides our world from the wild country beyond it. This sight is not a gift. It is a wound in the realm's flesh.*

*All persons possessing such sight shall present themselves at their district warden-house within thirty days to be registered.*

*Those who register shall be provided for.*

*Those who do not shall be found.*

*And those who are found having concealed themselves shall be put to death, that the realm may be made whole.*

There was more—the usual seals, the usual signatures—but Mirabel stopped reading. Around him, the street had gone quiet in that specific way streets go quiet: everyone still moving, buying bread, arguing about the price of candles, but all of it performed slightly too loudly, slightly too busily, like actors who have spotted fire backstage.

"They're saying it in the fish market already," said Mrs. Halloway from the wig-shop, appearing at Mirabel's elbow with the speed of a woman who had never once missed anything. "Register or die. My cousin's neighbor swears her boy has the sight—sees his dead grandmother at supper, polite as you please—and now they're saying he'll be *provided for*, whatever that means. Provided for like a ham, I expect."

"Mrs. Halloway," Mirabel said mildly, "perhaps we shouldn't discuss this in the open street."

"Perhaps *you* shouldn't," she said, and looked at him a moment too long before retreating behind her window of powdered curls.

Mirabel stood very still.

Nine years. Nine years of ledgers and ink-stained cuffs and Corvane's dry voice correcting his margins. Nine years of being nobody in particular, a clerk with neat handwriting and no remarkable qualities whatsoever, and he had believed—had genuinely, foolishly believed—that the crown had no interest in nobodies.

He went back inside and locked the door behind him, which was absurd, since the proclamation was nailed to the outside of it.

* * *

By midday, the whole guild had read the thing, and the whole guild was whispering about it in the way of people who believe walls have recently developed opinions.

The counting room, usually a haven of shuffling paper and Pellam's terrible humming, had become a low murmur around the long table. Sela Voss sat across from Mirabel, ostensibly reconciling a column of figures for a client who had purchased a memory of snow, though she hadn't turned a page in ten minutes. Her dark eyes kept flicking up—at him, at the window, at the ceiling, as if assembling the room from scattered pieces.

"You've read it," she said. Not a question.

"I've read it."

"And?"

"And it's very neatly written," Mirabel said. "Whoever does the crown's proclamations has excellent penmanship. You could learn something."

"Don't." Sela's voice was quiet, which anyone who knew her understood was worse than loud. Sela loud meant she was angry about something ordinary. Sela quiet meant she was frightened, and Sela Voss was not frightened by anything so mundane as dying; she'd once told a debt-collector that if he touched her abacus she would haunt him professionally. "Don't make jokes at it. Jokes are how you pretend things aren't happening, and I need one person in this building who isn't pretending."

Mirabel looked at her. She was twenty-six, sharp-faced, perpetually inked to the elbows, and she had covered for him exactly four times over the years without ever once asking what she was covering for—the time he'd needed the map-room alone until dawn, the time a client's impossible commission had left him shaking, the two occasions his midnight inks had glowed faintly through the floorboards of the clerks' dormitory and he'd claimed, lamely, to be conducting experiments in luminescent varnish. She had accepted the varnish explanation with a flat stare that said she knew it was nonsense and was choosing, deliberately, to let him keep his nonsense.

"I'm not pretending," he said finally. "I'm balancing. If I stop balancing, I'll start thinking, and if I start thinking—" He turned his palm up on the table, briefly, so only she could see it. There was a smudge of silver-green ink along his thumb that no ordinary ink ever left. "Thirty days, Sela."

Her jaw tightened. "You could register."

"You've met me. Do you honestly think I'd hand my name to a warden-house and trust it to 'be provided for'?"

"No," she admitted. "I think you'd hide in a drawer if you could fit. Which raises the question of what you're going to do instead, because hiding in drawers is not a strategy, Mirabel, it's a personality trait."

Before he could answer, the counting room door opened, and the whispering died so completely that Mirabel could hear the wig-shop's clock ticking through the wall.

Corvane entered the way he always did: without hurry, without expression, carrying a single folder as though the entire building's fate were a matter of filing. Guildmaster Corvane was a tall, spare man somewhere past sixty, gray as ash and nearly as smooth, with the peculiar gift of making silence feel like agreement. In nine years Mirabel had seen him angry exactly never, and that had always struck Mirabel as either tremendous discipline or tremendous concealment.

"My children," Corvane said, which was how he addressed all of them, including the ones older than himself. "I know the proclamation has caused distress. I ask you to hear me before the distress does your thinking for you."

He laid the folder on the table and did not open it.

"The Veilhunt is not aimed at us. Consider what we are: clerks, bookkeepers, traders of curiosities. We deal in the *records* of impossible things, not the things themselves. A man who sells a bottled memory is a merchant. A man who charts the unmapped—" Corvane paused, precisely, the way he paused before every important word, "—would be another matter entirely. But we employ no such man. The guild's charter is explicit. Our registry lists every member, every trade, every transaction. We are transparent. Transparency is armor."

A few shoulders around the table eased. Mirabel watched them ease and felt something cold settle in his stomach, because he knew—for certain, absolutely knew—that the guild's registry listed no such man, and that the absence was a lie Corvane had been maintaining for nine years with Mirabel's grateful cooperation.

"What about the others?" asked Odile from acquisitions, whose hands were never steady but were especially unsteady now. "The ones in the city. The ones who'll run."

"Some will run," Corvane agreed. "Some will be caught running, which will be worse than registering. Some will register and discover that 'provided for' means what Mrs. Halloway's fish market thinks it means." He said it without flinching, and that, too, was part of his gift: he could name horrors the way other men named the weather. "But hear me. Panic is how the crown wins. A frightened gifted person makes mistakes. A calm one survives. So we will be calm. We will keep our ledgers. We will conduct our trades quietly and lawfully, and in thirty days the wardens will walk past our door because there is nothing here worth finding."

His gaze crossed the room—level, unhurried, touching each face—and when it reached Mirabel it did not pause, did not linger, did not so much as blink. That was the most frightening thing of all. Corvane looked at him exactly as he always did, and Mirabel could not decide whether that meant *you are safe* or *I have already accounted for you.*

"Back to work," Corvane said, collected his folder, and left. The whispering resumed within a minute, but it had changed texture—soothed, dulled, half-believing. Corvane's voice did that. It made catastrophe sound like a scheduling conflict.

Sela leaned close. "You didn't believe a word of that."

"I believed the part about panic," Mirabel said. "The rest was a lullaby." He rubbed the silver-green smudge on his thumb, watching it catch the light. "Sela, he didn't say the registry was false. He said it was *transparent*. Those are different claims, and he knows the difference better than anyone alive."

* * *

That night, Mirabel did not sleep.

He climbed to his narrow room above the counting room, barred the door, lit no lamp—for lamps showed through windows, and windows had witnesses now—and took from beneath a loose floorboard the bundle that was, in every way that mattered, his actual life.

Eleven maps. No—count again, in the dark, by touch. Twelve. Twelve maps of a country that existed nowhere except in ink and in the strange, aching sight behind his eyes: the unmapped realm, the land beyond the Veil, stitched together from forgotten geography. Coastlines no ship had ever sailed drawn in silver-green. Mountains named in a language he had never learned but somehow wrote fluently after midnight. Cities built of everything the world had stopped remembering. He had been drawing it since he was a child, before he knew it was forbidden, before he knew it was *anything*—he had thought everyone saw it, the way he'd thought everyone saw colors behind their eyelids, and the discovery that he was alone in it had been the loneliest moment of his life.

Now the crown called it a wound. Now the crown called men like him a sickness to be cut out.

Mirabel knelt on the cold floorboards with the bundle in his lap and told himself, reasonably, that burning them was simple sense. Evidence. The Veilwardens would search eventually—proclamations like this always ended in searches; history was clear on the matter—and twelve impossible maps hidden under a clerk's floorboards were not transparency. They were a gallows, pre-built, waiting for his neck.

He chose the smallest one: a sketch of the unmapped coast, done at fourteen, all eager lines and no discipline, the work of a boy who hadn't yet learned to be afraid. He carried it down the back stairs to the guild's cold hearth, knelt, and held a taper to the corner.

The paper caught. Fire walked up the coastline, eating the silver-green sea, and Mirabel watched the little country he had loved longest burn and felt—nothing he had prepared for. He had expected relief. What he felt was grief, sudden and enormous, as if he had held a match to a friend. The flames turned the mountains orange, then gray, then ash, and the last thing to go was a tiny inked figure on the shore, no bigger than a grain of rice, waving.

He had forgotten he'd drawn that. Fourteen-year-old Mirabel had given the empty coast a witness, so the country wouldn't be lonely.

The ash settled in the grate. Mirabel sat back on his heels in the dark and discovered that he could not pick up the second map. His hands simply declined. Eleven remained under the floorboard, and every argument he owned—sense, survival, Corvane's beloved arithmetic—told him to feed them all to the fire, and his hands said *no* with the mute stubbornness of a heart that had finally been asked to do something unforgivable.

"One," he whispered to the cold hearth. "I burned one. That's—compromise. That's practically wisdom."

The floorboard went back down. The bundle stayed.

He was washing ash from his fingers at his basin when he heard it—faint, from below, from the direction of the map-room where no one had any business being at midnight: a sound like paper breathing. And beneath it, so soft he might have invented it, a voice with no mouth and no distance, speaking in the tone of someone calling across water:

*Ivo.*

Mirabel froze, wet hands dripping.

No one had called him that in twenty-two years. No one living knew the name at all—only his mother, long dead, who had used it before the world taught him to answer to Mirabel instead.

The voice came again, patient, unhurried, from everywhere and nowhere, and it did not sound like a threat.

It sounded like a summons.
