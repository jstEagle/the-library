## 3. The Client Who Paid in Ash

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its accounts the way a drunkard keeps his promises: irregularly, optimistically, and with a great deal of creative arithmetic. Mirabel Quintana had spent six years learning to make sense of it anyway.

Tuesday's ledger, for instance, contained the following entries:

*One (1) bottled Tuesday, sold to Mrs. Halloway of Copperlane, who wished to relive her wedding day. Paid in full: three memories of rain and one jar of honey that will never run out but also never quite tastes of anything.*

*One (1) door that only opens for liars, commissioned by an anonymous buyer. Deposit received in secrets. Balance outstanding.*

*One (1) hour of silence, purchased by the Cathedral of Saint Vellum for use during particularly tedious sermons. Payment pending.*

Mirabel dipped his pen, blotted it on his sleeve out of habit, and wrote *payment pending* in the margin with the small, weary satisfaction of a man who has learned that most debts in the impossible trade are settled eventually, just never on time and rarely in coin.

It was good work, if you didn't look at it too closely. That was the trick of the Guild, and Mirabel had made a religion of not looking too closely. He balanced books. He filed contracts. He drank terrible tea with Sela in the clerks' alcove while she complained about the new ink rationing and he laughed in all the right places. And twice a week, when the guildhouse slept, he locked his door, lit no candles, and drew maps of a country that did not exist — or rather, that existed so thoroughly in ink and memory that "did not exist" felt like an insult to it.

He was very careful. He had been careful for years. Careful was practically his middle name, right after *unremarkable* and just before *please-don't-notice-me*.

Which is why, when the hooded figure appeared in the guild's receiving room on Wednesday morning, Mirabel's first instinct was not alarm. It was mild professional irritation. Clients before ten o'clock were almost always trouble.

"The clerk will see you now," announced Pemmy, the guild's door-warden, a woman built like a wardrobe and about as easy to move. She gave Mirabel a look over the client's shoulder that said, plainly, *this one gives me a bad feeling, and I once wrestled a memory-eating moth barehanded, so my feelings are worth something.*

"Good morning," Mirabel said, arranging his face into the pleasant blankness the job required. "Welcome to the Guild of Impossible Things. How may we help you do something you shouldn't be able to?"

He was rather proud of that line. He'd invented it himself. Most clients smiled at it.

The hooded figure did not smile. They sat — or seemed to sit; the chair creaked uncertainly, as chairs sometimes did around people who weren't entirely present — and placed both hands flat on the desk. The hands were gloved in grey leather, cracked with age.

"I want a map," the client said. The voice was rough, like gravel poured through cloth. "To the unmapped realm."

The words landed in the room and stayed there, heavy as dropped stones.

Mirabel kept his expression pleasant and his heartbeat unpleasant. "I'm afraid I don't know what you mean."

"You're the cartographer." Not a question. "They say there's a man here who can chart places that exist only in ink. Places nobody remembers properly. I want a way in."

"Sir or madam or otherwise esteemed client," Mirabel said, folding his hands, "the Guild sells many things. Bottled Tuesdays. Doors with opinions. A certain quantity of luck, though I'll caution you our supplier has been unreliable since the incident with the lottery. What we do not sell is passage to fairy countries."

"It isn't a fairy country." The gloved hands pressed flatter against the desk. "It's real. It's *more* real than this. You know that. I can see you know that."

And there it was — the horrible, familiar lurch of being *seen*. Mirabel had built his whole life on the principle that nobody looks closely at a bookkeeper. Bookkeepers are furniture. Bookkeepers are wallpaper with excellent penmanship. But this client looked at him the way you look at a map when you're lost, and Mirabel found himself, absurdly, wanting to help them.

That was the danger of the gift, he'd long ago decided. Not the drawing. The *wanting*. Every cartographer who ever got caught, he suspected, got caught because some part of them was tired of hiding.

"I'm going to be honest with you," Mirabel said, which was technically true and morally flexible. "Even if such a place existed, and even if someone could chart it, the crossing would kill you. The border between what's mapped and what isn't doesn't take kindly to visitors. It takes them apart and uses the pieces for margins."

"I'm not asking to visit." The hood tilted. Under its shadow, something glinted — eyes, wet and steady. "I'm asking to *go home*."

Mirabel's pen, which he had been pretending to adjust, went still.

"Home," he repeated carefully.

"You wouldn't understand." The client rose. The chair exhaled in relief. "You have a home. I can tell. You have tea stains on your ledger and ink under your nails and you complain about the tea, which means you expect tomorrow to come. Some of us don't get tomorrows anymore. We get *proclamations*."

The word dropped between them like a coin down a well.

Since the Veilhunt proclamation had gone up — nailed to every door in the city, Mirabel's own included, the wax seal fat and red as a wound — the guild had been very quiet in a very loud way. Corvane had called a meeting and urged calm, in the particular calm tone of a man reading from prepared notes. *We are tradespeople,* he'd said. *We deal in curiosities, not sedition. No one here has anything to fear.* Half the room had believed him. The other half had started sleeping at their desks, ready to run.

Mirabel had burned a map that night. His favorite — the harbor city with the tide that ran backward every seventh day. He'd fed it to the fire page by page and watched seven years of careful joy curl into black lace, and told himself it was prudence, and hated how much it felt like amputation.

"I can't help you," he said quietly. "I'm sorry. Truly."

For a moment the hooded figure simply stood there. Then they reached into their coat and set something on the desk between them.

A small pouch. Drawn shut with cord. It made no sound when it landed, which was the first strange thing. Money clinks. This pouch landed like snowfall.

"For your trouble," the client said. "And in case you change your mind. People usually do, when they understand what's at stake."

Then they turned and walked out, past Pemmy, past the shelves of bottled weather, and through the front doors into the grey morning, and the doors did not so much as sigh behind them.

Mirabel looked at the pouch for a long moment.

Every instinct he owned — the careful ones, the cowardly ones, the ones that had kept him alive and invisible for thirty-one years — said *leave it. Call Pemmy. Have it thrown in the river.*

He untied the cord instead. Because curiosity was the other thing he'd never managed to burn.

Inside was ash. Fine grey ash, sifted soft as flour, filling the pouch perhaps halfway. He tipped a little into his palm, frowning.

And then the smell reached him.

Woodsmoke. Bread. Rain on warm stone. Rosemary growing wild off a garden wall somebody had never gotten around to trimming. River water in summer, green and lazy. A specific kind of soap his mother used to buy from a woman with a laugh like a hinge.

Mirabel stood up so fast his stool went over backwards, and he didn't even notice.

Because he knew that smell. Everyone from the low country knew that smell. It was the smell of Hearthlow — the village where he'd spent every childhood summer, where his aunt had kept a house with blue shutters, where the bread came from an oven older than the parish and the whole town smelled, perpetually, gloriously, of *home*.

Except Hearthlow wasn't there anymore.

Three weeks ago, the Veilwardens had come to Hearthlow. There had been a list. There was always a list. And when the wagons rolled away, the crown's men had put the village to the torch — officially to burn out "contamination," unofficially because fire leaves less paperwork than trials. Forty households. The blue shutters. The ancient oven. All of it ash, all of it smoke, all of it gone in an afternoon while the rest of the country looked politely elsewhere.

This was Hearthlow's ash. It had to be. Nothing else smelled like that — nothing else *could*, because smell was memory, and memory was the only place Hearthlow still existed.

The client hadn't been paying him in currency.

They'd been paying him in evidence.

Mirabel's hand shook, and a fine drift of grey spilled across the ledger, settling over the neat columns of bottled Tuesdays and liar-doors, dusting the impossible trade with the residue of the entirely possible cruelty that paid for it.

*Refusing the job doesn't undo what's been done.*

The thought arrived without permission and refused to leave. He had said no. He had done the correct, careful, self-preserving thing. And a person whose entire village had been murdered had walked out of his office with nothing — no map, no answer, no justice — while he sat here surrounded by comfortable ledgers recording comfortable transactions, taking a salary from an institution that had, three days after the Veilhunt began, quietly renewed its crown charter without asking a single difficult question.

Corvane had signed that renewal. Mirabel had watched him do it. He'd thought, at the time, *sensible. Prudent. What else could he do?*

Now the ash sat on his ledger and asked the question for him.

He crossed to the window. Below, the street went about its morning: a fishmonger shouting about herring, two children chasing a hoop, a Veilwarden patrol marching four abreast with their grey cloaks and their grey faces, checking doors, checking lists. One of them paused at the guild's threshold, looked up at the sign — the painted cat sitting inside a painted impossible knot — and then moved on.

For now.

Mirabel looked down at his palm. The last grains of ash were already fading, the way ash does, the smell thinning with every second until it was only a ghost of a ghost. He closed his fist too late. Home slipped out between his fingers, the way it always did.

"No," he said aloud, to the empty room, to the ash, to himself. "No, I don't think I can keep doing this."

It was not a plan. It was barely a sentence. But it was the first honest thing he'd said in six years, and it frightened him more than any warden ever had.

Behind him, on the desk, the ledger lay open — and along its bottom margin, where the spilled ash had settled thickest, the grey powder had arranged itself, quite distinctly and quite impossibly, into a single line of small, neat handwriting:

*She knew your name.*

Mirabel stared at it. The ash held its shape for three heartbeats. Then the morning draft from the window stirred, and the letters scattered, and the message was gone — leaving behind only the smell of woodsmoke, and bread, and a question with teeth in it.

*Who* knew his name? And how?

Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard creaked. Footsteps — unhurried, deliberate, descending toward the receiving room.

Mirabel swept the ash into his pocket, straightened his overturned stool, and picked up his pen just as the door opened.

Sela stood in the doorway, pale as unbleached paper, clutching something folded tight against her chest.

"Mira," she said, and her voice was wrong — stripped of all its usual jokes, down to the bare wire underneath. "You need to see this. Now."
