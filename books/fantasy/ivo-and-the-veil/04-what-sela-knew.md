## 4. What Sela Knew

Sela Voss had worked at the Guild of Impossible Things for six years, and in that time she had learned three iron rules of the trade.

One: never accept payment in anything that breathes, weeps, or sings. Two: if a client asks whether a thing is *truly* impossible, the answer is yes, and the fee doubles. Three: whatever happens after midnight on the third floor, mind your own ledgers.

She had never broken rule one or two. She was about to break rule three so thoroughly that it would need to be rewritten around her.

It started, as most catastrophes at the guild did, with an inventory discrepancy.

The guild kept its accounts in triplicate: one copy for the crown, one for the guildmaster, and one — the true one — for whoever was diligent enough to notice that the first two were works of fiction. Sela was diligent. It was her defining flaw, according to Mirabel, who once told her she alphabetized her feelings and would probably die reconciling an emotional statement of accounts.

"Someone's been buying lampblack," she muttered to herself on a grey Tuesday, running her finger down the requisition column for the fourth time. "Four ounces a week. Every week. For eight months."

Lampblack was cheap. Lampblack was nothing. But four ounces a week was enough ink to write a novel, forge a kingdom's worth of signatures, or — and here her finger stopped on the column — draw maps. A great many maps. Maps the size of tablecloths.

And there was exactly one person on the third floor who stayed up past midnight, whose window glowed on foggy nights like a lantern dropped in milk, and who had recently developed the nervous habit of hiding paper under his shirt whenever she walked into the records room, as though she hadn't already seen him do it eleven times.

"Sela!" Mirabel called from across the hall, waving a stack of receipts. "Do you have the manifest for the whisper-trade shipment? Corvane wants it before dusk."

"Coming," she said, and did not come. She stood at the window instead and watched his silhouette move behind the frosted glass of the map-room, hunched over something, one hand moving in slow, careful strokes. The light around him did something odd then — it warmed, the way coals warm just before they catch — and for half a heartbeat Sela would have sworn the glow came not from his lamp but from the page itself.

Then the light went ordinary again, and Mirabel sneezed, and knocked over what sounded like an entire jar of pens, and swore with the creativity of a man who had been alone too long.

Sela looked down at her ledger. Four ounces a week. Eight months.

"Well," she said quietly, to no one, "that's impossible."

Which, at the Guild of Impossible Things, was practically a job description.

* * *

The Veilwarden arrived on Thursday, which everyone agreed was tactless of him, because Thursday was delivery day and the courtyard smelled overwhelmingly of the crate of singing cheeses that had come in from the coast. He was a long, grey man named Warden Hesk, with a voice like a drawer closing and a warrant signed in the crown's new red ink — the ink they'd started using since the proclamation, since the Veilhunt, since the word *sight* had become something you could be hanged for.

Corvane met him in the atrium with the smooth courtesy of a man greeting weather he'd been promised. "Warden. An honor. We are, as ever, entirely compliant."

"I'm sure." Hesk produced a list. "New protocols. All guild staff will be interviewed regarding unregistered gifts. Sightings of glowing inks. Unusual cartographic activity. You'll provide me a room, your records, and your cooperation."

"You'll have all three, and my condolences besides. This way."

Sela was at her desk in the records room when they passed, and she watched Mirabel look up from his filing with the expression of a man watching his own funeral procession go by on the wrong day. His eyes found hers. They said, very clearly: *I am going to be sick into this filing cabinet.*

They also said, less clearly but more urgently: *the map is under the floorboard under the ink cabinet.*

How she knew that second part, she couldn't say. Perhaps it was the way his gaze flicked there and back, quick as a swallowed word. Perhaps it was that she had spent six years learning to read him the way he read ledgers — closely, fondly, and always looking for what was hidden between the lines.

Hesk began his interviews in the small room off the atrium. One by one, clerks went in and came out looking rinsed. Tam went in cheerful and came out pale. Old Mistress Ivene went in pale and came out cheerful, which unsettled everyone more.

When the door opened and Hesk said, "The next clerk — Voss," Mirabel stood up so fast his chair fell over.

"I'll fetch tea," he announced, to the room in general, in the voice of a man who had never once fetched tea in his life.

"Sit down," said Hesk.

Mirabel sat down.

The interview room smelled of cold iron and the singing cheeses, faintly, from the courtyard. Hesk sat across the table with her file open — her actual file, thin and boring, six years of punctuality — and asked her name, her tenure, her duties.

"Ledgers," Sela said. "Reconciliation. Inventory."

"No gifts?"

"I can count faster than anyone in the building. It's not a gift, it's a tragedy."

Something flickered at the corner of Hesk's mouth. Not a smile. A filing. "We've received reports," he said, "of unusual activity on the third floor. Nighttime activity. Glowing inks."

"Ah," said Sela.

Her heart was doing something acrobatic and unlicensed. She thought, with strange clarity: *this is the moment.* Not some grand crossroads years away. Just this: a grey man, a thin file, a question hanging in the air like smoke. She thought about rule three. She thought about midnight windows and four ounces of lampblack a week and a man who hid paper under his shirt as though she were terrifying, and who had once spent an entire payday buying her soup when she was sick, and who drew maps of places that didn't exist because — she understood this suddenly, completely, the way you understand a sum once it balances — because for him they did.

"The third floor," Sela repeated. "Yes. That'll be the phosphor."

Hesk waited.

"We use it for marking rare stock," she heard herself say, smooth as fresh ink. "Items that shouldn't be handled after dark. Glowworm compound, mostly, cut with lampblack. It stains everything. Half our inventory glows if you look at it wrong. Honestly, Warden, if glowing ink were evidence of the sight, you'd have to arrest the entire supply closet."

A silence. Hesk regarded her the way a man regards a lock he suspects of being better than it looks.

"You're telling me," he said slowly, "that your guild stores glowing materials. In quantity. On the floor where your cartographers work."

"It's a guild of impossible things, Warden. Our supply closet has opinions. Last month the brooms unionized." She spread her hands. "I keep the ledgers. I promise you, if there were anything worth arresting in this building, it would be itemized, cross-referenced, and filed under *Pending Crown Review*. That's how I'd know."

That, at least, was true, and truth is the best mortar for a lie. Hesk wrote something down. The pen scratched like a mouse in a wall.

"And Quintana?" he said. "Your colleague. He keeps late hours."

"He's an insomniac with poor taste in hobbies," Sela said. "He copies old charts for practice. Badly. I've seen them. Coastlines like melted candles." She shrugged, warm and easy, a woman discussing a coworker's embarrassing pottery. "If he had the sight, Warden, his maps would be good. They're terrible. I'd show you one, but I think he burns them out of shame."

Every word was a stone she was laying across a river, and beneath every stone was black water, and she had never lied to an officer of the crown in her life, and her hands on the table were perfectly still because she had decided they would be.

Hesk closed the file.

"Thank you, Clerk Voss." He stood, buttoned his grey coat. "Compliance is noted. The crown values citizens who report accurately."

"The crown and I have that in common," said Sela.

At the door she paused. "Warden — the interviews. Will there be many more?"

"Until the list is complete." He said it mildly, the way you mention rain. "The Veilhunt is thorough. Thoroughness protects the innocent."

"Of course," said Sela. "That's exactly what it does."

She got out before her face could finish deciding what it wanted to do.

* * *

Mirabel was waiting in the stairwell, holding a teapot he had apparently grabbed as a prop and then forgotten to put down. It was empty. He was gripping it like a shield.

"You," Sela said, "owe me an explanation, a new set of nerves, and approximately thirty-two ounces of lampblack."

His face did several things in sequence. Relief arrived first, enormous and clumsy; guilt arrived immediately after, as guilt does, wearing relief's coat. "Sela—"

"Not here." She took his sleeve — the empty teapot clinking against her wrist — and pulled him up the stairs, past the second-floor landing where the taxidermied griffin had been staring disapprovingly at staff since before either of them was hired, and into the map-room, where she shut the door and turned to face him.

He looked terrible. He looked, she thought, like a man who had been carrying something heavy for a very long time and had only just realized other people could see the shape of it through his coat.

"Glowworm compound," he said weakly. "Is that real? Do we actually—"

"There is no phosphor, Mirabel. There's no glowworms. There's four ounces of lampblack a week missing from inventory for eight months, and a window that lights up like a festival, and paper under your shirt, and right now there is a grey man downstairs writing down names, and I looked him in the eye and committed treason, so you are going to show me what's under the floorboard by the ink cabinet, and then you are going to explain why my best friend has been lying to me since spring."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, Hesk's voice murmured through another interview, muffled and procedural.

Mirabel set down the teapot. He knelt. The floorboard came up, and underneath, wrapped in oilcloth with the tenderness most people reserved for infants, lay maps.

Sela crouched beside him and looked, and forgot how to breathe properly.

They were beautiful. That was the first thing — not strange, not alarming, just *beautiful*, in the way that makes beauty feel like an accusation. Coastlines no atlas held, drawn with a confidence no forgery could fake. Mountain ranges with their shadows falling the correct way, which no invented range ever managed. Cities stitched along rivers that bent like handwriting. And the colors — the colors moved, faintly, the way heat shimmers, blues deepening when she tilted her head, a gold road brightening as though pleased to be looked at.

"They're real," she whispered. It wasn't a question. Her whole life she had filed things under possible and impossible, and here was a third drawer she'd never known the cabinet had.

"They're real," Mirabel said. "To me. I don't know how else to say it. I've been able to see — *there* — since I was a boy. I chart it because nobody else can. Because if I don't, it's only ever going to exist in my head, and that seemed..." He laughed, hollow. "That seemed like a waste of a gift."

"How long have you known I'd cover for you?"

He blinked. "What?"

"The lying. Today. Did you know I would?"

"I hoped," he said. "I hoped and I hated myself for hoping, because you should never have to. Sela, what you did down there — Hesk could come back. If anyone checks the storeroom, if anyone asks about glowworms—"

"Then we'd better make glowworms real," Sela said, "wouldn't you say? I'll requisition some. I know a woman at the chandler's who owes me a favor and no questions." She sat back on her heels and looked at him — really looked, the way she'd never let herself before, because looking closely at Mirabel had always felt like reading someone's private correspondence. "You absolute idiot. Eight months. You could have told me in spring."

"Telling people is how they get hanged lately."

"And lying to them is how they get sold," Sela said, before she could stop herself.

The words came out sharper than she meant, and she saw them land, saw him flinch — and she didn't know yet why the word *sold* made him flinch, wouldn't know for another few nights, when she found a certain ledger in a locked drawer with certain names in a certain hand. But she noted the flinch. Sela Voss noticed everything. It was her defining flaw.

"From now on," she said, gentler, taking one of the impossible maps and rolling it closed with reverent hands, "you don't carry this alone. That's not a request. I've already lied to the crown for you; you don't get to protect me from the paperwork."

Mirabel laughed — a real one, cracked down the middle but real — and wiped his eyes with the back of his hand, smearing a little ink across his cheekbone, because of course there was ink on his hands, there was always ink on his hands.

"Deal," he said.

They shook on it, formally, absurdly, over an open floorboard full of forbidden countries, and Sela felt something settle in her chest — not fear, though fear was there too, patient as winter. Something warmer. Loyalty, kindling. The dangerous, wonderful knowledge of exactly whose side she was on.

Below them, the front bell rang. Voices in the atrium — Hesk concluding his business, Corvane's smooth replies, the scrape of the grey man's boots on stone.

And then, quieter, almost lost beneath it: Corvane speaking to someone else, someone who had not come in through the front door. A low voice answering. And Corvane saying, in the pleasant tone he used for closing deals:

"Yes. The last name on the list. But not yet — not until I'm certain the timing serves us."

Sela looked at Mirabel. Mirabel, frowning at the ceiling, had clearly heard it too, and clearly hadn't understood it, and was already turning back to his maps.

Sela did not turn back. She kept listening, her ear to the floorboards, above the buried countries, and began — carefully, silently, in the ledger of her own memory — to open a new account.
