## 5. The Guildmaster's Quiet Arithmetic

Corvane had three ledgers on his desk, and only one of them was a lie.

The first lay open to the left of his elbow: the guild's public ledger, fat and honest-looking, recording every impossible trade the Guild of Impossible Things had brokered that season. A bottled thunderstorm sold to a drought-stricken duchy. A lullaby that could put a fortress to sleep, purchased by a general with a conscience and returned within the week because his soldiers wept in their bunks. Six feet of midnight ribbon, provenance unknown, buyer anonymous, price scandalous. Ordinary business. The kind of business that kept forty-two people fed and roofed and, until recently, safely beneath notice.

The second ledger sat closed at his right hand: the guild's true accounts, the ones he showed to no one, not even the treasurer. Debts owed to him. Debts he owed. The quiet arithmetic of survival.

And between them, square and unremarkable, bound in grey calfskin with no title at all, lay the third.

Corvane did not open it immediately. He was a man who believed in ritual, or at least in the useful costume of it. He poured himself a measure of plum brandy — the good bottle, the one he saved for nights when the arithmetic turned ugly — and he let the fire settle, and he looked out the window at the guild's courtyard below, where the evening shift was coming in from the counting-house.

Tomasin was arguing with the delivery boy about a crate of bottled fog that had arrived two sizes too large. Old Wren sat on the step sharpening a quill knife she hadn't used in years, purely for the pleasure of complaining about how dull it was. And there, crossing the cobbles with his collar up and his satchel clutched like a shield, went Mirabel Quintana, late again, hurrying toward the dormitory stairs with the particular guilty walk of a man who thought nobody watched him.

Everybody watched everybody here. That was the first thing you learned as guildmaster. The second thing was that watching was cheap; knowing what you saw was expensive.

Mirabel Quintana. Clerk, third grade. Punctual in everything except arrival. Neat handwriting with a curious flourish on the letter *m*, as though even his alphabet couldn't resist drawing little doors. Polite. Forgettable. So aggressively forgettable that Corvane had once spent an entire quarter trying to remember what the man actually looked like and had failed twice.

Which was, of course, precisely the point.

*You don't survive thirty years running this place by being clever,* Corvane's predecessor had told him, the night before she'd handed over the keys and vanished into a country that appeared on no map, which everyone agreed was very like her. *You survive by noticing who else is clever, and pricing them accordingly.*

He raised his glass to the window, to Mirabel's retreating back, in a small private toast the man would never see.

"To your health," he murmured. "While it lasts."

Then he opened the grey ledger.

* * *

The pages were written in his own hand, which was deliberate. No clerk copied this book; no clerk read it. If the crown wanted proof of its bargains, the crown got Corvane's handwriting and nothing else, because a secret shared by two hands is a partnership, and a secret held by one is merely a fact, and facts can be denied.

The ledger began four years ago, though the thinking behind it began much earlier than that. He turned past the early entries without lingering, the way a surgeon turns past old scars.

**Hallowmas. Pell Ondray, gift: finding lost things. Sold to the Office of Veils. Price: charter renewal, full term.**

Pell had been a sweet-tempered boy who could locate anything — lost rings, lost heirs, lost afternoons. The Office of Veils had wanted him for their search divisions. Corvane had told the guild Pell had taken a position in the southern houses. Half the guild had cried at his farewell dinner. Corvane had paid for the wine himself, and had meant the gesture sincerely, insofar as he meant anything.

**Midwinter. The sisters Anthe and Grey Moll, gift: speaking with drowned things. Sold. Price: exemption from inspection, one year.**

They had gone quietly. Drowned things, it turned out, had been saying very little worth hearing lately, and the sisters were tired. He told himself that sometimes. It helped, in the way that a coin helps a man bleeding out: not much, but it fits neatly in the wound.

**Springtide. Ivo Cassel—**

No. That entry wasn't right yet. He turned the page.

There were eleven names now. Eleven names across four years, each one traded for something the guild could not have bought with all its impossible inventory combined: the charter. The royal warrant that hung behind glass in the front hall, gold seal and all, declaring the Guild of Impossible Things *protected, licensed, and under the particular friendship of the crown.*

Particular friendship. Corvane had laughed the first time he read those words. He didn't laugh anymore. Particular friendship, translated out of courtly language, meant: *we know what you are, and we have decided, for now, not to burn you.* And the price of that decision rose every year, the way all prices rise, and the currency never changed.

Names. Gifted names. Because the Veilhunt did not begin with proclamations nailed to doors — whatever the heralds said now, whatever panic was spreading through the city this season — the hunt had begun years ago, quietly, in rooms like this one, with men like Corvane doing sums.

That was the thing nobody understood about terror. It was never born screaming. It was born tidy.

* * *

He drank, and turned to the last page, and there it was.

**Mirabel Quintana, clerk, third grade. Gift: suspected cartography of unreal realms. Status: unsent.**

Unsent. The word sat there like a coin balanced on its edge.

Corvane had been watching Mirabel for two years, which was longer than Mirabel likely suspected and shorter than Corvane would have preferred. The evidence was circumstantial, and Corvane was a man who dealt in evidence the way moneylenders dealt in coin: he weighed it, he tested its edges, he bit down hard before trusting it.

Item: the ink. Guild stationery was issued in standard blacks and browns, counted out weekly by the quartermaster, and yet Mirabel's ledgers occasionally bloomed with colors no supplier stocked — a green like deep water, a violet that seemed to move if you stared. When questioned, Mirabel blamed "a traveling merchant." There was always a traveling merchant. In Corvane's experience, the traveling merchant was the most overworked fictional character in the world.

Item: the hours. Mirabel kept late ones, and his candle burned colors it shouldn't. Sela Voss covered for him twice with excuses so smooth they had to be rehearsed — good girl, loyal as a hound, wasted on clerking — and Corvane had noted her name in the margin of his memory, not the ledger. Not yet. Loyalty was a currency too, and he hated spending it.

Item: the maps themselves. This was the item that mattered. Three months ago, a misdelivered parcel — Tomasín's fault, the man could lose a parcel in a room with one door — had landed on Corvane's desk instead of Mirabel's, and beneath it, half-slid from Mirabel's own satchel when the two bundles knocked together, a sheet of paper Corvane was never meant to see.

A coastline. Drawn in that impossible green ink, precise as scripture, of a coast that existed nowhere. Not nowhere in the way of a fantasy — Corvane had seen a thousand fantasies, and fantasies were lazy, vague, all swirls and dragons. This was *surveyed*. Soundings marked in fathoms. A lighthouse placed with the confidence of a man who had stood in its gallery. And along the margin, in tiny letters, a note in Mirabel's hand: *the tide comes in wrong here. it comes in from both sides.*

Corvane had held that page for a long time that night, longer than was prudent, feeling the paper do something paper should not do. It had been warm. Maps were not warm.

He had put it back exactly where it had been, and he had said nothing, and he had opened the grey ledger and written the entry.

Not because he was certain. Certainty was for fanatics and young men. But because the arithmetic did not require certainty. It required only probability, and probability said: a clerk who draws real maps of unreal places during a season when the crown has begun paying for exactly such clerks is not a colleague. He is an asset. And assets are either protected or spent.

The guild could not protect him. That was the whole of it, the ugly whole of it, and Corvane wished — he genuinely, tiredly wished — that it were otherwise. Protection required leverage, and the guild's leverage had been spent four years ago buying the charter, and now the charter itself was the debt, renewed annually, payable annually, and the Office of Veils had made it plain at Midwinter's meeting what the next payment would be.

"Two more," the Veilwarden-captain had said, stirring her tea with the serenity of a woman discussing wallpaper. "Gifted ones. You may choose them yourself, Master Corvane. We find your choices are always so... reasonable."

Reasonable. He had gone home and drunk the good brandy and hated her, and then hated himself more efficiently, because self-hatred was at least a productive emotion, and then he had come back to work.

Two more. And when he laid the probabilities side by side — the glowing ink, the warm maps, the tides that came in from both sides — one name rose to the top of the column like oil rising through water.

Mirabel Quintana. Unsent. For now.

* * *

Here was the defense he would make, if anyone ever asked — and no one ever would, because the people who might ask were the people whose names filled the ledger, and the dead ask nothing.

*I saved forty lives for every one I spent.* Forty-two souls under this roof, fed on impossible margins in an impossible trade, in a city where the crown had begun nailing proclamations to doors. Eleven sold, thirty-one sheltered. Any merchant in the city would take those odds without blinking. Any general. Any king. The arithmetic was sound. The arithmetic was *kind*, compared to what the Office of Veils would do if the guild refused — refuse, and lose the charter, and lose the protection, and watch the wardens walk in and take not eleven but all forty-two, sorting them in the courtyard with the gentle efficiency of men culling livestock.

He had run that calculation a hundred times. It came out the same every time. It came out the same, and it changed nothing, because there was a flaw in the sum, and he knew exactly where it was, and he looked away from it every single time the way a man looks away from a loose thread on a beloved coat.

The flaw was this: the arithmetic treated the eleven as cost. Numbers. Units. But Pell Ondray had laughed with his whole body. The Moll sisters had once talked a drowned bell into ringing again just to hear it. And Mirabel Quintana —

Mirabel Quintana drew maps of places that loved him back. Corvane had understood that, holding the warm page. The coastline hadn't been surveyed. It had been *remembered*. Somewhere, somehow, a country that did not exist had a cartographer, and the cartographer was a shy, late-arriving, forgettable clerk who flourished his m's like little doors, and the country knew his name.

You could not spend that. Corvane knew it the way he knew the taste of the good brandy. And yet the ledger lay open, and the entry waited, unsent, and the Veilwarden-captain stirred her tea, and the arithmetic did not care what he knew.

He dipped his pen. He did not write. He sat with the pen poised above the line, a small black hesitation, and outside the window the courtyard lamps came on one by one, and someone — Wren, by the sound of it — was shouting cheerfully at Tomasin about the fog crate, and life went on below him, warm and ordinary and utterly unaware of the price list in the grey book.

"One more year," he told the empty room. It was what he told himself every year. "One more year, and I'll find another way."

The room, being a room, said nothing. But the ledger lay open, and the pen dripped one small black bead onto the blotter, like a period at the end of a sentence he hadn't finished writing.

He blotted it. He closed the book. He locked it in the drawer with the false bottom, beneath the deeds and the blackmail and the other small mercies of a long career, and he finished his brandy, and he blew out the lamp.

* * *

He was nearly asleep when the light started.

It came under his door — a thin, restless glow, greenish, moving. Corvane was old enough that he no longer leapt out of bed at strange lights; he lay still and listened first, which had kept him alive through two purges and one marriage. The glow passed his door, traveled along the corridor toward the map-room, and paused there.

Then, faintly, distinctly, he heard the sound of ink being poured. From a bottle that, as far as he knew, was locked in Mirabel's satchel three floors down.

Corvane sat up in the dark. His heart performed a slow, heavy knock, like a creditor at the door.

Down the hall, the green light brightened — and beneath it, threading up through the floorboards, through stone and timber and thirty years of guildmaster's composure, came a smell that had no business in a London counting-house. Salt. Wildflowers. Rain on a coast that did not exist.

The tide, coming in from both sides.

Corvane reached for the drawer with the false bottom, and his hand, for the first time in four years, shook.
