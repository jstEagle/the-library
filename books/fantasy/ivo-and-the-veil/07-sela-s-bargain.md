## 7. Sela's Bargain

Sela Voss had three rules for surviving the Guild of Impossible Things, and she recited them every morning the way other people said prayers.

One: never ask what's in the jars on the third floor. Two: never accept payment in anything that breathes. Three: never, under any circumstances, open the guildmaster's private ledger.

She had kept all three rules for eleven years. She was proud of that. She had survived a fire-breathing invoice dispute, a clerk who turned out to be a swan in a very good hat, and the Great Ink Flood of two winters past, when the second-floor fountain pen had achieved sentience and opinions. Through all of it, her rules had held.

Then came the night Mirabel Quintana's name appeared at the bottom of a list of the dead-not-yet-dead, and rule three went the way of rule two during the unfortunate incident with the singing goat.

* * *

It began, as most catastrophes at the guild did, with a misplaced teacup.

Sela had gone up to Corvane's study after hours to return it — the guildmaster's good porcelain, left behind in the conference room where he'd spent the evening with a Veilwarden liaison whose smile Sela trusted about as far as she could throw the building. She'd told herself it was simple tidiness. She was good at telling herself things. It was practically her job; half of bookkeeping was persuading numbers to be something they weren't.

The study smelled of lamp oil and old vellum and, underneath it, something faintly scorched — like a letter burned and regretted. The teacup sat on its saucer by the door, exactly where such cups belonged. Sela set it down, turned to leave, and would have left, honestly, if the ledger hadn't been lying open on the desk.

That was the thing she'd remember afterward: it was *open*. Corvane was a man who locked his inkwells. He locked his drawers, his cabinets, his correspondence, and — according to rumor — one small box whose contents had made a junior clerk weep and resign into the sea. The man did not leave ledgers open. Leaving this one open was either an accident so improbable it qualified as an impossible thing, or it was bait.

Sela stood in the doorway for a long moment, doing arithmetic. Risk of looking: high. Cost of not looking: also high, though she couldn't have said why yet. Curiosity, in her experience, was just fear wearing a nicer coat.

She looked.

* * *

The ledger was bound in grey calfskin, unmarked, the kind of book designed to be forgettable. The columns inside were neat — Corvane's hand, precise as stitching. Names. Dates. And a third column headed, in the guildmaster's tidy capitals, *CONSIDERATION RECEIVED.*

Sela read the first name and felt the floor tilt politely, the way floors do when they want to give you a chance to sit down before you fall down.

*Tomas Ellery, mapwright. Registered under crown writ, 14th of Embermonth. Consideration received: charter renewal, full terms.*

Tomas Ellery had worked in the guild for twenty years. He'd taught Sela how to grind lapis without crying. Three weeks ago he'd been taken by Veilwardens, quietly, between one Tuesday and the next, and the guild had been told he'd retired to his sister's farm in the lowlands. Someone had even passed around a card to sign. Sela had written *Enjoy the hens.*

There were six names. Six. Each with a date, each with a consideration received — a lowered tariff here, a blind eye there, the guild's precious charter renewed again and again in exchange for human beings handed over like overdue accounts.

And at the bottom, in fresher ink, the letters still sharp-edged, not yet smudged by handling:

*Mirabel Quintana, cartographer. Pending. Consideration under negotiation.*

Pending.

Under negotiation.

Sela stood very still in the lamplight and listened to her own heartbeat, which sounded less like a drum and more like someone knocking on a door from the inside.

Mirabel. Who brought her honey cakes on the anniversary of her hiring because he remembered dates the way other people forgot them. Who blushed when praised and went pale when lied to and had once spent an entire afternoon helping her re-shelve the taxonomy of weather phenomena because she'd sprained her wrist falling off a ladder chasing a runaway cloud chart. Who sketched in the margins of everything, always, little coastlines and impossible harbors, and thought no one noticed.

Who had glowing ink under his fingernails some mornings, and a way of looking through windows as if the view continued past the glass into somewhere else.

Oh, she'd known. Not known-known. But she'd noticed, the way you notice a friend is limping and decide, kindly, not to mention it. And now the guildmaster had noticed too, and had done what the guild did with everything valuable.

He'd priced it.

* * *

Sela sat down in Corvane's chair without meaning to. Her legs had simply resigned from the arrangement.

Think, she told herself. You're the one who balances the books. Think like a book.

Option one: tell Mirabel. Simple, immediate, catastrophic — because Mirabel was the worst liar in the building, possibly in the city, possibly in any realm including ones that specialized in lying. He'd go straight-faced to Corvane and ask him about it, earnestly, with those honest eyes, and Corvane would smile and adjust the ledger and move the date up from *pending* to *tonight.*

Option two: go to the authorities. Which authorities? The crown was running the Veilhunt. Reporting the guild's betrayal to the people being paid per head was like reporting a pickpocket to the fence.

Option three: take the page.

Her eyes went to the ledger again. One page. Six names plus one pending. If the page vanished, Corvane had no proof of delivery, no record to hand over, no neat little bill of sale. He couldn't sell what he couldn't document — the crown's wardens wanted names in writing, witnessed, sealed. That was the whole horror of it: the bureaucracy was thorough. Thoroughness cut both ways.

And theft from the guildmaster's private ledger meant — she ran the tally in her head, professional habit — dismissal, certainly. Blacklisting from every chartered house in the city, probably. The wardens asking questions about her own registration status, possibly. Exile at best. At worst...

At worst was a word she declined to finish conjugating.

Sela looked at the pending line a while longer. *Mirabel Quintana.* His name looked wrong there, the way a honey cake would look wrong in a evidence locker. She thought about the card she'd signed for Tomas Ellery. *Enjoy the hens.* She had signed her name to a lie without knowing it, cheerfully, in her best round hand.

She was not going to sign another one knowingly.

"Well," she said aloud, to the empty study, because talking to herself steadied her hands and her hands needed steadying very badly indeed. "Rule three had a good run."

* * *

Copying it would have been smarter. She knew that. A copy left the original intact, preserved deniability, kept her fingerprints metaphorical rather than literal. She even got as far as finding a blank folio in the drawer.

But a copy could be dismissed. A copy was a claim, and claims required belief, and belief required her alive and present and persuasive — none of which the coming weeks guaranteed. The original page, torn from the binding itself, bore the guild's watermark thread, Corvane's ink batch, the seal-stub of his personal wax pressed at the footer of every entry. It wasn't testimony. It was the thing itself.

Also — and she admitted this only to herself, quickly, like swallowing something hot — a copy would mean leaving the original in Corvane's possession one minute longer than necessary, and if anyone else opened that ledger before Mirabel was safe, *pending* had a way of becoming *concluded.*

Some choices you make with your head. This one she made with the whole of her, head included but not presiding.

She tore the page out along the spine-seam, slowly, the way you pull a splinter. The paper resisted, then gave with a sound like a small sigh, and the ledger lay suddenly lighter and uglier, a mouth with a tooth missing. Her hands were shaking. She folded the page into quarters and put it inside her bodice, against her heart, which struck her as melodramatic until she realized she'd done it because it was warm there and her fingers were freezing.

Then she put the blank folio back in the drawer, squared the ledger closed — closed, at least, better than she'd found it, though the missing page would announce itself soon enough — picked up nothing, touched nothing else, and walked out of the guildmaster's study at a pace she carefully calibrated to be unhurried.

Unhurried, it turns out, is very hard to walk when your entire skeleton is screaming.

* * *

The corridor outside was dark except for the night-lamp at the stairwell, and the guild at night was a different animal than the guild by day. By day it creaked and muttered and occasionally sneezed ink. By night it listened. Sela made it past the map-room — where a light was burning, damn it, *someone was in the map-room* — and had nearly reached the stairs when a voice said:

"Miss Voss."

Corvane.

He was standing by the map-room door, lamp in hand, and behind him in the room's glow she could see the hooded shape of the Veilwarden liaison, examining a wall of charts with the idle hunger of a man pricing jewelry he intended to confiscate rather than buy.

"Guildmaster." Her voice came out level. Eleven years of saying *the accounts don't lie, sir* had trained her voice well. "I was just—"

"The teacup." He nodded toward her empty hands, mild as milk. "You brought it up. Thank you. I saw you pass the study earlier."

For one vertiginous moment she thought: *he knows, he's watched me the whole time, the open ledger was a trap and I sprang it.* Then she looked at his face — tired, preoccupied, already turning back toward the liaison — and understood something worse. He didn't know. He didn't need to know. To Corvane she was furniture that filed things. He was making conversation.

"You'll lock up when you go," he said. It wasn't a question. "We've had enough surprises this season."

"Yes, sir," said Sela Voss, who had just committed the largest surprise in the guild's history and was carrying it against her heart.

She descended the stairs one step at a time, counting them, because counting was a thing her mind could hold onto instead of shrieking. Behind her, above her, Corvane's lamp moved back into the map-room, and she heard him say to the liaison, pleasantly, "Now — regarding the remaining registrations, I believe we can conclude our business within the fortnight..."

*Within the fortnight.*

She stopped counting steps and started running.

* * *

Her room was three flights down and eleven years hers: narrow, tidy, decorated with pressed flowers from invoices long settled and one framed embroidery reading *BALANCED TO THE PENNY*, a gift from Mirabel, who had laughed for a full minute at his own joke and then gone scarlet when she'd actually hung it up.

She barred the door. She lit no lamp. In the dark she took out the page and unfolded it by moonlight, and read the six names again, and the seventh, and let herself feel it properly now that no corridor was listening: the cold, the fury, the grief for Tomas Ellery and the five others whose names she now knew and whose faces she was ashamed to realize she'd mostly forgotten, because that was what the guild did — it made people into entries, and entries got archived.

Not anymore. Not this entry.

She thought about Mirabel asleep two floors above her, in his narrow room with its suspicious number of ink bottles, dreaming whatever map-dreamers dreamed, trusting the walls around him because he was the sort of person who trusted walls. Tomorrow — no, tonight, within the hour — she would wake him. She would show him the page. She would watch his face do the thing it did when the world confirmed his worst suspicion, that terrible gentle surprise of his, and then they would run, or hide, or fight, or something, she hadn't gotten that far in the plan, the plan currently ended at *show Mirabel* and she'd work backward from there—

A sound stopped her.

Faint, from the direction of the map-room, muffled through stone and floorboards: a bell. No — not a bell. A horn. The gate-horn, the one that meant official visitors at an unofficial hour, and beneath it, rising through the building like water through cloth, voices. Many voices. Boots. The particular disciplined clatter of armed men who had been given a list and a quota.

Sela stood in her dark room with a stolen page against her heart and understood, with the clarity that arrives precisely too late to be useful, two things.

First: Corvane had not been negotiating within the fortnight. He had concluded his business tonight, and the ledger she'd robbed was already obsolete — the sale made, the buyers arrived, the ink barely dry.

Second: the raiders would go to the rooms on their list in order, top down, alphabetical or by rank, and *Quintana* sat near the top of any sensible list, and Mirabel — poor, honest, hopeless-at-lying Mirabel — was asleep two floors above her with no idea, no packed bag, no drawn door, no plan at all.

Her rules, all three, lay in ruins around her, and Sela found she didn't miss them. Rules were for keeping yourself safe. She was past that now, off the ledger entirely, an unrecorded transaction moving through the dark.

She shoved the page into her sleeve, unbarred the door, and ran — up, not down, taking the stairs three at a time, past the boots already flooding the ground floor, toward the room where the last name on the list was sleeping.

"Mirabel," she whispered at his door, hammering softly with both fists. "Mirabel, wake up, wake up — they've come for you—"
