## 11. Sela on the Run

The stolen page weighed less than a feather and more than a mountain.

Sela Voss had carried many things in her years at the Guild of Impossible Things — ledgers of traded shadows, invoices for bottled thunder, a receipt once for half a memory (the seller's choice which half; the buyer had been disappointed). She knew how to value things by weight. This single sheet of Corvane's private arithmetic, folded eight times and pressed against her ribs beneath her shawl, was the heaviest thing she had ever smuggled.

Six names. Six gifted souls sold to the crown for the price of the guild's charter. And at the bottom, in the guildmaster's tidy, patient hand: *M. Quintana — held pending.*

Pending. As if Mirabel were a shipment of ink awaiting favorable weather.

She walked quickly through the Lamplighters' Quarter without running, because running invited questions, and questions invited Veilwardens, and Veilwardens invited outcomes Sela preferred not to itemize. Her heart kept its own ledger, thudding entries against her ribs: *one page, one friend, one life, forfeit.*

Behind her, the Guild of Impossible Things still stood, its crooked sign creaking in the morning wind. She did not look back. Looking back was how people got caught; she'd read that somewhere, probably in one of Mirabel's adventure novels that he pretended not to own.

*You are now a thief,* she told herself, turning down Tanner's Row. *Also a liar, as of last week, when you lied to a Veilwarden's face about glowing ink. You are becoming quite the criminal. Mother would be proud. Mother would also faint.*

* * *

Her first plan — hatched at three in the morning while hiding in a coal cellar that smelled of other people's secrets — had been simple: take the page to the magistrate. Then she remembered that the magistrates answered to the crown, and the crown had invented the Veilhunt, and bringing proof of corruption to the corrupt was like returning a borrowed knife to someone who'd used it to rob you.

Her second plan was to run to the coast and take ship somewhere the crown's writ ended. But ships cost money she didn't have, and besides, the page would burn just as well in another country. The point wasn't to save herself. The point was to save Mirabel, and the four names above his, and every name that would come after if no one spoke.

Which left her third plan, the one she kept circling like a cat around a bath: the crown's own archivist.

Everyone in the Guild knew of Master Oren Fitch, though almost no one had met him. He kept the Royal Archive beneath the Hall of Records, a man said to have catalogued forty thousand documents and befriended none of them. There were stories. That he had once refused to surrender a file even to the King's own spymaster, citing — with visible pleasure — an irregularity in the request form. That he wept when a clerk spilled tea on a tax roll from the reign of the second Aldric. That he hated the Veilhunt, not out of kindness, but because the wardens' paperwork was *sloppy*. Executions filed under the wrong classification. Confiscated maps catalogued as "miscellaneous." A purge conducted, in Fitch's considered opinion, like a butcher performing surgery with an axe.

It was, Sela admitted, a thin reed to lean on. But she had watched Mirabel survive on thinner ones for years — hiding a whole luminous country inside a clerk's careful life — and thin reeds, bundled together, made boats.

* * *

She spent two days getting there, because a woman traveling alone toward the Hall of Records with a guilty face might as well wear a sign. So she practiced not having a guilty face. She bought a basket and filled it with onions, on the theory that no one interrogates a woman carrying onions. She adopted the expression of someone mildly annoyed about prices, which required no acting whatsoever.

On the second evening she reached the capital's older quarter, where the Hall of Records squatted like a fat grey toad that had decided long ago it was too important to move. Its windows were few and high. Its doors were oak banded in iron. Above the entrance, chiseled into stone: *ALL THINGS KNOWN, KEPT.*

"Arrogant," Sela murmured, adjusting her onion basket. "But at least they're honest about it."

Getting in was the problem. The public reading room closed at dusk. The archive below was open to precisely nobody, except by writ of the crown or the personal invitation of the archivist himself.

Sela sat on a bench across the square, pretending great interest in her onions, and watched the door until the lamps were lit. Clerks came and went. Two Veilwardens stood post until evening, then handed off to two more. And once — just once — a small stooped figure emerged to collect a delivery of crates, checked each manifest against some inner catalogue with the expression of a man personally offended by at least three of them, and went back inside.

Master Oren Fitch. Or so she hoped. He moved like a man who had spent fifty years among shelves and had come to prefer them.

Sela waited until the square emptied, then crossed to the delivery door, where a bored porter guarded a slot barely wide enough for a crate.

"Delivery," she said.

"For whom?"

"The archivist."

The porter looked at her onions, her shawl, her face. "He doesn't take deliveries after hours. Come back tomorrow. Fill out form eleven."

"I can't come back tomorrow," Sela said. "By tomorrow I may be dead, and dead women fill out terrible forms."

The porter blinked. "That's — not standard."

"No," Sela agreed. "None of this is. Please tell him there's a woman here with a document he'll want to see, and if he doesn't want to see it, tell him it concerns the Veilhunt's filing practices, and watch what happens to his face."

The porter hesitated. Then, perhaps because boredom is the mother of curiosity, he knocked, muttered through the crack, and disappeared. Sela stood in the cold with her onions and rehearsed what she would say, discarding versions. *I'm a thief.* True but alarming. *I'm a patriot.* False and worse. Finally she settled on the only honest opening available:

"You don't know me, and you have every reason to hand me over, and I'm asking you to listen anyway."

* * *

The door opened onto a corridor lined with lamps turned low, and at the end of it, a round little room that smelled of dust, lamp oil, and something faintly sweet — old glue, maybe, or the ghost of a hundred thousand leather bindings. Shelves climbed the walls and vanished into shadow overhead. Every surface held paper. Paper held down by glass weights, paper in pigeonholes, paper stacked in towers that defied several laws Sela had assumed were non-negotiable.

And behind a desk built for a much larger man sat Master Oren Fitch, who turned out to be small, round-shouldered, spectacled, and approximately as threatening as a teapot. His eyes, behind the spectacles, were quick and unamused.

"You have ninety seconds," he said. "I was cataloguing."

"Cataloguing what?"

"A seizure inventory from the Veilwardens." His mouth thinned. "Forty-one maps entered as 'miscellaneous.' Forty-one. Maps are not miscellaneous. Maps are *cartographic*." He caught himself, glared at her as though she'd tricked him into an opinion. "Eighty seconds. Speak."

Sela set down her basket, drew out the folded page, and unfolded it on his desk with hands that only shook a little.

Fitch looked. She watched him look — watched the quick eyes go down the column of names, stop at the bottom, and go back up again, slower. When he reached the bottom the second time, he took off his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve, put them back on, and read it a third time, as if hoping the page would apologize.

"This is Corvane's hand," he said quietly. "Guildmaster Corvane. Of the Guild of Impossible Things."

"Yes."

"This is a list of gifted persons sold to the Office of the Veilhunt. In exchange for—" his finger tapped a notation at the top "—a charter renewal and tariff exemption. Dated before the Proclamation. Before the hunt was *announced*."

"Yes."

Fitch sat back. For a long moment the only sound was a clock somewhere in the shelves, ticking with the smugness of clocks everywhere.

"Do you understand what you're claiming?" he said. "That the Veilhunt — the crown's great moral purification, all those proclamations about cleansing the realm of unnatural sight — was scheduled *around* a trade negotiation? That people died to improve a guild's profit margin?"

"I'm not claiming it," Sela said. "I'm showing it. There's a difference. Claims can be argued with. Ink is ink."

"Ink can be forged."

"Then forge-check it. You're the archivist. You have samples of his hand going back twenty years, I'd wager, in trade filings. Compare them. That's why I came to you and not a magistrate — a magistrate will decide first and examine later. You'll examine first and hate deciding at all."

Something flickered across Fitch's face — not quite a smile, but the ancestor of one. "You've thought about this."

"I've had two days and nothing else to think about."

"And your interest? These names mean nothing to me. Why does a guild clerk risk exile and worse to carry their bill of sale across the city?"

Sela had prepared answers. Patriotism. Justice. The usual coin of such conversations. What came out instead was simpler.

"One of them is my friend. He's not on this page yet — it says *pending* — but pending has a way of becoming done when no one's watching. And the others are people I shared bread with. Tam Weller, who could make a locked door forget it was locked. Mistress Anthe, who heard what rivers were thinking. They weren't criminals. They were *inventory*." Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it. "You hate sloppy work, Master Fitch. Well. Here is the sloppiest work in the realm, and it's wearing the crown's seal."

Fitch was silent for a while. He rose, went to a shelf, ran a finger along spines without seeing them, and came back.

"When the seizures began," he said, "I filed a formal objection. Not about the executions — I want that understood, I filed nothing noble. About the *records*. Confiscated maps uncatalogued. Detainees logged under initials. One warden spelled the same surname three different ways in a single report." He shook his head. "My objection was returned stamped *noted*, which is the crown's way of saying *stop writing to us*. I told myself: you keep the archive, Fitch. Let the world outside do whatever the world outside does. Archives survive regimes. That's rather the point of them."

"And now?"

"Now you've brought the regime's sins into my archive and asked me to file them under *treason*." He looked at the page a long time. "There is a version of tonight where I call the wardens. It would be correct procedure. It would protect the archive. It would get you hanged and my conscience filed away in a drawer I've kept shut for thirty years." He sat down heavily. "I find, at my age, that drawer has begun to rattle."

"So you'll help?"

"I'll help," Fitch said. "Conditionally. Sit down, Miss—"

"Voss. Sela Voss."

"Sit down, Miss Voss, and tell me everything, beginning with how a guild clerk came to steal from her own guildmaster, because I promise you the details matter. Documents have contexts, and contexts are where lies hide." He pulled a fresh sheet toward himself, dipped his pen, and paused. "Also, is that basket actually full of onions?"

"It's full of onions."

"Good instinct," said Fitch gravely. "No one searches an onion basket. Now — from the beginning."

* * *

They worked past midnight. Sela talked; Fitch wrote, cross-examined, wrote again. Twice he sent her to fetch specific volumes from the shelves — trade filings from the guild, charter records, the Veilhunt's own seizure logs — and each time she returned, he laid the documents side by side like a physician comparing symptoms.

It was near the third bell when he stopped writing altogether.

"What is it?" Sela asked.

Fitch didn't answer at once. He laid two pages flat: Corvane's sold-names ledger, and a royal warrant authorizing the Veilhunt's first seizures. He aligned their dates.

"The Proclamation was posted on the ninth of Frostmonth," he said slowly. "This sale was concluded on the fourth. Five days. Which means the crown knew exactly whom to seize first — six names, delivered ready-made, before the hunt had officially begun." He looked up, and behind the spectacles his eyes had gone very still. "Miss Voss, this isn't a guild betraying its members to save itself. Or rather — it is, but it's also something larger. Someone on the crown's side wanted this list. Wanted the *hunt* shaped around it. Do you know who countersigned the charter renewal?"

Sela leaned over. The signature meant nothing to her — a name, a flourish, a seal.

"Lord Chancellor Havermar," Fitch said. "Who, I happen to know from forty years of filings, holds a private trading interest in rare components. Inks, bindings, preserved curiosities. Impossible things, Miss Voss. With the gifted cartographers and hearth-witches gone, the market in impossible things stops being a guild matter and becomes a *monopoly*." He set down his pen with the care of a man handling something explosive. "Your guildmaster didn't sell six people to save forty. He sold six people to become sole supplier to a lord who engineered the famine."

The clock ticked. Somewhere far above, the city slept on, unaware its proclamations had been bookkeeping all along.

"Fitch," Sela said, "Mirabel — my friend, the pending name — he found something. Weeks ago. His maps started bleeding color into the real world. He said the covenant between our realm and the unmapped lands was failing, that the last cartographer who renewed it was betrayed, and that he had to cross over and renew it himself. I thought grief had bent him. I hid him in a drawn doorway during the raid and I thought — I thought the worst thing happening in the world was the Veilhunt." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "What if he was right about both halves? What if the betrayal he named and the betrayal on this page are the *same* betrayal, wearing different coats?"

Fitch removed his spectacles entirely. Without them his face looked suddenly older, and more decided.

"Then the truth you're carrying is bigger than a ledger page," he said. "And it needs a bigger vessel than either of us." He began, briskly, pulling volumes from the shelf — warrants, charters, correspondence indexes. "Because if Lord Chancellor Havermar engineered a purge for profit, then the proof won't be in one stolen page. It'll be threaded through forty years of filings, and I am the only man alive who knows where every thread is knotted." He glanced up at her. "We'll need the rest of the night, Miss Voss. Possibly several nights. Possibly, if we're clumsy, considerably fewer."

"How much fewer?"

"Oh," said Fitch, dipping his pen, "on the order of minutes. Because I've just realized whose seal is on the delivery manifest from three weeks ago — the one I flagged as irregular and couldn't place." He turned a document toward her, and there it was: the chancellor's crest, pressed into wax beside an order routing seized maps — *all* seized maps — not to the archive, but to a private warehouse on Candlemaker's Wharf.

Every map taken in the Veilhunt. Every chart of every gifted hand. Collected, not destroyed.

Someone wasn't hunting the sight. Someone was *harvesting* it.

And the warehouse key, Fitch said, tapping the manifest's final line, had been signed out yesterday — by Guildmaster Corvane himself.
