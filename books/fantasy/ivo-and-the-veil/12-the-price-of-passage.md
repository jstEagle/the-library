## 12. The Price of Passage

The river had no name, which was fitting, because neither did the country it ran through—not anymore, not officially, and possibly never. It moved the way ink moves when you tilt a page: slow at first, then all at once, with a kind of dark intention.

Mirabel stood on the near bank and tried to remember how many days he had been walking since he'd left the Warden of Margins at the edge of the fen. Three? Four? Time in the unmapped lands didn't so much pass as drift, like a leaf deciding whether it wanted to be downstream or simply elsewhere. His boots were ruined. His coat, which had once been respectable guild-issue gray, was now the color of every puddle he'd ever stepped in, which is to say honest.

The ferryman arrived without arriving. One moment the bank was empty; the next there was a boat, long and low, its hull made of something that looked like birch bark but gave off a faint smell of old libraries. The ferryman himself was tall in the way that reeds are tall—thin, patient, bent slightly by currents no one else could feel. He wore a cloak of stitched-together map fragments, and where his face should have been there was a smooth expanse of pale vellum, blank except for two faint creases where eyes might go, if eyes were the sort of thing one bothered with.

"You'll be wanting to cross," the ferryman said. His voice was dry paper turning over.

"I'm wanting to reach the covenant-stone," Mirabel said. "I understand the river's the only way."

"The stone sits at the heart of everything unmapped," the ferryman agreed. "And the heart of everything unmapped sits on the far side of me. So yes. You'll be wanting to cross." A pause, the length of someone deciding whether to mention an awkward relative. "There's a fare."

Mirabel had prepared for this. He'd spent three days—two?—inventing currencies. He had a stub of charcoal, half a biscuit gone pleasantly geological, a brass button he'd found and grown fond of, and, folded into his inner pocket, the last surviving scrap of his own handiwork: a small sketch of a doorway, drawn in real ink from the other side of the world, still faintly warm.

"I have things," he said, and began listing them with the cheerful desperation of a man haggling in a language he doesn't speak. "Charcoal. A button. A biscuit of considerable historical interest. And—" he patted his pocket, "—a door."

The ferryman regarded him. Or rather, the blankness of his face arranged itself into what Mirabel's imagination generously interpreted as regarding.

"Those are objects," the ferryman said. "I run a river, not a pawnshop. The fare for crossing unmapped water is memory. One memory, freely given. It has to weigh enough to matter." He tilted his reed-thin head. "You look like a man carrying several that would do nicely."

* * *

They sat on the bank while Mirabel argued, because arguing felt better than weeping, and he suspected the ferryman had seen plenty of both.

"Surely there's a sliding scale," Mirabel said. "A minor memory. The taste of porridge. The name of a clerk I disliked. I disliked several clerks; I'd happily give you the whole department."

"Porridge isn't heavy enough." The ferryman drew a line in the mud with one long finger, and the line became a small river, and a tiny paper boat sailed down it, just to demonstrate, apparently, that he could. "The river runs on weight. Light memories float away before they reach the current. The water needs ballast. Something that holds you to your shape."

"My shape is holding up fine, thank you."

"It isn't." The ferryman said this without cruelty, the way you'd tell a man his bootlace was undone. "You've been invisible for years, cartographer. Living behind ledgers. Hiding your hands. A man who hides long enough starts to thin out at the edges. The river can see it. You're mostly margins already."

Mirabel looked down at his hands. They seemed solid enough. But then, he'd always been good at seeming.

"What happens to the memory?" he asked. "When you take it?"

"It goes into the river." The ferryman gestured at the dark water with something almost like reverence. "Nothing here is destroyed. That's the whole trouble with this country—it remembers everything and forgets nothing, which is why it's coming apart. Your memory will sink to the bottom and become part of the riverbed. Some nights, if the water's very still, you can see them down there. Faces. Kitchens. First kisses. A truly alarming quantity of dogs."

"And I won't remember it at all?"

"You'll know you had something there. Like a book missing from a shelf—the gap stays, the shape of the absence. But the book itself belongs to the river now."

Mirabel turned the brass button over in his fingers. He'd grown fond of it in four days, which told him something uncomfortable about how easily he grew attached to things that couldn't leave him.

"How do I choose?" he asked quietly.

"That's the fare within the fare," the ferryman said. "Everyone who stands on this bank has one memory they're hoping I don't ask for. My job is to ask for exactly that one. Not because I'm cruel." The blank face tilted. "Because the river knows. It only takes what's truly given, and only what's truly given weighs anything. If you offer me porridge, the boat sinks. If you offer me the thing you're guarding—" he spread his long hands, "—the boat floats like it was born on water. Which, in a sense, it was."

* * *

Mirabel walked a little way up the bank, because some inventory-taking requires privacy, and began the terrible arithmetic.

His mother had died when he was nine, in the ordinary unfair way that mothers died in the quarter where he'd grown up—fever, winter, a doctor whose remedies cost more than the cure would have. What remained of her lived in a handful of memories, and he knew each one the way a miser knows coins.

There was her voice, humming while she mended. He could keep that; it lived in his ears more than his mind.

There was her laugh, rare and startled, like a bird surprised by its own joy. Keepable.

There was her kitchen—the smell of cinnamon she could never afford but bought anyway, once a year, because some economies are about dignity rather than money. Keepable, probably. Smells were stubborn.

And there was her face.

He tried to hold it now, sitting on the cold bank of a nameless river, and found it slippery in the way that matters most. He could remember that her eyes had been brown—but brown like what? Chestnut? Earth? He could remember she'd been beautiful, but beauty is a verdict, not a description. Every year the picture faded a little more, the way all his maps faded when he didn't redraw them, and he had never dared redraw her, because drawing her felt like admitting she needed saving from forgetting, and he hadn't been ready to admit that.

So of course. Of course that was the one the river wanted. The river wasn't cruel; it was precise.

"There's another way," he said, walking back. "There's always another way. People say there isn't, but people also said the world was flat, and here we are standing on a country that's mostly rumor."

"There's another way," the ferryman agreed amiably. "You can stay on this bank. The bank is lovely this time of year. The bank is lovely every time of year; that's the problem with banks. Meanwhile the covenant fails, and both realms unravel, and everyone you've ever known becomes a sketch of themselves and then not even that. But the bank remains. Banks are very durable."

"You're enjoying this."

"I'm enjoying the company," the ferryman said. "It's been eleven years since anyone stood here. The last one didn't argue. She just paid and got in the boat and cried the whole way across, silently, which I confess I respected." He paused. "She was called Alderyn."

Mirabel went still. "The last cartographer."

"The last cartographer," the ferryman echoed. "She paid a memory too. Hers was heavier than most." He did not elaborate, and Mirabel understood, with a small cold shock, that the ferryman was being kind by not elaborating—that whatever Alderyn had given up, it was not his business yet, though it might become his business soon, at a grave, with a Warden standing witness.

"Why does the stone need us to pay?" Mirabel asked. "Why does everything in this country cost the exact thing you can least spare?"

The ferryman considered this for a long moment. Somewhere out on the water, a fish surfaced—or something fish-shaped, drawn in confident strokes—and sank again.

"Because a covenant is a promise," he said finally, "and a promise made of nothing costs nothing and binds nothing. Alderyn crossed with her fare. Whoever renews the covenant next must cross with theirs. The stone doesn't want payment, cartographer. It wants proof that you understand what keeping something costs. Anyone who hasn't paid will promise lightly. And light promises are how we all ended up here, on a river with no name, waiting eleven years for someone to show up and do the arithmetic properly."

* * *

Mirabel sat back down on the bank and did the arithmetic.

He thought about Sela, somewhere on the other side of the veil, stealing pages and running through streets that might, even now, be softening at their edges. He thought about the guild, about Corvane's quiet ledger with his name at the bottom. He thought about the door in his map that had opened, and the voice that had called his name—Ivo, it had said, Ivo—in a voice he had not heard in twenty-five years and recognized instantly, the way you recognize your own heartbeat.

He had told himself the voice was memory. He had told himself a great many soothing things.

If he gave up her face, would the voice stop meaning anything? Would he cross the river and reach the stone and hear his mother's name again and feel nothing but the shape of an absence?

Or—

and here was the thought he'd been circling for three days, four days, like a moth around a lamp he refused to name—

or was the face already going? Was he standing on this bank guarding a coin that was wearing thin in his pocket regardless, hoarding a fading sketch and calling it treasure? The river hadn't put the fade there. Twenty-five years had. All the river offered was a trade: give up the losing battle, and gain the crossing. Gain the stone. Gain Sela's world staying solid under her feet. Gain, perhaps, at the end of it all, whatever waited at the heart of the unmapped lands wearing his mother's voice.

"You keep it safe, you know," the ferryman said softly. Mirabel startled; he hadn't heard the long body move. "What you give the river. It's not lost. It's kept. There's a difference, though I grant it's a difference that feels identical from the bank."

"Comforting," Mirabel said. "In the way that a receipt is comforting."

"A receipt is extremely comforting," the ferryman said. "Ask anyone who's ever been robbed."

Mirabel laughed—a short, cracked sound, surprised out of him—and found that the laughing helped, the way it always did, the way gentle humor helps at funerals: not by denying the grief but by giving it room to breathe.

"All right," he said. "All right. Her face. I pay with my mother's face."

The blank countenance did something Mirabel had no word for. Sympathy, perhaps, in a grammar older than faces.

"Freely given?" the ferryman asked. "Not traded under protest, not surrendered in despair, but given? The river can tell the difference. Believe me. I have seen men try to cheat it with counterfeit willingness, and the boat rode so low in the water we nearly drowned in a river two inches deep."

Mirabel closed his eyes. He built the picture one last time, carefully, deliberately, the way you look and look at a place before a long journey, trying to fix it: the kitchen, the cinnamon, the winter light, and above it all her face—brown eyes, warm, tired, amused at whatever small solemn nonsense he was saying at nine years old, looking at him the way no one had ever looked at him since, as though he were a finished thing and a good one.

"Freely given," he said. "For Sela. For both worlds. For her, if she's anywhere in this. Freely."

"Then step aboard," said the ferryman, "and pay at midstream. That's the custom. Debts are settled where the water is deepest, so the river hears them best."

* * *

The boat moved without oars, without wind, the way a sentence moves once you've committed to its first word. The banks slid away. The water beneath them was not water, quite—it was depth itself, and far down, dim as stars through fog, Mirabel saw them: the memories. Kitchens glowing like lanterns. A wedding dance turning slowly. Dogs—truly an alarming quantity of dogs, romping on some eternal riverbed lawn. And nearer the surface, fresher, a woman's face looking up through the dark with an expression he couldn't quite read, and felt, absurdly, jealous of whoever owned it.

"Midstream," the ferryman said.

Mirabel knelt at the center of the boat. He cupped his hands as though scooping water, though there was nothing in them but the memory itself, rising now, heavy, obedient, grieving.

"Her name was Liora," he said aloud, because it seemed important that the river have the whole record. "She hummed off-key. She bought cinnamon she couldn't afford. She called me Ivo." His voice caught on the name and steadied. "She had brown eyes. I'm sorry—I don't remember what kind of brown anymore. I'm giving you what I have."

The river received it.

There was no drama. No lurch, no tearing. The memory simply left, the way a held breath leaves—relief and loss in the same instant, indistinguishable. And then there was a gap in him, clean-edged, book-shaped, and he knelt in the boat knowing that a woman had loved him, knowing her name, her off-key humming, the cinnamon, the word *Ivo*—knowing everything except the one thing a child keeps longest, and feeling the absence the way you feel a tooth: constantly, with your tongue, unable to stop.

"Well," he said, after a while. His voice came out smaller than intended. "That's that."

"That's that," the ferryman agreed. He steered with a slight motion of his whole body, reed-bent. After a silence he added, in a tone that was almost gentle: "For what it's worth, cartographer—you floated true. The boat hasn't ridden this high in eleven years."

Mirabel managed a wet laugh. "Do you say that to everyone?"

"No," said the ferryman. "To almost no one. Most fares fight the current the whole way across, clutching what they gave up as if they could take it back. You let it sink. That's rarer than courage, honestly. Courage is common on this river. Letting-go is the scarce commodity."

Ahead, the far bank was resolving out of mist—and beyond it, Mirabel could see the land change, rising toward a distant upland where something vast and pale caught the light like a tooth of the world. The covenant-stone. Even at this distance it pulled at him, the way north pulls at a compass needle, the way home pulls at the homesick.

"One more thing," the ferryman said as the hull whispered against the shallows. "A professional courtesy. When you reach the stone, it will ask you for your true name. Freely given, in front of witnesses, written where no one can ever unwrite it. No more hiding, in either realm. Most folk balk at that harder than they balked at my fare." The blank face turned toward him. "But you've already practiced, haven't you? Giving up the hiding. You did it the moment you paid."

Mirabel stepped onto the far bank. His legs were unsteady. The gap in him ached like weather.

Behind him, the ferryman was already fading back into the mist, but his voice carried, dry paper turning one last page:

"She's down there, you know. In the river. Looking up. And I'll tell you what I told no one else in eleven years—" the mist took him almost entirely, leaving only the words, "—she looks proud."

Mirabel stood a long moment on the shore of the unmapped lands, weeping without quite knowing at what, and then turned toward the pale tooth of the mountain, and began to climb.
