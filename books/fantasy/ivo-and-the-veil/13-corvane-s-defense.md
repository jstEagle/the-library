## 13. Corvane's Defense

They came for him at the third hour past midnight, which Corvane considered almost flattering. In his experience, angry mobs preferred the second hour—early enough to feel righteous, late enough to have fortified themselves at the Broken Compass down the street. The survivors of the Guild of Impossible Things arrived sober, grim, and carrying torches they had evidently forgotten to light, because Pip Wrenlow had tripped over the threshold and doused them all in the rain barrel.

Corvane had been awake anyway. He was always awake now. Sleep, he had discovered, was a country he no longer held papers for.

He opened the door before they could knock.

"Come in," he said. "You're letting the cold in, and Mrs. Halloway's ghost complains about drafts."

There was no Mrs. Halloway's ghost. There had never been a Mrs. Halloway. But the guild members hesitated on the doorstep like cats presented with an open door they'd spent years scratching at, and that hesitation bought him the thing he needed most: not safety—he did not expect safety—but an audience.

Forty-one of them crowded into his study. Forty-one faces he knew by name, by salary, by the particular way each of them stole quills from the supply closet and pretended they hadn't. Forty-one people whose names were *not* in the ledger.

That was the arithmetic. He had done it a thousand times since the Night of Long Knives, turning it over like a coin worn smooth: six names given, forty-one kept. Six for forty-one. Any merchant in the city would have called it a triumph of trade.

"Master Corvane," said Odile Fenn, the guild's mistress of bindings, and her voice had the flat quality of someone who had rehearsed this on the walk over. "We want the truth."

"The truth," Corvane repeated pleasantly, "is the single most expensive commodity this guild trades in. Are you prepared to pay for it?"

"We buried three people today," said Odile. "The ones the wardens didn't take whole."

"I know," Corvane said. "I dug two of the graves myself. You'll notice I did not delegate that."

Something flickered across several faces at that—the uncomfortable recognition that tyrants do not usually handle their own shovels. Corvane catalogued the flicker and moved on, the way he moved through every negotiation: one inch of ground surrendered only to gain a mile.

* * *

He let them arrange themselves first. This too was craft. Angry crowds stand in a horseshoe; grieving crowds cluster. He needed the clustering. So he poured tea—forty-one cups, because of course he had counted them coming up the stairs—and waited while the smell of it did its slow work. Grief and tea are old colleagues. They soften a room together.

Pip Wrenlow, youngest of the clerks, took his cup with shaking hands and said, "You gave them our people."

"I gave the crown six names," Corvane corrected gently. "I did not give them our people. Our people were taken. There is a difference, and the difference is the forty-one of you standing here drinking my tea instead of floating in the canal with your hands bound."

Silence. Good. Silence meant they were listening, and listening crowds could be led; shouting crowds could only be survived.

"You think it was easy?" He set down the pot. "Let me show you what easy looks like." He went to his desk, unlocked the drawer that no one had ever seen him open, and laid the ledger face-up on the table between them.

The room made a sound—a collective indrawn breath, the sound of forty-one people realizing that the book of their fates existed, had always existed, sat three feet from where they took their morning assignments.

"Read it," Corvane said. "Go on. Read every name. And then read the column beside it."

Beside each sold name, in his own small precise hand, was written a number. Beside Aldous Merrick: eleven. Eleven guild members who would have been arrested alongside him when the wardens traced the smuggling route through his accounts. Beside Tam Vessic: nine. Beside the others, smaller numbers, until the column summed itself, silently, terribly, to forty-seven.

"Every name I gave them bought lives," Corvane said. "Not metaphorically. Arithmetically. The Veilwardens came to me in the spring with a charter offer: cooperate, and the guild stands. Refuse, and they burn us to the foundation stones and take everyone inside, gifted or not. You've all seen what happens to unregistered buildings full of suspicious paper." He nodded toward the window, beyond which half the district still bore scorch marks from the purges. "I chose six so that forty-one could wake up this morning. If you can find me a better trade, I will sign it tonight."

* * *

It was old Master Quillon—the archivist emeritus, ninety if he was a day, who had once tried to catalogue a paradox and been missing for a week—who spoke next, and Corvane had been waiting for him the way a duelist waits for the opponent's best blade.

"You keep saying *chose*," Quillon rasped, "as though the choosing makes it clean. A man who pushes six strangers off a ferry to save forty has still pushed six strangers off a ferry."

"Yes," Corvane said. "He has. And the ferry sinks either way, and forty families light candles instead of sixty-eight." He met the old man's eyes. "I have never claimed to be innocent, Quillon. I claim to be *useful*, which is the only virtue that survives a purge. Innocence is a luxury for people other kingdoms haven't noticed yet."

"But Mirabel—" began Pip.

"Ah." Corvane smiled, and it cost him more than any smile he had ever spent. "Yes. Let us speak of Mirabel Quintana, since you all loved him, as did I, in whatever way it is I love things. Do you know what Mirabel is? He is a man who can draw doors into other worlds. Do you know what the crown does with men who can draw doors into other worlds? They do not hang them, children. Hanging is quick. They put them in rooms with candles and maps and questions, and they take apart what he can do piece by piece until there is nothing left of the gift or the man, and then they point what remains at the borders of every realm we have treaties with." He let that sit. "Mirabel's name was at the bottom of that ledger for four months. Four months I lied to men with iron badges to keep it there. Every day I delayed was a day I gambled forty-one lives against one friendship. When the raid came, the debt had come due, and I paid what I could afford."

"And what you couldn't afford," said Odile quietly, "was being the kind of guild worth saving."

For a moment—one moment, brief as a candle guttering—Corvane felt the truth of it go through him like a splinter under the nail. Then he did what he had trained himself forty years to do with truths: he set them on the table, examined their weight, and charged admission.

* * *

"Some believe him."

Sela Voss stood in the doorway of the map-room—ruined, scorched, the drawn door through which Mirabel had vanished still faintly luminous at its center, refusing to fade—and listened to the murmur rising from Corvane's study below. She had come back for the archivist's key she'd hidden in the atlas case, and she had stayed because she could not make her feet obey her.

She heard Pip Wrenlow's voice, young and cracking: "But he *lied* to us. For years. While he shook our hands."

She heard Quillon: "And you have never lied, boy? Never once told a dying client their map would lead somewhere safe?"

And she heard Corvane, warm and reasonable and terrible: "I am not asking for forgiveness. Forgiveness is for after. I am asking for the guild to survive, and survival requires that someone be willing to be hated. I volunteered. It is the last position I will ever hold here, and I intend to perform its duties competently."

Sela pressed her hand against her coat, against the stolen page folded flat over her heart, and understood suddenly why her knees were shaking—not from fear of Corvane, but from the horrible, unforgivable suspicion that he might be *right about some of it*. That was his real weapon. Not the ledger. Not the crown. The fact that the mathematics worked, if you agreed to stop counting people as people.

She thought of Mirabel's midnight inks glowing blue-green through the cracks of his desk. She thought of the page in her coat with his name at the bottom of a column of the sold.

"No," she whispered to the empty map-room, to the luminous door, to nobody. "Mathematics that require you to stop counting people aren't mathematics. They're just arithmetic with the souls removed."

The door in the floor shimmered. Sela froze. And then—from somewhere beneath the parchment, beneath the world, thin as ink in water—a voice that sounded almost like Mirabel said her name.

* * *

Downstairs, Corvane concluded the evening the way he concluded all negotiations: with a gift.

"I'm not asking you to stay," he told them, standing among his cooling teacups. "Those who wish to leave may leave tomorrow, with severance, with references, with my blessing. Those who stay will help me rebuild what the wardens left us. Either way, remember this night accurately. Not as I tell it. As it happened. Because accurate memory is the only ledger anyone should trust—and I say that as a man whose entire life has been a forgery of one."

They filed out slowly. Some wouldn't meet his eyes. Some—Odile, to his surprise; Pip, less to his surprise—paused at the door and inclined their heads a fraction, hating themselves for it, and Corvane accepted the gesture without gloating, because gloating was for men who had won something rather than merely postponed a loss.

When the study was empty, Corvane sat down at his desk and drew out a fresh sheet of paper. At the top, in his small precise hand, he wrote:

*Accounts outstanding.*

Below it: *Six names, settled.* Below that: *One guild, unsettled.* And at the bottom, after a long pause, in letters smaller than any he had ever used:

*Mirabel Quintana — status unknown. Probability of return: non-zero. Consequence if returned: total.*

He stared at those words for a long time. Then he added one final line, and it was the closest thing to a prayer he had produced in thirty years:

*If he comes back, do not let him read this ledger. Let him hate me for the simple version. The complicated version would break him, and broken cartographers redraw the world.*

Above his head, unnoticed, the lamp flame bent sideways—as though a draft had entered the sealed room from nowhere at all, smelling faintly of rain on stone, and river water, and home.
