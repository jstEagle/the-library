## 14. The Archivist's Cellar

The crown archives smelled of dust, tallow, and the particular melancholy of paper nobody had loved in a hundred years.

Sela Voss stood at the top of a spiral stair that went down rather than up, which she had always felt was a small architectural betrayal, and watched a stooped figure shuffle toward her through the gloom below, holding a lantern that seemed to be held together mostly by wax.

"You're the clerk," the figure said. Not a question.

"I'm the clerk."

"The one who's been sending letters signed with three different names, each one more threatening than the last." The lantern rose, illuminating a face like a walnut left out in interesting weather — deep creases, sharp eyes, eyebrows that had clearly given up on dignity decades ago. "I liked the second name best. 'A Concerned Citizen.' Very menacing. I've been threatened by actual Concerned Citizens before. They're terrifying."

Sela had spent four days finding this man. Four days of sleeping in doorways with a stolen ledger page sewn into her coat lining, of learning which alleys the Veilwardens patrolled and which they merely strolled, of writing letters to the crown's archivist because he was, according to three separate rumors and one very drunk former guild accountant, the only person in the palace who hated the Veilhunt more than he feared it.

His name was Odo Fenwick. He looked like a man who had been forgotten by history and had decided, on reflection, to return the favor.

"Master Fenwick," Sela said. "I need—"

"Down," he said. "Talking happens down. Up here the walls have opinions." He turned and shuffled back into the dark, apparently confident she would follow, which was either trust or indifference, and after four days on the run Sela found she didn't much care which.

* * *

The cellar beneath the archives was not what she expected. She had imagined rows of state documents, sealed writs, the machinery of power laid out in neat cabinets. Instead there were shelves sagging under ledgers of grain tariffs from reigns so old the kings in them were only coin-portraits now, boxes of unopened correspondence marked RETURN WHEN CONVENIENT, and one entire wall devoted to complaints about the palace drains, going back ninety years.

"It's all important," Odo said, catching her look. "That's the joke nobody tells you about archives. Everything is important. That's why nothing gets read." He set his lantern on a table buried under an avalanche of scroll-cases and gestured to a stool that had lost its argument with gravity some time ago. "Sit. You wrote that the Veilhunt has a ledger behind it. Everyone says that. Fear always has a ledger behind it. What makes yours worth my neck?"

Sela reached into her coat. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; they hadn't been steady since the Night of Long Knives, since she'd crouched in the map-room's dark while half her colleagues were marched out the front doors, since she'd walked out the back with Corvane's private arithmetic folded against her ribs like a knife she couldn't take out.

She unfolded the page and laid it flat between them.

Odo bent over it. For a long moment he said nothing, and Sela watched his lips move faintly as he read — six names, six dates, six sums paid by the crown to the Guild of Impossible Things for the delivery of persons gifted with the sight. The last name on the list was Mirabel Quintana. The date beside it was blank. *Unsent. For now.*

"This is forgery," Odo said at last.

"No."

"Good," he said. "Because if it were a forgery I'd have to arrest you, and my knees can't manage arrests anymore." He straightened up, wincing, and fixed her with those sharp walnut-shell eyes. "But a stolen page proves theft, girl, not design. Corvane sold names. That makes him a coward and a collaborator. It doesn't make the hunt a scheme. Crowns don't need schemes to hunt people. Fear is cheaper."

"That's what I thought too," Sela said. "For about a day. Then I started thinking like a bookkeeper instead of a frightened person, and frightened people miss things bookkeepers catch."

Odo's eyebrows performed a slow, skeptical ascent. "Go on."

"Look at the sums. Six payments, escalating. The first two are modest — expenses, really. But the last four double each time. Why would the crown pay *more* per head as the hunt got easier? You'd think gifted folk would be harder to find as they fled, but the price goes *down* in difficulty and *up* in cost. That's backwards. Unless—" she tapped the page, "—unless the crown isn't setting the price. Unless someone's selling to the crown and marking it up."

Odo was quiet again. Somewhere above them, boots crossed the archive floor, paused, moved on. Sela realized she'd stopped breathing and made herself start.

"And where does a guildmaster learn to mark things up?" Odo murmured. "From his suppliers."

"Corvane doesn't supply the crown. He supplies *trade partners*. Impossible things move through maybe five houses in this city — the guild, and the partners who broker what the guild finds. If the sighted are being rounded up, who loses? The gifted. Who else loses?" She let the question sit. "Everyone who ever bought anything impossible from a gifted hand. And who gains?"

Odo lowered himself onto the surviving stool with the groan of a man whose body filed formal objections to everything. "The houses that still hold stockpiles," he said slowly. "Corner the market. Starve the competition. Make the gift itself contraband so that whatever remains becomes priceless." He rubbed his jaw. "It's an ugly theory. It's also exactly how wool merchants think, applied to human beings, which means it's probably correct. Merchants are the most predictable creatures alive. Give me a horror and I'll show you an invoice underneath it."

"Then help me prove it," Sela said. "You know these archives. There must be trade filings, import ledgers, something."

"There's everything," Odo said. "That's the problem. Come on. And mind the third shelf — it bites."

* * *

They worked through what Sela would later remember as the longest night of her life, though the nights that followed kept competing for the title.

Odo's method, it emerged, was less scholarship than siege warfare. He knew the archives the way a rat knows a granary — every gap, every misfiled treasure, every box that had been shoved somewhere inconvenient and never retrieved. He hauled down customs manifests from the year the Veilhunt was proclaimed. He dug out the partnership rolls of the five great houses of impossible trade: House Meridian, the Calloway Trust, the Vane Combine, Ostrec & Daughters ("the daughters do everything," Odo noted, "the ampersand does nothing"), and the Guild itself.

And he talked. That was the thing Sela hadn't expected — under the dust and the dryness, Odo Fenwick was *furious*, and had been for months, and had had no one to be furious at.

"Do you know what they did in the provinces?" he said, dropping another armload of ledgers onto the table. "Vellum-on-Brine, the little coastal town? They registered a woman who could mend broken pottery by singing to it. *Registered*, as the proclamation required. Honest as bread. The Veilwardens came within the week. Her neighbors swore later they'd only reported her because the notice said reporting was patriotic." He slammed a manifest open. "Patriotism, delivered by cart. I archived the report myself. I archived *her*. Name, gift, address, like she was a shipment of candles. I've been an archivist forty years and I never once wondered what the archiving was *for* until they started archiving people into graves."

He caught himself, glared at the ledger as if it had witnessed the outburst, and added, in a much smaller voice: "Don't tell anyone I said that. I'm very respected."

"You're very hidden," Sela said gently. "It's not the same thing."

"No," Odo agreed. "It is not. Which is why you're here instead of in a cell. Pass the Brine manifest."

The pattern surfaced around midnight, or what Sela guessed was midnight; cellars kept their own hours. It surfaced the way patterns always did for her — not in any single document but in the margins between them, the way Mirabel had once taught her to read maps: *the truth isn't in the lines, Sela, it's in the spaces where the lines refuse to go.*

House Meridian's imports of veilsilk — the rare thread used to bind sighted workings — had tripled in the two years before the proclamation. The Calloway Trust had quietly bought up every charter-license for sighted craftsmanship in the eastern provinces. The Vane Combine had taken out loans against projected revenues, and the revenue projections, filed innocently in a customs appendix nobody was meant to read, assumed — in black and white, in a clerk's tidy hand — *"a consolidation of the gifted trade following regulatory action."*

Regulatory action. Filed two years before the crown proclaimed the Veilhunt.

Sela stared at the line until the letters swam. "Odo."

"Mm." He was asleep sitting up, chin on chest, lantern guttering. She poked him. He came awake instantly, which she suspected was a skill learned across forty years of being caught napping.

She turned the appendix toward him and put her finger under the line.

He read it twice. When he looked up, all the drowsiness had burned off him like fog in sun.

"They wrote the hunt into their accounts," he said. "Before it existed."

"As a projection. As if it were weather." Sela's voice came out steadier than she felt. "You can't project a pogrom two years early unless you're planning to order one. This isn't fear, Odo. Fear doesn't file paperwork in advance. This is an investment."

Odo sat back. In the lantern light his walnut face did something complicated — grief and vindication wrestling for the same chair. "Meridian, Calloway, Vane," he said. "Three houses. And Corvane's guild as the fourth partner, selling the raw material." His mouth twisted. "People. Selling people as the raw material."

"And the fifth house?"

Odo frowned. "What fifth house?"

"The partnership rolls. Five houses of impossible trade. We've accounted for four."

Odo was already moving, hauling himself up the shelving with surprising speed for a man whose knees filed objections, pulling down a roll sealed with the archives' oldest wax. "The original compact," he muttered. "When the trade houses formalized. Five signatories. Let's see who had the good sense to keep their name off the recent paperwork."

He spread the roll flat. Five seals. Five names.

Four Sela recognized from the partnership rolls.

The fifth was written in a hand she knew better than her own — the careful, upright script that had signed her wages every month for six years, that had written *calm, we are safe within these walls* on the guild's notice board the day the proclamation went up.

Corvane. But not Corvane alone. Beside his name, in the same ink, a co-signatory she'd never heard of:

*Perpetua Ilvane, Keeper of the Fifth Seal.*

"Who," Sela said slowly, "is Perpetua Ilvane?"

Odo had gone very still, in the way of a man standing in a cellar who has just heard the first pebble of a landslide tick past his boot.

"Nobody," he said. "That's the point of a fifth seal. Four houses in the light, one house in the dark, holding the compact together. Every cartel keeps a silent partner, girl. The one who owns the debts." He traced the name with one cracked fingernail. "And if the other four houses are cornering the market in impossible things..." He looked up at her. "Then the fifth house owns the market. All of it. Including, I'd wager, the debts your guildmaster took on to buy his charter."

Outside, far above, a bell rang the hour — Sela counted four strokes, muffled by stone. Dawn was a rumor. The city above them was waking up, and somewhere in it, Veilwardens were waking up too, and somewhere in a ruined map-room, a man who trusted her was waiting for her to come back with proof, or not come back at all.

"We have it," she said. "Projection, purchase, profit. The whole chain. It's enough."

"It's enough to hang them," Odo agreed. "Which is unfortunately also enough to get us hanged, because proof in a cellar is just paper with ambitions. Proof needs a room full of witnesses and a reason for the wardens to listen." He began, briskly, copying the key passages in a quick cipher hand. "Your guildmaster will deny it. Houses Meridian and Calloway will deny it harder. So you'll want the one thing none of them can deny."

"Which is?"

Odo blew out a long breath. "The fifth seal, girl. Find me Perpetua Ilvane, and I'll find you the signature that ordered the hunt. Because I promise you it exists. Cartels this careful always write the order down." He glanced up, and for a moment the humor drained out of his face entirely, leaving something older and sadder underneath. "And when you find it — when you see whose hand signed it — I suspect you're going to wish it had stayed in the dark."

Sela folded the copied pages into her coat, next to the stolen ledger page, next to the memory of Mirabel's face when she'd lied to the Veilwarden for him.

"How bad can it be?" she asked.

Odo snuffed the lantern. In the sudden dark, his voice was dry as the dust around them.

"In forty years of archiving, I've learned one rule without exception," he said. "The worse the truth, the tidier the handwriting."
