## 15. What the Stone Asks

The covenant-stone did not look like the end of anything.

That was Mirabel's first thought, and he was almost offended by it. He had crossed a country stitched from forgotten geography—waded rivers that had never been named, climbed hills that existed only because someone had once misremembered them fondly—and at the heart of it all, the thing that held two worlds together turned out to be a rock about the size of a well-fed pig, sitting in a meadow of grass that grew in every direction at once.

"It's smaller than I imagined," he said.

"Everything is," said the Warden of Margins. "Imagination does the enlarging. That's rather its whole job."

The Warden had walked beside him for three days now, or what passed for days here, where the light came up out of the ground instead of down from the sky and shadows fell toward you like old friends. The Warden was difficult to look at directly—not because it was terrible, but because it kept being slightly more than whatever your eyes settled on. A tall figure, then a landscape, then a margin note in a dead cartographer's hand. Mirabel had learned to look at it the way one looks at a candle: necessary, and best not stared at.

They stood before the stone now. It was grey, unremarkable, veined with lines that might have been cracks or might have been writing. As Mirabel watched, one of the veins pulsed faintly—a dull amber, like a heartbeat heard through a wall—and then dimmed again.

"How long?" he asked.

The Warden was quiet a moment. "Since Alderyn died? Sixty years of thinning. Since her guild sold her name to a man who wanted her maps and not her truth—" the vein flickered, "—faster. What you saw on your side, the ink bleeding through, the colors running—that is the stone forgetting how to hold the border. And when it forgets entirely, there will be no border. There will be one realm, and it will be neither of yours."

Mirabel looked at his hands. They were ink-stained; they had been ink-stained since he was nine years old, when he'd first drawn a coastline no one had taught him and his mother had gone very quiet and told him never, ever to show anyone. He had spent thirty years hiding those hands inside ledgers. Balancing accounts for impossible trades. Being nobody, thoroughly and professionally.

"And renewal," he said. "Tell me exactly what it requires. I've come too far for riddles."

"No riddles," the Warden said. "Only terms." It gestured with something that might have been an arm, might have been a compass needle. "A covenant is a promise made by a realm to itself, witnessed by stone. To renew it, a cartographer must give three things. First, a true map—the land as it is, not as it flatters itself. You can do this. I have seen your work. It made the rivers blush."

"The rivers *what*?"

"They are vain creatures. Second, a price paid willingly—you have already paid it. The ferryman keeps your mother's face in his ledger now. He tells me she smiled often. I am sorry."

Mirabel's throat tightened. He had been carrying the absence for two days like a stone in his boot—knowing something precious was gone, unable to remember what. He nodded. "And third?"

"Third," the Warden said, and here its voice changed, became older and sadder, the voice of something that had watched this ceremony fail before, "the cartographer's true name. Given freely. Spoken aloud, written into the stone, bound into the covenant so that every mile of border, every year of peace, carries your name in its grain."

Mirabel laughed. It came out wrong, high and brittle. "My name? My name is Mirabel Quintana. I've used it for thirty years. Here—" he stopped.

Here was the problem, wasn't it. Here was the whole shape of it, laid bare in a meadow where grass grew sideways.

*Quintana* was the name on the guild ledger. The name he'd signed in careful clerk's script beneath ten thousand entries of impossible trade. But it wasn't the name his mother had given him, whispering it in his ear as a secret, telling him that names were doors and some doors must stay shut. It wasn't the name he'd whispered back to his maps at midnight, alone, the only witness a candle. It wasn't—

"Ivo," he said quietly. The syllables felt strange in his mouth, like a coat that still fit but hadn't been worn since childhood. "My true name is Ivo."

The veins in the stone brightened at the sound. Just slightly. Just enough.

"There," the Warden said gently. "You see? The stone knows you already. It has known you since you first drew a shore no one taught you."

He sat down in the sideways grass, because his legs had opinions of their own, and they had voted to sit.

"You don't understand what you're asking," he said. "Or—you do, which is worse. On my side of the veil, right now, there is a proclamation nailed to every door. *All who possess the sight shall register their names with the crown, or be taken.* Do you know what happens to people who register? Nothing good. Nothing quick. And do you know what happens to people who are *found* without registering? Also nothing good, but slower, and with more shouting."

"I know," the Warden said. "I read your proclamations. They drift across sometimes, caught on the border like leaves. Your crown has a florid hand. Very dramatic serifs."

"This isn't funny."

"No," the Warden agreed. "But grief and humor live in the same house, and neither pays rent."

Mirabel pulled his knees up. Somewhere behind him, a river that had never been named went on not being named, cheerfully. "If I give my name to the stone, the stone writes it into everything. Every border, every boundary, both realms. Which means—" he worked it through aloud, hating each step, "—which means anyone who touches the covenant, anyone who reads the stone, anyone with the sight or half the sight or a rumor of the sight, could learn it. The Veilwardens would know. Corvane would know. I would walk back into that city wearing my name on the outside of my skin."

"Yes," said the Warden.

"And they kill people like me."

"Yes," said the Warden, and then, softer: "And if you do not give it, they will kill everyone like everyone. The realms unravel, Ivo. Not dramatically, at first. A street that was never quite remembered dissolves into pencil. A person whose mother died before she could be properly loved fades at the edges. Then faster. Then all at once. I have seen a realm forget itself. It does not scream. It simply stops."

Mirabel looked at the stone. Its veins were dimming again, patiently, the way a fire dims when no one feeds it. Sixty years of neglect. One woman betrayed, and sixty years of consequences, and now it was his turn at the altar—or the ledger, which amounted to the same thing with better lighting.

"Alderyn gave her name," he said. "And her guild sold her anyway. Sold her *name*, literally, to whoever paid. If I give mine, what's to stop the same thing happening? What's to stop some future Corvane reading it off the stone and trading it for a charter?"

The Warden was silent for a long moment. When it spoke, it sounded tired in a way that made Mirabel think of clerks at the end of a fiscal year.

"That is the question, isn't it. And I will answer it honestly, because you have earned honesty and I have grown weary of mystery—it protects nothing. A name given freely is a door opened. Doors can be walked through. Alderyn gave her name and was betrayed, and the covenant weakened, and here we are. Giving your name does not make you safe. It makes you *known*. Those are different things, and your world has confused them for a long time."

"Known gets you killed."

"Hidden gets you erased," the Warden said. "I have watched you, these three days. You flinch when I say your name—even the false one. You apologize to furniture. When the ferryman asked what you carried, you said 'nothing much,' while holding a bag full of the most beautiful maps I have seen in a century. You have spent thirty years practicing being small, and you have become *very good at it*, and I want you to notice, right now, in this meadow, that it has not saved you. It has not saved Sela Voss, wherever she is. It has not saved the six names in Corvane's ledger. Smallness was supposed to be shelter. It turned out to be a slow leak."

Mirabel's eyes stung. He blamed the sideways grass. It was probably allergenic. Everything in an unmapped realm probably was.

"Sela stole a page for me," he said. "She doesn't even know if I'm alive. She chose exile—or worse—for a man who signed his letters with initials."

"Then it seems to me," the Warden said, "that someone decided you were worth being known for. Perhaps it's time you agreed with her."

He didn't decide quickly. He wanted that recorded somewhere, in case anyone ever wrote this down: he did not leap nobly to his feet with his chin raised and the wind catching his hair. There was no wind. This was a meadow. He sat in the grass for what might have been an hour or a season, arguing with a rock.

Because that was the other thing no one told you about destiny: it mostly involved sitting.

He argued that he wasn't brave. The stone didn't dispute this, which he found rude. He argued that there might be another way—some technicality, some clause, some clever clerk's loophole, and surely if anyone could find a loophole it was him, he'd spent a decade finding loopholes in trades involving bottled Tuesdays and secondhand lullabies. The stone pulsed once, slowly, in a way that managed to convey *no*. He argued that Sela would tell him to run. Then he stopped arguing, because he knew that was a lie. Sela would put her hands on her hips and say, in that voice she used for clients who tried to pay in promises, *Mirabel Quintana, you absolute ledger-keeper, you have been hiding in plain sight so long you've forgotten there was ever a reason.* She'd say it and then she'd probably cry a little and pretend it was dust.

He thought about his mother's face, which he could no longer see, and understood suddenly that this was part of the lesson too. He had given up the memory of her face to get here. He could not hold onto the memory of her caution as well. Some inheritances you keep. Some you spend.

"What happens," he asked finally, "if I say yes?"

"Then the stone takes your name into itself, and the border knits, and you will feel it happen. I will not lie to you: it hurts. Alderyn described it as 'being read aloud by the world.' She also said, afterward, that it was the first time in her life she had felt fully present, so perhaps the pain and the presence arrive together and cannot be separated. That seems to be how most worthwhile things work."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I show you the way home, and you hide again, and I begin teaching the rivers to swim, because they will soon need to."

Mirabel looked at his ink-stained hands. Thirty years of ledgers. Thirty years of balancing accounts for impossible things, and never once putting himself in the column of things worth accounting for.

He thought: *Corvane did arithmetic. Forty lives against six. Clean columns, tidy sums.* And he thought: *but arithmetic is just mapping with numbers, and I know what happens when you map only what's convenient. You lose the coastlines. You lose the people living on them.*

There was no version of the honest map that left him off it.

"All right," he said, and stood up, and his knees cracked in a way that felt appropriately ceremonial. "But I want it noted that I'm terrified. Put that in the stone's margins. *He was terrified, and he did it anyway.* Future cartographers should know the terms included terror."

"The stone keeps excellent marginalia," the Warden said, and Mirabel could have sworn it sounded pleased.

He stepped forward. Up close, the stone's veins were writing—he could read fragments now, in scripts from a dozen dead alphabets, and threaded among them, faded but unmistakable, a woman's hand: *Alderyn, called the Coastwalker, gives her name freely.* Below it, blank space, waiting sixty years, patient as paper.

He drew the little knife from his belt—the one he'd used to trim quills for twenty years, the most honest tool he owned—and pressed it to his palm.

And that was when the stone screamed.

Not the stone—the *border*. The veins flared white, then guttered, and far away in the direction of the veil, Mirabel felt something tear, the way you feel a stitch pop in a seam you're wearing. The sideways grass shuddered flat. The unnamed river ran backward, briefly, in alarm.

The Warden's form snapped taut, all margin and no margin, all edges. "*No,*" it said. "It is too soon. It should not be failing yet—"

"What's happening?" Mirabel's cut palm hung forgotten, dripping.

The Warden turned toward the veil, and though it had no face, Mirabel heard the horror in it plainly.

"The covenant is not waiting to be renewed," it said. "It is ending *now*. Someone on your side is pulling at the anchor. Come away from the stone, Ivo—no. No, wait. Stay. Stay *close* to the stone."

Above the meadow, where the sky should have been, the light was beginning to run like wet ink.
