## 16. The Unraveling

Mirabel felt it first as a wrongness in his teeth.

He had been sitting at the edge of the Warden's garden—a garden that grew maps the way other gardens grew beans, rows of half-remembered coastlines sprouting from dark soil—when the horizon shivered. Not moved. *Shivered*, like a dog shaking off rain. The mountains to the east, which had stood patient and purple for as long as he'd been in this country, went briefly translucent, and through them Mirabel could see the sky behind them.

"That's not supposed to happen," he said.

The Warden of Margins did not answer at once. It was busy doing what it always did at dusk: walking the boundary of its garden with a long brass stylus, touching it to anything that looked tired. A fence post. A sleeping hedgehog made of marginalia. A small hill that had been dreaming of being a mountain and had grown ambitious overnight. The Warden touched each one, murmured something in a language that sounded like paper being folded, and each thing firmed up again, satisfied.

But when it reached the eastern horizon, the stylus passed straight through.

The Warden stopped. Mirabel had learned to read its silences the way he'd once read ledgers—the pause before an entry meant trouble, the double-check of a figure meant worse. This silence went on long enough that he counted his own heartbeats against it and lost track somewhere past forty.

"Cartographer," the Warden said at last, and its voice had a new texture, like a page gone damp. "How long has it been since you left your world?"

"Nine days. Ten, maybe. Time's slippery here."

"It is not slippery." The Warden turned, and where its eyes should have been there was only the pale suggestion of eyes, an engraving of eyes, the idea of looking without the practice. "It is *thinning*. Both of them are. Your world and mine were never two worlds, Mirabel Quintana. They were one book, held open by the covenant—and someone has let go of the spine."

Mirabel stood so fast he knocked over his inkpot, which was a real loss, because ink here took weeks to make and tasted faintly of his childhood. He didn't remember why he knew that. He'd traded the memory of his mother's face to the ferryman, and with it, apparently, some smaller memories had stuck to the big one like burrs—the smell of her kitchen, the taste of the tea she drank too strong. He tried not to grieve them. He failed most days, but quietly.

"The stone," he said. "The covenant-stone. How long do we have?"

The Warden looked east again, at the mountains that were only mostly there.

"You should walk quickly," it said.

* * *

In the city of Vantrell, on the far side of the Veil, a baker named Odette Ferrun woke to find her street missing.

Not destroyed. Missing. She leaned out her window with the dawn loaf still warm under her arm and looked down at where Cobbler's Row had been all her life, and saw instead a wide blankness, the color of unwritten paper, running from the corner to the fountain. Her neighbors' houses were still there—she could see old Pem Halloway's shutters, the crack in the tanner's chimney she'd been meaning to complain about for six years—but between them, where the street itself should have been, there was nothing. Not rubble. Not fog. Nothing, in the way that a page is nothing before someone decides what happens on it.

"Hallo," she called cautiously, to the nothing.

The nothing did not answer, but it did, she thought, seem slightly embarrassed.

By noon, half of Vantrell had come to look, and the other half had come to loot, because catastrophe draws both kinds. The crown's criers were out by midday with a proclamation blaming the Veilhunt's enemies, then a second proclamation blaming the Veilhunt's targets, then a third proclamation apologizing for the confusion and asking everyone to remain calm while the crown identified further parties to blame. Odette read all three nailed to her door and found them less nourishing than her bread.

What frightened her—not the blank street, oddly, which felt more like a clerical error than a disaster, but something else—was her son.

Tammy was seven. He sat at her table eating his supper, and he was *faint*. Not sick-faint. Faint the way a watermark is faint. When he moved his hand in front of the candle, she could see the flame through his fingers.

"Mum," Tammy said, "am I in trouble?"

"No, love."

"Only Master Ellery at school says people who aren't remembered proper go thin, and nobody remembers me 'cept you and Gran, and Gran's gone a bit strange lately, keeps calling me by the cat's name."

Odette sat down across from him and took his hand, and her hand, thank every saint, stayed solid around his mostly-solid one. "I remember you," she said firmly. "I remember you were born in a thunderstorm and screamed louder than the thunder, and the midwife said she'd never heard the like, and I said *that's my boy*. I remember you ate a beetle on a dare and cried about it after, which seemed to defeat the purpose of the dare. I remember—" and here her voice caught, because remembering was suddenly work, like hauling water uphill, like the well was drying— "I remember everything, Tammy Ferrun, and you will sit there and be remembered until this nonsense is sorted."

Tammy brightened somewhat. His fingers solidified to about eighty percent.

Across Vantrell, other mothers were doing the same arithmetic, and some were finding they had less to count on. In the poor quarters, where lives were narrow and witnesses few, people who had lived quietly—who had never married, never quarreled publicly, never given anyone reason to hold them in mind—were going soft at the edges, like chalk drawings in rain. The city's priests worked feverishly, reciting parish rolls aloud in the squares, and it helped, a little, the way bailing helps a leaking boat. But the boat was leaking faster than names could be said.

And in the richest quarter, behind walls paid for by the trade in impossible things, a man sat alone with a ledger that no longer balanced.

* * *

Corvane had spent forty years believing that everything was arithmetic.

Six sold to save forty: acceptable. One guild chartered against six gifted members handed over: regrettable, but the charter fed a hundred. A kingdom's fear managed rather than cured: efficient. He had built his whole life on the conviction that mercy was simply mathematics performed by someone brave enough to look at the numbers.

Now the numbers were looking back.

His office window overlooked the harbor, and the harbor was wrong. The sea came right up to the seawall, as seas do, but where the water met the stone there was no line—no crisp border, no foam, no edge. Sea faded into wall the way one color fades into another in a cheap painting, and Corvane, who had not been near a map in twenty years (he kept cartographers at arm's length; they asked questions), recognized the effect anyway. He had seen it in unfinished work. In sketches abandoned halfway.

The world was becoming an unfinished sketch.

Behind him, the door opened without a knock, which meant Sela Voss, or meant ruin, and it proved to be both.

"You have to see the archives," she said. She was mud-splattered, wild-eyed, and holding a single ledger page the way one holds a live coal. "Corvane. The Veilhunt—it wasn't fear. It was never fear. Your partners engineered it. The panic, the registrations, the lists—they *built* the market. Every gifted person taken off the board raised the price of what remained. You weren't saving the guild. You were stocktaking."

"I know," Corvane said.

Sela stopped. Of all the responses she had rehearsed on the road—defiance, denial, a thrown inkwell—she had not rehearsed for agreement.

"You *know*?"

"I've known since the third registration." He turned back to the window. Out in the harbor, a fishing boat was becoming less certain of itself, its hull going sketchy at the waterline, its sailors shouting prayers and, in one case, inventory lists of everyone who loved them. "I told myself the arithmetic still held. Forty lives for six. Then eighty for twelve. The sums kept working, Voss. That's the horror of it. Right up until tonight, every sum worked." He laughed, a dry sound with nothing in it. "I forgot to check what we were summing."

"The covenant's failing." Sela came forward, slapping the ledger page down on his desk. "My contact in the archives says the old records show it fails within days now, not months. Coastlines first, then memory, then—well. Then whatever comes after memory. And the only man who can stop it is the one whose name is on this page, and you *sold him*."

"He isn't dead."

"He's in the unmapped realm!"

"Then he is exactly where the covenant can be renewed," Corvane said, "if he can reach the stone, and if he is willing to pay what the stone asks, and if any of us deserve it—which I grant is the weakest term in the equation." He was already moving, pulling a ring of keys from his coat, and Sela stared, because Guildmaster Corvane did not run, Corvane *calculated*, and yet here he was calculating at a dead sprint. "The map-room. There's a drawn door. If the borders are thinning, the way through may be wider than it's ever been—or gone entirely. We'll know shortly."

"Why are you helping him?" Sela demanded, following. "After everything. Why now?"

Corvane paused at the top of the stairs. For a moment the mask slipped entirely, and underneath was just an old man who had done sums in the dark for so long he'd forgotten there was a morning.

"Because I finally ran the full account," he said, "and I am owed nothing, and he is owed everything, and I would like, before the world finishes unraveling, to owe slightly less."

* * *

Mirabel walked quickly.

The Warden went with him as far as the ferryman's river, though "walked" understated it; the Warden moved the way weather moves, arriving everywhere along its path at once. The country around them was coming apart gently, which was somehow worse than violence. A forest thinned into pencil lines and then, offended, thickened back into trees, but the trees were now slightly different trees, as if the realm had redrafted them from a rough copy. A village they passed was populated entirely by people who kept glancing at their own hands to confirm them. A river ran uphill for a quarter mile, thought better of it, and lay down.

At the riverbank, the ferryman waited. He was the same as before—tall, courteous, face like a closed book—and his boat sat low in water that was no longer entirely wet.

"You again," the ferryman said. "You've already paid the fare. Memory of a mother's face. A fine price. I gave good change."

"I need to cross again. To the stone. Quickly."

"Everyone needs things quickly, these last hours." The ferryman studied him. "The fare for haste is higher."

"I have nothing left worth taking."

"Oh, I don't know." The ferryman smiled without opening the book of his face. "There's the name you're carrying. The true one. The one you haven't spoken since you were a boy. I could take that now, spare you the stone's price altogether—you'd cross comfortable, unburdened, and the realms could unravel at their leisure."

Mirabel's hand went, without thinking, to his breast pocket, where he kept the scrap of paper on which he'd written the name in his own shorthand, a cipher only he could read. *Ivo.* His mother's voice saying it. The feel of being someone small and known and safe.

"No," he said.

"No?"

"No. That name isn't mine to spend on comfort. It's the payment. The stone asks for a true name freely given, and freely means *not bought off the ferryman at a discount*." He stepped toward the boat. "So charge me what you like for speed. Take my sleep. Take my sense of direction—I've clearly misused it. Take the memory of how to whistle. But the name goes to the stone or nowhere."

For a long moment the ferryman said nothing, and the river said nothing, and the unraveling country said nothing, and Mirabel stood in the quiet with his heart going like a hammer in a bell tower.

Then the ferryman stepped aside and gestured aboard, and there was something in his posture that might, in a creature with a face, have been respect.

"Haste, then, is free," he said. "Consider it interest returned. Your mother overpaid, all those years ago. Mothers generally do."

Mirabel froze with one foot on the gunwale. "You knew her?"

"I know everyone who pays in faces." The ferryman poled off from the bank, and the boat slid onto water that was remembering, slowly, how to be water. "She crossed once. Long ago. Paid for passage with a memory she couldn't afford to lose and asked me, as change, to look after a boy who would someday come this way and be too stubborn to accept a bargain."

The riverbanks blurred past. Ahead, at the heart of the failing country, the covenant-stone rose against a sky that was fraying at the edges like old cloth—and beneath it, impossibly, absurdly, warmly familiar, a drawn doorway hung in the air, glowing gold at the seams, swinging open.

Through it, faintly, he could hear Sela Voss shouting his name, and Corvane's voice behind hers, and beneath both of them, clearer than all of it, a sound he had traded away and never stopped hearing:

*Ivo. Supper's ready, Ivo.*

The stone was waiting. The name was in his pocket. And delay, Mirabel understood, looking at the fraying sky, was finished as an option—for him, for the guild, for the baker's son going faint at his supper, for every soul in two realms who had ever hoped to be remembered properly.

"Row faster," he said.

"Cartographers," the ferryman muttered, and rowed.
