## 17. Return Through Burning Pages

The border between the realms had never been a wall. That was the first thing Alderyn's grave had taught him, and it was the thing he clung to now as the world came apart around him like a page left out in rain.

Borders were seams. And seams could be followed.

Mirabel ran through a country that was forgetting itself.

He had crossed into the unmapped lands through a doorway he'd drawn himself—a modest archway in ink, the kind of door a clerk might sketch in the margin of a ledger when the ledger grew too dull to bear. Getting in had taken an afternoon and a held breath. Getting out was proving to be another matter entirely, because the way back was closing, and it was closing the way a wound closes: reluctantly, painfully, and not quite straight.

Behind him, the covenant-stone stood dark. He had failed there—or rather, he had learned what the stone asked and had run from the asking, and the realms had noticed. The unraveling that should have taken weeks was taking hours. Coastlines in both worlds were going soft. He had felt it even here, in a land made of forgotten geography: rivers unspooling into smudges, mountains losing their conviction, whole forests thinning to pencil-suggestions of themselves.

Ahead of him, the seam home flickered like a candle in a draft.

"Come on," Mirabel panted, skidding down a hillside that kept rearranging its slope under his boots. "Come *on*."

The Warden of Margins walked beside him.

It did not run; the Warden did not seem built for running, or perhaps it considered running beneath its dignity, which was considerable. It moved the way weather moves—unhurried, inevitable—and yet somehow it was always exactly where Mirabel needed it to be, one stride ahead, one hand extended over rough ground as if smoothing it flat for him.

"You are going the wrong way," the Warden said. Its voice was the sound a map makes when you unroll it: a long dry whisper with something underneath, patient and old.

"I'm going *home*."

"That is what I said." The Warden's face—if the arrangement of shadows where its face should be could be called a face—tilted toward the flickering seam. "You are going the wrong way about it. You are trying to leave by the road you came in on. Roads close, little cartographer. Seams do not."

"The stone asked me for my name."

"It asks everyone for their name. It is a very polite stone, considering what it holds up."

"And I ran."

"Yes." The Warden let the word sit between them like a stone dropped in a well. They were crossing a plain now that had once, perhaps, been someone's memory of a coastline—the sand still remembered being sand, but the sea had been forgotten so thoroughly that only a groove remained where the tide had loved it. "I have watched cartographers come to that stone for six hundred years. Do you know how many have run?"

"How many?"

"All of them." A pause. "Once. You are the second."

Mirabel nearly tripped over a furrow that hadn't existed a moment before. "Who was the first?"

"Alderyn." The Warden said the name gently, the way one says the names of the dead. "She ran to the edge of the realm, all the way to her own drawn door, and she stood before it with her hand on the frame. And then she turned around and went back and gave the stone everything it asked, and it cost her more than it will cost you, because she went back alone, and no one steadied her hand, and no one remembered her properly afterward except me." The Warden stopped walking. "I am telling you this because I am about to do something I have not done in six hundred years, and I would prefer you understood it was not sentiment."

"What are you going to do?"

"I am going to hold the border open." The Warden raised one long arm—not toward the seam ahead, but behind them, toward the darkening heart of the unmapped lands, where the covenant-stone waited with its terrible polite question. "A border is a promise, and promises are made of attention. While I attend to it, it will stay. It will fray at the edges, because everything frays, but it will not close. You will have time."

Mirabel stared at him. "Time for what? I can't renew the covenant without—" He stopped. "The stone wants a true name freely given. I heard it. I *felt* it. I'm not ready."

"No one is ready. Alderyn wasn't ready. The first cartographer, the one whose name has been forgotten even by me, was not ready." The Warden's shadow-face turned fully toward him, and Mirabel felt seen in a way that had nothing to do with eyes. "But hear me, Mirabel Quintana. The stone does not want your name because names are precious. It wants your name because a covenant written anonymously is a covenant no one must keep. Your guildmaster understands this better than you do. Every lie he has told, every name he has sold, rests on the same foundation: that a man who cannot be named cannot be blamed. The Veil you hide behind and the Veil your enemies hunt you through are the same veil. The stone offers to burn it. Burning hurts."

Somewhere far off, a mountain sat down. Not collapsed—sat, wearily, like an old dog lowering itself onto a cool floor, and the ground shuddered with the relief of it.

"But not today," the Warden continued. "Today you go back, because there is a woman in your world who stole a ledger page for you, and a guild full of people who will be blamed for what Corvane did, and a room full of maps that are the only other anchors either realm has left. Today you go back and you fight for the time to answer the stone properly. And I—" the Warden's voice did something Mirabel had never heard it do; it softened, "—I will hold the door."

"Hold it how? For how long?"

"For as long as I last." The Warden smiled, or performed the shape of a smile, and it was somehow the kindest thing Mirabel had ever seen. "I am older than the covenant, little cartographer. I was here when the margins were all there was. But I am made of edges, and edges are the first thing the unraveling eats. When it reaches me, I will spend myself holding the seam, and I will not be sorry, and neither should you be. This is not sacrifice. This is arithmetic. One old warden against two worlds—I like my odds."

"That's not arithmetic, that's—"

"Go." The Warden pressed one long finger against Mirabel's sternum, and the touch went through him like cold water through cloth, finding every place he had ever hidden anything. "Your friend is in danger. Your enemy is burning the evidence. And somewhere in your pocket there is ink that remembers who you are. Run, Mirabel Quintana. Run the wrong way beautifully."

And the seam ahead tore open like a letter being opened by impatient hands, and Mirabel ran through.

* * *

Falling through a border, it turns out, feels like being read aloud.

That was Mirabel's only description for it afterward, and even that was inadequate. There was a sensation of being *pronounced*—of every syllable of himself being sounded out carefully by something vast and patient—and then the smell hit him, and the smell was smoke, and he knew where he was before he opened his eyes.

He opened them anyway.

The guild's map-room was burning.

Not all of it. Not yet. Smoke curled along the ceiling in fat grey ropes, and one of the great shelving units along the eastern wall was alight, maps curling at their edges like leaves in autumn, and three men Mirabel didn't recognize were feeding the fire with armfuls of atlases while a fourth stood at the door checking names off a list. But the heart of the room—the great table where impossible trades were balanced, the ledgers, the brass instruments, the tall windows that caught the morning light just so—all of it still stood, choked in haze, waiting.

And at the center of it, calm as a man ordering dinner, stood Guildmaster Corvane.

"Burn the west shelves last," Corvane was saying. His voice carried the way it always carried, reasonable and low, the voice of a man doing arithmetic rather than arson. "The trade ledgers are indexed there. We'll want the indices intact until the charter transfer is finalized. Then everything."

Mirabel crouched behind the ruins of the drawn door—the archway he'd sketched months ago, now scorched and sagging, its ink blistered—and tried to think.

His body had decided things already. His hands were shaking. His lungs hurt. Somewhere between realms he had lost his coat and gained a fine layer of ash that made him look, he suspected, like a chimney sweep's ghost. The sensible plan—the plan any sensible person would adopt—was to wait for nightfall, slip out through the cellars, find Sela, and return with help.

But the fire was eating the maps.

And not just any maps. He could see it now, through the smoke: the western shelves held the guild's collection of *living* charts—the ones drawn by gifted hands, the ones that breathed. Each one was an anchor. Each one was a thread in the seam the Warden was holding open with its fading body. Corvane didn't know that, Mirabel realized. Or worse—Corvane knew exactly that, and burning them was not destruction but consolidation, clearing away every rival anchor so that whatever bargain he'd struck with the crown's trade partners would be the only game left in town.

Either way, the maps were dying while he hid.

"Oh, you absolute fool," Mirabel whispered—to himself, though a passing tendril of smoke might reasonably have taken it personally. "You spent two chapters' worth of courage getting home and now you're going to hide behind a doorframe."

He stood up.

The effect was, he would reflect later, less dramatic than he'd hoped. No one noticed him at first. The fire-crackle covered his footsteps, and the smoke lay between him and the door-checker like a curtain. He made it four steps across the map-room floor—an eternity, each one loud as a drumbeat to his own ears—before the man at the door looked up.

"There's someone—"

"I see him," Corvane said.

He didn't sound surprised. That was the worst part. Corvane turned from the fire with the expression of a man who has found a coin he'd written off as lost, and his eyes went over Mirabel—ash-grey, coatless, half-feral from the unmapped lands—and something almost like fondness crossed his face.

"Quintana." He said it the way he said everything: quietly, precisely, as though filing it. "We had you down as dead. The wardens reported the map-room breach closed three days ago. I confess I signed the condolence letter myself. Very moving. Sela Voss cried, if you're interested."

"Where is she?"

"Gone. Fled after the raid, with a page she had no right to. There's a price on her head that would make your eyes water." Corvane stepped closer, and behind him the fire climbed another shelf, and a chart of the Summer Isles—drawn by a gifted girl they'd all called Pip, taken in the Night of Long Knives—curled and blackened and was gone. "Which raises an interesting question. How does a dead man walk out of a burning room? Because I've read your file, Quintana. I wrote half of it. And nothing in it explains you."

Mirabel's hand found his pocket. Inside it: the stub of ink-horn he'd carried across the border, and the folded scrap of his mother's map—the one drawing he'd saved from the purge, the one that had called his name through a drawn door. The ink inside was warm. Warmer than it should be. Warm the way a hand is warm.

"You sold us," Mirabel said. "Six names. Forty lives saved, you'll say. I've done the arithmetic too, Corvane. I lived in your ledgers long enough to learn the sums."

Something flickered in the guildmaster's face—there and gone, quick as a fish turning. "You don't know what it cost."

"No," Mirabel agreed. "But I know what it bought. I've seen the crown archives' side of your trade. Well—not me. Someone who loves this guild more than you do has seen it." He took a step forward, and to his own astonishment his voice didn't shake. "The Veilhunt isn't fear, Corvane. It's market-cornering. Your partners engineered the whole proclamation. You didn't save forty lives. You sold six and mortgaged the rest."

For a moment—one long, suspended moment, while the fire roared and the smoke thickened and somewhere beyond the walls the city itself was going soft at the edges—Corvane simply looked at him.

Then the guildmaster sighed, and nodded to the men at the door, and said, "Take him."

They were big men. They were also, Mirabel noted with the strange clarity that terror lends, standing directly in front of the western shelves—the living charts, the anchors, the last threads. And the ink in his pocket was burning hot now, and the Warden's words were in his ears—*run the wrong way beautifully*—and Mirabel Quintana, who had spent eleven years being invisible, who had balanced accounts for impossible things and hidden his gift behind other men's numbers, reached into his pocket, uncorked the ink-horn, and poured it out across the floor.

The ink did what his ink had begun doing when the covenant started to fail: it bled color into the world.

Only this time, Mirabel directed it.

He had crossed into a country stitched from forgotten geography. He had drunk from unmapped rivers and traded a memory to their ferryman. He had stood at a covenant-stone and heard it ask his name. And all of that was in him now, soaked into his hands, and when the ink spread across the map-room floor it did not make puddles—it made *places*. A coastline unfurled between Mirabel and the advancing men, sudden and salt-smelling, waves breaking in miniature against shelving legs. A forest sprang up in the aisle between ledgers, saplings whispering up through floorboards. The big men stopped. Even brave men hesitate at a beach where no beach was, especially when the beach appears to have opinions.

"Don't hurt them," Mirabel said—to the ink, to the places, to whatever of the unmapped lands had followed him home and was pouring out of his pen-hand. To his mild surprise, they listened. The forest merely tangled the men gently, the way brambles tangle a stray sheep. "I mean it. None of them. Not even him."

"Sentimental," Corvane observed. But he had gone pale, and his eyes kept darting to the burning shelves, and Mirabel understood suddenly that the guildmaster was afraid—not of Mirabel, but of the *arithmetic changing*. Of forty becoming thirty-nine. Of the neat, terrible sum coming undone.

"Give it up, Corvane." Mirabel walked forward along his own coastline, waves lapping at his heels. Behind him, through the torn seam that only he could see, he felt the Warden holding—fading, but holding, a hand pressed flat against the border of two worlds. He didn't have much time. Neither did the room. "The fire won't fix this. Burning the maps won't fix this. The realms are coming apart, and the only thing that stitches them is a name freely given, and I am *this close* to giving mine, and when I do, every lie in your ledgers comes apart with the Veil. Help me, and maybe the guild survives what's coming. Fight me, and I promise you—" he gestured at the burning shelves, at the blackening charts, at the smoke-shrouded heart of the only home he'd ever had, "—you'll watch it all come down twice. Once here. Once everywhere."

Corvane looked at him for a long moment.

And then, with the terrible calm of a man who has always believed the ledger balances in the end, the guildmaster reached into his coat and drew out not a weapon but a key—a small iron key that Mirabel recognized with a lurch, because it belonged to the vault beneath the map-room, the vault where the guild kept the one thing it had never shown its clerks.

The covenant-stone's anchor. The piece of the other realm that the first cartographer had carried back, six hundred years ago, so that the two worlds would always have a handhold on each other.

"You're right about one thing, Quintana," Corvane said, backing toward the vault stair. "Everything is coming down. The only question is what's still standing when the dust settles—and I have spent thirty years making sure it will be the guild. Whatever it costs." He turned the key over in his fingers, and above him the ceiling beams groaned, and the fire found the last of the living charts. "Even the guild from *me*."

And he went down the stairs, taking the anchor—and the last chance of two worlds—with him.
