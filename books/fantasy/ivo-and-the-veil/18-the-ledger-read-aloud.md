## 18. The Ledger Read Aloud

*Mirabel*

The map-room smelled of smoke that hadn't happened yet.

That was the thing about a room Corvane had ordered burned and then un-ordered burned—twice in one hour, his nerve failing him both times—the thing still carried the anticipation of its own ending. Char had been dragged to the corners in buckets, ready for the lighting. The great tables where I had spent eleven years balancing accounts of impossible trades stood bare for the first time since I'd known them, every ledger removed on Corvane's instruction, as if emptying the room of paper might empty it of evidence.

It had emptied it of everything except us.

Perhaps forty people remained of what had once been two hundred. Clerks, binders, an apprentice ink-grinder named Pem who couldn't have been more than sixteen and who kept looking at the door as though the Veilwardens might come through it politely, by appointment. They stood in loose, frightened clusters, and above them, on the dais where the guildmaster's desk had always sat like a small throne, Corvane waited with his hands folded and his face arranged into the expression he wore for funerals and audits: grave, reasonable, sorry about all this.

"The guild survives," he was saying, in the warm gravel voice that had talked three crowns and two revolutions out of closing us down. "The guild has *always* survived. That is its purpose. Not any one of us. The whole."

I stood at the back with Sela's shoulder pressed against mine, and inside my coat, against my ribs, I could feel the archivist's papers like a second heartbeat.

"You're shaking," Sela murmured.

"I'm deciding," I said.

"Deciding what?"

"Whether I'm brave." I swallowed. "I'll let you know when it's over."

The doors opened without anyone knocking, which is how you know it's the crown and not a client. Six Veilwardens came in first, grey-cloaked, their registration-staves humming that thin wasp-note I'd heard through walls my whole life and never stopped fearing. Behind them came Warden-Captain Hessa Vane, whom I recognized from the proclamation portraits—a broad, tired woman whose face suggested she had signed more death orders than she could carry comfortably, and knew it.

"Corvane," she said. "You sent word of sedition in your house."

"I sent word of *theft*," Corvane said smoothly. "A clerk stole guild property. She's here now, as it happens. And—" his eyes found me, and there it was, the arithmetic behind them, cold and clean as double-entry bookkeeping "—I believe you're looking for a cartographer."

Every head turned toward me. Forty faces, and in most of them—not all, but most—I saw something worse than fear. I saw relief. *Not me. Not me. Let it be him.*

Sela stepped forward before I could move, which was typical of her, and held up the stolen page so the light caught it.

"No," she said. "First, we read."

*Sela*

My hands were steady. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only in my own head: my hands did not shake. Everything else shook—my knees, my voice on the first word, my entire understanding of what I was willing to lose—but the hands held the page flat, because a page deserves to be read properly, and because if I let them tremble even once I think I would have stopped.

"This is the guild's private ledger," I said. "The real one. Not the one we show inspectors. It records trades. And the guild, as you all know, trades in impossible things." I looked at Corvane. He watched me with the patience of a man watching weather. "What you may not know is what else it has traded."

"Mistress Voss," Corvane said gently, "is confused and frightened—"

"*Hedwin Marlowe,*" I read, loud enough to hit the far wall. "*Gift: finds lost roads. Sold to the Crown, ninth of Thawmonth. Price: renewal of charter.*"

The name went through the room like a dropped glass. Hedwin Marlowe had repaired the guild's roof every spring for twenty years. He'd been taken in the Night of Long Knives. Some of these people had hidden under their beds while it happened; some had simply stayed in their rooms, listening, the way I had listened, memorizing boots.

"Hedwin?" said old Mistress Callow, the paper-maker, in a voice like something tearing.

"*Anselm Bright,*" I read. "*Gift: speaks with rivers. Sold, eleventh of Thawmonth. Price: exemption for guildmaster's household.*" My throat closed and I made myself open it. "*Tamsin Oduya. Gift: remembers rooms that no longer exist. Sold. Ferren Quill. Gift: mends broken maps. Sold. Odile Vance. Gift:—*"

"Stop." Corvane's voice had changed. The warmth was gone out of it, and what remained was worse, because it was honest. "Stop, Sela. You don't understand what you're holding."

"I understand it perfectly," I said. "It's a bill of sale. We're the goods."

"You were never meant to see it. None of you were. That was a mercy." He came down off the dais slowly, hands still folded, a man approaching a fire he intended to talk out rather than put out. "Six names. Six. Do you know how many gifted people the Veilhunt took in this city last season? Three hundred and forty. I bought the safety of two hundred with six, and I would do it again, and I will *not* apologize for arithmetic."

"Then don't apologize," said a new voice. Mirabel stepped out beside me, and I felt the room tilt toward him the way a compass needle tilts—reluctantly, and then all at once. "Do the math instead. Out loud. In front of everyone."

*Mirabel*

Here is what nobody tells you about telling the truth: it is much easier than lying, and infinitely harder. Lying, I had done daily for eleven years, in ledgers and hallways and the careful blankness of my face. Truth required only that I stand still and open my coat.

I drew out the archivist's papers. Three documents, copied in the crown's own archive hand, each bearing seals that even the Veilwardens snapped upright to look at.

"These are requisitions," I said. "Dated *before* the Veilhunt was proclaimed. Before the first arrest. This one authorizes the purchase of confiscated 'sight-assets' at fixed rates—by the Meridian Trading Combine. This one is a correspondence between the Combine's factor and—" I turned it around so the seal faced the room "—a signatory of the Guild of Impossible Things."

"That proves nothing," Corvane said. "Signatures can be forged."

"They can," I agreed. "But look at the dates, Corvane. Look at them the way you taught me to look at ledgers. The hunt wasn't proclaimed until the fourth of Seedmonth. These purchases were contracted in the winter. Someone built the market before they built the fear. The Veilhunt isn't protecting the realm from the sighted. It's a *harvest*. You didn't save two hundred lives with six. You supplied a warehouse."

The silence afterward had texture. I could feel it pressing on forty pairs of ears.

Warden-Captain Vane had not moved. She stood very still among her staves, and I watched her read the papers upside down across the distance, because captains learn to read anything upside down, and I saw the moment the dates landed on her. Her jaw tightened. Not denial. Recalculation.

"Warden," Corvane said, and his voice found its warmth again, aimed now like a tool. "This man is a registered fugitive. A cartographer of the sighted kind. Whatever papers he waves, your duty is clear."

"It is," Vane said.

And she looked at me. And I understood, in that long moment, that her duty *was* clear—that it had been clear for years, maybe, and heavy, and that she had been carrying it the way you carry a sack of wet sand, waiting for someone to give her permission to set it down.

So I gave her the truth instead.

"Sela," I said. "Read the last page."

*Sela*

There was one name left. I had read six aloud already, and my voice had gone hoarse doing it, and the room had changed around me—Mistress Callow weeping openly, young Pem standing with his fists clenched, two clerks who had been Corvane's men all year quietly moving away from him like tide going out.

The seventh name was the reason I had stolen the page at all. But I hesitated, because reading it aloud in front of Veilwardens was different from finding it at midnight. Finding it was a choice between Mirabel and myself. Reading it was a choice between Mirabel and everything.

He touched my elbow. Just that. Just *here*.

"Mirabel Quintana," I read. "Cartographer. Gift: charts realms that exist only in ink and memory. Status: unsent. Note in the guildmaster's hand: *hold until leverage requires.*"

"Leverage," Mirabel repeated softly. He wasn't looking at the wardens. He was looking at Corvane, and there was no hatred in his face, which somehow made it unbearable. "Eleven years, Corvane. You taught me to balance books. You kept my name at the bottom of one the whole time. Not sold—*held*. Like coin you might need later."

"You were safe," Corvane said. "As long as you were useful, you were safe. Ask the other six how safe being useless kept them."

"Ask them," Mirabel said. "Go on. Ask them anything."

And that was when Captain Vane finally moved. She crossed the floor to me, took the ledger page from my hands—gently, I'll say that, gently—and read it herself, top to bottom, while her wardens watched her the way sailors watch a compass in a storm.

Then she turned to Corvane.

"Captain," Corvane began, "your charter—"

"My charter," Vane said, "was issued by a crown that has been selling the same people it ordered me to hunt. I've executed nine people under that proclamation." Her voice didn't rise at all. That was the terrible part. It stayed level as a ruled line. "Nine. And I wrote to the archive after the third one, asking for the legal basis of the seizures, and I was told it was above my rank. It was above my rank because it was *inventory*."

She handed the page back to me. To me, not to him.

"Master Quintana," she said. "You're under crown protection, effective now. All of you are." She raised her voice slightly, enough to reach the wardens at the doors. "Staves down."

They wavered—you could see it, the hum of those instruments stuttering like insects in cold weather—and then, one by one, they lowered them. Not because they loved us. Because loyalty to an institution only holds while the institution holds still, and theirs had just been shown to be moving underneath them, quick as water.

Corvane stood alone on the floor of his own map-room, and I watched him understand that the ledger had balanced at last, and that he was the entry marked *loss*.

"You'll ruin it," he said to Mirabel, and his voice cracked down the middle, the warmth gone entirely now, leaving only an old tired man. "The guild. The realms. You'll ruin everything trying to save everyone, and there won't be anything left to save."

"Maybe," Mirabel said. "But I'd rather ruin it honestly than preserve it as a lie. You taught me that too, actually. Entry one, day one: *a ledger that lies protects nothing.*"

"That was about money."

"Everything is about money," Mirabel said, "until it isn't."

*Mirabel*

Afterward—after the wardens had escorted Corvane out past forty people who would not look at him, after Sela had sat down abruptly on a stool and discovered her hands were shaking now, all the trembling arriving at once like a delayed invoice—I stood in the middle of the empty map-room and felt the papers against my ribs go quiet.

It wasn't over. I knew that the way you know weather coming in an old scar. Somewhere beneath the city, beneath the world, the covenant-stone was waiting, and it wanted a name freely given, and I had just spent my last hour of anonymity in front of the crown itself.

Sela appeared at my shoulder, red-eyed, holding the ledger page rolled like a baton.

"Well," she said. "You're famous."

"Dreadful."

"Terrible. There's only one cure." She nudged me toward the drawn doorway still faintly glowing on the far wall—my doorway, the one that opened onto the unmapped country, where a stone waited with my name in its mouth. "Go finish it. And Mirabel?"

"Yes?"

"When you sign it—sign it neatly. I've seen your handwriting. If you're going to bind two worlds together, the least you can do is make it legible."

I laughed, which surprised us both, and stepped toward the light.

Behind me, the doors banged open again, and a runner in guild colors skidded into the room, white-faced, shouting words that froze the blood in the room's warm air:

*"The river—the harbor—the streets—they're fading—it's starting—"*

The covenant wasn't waiting anymore.
