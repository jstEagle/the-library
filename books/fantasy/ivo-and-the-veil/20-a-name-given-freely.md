## 20. A Name Given Freely

The covenant-stone did not look like the end of anything.

It looked like a doorstop. That was Mirabel's first honest thought as he stood before it in the hollow beneath the world's oldest hill, and he almost laughed—a thin, cracked sound that came out more like a cough. After everything: the ferryman's gray river, the memory of his mother's face traded away like a coin bitten for gold, the burning pages of the guild's map-room still smoking somewhere behind him in another reality entirely—after all of that, the stone that held two realms together was a squat, lichen-spotted boulder no bigger than a millstone, sitting in a bowl of moss like an egg in a nest.

"It's smaller than I imagined," he said.

"Everything true is," said the Warden of Margins.

The Warden stood at the edge of the hollow, where the moss gave way to a country stitched from forgotten geography—coastlines that had slipped out of sailors' memories, rivers whose names had been forgotten by everyone who'd ever drunk from them. The Warden had been older than the covenant when the covenant was young, and it showed: their outline kept shifting, the way a word does when you stare at it too long. Sometimes they were tall as a heron. Sometimes they were barely taller than Mirabel, and bent, and kind.

Right now they were bent and kind, which Mirabel had learned to read as *worried*.

"Alderyn stood here," the Warden said. "Three hundred years ago. She stood exactly where you're standing, and she laughed too."

"I wasn't laughing."

"You were about to. It's how cartographers cope. You people are worse than priests." The Warden drifted closer, and the moss dimpled under them without being crushed. "She said the same thing. *It's smaller than I imagined.* And then she asked me if it would hurt."

Mirabel looked down at his hands. They were steady, which surprised him. He had expected them to shake. He had spent his whole life keeping them steady over ledgers and inkwells while his heart hammered out secrets, so perhaps steadiness was simply what his hands did now, whether he wanted them to or not.

"And you told her?"

"I told her the truth. That it would hurt more than anything she had ever felt, and less than she feared, and that afterward she would not be able to imagine having chosen otherwise." The Warden paused. "I have never decided whether that was a kindness or a cruelty."

"Both," Mirabel said. "Most true things are both. You taught me that."

"I taught you nothing. I showed you a grave and let you do the teaching yourself. That's the only method that works on your kind."

*Your kind.* Cartographers. The gifted. The ones who could see the seams of the world and, worse, draw them. The ones the crown was hunting through the streets of Mirabel's city even now, even tonight, with lists supplied by Corvane's careful arithmetic. He thought of Sela—Sela, who had stolen a page and run into the dark with it, who was somewhere on the other side of the Veil right now carrying proof that might save forty lives or get her hanged, depending on whether truth moved faster than fear. He thought of the half-empty desks in the guild hall. Of Tomas, taken in the Night of Long Knives, who used to whistle while he filed. Of the client who paid in ash.

He thought of his mother's face, which was not a face anymore but a warm shape, a voice, a smell of bread and rain—the ferryman had left him the love and taken the picture, and somehow that was both mercy and theft, and he was done pretending he could sort out which.

"All right," he said. "Tell me again. All of it. Slowly. I want to know exactly what I'm agreeing to."

The Warden settled beside the stone, folding themselves down like a tent coming off its pole, until they were nearly Mirabel's height and their eyes—both of them, all four, however many there were this hour—were level with his.

"The covenant is a name," they said. "That is all it has ever been. Not a spell, not a chain. A name, given freely, written in blood and ink, witnessed by both realms. Your realm agreed to be mapped. My realm agreed to be remembered. The name is the hinge. While it holds, the borders hold. When Alderyn gave hers, the realms knitted together like a wound closing."

"And when she was betrayed—"

"When her own guild sold her name to men who wanted the border *thin* rather than *strong*—because a thin border leaks, and leaks are profitable if you control the channels—" the Warden's outline flickered, sharp-edged for a moment, all heron, all talon, "—the covenant began to fail. Not all at once. Names are patient things. But failing. Coastlines forgetting themselves. Streets going soft at the edges. People fading where they were never truly remembered." They softened again. "You've seen it."

"I've drawn it," Mirabel said quietly. "Ink bleeding where it shouldn't. A door opening that I didn't draw. Something calling my name." He swallowed. "Calling *Ivo*."

The name sat in the air between them. His mother's name for him. The one he had buried under *Mirabel* the way you bury a seed and then pretend you don't remember what you planted, because remembering means hoping, and hoping means something can be taken from you.

"That is the second thing you must understand," the Warden said. "A true name freely given cannot be a borrowed one. *Mirabel Quintana* is a fine name. It is the name of the clerk, the ledger-keeper, the man who hid. It will not serve. The stone asks for the name that was yours before you learned to hide. The name your mother gave you."

"Ivo."

"Yes."

"And once it's spoken—once it's *written*—"

"Every realm will know it. Every warden, every archivist, every clerk in every guild, every hunter with a list. There is no taking it back, Ivo. There is no drawing a curtain over it. You will spend the rest of your life being findable." The Warden let that sit. "You have spent your whole life being unfindable. I am told it was comfortable. In the way that a cellar is comfortable."

Mirabel laughed then, properly, and it hurt his ribs, which he took as a good sign—it meant he still had ribs, still had a body, still had things that could hurt.

"A cellar," he said. "Yes. With very good lighting. I kept it tidy. I balanced accounts for impossible trades and I told myself it was enough, telling myself it was enough." He crouched by the stone. Up close, the lichen resolved into letters—not any alphabet he knew, but the kind of writing that maps make when they write themselves, the script of coastlines and riverbeds. Some of the letters had gone pale. Faded. One whole line of them had crumbled to green dust.

"How long?" he asked.

"If you do nothing? A season. Perhaps two. Then the borders go, and both realms unravel like wet paper." The Warden tilted their shifting head. "You could still walk away. I want that said plainly, so that no one—least of all you—can pretend you weren't offered the door. Walk away, live hidden, die old, and let the worlds come apart behind you. Many would. Most would. I would not think less of you, and I am very old, and my thinking less of people has historically carried some weight."

"That's the gentlest threat I've ever received."

"It isn't a threat. It's arithmetic. Corvane does arithmetic too, you know. Forty lives against six. Comfort against catastrophe. He believes himself kind." The Warden's voice went quiet. "The difference between you and Corvane is not courage, Ivo. Courage is cheap; even cowards spend it once. The difference is that he counts lives, and you count *worlds*, and you have never once counted yourself among the costs worth saving."

Mirabel looked at the faded letters a long moment.

"Well," he said. "Someone has to be bad at arithmetic around here. It might as well be the cartographer."

* * *

There was preparation. Of course there was; there is always preparation, and it is always the same shape: small rituals to keep the hands busy while the heart catches up.

The Warden brought water from a river that had been forgotten by its own source—a river that existed now only because the unmapped realm remembered it, stubbornly, out of spite or love. Mirabel washed his hands in it. It was cold in the way that things are cold when they have been waiting a long time.

He took out his kit. The leather case was scorched along one edge—he'd carried it through burning pages to get here, and it bore the record—and inside, his pens were intact, every one. The good nib. The stubby one he chewed. The pen Sela had given him three years ago, saying, *Every cartographer needs a pen he didn't buy himself, or he starts thinking the world belongs to whoever paid for the ink.*

"Sela chose well," the Warden observed, watching him lay the pens out on the moss.

"She chooses everything well. It's infuriating." He lined up the ink pots. "If I don't come back through—"

"You'll come back through."

"—if I *don't*," he insisted, "she keeps the ledger. The new one. The honest one. No names sold, no names hidden. Everything written down where anyone can read it. She'll hate it. She'll do it perfectly."

The Warden said nothing, but the moss grew a small ring of white flowers around them, which Mirabel had learned was either agreement or amusement, and with the Warden it was usually both.

Then there was nothing left to prepare, and the hollow was very quiet, and the stone waited with the patience of a thing that had waited three hundred years and could wait three hundred more but would really rather not.

"How does it work?" Mirabel asked. "Mechanically. I'm a professional. I want the procedure."

"You cut your palm. You speak your name. You write it on the stone—in blood first, then ink over it, because blood is the promise and ink is the *record*, and a covenant needs both. The realms will take the name into themselves. It will hurt." The Warden met his eyes. "I will not lie to you about the hurt. It is the pain of becoming visible. Imagine every wall you ever hid behind, removed all at once, in daylight, while the whole world looks."

"Wonderful."

"It is also," the Warden added, "the last pain you will feel alone. Afterward, you are part of the hinge. Both realms hold you. Nothing in either world will be able to say it does not know your name." A pause, and something almost like a smile moved through their shifting features. "Including, I suspect, a certain clerk who is at this very moment doing something reckless on your behalf."

"You can see that?"

"I can see the shape of loyalty across a border. It's very distinctive. It burns."

Mirabel breathed out. Drew the good nib from its slot—one last time, for the old life—and set it aside. He took out the knife he'd carried since the map-room, the one Sela had pressed into his hand in the dark, saying *go, go, I'll hold the door.*

"Hold the door," he murmured. "All right."

He opened his palm.

* * *

Pain, the Warden had said, would come later. What came first was stranger: silence. The hollow went utterly quiet—not empty quiet but *attentive* quiet, the quiet of a room when someone important enters. The moss stopped growing. The forgotten river held its breath somewhere out beyond the hill. Even the Warden went still, and the Warden was never still; their stillness was like a held chord.

Mirabel pressed his bleeding palm to the stone.

"Ivo," he said.

His voice sounded small in the hollow. Small and clear, the way a bell is small and clear.

"My name is Ivo. Ivo Quintana—Ivo Marchetti, after the father I never met, though my mother said the name suited me better than it ever suited him. My mother gave me Ivo. She said it meant yew-tree, in the old tongue. She said yews live in churchyards and outlive everything that plants them." His throat tightened. He couldn't remember her face. He could remember her words, and it turned out words were enough; it turned out the ferryman had known that, and left him the load-bearing walls. "She named me for something that grows slowly and holds the ground. I spent thirty years trying to be anything else. I hid. I hid so well I nearly vanished, and I called the vanishing safety, and I called the hiding humility, and none of it was true. This is true:"

He dipped the pen. Ink over blood, the Warden's voice murmuring in his memory: *blood is the promise, ink is the record.*

"This is true: I am a cartographer. I chart realms that exist in ink and memory. I see the seams of the world, and I am done apologizing for it. I give my name to both realms, freely, openly, to be known by anyone who cares to know it. Map me. Remember me. Here is the hand that draws; here is the name it signs."

And he wrote.

*Ivo.*

The moment the last stroke landed, the world broke open.

Not violently—that was the terrible part. It broke open gently, the way dawn breaks, the way a fever breaks, all the held pressure releasing at once into light. The stone drank the ink. The faded letters refilled like veins taking blood. And then the covenant came up through the ground and into *him*, and Mirabel understood, finally, why Alderyn had asked if it would hurt, and why the answer had needed three hundred years of careful phrasing.

It was every wall falling at once. It was the cellar doors blown wide in daylight. It was thirty years of hiding ending in a single unendurable instant of being *seen*—by the stone, by the hill, by the forgotten river, by the far-off city where a girl with a stolen ledger page was running through the dark, by the crown's wardens, by Corvane, by his mother, wherever mothers go, by both entire realms at once, all of them looking, all of them *knowing his name*—

His knees went. The hollow tilted. Somewhere very far away he heard himself making a sound he would later deny making.

And then—steadiness.

A hand closed over his own. Not flesh, exactly; more like the memory of a hand, the idea of one, cool and certain, guiding the pen through the final flourish he hadn't known the covenant required. The Warden's lingering touch, older than the covenant, holding his grip steady the way you steady a child learning letters.

"*Yew-tree,*" the Warden said softly, reading the name as it sank into the stone. "They plant them over graves, you know. And they outlive the graves. And the churches. And the towns." The hand tightened, just slightly. "Well chosen, whoever she was. Hold the line, Ivo. Almost done. Let it finish."

He held. The light poured through him and out through the stone and away along lines he suddenly *saw*—covenant-lines, border-seams, running out under the hills and across the Veil and into the other realm, knitting, closing, a wound drawing shut after three hundred years of bleeding. He saw coastlines remember themselves. He saw a street in his own city firm up at its soft edges. He saw, impossibly, briefly, a candlelit cellar where a woman with ink-stained fingers looked up from a ledger as if someone had spoken her name, and smiled, and he knew—even across a realm, even through pain—that she'd felt it. That she knew he'd done it. That she was already, furiously, lovingly, going to tell him exactly what she thought of him doing it without her.

The light crested. Broke. Began, gently, to recede.

When it was gone, Mirabel was lying on the moss with his palm bandaged in strips of the Warden's own shifting substance—which smelled, oddly, of cedar and old libraries—and the covenant-stone was blazing softly with fresh letters, and the first letter of the newest line was unmistakably, gloriously, an *I*.

"There," the Warden said. Their outline had settled—settled, for the first time since Mirabel had known them, into a single fixed shape: tall, bent, kind, finished. As if the covenant's renewal had let them rest. "Done. Witnessed. Bound." They looked down at him, and their eyes were wet, which Mirabel had not known was possible, and which he generously decided never to mention. "You are the most findable man in two worlds, Ivo Quintana. How does it feel?"

Mirabel lay on the moss and breathed, and hurt, and lived.

"Like a cellar door," he said, "opening onto a street. On market day. With everyone I've ever wronged and everyone I've ever loved standing on it." He started laughing, and couldn't stop, and found he didn't want to. "Terrifying. Absolutely terrifying. Someone should warn them I'm coming."

"Too late," said the Warden, and pointed one long finger at the hollow's entrance, where the air had begun—gently, patiently, in the manner of all newly mended things—to open like a page.

Through it, faint but unmistakable, drifting across the border on the smell of smoke and rain: voices. Many voices. And one of them, rising above the rest, furious and relieved and absolutely certain of itself, calling a name that was newly, officially, irrevocably his.

*"IVO!"*

"Oh no," said Mirabel, and got up.
