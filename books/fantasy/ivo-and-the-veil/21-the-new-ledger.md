## 21. The New Ledger

The charter arrived on a Tuesday, which Sela Voss considered fitting, because Tuesdays had always been the guild's day for paperwork and small humiliations, and this was both.

It came by courier—a nervous boy of perhaps fifteen who stood in the doorway of the half-ruined guildhouse holding the document at arm's length as though it might bite him. Given everything the crown's seals had done to people she loved, Sela could hardly blame him for the caution.

"Delivery for the Guild of Impossible Things," he said. "Reopened."

"That's us," Sela said. "Or it will be, once we've swept up."

She signed for it with her new title inked beneath her name: *Keeper of Truths*. The boy read it upside down, frowned, and asked if that meant she was a librarian.

"It means," Sela said, "that I am the person you lie to last."

He left quickly after that.

* * *

The guildhouse had been burned only in part—Corvane's order to torch the map-room had been countermanded by Mirabel's arrival through its burning pages, and by the time the wardens understood what they were looking at, no one much felt like setting fires anymore. So the east wing still stood, smelling of smoke and old vellum, its windows patched with sailcloth until glaziers could be found who were willing to work in a building that had recently made the crown's own soldiers weep.

Sela had taken the corner office that used to belong to the Registrar of Debts. She'd chosen it deliberately. The Registrar of Debts had been the one who entered the sold names into the ledger, in his tidy hand, with his tidy columns, and Sela wanted to work every day in the room where that kind of tidiness had lived, so she would never mistake tidiness for innocence again.

The charter itself was short. That surprised her. She had expected parchment dense as a hedge, clauses within clauses, the usual legal topiary. Instead there were three provisions, written large:

*No name given to this Guild shall be sold, traded, bartered, or disclosed without the free consent of its owner.*

*Every gift registered with this Guild is registered for protection. Registration confers shelter, not scrutiny.*

*The Keeper of Truths shall hold the ledgers of the past open to any who ask, in perpetuity, that the Guild may never again mistake survival for virtue.*

Beneath them, two signatures. The Queen's—which everyone had expected—and Mirabel Quintana's, which had caused a small riot among the clerks when they first saw it, because Mirabel Quintana had spent eleven years signing things as *M. Q., Clerk Third Class*, in handwriting so deliberately forgettable that three separate auditors had once concluded he did not exist.

Now his name sprawled across the charter like something let out of a cage.

Sela pinned the charter above her desk, where the Registrar of Debts' portrait had hung. She had burned the portrait. It had seemed ceremonial and correct at the time, though afterward she'd felt vaguely guilty about it, the way one feels about kicking a chair.

* * *

The first week of reopening, they registered forty-one gifts.

This was more than anyone expected. Sela had braced herself for empty benches and suspicious silence—for a city that had watched half the guild's staff marched away in chains and might reasonably conclude that the safest thing to do with an impossible talent was to bury it in the garden and say nothing.

Instead they came in ones and twos, then in fives.

A washerwoman whose hands could find lost things—keys, rings, children—in anything. A night-soil collector who dreamed tomorrow's weather accurately enough that the fishwives paid him a copper a week for forecasts. A boy of nine who could walk into any drawing and come back out again, provided someone held his coat, which his mother did, every time, standing at the edge of the picture with her arms crossed like a woman waiting out a tantrum.

Sela took each name down in the new ledger—the honest one, bound in undyed leather, its pages unruled because rules implied limits and they were all rather tired of limits. Beside each name she wrote what the gift was, and what the gift needed, and what the gift feared. The third column had been Mirabel's idea.

"Fear tells you how to protect them," he'd said, watching over her shoulder the way he did, hands clasped behind his back like a man trying not to touch anything valuable. "A registry that only lists powers is just a menu. A registry that lists fears is a promise."

"You're getting disturbingly wise," Sela told him. "It doesn't suit you. You used to be the quiet one who alphabetized things."

"I'm still the quiet one who alphabetizes things. I've simply expanded my portfolio."

His hands shook when he said it. They still shook, most days. The covenant had nearly killed him—had, by the Warden's own account, *half* killed him, whatever fraction of a death that was—and some mornings he came down to the registry room gray-faced and moving carefully, as though the world were a floor of ice he didn't entirely trust. But he came down. Every morning he came down, and sat at the long table, and helped people write their names in a book designed to keep them safe, and Sela had decided not to comment on the shaking because she suspected that being commented upon was the one thing that might stop him.

Besides, her own hands weren't exactly steady either. Nights, sometimes, she still heard the Veilwardens' boots on the stairs, and woke reaching for the stolen ledger page that had started everything—that single torn sheet with six names on it, hers almost among them, saved now under glass in the archive room like a relic. Which, she supposed, it was. People had begun leaving flowers near it. She'd caught a clerk's apprentice doing it twice and pretended not to see, because the alternative was explaining that the page also smelled faintly of Corvane's pipe tobacco, and relics ought to be allowed their dignity.

* * *

Corvane himself was gone—not dead, though Sela sometimes thought death would have been simpler for everyone, including him. He had walked out of the guild the morning after Mirabel mapped him honestly, carrying nothing but the clothes he wore and a small case of personal papers, and had presented himself to the crown's magistrates for judgment before anyone could decide whether to arrest him.

The trial had lasted four days. Sela had attended all of them, sitting in the gallery with her arms crossed, waiting to feel satisfaction.

It hadn't come. That was the trouble with a man like Corvane: he pled guilty to everything and defended everything simultaneously, in the same breath, with the same terrible calm. *Yes, I sold six names. Yes, those six died or nearly died. And yes, forty others lived, and you would have done the same, and if you say otherwise you are lying to flatter yourselves.* Half the courtroom had hated him. The other half had shifted uncomfortably in their seats, and Sela had watched them shift and understood, with a sour taste, that the uncomfortable half was the dangerous one—not because they were wrong, but because they were half right, and half-right men build institutions faster than whole ones.

The sentence had been exile from the guild's charter lands and permanent prohibition from holding office over any person gifted or ungifted. He'd accepted it with a nod, gathered his papers, and paused at the door to look back—not at the judges, but at Sela, specifically, as though he'd known she'd be there, arms crossed, waiting to feel something.

"Keep the ledgers open," he'd said. "That part you'll do better than I did."

Then he'd left, and Sela had gone home and drunk half a bottle of plum wine and cried about it, furiously, in a way she refused ever to explain to anyone, including Mirabel, who had knocked gently on her door that evening and, receiving no answer, had left a plate of honey cakes outside it and gone away, which was why he remained her closest friend even when he was insufferable.

* * *

By the third week, the queue outside the guildhouse stretched to the fountain.

Sela instituted a ticket system. This was her first act of genuine institutional reform, and she was quietly proud of it, because the old guild had operated on patronage—who you knew, who owed whom—and patronage was just favoritism wearing a nice coat. Tickets were democratic. Everyone waited. The washerwoman waited behind the fishwives; the boy who walked into drawings waited behind a tax assessor who could make sums refuse to come out wrong.

The tax assessor was an interesting case. He sat across from Sela, twisting his hat, and explained that his gift wasn't really a gift, it was more of a compulsion, numbers simply *told him* when they'd been handled dishonestly, they got a sort of greasy feeling—

"And you hid this," Sela said, "for how long?"

"Nineteen years." He winced. "During which I may have, indirectly, collapsed two smuggling rings and one municipal bond scheme, entirely by accident, by reporting figures that felt greasy."

Sela wrote his name down, and his gift, and his fear—which turned out to be *being useful*, poor man, because usefulness attracted attention, and attention had lately been fatal—and slid the new registry toward him.

"Sign here. What you get in return is this: if anyone comes asking after your gift—for taxes, for armies, for anything—you send them to me. The Keeper of Truths. And I will tell them the truth, which is that your gift belongs to you, and this guild exists so that nobody has to hide in a cellar to keep what's theirs."

The tax assessor stared at her. Then, unexpectedly, his eyes filled.

"I hid for nineteen years," he said again, "and it took me four minutes to sign that."

"Yes," Sela said. "We're hoping to improve the wait times."

* * *

Mirabel found her at dusk, when the queue had gone home and the light through the sailcloth windows had gone amber and forgiving. He was carrying two cups of tea, which meant he wanted something, because Mirabel only carried tea when he wanted something; when he wanted nothing he carried nothing, having learned economy from a lifetime of making himself unnoticeable.

"Before you ask," Sela said, "no, I haven't finished the cross-indexing, and yes, I know the archive smells like smoke, and no, we cannot afford a second archivist."

"I wasn't going to ask any of that." He set a cup beside her. "I was going to ask whether you've slept."

"I sleep."

"In a bed?"

"Technically."

He pulled a chair around backward and sat on it the wrong way, arms folded on the rest, which was a posture he'd adopted since the covenant—as though furniture, like the world, no longer deserved formalities. Up close she could see the fine tremor in his fingers wrapped around the cup, and the way he'd learned to steady the cup against the chair instead of trusting his hands.

"The Warden came to me again," he said. "In the margins. Last night."

Sela put down her pen. The Warden of Margins was a subject they discussed rarely and carefully, the way one discusses a relative who has passed beyond ordinary correspondence. "And?"

"It says the borders are holding. Better than holding—it says the realms have started *remembering* each other. Coastlines on my old maps are drifting toward the real coastlines, matching them, like two people learning to walk in step." He smiled, and it was the tired, wondering smile she'd gotten used to, the one of a man still astonished daily that the world hadn't ended. "There's a stretch of shore north of Harrowmere where the tide comes in twice now. Once wet, once ink. The fishermen are furious. The children think it's the finest thing that has ever happened."

"They would."

"There's something else." He turned the cup a quarter-turn, a full rotation, another. Nervous. Mirabel Quintana, who had faced a covenant-stone and a guildmaster and the unraveling of two worlds, was nervous about something involving tea and a quarter-turn of a cup. "I've finished it. The map. The honest one. All of it—the unmapped realm, the margins, the ferryman's rivers, the grave. Everything I saw. Drawn true, named true, signed—" he stopped turning the cup— "signed with my name. My whole name."

Sela looked at him for a long moment. She thought of the ledger page under glass, six names in tidy columns. She thought of the charter provisions. She thought of the boy who walked into pictures, and his mother holding his coat.

"You're going to publish it," she said.

"I'm going to publish it."

"Under 'Ivo.'"

Something moved through his face at the name—grief and gratitude tangled together, the memory he'd traded away and somehow, impossibly, been given back, or most of it, enough of it. "Under Ivo," he said quietly. "My mother's name for me. If I'm done hiding, I should probably start at the beginning."

Sela picked her pen back up, mostly because her eyes had decided to misbehave and she needed something to look at that wasn't his face.

"Well," she said, when her voice was reliable again. "You'll need a dedication page. And a disclaimer. And someone to check your spelling, because I have seen your marginalia, Mirabel—Ivo—and 'peninsular' defeated you for three straight years."

"I was hoping," he said, with the ghost of his old dry smile, "the Keeper of Truths might review it. Before it goes out into the world. Someone who lies professionally well and has retired from the practice."

"Retired," Sella agreed. "Mostly. On Tuesdays."

Outside, the last of the queue was shuffling home through the amber light, forty-one names and counting, each one written down in a book built to protect them. Inside, the new ledger lay open on her desk, its blank pages waiting, patient as dawn.

Sela dipped her pen, and began tomorrow's column.
