## 22. Ivo and the Veil

The printing press in the cellar of the rechartered Guild of Impossible Things had once been used to forge royal seals. Now it printed maps.

There was a certain poetry to that, Sela said, which was the sort of thing she said when she wanted me to stop fussing with the type and come look at something. I did not stop fussing. The type mattered. Every letter of it mattered, because for the first time in my life my name would appear on my work—not a guild mark, not an anonymous attribution to *a cartographer of the Guild*, but a name, mine, spelled out in ink that anyone could read.

*Ivo Quintana.*

I had practiced saying it aloud in the weeks since the covenant-stone, quietly, the way one practices a language half-forgotten. It still sat strangely in my mouth, like a coat tailored for someone slightly braver than I remembered being. My mother's name for me. The name she had sung across a kitchen while bread rose on the sill, the name I had traded away to a ferryman without ever knowing what I'd lost—only that there was a shape in my chest where something warm had been, a socket missing its tooth.

And then, at the stone, when the new covenant took my blood and my true name together, the ferryman's bargain had unraveled like a knot finally understood. The memory came back all at once: her face, her hands dusted with flour, the way she said *Ivo* as if it were a small joke between us, as if the world were already in on it. I had wept in front of the Warden of Margins, who had regarded me with the patient expression of a creature older than grief and said, "Yes. That is what it costs. And that is what it returns. Both are always true."

I pressed the last letter into place. Q-U-I-N-T-A-N-A. Then, beneath it, smaller: *Cartographer.*

Not *clerk*. Not *keeper of accounts*. Not *one who balances the impossible so no one notices it happening*.

"Mirabel," Sela called again from the top of the stairs. "Come up. You'll want to see this."

"I'm working," I called back.

"You've been working for six hours on a title page."

"It's an important title page."

"It's four words long."

"*Five,*" I said, "if you count the article," and heard her laugh echo down the stairwell, that easy unguarded laugh she'd only learned to let out after Corvane fell, after the ledger was read aloud, after the guild stopped being a place where laughter had to be checked against the exits.

I gave the type one last critical glance and went up.

* * *

The guild hall looked different in morning light. Everything did, now, but the guild most of all.

We had kept the building—the crown, in its embarrassed way, had insisted upon it, once the archivist's documents made plain whose purse had funded the Veilhunt and why. But the charter above the hearth was new, written in Sela's careful hand because she refused to let anyone else do it: *No name sold. No gift hunted. No truth withheld from those it concerns.* Three lines. Corvane's old charter had run to forty-seven clauses and not one of them had mentioned people.

Corvane himself was gone. Not dead—I had mapped him too honestly for him to survive his own portrait and then simply die, which would have been tidy, and the world had shown little interest in tidiness lately. He lived now in a room in the House of Quiet Reckonings, a place that was either a prison or a monastery depending on whom you asked, writing letters of restitution that arrived by the cartload. He had written to me eleven times. I had answered twice. In his last letter he had asked whether the map I'd drawn of him—the honest one, the one that had broken him open like a geode—still existed.

It did. I kept it in the map-room, in the drawer reserved for things that were true but not yet finished being true. Someday, I thought, he might be ready to look at it again. Someday the man he might have been would be recognizable in the man he was. Maps, I had learned, were not verdicts. They were invitations to find your way back.

The hall was busy this morning. Half the staff were new—gifted folk who had come out of hiding once the covenant was renewed openly, once the Veilhunt collapsed under the weight of its own ledgers. A boy by the window could make locked doors forget they were locked. Two sisters at the far table could taste lies in the air, like copper. An old woman who introduced herself only as "the woman who remembers rain" had taken over the front desk and terrified visiting officials with cheerful efficiency.

They had all, at some point in the past weeks, found their way to my workroom and asked, shyly or boldly or sideways, whether I would chart their gifts. Whether I would put them down properly, in ink, named.

That was the thing about drawing a door and walking through it. Other people noticed the door was possible.

Sela stood at the front window with the woman who remembers rain, both of them looking out at the street. When I reached them, Sela didn't point. She just stepped aside.

A crowd had gathered outside the guild doors. Not a mob—a queue. It wound down the steps and along the cobbles and out of sight past the chandler's shop. People holding papers, children holding drawings, an old soldier holding what looked like a scrap of cloth embroidered with a coastline that did not exist anywhere official. They had come to be mapped. All of them. On the day the guild reopened its doors to the public, they had lined up before dawn.

"They started arriving at the fourth bell," the woman who remembers rain said. "I told them the doors opened at ninth. They said they'd wait." She smiled with all the menace of a contented cat. "One of them is a tax assessor. I let him wait longest."

"Why?" I asked, though I knew. I think I had known since the stone, since the moment the two realms settled against each other like hands laced together. "Why come here?"

Sela looked at me with the expression she'd worn the night she hid me in the map-room, the night she stole a page from a devil's ledger and chose me over her own neck. "Because you signed your name," she said. "Because everyone in that line has spent their whole life being told their gift makes them a target or a freak or a rumor. And then a cartographer wrote a book with his mother's name in it and put the unmapped realm on the same page as ours, and drew the border as a handshake instead of a wall." She shrugged. "Turns out hope is contagious. Who knew. Certainly not the crown."

The woman who remembers rain snorted. "The crown knew. The crown just hoped nobody would notice hope was cheaper than fear and considerably better for trade."

Below us, the queue shifted and resettled like a sleeping animal. Someone near the front began to sing, very softly, a tune I almost recognized—and then did recognize, and had to grip the windowsill, because it was the song my mother had sung, the bread-rising song, and hearing it in a stranger's voice on a guild street felt like the world handing me back something I hadn't dared ask for.

"The press is ready?" Sela asked.

I nodded. "First copies by noon."

"Then let's go make history boring," she said. "History should be boring. Exciting history is usually someone else's catastrophe."

* * *

At noon we pulled the first sheet.

It came off the press damp and smelling of oil and iron-gall ink, and I held it up to the light of the cellar lamp with both hands, the way you hold a newborn or a very expensive vase.

The map filled the whole sheet. On the left, our realm—every coast corrected, every river given its true course, every city labeled with its proper name and, beneath each name in smaller type, the names of the gifted folk who lived there, if they had consented to be listed. Consent mattered. We had built the whole registry around it. On the right, the unmapped realm, rendered as faithfully as I could manage from memory and from the Warden's patient dictation—which was a strange experience, being dictated to by a creature who spoke in the grammar of tides, but I had managed, and the margins of that half of the map bloomed with marginalia: the ferryman's rivers, the grave of Alderyn marked with a small star and the words *She was betrayed; be better*, the covenant-stone at the heart of everything drawn not as an object but as a hinge.

And across the middle, where any old map would have shown a border—a wall, a warning, a red line with teeth—I had drawn the veil itself. Not as a barrier. As a curtain, half-drawn, with light coming through. And beneath it, in letters large enough to read across a room:

*This is not a border. It is a door. Doors are meant to be opened by consent, from either side, by anyone brave enough to knock.*

And beneath that, the title, and beneath the title, the name.

*Ivo and the Veil.*
*A map in two realms, honestly drawn.*
*By Ivo Quintana, cartographer.*

Sela read it three times. Then she said, "You know everyone will call you Mirabel anyway. Habits die hard. I will probably call you Mirabel until the day I die."

"That's fine," I said. "Both of them are me. That was rather the point."

"No," she said, and touched the edge of the sheet, careful, the way she touched rare things. "The point was that you stopped choosing. You're both. You just finally admitted it in print." She looked up. "Pull the next one. The queue's not getting shorter, and the woman who remembers rain has started charging the tax assessor rent."

* * *

We hung the first copy in the guild hall, above the new charter, and I stood back and watched people see it.

That is a thing I recommend to anyone who makes anything: watch people see it. Watch the boy who unlocks doors find his own name in small type beneath the city of his birth and go absolutely still, the way a deer goes still, the way a person goes still when they realize they have been *counted*—not as a threat, counted as a neighbor. Watch the sisters who taste lies find each other's names side by side and hold hands without looking at each other, because some things are too large to face head-on. Watch the old soldier lay his impossible coastline against my impossible coastline and discover they matched, stitch for stitch, and begin to cry without any noise at all.

Watch them, and understand what Corvane never did: that a ledger of names can be a weapon or a lantern, and the difference is not in the names. The difference is in whether anyone was asked.

In the late afternoon, when the queue had thinned to stragglers and the lamplighter was making his rounds, a girl of perhaps ten came to the desk holding a piece of paper folded into eighths. She unfolded it on the counter with great ceremony. It was a drawing—a child's drawing, crayon, of a house with too many windows and a garden where the flowers were drawn as spirals.

"My gran says I draw places that aren't real," the girl announced, in the tone of one reciting an injustice. "But I *dreamed* this house. Last week. There was a lady in it who wasn't a lady, she was more like weather, and she said somebody would come who could tell me what my drawings are."

The woman who remembers rain glanced at me. I glanced at Sela. Sela raised one eyebrow in a way that communicated approximately nine paragraphs.

"What was the weather-lady's name?" I asked the girl, kneeling to be at eye level with the counter.

"She didn't say. But she smelled like rain on hot stones." The girl frowned. "Is that weird?"

"Not even slightly," I said. "In the unmapped realm, that's practically a formal introduction." I smoothed the crayon drawing flat. The spiraled flowers were not imagination. I knew that garden. I had walked through it on my way to the covenant-stone, and the flowers there grew in spirals because they remembered being ferns in a country that had forgotten ferns. "Would you like to learn how to draw it so other people can visit?"

Her whole face changed, the way faces do when a door they thought was painted on turns out to open. "Other people can *visit?*"

"If we draw carefully," I said. "That's the whole job. Careful drawing, honest names, and asking permission—from the places, too. They appreciate it."

She looked at me with the naked, uncomplicated faith of a child being told the world is bigger than advertised, and I felt the weight of it settle onto my shoulders—and found, to my surprise, that it fit. That it fit the way *Ivo* fit. Like a coat tailored, all along, for exactly this.

* * *

That evening, Sela and I sat in the map-room with the good wine—the bottle Alderyn's grave had somehow sent us, via channels the Warden declined to explain, with a note in tide-grammar that translated roughly to *she would have wanted the good stuff wasted on you specifically*.

Outside, the city went about its business. Somewhere a door forgot it was locked, presumably on purpose. Somewhere the sisters tasted the evening air and pronounced it mostly honest, which for a capital city was a record. The veil between the realms shimmered, faintly visible now at dusk to anyone who cared to look—a curtain of light over the eastern hills, half-drawn, patient.

"Do you miss it?" Sela asked. "Being invisible?"

I considered it honestly, because she deserved honesty and because I had promised myself, signing that covenant, that I would never again mistake silence for safety or hiding for humility.

"I miss the efficiency," I said. "It's much faster to balance impossible accounts when no one asks questions. And I miss—" I turned the glass in my hand. "I suppose I miss believing that hiding was a kind of kindness. That staying small protected people. It didn't. It just meant the ledger filled up with names while I kept mine clean-looking." I met her eyes. "My mother gave me a name and I spent thirty years treating it like contraband. She sang it across a kitchen, Sela. Like it was nothing. Like it was the easiest thing in the world."

"And now?"

"Now I sign it forty times a day and my hand cramps," I said, and she laughed, and I laughed, and the wine was very good, and the map above the charter caught the lamplight so that the half-drawn curtain seemed to move, gently, like something breathing behind it.

Later—much later, when Sela had gone home and the guild slept—I climbed to the workroom one last time. The press stood quiet. The type sat in its tray, already reset for tomorrow's printing: the second edition, with corrections submitted by readers in *both* realms, because the unmapped folk had opinions about my rendering of their coastline and the Warden had delivered them in a sealed gourd with the expression of a creature enjoying itself immensely.

On my desk lay a fresh sheet of paper, and beside it, the pen.

I did not know yet what I would draw. Perhaps the girl's house with its weather-lady. Perhaps the ferryman's rivers, which deserved a proper atlas. Perhaps, someday, when enough letters had accumulated and enough restitution had been made, a small careful map of a man learning to carry his own arithmetic—drawn kindly, drawn truly, left where he could find it.

But first, as I did every night now, as I expected to do every night for the rest of my life, gladly, with a cramping hand—

I signed my name.

*Ivo Quintana.*

And somewhere beyond the veil, faint as rain on hot stones, something old and patient and fond whispered it back.
