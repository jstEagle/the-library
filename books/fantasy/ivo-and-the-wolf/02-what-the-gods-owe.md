## 2. What the Gods Owe

The pre-exile ledgers lived in the deepest tier of the Vellum Guild's archive, down a stair that smelled of cold stone and older paper, where the lamps burned a color that was not quite yellow and the silence had weight enough to lean against.

Ines Winterbourne went down on the last night of the quarter, as she always did, to reconcile the dead accounts. It was work no one else wanted and no one else could do — not because it was difficult, but because it required a particular temperament: the patience to sit with books whose ink had gone brown with age, whose columns recorded transactions between parties long dissolved, and the discipline not to flinch at what commerce the guild had once conducted when it was younger and less careful about its appetites. Bottled grief from the weeping houses of old Ashvale. Years borrowed against unborn children. A door, sold in 1189, to a buyer whose name had been scraped from the margin by someone who feared even writing it twice.

She carried her lamp low and her ledger-case high, out of habit, though nothing down here had ever reached for her before.

The tier was arranged by century, and the century she needed was the eleventh — the century of her exile, though she did not let herself think of it that way yet. She thought of it as *the audit*. Everything in her life since had been an audit: columns checked, sums balanced, her own history reduced to a single entry she was permitted to read but never to amend. *Ines Winterbourne, storm-caller, exiled for the calling of the Great Storm of 1191.* That was all the record said. That was all anyone would say. Eleven years of meticulous bookkeeping, and still the entry refused to balance, because somewhere there was a debit with no corresponding credit, a loss with no listed cause, and Ines Winterbourne had built her whole careful life on the principle that every imbalance could be found if you were willing to look long enough.

She had never been permitted to look. Until now.

It had been Halvard's doing, or Halvard's carelessness — she suspected the two were the same thing in him, the way a locked drawer left ajar is both gift and test. Three days ago he had set her to cataloging the recovered holdings of a dissolved branch-house in the north, crates of ledgers salvaged from a counting house that had burned, and among them, misfiled under *Agricultural Contracts*, she had found a volume bound in grey leather with no title and no guild seal. A private book. A book that should not have survived.

She had told no one. She had waited three days, which was either prudence or cowardice, and then she had come down into the dark with her lamp and her copying kit and her heart beating like something trying to get out of a locked room.

* * *

The grey ledger opened to a smell she knew and hated: vellum cured too fast, in haste, by hands that had more need of pages than of patience. The early entries were ordinary enough — tithes, transfers, the quiet traffic of a guild branch doing business with a countryside that no longer existed.

That was the sentence that stopped her. She read it again. *Business with a countryside that no longer existed.*

Because she knew these place-names. Not from maps — she had checked, years ago, in the small hours, with the guilty thoroughness of a woman looking up her own diagnosis. There was no Vel Sarenne on any map in the guild's possession, nor in the royal cartography offices, nor in the atlases chained in the university library where she had once spent a week pretending to research shipping routes. The names were simply *gone*, the way a tooth is gone: not absent so much as amputated, with a smoothness where the absence should ache.

But here they were, in brown ink, in a steady professional hand: *Vintner's tithe, Vale of Sarenne, paid in kind.* *Ferry rights, River Seren, renewed.* *Storm-warding fee, collected quarterly, all parishes of Vel Sarenne.*

Storm-warding fee. Her lamp flame leaned, though there was no draft.

She turned the pages faster now, past contracts and receipts, past a page of names struck through in red — struck through so hard the stylus had torn the vellum, as if whoever held the pen had wanted not just to delete the names but to wound them — until she reached the section near the end that was not business at all.

It was headed, in a different hand, larger and older, with letters that seemed to have been drawn rather than written: **THE LEDGER OF WHAT IS OWED**.

And beneath it, in a column of debts, third from the top:

*Ines Winterbourne, daughter of Maren, of the parish of Hollowmere, Vel Sarenne — debtor to the Nine Who Kept the Sky. Debt incurred before birth. Debt inherited. Debt unpayable in coin. Terms held in trust by the Keeper of the Door.*

Ines sat very still.

The lamp made a small sound, wax settling, and in the enormous quiet of the archive tier it sounded like a knuckle rapping on wood.

She read the entry again. Then again, the way one reads a sum that will not come right, certain that the error must be hers, because the alternative — that the ledger was correct — meant that everything she understood about herself was a rounding error someone had chosen not to correct.

*Debt incurred before birth.* She had been born in Hollowmere. She knew this the way you know your own heartbeat: not from evidence but from continuity. But she had never known *where* Hollowmere was. When she asked, as a child, Halvard had said *north*, and when she asked what happened to the north, he had said *nothing happens anywhere, child; places only seem to end because maps do*, and she had been eleven and had accepted this, because Halvard's voice had the particular gravity of a man who has decided what you are allowed to know.

*Daughter of Maren.* She had no mother. This was another thing she knew without evidence. Halvard had raised her; the guild had schooled her; her origins were a closed account, and she had learned young that closed accounts were not to be pried at, because prying was how apprentices became collectors' stories, warnings told to new intake over cheap wine.

And yet here was her name. Not *Winterbourne* alone — the surname Halvard had given her, the one that sounded respectable and rootless, suitable for a foundling — but *Ines Winterbourne, daughter of Maren*, written down before she could write, in a book from a country that had been erased, owed to gods who had been dead for three hundred years.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed this with the detached professionalism she brought to everything, the way she might notice a shipment running short, and she despised herself for it, and she kept reading.

There were other names in the column. Dozens. Whole families of Vel Sarenne, each marked *debtor*, each debt *inherited*, each *unpayable in coin*. And beside each name, in the margin, in the same drawn-looking letters, a word or two that might have been a god's name or a god's office: *Sky-Keeper. Well-Mother. The Wolf at the Threshold. The Nine Who Kept the Sky.*

The Wolf at the Threshold.

Something moved through her that was not memory exactly, because she had no memories of Vel Sarenne — she had checked that too, interrogated her own childhood like a hostile witness, and found nothing, nothing, a perfectly blank ledger where a life should have been. But it was adjacent to memory. It was the shape memory leaves behind, the way a pressed flower is not the garden. A threshold. Grey fur at the edge of a fire's light. A voice too large for the throat it came from, saying words she could almost —

The lamp guttered. Down here, in the deep tier, flames sometimes behaved strangely; everyone said so; it was one of the reasons no one liked working below. Ines steadied the glass with both hands and made herself breathe.

*Gods thought extinct.* Every schoolchild in Ashvale could recite it: the old gods died when their countries forgot them, and the last of them perished centuries ago, and what remained were saints and superstitions and the guild's honest trade in grief and years. Extinction was the comfortable doctrine. Extinction meant the world was finished being made, and therefore could be audited.

This ledger disagreed. This ledger kept accounts with things that were supposed to be dead, and accounts, Ines knew better than anyone alive, do not stay open unless someone is still paying interest.

* * *

"You shouldn't have brought it down here."

Halvard's voice came from the top of the stair, and Ines did not startle, because she had heard his tread four steps ago and had decided, deliberately, not to hide the book. She was tired of hiding things. She had been hiding one thing for eleven years and it had grown so heavy she had forgotten it was a choice.

He came down slowly, one hand on the rail, the way he always descended into the archive, as if the past were a cold pool he preferred to enter gradually. He was old in the way of good timber — bent but sound — and his eyes, pale as skimmed milk, went straight to the grey ledger open on the table, and then to her face, and stayed there.

"Halvard." She turned the book toward him, gently, the way one turns a blade. "Tell me this is a forgery."

He looked at the page for a long time. The lamp between them threw his shadow up the shelving, huge and stooped, like something hunched over its own hoard.

"Where did you find it," he said, which was not an answer, which was itself an answer.

"The north branch salvage. Misfiled under agricultural contracts." She kept her voice level, clerk-calm, the voice she used for reporting discrepancies. "Halvard. *Vel Sarenne.* There's a tithe roll for it. Ferry rights. Storm-warding fees collected quarterly from parishes I cannot find on any map in this building, and I have looked in all of them." She stood. "And my name. My name is in it, listed as a debtor to gods that have been extinct since before the guild had a charter. So either this book is a fraud elaborate enough to include my mother's name, which no living person knows —"

"No living person," Halvard said quietly, "knows."

The words dropped into the space between them and neither of them picked them up.

"Sit down, child," he said.

"I'm forty-one years old."

"And I've been lying to you for thirty-four of them, so sit down, and let me decide how much of the rest you'll hear standing."

She sat. It was not obedience; it was tactics. Standing, she would have shouted. Sitting, she could watch his hands, and Halvard's hands had taught her more than his mouth ever had — the way they went still when he was choosing truth, the way they smoothed his coat when he was choosing comfort.

They went still now.

"The book is real," he said. "The country was real. Vel Sarenne. You were born there, in Hollowmere, in the shadow of the Sky-Keepers' hill, and your mother's name was Maren, and she was a storm-caller of the old line, and so are you." His milk-pale eyes did not waver. "And in the year 1191, the year of your exile, the gods of Vel Sarenne were not extinct. They were *bound*. Bound and buried beneath their own hill by men who wanted their sky, and the storm that broke the night you were cast out was not called by you."

The lamp flame stood perfectly upright, as if listening.

"Then who—" Ines began, and stopped, because her voice had cracked along a seam she hadn't known was there, and she would not give him the sight of it. She folded her hands on the table, clerk-calm, and started again. "You signed my exile record. You witnessed it. Your seal is on the document that says I called the Great Storm, that I killed — that the deaths of that night were mine."

"Yes."

"*Yes?*"

"Yes, I signed it, and yes, it is a lie, and yes, I would sign it again tomorrow, and you may hate me for it for as long as it keeps you alive." For the first time in thirty years, Halvard's voice shook. "Ines. Listen to me as your mentor, since I have no right to be heard as anything else. That page you're sitting next to — the debt-page, the one with your name — burn it. Burn the book. Tonight, before first bell, before Corvane's people run their quarterly inventory and find a volume in the archive that predates the guild's charter and shouldn't exist. Because that book isn't a record, child. It's a *summons*. Debts in it don't sleep forever. And the things it names as creditors —" he swallowed — "they've begun collecting again. I've seen the signs. Wells refilling. Orchards blooming out of season. Prayers answering themselves. The old world is coming back through the cracks, and when it comes, it will come first for the names in that book, and yours is third from the top."

Ines looked down at the page. At her own handwriting-ready name in someone else's ancient ink. *Debt incurred before birth. Debt inherited. Debt unpayable in coin.*

Every instinct she had honed in twenty years of guild service said: destroy the liability. Close the account. Burn it, as Halvard said, and go back upstairs, and reconcile the quarter, and be the meticulous, exiled, half-a-woman she had made of herself, safe inside a lie with his seal on it.

But Ines Winterbourne had built her whole life on one principle, and the principle was this: *an unexamined entry is not a settled one.* Somewhere in the dark below her orderly existence there was a debit with no cause, a loss with no listed origin, a mother with a name, a country with a title, a storm with an author — and she had spent eleven years being forbidden to look, and the forbidding, she now understood, had been done by the man she loved best in the world, for reasons he clearly believed were merciful.

Mercy, she thought, looking at his trembling hands, could be its own kind of theft.

"No," she said.

Halvard closed his eyes. "Ines—"

"No. You don't get to choose what I owe." She was already reaching for her copying kit, unrolling the leather, wetting the pen with hands that had finally, blessedly, gone steady. "If my name is in this book, then this book is mine — the way a debt is mine, the way a mother's name is mine, the way the truth of one night in 1191 is mine, and I have been poor in all three for far too long. I'm not burning it, Halvard. I'm going to copy it. Every page. Every name. Every term and clause and creditor. And then I'm going to find out what the Nine Who Kept the Sky are owed, and by whom, and why my mother's name isn't in this column —" her pen paused above the vellum, dripping — "because I looked, Halvard. Maren isn't listed as a debtor. She's not listed at all. Which means either she paid in full—"

"—or she paid in the only coin those gods accept," Halvard whispered.

The lamp flame leaned toward the stair, toward the upper world, toward the city where wells were refilling and orchards were blooming and prayers were answering themselves, and Ines Winterbourne bent over the grey ledger and began, in her flawless clerk's hand, to copy her own debt.

Outside, far above the archive tiers, the first thunder of the season rolled across Ashvale — though the sky, every almanac agreed, was cloudless.
