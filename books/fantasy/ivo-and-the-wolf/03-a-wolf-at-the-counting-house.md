## 3. A Wolf at the Counting House

The vaults of the Vellum Guild lay beneath the counting house like a heart beneath a ribcage, and Ines Winterbourne had spent six years learning their every beat.

She descended the spiral stair at the hour when even the most devoted clerks had gone home, when the lamps in the upper rooms were snuffed one by one and the building settled into its long, paper-scented sleep. The stair was worn into a shallow trough by two centuries of feet — guild feet, ledger-keeper's feet, her own among them now. She carried a single candle and the night's work: three items requiring cataloguing, one of which had been giving her a headache since sundown.

The vault door recognized her. It was an old thing, older than the guild's charter, banded in bronze that had been poured with prayers mixed into the metal — you could still read fragments of them if you tilted your head, *keep out, keep out, keep what is kept* — and it swung inward at the touch of her seal-ring without so much as a groan. The air beyond was cold and dry and utterly silent, the particular silence of a place where nothing was permitted to decay.

Shelves rose to the ceiling in ranks. Bottled grief gleamed on the eastern wall, each vial stoppered with grey wax, each labeled in her own careful hand: *grief, maternal, moderate; grief, first love, severe; grief, unnamed, do not open.* Borrowed years sat in casks along the north side, ticking faintly, like sleeping clocks. There was a shelf of doors — actual doors, standing upright in their frames with nothing behind them but brick — and Ines had learned never to look too long at the one painted the blue of a summer sky, because sometimes, if she looked too long, it looked back.

Tonight's problem waited on the intake table under a glass cloche: the prayer scroll from Chapter Two's shipment, the one that had answered itself.

She set down her candle, pulled on her white cotton gloves, and lifted the cloche. The scroll lay coiled like something asleep. It had arrived sealed, addressed to no one, found by a collector in the ruins of a chapel outside Ashvale where no congregation had prayed in forty years. And sometime between the chapel and the counting house, the ink had moved. Words had appeared below the prayer — words in answer — in a hand no clerk could identify, in a language Halvard swore predated the guild itself.

Ines unrolled it now and read it for perhaps the hundredth time.

*A prayer for rain,* said the original script, faded brown. *A prayer for rain, for the fields, for the children, for the dead orchards.*

And beneath it, in fresh black ink that seemed to have been written from inside the page:

*Yes.*

That was all. One word. But Ines had catalogued impossible things for six years, and she knew the taxonomy of them the way other people knew the names of birds. Bottled grief was impossible, but it obeyed rules — it could be decanted, diluted, sold. Borrowed years obeyed rules. Even the doors obeyed rules, though nobody had ever written down what those rules were. This scroll broke the fundamental rule of the trade: things in the vaults were *dead*. They were inventory. They had been taken out of the world and therefore the world had let go of them, and they sat in the dark being merchandise until someone bought them.

This scroll was not merchandise. This scroll was *correspondence*.

"Six years," she said aloud, to the empty vault, because talking to herself was one of the small liberties of exile. "Six years I've kept your ledgers, and nothing has ever answered anything."

The vault did not reply. The vault never replied.

But something else did.

"You're right," said a voice from the shadow between the grief-shelves and the doors. "It doesn't. That's rather the point of this place, isn't it? A room built to make sure nothing answers."

Ines did not scream. She would be proud of this later, in the way one is proud of small survivals. What she did instead was very slow and very deliberate: she closed her hand around the candlestick, turned, and raised the light.

A wolf stood between the shelves of bottled grief.

Not a large wolf — or rather, not a wolf shaped for largeness. He was lean and grey, the color of old pewter, of rain over slate, with pale eyes that caught the candleflame and held it the way water holds a coin. He stood the way wolves stand when they are not afraid and not hunting: weight even, ears forward, entirely present. There was dust on his paws. There should not have been dust on his paws; the vaults were swept twice daily and warded besides, and nothing entered without the guild's seal.

Nothing entered. Nothing left. That was the whole architecture of the place.

"The wards," Ines said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt, which she counted as a professional victory. "You're inside the wards."

"I've always been inside the wards," the wolf said. His voice was wrong in a way she couldn't immediately name — not growled, not shaped by a throat the way a throat shapes sound. It was more as if the words arrived already formed, like letters slipped under a door. "That's rather my complaint about them."

"Then you're either a ghost, a thief, or a god." She set the candlestick down slowly, keeping her hand near it. "And ghosts don't leave pawprints in fresh dust, and thieves can't usually talk. So."

"So," the wolf agreed, and there was — she searched for the word and found it reluctantly — *amusement* in him. Not menace. Amusement, dry and patient, like a man who has waited a long time for a joke to land. "You always did make lists when you were frightened. Categories. If it can be named, it can be shelved."

The bottom dropped out of her stomach.

Because she did make lists when she was frightened. She had made lists since she was a child, since before the exile, since the storm — and no one in Ashvale knew that. No one in Ashvale knew *anything* about her before the exile except what the record said, and the record said almost nothing. Halvard knew her, but Halvard had never heard her talk to the shelves, had never seen her alphabetize her fear.

"I don't know you," she said.

"No," the wolf said gently. "You don't. That was done to both of us, in a way. My name is Ivo."

The name went through her like a needle finding an old scar. She didn't know why. She had never heard it. And yet some part of her, some part below the ledgers and the gloves and the careful exile of six years, heard it and said: *yes, that's right, that was always going to be his name.*

"Ivo," she repeated, testing it. "Wolves don't have names."

"This one does. Wolves also don't usually have opinions about prayer scrolls, and yet here we are." He stepped forward, unhurried, and sat — actually sat, like a dog at a hearth, which somehow made it worse and better at once. "May I see it?"

"See what?"

"The scroll. The one that answered. I've come a very long way to see it, and the way was unpleasant, and I would prefer the trip not be wasted."

Every instinct honed by six years of guild service told her to refuse. Unknown entities did not handle inventory. Unknown entities were reported to Master Corvane by morning, and Corvane would smile his thin smile and the collectors would come, and whatever the wolf was, it would end up in a jar with a wax stopper and a label in her handwriting.

But the instinct underneath that one — the older one, the exiled one, the one that had been starving quietly for six years — said something different. It said: *he knows how you make lists when you're frightened. Nobody living knows that.*

Ines lifted the glass cloche aside and stepped back from the table.

Ivo rose, crossed the floor without a sound despite his size, and put his nose nearly to the scroll. He breathed in — a long, deliberate inhalation, the way one smells a letter for poison, or for home.

"Yes," he said softly. "It's begun again."

"What's begun?"

He looked up at her then, and the candlelight moved in his pale eyes, and for a moment — just a moment — she saw something behind them that was not a wolf at all. Something vast and weathered and patient, something that had watched centuries go by the way one watches rain.

"Ines Winterbourne," he said, and her name in his mouth sounded like a word in a language she'd forgotten she spoke. "Before I answer that, I need you to understand something. I am not here to hurt you. I am not here for the guild. I am here because your name is written in a book older than this building, and the debt on that page has come due, and I am — " a pause, and something almost like weariness moved through his voice — "I am the collector sent to collect it. Though I confess I argued for the position."

"My name is not in any book." Her voice cracked on it, and she hated that. "I've kept the ledgers for six years. Every debt, every entry, every name. I would know."

"Not *this* book." Ivo's gaze slid past her, up, toward the far corner of the vault where the oldest acquisitions were kept — the ones even Corvane only glanced at, the ones with dust thick enough to write in. "Third shelf. Behind the reliquary of Saint Adelheid's finger. A ledger bound in storm-grey hide. You'll find it, Ines. You've always been able to find things. It's why they keep you."

"Why *who* keeps me?" she whispered. "Why does anyone keep me?"

But the wolf only looked at her, patient as winter, and waited.

* * *

Later — much later, though the vaults kept no honest hours — Ines would try to reconstruct how she had decided. There was no decision, really. There was a moment where six years of careful, shelved, catalogued exile simply became unbearable, the way a dam becomes unbearable, and she crossed the vault floor and knelt before the third shelf and moved the reliquary aside with gloved hands, and there it was.

A ledger bound in hide the exact grey of the wolf's coat. No title. No guild seal. Its pages gave off a smell like the air before lightning.

Her hands shook as she opened it. The parchment inside was not parchment. It was skin that had never been anything else, thin and translucent, and the writing upon it was not ink. It moved, faintly, the way heat-shimmer moves above a road in summer.

Page after page of names. Debts. Terms in languages she didn't know and terms in languages she did. And there, two-thirds through, in a column headed with a sigil like a wolf's print overlaid on a lightning-bolt:

**Winterbourne, Ines. Owed: one country. One name. One sky.**

Owed *to*, she corrected herself, reading again, her heart slamming against her ribs. Not owed by. Owed *to*. She was the debtor. Someone — something — had given her a country, a name, a sky, and the bill had arrived.

"That's impossible," she said. "I was born in Ashvale. I've never — "

"You weren't," Ivo said quietly, behind her. "You were born somewhere that no longer appears on maps. Somewhere whose name was taken so thoroughly that even its own children forgot they'd lost it. You were born in Vel Sarenne, Ines. And so was I."

The name landed in her like a stone into deep water. Vel Sarenne. She didn't know it. She had never heard it. And yet grief rose in her, sudden and enormous and homesick for a place she could not remember, the way one wakes grieving from a dream one cannot recall.

"I don't understand," she said. "If it was erased — who could erase a country? Who has that power?"

"Gods," Ivo said. "Or something wearing their manners. It doesn't matter tonight. Tonight matters only this: the scroll answered because the old gods are waking, one by one, wherever something of theirs was buried and forgotten. And your debt has come due, and debts of this kind are collected in person." He inclined his great grey head, and there was something in it that was almost, terribly, a bow. "I volunteered, Ines. Of all the collectors in all the dark places, I asked to be the one sent for you."

"Why?" The word tore out of her rougher than she meant it. "Why me? Why would you — you don't know me. You said yourself I don't know you. Whatever I was before the exile, I'm not that person anymore. I'm a ledger-keeper. I count other people's griefs and sell them back to them at a markup. I'm nobody's miracle."

The wolf was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice had changed — lower, older, stripped of its dry amusement, down to something raw.

"You counted my grief once," he said. "Do you understand? Six years ago, the night of your exile, when the storm took everything — I was there. I was bound and bleeding and the sky was coming down, and everyone ran, everyone always runs, and one girl stopped in the rain and looked at me as if I were a thing that could be *catalogued*. As if I were real. As if I mattered enough to be named." His pale eyes held hers. "Nobody had looked at me like that in nine hundred years, Ines. I have been a god. I know exactly what it is to be worshipped and exactly what it is to be *seen*, and I promise you they are not the same thing, and the second is worth infinitely more."

Ines could not speak. The vault was very cold and very quiet and the candle had burned down to a stub, and somewhere above them the counting house slept on, dreaming its paper dreams, not knowing that beneath its floors a god sat in the dust telling a ledger-keeper she had once been kind.

"I don't remember," she managed at last. "I swear to you, Ivo, I don't remember any of it."

"I know," he said. "They took it. The same ones who took the country. But memory is a debt too, and debts of this kind are also collected in person." He rose, shook the dust from his coat, and looked toward the vault door with an expression she could only call resolve. "Come find me when you've read the rest of the page. All of it. Especially the terms."

"The terms?"

"Debts of this kind are paid in strange coin." His eyes glinted. "And yours, I think, will cost you everything you believe about yourself."

He walked to the shadow between the shelves, and the shadow received him, and he was gone — no door, no sound, no disturbance of the dust. Only the faint smell of rain-before-lightning, and the ledger open on her knees, and the words moving on the page like heat-shimmer:

**Winterbourne, Ines. Owed: one country. One name. One sky.**

Ines sat alone in the vault until the candle drowned in its own wax, and then, because she was who she was — because she made lists when she was frightened, because she catalogued what terrified her — she took out her pen and copied the page, line by line, term by term, into her own neat hand.

And at the bottom of the ancient entry, in fresh black ink that had not been there when she began, new words surfaced beneath her copy like a body rising in still water:

*Terms due within seven days. Collector: IVO.*

Below that, smaller, in a hand she was beginning to know:

*Seven days, Ines. Then others come — and they are not patient, and they are not me.*

She blew out the candle and climbed the stairs into the sleeping counting house, carrying the copied page against her chest like contraband, like a heartbeat, like the first page of a life that had not yet been written.

Above her, through the high windows, the sky over Ashvale was beginning — very faintly, impossibly — to rain.
