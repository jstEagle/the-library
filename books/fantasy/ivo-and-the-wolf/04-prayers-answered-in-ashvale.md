## 4. Prayers Answered in Ashvale

The well on Tanner's Row had been dry for eleven years.

Mira Kessel knew the number precisely because she had counted every one of them, hauling water up from the river in wooden yokes that bit her shoulders raw, watching her father's tannery sicken and her brothers leave for the coast one by one. Eleven years of dust. Eleven years of lowering a bucket down a throat of stone and hearing it land on nothing.

So when she went out before dawn to fetch the day's first water — out of habit, not hope — and heard, instead of the flat knock of an empty bucket, the deep wet *gullop* of a full one, she sat down on the well's rim and cried into her apron until the sun came up.

The water was cold. It tasted of iron and something older, something green, like the memory of a forest that had never grown on Tanner's Row. She drank it anyway. She filled every pot in the house. By noon she had told four neighbors, and by dusk the whole street had come with buckets and jars and one enterprising cooper with a barrel cart, and they stood around the well like mourners at a resurrection, passing the cup from hand to hand, each person drinking and then looking at the others as if to ask: *did you taste it too?*

Old Tam Wicker, who had buried two wives and most of his teeth, knelt down at the well's edge and pressed his forehead to the wet stones.

"It's the Lady Under Hill," he said. "She's waked. I told my first wife so, forty years back, when the orchards still—"

"Orchards don't wake," said his neighbor, but gently, because Tam was old and grief makes prophets of us all.

Except that three streets over, in the crook of Candlemaker's Lane where a pear tree had stood dead as a fence post since the Sickness, the pear tree was standing in full white bloom. In the dark of evening it glowed faintly, petals opening out of season and out of reason, and the candlemakers came out of their shops with their hands still waxy and their faces lit like children's, and no one spoke for a long time, because what is there to say when the impossible puts on flowers?

A girl named Odile picked up a fallen petal and put it in her pocket. She would keep it for the rest of her life, though it would never wilt, and she would tell her grandchildren about the night the tree bloomed, and how everyone said it was a blessing, and how only later did they learn to be afraid.

* * *

Ines Winterbourne heard about the well before she heard about the tree, and she heard about both before the Guild did, which was itself a small miracle, given how quickly the Vellum Guild's ears turned rumor into inventory.

She was in the counting room above the vaults, reconciling the autumn ledger against the winter projections, when Halvard came up the stairs two at a time — which he never did; Halvard climbed stairs the way he did everything else, with the deliberate economy of a man who had learned that haste costs more than it saves.

"Tell me you've heard," he said.

"I've heard the well on Tanner's Row is wet." Ines did not look up from her columns. Her pen moved in small, exact strokes. "I've also heard the pear tree on Candlemaker's Lane is blooming, that the fevered child on Rope Street woke whole this morning, and that the millpond at Grayford froze solid in summer and the ice spelled out words no one can read. Rumor compounds faster than interest, Master Halvard. I keep accounts of both."

"And what do your accounts say?"

She set down her pen. That was the thing about Ines: she gave her full attention rarely, and when she gave it, people remembered why she'd been the Guild's best ledger-keeper before anyone knew her name was worth anything at all.

"They say," she said slowly, "that miracles are not free."

Halvard frowned. "Everything has a price. That's the first lesson of this house."

"Then name the price of a dry well filling after eleven years. Name the price of a dead tree blooming out of season. The Guild trades in bottled grief and borrowed years, Master Halvard, and we price those things carefully, because we know what they cost someone, somewhere, sometime. So who paid for the well? Who is owed for the pear tree?" She tapped the ledger before her. "Miracles without invoices frighten me more than any debt I've ever recorded."

For a moment Halvard said nothing. Then, quietly: "You sound like someone who's read pages she shouldn't have."

"I sound like someone who copies things," Ines said, "before anyone tells me to burn them."

He held her gaze a long moment — the old man's eyes gray as slate weather, unreadable and yet, lately, troubled in a way she couldn't parse — and then left without another word, his footsteps slow on the stairs going down.

Ines looked at the ledger. Then she opened the drawer where she kept the copied page — the pre-exile ledger, the one with her own name written in faded ink under a column headed *DEBTS OWED TO THE OLD ONES* — and read it again for the hundredth time.

*Ines Winterbourne,* it said. *Debtor.*

And beneath the names of gods everyone believed were ash and story, a line she hadn't noticed the first nine times: *Payment rendered in kind. The sky remembers its own.*

Outside the window, far off across the rooftops of Ashvale, she could have sworn the air smelled faintly of rain that wasn't falling.

* * *

By the third day, the city had gone mad with grace.

The fountain in Merchants' Square ran wine — thin, sour plonk, but wine — and the square filled with people drinking and laughing and weeping, and someone started singing an old song no one had taught them, a round with words in a language nobody spoke, and somehow everybody knew the tune.

The beggar at the Salt Gate whose legs had been ruined twenty years ago walked. He walked badly, lurching like a man learning, and he wept the whole way down the street, and people followed him in a growing procession, touching his shoulders, and by the time he reached the cathedral steps there were two hundred souls behind him chanting prayers to no god in particular, or to all of them at once.

Fishermen pulled nets heavy enough to strain their backs from waters that had been fished empty for a generation. A widow found her husband's wedding ring in her garden soil, polished bright, though he'd been buried at sea. Candles burned without consuming themselves. Bread doubled in ovens. Somewhere in the Warrens, a woman swore the ghost of her drowned son sat with her through the night and was gone by morning, leaving the smell of river water and a child's drawing scratched into the table.

And everywhere, everywhere, people prayed. Loudly, desperately, greedily. They prayed in the squares and the temples and the gutters. They prayed to names they invented on the spot, hedging their bets, and the terrifying thing — the thing Ines understood while the whole city sang — was that *it seemed to work regardless.*

* * *

Corvane summoned her at midday on the fourth day.

The Guildmaster received her in the map room, which was fitting, because Corvane thought of the world as territory and the Guild as its surveyor. He stood at the great table with his hands flat on a chart of Ashvale, and he did not look up when she entered, which was also fitting. Corvane acknowledged people the way he acknowledged weather: as conditions to be managed.

"Wells," he said. "Trees. Wine. Lame men walking." Now he looked up, and his eyes were bright with the particular hunger Ines had learned to dread. "Do you know what this is, Winterbourne?"

"An accounting problem," she said.

"A *market*." He straightened. "Unmediated supply. No guild controls the source, which means no guild sets the price, which means—" he smiled, and it was almost boyish, which made it worse, "—the greatest arbitrage opportunity in the history of the trade. If we can identify the supplier, we contract. We broker. We take commission on every answered prayer in Ashvale and half the coast besides. And you—" he tapped the map, right where the Vellum Guild's seal sat stamped over the city like a thumbprint, "—are the best reader of ledgers I have ever employed. Exiled or not. Find me the source, Winterbourne. Find me who's paying for all this."

"And if the answer is that no one's paying?"

"Then it's free money, and I'll love it even more."

"That's not how anything works," Ines said. "Not grief, not years, not doors, not grace. Something is being spent. Someone is being spent. And when the bill comes due, Guildmaster, it will come due against whoever's nearest, and I promise you the Guild's seal will not hold it off."

Corvane studied her for a long moment. There was something in his face she couldn't read — patience, maybe, or appetite, or the particular blankness of a man listening to a clock he intends to wind differently later.

"You always were a gloomy accountant," he said pleasantly. "Find me the source. You have until the week's end. Dismissed."

* * *

She went down to the vaults that night, ostensibly to check the climate wards on the bottled-grief racks, actually because the vaults were the only place in Ashvale where she could think without the sound of celebration bleeding through the windows.

The wolf was waiting for her among the shelves.

He sat in the aisle between Bottled Longing and Borrowed Years like he'd been catalogued there himself, silver-gray, enormous, utterly silent, his eyes catching the wardlight in a way eyes shouldn't.

"You felt it too," Ivo said. His voice was that old, layered voice, like wind through a canyon. "The city waking. Like sleepers turning over."

"The city isn't waking," Ines said. "It's being *spent*. Every answered prayer is a withdrawal, and no one's asking from whose account." She hugged her arms against the chill that lived in the vaults year-round. "Ivo. The page in the old ledger. My name, and the debts. What happens when old gods start paying out?"

He was quiet long enough that the silence developed texture.

"Then the debts are being called," he said finally. "All of them. Everywhere. And the ones who owe—" his gaze settled on her, patient and terrible, "—will be found."

Ines thought of the well on Tanner's Row, the cold green taste of miracle-water she'd never drunk but could imagine. Thought of the beggar walking, the widow's ring, the girl with the petal in her pocket. All of it bought on credit, all of it beautiful, all of it borrowed against a name written in a book she'd copied in secret because her mentor told her to burn it.

"There's something else," Ivo said, softer now. "Something you should see. Not here. Come to the roof at moonset, and bring nothing written down."

"Why not written down?"

"Because," said the wolf, "what I have to show you was erased from every map and ledger in the world. And erasure, little keeper, is the one debt the Guild cannot collect."

Above them, through the vault's high grating, the sky over Ashvale was clear and starry and smelled, unmistakably now, of coming rain.
