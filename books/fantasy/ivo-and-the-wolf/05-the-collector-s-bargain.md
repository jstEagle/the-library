## 5. The Collector's Bargain

The summons came at dawn, folded into the beak of a paper crow that dissolved the moment Ines touched it, leaving only the smell of hot wax and a single word in Guildmaster Corvane's hand: *Come.*

She had known it would come. For three days the city had been drowning in small miracles — wells that sang when you drew from them, orchards blooming out of season until the streets of Ashvale drifted with petals that smelled of honey and something older, something like rain on stone that had never been rained on. The Vellum Guild had posted runners at every corner, buying miracles by the ounce, sealing them into vellum packets the way one might pack saffron or grave-dirt. And Ines had spent those three days doing what she always did: keeping the ledgers. Recording. Measuring. Pretending her hands did not shake.

Now she climbed the spiral stair to Corvane's office above the Counting House, past shelves of bottled grief arranged by vintage, past the door to nowhere that breathed cold air against her ankle as she passed it, as it always did, like a dog hoping to be noticed.

Corvane was at his window.

He was not a large man. He had never needed to be. He stood the way a ledger stands — balanced, closed, every figure in its place — and he watched the city below with the expression of a merchant watching a market he intended to own before lunch. His coat was grey vellum-wool, his rings were plain iron, and his voice, when he finally spoke, was soft enough that Ines had to lean toward him to catch it. He knew this. He used it the way other men used shouting.

"You've seen the reports," he said. It was not a question. "Wells. Orchards. A woman on Candlemaker's Row whose dead husband sat up and asked for tea, then lay back down satisfied. Miracles, Winterbourne. Walking through my city like loose coin."

"I've seen them," Ines said.

"And?"

"And they're wrong." She heard her own voice come out flat and certain, and was briefly grateful for it. "Miracles have a texture, Guildmaster. I've catalogued enough of them. They feel like *answers* — warm, finished, sealed at both ends. These feel like questions being answered by something that's been waiting a very long time to be asked." She hesitated, then made herself say the rest. "They feel like debts being collected."

For a moment Corvane said nothing. Below the window, a flower-seller was weeping over roses that had opened overnight on bare branches, and a crowd had gathered to watch, and someone was already selling seats.

"Good," Corvane said. "That's exactly why I need you."

He turned from the window then, and crossed to his desk, and Ines saw what lay upon it: a contract. Not a guild contract — she knew those the way she knew her own handwriting, all clauses and counter-signatures and seals of red wax stamped with the Vellum sigil, an open book with a keyhole where its heart should be. This was older. The vellum was not white but the colour of old bone, and the ink was not black but a brown so dark it was nearly red, and there was no seal at all, because — she understood with a lurch — the contract had never been signed by anyone. It had simply been waiting.

"Three days ago," Corvane said, "a collector brought me a prayer scroll that answered itself. You logged it. Item four-one-one, shelf nine, under *anomalies.*"

"I remember."

"What you don't know is that since then, eleven more have come through our doors. Prayers written years ago, decades ago, some centuries — prayers addressed to gods whose names we stopped recording because recording them stopped making sense. And every one of them has been answered. Every one." He laid two fingers on the bone-coloured contract. "And every answer came with terms."

Ines looked at the contract and felt the room tilt very slightly, the way it did when she stood too long among the doors to nowhere. "What kind of terms?"

"The usual kind. Worship. Offering. Acknowledgment." Corvane smiled, and it was the worst thing Ines had seen all week, worse than the self-answering scroll, worse than the orchards blooming out of grief-season. "The guild is in the business of acknowledgment, Winterbourne. We are the largest broker of impossible things on this coast. If the old gods want their names spoken again, then by every law of trade, we should be the ones speaking them — for a commission."

"You want to sell miracles."

"I want to *distribute* them," Corvane corrected gently. "There's a difference. Distribution implies infrastructure. Contracts. Ledgers." His eyes found hers, mild and bottomless. "Ledgers, Winterbourne. Yours."

The word landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well. She thought of the page she had copied three nights ago, hidden now inside the false binding of a shipping manifest, her own name written in a ledger older than the guild itself: *Ines Winterbourne, debtor.* Debtor to whom, the page had not fully said. Debtor for what, it had only hinted, in that brown-black ink that was nearly the colour of dried blood. Halvard had told her to burn it. She had copied it instead, because dread and hunger, she was learning, lived in the same house and shared the same bed.

"And me?" she said carefully. "What exactly would I be brokering?"

"Terms." Corvane slid the contract across the desk toward her. "You have a gift for reading debt-clauses. Don't look surprised — I've read your marginalia. You see obligations the way other people see faces. I need someone to sit across from whatever is answering these prayers and negotiate rates. Percentage per miracle. Exclusivity clauses. Territory." He paused, delicately. "And I'm told the old gods respond well to people who already owe them something. Common ground. Rapport."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere below, the crowd around the flower-seller cheered, and the sound rose up through the floorboards like water rising in a hold.

"You know," Ines said.

It was not really a question either. Corvane's smile deepened by a single degree.

"I know everything that passes through this building," he said. "Including, eventually, everything about the people who work in it. Your exile record is exemplary, Winterbourne. Immaculate, even. Almost too immaculate. There's a version of the storm-night account in the restricted archive that differs from the public one in several interesting particulars." He straightened a pen-tray that did not need straightening. "I have no interest in that version. Unless, of course, circumstances change."

*Unless you refuse me,* the silence underneath his voice said.

Ines looked down at the bone-coloured contract. Up close she could see that the writing moved — slowly, the way frost moves across a window, so that if you blinked you lost the change. Clause by clause, it was rewriting itself to fit whoever read it. That was how the old gods negotiated, she thought. Not with terms but with mirrors.

And beneath the fear, beneath the stone-cold weight of Corvane's knowledge of her, something else stirred — something that had been stirring for three days, since the first report of the singing wells. Anger. Not the hot kind; she had burned that out of herself years ago, or believed she had. This was the cold kind, the ledger-keeper's kind, the anger of a woman who has spent seven years recording other people's impossible things and has just been handed one too many.

"You're asking me to sell answers," she said, "from gods who are returning from extinction, to a city that forgot them on purpose. On commission."

"At a very competitive rate."

"And when the answers stop being wells and orchards? When someone prays for something cruel, and the thing that's listening decides to grant it?" She tapped the moving clause with one finger, and the frost-writing flinched away from her touch, which she chose not to think about. "You can't put exclusivity clauses on hunger, Guildmaster. You can't negotiate territory with something that remembers owning all of it."

"No," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "But we can try. And if we fail, we fail profitably, which is more than most enterprises manage." He leaned forward, and for the first time his softness dropped away entirely, and what was underneath was not cruelty, which would at least have been honest, but arithmetic. "Refuse, Winterbourne, and I release the restricted record. The true one. Do you know what happens to a storm-caller convicted of calling a storm that killed forty-one people — including, I believe, a child — after her exile has already been served?" He let the pause stretch. "Exile again. Permanently, this time. Or worse. The guild has friends in the Magistracy who owe us favours, and favours, unlike miracles, never run out."

The words settled over her like snowfall, patient, certain of their own weight.

Ines thought of the false binding. Of the copied page sleeping inside it. Of a wolf-shaped absence she had not yet named, pacing somewhere in the vaults of her mind since the night the prayer scroll answered itself — though that was nonsense, she had never met any wolf, she only dreamed of one, had dreamed of one for years, amber-eyed, patient, waiting at the edge of a country she could not find on any map.

She thought of forty-one people she had never killed, and one child, and a lie so old it had grown roots and started bearing fruit in restricted archives.

"May I take the contract to study?" she asked.

Corvane's eyebrows rose, genuinely pleased. "I hoped you'd say that."

"I haven't agreed," Ines said. "I'm a ledger-keeper. I don't sign anything I haven't read to the last clause. Even contracts that rewrite themselves." She lifted the bone-vellum carefully, the way one lifts a sleeping animal that may yet bite. "Give me until tomorrow's bell."

"Until tomorrow's bell," Corvane agreed, and turned back to his window, dismissal complete, already counting his commission on miracles not yet sold.

* * *

She did not go back to her ledgers.

She took the stairs down instead, past the bottled grief, past the breathing door to nowhere, out into the morning street where the petals were still falling off trees that should have been bare, and walked without deciding to walk until she reached the river, and the river-smell, and the grey light coming off the water like a held breath.

Cornered. That was the word. She turned it over as she walked, testing its edges the way she tested coins. Corvane had built the corner carefully: broker the gods' return and become the guild's instrument in a trade she could feel, in her ledger-keeper's bones, would end badly for everyone who wasn't holding the pen — or refuse, and lose the fragile legal fiction that stood between her and the gallows' gentler cousin, permanent exile, or the Magistracy's less gentle one.

Except.

Except that a lie was still a lie, even archived. Even restricted. Even weaponized. Seven years she had carried the exile like a stone sewn into her coat, telling herself it was justice she hadn't earned, punishment for a crime she couldn't remember committing — and some stubborn, meticulous, unforgiving part of her had never once believed her own guilt, and had kept the books on that too, in the private ledger behind her ribs, marked *unresolved.*

Corvane thought he was threatening her with the truth. But what if the truth was the one thing she wanted more than safety?

She stopped at the river rail. Below her, the water moved, and for half a heartbeat — a trick of the light, surely, a trick of these strange miraculous days — the current seemed to flow the wrong way, upstream, toward some country the maps had forgotten, carrying petals with it. Petals that smelled of honey and rain on ancient stone.

*Debtor,* the old page had said. *Owed.*

"Well," Ines Winterbourne said aloud, to the river, to the petals, to whatever old and patient thing was listening now that the old gods had their ears back, "you have me at a disadvantage. Apparently I owe you. So let's discuss terms."

The water kept flowing the wrong way. And far downstream — or perhaps far *elsewhere*, in a direction rivers do not normally go — she thought she heard, very faintly, a sound like a wolf laughing.

Behind her, back toward the Counting House, a bell began to ring. Not tomorrow's bell. Not any scheduled bell. The alarm-bell, high and thin and urgent, and beneath it, rising from the direction of the vaults, shouting — collectors' voices, many of them, and one voice that was not a collector's at all, low and old and unmistakably amused, saying words she could not make out but somehow, impossibly, recognized.

Something in the vaults had woken up. Something with amber eyes.

Ines ran.
