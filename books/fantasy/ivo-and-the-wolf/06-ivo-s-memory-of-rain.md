## 6. Ivo's Memory of Rain

I have been asked, in the long centuries of my listening, to describe many things. The taste of a prayer before it is spoken. The weight of a name when it is set down in ink by someone who means it. The sound a country makes as it is unwritten — which is not silence at all, but a tearing, like wet vellum.

No one has ever asked me to remember rain.

Perhaps that is because everyone believes rain is simple. It falls; it wets; it ends. But I was there on the night the rain came to Ashvale like a verdict, and I will tell you now what I told her, sitting in the dark of the counting house with the ledgers shut and the lamps turned low, while she held her own copied page in both hands as though it might bite her.

I will tell you about the rain, Ines Winterbourne. And you will understand why they made you carry it.

* * *

She had asked him plainly, the way she did everything — no pleading, no ceremony, just the flat demand of a woman who kept accounts and wanted hers balanced. *Tell me what happened that night.*

Ivo had turned his great grey head toward the window, where Ashvale's rooftops cut the sky into crooked pieces, and for a long moment he said nothing at all. She had learned already that his silences were not empty. They were full, the way a well is full, and if you waited, something would rise up out of them cold and clear.

"You were nineteen," he said at last. "You had a chipped tooth on the left side of your mouth, from falling out of a plum tree you had climbed because your mother told you not to. You bit your lip when you concentrated. You wore your hair braided with blue thread because blue was the color of the storm-sky over Vel Sarenne, and you thought — wrongly — that no one noticed."

Ines's hands went still on the table. "I don't remember any of that."

"No," Ivo said gently. "Exile takes the small things first. It eats inward, like rot in timber. The big things it leaves standing so you will keep grieving them." He rose, padded once around the room — a habit she had come to recognize, the circling of something that had once marked territories the size of horizons — and settled again near the cold hearth. "So let me give you back the small things. They are the proof. The big things you already know, even if you do not know you know them."

The lamp guttered. Outside, the city went on with its ordinary noises: cart wheels, a drunk singing three streets over, the guild bell tolling the hour of ledgers-closed. Inside, the wolf began to speak, and the room grew older around his voice.

* * *

I knew your mother before I knew you.

That is how it must begin, because that is how it happened. Sera Winterbourne came to the border of my ground on a night much like this one, carrying a storm-token and a grievance, and she did not flinch when I stepped out of the treeline wearing my true shape — which, I will have you know, was considerably more impressive than this diminished thing you see before you. Gods are larger in their own country. Everything is larger in its own country. That is rather the point of having one.

Your mother was a storm-keeper. Not a caller — a keeper. There is a difference, though the guild's ledgers would never record it, because the guild has never understood anything it could not put a price on. A caller seizes the sky and bends it. A keeper *tends* it. Vel Sarenne's storms were not weather, Ines. They were memory. Rain fell on our country the way ink falls on a page, and every drop carried something the land needed to hold onto — griefs too heavy for the living, joys the dead had left behind, oaths sworn under old stars. Your mother walked the ridgelines with her token and she *kept* all of it. She was, in the oldest sense of the word, a librarian of the sky.

You see why I liked her immediately. I have always had a weakness for people who keep records.

And you — I met you when you were six years old, chasing a dog through my birch trees, and you looked up at me — at all of me, antler-shadow and winter-eyes and the smell of deep snow — and you said, "You're in my mother's ledger. Entry forty-one. You owe us one spring."

I did owe them one spring. I had owed it for four hundred years, and no one had ever dared collect. This small furious child stood in front of me with mud to her knees and demanded payment.

I paid it, of course. I made the thaw come early that year, and your mother laughed until she cried, and after that I was — whatever a god can be to a family. A fixture. A rumor with a fondness for their hearth. When you were twelve you left offerings that were frankly insulting — a button, half an apple, a drawing of me that made me look like an angry turnip — and I treasured every one, because they were given without fear, and fear is the only coin most worshippers ever have.

That was Vel Sarenne, Ines. That was what was taken from the world. Not a country. A *relationship*. The old gods were not distant things to be petitioned through priests and intermediaries. We lived in the hedges. We drank from your wells. We argued with your grandmothers about the proper way to salt fish. And when Mavros decided that such intimacy was beneath him — that worship should be *harvested*, not shared — he did not merely kill us. He killed the way of being known.

But I am getting ahead of the rain. Forgive me. Memory is a marsh; you cannot walk it in a straight line.

* * *

"The night of the storm," Ines said quietly. Her voice had changed. He heard it — the careful flattening of a woman holding something down. "Tell me about the night of the storm."

"Ah," said Ivo. "Yes. The night of the storm."

He looked at her for a long time. Then he lay down, folding his paws, the posture of a creature making itself small before a hard truth, and began.

* * *

Mavros came to Vel Sarenne in autumn, wearing the body of a pilgrim, and we let him in because we were gods who trusted our own borders.

Understand what he was. You have seen him since, or you will — the beautiful one, the reasonable one, the one whose offers always cost exactly what you cannot spare. But then he was younger, and crueler in the way of young things, which is to say efficiently. He had watched the other countries' gods grow fat on distance and doctrine, worshipped through glass and gold, and he had concluded that closeness was a disease. That a god who lived in a hedge could be ignored by a child with a stick, and a god who lived in heaven could never be ignored at all.

He came to convert us. When we refused — and we refused, Ines; I want you to know that we refused, all of us, even the small river-gods, even the ones who had grown strange and shy in the deep hills — he came back with something else. Not an army. Armies leave traces. He came back with a *contract*, written in a hand you would recognize, because its descendants still write for the Vellum Guild. A document that unmade names. The same craft that later erased the whole country, tested first on smaller things.

On gods.

He took the river-god Vess first, at the ford below your village. I felt it happen forty miles away — felt Vess go out like a candle pinched between wet fingers, and worse than the going-out was the *after*: the river kept flowing, but nothing lived in it anymore. Water without a god in it is only wet. The fish stayed. The reeds stayed. The *Vessness* of it was gone, and the people who drank from that water forgot, within a week, that it had ever had a name.

One by one. That was how he worked. One by one, and each time he used the storm-season as his cover, because storms were ours, and a death inside a storm looks like weather.

Until the last night. Until the ridge above your village, where your mother had gone to keep the autumn gales, as she did every year, walking the high paths with her token raised and her braid whipping in the wind. You were supposed to be asleep. You were nineteen and you had never once done what you were supposed to do, and so instead you followed her, because you had begun keeping the eastern stretches yourself that year and you would not let her walk alone.

I was there too. Remember that. Whatever else is true, I was there.

Mavros came for her at the crest of the ridge, in the black heart of the storm, and your mother — five feet two inches of stubborn librarian — stood between him and the token-stone and told him no. She told him no in the old tongue, with the whole gathered memory of the sky behind her voice, and for one moment, one shining moment, he actually stepped back.

Then he called the guild's scribes out of the dark, and they began to write her name into the unmaking contract, and she saw what they were doing, and she did the only thing left.

She called the storm down on all of us.

Not to harm. To *hide*. Do you understand? Every death Mavros had dealt had been hidden inside weather, because weather conceals, weather blurs, weather gives the scribes their shadow to work in. So she seized the one weapon they were using against her and turned it into a shroud — pulled the entire storm down onto the ridge, wind and thunder and rain like the sky coming apart, so dense and wild that no contract could be read aloud, no name could be spoken clearly enough to be unwritten. She bought herself minutes with the whole fury of Vel Sarenne's remembered sky.

It should have been enough. With minutes, I could have reached her. I was running — I was running faster than I have ever run, through rain that hit like thrown stones, and I want you to know that I was running, Ines, I have carried that for eleven years, that I was *running* —

"But you didn't reach her," Ines said. Her voice was very quiet. Her hands were white on the edge of the table.

"No," Ivo said. "Because Mavros had planned for me. He had planned for all of us. The storm she called — it needed a keeper's blood to sustain it, and she was spending herself to hold it, and when she faltered—" His jaw tightened. Even now, even in this diminished shape, the sound that came out of him was not entirely a wolf's. "When she faltered, the scribes finished the line. And here is the cruelty of it, the exquisite, engineered cruelty: the contract required a scapegoat. An unmaking leaves a hole in the world's memory, and holes must be filled or the world notices. Someone had to be recorded as the cause. Someone human, someone present, someone whose guilt would explain the storm and the death and the grief in a shape the surviving world could swallow."

The lamp flame bent, though there was no draft.

"They wrote your name," Ivo said. "They wrote that Ines Winterbourne, daughter of Sera, called the storm that killed her mother and broke the old covenant, and that for this crime she was exiled beyond the borders of — and here the scribes paused, because Mavros had decided the country itself was next — beyond the borders of *the region formerly known as* —"

He stopped. Ines realized she was standing. She did not remember standing. Her chair was tipped over behind her and she was gripping the table's edge and the copied page was crushed in her fist, and tears were running down her face without her permission, silently, the way rain comes through a roof you thought was sound.

"They erased her twice," she whispered. "Her name in the contract. Her country in the exile record. They wrote me out of the world and her into nothing, in the same sentence."

"Yes."

"And everyone believed it. Halvard believed it. The guild believed it. *I* believed it." She laughed, and it was the worst sound Ivo had ever heard, worse than the tearing of a country, because it was the laugh of a woman watching eleven years of shame rearrange themselves into evidence. "I mourned her and hated myself in the same breath for eleven years. I catalogued bottled grief by the jarful and never once wondered why mine fit the inventory so well."

"I know," Ivo said softly. "I watched you do it. From outside the walls, across the years. Do you know what it is to be a god with no country, Ines? It is to be a guest everywhere and at home nowhere, haunting the edges of the world that unmade you, watching the one living soul who still owes you a spring grow old in exile for a crime committed *against* her." He rose and crossed the floor, and pressed his forehead, briefly, against her hand — the gesture of a god to a keeper, old as the old hills. "I could not stop it. I could not even testify. Who would take the word of a wolf? What court hears a god whose nation no longer exists?"

"Then there is no law for this," Ines said. Her tears had stopped. Something else had arrived in their place, and Ivo, who had watched empires be born and die in the space of a single expression, recognized it and loved it and feared it a little: the stillness at the center of a storm.

"No," he said. "There is no law for this. No court, no guild, no throne. Mavros saw to that. He built himself a world with no room in it for justice — only contracts, only prices, only debts that run one way."

"Then we go outside the law."

"We go outside the law," Ivo agreed. "All the way outside. Past the maps. Past the memory. There is a door, Ines — a door out of the country that isn't, and your debt-page is the key to finding it, and on the other side of that door there are witnesses. Living and dead. Old gods and old stones and the rain itself, which remembers everything your mother ever poured into it. Justice does not need a court. It needs only a place to stand."

Outside, far off over the rooftops of Ashvale, thunder rolled — soft, directionless, ordinary thunder, the kind that meant nothing.

Except that Ines lifted her head toward it, and Ivo saw her listen the way her mother used to listen, head tilted, eyes half-closed, a keeper hearing a voice in the noise.

"Ines," he said carefully. "What is it?"

"That thunder," she said slowly. "It's wrong. It's *answering* me." She looked down at the crushed page in her fist, at her own name written in a dead scribe's hand, debtor to gods thought extinct. "Ivo — if the old gods are waking, if prayers are being answered all over this city — then somewhere out there, someone is collecting on those debts. And I want to know why mine is the only one anyone's afraid to call due."

Ivo's ears flattened. Far to the south, past the salt marshes, past the borders that were not borders anymore, something vast shifted in its sleep — and smiled.

"Then tomorrow," he said, "we find out what the gods owe."
