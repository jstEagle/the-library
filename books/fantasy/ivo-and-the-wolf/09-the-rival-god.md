## 9. The Rival God

Mavros had forgotten what it felt like to be full.

For three hundred years he had lived on scraps — the dregs of fear from a city that no longer remembered why it feared, the thin gruel of superstition, the occasional fever-dream of a dying man that tasted of copper and salt. He had worn a hundred shapes in that time: a beggar with beautiful hands, a moneylender whose ledgers never balanced but never accused him, once, for eleven years, a statue of a saint in the Cathedral of the Ninefold Dawn, watching worshippers kneel to a god who did not exist while he drank their kneeling like wine.

But this — *this* —

He sat in the bell tower of the abandoned chapel on Candlemaker's Row and let it pour into him.

Below, Ashvale was answering its own prayers. A well in the Tanner's Quarter that had run bitter for a generation now ran sweet; three hundred people had drunk from it that morning, and each of them had whispered *thank you* to whatever was listening, and each whisper had fallen into Mavros's mouth like honey off the comb. An orchard behind the tannery — dead since the Blight Years, its trees nothing but gray fingers — had burst into blossom overnight, out of season, absurd, glorious. The blossoms were white as bone and smelled of nothing at all, which should have been the first warning anyone asked for. No one asked. They only stood beneath the branches with their faces turned up, weeping, praying harder.

*More,* they prayed. *Please. More.*

And Mavros, who had been starving so long he had forgotten his own name meant *darkness*, opened his hands and gave it to them.

It cost him almost nothing. That was the beauty of it — the obscene, delicious beauty. The old gods of Vel Sarenne had spent themselves like spendthrifts, pouring power into rain and harvest and the turning of seasons until the land itself ran dry and the door could be sealed and the country erased. Mavros had watched them do it. He had helped them do it. And when the moment came — when the sky over Vel Sarenne tore open with a storm that was not weather, when the gods gathered at the threshold to make their last payment — Mavros had looked at the price written in the ledger of heaven and done the arithmetic every god eventually does.

He had stepped back. He had told them where the hunters would come from. He had held the door's hinge closed with one hand while his brothers and sisters poured through it into oblivion, and he had kept the other hand free.

Survival, he had learned, was simply generosity practiced by someone else.

Now the prayers came up to him like smoke from ten thousand chimneys, and he grew fat on them, and still he was hungry, because answered prayer is a loan and not a gift, and every soul in Ashvale who whispered *thank you* was signing something without reading it.

He heard them all. The fishwife praying her husband's boat would hold. The child praying her fevered brother whole. The old clerk praying, with the particular shame of the pragmatic, that his wife would stop asking where he went on Thursdays. Each prayer arrived wrapped in its own flavor — hope tasted of green apples, desperation of iron, gratitude of warm bread — and Mavros sorted them as he had once sorted souls, by vintage and body.

One flavor he had not tasted in three hundred years.

Storm-calling. Faint, buried under exile and guild-ink and self-deception, but unmistakable: the taste of Vel Sarenne's sky, which had been the finest vintage in creation, thunder aged in mountain cold, lightning with a finish like struck flint. It came from the Vellum Guild's counting house, from a woman who catalogued bottled grief and believed herself powerless.

Mavros smiled, and the candles in the chapel guttered though there was no wind.

*Ivo is awake,* he thought, tasting the second flavor beneath the first — wolf-smoke, old ground, divinity gone feral and lean. *Of course he is. Of course he found her.*

The last god of Vel Sarenne besides himself, the hound of heaven, the one who had chased him across the burning border while the country folded itself up like a letter no one would ever read again. Ivo had sworn an oath at the threshold, Mavros remembered — had howled it into the closing dark with his throat torn and his godhood bleeding out of him. *I will find the debt. I will find the debtor. I will open what you closed.*

Three hundred years, and the fool had kept the oath. Gods were like that. It was why they died.

Mavros rose, and his shape rose with him, assembling itself out of shadow and borrowed reverence: tall, gray-robed, faceless except when he chose otherwise, and tonight he chose the face of a kind uncle, a patient teacher, someone you would tell your troubles to in a confessional or across a kitchen table. He had worn kinder faces than his true one for so long that kindness fit him like a glove fits a hand — concealing everything, changing nothing.

Below him, Ashvale glowed with small miracles, and every miracle was a thread, and every thread ran up to his fist.

*Let Ivo find his debtor,* Mavros thought. *Let him bring her to the door. Doors need two things to open: a key and a reason. I will take away the reason, and keep the key, and lock the past so deep that even memory cannot dig it up.*

He stepped off the bell tower into the air, and the air, being newly devout, bore him gently down toward the counting house, where a woman with storm in her blood was working late over ledgers she did not yet know were about her.

* * *

Ines knew someone was in the archive before she saw him, because the candles stopped flickering.

That was the wrong way around. Candles flickered when drafts came through, when doors opened, when the world intruded. These flames had gone perfectly, terribly still, each one standing upright like a soldier at attention, and Ines set down her pen with the deliberate slowness of a woman who has learned that sudden movements invite sudden consequences.

"You work late," said a voice like warm oil poured over cool stone.

He stood between the shelves of bottled grief, and the bottles leaned toward him. She noticed that first — the way the grief tilted in its glass, straining like plants toward light. He was tall and gray-robed and his face was the most reassuring face she had ever seen, the kind of face that made you want to confess things, and she hated it instantly and completely, because she had grown up in a country that no longer existed and some part of her, deeper than memory, knew what gods looked like when they wanted something.

"I'm cataloguing," Ines said. "This floor is guild property. Visitors sign the ledger."

"Ah." He touched one bottle — a widow's grief, vintage four years past, purchased from a debtor in the Salt Quarter — and the grief inside went quiet, the way a dog goes quiet when a stranger shows its teeth too calmly. "Ledgers. Yes. You love them, don't you? Little books where everything balances. Where every debt is written down and therefore survivable." He drifted closer. "I have known ledger-keepers before. In another life. They believed the same thing: that if you write it all down, the truth becomes manageable. Ink is such a brave little wall."

"Who are you?" Her voice held. She was proud of that.

"They called me many things, before the calling stopped." He smiled, and the smile was kind, and the kindness was the worst thing she had ever seen. "You may call me Mavros. Your friend the wolf certainly does. Though I suspect he uses other words. *Traitor.* *Thrice-burned.* That sort of vocabulary. Wolves are sentimental."

Her heart was doing something complicated against her ribs. "You know Ivo."

"I knew everyone in Vel Sarenne." He said the name lightly, carelessly, the way one says the name of a town one has driven through. "I knew your mother, too, though she'd be mortified to hear it. Storm-callers were always so serious. So certain the sky owed them honesty." His faceless kindness tilted. "How is your calling these days, Ines Winterbourne? Since the exile? Since the trial where they took your name and your sky and gave you a ledger pen instead?"

"I don't call storms."

"No," he agreed softly. "You don't. Isn't that interesting. You reach for it sometimes — I've watched you, these past weeks, since the prayers started answering. When the thunder rolls over Ashvale you put your hand flat on the table like a woman steadying herself on a ship's rail. Like a sailor who's been told the sea is forbidden to her." He came closer still, and the candlelight bent around him rather than touching him. "I can give it back."

The words landed in the silent room like a coin dropped down a well.

"That's not yours to give," Ines said.

"Nothing is anyone's to give. That's the secret nobody tells you." He gestured, and the window behind her filled — not with the street below but with images, moving like reflections on water: the sweet well, the blooming orchard, a lame man dancing in the Tanner's Square, a mother holding a child whose fever had broken at dawn. "I am giving freely, all across this city. Do you know why? Because giving is how you buy. Every answered prayer is a hook with the barb filed smooth. They feel only the sweetness. They never feel the line." He lowered his voice, confiding, intimate, the voice of a man sharing a financial tip. "Your guild sells impossibilities at ruinous markup. I undercut them. My prices are simple: remember less, receive more. Give me your memories of home, and I'll give you miracles. Most people's homes aren't worth much anyway. Nostalgia is a bad investment."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you refuse, and I admire you, and I go on being generous elsewhere." He spread his hands. "I am not Corvane. I don't send collectors. I only ever offer. That is my nature — I was the god of fair bargains, once, in a country that punished us for it. Fair bargains! Can you imagine a more dangerous doctrine? We taught people to read before they signed." Something moved behind the kind face, briefly — something old and wounded and furious — and was gone. "They erased us for teaching arithmetic, Ines. Your guild erases people for arithmetic every day and calls it commerce."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because I want to offer *you* a bargain, and unlike Corvane, I show you the terms first." He drew from his sleeve a page — vellum, ancient, ink the color of dried blood — and laid it on her desk beside her neat columns. She knew it before she read it. She had copied it once, in Halvard's presence, with her hands shaking. Her own name, in the pre-exile hand, listed among debts owed to extinct gods.

DEBTOR: WINTERBOURNE, INES.
OBLIGATION: THE DOOR. ONE NAME RESTORED. ONE LIE UNMADE. ONE POWER RELINQUISHED.

"The door out of Vel Sarenne," Mavros said. "Still real. Still waiting. Your wolf-god has told you as much, hasn't he? Told you that you're the key. Told you that if you pay — your name, your lie, your power — the door opens, and the country comes back, and everyone who was scattered gets to go home." He let the vision of it hang in the air between them, gorgeous and terrible: a nation unfolding from erasure, borders drawn back onto maps, the dead gods' names spoken aloud again. "It's a beautiful story. He believes it. He's spent three centuries believing it, poor loyal thing."

"But?"

"But consider the price." He tapped the page, once. "*A power relinquished.* Your storm-calling, gone forever — and it isn't even yours, did he tell you that part? It belongs to the sky of a dead country. You'd be paying with stolen goods. *A lie unmade* — meaning you stand before witnesses and declare your exile false, which makes you a liar in the eyes of every court and guild from here to the coast, an outlaw twice over, hunted by men like Corvane who have long memories and longer contracts. And *a name restored* — restored to a country that no longer exists, in a language no one living speaks, witnessed by gods who are mostly dead and one who is me." He smiled again. "You would give up everything you have clawed together — your position, your craft, your careful little balanced life — for a homeland made entirely of promises. And at the end of it, Ines, at the very end, you would be powerless and landless and named, standing in a field that may or may not remember you."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above, thunder muttered over Ashvale, and Ines's hand, traitorously, pressed flat against the desk.

"And your alternative?" she asked.

"To help me seal it instead." He said it gently, the way a surgeon says *it will only hurt for a moment*. "One signature. One clause added to one page — *debt discharged, door sealed, in perpetuity.* No exile to undo, because there will be nothing left to return to. No lie to unmake, because the lie becomes the truth by default. And your power—" his voice went soft as ash falling, "—your power I will return to you myself. Freely. Fully. Not lent by any dead country's sky. Yours, Ines Winterbourne, storm-caller, for the rest of your life. You could stand on the cliffs above Ashvale and pull lightning down like drawing water from a well. You would never again press your hand flat on a table to steady yourself."

She stared at him. The candles burned straight and motionless. The grief in the bottles had all turned toward him like flowers toward a sun that gave no warmth.

"You'd give me back what was taken," she said slowly, "if I help you make sure no one else ever gets back what was taken from them."

"I'd give you back what was *yours*," Mavros corrected, "in exchange for leaving buried what was *everyone's*. Yes. I see how you'd phrase it. You have a ledger-keeper's conscience — it will trouble you. Let it trouble you for a night. But ask yourself, when you're lying awake: who actually benefits from opening that door? The dead gods, who cannot return. The scattered exiles, who have made lives elsewhere, who remember a country that has become a story, and stories improve with distance. Or you — who get your sky back, your self back, your *power* back, without paying a copper of the true price." He straightened, and for just a moment the kind face seemed to sit slightly wrong on the skull beneath it, like a mask hung a half-inch crooked. "I survived the fall of a nation by understanding one thing, Ines: resurrection is expensive, and the ones selling it always leave out the shipping costs. I am offering you the honest version. Keep what's yours. Bury what's dead. Walk away whole."

"And Ivo?"

Something flickered across the face — quick, involuntary, and for the first time she saw the true thing underneath: hatred, old and polished smooth as river stone.

"Ivo," Mavros said, "is a dog who has been digging at a grave for three hundred years. Dogs that dig must eventually be put down, or given a bone. If you seal the door, I will give him the only mercy left: I will let him forget. He'll be an ordinary wolf. He'll sleep in the sun. He'll follow you home, if you like, and never know what he lost." The kindness returned, seamless. "Everyone keeps something, Ines. Even the loyal. Especially the loyal."

He was moving toward the window now, his robe already dissolving into the ordinary dark of the street below, and Ines found her voice one last time.

"Why me?" she asked. "Why does my name carry this debt? What did my family *do*?"

Mavros paused, half-shadow, half-man, and looked back at her with an expression she would turn over for many nights afterward — pity, genuine pity, from a thing that had betrayed a nation.

"Wrong question," he said. "Ask instead what your family was *owed*. Ask why a storm was needed that night, and who ordered it, and what it was hiding. Your wolf knows. He'll tell you, if you let him — and then you'll understand why sealing the door is the only mercy left for anyone." He stepped backward into the dark, and the dark accepted him like water accepting rain. "You have until the collectors come. Corvane moves quickly when he smells profit, and I have made him smell a great deal of profit lately. Choose, Ines Winterbourne. Whole, or named. I sincerely hope — and I almost mean it — that you choose yourself."

Then he was gone, and the candles remembered how to flicker, and Ines stood alone in the archive with her neat columns and her shaking hands and the ancient page bearing her name, listening to thunder roll over a city full of strangers' answered prayers.

She looked down at the ledger open before her — the guild's ledger, Corvane's ledger, the book where everything balanced — and then at the older page, the debt, the door.

*A power relinquished.*

Slowly, deliberately, she reached out and pressed her palm flat against the desk, the way she always did when the sky spoke, and for the first time since her exile she did not apologize for it.

Outside, the storm was building. And somewhere below, in the vaults, a wolf lifted his head and began, quietly, to growl.
