## 10. Flight Through the Salt Marshes

### Ines

They left Ashvale before the bells rang terce, when the fog still owned the streets and the guild's collectors were, if Ines was lucky, still asleep in their rented rooms above the Saltcellar. She had packed nothing but what a ledger-keeper could carry: three books, a penknife with a broken tip, a change of clothes rolled tight as a scroll, and the page that had started all of it — her own name in faded iron-gall ink, debtor to gods who were supposed to be dead.

The page rode against her ribs now, wrapped in oilcloth, warm from her body as though it had a pulse of its own.

"You're limping," Ivo said.

"I'm walking quickly."

"Wolves know the difference." He fell into step beside her, gray as the fog itself, so ordinary-looking that a milk-cart driver passed them without a second glance. Only his eyes gave him away — amber, patient, and old in a way that made Ines's skin prickle when she caught them directly. "Your left knee has been complaining since the bridge over the Mirefen cut. You favor it by half an inch."

"My left knee," she said, "is none of your business."

"Everything about you is my business. It's been my business for longer than you've been keeping ledgers." There was no flirtation in it, which somehow made it worse — or better. He said it the way he said everything, like a fact already entered in a book she hadn't been allowed to read.

They went out through the tannery gate, where the watchman owed Halvard a favor and Halvard had spent that favor on Ines without telling her. The man simply looked at her, looked at the wolf, and lifted the bar. "Never saw you," he said to the fog. "Neither did it."

Beyond the walls, Ashvale ended the way all cities end: badly, in a sprawl of middens and market gardens, and then not at all, in the wide gray breathing country of the salt marshes.

Ines stopped at the edge of the first causeway and looked out at it. The marsh stretched to the horizon like a ledger with no entries — flat, pale, waiting. Channels of brackish water stitched through the samphire and mud in silver seams. Somewhere out there, a curlew cried, long and descending, like a question no one intended to answer.

"The road south goes around the marsh," she said. "Three days' detour through Harrow Fen, where there are inns and roads and people who will sell us bread instead of drowning us."

"The road south is watched. Corvane's collectors travel fast when they think they know where you'll be." Ivo stepped onto the causeway, his paws finding the stones with a certainty that seemed almost insolent. "And we are not going south, Ines. We are going *through*."

"Through the marsh."

"Through the place where Vel Sarenne bleeds through." He glanced back at her, and for a moment the fog seemed to bend toward him, listening. "You wanted geography for your exile. Here it is. Under our feet."

She followed him because the alternative was standing alone at the edge of a city that wanted her dead or owned, and because — though she would never have written this in any ledger — following him felt less like obedience than like remembering something.

* * *

### Ivo

He had forgotten how much ground could hurt.

Not the way teeth hurt, or snares — those were clean pains, honest ones. This was older. Every mile they put between themselves and the coast, every mile deeper into the drowned lands, Ivo felt himself thinning, the way a voice thins across a valley. His godhood had been bound to Vel Sarenne when the country fell; whatever remained of him lived in the places where the erased land leaked back into the world, and the marshes were one great wound through which it leaked freely.

Which meant the marsh should have fed him. Instead it starved him, slowly, the way a memory starves — each step giving him something and taking more.

He did not tell Ines this. She had enough arithmetic to carry.

Instead he listened to her breathe behind him, counted her footfalls the way he had once counted prayers, and found, to his surprise, that the counting steadied him. A thousand years of worship and he had never once been *accompanied*. Prayers came to him like debts; pilgrims came like customers. Ines walked beside him complaining about her knee and the damp and the state of her boots, and every complaint was a small miracle he had no name for.

"The water's rising on the causeway," she said, an hour past dawn.

"It does that. The tide thinks for itself here."

"That's not reassuring."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be true." He paused where the stone path dipped beneath a channel of green-gray water, and watched her assess it — the quick flick of her eyes measuring depth, current, the weight of her pack, the cost. She thought in costs. He was beginning to understand that this was not coldness in her; it was how she kept herself alive. Every entry balanced. Every debt acknowledged. A girl who had been exiled for a crime she didn't commit had learned never to let anyone else do her accounting.

"There's a crossing two hundred paces east," he said. "Older stones. Vel Sarenne built them; they haven't sunk yet."

"How can you tell?"

"Because they're straight." He looked at her until she understood, and then he watched her look out at the marsh and see it differently — not emptiness but architecture, not waste but a country holding its breath under the water. Her lips parted slightly. For a moment she stood very still, and Ivo thought: *there she is. Whoever she was before the exile, there she is.*

"Ines," he said quietly. "What is it?"

"The birds," she said. "Listen."

He listened. Beneath the curlew's crying, beneath the wind, there was another sound — faint, rhythmic, rising off the water like heat off a road. Singing. Dozens of voices, wordless, in a mode that made his ancient bones ache with recognition.

A work song. A harvest song. Sung by no throats at all.

"They're not haunting us," Ines whispered. "They're *working*. The marsh is full of them. People who aren't here anymore, doing what they always did."

"Vel Sarenne doesn't haunt," Ivo said. "It persists. There's a difference, and the difference matters." He began walking east along the channel, and after a moment her footsteps resumed behind his, close enough that he could feel her hesitation like warmth on his flank. "Hauntings want something from you. Persistence only wants to be remembered."

"And which are you?"

He nearly laughed. "Ask me again when we're through the marsh."

* * *

### Ines

By midday the fog had burned off into a hard white glare, and Ines had learned three things about the salt marshes.

First: the water lied. Channels that looked ankle-deep swallowed to the waist; mud that looked solid bore her weight like a floor, then like a mouth. She learned to trust Ivo's line across the flats absolutely, placing her boots exactly where his paws had pressed.

Second: the singing got louder whenever she stopped moving. As though the marsh noticed her. As though it were checking her face against some list.

Third: Ivo was getting worse.

She couldn't have said how she knew. His gait was even, his ears sharp, his answers as dry as ever. But there was a translucence to him now, especially at the edges — when he crossed in front of the sun she could see the light through the fur of his shoulders, thin as paper. And twice, when he stepped over a channel, his hind paw came down a heartbeat late, as if it had to be remembered before it could be used.

At noon they sheltered in the lee of a drowned chapel — just a gable and one arch rising from the mud, its bell long gone, its stone furred with samphire. Ines ate hard bread and dried fish and pretended not to watch him lie down with his head on his paws.

"You're fading," she said finally, because she was bad at pretending.

"I'm spending," he corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference matters." He said it gently, borrowing her own cadence, and she hated him a little for it. "Every league from my old ground costs me. When we reach the heart of what's left of Vel Sarenne, I'll stop paying. Until then—" He shrugged, a gesture absurdly human in a wolf's body. "Until then I am somewhat more theoretical than usual."

"Theoretical gods can't fight collectors."

"No," he agreed. "But theoretical gods can't be captured either. There's a symmetry there that I find comforting and you clearly don't."

She threw a crust of bread at him. He caught it out of the air with a delicacy that was frankly showing off, and ate it, and something in her chest loosened that she refused to examine.

While he rested, she unwrapped the ledger-page and read it again by the flat white light. Her own name. *Ines Winterbourne.* Below it, in the old script the guild called First Hand, the debt-clause she still couldn't fully parse: words like *threshold*, *in full*, *name restored*, *lie unmade*. And below that, in a different ink entirely — darker, almost brown — a single line she had not been able to read the last four times she'd tried, and which resolved suddenly now, in the marsh-light, as though the country itself had decided she was ready:

*A door opens for the one who pays. No other may pass. No other may pay.*

"No other may pay," she murmured.

Ivo's head came up. "Read it aloud."

"'No other may pass. No other may—'" She stopped. He was looking at her with an expression she had never seen on a wolf's face before, and she understood, with a lurch, that he had known this clause all along. That he had been planning something. "Ivo. What did you intend to do?"

"Nothing yet."

"*Ivo.*"

"Walk," he said, rising, translucent at the edges, stubborn as stone. "We've lost enough time to sentiment. The tide turns in an hour, and when it turns, the channels fill faster than you'd believe."

She wrapped the page away and followed him, and did not drop the subject, and knew he knew she hadn't, and knew he was counting on the marsh to drown the conversation. It was, she reflected grimly, exactly what she would have done. She was beginning to suspect that was not a coincidence.

* * *

### Ivo

The marsh dreamed harder as the afternoon wore on.

Ivo felt it pressing in — the erased country bleeding upward through the salt, thickening the air the way honey thickens tea. The singing had words now, if you had ears old enough. He had ears old enough. They sang of harvests under water, of a king's road that ran where the deep channel ran, of a city called Sarenne-the-Bright whose towers had not fallen but *been forgotten*, which was a different and crueler thing.

And beneath the singing, fainter, another sound. Hooves? No. Boots. Many, spaced wide, moving with the unhurried confidence of men being paid by the day.

Collectors. Already. Corvane must have moved the moment the tannery gate closed behind them — or someone else had told him. Someone inside the guild. The thought arrived cold and complete, and Ivo filed it away, because Ines was speaking.

"—so either the marsh is haunted, which I've accepted, or the marsh is *crowded*, which I haven't." She was picking her way along the spine of an old dike, arms out for balance, hair whipping loose from its braid. "Because I keep seeing things at the edge of my eye. Lights. A woman hanging washing on a line that isn't there. Just now, a child running ahead of us on the path, and when I blinked—"

"When you blinked, she was still there," Ivo said. "You just couldn't see her. Those aren't different things, here."

Ines stopped walking. He felt her go still in the way ledgers go still — total, deliberate, the stillness of someone deciding whether to open a book whose contents might ruin her.

"Ivo," she said. "That child. Did she look at me?"

"Yes."

"What did she see?"

He considered lying. He was, historically, quite good at it; gods invented the practice. But she was standing on a drowned dike in a dead country with the light coming through his own shoulders, and lies felt obscene here, in the one place in the world where everything hidden insisted on surfacing.

"She saw someone she recognized," he said. "Someone with her mother's face. Or near enough."

The color left Ines's cheeks all at once, like tide going out. Behind her, far off across the flats, the false sunset of the low sun turned the water to hammered copper, and in that copper light Ivo saw the child again — clear-eyed, dark-haired, waving — and saw, beyond her, at the marsh's far edge, the small dark shapes of men with hooks and chains, spreading out along the causeway they had left behind them like ink dropped in water.

"Ines," he said. "Don't turn around. Walk faster. And when I tell you to run—"

Behind them, faint but unmistakable, a collector's horn sounded across the marsh — one long note, then two short, the guild's signal for *prey sighted*.

The singing stopped.

Every ghost, every light, every impossible washerwoman on her impossible line — gone, all at once, as if the whole country had drawn breath. And in the ringing silence that followed, the water in the channels beside the dike began, very softly, to rise.

"Run," Ivo said.
