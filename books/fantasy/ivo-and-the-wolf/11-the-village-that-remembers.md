## 11. The Village That Remembers

The marsh ended the way grief ends — not all at once, but in a slow shallowing, the black water giving way to brown, the brown to a crust of salt that crackled underfoot like old parchment. By the third day after the crossing, Ivo could walk again without leaning on me, though he still moved like something borrowed, his shadow thin at the edges. He had spent too much of himself shattering Mavros's hold on the door behind us, and divinity, I was learning, did not regenerate in exile. It pooled where it was remembered and drained where it was not.

"We should be near the reed-cutters' road," Ivo said, nose lifted to a wind that smelled of woodsmoke and, beneath it, something older — tallow, juniper, rain held in reserve.

"There is no reed-cutters' road," I said. "There's no record of any settlement within forty miles of the Salt Reach. I've read the surveys."

Ivo looked at me with an expression I had come to dread: patient, faintly amused, the look of a creature who had watched empires misplace themselves. "You've read the surveys of a country that isn't," he said. "Ines. What do you think we've been walking through?"

I had no answer for that. For three days the marsh had been bleeding through — impossible things, half-remembered things. A stone bridge arching over water that had never carried one. Apple trees standing hip-deep in brackish fen, heavy with fruit no surveyor had ever counted. Once, at dusk, we had heard bells. Not the guild-bells of Ashvale, with their flat commercial clang, but deep bronze bells rung in patterns, three-and-two, three-and-two, a rhythm my body knew before my mind did, because my feet had tried to turn toward it and I had let them.

Now the salt crust rose into low dunes, and the dunes gave way to gorse, and the gorse to a stand of birches so white they looked like candles left burning in a window. And beyond the birches, folded into a hollow where two hills met like cupped hands, there was a village.

It was not on any map. It was not, by every law of cartography and guild registry, there at all. But its chimneys were smoking, and its fields were plowed in long dark stripes, and a woman was beating a rug on a fence rail with the unhurried violence of someone who has done it ten thousand mornings.

"Vell Sarenne," Ivo said softly, and the word went through me like a hook through cloth.

*Vel Sarenne.* The name Ivo had spoken once, in the vaults beneath the counting house, and which I had repeated since only in whispers, as if the guild's collectors might hear it through stone. The country erased from maps and memory alike. The debt written against my name in a ledger older than the guild, older than the exile that had made me what I was.

"I thought it would be ruins," I said. My voice came out strange. "Ruins, or nothing. Not — laundry."

"Erasure is a wound, not an execution," Ivo said. "The land remembers what the maps were made to forget. Come."

We came down through the birches, and the village saw us coming, because villages always do. Doors opened along the single crooked street. Faces appeared in windows — brown, weathered faces, faces with the same broad cheekbones and dark eyes I had spent a lifetime avoiding in mirrors, because mirrors in Ashvale showed me a stranger and I had preferred not to argue.

The woman with the rug stopped beating it. She looked at Ivo — at the wolf walking upright beside me in the manner of a companion rather than a beast — and she did not scream or fetch a pitchfork. She set down her beater, pressed her hand flat over her heart, and bowed her head.

And then, up and down the street, door by door, window by window, every one of them did the same.

"What are they doing?" I whispered.

"Greeting you," Ivo said. "Or greeting what they think you are. Be careful which."

* * *

They fed us first, because that is what such places do; hunger is a language everyone speaks. We were brought to a long low house at the village's heart, its walls hung with dried herbs and strings of river pearls, and set before a table of bread dark as peat, fish baked in salt, honey from hives kept in hollow logs. An old woman presided over the pouring of something amber and fierce that burned going down and warmed going further down than that.

Her name was Maren. She was the elder, though no one announced it; it was simply apparent, the way gravity is apparent. Her hands were knotted like tree roots, and her eyes were the color of the marsh water we had crossed, and when she looked at me she looked at me the way one reads a difficult page — slowly, twice, with growing attention.

"You came through the Salt Reach," she said. It was not a question.

"The marsh," I corrected. "The salt marsh south of—"

"The Salt Reach," Maren said again, gently, immovably. "That is what it was called. That is what it is called here." She poured more of the amber liquor into my cup without asking. "Everything has two names now, child. The one the world took from us, and the one we keep in our mouths like a stone under the tongue. You'll learn them both, or you'll learn neither."

"I'm not staying," I said. "We're passing through. There's a door—"

"A door," said a young man across the table, and laughed, and stopped laughing when the elder raised one finger without looking at him.

"Yes," I said. "A door. Out of Vel Sarenne. Out of whatever this is. I have a debt to pay and a way to pay it, and I mean to pay it and leave."

Maren studied me over the rim of her own cup. "A Winterbourne," she said.

The word landed in my chest like a dropped coin. "How do you know that name?"

"Because it is written in this house." She rose, joints cracking like green kindling, and led us to the far wall, where among the herbs and pearls hung objects I had taken for charms or curios: a horseshoe bent double, a child's shoe filled with wax, a strip of bark scored with marks. And among them, on an iron nail, a small square of slate, drilled and threaded on a leather cord, its face scratched with lines that might have been weather or might have been writing.

"That's a storm-token," I said, before I knew I knew it. My mouth had gone dry. "That's — those aren't real. Those are folk superstition. Storm-callers don't use tokens. I would know. I was—"

"You were exiled for a storm you swear you never called," Maren said. "Yes. We heard. News travels strangely to places that don't exist — but it travels." She lifted the slate from its nail and turned it in her root-knotted fingers. "This token belonged to Ysolde Winterbourne."

The room tilted. I gripped the table. The bread, the honey, the fierce amber liquor — all of it receded, and there was only the name, spoken aloud in a kitchen that did not exist, in a voice that did not know what it was doing to me.

"My mother's name was Ysolde," I said. "She died before my exile. Before—" Before the storm. Before Halvard carried me, half-drowned and half-mad, out of the wreckage of a night I had never been allowed to remember clearly. Before the Vellum Guild took me in and taught me that numbers do not lie, which is the kindest lie there is.

"She did not die," Maren said. "She was spent. There is a difference, and the difference matters." She crossed the room and stood before me, close enough that I could smell the juniper smoke in her clothes. "Ysolde Winterbourne sealed a door with everything she had, and what remained of her walked back to Ashvale long enough to put a daughter in the world, and then the rest of her went out like a lamp. We felt it here. Every hearth in Vel Sarenne guttered at the same hour. We have mourned her for twenty years, child. And now you walk into our village wearing her cheekbones and deny knowing the road."

"I don't know the road," I said. "I don't know any of this. I grew up in Ashvale. I count other people's griefs for a living. I am nobody's daughter from nowhere."

Ivo made a small sound — not quite a growl, not quite a sigh. *Careful,* it said. *Or perhaps: there it is.*

Maren did not flinch. She reached out and took my hand — my ledger hand, my ink-stained, pen-cropped, meticulous hand — and turned it palm up, and set the storm-token in it.

The slate was warm. That was the first thing. Warm as skin, warm as a fever, though it had hung on a cold wall in a cold house. The second thing was the weight of it, which was wrong — too heavy for its size, as though it were not slate at all but compressed sky. And the third thing, the thing I will carry to my grave, was that the scratches on its face resolved as I looked at them, the way stars resolve, into lines of a script I had never learned and read anyway:

*For the storm that comes after me.*

The room went very quiet. Or perhaps the room had always been quiet and it was I who had been making noise — my breath, my pulse, the roar of blood in ears gone suddenly deaf to everything else.

"I can't take this," I said.

"You already have," Maren said.

"I don't know how to use it. I don't know what it does. I told you — my power, if I ever had any, it wasn't mine, it was lent, and the loan was called in, and I am finished with storms. They cost too much. They cost *her*." My voice cracked on the last word and I hated it, hated the tremor in it, hated the tears standing in my eyes in front of thirty strangers who bowed to me as if I were something owed reverence.

"Child," Maren said, and her voice gentled into something that was almost pity and almost warning, "we do not give you this so you can call storms. We give it to you so the storms know whose you are. There is a difference, and the difference may save your life at the door. Ysolde's token opens what her blood closed. You will want it. Believe an old woman who has watched men die of wanting it a moment too late."

I closed my fingers around the slate. It beat against my palm — faintly, arrhythmically, unmistakably — like a second heart waking after a long sleep.

* * *

They gave us a room above the granary, and I lay awake in the dark listening to the village breathe. Somewhere a dog turned over. Somewhere a woman sang a lullaby in a language I did not speak and understood completely, every syllable of it, the way you understand a thing you dreamed.

Belonging sat in the room with me like a guest who would not leave.

I had spent twenty years perfecting the art of not belonging. Exile teaches it; the guild perfects it. I had a desk with my name scratched into it — *Ines Winterbourne, Keeper of Impossible Accounts* — and a chair worn to the shape of my sitting, and a place in the great ledger of the world, however small, however shameful. Belonging to Ashvale had cost me everything true about myself, and I had paid gladly, because the alternative was the open road and the open question: *who are you, really?*

And now a village that did not exist had bowed to me in the street. An old woman had put my mother's name in my mouth like bread. A dead woman's heart was beating in my fist, and it fit my hand, and that frightened me more than Mavros, more than Corvane's collectors, more than the door itself.

Because belonging offered is a debt too. And I had learned, in the counting house, to read the fine print of debts. This one read: *stay. Be ours. Be hers. Stop being the woman who counts and become the woman who calls.* And I did not know if I could pay it and still be myself, or whether paying it meant the end of Ines Winterbourne entirely — the good parts along with the lies.

Ivo's head appeared at the loft ladder, eyes catching the moonlight like coins at the bottom of a well.

"You're not sleeping," he observed.

"Remarkable deduction. Are all gods this perceptive or just the wolf-shaped ones?"

He climbed up — awkwardly, still not fully recovered, still spending himself simply by being large in a world grown thin for him — and settled beside me with a gust of fur and juniper smoke. For a while neither of us spoke. Below us the village slept its forbidden sleep, keeping its forbidden names, and the stars over Vel Sarenne were not the stars over Ashvale; they were arranged differently, or I was different, or both.

"They bowed to me, Ivo," I said finally. "Thirty strangers bowed. To *me*. I falsify ledgers. I catalog bottled grief. Last week I stole a god from a guild vault and I've been running ever since, and none of that deserves —"

"It doesn't," Ivo agreed. "Reverence rarely tracks desert. It tracks hope. They've waited twenty years for someone to come down out of the birches wearing Ysolde's face, and you arrived, and their hope got legs. That's not your burden, Ines. But it is your weather."

"I didn't ask for it."

"No one asks for weather." He rested his chin on his paws. "But you asked for truth. In the vault, you copied the page instead of burning it, though your mentor begged you. You chose the open question over the comfortable lie. So here it is, arriving, as truth does: these people are yours. Not by right. By remainder. You are what's left of Ysolde Winterbourne, and they are what's left of Vel Sarenne, and leftovers recognize each other."

I turned the storm-token over in the dark. Its faint heat pulsed against my fingers, patient as a tide.

"And if I don't want to be a leftover?" I asked. "If I want to be whole? Whole and new and unowned by any of it — the guild, the gods, the dead?"

"Then you'll pay your debt at the door and walk out free," Ivo said. "And some part of you will always hear bells ringing three-and-two, and your feet will try to turn, and you'll spend the rest of your life declining to follow them." His eyes glowed briefly in the dark. "I did it for four hundred years. I can tell you the price exactly, if you'd like it itemized."

Despite everything, despite the fear sitting on my chest like a cat, I laughed — a small, cracked sound, quickly smothered. "No itemization. I get enough of that at work."

"Former work."

"*Current* work until the debt is paid. Corvane still holds my exile record. Mavros still wants the door sealed. We're lying in a village that officially does not exist, waiting for collectors who officially cannot find it." I said it lightly, and then heard what I'd said, and the lightness curdled. "Ivo. If the collectors can't find it — how did *we* find it?"

Silence. Beside me, the wolf went very still, in the particular way of a creature listening to something far away.

"Ines," he said quietly, "what did the elder say news travels on, to places that don't exist?"

"Strangely. She said it travels strangely—"

And that was when the dogs began to bark.

Not one dog. All of them, all around the hollow, a rising ring of sound, and under it — faint, from the direction of the birches, from the road we had come by — the creak of wheels and the jingle of harness and a sound I knew better than my own heartbeat: the flat, dry click of a guild collector's tally-counter, counting as it came.

I was on my feet with the storm-token clenched in my fist before I was awake enough to know I'd moved. Down in the street, doors were opening, lamps kindling, and Maren stood in the yard below with her arms spread wide as though she could hold back the night by main strength.

And at the edge of the firelight, tall and travel-worn and utterly impossible, stood a figure I knew better than I wanted to.

Halvard.

My mentor. My forger. The man who had begged me to burn the page — carrying a guild satchel, alone, with the collectors nowhere in sight, and a look on his face I had never seen him wear in twenty years of ledgers and lessons:

Terror.

"Ines," he called up to me, and his voice broke on the name. "Burn the token. Burn it now, before they—"

The tally-counter clicked, somewhere in the dark beyond the birches. Counting. Closer.
