## 13. What Storms Are For

The morning of the attempt, the sky over the village was the color of old pewter, and it would not rain.

Ines had noticed it first as a wrongness in her teeth — a pressure behind her molars like a held breath — and then in the way the birds flew low and silent over the thatched roofs, and then in the way the villagers themselves kept glancing up without seeming to know why. The elder, Maren, stood in the doorway of the longhouse with her mother's storm-token warm against Ines's palm, and said nothing at all, which was its own kind of instruction.

"You don't have to do this," Ivo said.

He sat on the low stone wall at the village's edge, where the fields gave out into moorland, and he looked worse than he had three days ago. She had told herself it was the journey — the salt marshes, the cold, the collectors' hounds baying somewhere behind them for two nights running. But the marshes were behind them now, and still he faded. His gray pelt had gone the color of ash at the edges, as though someone were rubbing him out with a thumb. When he spoke, his voice came from farther away than his mouth.

"The sky here is thin," he'd said last night, by the fire. "Vel Sarenne was unmade, Ines. Its name is gone from every map and every memory but ours. What do you think holds up its weather?"

She hadn't understood him then. She thought she understood him now, standing in the wet grass with her arms bare to the elbow and the token clenched in her fist, and understanding did not make it easier. It made it worse. Because if the sky was thin, then the thing she was about to try might be like drawing water from a well that had already been drunk dry — or like drawing blood from one.

Maren had given her the field beyond the wall because it was empty of anything that could be broken. That was the elder's way: practical kindness, mercy disguised as logistics. *Go past the barley, girl. Go where there's nothing to burn.* Behind her, half the village had come out anyway, because you cannot stop people from watching a storm being born, even when they are afraid of it. Especially when they are afraid of it. These were Vel Sarenne's scattered children; they had been taught, in the country that no longer existed, what storms meant. They knew this was not a spectacle. It was a rite.

Or it was supposed to be.

"Begin when you're ready," Maren called, "and stop the moment it feels wrong. Promise me that."

"I promise," Ines said, and heard how young she sounded, and hated it.

* * *

Here is what Ines Winterbourne remembered about calling storms:

She remembered that it had never felt like work. That was the obscenity of it, the thing the tribunal had found most damning — not that she'd called the storm that drowned the harbor quarter and killed eleven people, but that afterward, questioned under oath, she could not describe the calling as effort. *It was like opening a window,* she had said, and watched the judges' faces close like doors. A girl who opens windows onto catastrophe. A girl who does not bleed for it.

They had taken her name from the guild rolls, her seal from her hand, her city from beneath her feet. Exile, the sentence read, *for the crime of unlicensed weather,* and the word *unlicensed* had always struck her as the cruelest part, as though the storm itself were a forgery, a coin struck without mint-mark. For nine years she had carried the sentence like a stone in her chest and told herself: *I did it. Whatever else is true, I did it.* Guilt, at least, was a shape. It fit the hands.

Now Ivo had told her the storm was called to hide a god-murder, and Halvard had confessed to forging the record, and her mother had died sealing a door, and every shape she'd lived inside for nine years had turned out to be paper. So this morning she stood in an empty field in a country erased from the world, holding a dead woman's token, preparing to call a storm for the first time since exile — not to prove her guilt and not to prove her innocence, but to prove something simpler and more terrible: that the power was hers. That she had been, all along, exactly the dangerous thing they said she was.

Because if it wasn't hers —

She didn't finish the thought. She opened her fist and looked at the token instead.

It was small, hardly bigger than a knucklebone: a disc of storm-gray glass shot through with veins of silver, warm as a living thing. Her mother's. Maren had pressed it into her hand three days ago saying, *Your grandmother wore this before her, and hers before that. The storm-callers of Vel Sarenne did not summon the sky, child. They asked it. There is a difference, and the difference is everything.* At the time Ines had filed the words away the way she filed everything, in ledgers, in columns, in the neat handwriting of a woman who believed all mysteries could be balanced if you just wrote them down carefully enough.

She breathed out. She let the ledger go.

* * *

The asking began, as it always had, in the body.

Feet first — bare feet in wet grass, roots of her, drinking the ground's cold upward through her soles. Then the spine, uncoiling vertebra by vertebra until she stood taller than her height, a column, a mast. Then the arms, rising not because she lifted them but because something vast and patient leaned its weight against her palms from very far away, the way a horse leans into a halter when it trusts the hand that holds it.

And then the voice — not spoken, not in any throat, but shaped in the pressure behind her teeth, in the hum of the air, in the pewter light:

*I am here. I am asking.*

For nine years she had forbidden herself this. Nine years of flinching from thunder, of counting the seconds between flash and sound like a penance, of keeping her windows shut in summer. And now the old channel opened in her the way a river opens under spring ice, and it felt — gods forgive her, it felt like *homecoming*. Like a door in her chest swinging wide onto a room she'd been told burned down years ago. Power moved up through her feet and her spine and her open hands, and it was enormous, it was gentle, it was *hers*, and above the moorland the pewter sky began to churn.

The villagers made a sound. Not fear. Something older than fear.

The clouds darkened. The wind stood up. Somewhere in the deep of the churning gray, lightning gathered itself like a thought about to be spoken, and Ines laughed — actually laughed, a cracked and disbelieving sound, because it was working, it was *working*, after everything, the exile and the lie and the ledgers, she was still what she had always been —

The sky answered.

Not with rain. Not with the clean, fat drops she remembered from her childhood, the generous downpour of a sky asked kindly. What came instead was a *tearing* — a sound like the whole horizon ripping along a seam — and the light that followed was wrong, greenish, the color of bruises and old glass, and the pressure that had been leaning gently against her palms became a weight, became a *falling building*, became the entire collapsed sky of a murdered country pouring itself through one small channel of a woman standing in a barley field.

*Ivo—* she tried to say, and had no mouth to say it with.

* * *

Later, she would piece it together from what others saw, because she saw almost nothing. This is what they told her:

The bolt came down green and forked like a root, and it did not strike the token in her hand, nor the ground beside her, nor the lightning-rod oak at the field's far edge. It struck the place where a wolf had been standing on a low stone wall, because the wolf had leapt — had thrown himself, deliberately, bodily, into the path of a god's grief — and gods' grief does not distinguish between what it strikes. The thunder that followed knocked every watcher flat. The barley flattened in a ring thirty yards wide. And Ines Winterbourne stood at the center of it with her arms still raised, screaming without sound, holding open a door that the sky of a dead country was trying to pour itself through, because she did not know how to close it, because no one had ever taught her, because the ones who should have taught her were dead, or exiled, or forged, or gone.

What stopped it was not strength. She wanted, afterward, to believe it was strength — that she had wrestled the storm shut through sheer will, that the girl who opens windows had finally learned to bar them. It was not. What stopped it was the token cracking in her fist.

Her mother's storm-token split down its silver vein with a sound like a small bone breaking, and the warmth went out of it all at once, and the channel closed the way a hand closes when the wrist is cut — not closed, *finished*, spent, done. The green light guttered. The torn sky sagged back into ordinary pewter, then ordinary gray, then a thin, exhausted drizzle that fell on the flattened barley like an apology too late to matter.

Ines came back into her body kneeling, though she had no memory of falling. Her nose was bleeding freely. Her ears rang. Her hands, when she looked at them, were scorched pink across both palms, as though she'd caught something falling from a great height.

Which, in a way, she had.

"Ivo." Her voice came out ruined, smoke-raw. "Ivo, where—" 

She found him ten feet away, at the foot of the shattered wall, and the world went narrow and white at the edges.

* * *

He was breathing. That was the whole of the good news, and she clung to it the way a drowning woman clings to driftwood, because it was nearly the only good news there was.

He lay on his side in the wet grass, and the ash-color had spread. Where yesterday the fading had touched only the tips of his fur, it now ran in streaks along his flank like frost creeping across a window, and when she pressed her hands to him — careful of her burned palms, careless of everything else — the fur beneath came away slightly, softly, against her fingers, like the bloom on a plum, like something that had begun to forget how to be solid.

His eye opened. Gold, still. That hadn't faded. She fixed on it the way sailors fix on stars.

"You *idiot*," she said, and her voice broke in the middle of it, and she didn't care. "You absolute — I told you to stay back, I told you—"

"You didn't," he rasped. His voice was worse than hers, gravel dragged over gravel. "You promised *her* you'd stop. You never promised me anything."

"That's not—" She pressed her forehead to his shoulder, to the solid-warm-not-solid-cool of him, and felt him lean into it with what strength remained. "Why? Why would you — you knew what it might be. You *told* me the sky was thin. You knew."

"I knew," he agreed.

"Then why?"

The gold eye regarded her, and in it she saw something she had seen only once before, in the vaults of the Vellum Guild, the first night a wolf had spoken to her among the shelved impossibilities: patience. The terrible, ancient, bottomless patience of a thing that has watched centuries go by and learned that most questions answer themselves if you wait.

"Because I have been struck by lightning before," he said. "By worse than lightning. By the *hands* that made that lightning, in the country whose sky you just borrowed. And I am still here." A breath, rattling. "But you — Ines. If that bolt had reached you, it would not have burned you. It would have *taken* you. Filled you. A mortal channel, opened that wide, to a sky that desperate — it would have poured itself into you until there was no *you* left. You would have become a storm wearing a woman's shape. I have seen it. Once. A long time ago." His eye closed. Opened again, slower. "So I got in the way. It's what wolves do. We get in the way of the thing that's coming for the pack."

"I'm not your pack."

"No," he said, and something in his fading face shifted, wry and sad. "You're the one the pack follows. Worse luck for all of us."

* * *

They brought blankets, and broth, and Maren, who looked at the cracked token in Ines's palm for a long moment before she spoke, and when she spoke her voice was very quiet.

"My grandmother told me the tokens were anchors," the elder said. "Not batteries. Anchors. The caller asks, and the sky answers, and the token keeps the *proportion* — keeps the answer sized to the asker. Yours has been dead a long time, child. Dead since the country died, I'd guess. It couldn't hold the proportion anymore. So the sky answered with everything it had." She looked up at the thin drizzle, the scoured sky. "And it has everything. Did you feel it?"

Had she felt it. Ines turned her scorched palms over and stared at them.

She had felt it. That was the thing she could not say aloud, the thing sitting in her chest like a swallowed coal: she had felt the sky of Vel Sarenne answer her asking, and it had not felt like a stranger's power flowing through her. It had felt like *recognition*. Like a voice she'd known before she had words. Every storm she had ever called — the little ones as a child, the great drowning one they'd exiled her for — had been this same voice, this same vast and patient presence, lending her its weather the way a parent lends a child a coat.

*Unlicensed weather*, the tribunal had written. As if the license had ever been theirs to give.

"It was never mine," she said. She heard her own voice as if from a distance, flat with shock. "All of it. Every storm I ever called. It was never *mine*, it was — lent. Borrowed. From *here*." She looked at the flattened barley, the broken wall, the wolf fading on the grass. "From a country that doesn't exist. I've spent my whole life believing I was a weapon they rightly feared, and I was never a weapon. I was a *door*. Just — a door someone left open."

"And doors," said Ivo, faintly, from the grass, "can be closed. And opened. And walked through." He was smiling, she realized. The fool was smiling. "You wanted proof the power was yours. You got better proof than you asked for. Nothing of yours could have killed me like that. Only something *older* than you. Older than both of us." His gold eye found hers. "That sky remembers you, Ines. It answered you in a heartbeat, after nine years and an ocean of forgetting. Do you understand what that means?"

She shook her head. Her hands were shaking. She put them in her lap so no one would see.

"It means," Ivo said, "that when we reach the door — and we will reach the door — it isn't going to open for your debt alone. Debts are arithmetic. Doors don't care about arithmetic." His breath caught; the ash-streaks on his flank seemed, for a moment, to shimmer. "Doors open for what they recognize. And whatever that sky recognized just now, standing in a barley field in a dead woman's daughter — it recognized it *fast*."

Across the moorland, the drizzle thickened. And Ines, watching the rain come down over the country that had been stolen from the maps, felt the truth settle into her scorched hands like a second burning: she had come to this field to learn whether she was guilty.

She had learned instead that she was *claimed* — by a sky, by a debt, by a door — and that the collectors Corvane had sent would not be the last things to come looking for her.

Far off, at the edge of hearing, down in the valley where the road climbed toward the village, a bell began to ring. Not the village bell. The warning bell.

Maren's head came up like a deer's. "Riders," she said. "More than one. Moving fast."

Ivo tried to rise, and failed, and Ines knelt in the ruined barley with her mother's broken token in her fist and understood, with terrible clarity, that whatever came up that road, she would have to meet it carrying a power that was not hers, a wolf who could barely stand, and a truth she had learned less than an hour ago.

The bell rang on.
