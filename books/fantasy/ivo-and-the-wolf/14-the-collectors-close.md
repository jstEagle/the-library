## 14. The Collectors Close

I had forgotten, in the long centuries of my binding, what it was to be afraid for someone other than myself.

Gods are not made for fear. We are made to be feared, which is a different animal entirely — it sits in the chest like a warm coal, steady and flattering. But fear *for* — that is a cold thing, a wet thing, and it found me in the village of Vel Sarenne's scattered children on the morning the collectors came, and it has never wholly left me since.

The day began wrong in small ways.

The well water tasted of iron. The goats would not come down from the hillside but stood along the ridge in a line, all facing east, as still as carvings. Old Sefa burned salt at her threshold twice instead of once and could not say why, only that her hands kept doing it while her mind was elsewhere, thinking of bread. And the birds — the birds were gone. Not flown. *Gone*, the way a word is gone from the tip of the tongue, leaving only the shape of where it had been.

Ines noticed before any of them did. She always noticed. She stood in the doorway of the elder's house with her ledger under one arm — she carried it everywhere now, slept with it, spoke to it sometimes in a low voice when she thought no one heard — and she looked at the empty sky and said, quietly, "They're coming today."

Not *if*. Not *perhaps*. Today.

I pressed my flank against her leg, the way I had learned to do, the way a wolf does who has decided that this particular two-legged creature is his to keep. She rested her hand on my head without looking down. Her fingers were cold.

"How many?" asked Elder Maren, coming out into the grey light. She was a small woman bent like a question mark, with eyes the color of river ice, and she wore her mother's storm-token on a cord around her neck because Ines had refused to wear it herself. "How many collectors?"

"Corvane sends six when he expects resistance," Ines said. "Four when he expects surrender." She paused. "He'll send eight. He thinks I'm both."

* * *

They came at noon, out of the east, walking through the barley fields without bending a single stalk.

I want to be precise about that, because precision is all I have left of that hour: eight figures in the guild's dove-grey livery, moving in perfect step, and the barley parting around their boots as though the plants themselves had been paid to look away. Collectors of the Vellum Guild are not soldiers. Soldiers can be fought. Collectors are *contracts* given legs — each one bound by oaths written in ink mixed with their own blood, unable to stop, unable to bargain, unable to die until the debt they carry is discharged or destroyed. Their faces had the terrible smoothness of people who had signed away the right to expression. Only their eyes still worked, and the guild used those eyes the way a miser uses candle-ends: sparingly, and only for business.

The villagers fled indoors. Doors slammed. A child began to cry somewhere and was hushed with desperate tenderness. And in the middle of the village square, where the well stood and the old shrine-stone to the nine gods of Vel Sarenne leaned at its patient angle, the eight stopped as one and waited.

They did not have to knock. The doors opened themselves. That is the cruelty of contracts: they make the world complicit.

Elder Maren walked out first, spine straight despite its bend. Then Sefa. Then the brothers Ondrej and Tomas, who had taught me — though they didn't know what I was — to fish the mill-pool with nothing but patience and a bent pin. Then others, eleven in all, drawn out by whatever clause had been written into the collectors' arrival, their feet dragging and then, horribly, not dragging at all, their bodies deciding obedience was easier than resistance.

"None of them are bound to speak," said the lead collector. His voice was dry paper. He looked at the shrine-stone, at the well, at last at the door of Maren's house, where Ines stood. "Only you, debtor Winterbourne. Come out. Bring the page."

I felt her heartbeat through the place where her hand rested on me. Fast. Steady. Hers.

"Ivo," she murmured, so low that only a god's ears — even half-empty ones — could catch it. "Whatever happens, you stay behind the stone. You don't let them see what you are. Promise me."

"They'll take you."

"They might." Her thumb moved once across my ear, an absent benediction. "Promise anyway."

I promised. It was the first lie I had told her, and I intend to answer for it someday, in whatever ledger keeps accounts between wolves and women.

She stepped out into the square with the ledger held against her chest like a shield that had never once stopped a blade, and I slipped behind the leaning shrine-stone, and I watched.

* * *

Here is what watching costs a god who is becoming, against every law of his nature, merely devoted:

It costs everything.

I could hear the clauses. That is a curse of my kind — we hear the architecture of bargains the way mortals hear music. The lead collector's contract hummed around him like a cage of wire: *seize the wolf-god Ivo, property of the Vellum Guild per acquisition of record; seize the ledger-page bearing the debt of Ines Winterbourne; collateral acceptable in substitution.* Collateral. The word turned over in the air of the square like a coin too large for any pocket.

"You have something of ours," the collector said to Ines.

"I have a great many things of yours," Ines answered, and there it was — her voice doing the thing it did when she was most afraid, going flat and precise and terribly calm, the voice of a woman counting. "Eleven years of interest on grief-bottles sold back to the families who wept them. Four hundred percent on borrowed years. A village's worth of prayers your guild recorded as assets. Shall I itemize?"

"We are not here for your arithmetic."

"No," she said. "You're here for mine."

She opened the ledger. Even from behind the stone I saw the pages fall open to the place they always fell open now, as if the book itself had learned where its heart lay: the ancient leaf, older than the guild, older than the exile, bearing the column of names owed to the nine gods of Vel Sarenne — and among them, in ink that had never faded, *Ines Winterbourne, daughter of Ysolde, debtor.*

"The page," said the collector. "Or we begin taking the village. We are authorized to take it in increments."

And he proved it. He did not draw a weapon. He simply turned his head, and one of the villagers — Sefa, gentle Sefa, who burned salt at her door — made a small sound like a hinge, and folded down onto her knees, and I saw the years go out of her face the way heat goes out of a room when a window breaks in winter. Not death. *Collection.* The guild does not kill what it can amortize.

The square went very quiet. Somewhere a shutter creaked. The child who had cried earlier had gone silent too, and that silence was worse than any crying.

"Stop," Ines said.

The collector stopped. Everything about him stopped, the way a clock stops, all at once and forever until wound again.

"Name your terms," Ines said. Her voice did not shake. I have faced the hunger of the deep woods and the wrath of drowned gods and the slow erosion of ten thousand years of being forgotten, and I have never heard courage shaped like that voice — flat, exact, unornamented, a woman reading aloud the price of her own heart and signing without flourish.

"The page," the collector said. "Freely surrendered. In exchange, the village is released entire, and the debt-record passes to guild custody, where it will be—" and here the wire-cage of his contract hummed a new note, and I understood, and I think she understood too, "—archived."

Archived. Not destroyed. Filed away in the vaults of Ashvale, in the dark, where a debt could sleep for a century and wake owned by whoever bought the shelf it sat on. Where it could never open the door. Where Vel Sarenne would stay erased, and the nine gods would stay bound, and Ivo would stay —

Where *she* would stay exiled forever, nameless, stormless, a rounding error in someone else's book.

"Ines," I said.

I had sworn to stay silent. I broke the oath, because I am a poor god and a worse wolf, and because some things cannot be watched from behind stones.

Her head turned toward the shrine-stone. Her eyes found the gap where my shadow showed, and for one moment — one long, grain-of-sand moment — everything unsaid between us stood in the square with us, enormous, wearing the shape of a rain that had followed us out of Ashvale and a fire lit in a kitchen and her hand in the dark finding the ruff of my neck and saying *you feel like something I lost.*

"Don't," she said to me. Gently. The way you say it to a dog at a gate you must close.

Then she tore out the page.

* * *

You should understand what that tearing sounded like.

To mortal ears it was paper: a soft rip, almost apologetic. But I heard the true sound beneath it, the sound a debt makes when it changes hands — a low chord, nine notes, struck all at once and then muffled, wrapped in grey cloth, carried away. The bound gods of Vel Sarenne felt it wherever they were buried in their binding. I felt it in my teeth. Somewhere in the world, I knew, Mavros smiled without knowing why, the way a leech smiles.

Ines held the page out. The collector took it with both hands, reverently, the way men handle relics or poison, and slid it into a case of black leather that closed with a click like a small bone breaking.

"The village is released," he said. "The debtor remains under observation. Guild property claims on the wolf-god remain outstanding." His smooth face turned, at last, toward the shrine-stone, toward my shadow, and his candle-end eyes spent themselves fully for the first time. "We know you're there, Ivo. Property doesn't get to choose its hiding places. Only its owners."

"Then find better owners," Ines said.

"We intend to."

They turned and walked back through the barley, which again declined to touch them, and the eight of them diminished eastward into the grey noon until they were a rumor, and then a memory, and then less than that.

Sefa got up off her knees slowly, patting her own face as though checking that all of it had been returned. Maren put her arms around the nearest villagers and did not let go for a long time. And Ines stood alone in the center of the square with an empty ledger and her hand pressed flat over her sternum, as though holding shut a door.

I came out from behind the stone. Of course I did. I crossed the square with all four paws loud on the cobbles, making no attempt at stealth, and sat down hard against her legs, heavier than a wolf needs to be, giving her my whole ridiculous weight to lean on.

"He has it," she said. Her voice finally cracked, one hairline fracture, quickly mended. "Corvane has my debt. My mother's name. The clause that opens the door." She looked down at me, and her eyes were bright and furious and wet. "I just sold the only key in the world to the man who wants the lock melted."

"You bought twelve lives with it."

"Twelve lives." She laughed, and it was an awful sound, lovely and broken. "Ivo, I've read enough ledgers to know what that was. That wasn't a purchase. That was a *down payment.*"

* * *

That night the village did not sleep, and neither did we.

Maren built up the fire in her house and set out the forbidden things — the nine cups, the honey and dark bread, the sprig of rowan that had crossed three borders sewn into a hem — and the survivors of the square came one by one to touch them, silently, the way you touch the edge of a wound to confirm it's real. Ines sat apart with the gutted ledger open on her knees, staring at the stub of the torn page as if she could grow it back by sheer accounting.

I lay with my chin on her knee and listened to the country bleed through.

Because that was the thing none of them said aloud, and all of them felt: the page had not left neutral ground. It had been carried east, out of the marshlands, out of the bleeding margins where erased Vel Sarenne seeped into the world — and the moment it crossed that invisible border, the land itself reacted. The well water ran clear again and sweet. The birds came back all at once at dusk, a thousand small throats, singing songs no one in the village had taught them, songs in a language with no living speakers. And far to the east, I could feel — faintly, the way you feel weather coming in an old scar — the door.

It had woken. Torn from its page, homeless, the debt-clause drifted like a lampless ship, and doors that are meant to be opened do not rest easy in black leather cases in the vaults of greedy men.

"It knows where it is," I said. "Even archived. Debts always know where they're kept."

"Then Corvane is sitting on a lit fuse and calling it a cushion." Ines closed the ledger. Opened it again. Closed it. "He'll read it. He'll have scribes take it apart clause by clause within the week, and the moment he understands what he's holding—"

"He'll run to Mavros," I finished. "Or discover he already has."

She was quiet a long time. The fire ticked. Outside, the impossible birds sang the impossible songs, and one by one the villagers' windows went dark, and the smell of woodsmoke and barley and rain-that-hadn't-fallen-yet lay over everything like a blessing with teeth.

"Ivo," she said at last, very low. "When I handed it over — for half a second, I wanted to keep it. I stood there with twelve lives in the balance and I *haggled with myself.* Some part of me counted them against the door, against my name, against—" her hand curled, "—against you. Against getting out. Twelve lives weighed against one chance at freedom, and some part of me stood there weighing."

"That part of you is a ledger," I said. "You've lived inside one for eleven years. Ledgers weigh. It's all they know how to do." I pressed my head harder into her knee. "But you didn't obey it. That's the whole of it, Ines. Everyone has the ledger in them. Almost no one refuses it."

She was silent. Then her hand came down and buried itself in the fur of my neck, and stayed there, gripping, the way a drowning swimmer grips a spar — not elegantly, not gratefully, just *holding on*, and I bore her weight and thought: this is what I traded ten thousand years of worship for. One mortal woman's grip. It was, I can report with authority, a fair price.

"The fuse is lit," she whispered. "Corvane has the page, Mavros has Corvane, and Halvard—" she stopped. "Halvard hasn't come. He said he'd follow. It's been nine days."

"He'll come."

"And if he comes followed?" She looked into the fire. "If he comes followed by everything?"

I had no answer, and the night offered none. But near midnight, as the fire burned to embers and Ines finally slept sitting upright, her fingers still knotted in my fur, I lifted my head and looked east, past the sleeping village, past the marshes, toward Ashvale and its vaults and its black case with its clicking bone-lock.

Far off, at the very limit of what a half-empty god can feel, something vast and patient shifted its weight in the dark — and the door, the true door, the door out of the country that wasn't, sighed in its sleep and turned over.

Toward us.

Toward home.

And running ahead of that vast shifting, light-footed over the miles, came a scent I knew, old and papery and beloved: ink, and lamp-oil, and fear.

Halvard was coming. And he was not alone.
