## 15. Mavros's Cathedral of Answers

The cathedral had not been there a month ago.

Ines was certain of this, and certainty was the one currency she trusted above all others. She had walked this quarter of Ashvale twice in the spring, cataloguing a shipment of bottled lullabies for the Guild, and the lot where the cathedral now stood had been nothing but a burned-out tannery, its beams like black ribs against the sky. Now rose-colored stone climbed six stories into the grey air, buttresses flowering outward like the ribs of some enormous breathing animal, and spires of white glass caught even the sunless light of Ashvale's perpetual drizzle and made it shine.

People were streaming toward it. That was the second wrong thing. Ashvale's poor did not stream; they shuffled, they trudged, they kept their eyes on the mud. But these people walked with their chins lifted, their hands open at their sides, as if they expected the sky to put something in them.

"They say it opened three weeks ago," murmured Sefa, the young collector Corvane had assigned to shadow Ines — her minder, her leash, though the girl didn't know it. Sefa was nineteen and devout in the way of people who had never been answered by anything. "A priest just appeared. Or — no one remembers him arriving. He was just *there*, and the doors were open, and the first woman who went in came out weeping because her son's fever broke that same hour."

"And what did she pay?" Ines asked.

Sefa blinked. "Pay? It's free. That's the miracle of it. Everything is free."

Ines looked up at the white glass spires and felt the old ledger-hunger turn over in her chest, cold and attentive. Nothing was free. She had spent eleven years among impossible things; she knew their grammar better than scripture. Every gift had a clause. Every grace had a margin note.

"Stay outside," she told Sefa. "If I'm not out by dusk, take my notes back to Corvane."

"You're going in *alone*?"

"That's rather the point of me."

She crossed the plaza with the crowd pressing gently around her — gently, everything here was gentle, that was the horror of it beginning already — and passed beneath the doors, which stood twenty feet high and carved with a single repeated image: a hand, open, palm upward. An offering or an asking. From a god, she could no longer tell which.

Inside, the cathedral smelled of beeswax and rain and something underneath both, something older — the smell of a house you'd lived in before you could remember living anywhere, the smell of a word you'd forgotten but still knew how to say.

Ines stopped just past the threshold and let herself feel it fully, because she was a ledger-keeper first and a coward second: *home*. The air smelled like home. Not Ashvale-home, which for her had always been a rented room and a borrowed name. Something deeper, pre-verbal, warm as bread and dark as a root cellar. It wrapped around her like a coat sewn to her measurements, and she understood, with a sick lurch, that it would smell exactly right to every single person who walked through these doors. That was the trick. That was the bait. The cathedral smelled like whatever home meant to you.

And it cost you the memory of the real one.

* * *

The nave was vast and soft-lit, the white glass diffusing daylight into something like the light through closed eyelids. There were no pews. People knelt or stood or lay full-length on the pale floor, and everywhere, everywhere, prayers were being said aloud — not whispered, not mumbled, but spoken in clear, unashamed voices, the way children ask for things.

An old man near the pillar: "Let my wife remember me. She's forgotten me, three winters now, please, let her remember—"

A girl no older than Sefa, rocking slightly: "Rain for the east fields. Just rain. We'll do the rest ourselves, I swear it, just rain—"

A boy holding a wooden bird with a broken wing: "Fix it. Please fix it. Please fix it. Please—"

And the answers came. Ines watched it happen and her skin crawled even as part of her — the exiled part, the starved part — leaned toward it like a plant toward a window.

The old man's face went slack with joy; somewhere across the city, presumably, a woman set down her cup and said her husband's name as if waking from a long dream. Rain began against the white glass, soft and even, though the sky had been dry all morning. The wooden bird shivered in the boy's hands; splinters flowed backward into wholeness like water running uphill.

Every prayer answered. Instantly. Gently. Without negotiation, without contract, without a single line of terms read aloud.

Ines moved down the nave, and no one stopped her, and that too was wrong. A guild counting-house had locks on its ledgers. This place had none. Its treasure walked out the door in people's heads, and no one guarded it, because no one knew they were carrying anything away.

She found the cost written on the faces.

It took her a while to see it, because the faces were so *happy*. That was the first obscenity: the happiness was real. These were not the hollow-eyed faithful of the old gods' cults, worn down by fasting and fear. These people glowed. They walked out of the cathedral lighter than they'd come in, and Ines tracked them, watched a woman who'd prayed for her drowned brother's soul rise from the floor beaming, radiant, transformed —

and stop in the doorway, and look back at the city with a small, polite frown, like a traveler trying to recall whether she'd left the stove lit.

"What did you pray for?" Ines asked her quietly.

The woman smiled at her with enormous warmth. "I'm sorry — have we met? You look so familiar." And then, catching sight of Ines's guild badge, the smile widened. "Oh! Are you from the Guild? I used to work near the counting-house. I think. Someone worked near a counting-house. Isn't it lovely weather?"

It was raining. It had been raining for an hour. The woman stepped out into it without her hood up, humming, and turned left when her feet clearly wanted to go right, and Ines stood in the doorway and felt grief arrive in her chest like a stone dropped down a well.

Not the woman's grief. Hers. Because she had seen this before, once, in miniature — in the vaults, in a jar labeled *bottled grief*, watching the grey sediment swirl, understanding for the first time that grief was not a weight but a *cord*, tied at one end to you and at the other end to someone or somewhere, and that cutting it did not lighten you, it merely left you untethered, drifting, pleasant, lost.

The cathedral wasn't answering prayers. It was buying memories of home and paying for them in miracles, and the exchange rate was ruinous, and nobody read the terms because the terms were never shown.

* * *

She found Mavros at the far end of the nave, where the apse should have been.

He had built himself an altar of pale wood, unadorned, almost humble, and he sat behind it in the guise of a parish priest — grey robes, kind eyes, ink-stained fingers, the very portrait of a tired man who loved his flock. If she had not known his true shape, if Ivo had not described it to her in the marshes — the wings like wet slate, the mouth that opened too wide, the voice like a landslide wearing silk — she might have knelt.

"Ines Winterbourne," he said warmly, rising. "Or should I say — ah, but names are such tender things here. Half my congregation has given theirs away. Come in, come in. You're letting the draught in, and my people are so sensitive to draughts now. Home is a drafty concept, once you've sold it."

"You know why I've come."

"To accuse me. To gape. To count." He smiled. "You've been counting since you walked in. I can hear it. Little abacus clicking away behind your eyes. How many souls per day, what's the yield, what's the margin. Don't be ashamed — it's how your kind shows love."

"I don't love any of this."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You love something else. The wolf, isn't it? And a country that doesn't exist. Which makes you the only person in Ashvale who can look at my cathedral and see theft, because everyone else has forgotten there was ever anything to steal." He gestured, and the nave seemed to lean closer, the praying voices softening as if hushed by a mother's hand. "Look at them, Ines. Really look. When did any of them last sleep without pain? When did any god — your wolf-god, your bound and buried chorus of Vel Sarenne — ever answer so *kindly*? The old gods demanded sacrifice, covenant, debt. Ledgers." His eyes glittered. "I ask only for what they weren't using."

"Their homes."

"Their *memories* of home. A distinction you of all people should appreciate. Memory is a story we tell ourselves, and stories improve with editing. These people will live happier without the drafty rooms and the dead brothers and the mothers who died sealing doors—" He caught himself, smoothly, but not before Ines saw it, saw the flicker, the slip — he knew about her mother. "Without all that heavy furniture," he finished. "Grace should be light. That is my entire theology."

"It's not grace. It's repossession."

"Such a guild word." He laughed, and the sound was beautiful, and she hated it. "Very well. Let us speak guild to guild, since you insist on terms. You want the door of Vel Sarenne. I want it sealed. You have a debt-page; I have a congregation of ten thousand who will forget, by winter, that any door was ever missing. Time favors me. Forgetting always does." He leaned across the altar, and for one instant the kindly priest-shape thinned, and she saw through it: the vast dark thing underneath, patient as rot. "But I am generous, Ines Winterbourne. Ask me for something. Anything. Your exile undone. Your storm returned. Your mother's face — oh yes, I keep those too, the ones sold willingly. One prayer. One small memory of home, freely given, and I will answer it before you finish speaking. No collectors. No Corvane. No ledgers. Just grace, cheap and immediate, the way it should have been all along."

And here was the terrible thing, the thing she would not tell Ivo, the thing that sat in her chest afterward like a swallowed coal:

For one heartbeat — one — she wanted it.

She stood in the smell-of-home air, in the honeyed light, with ten thousand voices asking softly around her, and she thought: *one memory. One. I could give the tannery street, the grey room, the taste of the porridge in the guild refectory — I could give the exile itself, the worst years of my life, and get back my storm, my name, my mother's face —*

That was how it worked. That was the genius of it. It didn't offer you someone else's treasures. It offered you *your own worst memories back to you as payment*, and called it mercy, and the arithmetic felt so easy, so clean, so blessedly final—

She thought of Ivo in the marshes, too weak to stand, leaning his whole weight against her side. She thought of the elder's hands closing her mother's storm-token into her palm. She thought of Halvard's face when he'd said *your mother died sealing the door* — and of Mavros's slip just now, *the mothers who died sealing doors*, and understood that somewhere in this cathedral's coffers, perhaps, was a memory of her mother that was not hers to sell and not his to keep.

"No," Ines said.

Mavros sighed, genuinely regretful, a craftsman watching fine clay refuse the kiln. "No," he echoed. "They all say no, at first. Even I said no, once, in a country whose name you're trying to buy back." He settled behind his altar again, the priest once more, benign and tired. "Then go, little ledger-keeper. Read your debt-clause. Learn its terms. And when you discover what full payment costs — and you will, you're far too thorough not to — remember that I offered you a discount."

She turned and walked up the nave, past the kneeling, glowing, lightening people, past the boy with the mended bird who was now trying to remember why the bird had mattered, past the old man who had gotten his wife's memory back and could no longer remember the wedding.

At the doors, the smell of home reached after her like a hand on her shoulder, gentle as forgiveness, and she shook it off and stepped into the honest rain.

Sefa was waiting under the eaves, wide-eyed. "Well? Did you see him? Is it true everything's free?"

Ines looked back once at the white glass spires, drinking the grey light.

"Nothing is free," she said. "Come on. We need to find Ivo. I know what the door costs now — and I know why Mavros is in such a hurry to seal it before anyone reads the terms."

Because she had read them, in the apse, in the flicker of his slipped tongue and the hunger of his offer. Full payment was a name restored, a lie unmade, a power relinquished — and Mavros feared, more than the door itself, the one debtor stubborn enough to pay in full.

Behind them, faint through the closing doors, the congregation prayed on — answered, radiant, and forgetting — and the sound followed Ines all the way down the wet street like a hymn sung by people who no longer knew what they were singing about.
