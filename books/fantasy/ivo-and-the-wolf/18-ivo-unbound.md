## 18. Ivo Unbound

I had been a god for longer than there were words for being one, and I had never once been afraid.

Fear was a mortal country. I had crossed its borders a thousand times in the shape of a wolf, had lain down in its fields, had drunk from its rivers and felt the cold go all the way through me the way cold only goes through things that can end. But it had always been tourism. Pilgrimage. A god slumming in the flesh of the finite, knowing that at any moment he could rise out of it like steam off water.

Now I stood on the threshold of Vel Sarenne with Mavros's shadow pouring over the door like tar, and I was afraid the way mortals are afraid — wholly, in the body, in the marrow.

The door did not look like a door. It looked like an absence wearing the suggestion of one: a tall seam of darker dark in the cliff face where the erased country bled into the world's edge, humming with the frequency of a name trying to remember itself. Ines stood before it with her ledger open against her forearm, her true name half-spoken, her storm-calling gathered in her hands like something she was learning to hold for the last time. Behind us, Halvard's blood was still cooling on the stones. The collectors had scattered or fled or knelt, dazed by what Ivo the wolf-god had just torn out of their master's chest.

And Mavros — Corvane-shaped, Corvane-faced, but no longer pretending — rose from the wreckage of his own vessel like smoke deciding to be a man again.

"You should have taken my offer," he said. His voice came from everywhere now, from the salt wind and the wet stone and the memory of every prayer ever answered cheaply. "I offered you freedom, little debtor. Instead you bring me a god who has forgotten what he is."

"I haven't forgotten," I said.

It was not entirely true. That was the terrible arithmetic of divinity: the more of it you spent, the less of yourself remained to spend it *from*. When I had shattered Mavros's hold on the door — when I had put my hand into the seam of him and pulled, the way you pull a nail from old wood — I had paid for the pulling. Divinity does not work like muscle. It works like candlelight. Every act of power is wax burning, and I had burned a great deal.

I could feel the edges of myself going soft. Names of storms I had carried since before Ashvale had a harbor. The weight of ten thousand winters I had walked through as winter's shepherd. The taste of the first prayer ever spoken to me, a child's voice asking the snow to be gentle, and my answer, which had been yes.

These were leaving me. Not all at once. Like coins slipping through a coat with a torn pocket, one by one, and each loss made the next easier to lose.

"Ines," I said.

She turned. Her face was streaked with ash and Halvard's blood and the particular grime of someone who has run a very long way and intends to keep running. She had never once looked at me the way people look at gods. Even now, even knowing what I was — what I had been — she looked at me the way you look at a companion on a hard road. That was the whole reason. If I am honest, and I find that near the end of a thing honesty comes easily, that was always the whole reason.

"When I burn this," I told her, "there will not be enough of me left to be what I was. Understand that. Whatever stands up afterward will be — smaller. Fur and appetite and whatever loyalty survived the fire."

Her jaw set in the way I had learned meant she was about to argue with a god, which she did with more frequency and less reverence than anyone in recorded history. "Ivo—"

"Understand it," I said again, gentler. "Because if you grieve him later, I want you to know you were warned by him first."

* * *

Let me tell you what burning divinity feels like, since no one else who has done it is alive to describe it, and I suspect that in a few minutes no one who has done it will exist at all.

It feels like remembering everything at once.

That is the cruelty of it. To unmake power you must first hold all of it in your attention — every miracle, every answered prayer, every century of worship stacked like grain in a barn — and then you must open the barn doors to the wind. You must watch it all go. And because divinity is made of memory as much as anything, what goes is not abstract. It is specific. It is *named*.

I remembered the plague year in the western valleys when they prayed to me for mercy and I gave them a mild winter instead, because mercy was beyond even me, and how the survivors still built me shrines of pine boughs and called it grace. I remembered the girl in Vel Sarenne — before the erasure, before the betrayal — who left honey cakes on a boundary stone for me every solstice of her life, and how after the country was struck from the maps I went looking for her grave and could not find it, because the country that held her grave had been made not to have existed. I remembered Ines. Before. A child standing in her mother's doorway with rain in her hair, watching a wolf watch her, neither of us afraid.

I took all of it, and I opened the doors.

Mavros saw what I was doing and understood it faster than I did — he had always been quick; treachery selects for quickness. "You'll kill yourself for a door," he snarled, and the Corvane-shape split along its seams, the man peeling away from the god beneath like bark from rotten wood. What was underneath was older and uglier: a thing made of withheld answers, of prayers heard and ignored, of the long profit of other people's hope. "There will be nothing left of you but an animal."

"Yes," I said. "That is rather the point."

Gods fight with concepts. Mortals fight with bodies. I had spent so long walking in a wolf's shape that I had learned the third way, the way between: I fought with a body that remembered being a concept. I put my teeth into the seam of the door's holding — because that was what Mavros had done, all those centuries ago; he had not destroyed the door, only gripped it, sunk his divinity into its hinges like a tick into flesh — and I *pulled*, and where my grip failed I put my fire instead.

The barn doors blew wide.

Light came off me in sheets. I felt the plague winter go, and the honey cakes, and the names of storms, and the long slow centuries of snowfall I had counted like a miser counts coin. I felt my dominion over thresholds crumble — oh, the irony of that, the door-god spending his door-ness to free a door. Each loss hurt in a place I did not have anatomy for. Each loss also made me lighter. By the end I was not so much burning as *pouring*, all that hoarded eternity running out of me and into the hinges of a country's prison, and the prison groaned, and cracked, and —

Mavros screamed.

Not with his mouth. With the part of him that was still lodged in the door, the grip-part, the tick-part, and when the fire reached it he learned what every parasite learns eventually: that the host's fever can cook you too. His hold shattered. I felt it go — felt the door's hinges swing loose for the first time since the erasure, felt the seam in the cliff face exhale a breath it had been holding since before Ashvale was built.

And then there was very little of me left to feel anything with.

* * *

Falling out of godhood is quiet. No one tells you that. There is no thunder, no descent in glory. There is simply a moment when the enormous pressure that has pressed against the inside of your skull since the beginning of your existence — the weight of being *addressed*, of being needed, of being a word in other mouths — stops. And in the silence after it you discover how small you are. How warm. How *hungry*. Hunger was the first mortal thing to come back to me, arriving like a debt collector, insistent and undignified.

I collapsed. I want to be precise about this, because precision is one of the few divine habits worth keeping: I did not kneel, I did not sink gracefully to my haunches. I fell over, sideways, like a felled tree, and the ground came up and hit me, and it *hurt*, and the pain was wonderfully, horribly ordinary.

When I could see again, I was a wolf.

Not the god-shaped wolf, the one I wore like a coat with all my senses sharpened past mortal limits. This was the animal itself. Ears that heard merely well. Eyes that saw merely far. A body that ached, that trembled, that wanted meat and sleep and — I discovered with some embarrassment — to be touched. My divinity was gone down to embers. What remained was fur and appetite and, as promised, loyalty. The loyalty was the largest thing left in me. It filled the space where the centuries had been, wall to wall.

Ines was kneeling beside me.

She had her hands in my ruff — when had she gotten there? — and she was saying my name over and over, not the way people say names at gods, but the way you say a name to check that its owner is still in it. "Ivo. Ivo. Stay. Stay with me, stay—"

*I'm here,* I tried to say.

What came out was a sound. A whine, high and thin and utterly without majesty. Somewhere inside me, the ember of what I had been flinched with shame. The rest of me — the new me, the small me — found, to its astonishment, that it did not care. Shame required an audience of worshippers. I had none. I had one woman with ash on her face and blood on her sleeves, and her fingers buried in my fur, and I leaned into her hands with my whole diminished weight and let her hold me up.

"You stupid," she said, and her voice broke in the middle, "magnificent, *stupid* — you said understand it, you warned me, and I still — Ivo, you're *heavy*, when did you get heavy, gods aren't heavy—"

*Mortals are,* I thought. *This is what the weight was always for.*

Behind her, the door stood open.

Not fully — it was a seam of light now rather than a crack, breathing slow, like a lung remembering its work. Through it I could smell Vel Sarenne. Rain on slate. Woodsmoke. The particular green of orchards that had bloomed dead for three hundred years in a country that officially did not exist. My hackles rose. Not with fear. With homesickness — a homesickness borrowed from a people I had shepherded, lodged in me now like everything else that survived the fire.

And then the light shuddered.

I felt it before I saw it: a wrongness in the door's breath, a hitch, a catching. Ines felt it too. She went still against me, her hands tightening in my ruff.

From the wreckage at the cliff's edge, Mavros dragged himself upright.

He was wounded badly — I had cooked the greater part of him in the door's hinges, and what remained was gaunt, flickering, his edges eating themselves like paper in slow flame. The Corvane-vessel lay empty and twitching behind him, discarded at last, a suit of clothes with nothing decent left to wear them. He looked at the open door with an expression I recognized across all our long enmity, because it was the oldest expression there is:

Terror of being caught.

"No," he said. His voice had lost its everywhere-ness. It came only from his mouth now, thin and ragged. "No. I did not survive *everything* to be claimed by *this*." He staggered toward the threshold, and I understood — with the last cold clarity of a god, before even that clarity guttered out — what he meant to do.

He could not close the door from outside. Its terms were written in debts, and debts were not his domain anymore; I had burned that grip out of the hinges. But the door was a door, and doors have two sides, and a thing that slips *through* to the far side — into the country whose land remembers his betrayal, whose soil has waited three hundred years to collect — a thing that seals it *from within*, bracing his ruined divinity against the frame like a dying man barring a gate...

He would trap himself in the country that wanted him dead. And he would trap the door shut forever behind him. A tomb with himself as both corpse and lock.

"He's going for the door!" Ines was already moving, already reaching for the ledger, for her name, for the payment she had prepared — but the door's light was guttering, Mavros's broken shape flowing toward it like oil toward a drain, and I knew with the certainty of the small and mortal creature I had become that she could not speak a full name and outrun him in the same breath.

The embers in me stirred.

There was not much left. Enough to stand — barely. Enough to run. I had spent eternity on hinges and miracles and I had perhaps a wolf's length of fire remaining, and I knew exactly, precisely, with the last accounting I would ever make as a god, what it was for.

I ran.

Stone under paws, real stone, hurting real paws. Mavros heard me coming and shrieked something — a curse, a bargain, my name in the old tongue, offering me back my godhood, offering me *anything* — and I did not slow, because I was no longer a being who could be bought with what I had lost. I hit him at the threshold the way an avalanche hits a valley: with everything, all at once, holding nothing back for afterward.

We went through together.

The last of my fire went out of me in the passage — I felt it leave like breath leaving, like warmth leaving, like the final coin finally slipping through the torn pocket — and it caught him full in the back as we crossed, and spun him, and slammed him against the inner frame of the door not as a lock but as a *prisoner*, pinned to the country that had been waiting for him, while the seam of light behind us began, slowly, mercifully, to widen instead of close.

Then I was only a wolf, lying on the wet grass of a country that had no name yet, unable to rise, watching Mavros struggle against the earth that was beginning, gently, terribly, to pull him down and in.

And through the widening door, silhouetted against the world outside, Ines Winterbourne stepped forward to the threshold — ledger open, true name on her lips — to pay.
