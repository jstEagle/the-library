## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The trouble with talking to goats, Theo Dunmore had learned by her twenty-sixth year, was not that they were bad conversationalists. It was that they were *excellent* ones, and she could never let anyone know it.

"Again," said Juniper, the eldest doe, from atop her rock. "Tell the part about the rain again."

"You've heard the rain story four times this morning."

"And I will hear it five," said Juniper, with the serene entitlement of a creature who had once head-butted a county fair judge. "It is a good story. The rain comes sideways. The dog runs in circles. Tell it."

Theo leaned on the fence rail and looked out over Merrow's Hollow — the terraced pastures going gold at their edges, the millrace glinting below, the chapel bell that never quite rang true — and told the rain story again. Sideways rain. Circling dog. Juniper chewed through every word like it was the sweetest clover in the valley, and when Theo finished, the whole small herd sighed in unison, a sound like wind through dry grass.

"That's the one where you fell in the ditch," said Bramble, the youngest, hopefully.

"No, that's the *other* rain story."

"Oh." Bramble considered this. "Is there a ditch in this one?"

"There is always a ditch in this one if you keep asking."

A goat laughed. People would not have believed goats could laugh, but it was unmistakable — a short, snorting exhalation of pure delight — and Theo felt the familiar ache open under her ribs, the one shaped exactly like everything she couldn't say out loud.

Because here was the thing about Merrow's Hollow: it was founded on quiet. Not silence exactly — the valley was full of noise, dogs barking, hammers ringing, old men complaining about fences — but a particular kind of quiet, the kind written into the town charter in ink gone brown as old blood: *no converse with beasts, no commerce with charms, no calling of things that answer.* The founders had come up over the pass two hundred years ago fleeing something none of them ever named, and they had built their new home like a fortress against enchantment, all straight lines and sensible fences and an annual ceremony designed to wring the magic out of a person like water from laundry.

The Oath of Quiet. Three days from now.

Theo's stomach did its annual slow roll.

"You're doing the face," observed Juniper.

"What face?"

"The face where your mouth smiles and your eyes go to the chapel. The Oath face."

"I don't have an Oath face."

"You absolutely do," said Hazel, who was vain and fat and wise about other creatures' flaws. "Your mother had it too, before—"

"We're not talking about my mother."

"The ditch story has your mother in it," Bramble offered helpfully.

"Bramble."

"I'm just saying. If we're choosing stories."

Theo crouched and scratched Hazel between the horns, because it was easier than answering, and because Hazel's skull was warm from the sun and smelled of dust and sweetness, and because there were mornings — most mornings, honestly — when the only honest conversation in Theo Dunmore's life happened on this side of a fence.

*You could tell them,* whispered a voice she kept locked in a very deep drawer. *You could tell Elder Marsh what you are and see what happens.*

She knew what happened. Everyone in the valley knew what happened, in the way everyone knows things without anyone saying them. Old Tam Weller's granddaughter, sixty years back, had claimed her cat told her where the lost wedding rings were. The family moved away by winter. Nobody wrote letters. The valley forgot her so thoroughly that even her name had worn smooth, like a stone in a creek bed. And that was the gentle version. There were older stories, from the founding years, that the elders did not tell and the gravestones did not record.

So Theo buried it. Every day, twice a day, she took the gift her grandmother had sworn her grandmother had died protecting, folded it up small, and shoved it down somewhere behind her sternum where it sat like a swallowed coal. She answered questions in the flat, sensible voice the valley approved of. She laughed at jokes about weather. She went to chapel and sang hymns about stillness and said nothing, nothing, nothing, while the goats talked and talked and talked.

*Lonely,* said Bramble suddenly, pressing his blunt forehead against her knee. It wasn't a question. Goats didn't do rhetorical.

"A little," Theo admitted. "Don't make the face."

"This is my face."

"It's a tragic face. You look like a wet fence post."

"I am a very handsome wet fence post."

She laughed despite herself, and the laugh echoed off the hillside, and for one unguarded moment she let herself imagine — the way she only allowed herself on high pasture days, when the wind came clean off the ridge — a life where she didn't have to fold herself up small. Where someone asked how her day was and she could say, *well, Juniper thinks the new ram is pretentious,* and the world would simply nod along.

Then the chapel bell tolled the hour, slightly flat, slightly wrong, the way it always had, and the moment closed like a hand.

Three days. The elders would come down from the meetinghouse with their ledger and their little brass bell that didn't ring but hummed, and each adult of the valley would lay a hand on the humming brass and swear the Oath: *I hold no converse with beasts; I hear no voices but God's and my own.* And the bell would stay silent for everyone else, and Theo would stand before it with her heart hammering like a woodpecker in a lightning storm, and pray — actually pray, though she'd never been sure to whom — that whatever the brass bell listened for, her secret sat deep enough to hide.

Twenty-six years. Twenty-five oaths. So far, silence.

"Soon?" asked Juniper, who always knew.

"Soon."

"And you'll come back after, and tell us the rain story, and everything will be the same?"

"Everything will be the same," Theo said, and hated how much she wanted it to be true, and hated more the small traitorous part of her that, some nights, wanted it *not* to be.

* * *

The afternoon went the way good afternoons go: slowly, and then all at once it was milking time. Theo ran the does up the lane, counted heads twice (Bramble had once hidden inside a hollow log for six hours purely to test whether anyone loved him), and got them penned as the light went long and amber across the valley floor.

That was when the dog appeared.

He came up the cart track from the direction of the crossroads, a big rangy thing with a coat like spilled ashes and one ear that stood up while the other flopped, as if he couldn't decide what kind of dog he was. No collar. No owner in sight. He walked into the yard like he'd been invited, which no stray in the history of Merrow's Hollow had ever done — strays knew better than to be seen here — and sat down in front of Theo with the gravity of a magistrate.

"Well," Theo said carefully, out loud, because a stranger's ears might be anywhere. "Hello."

*Not hello,* said the dog. *Listen.*

Her heart stuttered. A wild animal speaking was rare enough; a wild animal speaking *first*, unprompted, urgent, was rarer still. She glanced at the house — curtains drawn, her father napping off his shift at the mill — and knelt by the gatepost, pretending to fuss with the latch.

"I'm listening," she murmured.

*You're the one,* the dog said. It wasn't flattery; it was confirmation, the way you'd check a map against the road. *The one who hears. I've come a long way to find the one who hears.*

"Find me for what?"

The dog's ears went back. His eyes — amber, ringed with gray like old tree bark — flicked toward the crossroads, toward the pass road, toward the world beyond the valley's careful fences.

*The paper man is coming.*

Theo waited. The dog seemed to expect this to mean something.

"...What paper man?"

*Smells of paper,* the dog said, impatient, as though smell were explanation enough. *Old paper. Ink. A book carried close to the chest, the way you'd carry a child or a wound. He'll be here tomorrow. Maybe the day after. He smells of the door.*

Every hair on Theo's arms stood up. "The door? What door? There's no door."

*There is always a door,* said the dog, with the maddening calm of a creature stating the obvious. *Doors are just walls that haven't made up their minds. He is coming to yours.* He stood, shook himself, ash-colored fur rippling. *I've said what I was sent to say. You should decide what you're going to do before he arrives.*

"Wait — sent by whom? Sent by *what?* Who even knows I—"

But the dog was already trotting back down the cart track, unhurried, his crooked ear bobbing, and when Theo called after him — aloud, then in the way that carries farther — he gave no answer except one look over his shoulder. Not a warning look. Something worse.

A pitying one.

* * *

She didn't sleep well. That was fine; she hadn't expected to. She lay in her narrow bed under the eaves listening to the house settle and the millrace mutter in its stony throat, turning the dog's words over like river stones. *The paper man. Smells of the door.* It was nonsense. It had to be nonsense. Strays lied the way magpies stole — compulsively, beautifully, for no reason at all.

Except he hadn't smelled like a liar. Liars had a scent she'd learned young, something sour underneath the sweetness, and this dog had smelled of road dust and rain and, faintly, unmistakably, of old paper and lamp oil and something colder beneath it, something like the air in a cellar where the door has been shut a very long time.

At dawn she gave up on sleep, dressed, and walked out to the pen while the mist still clung to the pasture like a held breath.

Juniper was waiting at the fence, which meant Juniper had something to say.

"Well?" the doe demanded.

"Well what?"

"You were pacing all night. The floorboards told me. What did the dog say?"

Theo hesitated. Then, because it was Juniper, and because the mist made the world feel briefly forgiving, she told her. All of it. The paper man, the door, the pitying backward glance.

Juniper chewed on it for a long time, which was how she thought.

"My grandmother," she said at last, "was alive when the valley was younger. She said the founders didn't build the fences to keep magic *out*." A pause, dramatic, mouth full of early clover. "She said they built them to keep something *in*. Or maybe—" another chew, "—to keep something from remembering the way back."

"That's not comforting, Juniper."

"I'm not a comforting animal. I'm a truthful one. It's why you like me."

It was, annoyingly, exactly why she liked her.

Theo stayed at the fence until the mist burned off and the first cart rattled up the crossroads road — and stopped. She watched it stop. Watched a figure climb down, tall and narrow, in a long coat the color of undyed wool, carrying a leather satchel hugged tight to his chest the way you'd carry a child, or a wound.

The paper man. A day early.

He turned and looked up the hill — not at the farm, not at the goats, not at anything a sensible man would look at — directly at her, across half a mile of morning, as if the mist itself had pointed.

And in her pocket, her hand closed around the little brass token the elders issued for the Oath of Quiet, cold as a coin from a drowned pocket, and Theo Dunmore understood that three days from now, the bell might not be her biggest problem.

The stranger shouldered his satchel and started walking up the valley road. Toward the tavern. Toward the town.

Toward her.
