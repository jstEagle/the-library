## 2. A Date With a Dead Man

The goat had opinions about Theo's dress.

"It's the blue one or nothing," she told Clover, who was chewing on the sleeve in question with the determination of a creature who believed all fabric existed to be tested. "I don't own anything else that doesn't smell like the barn."

Clover released the sleeve and gave her a look of profound goatish pity, which was her way of saying *you're going to make a fool of yourself and I will be here when you get back, unimpressed.*

"Thank you for your support."

Theo changed twice anyway—the blue dress, then the green one, then back to the blue because the green made her feel like a hedge—and braided her hair, and unbraided it, and finally left it alone, which was as close to courage as she could manage on a Thursday evening in Merrow's Hollow.

She'd said yes to this date three times before she actually said yes. The first time, Jonah Reyes had asked so casually—leaning against the fence of her mother's pasture like he'd grown there, all long limbs and an easy grin—that she'd assumed he was joking and laughed. The second time he'd brought her a jar of his grandmother's honey, which was either very sweet or slightly alarming, and she'd said "maybe." The third time he'd simply stood there in the rain without an umbrella, holding the honey again because apparently he only owned the one jar, and something about the sheer stubborn dampness of him had worn her down.

That had been two weeks ago. His grandmother had died a month after that, and the date had kept getting postponed—first for the funeral, then for the settling of the estate, then for reasons Jonah never quite explained, just smiled apologetically and said, "Soon. I promise. There's something I want to show you first."

*There's something I want to show you first.* Most people meant a view, or a ring, or a terrible tattoo. Theo had spent those two weeks trying not to imagine what a man like Jonah Reyes might mean by it.

Now she walked down into the village as the light went amber, past the meeting hall where the elders were already hanging the white banners for the Oath of Quiet—three weeks early, which was strange, but everything about this year felt slightly off-key—and arrived at the Gilded Ewe with her heart doing something embarrassing against her ribs.

Jonah was already there, of course. He'd taken the corner table by the window, the good one, the one everyone wanted, and he'd somehow made it look like he belonged to it. He stood when he saw her, which nobody did anymore, and the grin that broke across his face was so genuinely glad that Theo's carefully rehearsed opening line evaporated somewhere around her collarbone.

"You came," he said.

"You sound surprised."

"I've been surprised since the day I met you. It's growing on me." He pulled out her chair. He'd ordered her cider already—he'd remembered, from one conversation weeks ago, that she liked the dry kind—and there was bread on the table, still steaming, and a candle he'd clearly bullied the innkeeper into lighting despite the season being entirely wrong for candles.

"You did all this," she said, sitting, "for a first date at a tavern."

"I did all this," Jonah said, settling across from her, "because I've been looking forward to it for two weeks and I refuse to let it be ordinary. Ordinary can wait until the second date."

"And you're confident there'll be a second date?"

"I'm hopeful. Hope is my whole strategy. It's carried me surprisingly far."

It should have been too much. It was, objectively, too much—candles and bread and remembered cider and a grin like a struck match. But Jonah wore excess the way some men wore hats: lightly, a little crooked, easy to forgive. And underneath it, Theo noticed, there was something else. His eyes kept flicking to the door. To the window. To the satchel propped against his chair like a sleeping animal.

"You keep checking the room," she said.

"Do I?" He laughed, and it was a good laugh, warm and self-deprecating. "Sorry. Bad habit. My gran used to say I had the manners of a man expecting company he didn't invite."

"What does that mean?"

"No idea. She said a lot of things." Something crossed his face then—quick, like a fish under ice—and was gone before Theo could name it. He lifted his cider. "To postponed dates and stubborn people."

"To stubborn people," Theo agreed, and clinked.

They talked. That was the thing she hadn't expected—to simply *talk*. About nothing, mostly. About whether the miller's new apprentice was secretly a fugitive prince (Jonah's theory) or just bad at his job (hers). About whether rain counted as weather or as a personal attack. He listened the way few people did—not waiting for his turn, but actually listening, leaning in, asking the second question instead of the first. When she made a joke about the elders' banner-hanging, he laughed so hard he knocked the candle sideways and had to catch it, and the innkeeper glared, and Jonah mouthed *sorry* with such theatrical remorse that Theo laughed too, really laughed, the kind she usually saved for the goats where no one could hear it.

This is fine, she thought. This is more than fine. This is—

And then the dog came in.

It was the stray from the ridge, the one who'd been haunting her pasture all week, the one who'd looked at her with those too-knowing eyes and warned her about *the paper man*. It trotted into the Gilded Ewe like it paid rent, weaving between tables while the innkeeper shouted halfheartedly, and stopped dead at the sight of Jonah.

Or rather—at the sight of the satchel.

The fur along its spine rose in a slow wave, from shoulders to tail, like wind moving through wheat. A low sound started in its chest, deeper than any dog that size had a right to produce.

"Oh no," Jonah said mildly. "Hello again."

"You know this dog?"

"He's been following me around the valley for days. Very rude about it." Jonah leaned down, extending a hand, all easy charm. "We've met, friend. I'm harmless."

The dog did not take the hand. The dog took one step forward, planted itself between Jonah's chair and the table, and growled directly at the satchel—a low, continuous, deliberate sound, the sound of an animal saying something it considered extremely important.

*Not him,* the growl said, in the wordless way animals say things. *That.*

Theo's stomach dropped. Because she could hear it—really hear it, beneath the sound, in the old language she'd been pretending not to understand since she was six years old. The dog wasn't growling at Jonah. The dog was growling at what Jonah carried.

"Maybe it doesn't like men," she managed. Her voice came out steady. She'd had practice sounding steady; she'd been practicing her whole life.

"Apparently not." Jonah withdrew his hand, unhurt, unbothered—but Theo caught it again, that flicker under the surface, the fish under the ice. He glanced at the dog the way you glance at someone who's said your name in a crowd. "Innkeeper! Could someone see this fellow out? Gently?"

A kitchen boy lured the dog toward the door with a heel of bread. At the threshold it turned and looked back—not at Jonah, not at the room, but at Theo. Directly, deliberately, the way it had looked at her on the ridge.

*You know,* that look said. *You heard me. Do something.*

Then it was gone into the dusk, and the innkeeper was apologizing, and Jonah was refilling her cider and saying something charming about how even the local wildlife had opinions on his character, and Theo sat there smiling the smile she'd practiced her whole life while cold water ran quietly through her chest.

Because she had heard the dog. She always heard them. And now she knew something impossible: that the dog was afraid of a book.

* *

"So," Jonah said, when the plates were cleared and the candle had burned down to a fat golden thumb, "the thing I wanted to show you."

Here it was. Theo set down her cup. "I've been wondering when we'd get there."

He reached for the satchel, and she noticed his hands weren't quite steady. He noticed her noticing, and grinned, and the grin steadied them both somehow. "Fair warning. This is going to sound insane. Everything about this sounds insane. I've had a month to get used to it and I still hear myself talking and think, *Jonah, mate, who are you kidding.*"

"Just show me."

He drew out a book.

It was leather-bound, old, the color of dark tea, scarred and soft at the corners like something much handled and much loved. No title on the cover. No markings at all, except a faint impression near the spine that might once have been letters, worn away by a century of thumbs. It was smaller than she'd imagined—she hadn't known she'd imagined it, but the moment she saw it she felt she had—and it seemed to drink the candlelight rather than reflect it.

"My grandmother's," Jonah said. "Found it in her attic after—" He paused, adjusted. "After she passed. Wrapped in oilcloth, inside a trunk, inside a locked room I swear I never knew existed. The whole attic smelled like cedar and lightning storms. And this was just... sitting there. Like it was waiting for me to find it."

"That's a strange thing to leave in an attic."

"She was a strange woman." He said it fondly, but his thumb moved over the cover in a small unconscious circle, the way you touch a scar. "Everyone thought she was just a bit odd—kept to herself, talked to herself, never went to the Oath of Quiet ceremonies. Refused, every single year. They fined her. She paid the fines and stayed home and sang to herself, badly, on purpose I think." He huffed a laugh. "I loved her enormously."

Theo's hands had gone cold around her cup. *Refused the Oath. Every year.* Nobody refused the Oath. Nobody in Merrow's Hollow had ever refused the Oath, not in living memory, because the Oath was the whole point of the valley—the great annual renewal of silence, the covenant that kept Merrow's Hollow safe and quiet and exactly what it pretended to be.

"Can I see it?" she asked. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears.

"That's the thing." Jonah turned the book slowly on the table, around and around, like a compass needle finding itself. "I was going to show you eventually anyway, but tonight felt right, and I need to tell someone before I convince myself I dreamed the whole month. Because here's what's insane, Theo. Here's why the dog hates it, probably, dogs hate weird things, right?" He laughed, high and thin. "The book has writing in it. Old writing. And some of it is..."

He stopped turning the book. Opened it, gently, to a page near the middle, and turned it to face her.

The paper was the color of old cream, dense with faded brown ink in a spidery, patient hand. And there, partway down the page, in ink so fresh it still shone:

*The goat-keeper hears what goats say. She hides it well. She will hide it until the paper man comes, and then she will not be able to hide anything at all.*

Theo did not move. Somewhere behind her, the tavern went on—laughter, clinking cups, the innkeeper's voice—and it all happened at a great distance, underwater, irrelevant. She was aware of her own heartbeat, absurdly loud. She was aware that Jonah was watching her face with an expression caught between hope and apology.

"Theo?" he said softly. "Your name's in it too. Lower down. Tonight's date. I checked—I thought I was losing my mind, I checked the almanac three times. It's dated a hundred years ago. The handwriting hasn't changed since the first page. Whoever wrote this wrote your name last week. In 1904."

Her mouth opened. Nothing came out.

*The goat-keeper hears what goats say.* Not accused. Not exposed. Just... written. Recorded. Known, a hundred years before she was born, by a stranger's patient spider-hand. Every careful silence she'd ever kept, every lie she'd told the elders, every conversation she'd buried in the barn with the only creatures allowed to know her—all of it already on paper, already public, already *prophecy*, sitting on a tavern table between the bread crumbs and the candle stub.

She thought of the dog on the ridge. *The paper man is coming.*

It wasn't a man. It had never been a man. It was this—a book, walking around in a satchel, wearing a charming stranger like a coat.

"Theo." Jonah's voice, gentle. "Say something. You've gone the color of the tablecloth."

"I—" She swallowed. Tried again. "What else does it say?"

"That's the other thing." He hesitated, and she watched him decide to trust her, watched the decision happen in real time, and hated herself a little for how much she wanted to deserve it. "Most of it's faded. Unreadable. Except the last lines. On the last legible page, it says..."

He turned to the end of the readable section. His finger found the line. He read aloud, quiet enough that only she could hear it over the tavern noise:

"*And the valley opens.*"

The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft.

Outside, far up on the dark ridge, a dog began to howl—one long, rising note, like a warning being sung, or a song being started.

Jonah closed the book carefully, the way you close a door on a room you're not sure is empty. "So," he said, with a wobbly attempt at his usual grin. "Told you it sounded insane. Second date's probably off, isn't it."

Theo stared at the leather cover, drinking the candlelight. Her whole hidden life lay open in it like a wound, written a century ago in a hand that hadn't finished writing yet.

"Jonah," she heard herself say, "who was your grandmother?"

He opened his mouth to answer.

And every lamp in the Gilded Ewe went out at once.
