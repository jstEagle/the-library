## 3. The Book That Writes Back

Theo Dunmore had a system for lying to her mother, and the system was goats.

It worked like this: when her mother called up the stairs—"Theo! Are you getting ready? Jonah will be here at seven!"—Theo would already be outside in the paddock, ostensibly doing evening rounds, actually having a full and animated conversation with Marigold about whether the man she'd agreed to have dinner with was going to be a disaster.

"He found it in his grandmother's attic," Theo said, scratching the spot behind Marigold's ear that made the goat lean into her like a falling tree. "A book. A hundred-year-old book. Who brings that on a first date?"

*Who wears a coat with buttons,* said Marigold, who held strong opinions about human fashion and none of them flattering.

"It's not the book I mind exactly. It's that he wouldn't let me touch it."

*Then don't go.*

"I'm going. Obviously I'm going. He's funny, and he remembered that I said I liked the plum cider, and he didn't flinch when Bramble screamed at him." Bramble screamed at everyone; it was less a test than a weather event. "I just—there was something about how he held it. Like it was breathing."

Marigold considered this, chewing with the slow deliberation of a creature who had never once been wrong about anything and knew it.

*Books don't breathe,* she said finally. *But paper remembers hands.*

"That's either very wise or you're just hungry."

*Both,* said Marigold, which was, as usual, correct.

From the kitchen window her mother's silhouette gestured with the urgency of a woman whose daughter was about to be late to the first date she'd agreed to in two years. Theo gave the goat one last scratch, told her she'd report back, and went inside to perform the ancient ritual of pretending she hadn't spent twenty minutes asking a goat for romantic advice.

* * *

The date itself had gone, by Theo's standards, suspiciously well.

Jonah Reyes had arrived at the Hollow Lantern with his collar crooked and cider foam on his upper lip within ten minutes of sitting down, and he'd laughed—at himself, mostly, which Theo ranked among the more attractive varieties of laughter. He asked questions like he intended to remember the answers. When she mentioned she kept goats, he didn't do the polite nod people did before steering the conversation somewhere more impressive; he leaned in and asked their names, and their personalities, and whether Bramble screamed at him specifically or as a matter of principle.

"Principle," Theo said. "You happen to violate several."

"I respect that in a goat."

He'd brought the book. Of course he'd brought the book—it sat on the table between them through the whole evening like a chaperone with a grudge, dark leather gone soft and brown at the corners, its spine cracked in the way of things that had been opened ten thousand times. Twice during dinner Theo caught herself reaching for it and twice Jonah moved it, casually, the way you'd move a glass away from an elbow—not protective, exactly. More like someone keeping a dog from a door they weren't sure was locked.

"You said your grandmother left it to you?" Theo asked, near the end, when the lanterns had burned low and the tavern keeper was stacking chairs with pointed musicality.

"She died in March. I never met her—she was the family mystery. Lived out here alone her whole life, wouldn't take calls, sent birthday cards with money in them and nothing else written on them." He turned his cup slowly on the table. "When we cleared out the house, this was under the floorboards in her bedroom. Wrapped in oilcloth. Like somebody was hiding it from the house itself."

"And you brought it tonight because...?"

Jonah opened his mouth. Closed it. Ran a hand through his hair in a way that suggested the honest answer and the socially acceptable answer were having a fistfight behind his teeth.

"Because I keep dreaming about it," he said finally. "Before I even found it. For months—dreams where I'm writing in it. My handwriting, but older somehow. And then I found it, and it was—" He stopped again, and this time what came out was quieter. "It was warm. Theo, the book was *warm*, like something had just finished using it."

Any sensible person would have laughed. Would have said *you found a weird old book in a dead woman's floor, that'll rattle anyone.* Theo, who had spent her entire life learning exactly which of her own experiences could safely be said aloud and which had to be folded small and buried deep, recognized the look on his face. It was the look of someone holding something true and knowing it sounded insane.

So instead of laughing, she said: "Can I see it?"

And Jonah Reyes—the charming, strange man who had guarded that book all night like a dragon with good table manners—looked at her for a long moment and said, "Tomorrow. Come by tomorrow. There's something I want to check first, and if I'm wrong, I don't want to scare you off before the second date."

"The second date is contingent on the book?"

"The second date is contingent on you still liking me after the book," he said, and paid the tab, and walked her home under a sky doing its best impression of spilled sugar, and at her gate he hesitated like a man standing at the edge of a diving board, and then kissed her cheek and said, "Nine o'clock. If I don't chicken out."

She watched him go. Behind the fence line, unseen in the dark, a low voice observed:

*His shadow walks wrong.*

Theo froze. The stray dog—the same yellow, wire-thin dog who had warned her about the paper man two days ago—sat at the gate post, ears forward, watching Jonah's retreating figure with the fixed attention of a creature reading a document.

"What do you mean, wrong?"

*It lags,* the dog said. *Like it's deciding whether to follow him.*

Theo looked. She saw only an ordinary young man walking down a lane under ordinary stars, shoulders hunched against a chill he probably wasn't feeling, a shape under his arm that might have been a book.

"Dogs," she muttered, "are alarmists."

*I'm a realist,* said the dog, and trotted off into the dark, leaving Theo standing at her own gate feeling the exact temperature of a sentence she couldn't yet translate.

* * *

Sleep declined to come.

This was not unusual. Theo's nights were often crowded—Bramble's opinions on the moon, the neighbor's cat filing complaints about the neighbor, once, memorably, a passing flock of geese reciting what could only be described as gossip. But tonight the animals were quiet, and the quiet was worse, because it left room for thinking, and thinking left room for the book.

*Warm like something had just finished using it.*

At half past midnight she gave up, lit the lamp on her desk, and tried to read three pages of a novel about a sea captain. At one she put the novel down. At ten past one she was pulling on her boots.

Her mother's house slept around her as she crept down the stairs. Outside, the valley lay silvered and still, the millrace murmuring its one long syllable, and the tavern dark except for the window where the keeper sometimes sat late over his accounts. Jonah had left the book with the keeper for safekeeping—he'd said so, pressing coins into the man's hand, saying he'd collect it in the morning—and the keeper, being a man who believed all problems could be solved by locking things in drawers, had put it in the drawer behind the bar.

Getting it out involved a window latch, a chair, and a level of guilt Theo managed to suppress by telling herself she was doing it *for* Jonah. This was, she admitted somewhere around the third drawer, possibly not true. Possibly she needed to know. Possibly she had spent her whole life being the only person in Merrow's Hollow who heard voices nobody else could hear, and some starved part of her wanted—desperated—to not be the only one anymore.

The drawer slid open. The book lay there in the lamplight she'd brought, and it was—

Warm.

Not metaphorically. Not the residual warmth of a room or a body nearby. Warm the way a stone is warm hours after the sun has gone, warm from the inside out, and when Theo lifted it into her lap and sat down right there on the tavern floor with her back against the bar, she felt it settle against her palms like a sleeping animal.

"Oh," she whispered. "Oh, you're real."

The cover bore no title. Just an embossed circle worn nearly smooth, and beneath it, tooled faintly into the leather, letters she had to tilt toward the lamp to catch: *V E S S A N Y*. The word meant nothing to her. No country by that name appeared in any atlas she'd ever seen, and Theo had read every atlas in the valley schoolhouse twice, mostly for the maps.

She opened it.

The pages were dense with handwriting—a flowing, slanted script, ink browned with age, margins crowded with corrections and crossed-out lines and little marginal sketches: a bell, a bird, a door. The opening page carried a date in the old style. Theo's arithmetic, done automatically, landed her a hundred years back. Give or take a season.

*A century-old book of prophecies,* she thought, and almost laughed at herself for the phrase, because prophecy was for storybooks, for the kind of tales her mother disapproved of, for—

Then she saw her own name.

It was halfway down the first page, in the middle of a paragraph, and at first she took it for coincidence—Dunmore wasn't a rare name, there were Dunmores three farms over—but no. The whole line was there. *The goat-keeper of the hollow, T. Dunmore, who speaks with beasts and tells no one.* Her breath went thin. She read it again. And again. The lamp flame stood perfectly still, as if it too were reading.

Below it, the entry continued, and here Theo's skin began to crawl in earnest, because the handwriting changed. The old slanted script gave way to something tighter, newer, and the ink—she touched it before she could stop herself, and her fingertip came away smudged.

Smudged. Wet. *Fresh.*

The new lines were dated tonight. Tonight's date, written in a book a century old, in ink that was still drying, and the words were these:

*The goat-keeper comes to the book alone, at night, against every caution she has ever been taught. She cannot help it. She has never once been able to help it.*

Theo looked around the empty tavern. Looked at the window she'd climbed through. Looked at her own hands, ink-smudged, holding the evidence.

"This isn't funny," she told the book, quietly, the way she spoke to frightened animals. Steady voice. Slow breathing. "Whoever you are, this isn't funny."

The book did not answer, because books do not answer, and Theo knew that better than anyone alive. But the warmth pulsed under her palms, once, patient, like a heartbeat waiting to be counted, and the fresh ink continued to spread beneath her fingers, letter by letter, forming words as she watched:

*She turns the page.*

"I absolutely do not turn the page," Theo said, and turned the page.

The next leaf was different. Older entries crowded it, dated across decades, and the handwriting cycled—three hands, maybe four, generations of the same slant, as though the book had been passed down a family of writers who were all, in some uncanny way, the same writer. She skimmed. Dates, fragments, warnings. *The hounds will smell it on her.* *Do not let her cross at the mill.* *If the Warden rides before the Choir gathers, everything fails.* Names she didn't know. A word that recurred like a refrain: *Vessany, Vessany, Vessany.*

And then, near the bottom of the page, the tight new handwriting again, fresh as blood, and this time the ink formed while she read, each letter arriving like a footstep in a dark hall:

*She asks the only question that matters. She asks it aloud, though there is no one to hear.*

Theo's mouth had gone dry. She became aware that she was, in fact, about to ask a question aloud, in an empty tavern, at one in the morning, to a book—which was precisely the sort of behavior that got girls in Merrow's Hollow talked about, and talking was the least of what it might get her, because if any of this was what it appeared to be, then the Oath of Quiet, the elders, the whole careful architecture of her hidden life was built on sand, on a foundation that had known about her all along, that had *written about her* before her grandmother was born—

She asked anyway. Of course she asked. She had never once been able to help it.

"What happens to me?"

The ink gathered. Paused. Theo realized she was holding her breath, and that the warmth of the book had risen to meet her face like breath from an open oven, and that somewhere far off—impossibly, underneath the silence—the millrace had changed its note.

The last legible line wrote itself slow and deliberate, each stroke pressed deep enough to dent the next three pages:

*and the valley opens.*

Then the ink stopped. The warmth faded—not gone, but banked, like a fire settled for the night. The book lay in her lap, ordinary again, a dead woman's leather and paper, except for the wet gleam of words that had not existed ninety seconds ago and now could never be unwritten.

Theo sat on the tavern floor for a long time.

Outside, the sky was graying toward the false dawn. Somewhere up the valley a rooster cleared its throat and thought better of it. And the thought that finally surfaced through the vertigo, through the cold spiral of *it knew, it always knew, my whole life was a paragraph in someone else's draft*—the thought that surfaced was not fear at all.

It was indignation.

Because whoever had written those lines had gotten one thing wrong, and Theo Dunmore had spent thirty years perfecting the art of noticing when creatures lied to her. The book said *she cannot help it.* Said it like a diagnosis. Like fate.

But she could help it. She helped it every single day. Every conversation swallowed, every question unanswered, every morning she smiled at her mother and said nothing about what the horses thought of the new farrier—that was helping it, that was the hardest work of her life, done daily, unthanked, invisible. Whoever wrote that line had never met her. Had merely *predicted* her, the way you predict weather, carelessly, from a distance.

"Well," Theo said aloud, to the book, to the gray window, to whatever patient thing was listening on the other side of the leather. "You don't know me as well as you think."

The warmth stirred. Faintly. Almost like laughter.

She should put it back. She knew she should put it back, climb out the window, go home, sleep, and spend tomorrow deciding whether to tell Jonah any of this or simply never speak to him again, which was the safer road, the road the valley would applaud. That was the plan. That was the sensible—

That was when she heard the dogs.

Not one dog. Many. Far off up the valley, past the terraces, past anywhere dogs were allowed to be at this hour, a chorus of baying rose and fell in a rhythm too organized to be wild, and beneath it, threading through it like a wire through wool, came a sound Theo had no name for: a pressure, a presence, moving downhill toward the hollow with the unhurried certainty of something that had read the same page she had—and hadn't stopped at the last legible line.

In her lap, the book grew warm again.

On the fresh page, one final word surfaced, small, almost apologetic, in ink that glistened:

*Run.*

Theo ran.
