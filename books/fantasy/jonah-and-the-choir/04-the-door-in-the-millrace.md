## 4. The Door in the Millrace

Jonah Reyes had rehearsed his confession eleven times on the walk over, and by the time he reached Theo's gate he had abandoned all eleven drafts in favor of blurting it out like a man with hiccups.

"The book follows me," he said.

Theo looked up from the goat she was scratching behind the ears — a big cream-colored doe with the patient expression of someone who has heard worse — and said, "Good morning to you too."

"Sorry. Hi. Good morning." He held up the leather book, which he had wrapped in a dish towel for reasons that felt sensible at three a.m. and ridiculous now. "I need to tell you something, and I'd like you to not look at me like I'm crazy until I'm finished, and *then* you can look at me however you want."

The goat made a sound. Theo tilted her head as though listening, then nodded once, which Jonah had learned over their two dates was something she did — a small private nod at nothing, at the air, at animals — and which he had decided not to ask about yet because everyone was entitled to a few mysteries.

"Okay," she said, unlooping the gate. "Talk."

They walked up through the pasture while he told her. He told her about the dreams first, because the dreams were the least insane part, and he wanted to establish a baseline of sanity before things got weird. In the dreams there was always water going the wrong way. There was a mill with no miller. And there was a door standing in open air, framed in pale wood, with nothing behind it but more air, and every night in the dream he walked toward it, and every night the book was already there, waiting on the threshold like a cat that had decided this house was its house now.

"Last Tuesday," he said, "I woke up and the book wasn't on my nightstand. It was on the windowsill. Facing the hills. I don't move in my sleep. My roommate can confirm I sleep like a dropped sandbag."

"Charming image."

"Thank you. Wednesday morning it was in the kitchen. Thursday, on the front step, pointing down the street toward your valley like a compass needle with a leather cover. I started putting it in a drawer. I put a dictionary on top of it. The dictionary is nine pounds; I checked." He patted the towel-wrapped shape. "This morning the dictionary was on the floor and the drawer was closed."

Theo had stopped walking. She was looking at the book the way people look at a dog that has just done something uncanny — not fear exactly, but a recalculation of the world's rules.

"It's been doing that since your grandmother died?" she asked.

"Since before. Since I found it in her attic in June. But it's gotten... eager. Lately it doesn't feel like an object. It feels like an appointment." He laughed, a little too high. "That's the crazy part. Well. That's one of the crazy parts. There's a ranking system."

"And where does the mill fit in the ranking?"

He blinked. "How did you know there was a mill?"

"Because there's only one old mill in this valley, and because last night my dog wouldn't stop staring at the north ridge, and because—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening around whatever the second *because* was. "Because I've been having the dream too. Since the night you showed me the book. Water running uphill. A door with no wall."

Jonah felt something loosen and tighten in his chest at the same time. Loosen, because he wasn't alone in it. Tighten, because if the dream had crossed from his head into hers, then it wasn't a dream. Dreams didn't commute.

"So we go together," he said, "or we both keep dreaming about it forever, and honestly I don't love my odds of sleeping ever again."

"I have goats," Theo said, but she was already turning toward the ridge, and the cream-colored doe followed her three steps before stopping at the fence line with the finality of a creature who knew exactly where the story was going and wanted no part of it.

* * *

The mill sat in the crook of the river where the valley pinched narrow, a stone shell with its roof long gone and its wheel frozen mid-turn, furred green with a century of moss. Jonah had driven past it a dozen times without once seeing it, which he now understood was a skill the mill possessed rather than a failure of his attention. Up close it refused to be ignorable. The stones were fitted so tightly there was no mortar between them, and the doorway gaped black, and the whole structure had the posture of something holding its breath.

"There," Theo said quietly.

The river came down from the north, quick and clear over brown stones, and at the millrace — the stone channel where water should have poured through the wheel — the water stopped. Not dammed. Stopped, the way a sentence stops. Then it turned. Jonah watched a leaf come downstream, reach the race, hesitate like a shopper at a door, and travel back up the channel against the current, smooth as a bead pulled on a string.

"Okay," he said. "That's the crazy part. That's number one on the list."

"Your list needs updating." Theo had gone very still, her face tipped slightly, the way she did when she was listening to nothing. "Jonah. Do you hear that?"

He listened. Wind in the alders. Water doing its illegal business. His own pulse. "Nothing."

"That's what I mean." Her voice had gone thin. "There are always birds down here. Always. And there's—" She pressed her lips together, and he watched her decide not to say the rest, and filed it away with the head-tilts and the nods, another coin in the jar of Theo's mysteries, which was getting heavy enough to rattle.

Inside, the mill was cool and dim, light falling through the empty roof in dusty columns. The great axle hung overhead like the rib of some enormous patient animal. And against the far wall — against what should have been wall, what *was* wall, gray stone, mortared by nothing, solid — stood a door.

It was a plain door. Pale wood, iron hinges, a latch worn bright by hands. It stood flush against the stone as though the stone had grown around it, or as though the door had arrived first and the wall had been an afterthought. No frame. No seam. Just a door, standing in a wall, in a ruin, in a valley that had never been anywhere important.

Jonah's mouth had gone dry. "In the dream," he managed, "there's a doormat."

"There is not a doormat."

"There isn't. I checked. I want it noted that I checked." He unwrapped the book from its dish towel with fingers that didn't entirely feel like his. The leather was warm. It was always warm now, blood-warm, and when he held it near the door the warmth sharpened into something almost like eagerness, like a dog feeling the leash come off its hook.

The book fell open in his hands. It had begun doing that too — he no longer needed to find the page; the page found him. The ink there was fresh, glossy, wet-looking, though when he touched it, it was dry. The same spidery hand that filled the rest of the book, the hand he knew from a hundred attic afternoons, his grandmother's handwriting, unmistakable, impossible:

*the mill remembers the way. bring the girl who talks to the quiet ones.*

Jonah read it twice. Then he read it aloud, because some things need air to be real, and because he wanted to watch Theo's face when she heard it.

She had gone white. Not dramatically — she wasn't a dramatic person; even her fear was tidy, buttoned-up, kept in good repair — but the color left her face the way water leaves a sink, all at once and completely.

"'Bring the girl,'" she repeated. "It knows about me."

"Theo—"

"It knows about me and I've never—" She shut her eyes. When she opened them, they were furious, which he was learning was her version of frightened. "I don't talk to anyone. That's the whole point of me. I keep everything *in*, that's my entire personality, and some dead woman's diary is writing me into—"

"She's not dead," Jonah said. "Or. I don't know. She wrote this a hundred years ago, or it wrote itself, or—" He ran a hand through his hair. "Here's the thing I haven't said out loud. Here's the actual bottom of the crazy list." He made himself look at the door instead of at her, because it was easier. "In the dreams, when I get close to the door, I hear singing. Very far off. Like a choir practicing in the next room of the world. And underneath the singing there's..." He swallowed. "There's my grandmother's voice. I'd know it anywhere. She used to sing me to sleep with a song about a bell that wouldn't ring. And in the dream she says *come home*, Jonah. She says it like I've been late for dinner."

The silence after that had texture. Dust drifted through the light columns. Somewhere above them, the frozen wheel creaked — a small sound, but in that silence it landed like a gunshot, and both of them flinched.

Then Theo grabbed his arm.

"Don't," she whispered.

"Don't what?"

"*Listen.* Don't listen to it. Whatever it's saying to you—" Her grip was hard enough to hurt. Her eyes were fixed not on the door but on the air beside it, beside the hinge, beside the dark seam where a door meets a wall, and she was tilting her head again, that terrible listening tilt, and this time tears were standing in her eyes without permission. "Oh," she said softly. "Oh, no. No, no—"

"Theo. What do you hear?"

Her voice came out flat and fast, the voice of someone reading bad news quickly to get it over with. "Animals. On the other side. Lots of them. They're—" She stopped. Started again. "They're begging us not to open it. They're saying *don't come, don't come, it's a trap, it eats the ones who knock.*" Her breath hitched. "There's a horse. An old horse, he's so scared, he keeps saying *the gray one is coming, the gray one rides at dusk*—"

"The gray one?"

"I don't know! I don't know what any of this is!" She let go of his arm and stepped back from the door with her hands pressed flat against her thighs, physically holding herself still. "Jonah, I hear them like they're standing next to me. I've never heard anything like this. This isn't — this is different, this is *loud*—"

And here was the thing Jonah would think about later, in the cold, in the dark, in all the hours afterward when he tried to reconstruct how two sensible people had walked through a door into the end of their ordinary lives: she was terrified. Genuinely, bone-deep terrified, tears on her cheeks, every instinct screaming at her to run. And she did not run. She stood there shaking, and then she wiped her face with the heel of her hand in one brisk motion, the way you'd fix a crooked picture frame, and she said:

"Well. We can't leave the horse."

He laughed. He couldn't help it — a cracked, nervous, helpless laugh. "We don't even know if the horse is real."

"Everything else is real. Why not the horse?" But she wasn't laughing, and she wasn't looking at the door anymore. She was looking at him, and what was in her face was worse than fear: it was recognition. "You're going to open it regardless. Aren't you. Even knowing what I just told you."

Jonah looked at the door. The book lay open in his hands, warm as a sleeping animal, and beneath his grandmother's fresh line the ink was moving — new words surfacing slowly, like fish rising, like something being written *right now*, by someone patient, on the other side of a hundred years:

*and the valley opens.*

"In the dream," he said quietly, "when I touch the handle, I'm not scared. It's the only place I'm not scared anymore. Isn't that the most suspicious thing you've ever heard? The haunted magic door is the one place I feel safe. My grandmother is on the other side calling me home, and something in me believes her, and I know — I *know* — that's exactly how traps work." He met Theo's eyes. "But she sang me that song about the bell. Every night for years. And whoever is writing this book knows her handwriting better than I do, because it *is* her handwriting, and I have to know. You understand? I have to know if she's asking me or luring me. Those aren't the same thing, and I can't live the rest of my life not knowing which one it was."

Theo was silent for a long moment. Through the broken roof, clouds moved. Down in the race, the water ran patiently backward, keeping its own counsel.

"If I say no," she said at last, "you'll open it alone."

"I'd rather not."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the truest one I've got."

She exhaled through her nose — a sound he would come to know well, the sound of Theo Dunmore adding one more item to the enormous invisible ledger of things she carried so nobody else had to. She rolled her shoulders back. She stepped forward, past him, close enough to the door that its shadow touched her boots, and she glared at the pale wood as if it were a person who owed her money.

"Fine," she said. "But I'm knocking first. Politely. Because apparently I'm the only one here with manners, including the horse."

She raised her fist — and paused, head tilting one last time, listening to the chorus of frightened voices on the far side of the wood. Jonah watched her face do something complicated: grief and wonder and terror braided into a single expression, like a knot no one could untie.

"They're still saying don't," she whispered. "All of them. Except—" Her brow furrowed. "Except one. Way under the others. Something small. It's saying..."

The book in Jonah's hands went hot.

"...it's saying *welcome home*," Theo finished, and her hand, of its own accord, or of the door's, or of the hundred years' worth of patience pressing through it — her hand closed around the latch, and lifted.

The door swung inward on darkness that smelled, impossibly, impossibly, of ripe apples and woodsmoke and rain on warm stone.

Neither of them remembered stepping through.
