## 5. Vessany

The first thing Theo noticed was the light.

It wasn't valley light. Valley light came filtered through fog and pine and a hundred years of careful, deliberate forgetting—pale, apologetic, always asking permission. This light arrived like it had been waiting for them. It poured over the threshold of the door in the millrace and pooled on the packed earth at their feet, warm as bathwater, gold as honey left in the sun.

Jonah stepped through first, because of course he did. He was the one with the book, the one with the dreams, the one who'd spent the last hour insisting—with the particular cheerfulness of a man trying to convince himself—that backward-flowing water was "probably fine, hydrologically speaking."

Theo followed because the animals on the other side had stopped begging her not to come. Somewhere in the last few steps their voices had changed—from *don't* to something she didn't have a word for yet. A hush. The way a room goes quiet when someone important walks in.

She stepped out of the millrace and into Vessany, and every hair on her arms stood up.

"Oh," she said.

Terraced hillsides rose away from them in long green steps, each one planted with orchards—apple, plum, quince, things she didn't recognize with leaves like silver coins. The terraces climbed so high they vanished into a haze that wasn't quite cloud, and the haze itself seemed lit from underneath, as if the whole sky were a lamp with the shade turned down low. Stone walls lined the paths between rows, old walls, lichened walls, walls built by hands that had expected to be building for centuries.

And everywhere—everywhere—the bells.

They hung in wooden frames at the corners of fields. They hung from branches, from crooked posts, from little stone towers no bigger than ovens. Small bells, big bells, cracked bells, bells green with a century of weather. Not one of them was ringing. The wind moved through the orchards and stirred the leaves and passed the bells by like strangers, and the silence they left behind was so complete that Theo could hear her own heartbeat and, faintly, beneath it, something else—a hum, very low, like a song held in the throat and never let out.

"It's beautiful," Jonah said. Then, quieter: "It's *wrong*. It's beautiful the way an empty house is beautiful."

"The book said 1904," Theo murmured. She turned in a slow circle. "A hundred and twenty years. Nothing's fallen down."

"Nothing's grown wild, either." He crouched and touched the path. The grass at its edge was trimmed. Not recently-trimmed—but *tended*, the way a garden is tended by someone who means to come back. "Theo. Look at this. Somebody's been keeping it."

"Somebody," Theo said, "or something."

The goats would have known. That was her first thought, absurd and immediate: if she had Juniper here, or old Bramble with his grumpy opinions about weather, they'd tell her in two heartbeats whether this country was safe. She caught herself reaching for that sense the way you reach for a handrail on dark stairs—and found nothing there to reach. Not gone. Just far. Whatever this place was, its animals—if it had animals—were keeping their own counsel.

That frightened her more than the empty bells did.

They walked. There didn't seem to be any other option; behind them the millrace door stood open in a freestanding stone arch that had no business existing, showing a sliver of gray Merrow's Hollow drizzle on the other side, like a painting of home hung in a window. Ahead, the terraces led down toward a cluster of rooftops—red tile, slate, thatch—all arranged around what was clearly a town square, and in the square stood a bell tower tall enough to be seen from anywhere in the valley below.

"Vessany," Jonah said softly. He had the book out now, walking with it open against his forearm like a waiter balancing a tray. "It's real. It's actually—it's all still *here*."

"You sound happy about that."

"I—" He laughed, but it came out strange. "I've dreamed about this square. I've dreamed about these exact roofs, Theo. Before I ever found the book. I used to think I was making it up. A town where nobody rings the bells." He looked up at the tower, squinting. "I could draw you that tower in my sleep. I *have* drawn it. My grandmother kept my sketchbooks in her attic too, and half of them were this place, and I always thought—"

He stopped walking.

Theo took two more steps before she noticed, and turned back. Jonah was standing rigid in the middle of the path, the book forgotten, staring at the nearest orchard terrace above them.

At first she couldn't see what he was seeing. Rows of apple trees. Stone wall. A wooden post at the corner where the wall met the path—

Where a scarecrow hung.

Except it wasn't dressed in patched flannel like any scarecrow she'd ever seen. This one wore a coat. A good coat, dark wool, buttoned to the throat, and instead of a sack head it had a face—a painted face, or a molded one, pale as porcelain, with careful features and a neat line of dark hair. And pinned to the coat, in letters painted red as fresh blood, was a board:

**TRAITOR**

"That's—" Theo started.

"That's me." Jonah's voice had gone very flat. "That is my *face*, Theo. That is my exact face. That's the mole. That's the—the cowlick, the way my hair does that thing on the left side, that's—" His breath went ragged. "I have never been here. I have never been *born* here. My grandmother was from three thousand miles away and died in a nursing home in Ohio."

The scarecrow's painted eyes stared down the hill at them. At him. Wind moved through the orchard, and the coat shifted on its frame with a soft creak of rope, like something settling its shoulders.

"Okay," Theo said, mostly to herself, because someone had to be calm and apparently it was going to be her. "Okay. Options. One: coincidence. Two: your grandmother's family was from here and you look like them. Three:"

"Three: this country has been waiting a hundred and twenty years to hang me in effigy." Jonah ran both hands through his hair. "For a crime I haven't committed yet. Or *did* commit. Or will commit, or—God, is this how prophecy works? Is this just—am I already the bad guy here?"

"You're a guy who brought me a book on a first date," Theo said. "Which, for the record, is the worst date gift I've ever received, and I once got a scented candle shaped like a trout."

That startled a laugh out of him—short, shaky, real. Some of the rigidity went out of his shoulders. "Noted. Terrible date. Zero stars."

"We're leaving a review either way." But she was watching the scarecrow while she said it, and she saw what he hadn't: farther up the hill, at the next corner of the next wall, another post. Another shape. Too far to make out the face.

She pointed. Jonah followed her finger and went pale.

"There's more than one," he whispered.

They counted as they walked, because counting felt like doing something, and doing something felt better than screaming. Seven scarecrows before they reached the outskirts of the town. All wore coats. All had faces. None of them had his face again—but two of them, Theo thought, might have been related to him: the same jaw, the same deep-set eyes, painted with the same terrible care. Family faces. Posted at field corners like warnings, like boundary markers, like *we remember*.

"They weren't put up yesterday," Jonah said hoarsely. He'd stopped touching the book. "Look at the ropes. Look at the weathering. These have been hanging since—since maybe the beginning. Since 1904. Someone hated us—or me, or mine—for a hundred and twenty years, and they *kept the hate alive*. You don't maintain scarecrows, Theo. You don't repaint traitors' faces for four generations unless—"

"Unless you're expecting them back." Theo finished it quietly.

The town swallowed them then, and the town was worse, or better, or both. Streets of clean cobbles. Shutters closed but not rotted. A fountain still running—actually running, water chuckling over a carved fish—and beside the fountain, a market stall with its awning furled, goods under oilcloth, everything waiting. A child's chalk hopscotch grid on the cobbles, faded but unsmudged, numbers still legible. Laundry lines strung between upper windows, bare, the pulleys oiled.

An entire country paused mid-breath.

"Do you feel watched?" Jonah asked.

"I feel *expected*," Theo said. "There's a difference. Watched is hostile. Expected is—" She searched for it. "Worse, actually. Watched, you can hide from. Expected, you're already late."

They reached the square. The bell tower loomed overhead, its great bronze bell hanging silent in the arch, big enough that Theo could have stood inside it. Ropes hung neatly coiled. Dust lay on the ringing chamber floor in an even, undisturbed blanket—one hundred and twenty years of dust, and not a footprint in it.

And nailed to the tower door, yellowed and curling but unmistakably official, was a paper.

Jonah read it aloud, because neither of them seemed able to do anything else. His voice started steady and lost altitude with every word.

*"By order of the Warden of the Silence: the traitor JONAH REYES, author of the false prophecies, having fled across the door, is to be taken on sight. He is known to return. Let every soul of Vessany be watchful. Signed under seal, the year of the going."*

Below the signature was a symbol Theo didn't know—a circle crossed by a single vertical line, like a bell seen edge-on.

"'Known to return,'" Jonah repeated. His voice had gone thin. "'Author of the false prophecies.' Theo, the book. The book in my bag is a hundred years old and it has my name on the cover and tonight's date inside it and now there's a *wanted poster*—" He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "My grandmother sent me this. I thought she sent me this. Her attic, her book, her handwriting—I'd know it anywhere. So either my sweet dead grandmother set me up, or—"

"Or the story isn't finished," Theo said. "Jonah. Look at me." He looked. His eyes were scared and bright and she realized, with a small internal jolt, that she liked him—really liked him—which made it much worse that he might also be a century-old prophesied traitor. "The book writes itself forward. We saw it. Maybe it hasn't decided what you are yet. Maybe *nobody* has. Maybe that's why the poster says 'known to return' instead of 'returned.' Maybe you get a vote."

"A vote." He laughed wetly. "In a country that's been sharpening the pitchforks since before my grandfather was born."

"Every country sharpens pitchforks," Theo said. "Doesn't mean you have to stand still for them."

Somewhere above them—a sound.

Small. Metallic. A single tap of metal on bronze, high in the tower, like a fingernail flicked against the great bell. Once. Twice. Then silence again, total, absolute, as though the whole town were holding its breath to see if they'd heard.

Theo's skin prickled. Every animal-sense she owned, the sense she'd spent a lifetime strangling, sat up at once like dogs hearing a whistle—except there was no animal up there. There was nothing up there. The dust in the ringing chamber was unbroken.

But something had touched that bell.

And from the far end of the square, from a street that had been empty a moment ago, came the unmistakable sound of footsteps. More than one pair. Unhurried. Deliberate. Coming closer.

Jonah's hand found hers. His palm was cold.

"Theo," he whispered. "Tell me you hear that."

She heard it. And beneath the footsteps, faint and rising, threading through the silent streets like smoke through floorboards, she heard something else—something that made her blood run cold and warm at the same time:

Humming.

Dozens of voices, all around them, hidden behind shuttered windows and closed doors, all humming the same low tune. The tune she'd heard in the wind among the bells. The held note of a song interrupted a hundred and twenty years ago—

—and, it seemed, about to be resumed.

* * *

The footsteps stopped at the mouth of the alley across the square.

Theo counted five figures in the shadows there, and none of them looked like a mob. They looked like neighbors. A woman in an apron with flour still on her hands. An old man leaning on a cane. Two teenagers, a boy and a girl, standing shoulder to shoulder. A round little man in a bell-ringer's coat with brass buttons gone dull. Ordinary people, except for their eyes, which all carried the same expression: the look of people who have rehearsed something for a very long time and hoped never to perform it.

The woman in the apron stepped forward first.

"You're him," she said. Not a question. Her voice was rough, like a hinge unused for decades. "You're the Reyes boy. You have his face. You have *her* chin."

"Her?" Jonah managed.

"Your grandmother's." The woman's gaze flicked to the book under his arm, and something complicated moved through her face—grief and fury sharing a chair. "We know that book. Half this town helped carry it to the door the night the song was sung. And now it walks back in wearing your coat." She drew a breath that shook. "The Warden said you'd come. The Warden said, *wait for the face.*"

"I don't—" Jonah's voice cracked. "I've never been here. I'm from Ohio. I sell insurance software. I asked her out forty minutes before we fell through a hole in reality, and I swear on everything, I don't know what I'm supposed to have done."

The old man with the cane spoke for the first time, and his voice was dry as seed husks. "That's what the last one said, too. Right there. Under that bell. Before they took him up the mountain to the Warden." He tapped the cobblestones with his cane, twice, like punctuation. "The face comes back. The face always says it doesn't know. And the Warden always says: bring it anyway."

Behind the shutters, the humming swelled—one long held tone, dozens of throats, patient as tide.

Theo stepped slightly in front of Jonah. It wasn't a decision; her body simply did it, the way it had always placed itself between threatened things and threats—goats from coyotes, her secret from the Oath of Quiet elders, and now this terrified software salesman from a townful of rehearsed grief.

"He's with me," she said. "Whatever he is, whatever he did or didn't do—he's with me. If you take him, you take both of us."

The woman in the apron studied her. Something flickered there—not softening exactly, but recognition. The look of someone seeing an unexpected piece on a board.

"And who are *you*?" she asked. "The face came alone, a hundred years ago. The face never came with anyone."

"She's a beast-speaker," Jonah said quickly, and then winced, because some secrets die hard even at the end of the world.

The effect was immediate and strange. The five figures went utterly still. The humming behind the shutters faltered—broke off mid-note—and in the sudden silence Theo heard, clearly, unmistakably, from behind a second-floor window to her left:

A dog. Whining. And then, in the mangled, hopeful grammar of dogs everywhere:

*You? You-you-you? Here? Here-here?*

Her heart slammed. After a lifetime of hiding, after the Oath of Quiet and the buried conversations and the goats who were her only confidants—here, in a vanished country, the first voice to greet her was a dog asking if she was really, truly, finally *here*.

"Yes," Theo said aloud, to the window, to the whole listening square, and her voice only shook a little. "Yes. I'm here."

The woman in the apron looked from Theo to the window to Jonah, and the rehearsed expression on her face finally, visibly, broke.

"Come inside," she said. "All of you. Quickly—before the Warden's riders see the square. And pray your beast-ear is as good as they say." She was already moving, herding them toward the alley with the brisk urgency of a woman who had waited a hundred and twenty years for this errand. "Because if the hounds are coming down off the mountain—and they always come when the face returns—then you, girl, are the only thing in Vessany they've ever obeyed."

Far up the terraced hills, faint on the wind, came the first baying.

Theo's hand tightened around Jonah's. The hunt had begun.
