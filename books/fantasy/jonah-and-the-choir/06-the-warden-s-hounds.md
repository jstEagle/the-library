## 6. The Warden's Hounds

The trouble with running through a country that didn't exist, Theo decided somewhere around the fourth orchard terrace, was that there were no roads to follow and no signs to read. Just stone walls older than her grandmother's grandmother, olive trees bent like old men telling the same joke for a century, and a sky so blue it felt like showing off.

"Are they still behind us?" Jonah panted beside her, one hand pressed to his side, the leather book strapped to his back like a backpack he'd never asked to own.

Theo didn't answer right away. She was listening.

Not listening the way people usually meant it—ears cocked toward hoofbeats or snapping branches—but listening the other way. The way she'd been doing in secret since she was six years old, crouched in the goat pen behind the Dunmore barn, asking a billy named Cornelius why he headbutted the fence and learning, to her horror, that Cornelius had opinions. Strong ones. Mostly about fence posts.

She could hear them now. Behind them, down the terraces, moving fast.

*Smell of magic,* something said, in a voice like gravel rolled in honey. *Two trails braided together. The book-thing and the girl-thing.*

"They're coming," Theo said. "Fast."

"How fast?"

"Do you know how fast a greyhound moves when it thinks dinner is insulting its mother?"

Jonah blinked at her. "That's oddly specific."

"It's how dogs think. Everything is either food, family, or an insult." She grabbed his sleeve. "These are not greyhounds."

They came over the ridge as she said it—not greyhounds, nothing so honest as greyhounds. They were hounds the way a cathedral is a shed: technically true, wildly insufficient. Each one stood the height of a pony, with coats like smoke given ambition and eyes the color of banked coals. There were five of them, fanning out across the terrace wall with the easy, terrible coordination of things that had done this ten thousand times.

And every single one of them was talking.

*Girl-thing smells of goats and secrets.* *Book-thing smells of ink and grandmothers.* *The Warden wants them breathing.* *Breathing is boring. Tearing is faster.* *Hush, Rill. The Warden said breathing.*

Theo stopped dead on the path.

"What?" Jonah whispered. "What is it? You've got your listening face. I've seen your listening face. It's very dramatic."

"They're arguing," she said faintly. "About whether to eat us."

"That seems bad."

"No, that's—" She laughed, a small unhinged sound she didn't recognize coming out of her own mouth. "Jonah, I understand them. Completely. They're *talking*, and I can hear every word."

He stared at her, and then at the hounds, and then back. "You speak dog?"

"I speak everything. Goats, mostly. A mule once. Chickens are just noise with a grudge." Her heart was hammering, but not only from fear. Twenty-six years of hiding this thing, twenty-six years of swallowing conversations whole, of pretending not to hear the barn cats complaining about the cold—and here, in a country erased from every map, in front of a man who already knew she was strange, it poured out of her like water finding a crack. "Don't tell anyone. I mean, obviously tell no one, but especially don't tell anyone *here*, because apparently here they hunt people like me."

"The hounds?"

"The hounds smell magic. That's what they keep saying. *Magic-magic-magic*, like bloodhounds but for souls instead of socks."

One of the hounds—the biggest, with a scar across its muzzle that looked earned rather than accidental—lifted its great head and tasted the air. Its coal-bright eyes found Theo.

And it went still.

*It hears us,* the scarred one said slowly. *The girl-thing hears us.*

*Impossible,* said the smallest, though its voice wobbled. *Only the Choir heard us. Only singers.*

*I am not a singer,* Theo thought at it, hard, the way she'd thought at Cornelius about the fence posts. *I'm a goat farmer from Merrow's Hollow and I would very much like you to sit down.*

The scarred hound sat down.

So did the other four, in a cascade of confusion, like dominoes made of smoke. One of them whined—an enormous, subterranean sound, a landslide with feelings.

Jonah made a noise like a kettle being personally offended. "You—you *sat* them. You sat five nightmare dogs with a *thought*."

"I sat goats for twenty years. Dogs are show-offs but they're not complicated." Theo's voice shook. She wrapped her arms around herself. "This isn't funny, Jonah. This is the thing. This is the exact thing my valley was built to bury. The Oath of Quiet, the elders, all of it—it exists because of people like me. And I just used it. Out loud. In front of witnesses."

"In front of me," Jonah said quietly. "Who is currently extremely impressed and also slightly in love with your goat-farmer powers, and who will take both of those facts to his grave if necessary."

Despite everything, warmth bloomed under her ribs. Then the scarred hound spoke again, and the warmth curdled.

*The Warden comes,* it said. Not threateningly. Almost apologetically, the way a dog announces thunder. *Run, girl-thing. We will be slow to rise. We will limp, a little. It is all we can do.*

"Why?" Theo asked aloud, forgetting for a heartbeat that speaking to them was itself a confession. "Why help us?"

The hound's coal eyes held hers. *Because you asked. No one has asked us anything in a hundred years. The Warden only commands.*

Then all five hounds rose, stretched elaborately—as theatrical a display of stiffness as Theo had ever witnessed, and she'd watched Cornelius fake a limp for three days to avoid hoof-trimming—and began to limp, magnificently, in the wrong direction.

* * *

They ran.

Up the terraces, through olive rows silver-green and endless, along walls stitched from stones the size of loaves of bread. Vessany unspooled around them as they climbed: orchards heavy with fruit no one had picked in a century, a village below with shutters open and tables set for meals a hundred years cold, and everywhere, everywhere, bells. Bell towers without ringers, bells green with age, one enormous bell lying on its side in a field like a whale that had given up.

"Why does nobody ring them?" Jonah gasped as they ran.

"I don't know. Maybe that's chapter eight's problem." Theo vaulted a wall, landed badly, kept going. "Right now our problem has hooves and a horse."

Because she could feel it now, behind the limping hounds—a pressure, like weather. Like the moment before a summer storm, when the goats go quiet and stand all facing the same direction. The animals of Vessany were doing exactly that. In the orchards, birds sat motionless on branches. In a fallow field, a herd of pale deer stood with their heads turned toward the valley floor, all of them, silent, watching.

*He comes,* the deer said, in a chorus of soft alarm. *The gray one. The quiet one. Hide, hide, hide.*

"The deer say hide," Theo reported.

"The deer talk too?"

"The deer are very anxious. Deer generally are. It comes with the legs."

They ducked into an old stone barn half-swallowed by ivy, and Theo pressed her eye to a gap between the boards, and that was when she saw him.

The Warden came up the terraces on a pale horse, and Theo's first thought—absurdly, traitorously—was that the horse was beautiful. White-gray as morning fog, moving like water. The figure on its back wore gray too, gray cloak, gray hood, and where a face should have been there was only shadow and the suggestion of stillness. He did not ride so much as arrive, continuously, at every point along the road at once.

Behind him, the five hounds limped and whimpered their magnificent, useless whimpers.

The Warden paused beside the scarred hound. Even at this distance, Theo felt the attention like a hand on the back of her neck.

*Why do you limp, Rill?*

The hound's mind went smooth and blank as a pond. Theo felt it happen—felt the hound slam its own thoughts shut like a door. But the Warden tilted his hooded head, and reached down, and touched the hound's muzzle where the scar crossed it, and whatever passed between them made the great beast shiver.

Then the Warden dismounted.

He knelt at the place where Theo and Jonah had rested by the wall—thirty seconds, no more, they'd been so careful—and drew something from his cloak. From this distance it looked like a length of gray thread. He tied it around a low branch of the nearest olive tree, and the air around it *bent*, the way heat bends above a road in August.

*Marked,* the deer whispered from the field, all of them at once, a shudder of a word. *Marked, marked. Nowhere to run that he cannot follow. The mark knows your names.*

"The mark knows our names," Theo breathed.

"You've got to stop doing that," Jonah whispered back, sliding down the wall beside her, gray-faced. "The helpful horror narration. My heart can't take it."

"He tied something to the tree. Thread, maybe. It—" She struggled for words. "It bends. It's like a hook set in the world, and we're the fish."

"So we pull the hook out."

"We are not touching that thing. Did you see how the air moved around it? My valley's elders would faint. Actually, they'd probably build a chapel around it and charge admission."

But even as she joked, her hands were shaking, and not from the running. Because she'd heard the Warden speak to the hound. Not in words—there had been no words, just pressure, just command, the mental equivalent of a boot on a neck. And she'd heard what the hound answered with, underneath its blankness, buried so deep it might not have known it was there:

*Once I sang too.*

* * *

They waited until the gray figure rode on—down-valley, toward the village with the cold tables, searching door by door—and then they ran again, harder, higher, following a ridge line Jonah swore he'd seen in a dream, which Theo chose not to examine too closely because examining it meant admitting the book on his back was steering them, and she wasn't ready for that conversation.

By late afternoon the light had gone gold and long, and the terraces had given way to wilder country—chestnut woods, a stream that chuckled to itself in a language Theo couldn't quite catch (water talked, but it gossiped so much it was nearly useless), and finally, mercifully, a hollow between two hills where a farmhouse leaned against the slope like a tired traveler against a wall.

Smoke rose from its chimney.

"Somebody lives here," Jonah said. "In the erased country. Somebody actually lives here."

"Or it's a trap."

"Optimist."

"I raise goats. Optimism is a professional requirement." But she stopped at the edge of the yard, because the animals had come out to meet them—or rather, to meet *her*. A goose first, wings half-spread, hissing what was either a threat or a welcome; geese were famously ambiguous. Then two shaggy pigs, a one-eared cat, and a donkey with the weary dignity of a civil servant.

*New-smelling,* the donkey said. *Tired-smelling. Maren will feed them. Maren always feeds strays. Last month she fed a fox and the fox stole a spoon.*

"There's someone called Maren," Theo translated, then caught herself and flushed. "Sorry. I keep—I'll stop narrating."

"Don't," Jonah said. He was looking at her with an expression she couldn't name yet, warm and wondering and a little bit sad. "Honestly, Theo. Don't stop. Do you know what it's like, watching you do this? All my life I thought I was the strange one—the boy with the book that writes back. And you've been carrying something twice as heavy, alone, since you were a kid, and you're still—"

"Still what?"

"Still funny about it."

Theo opened her mouth to reply—which was dangerous, because she'd recently discovered that if she started crying in front of a man she'd met two days ago she might simply never stop—and that was when the farmhouse door banged open.

A young woman stood in it, dark-haired, flour-dusted, holding a rolling pin with the confidence of someone who had used it before. Behind her shoulder, the goose honked what Theo now understood, with perfect clarity, to be: *Strays! Strays! Tell her! Big ones!*

"Maren," the donkey confirmed gloomily. "Now she will feed them and they will bring more strays and the fox will steal another spoon."

Maren's eyes went from Theo, to Jonah, to the book on Jonah's back—and widened. She said something in a language Theo didn't know, quick and urgent, and then, catching their blank looks, switched to accented but fluent English:

"You're him. You're the face on the scarecrow. You're the face in the execution records." She stepped aside, gripping the rolling pin like a talisman. "Get inside. Both of you. Quickly. The Warden marked the eastern ridges at noon, and his hounds—" she faltered, frowning at Theo with sudden, strange intensity, as if noticing something about her for the first time, "—his hounds have been *limping* all afternoon, and I have lived in this country my whole hidden life, and his hounds have never once limped."

Theo looked at Jonah. Jonah looked at Theo.

Inside the farmhouse, something smelled gloriously of bread, and the goose followed them all the way to the door, still honking, *Spoons! Guard the spoons!*
