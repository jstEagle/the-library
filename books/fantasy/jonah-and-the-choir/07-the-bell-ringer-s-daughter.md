## 7. The Bell-Ringer's Daughter

The house at the edge of the terraces had a bell hanging where a doorbell should be, except it was the size of a soup pot and made of green bronze, and when Maren pulled its rope the sound didn't so much ring as *settle* over them like a blanket shaken out.

"That's the welcome bell," she said. "It only rings for people who mean no harm. If you meant harm, you'd have heard nothing at all, and then my dogs would have heard nothing at all, which is how they know."

"You don't have dogs," Theo said.

"Not yet," said Maren, with the confidence of a woman who had never once been wrong about dinner or doom.

She was perhaps fifty, broad-shouldered, with forearms that suggested a lifetime of hauling rope, and hair the color of unpolished brass twisted up under a kerchief. She had found them half-drowned in the millrace spillway below her orchard — Jonah still clutching the book to his chest like an infant, Theo coughing up water that had, briefly, been flowing uphill — and she had not asked a single question until she'd gotten them indoors, dried, and seated at her kitchen table in front of bowls of something that smelled like onions had died happily there.

"Barley and plum," she said, setting down a third bowl for herself. "Eat first. Questions keep."

Theo looked at Jonah. Jonah looked at Theo. The book sat between them on the table, leather cracked, patient as a cat.

They ate. It was, Theo thought dimly, the best thing she had ever tasted, which she suspected had less to do with barley and plums than with the fact that she hadn't eaten since a tavern in Merrow's Hollow, in another country, in another *week*, possibly. Around them the kitchen did what kitchens do when they are loved: ticked, hummed, breathed. Copper pots hung from hooks. A kettle muttered to itself on the stove. On every wall, where another family might have hung portraits, hung bells — dozens of them, small ones, great ones, one enormous clapper mounted alone above the mantel like a trophy or a relic.

"You're staring at my family," Maren said pleasantly.

"Your — family?"

"Bells." She ladled more stew into Jonah's bowl without asking, the way mothers do, the way nobody had done for Theo since she was very small. "The Joubert line were bell-ringers of Vessany for four hundred years. My grandmother rang the noon bell the day the country went quiet. She said it was the hardest thing she ever did — ringing a bell that would be the last thing anyone heard." She wiped her hands on her apron. "Now then. You came through the millrace door, which opens maybe once a decade, and you're not singers, and you're not Wardens, because Wardens don't look that frightened. So who are you?"

Jonah set down his spoon. "My name is Jonah Reyes."

Maren went very still. It was a particular kind of stillness — not surprise, Theo realized, but the stillness of someone confirming something they had already dreaded.

"And I found this book," Jonah went on, "in my grandmother's attic, in—" he gestured vaguely eastward, toward everything— "Oregon. And I know how that sounds. But it has my name in it. Written a hundred years ago. And people here keep looking at me like—"

"Like they've seen your face before," Maren finished quietly.

"Yes," Theo said. "A scarecrow. In the orchard. With his face on it. And a word underneath."

"*Traitor*," Maren said. Not unkindly. Just accurately, the way you'd say *rain*.

The kettle chose that moment to shriek, and everyone jumped, and Maren got up to deal with it, and Theo used the cover of the noise to grab Jonah's wrist under the table. His pulse was going like a rabbit's.

"It's fine," she whispered.

"It is objectively not fine," he whispered back. "There is a scarecrow of me, Theo. A *scarecrow*. Do you know what you have to do to get a scarecrow? You have to be a recurring problem for agriculture."

"I think the word you're looking for is 'traitor.'"

"I'm being blackmailed by produce."

Maren returned with the kettle and poured tea into three cups that did not match each other or anything else in the room, which somehow made it feel more like hospitality, not less. Then she disappeared into the back of the house and came back with a ledger — an actual ledger, bound in gray cloth, its spine sun-bleached — and laid it on the table with both hands, the way you'd lay down a sleeping animal.

"The town records of Bellhollow," she said. "What's left of them. When the Choir sang us out, most things went with the song. Paper remembers longer than stone, if the paper was loved enough." She opened it. "I keep the death registers now. Somebody has to."

The pages were dense, careful handwriting, names and dates in faded brown ink. Maren turned them slowly, letting Theo see each one — generations of ordinary endings, fevers, falls, old age, one memorable entry reading simply "*goose-related*" — until she reached a page near the end and stopped.

"There," she said.

Theo leaned in, and her stomach dropped through the floorboards, through the earth, through whatever thin membrane separated this country from her own.

The entry read:

*Reyes, Jonah. Convicted of prophecy-forgery and theft of the Song. Sentence: the quieting. To be carried out at the bell-stand, Bellhollow, on the —*

And there the date stood, in ink older than either of their grandparents:

*the 14th of Harvestmonth.*

"Harvestmonth is next week," Maren said gently. "It begins Tuesday."

* * *

Jonah didn't say anything for a long time. He sat with his tea untouched, looking at his own name in a dead language in a dead country's ledger, and Theo watched him do the arithmetic she was also doing: the book had been written a century ago; the book had followed him across an ocean and a continent and a door; the book had brought him here, to the one place where his face was already filed under *pending execution.*

"It's not me," he said finally. "It can't be me. I've never been here. I'm a — I teach middle school science, I'm between jobs, I have a *plant*, Theo, a pothos named Gary, Gary needs me—"

"Jonah."

"He signed it." He tapped the bottom of the entry, where a signature curled beneath the Warden's seal. "Look at the handwriting. That's my grandmother's handwriting. I'd know it anywhere. She wrote my birthday cards in that hand. She wrote *love, Nana Reyes* in that hand, and apparently she also wrote *execute my grandson* in it, and I would really like someone to explain to me what kind of woman does both."

"A tired one," Maren said. "Or a clever one. Or both. Sit."

He sat. He had been standing without noticing he'd stood.

Maren closed the ledger and put her hand flat on top of it, as if holding something down. "Let me tell you what I know, and you can tell me if any of it fits. A hundred years ago, Vessany was going to be conquered. There was an army coming over the passes — you can read about the war in your world's history books if you squint, though your historians lost our name somewhere around 1904. The Choir had one weapon left. Not a sword. A song. They sang the whole country *out* — out of maps, out of treaties, out of memory. Every border, every record, every mind outside these valleys forgot we existed. Even the army forgot what it had come to take." She smiled, and it was a tired smile with bells in it. "It worked. We've been hiding inside our own erasure for a hundred years. The door opens rarely. Mostly by accident. Mostly for people the song wants."

"And me?" Jonah said. "Why does the song want *me*?"

"Because your grandmother was one of the singers," Maren said. "One of the nine. And because—" she hesitated, and Theo saw her choose her words the way you choose footing on scree— "because the song was never finished. It was paused. Mid-note. A song that pauses must someday resolve, and the book your grandmother wrote is the resolution trying to draft itself. Prophecy isn't fate, boy. It's a letter from someone who can see the weather, telling you to bring an umbrella. Your grandmother saw a storm. She wrote down what she saw. What she *saw* was you, standing where you're standing, in the week you're standing in it."

"But the execution—"

"Is what she saw," Maren agreed. "Not necessarily what happens. Records in this country have a way of being premature." She stood, briskly, before the silence could set like concrete. "You'll stay the night. Both of you. The Warden's hounds don't come within a mile of a ringer's house — old law, older courtesy. And you," she said to Theo, pointing with the teapot, "look like you haven't slept in a week, and you keep glancing at my window like you're expecting something to climb through it."

"The hounds," Theo admitted. "We were chased. By hounds that smell magic. And the Warden. He was on a pale horse and he — marked our trail. I don't know how close he is."

Maren considered this. "Hounds that smell magic," she repeated. "And yet here you are, smelling strongly of millrace and miracle, sitting in my kitchen." Her eyes narrowed — not with suspicion, Theo realized, but with the sharp bright interest of a woman meeting a rumor in the flesh. "How odd. Almost as if they *let* you go."

"They did," Theo said quietly. "Sort of. I can talk to animals. I've been able to since I was a child. Where I come from, that's — you hide it. You spend your whole life hiding it. But the hounds, when I spoke to them, they listened. They turned around." She wrapped her hands around her cooling cup. "I don't understand why."

Maren looked at her for a long moment, and whatever she was thinking, she folded it away carefully, like linen.

"Well," she said. "In this house, girl, talking to animals is the least strange thing anyone's done all week. I once had a conversation with a rain barrel. It won."

* * *

They slept in the loft above the kitchen, on straw mattresses that smelled of lavender and apples, under a roof where the beams were carved with tiny bells, hundreds of them, worn smooth by a century of absent fingers. Theo lay awake listening to the house settle, and to something else — a faint, far-off sound from the direction of town, almost too low to hear. Like a bell ringing underwater. Like a bell ringing in a dream.

Beside her, Jonah was asleep sitting up, the book open on his chest, rising and falling with his breath.

She should wake him. She should take the book away from him, actually, because she had noticed — she was almost sure she wasn't imagining it — that the pages had been whispering. Not words. More the way a kettle whispers before it boils. Ink thinking about becoming letters.

Instead she lay there and let herself have five minutes of something she hadn't felt in twenty-six years of hiding in plain sight: the feeling of being *expected*. Maren had set out three bowls without asking how many they were. Maren had known Jonah's name before he said it and fed him anyway. Somewhere in this impossible erased country, there was a ledger with her own future in it too, probably — everyone here seemed to keep records of things that hadn't happened yet — and for the first time in her life, Theo found she didn't dread it.

Downstairs, a floorboard creaked. Maren, moving through her midnight kitchen, doing whatever bell-ringers' daughters do at midnight. Probably talking to the rain barrel.

Theo slept.

* * *

Morning arrived smelling of cinnamon and woodsmoke, and with it arrived the execution records.

Not the ledger — Maren had that locked away — but the rest of it. Because it turned out that Bellhollow kept its condemned in other ways too: painted boards nailed to the bell-tower door, wax impressions pressed into the church steps, and — this was the one that finally broke Jonah — a set of handbills, yellowed and soft as cloth, stacked in Maren's pantry under the flour.

"Why," Jonah said, holding one up with two fingers, "do you have wanted posters of me *in your pantry*."

"Paper keeps better somewhere cool and dry," Maren said, kneading dough. "Also the flour likes company. Don't ask."

The handbill showed his face — unmistakably his face, down to the crooked left eyebrow — above text in the same flowing script as the ledger. Theo had been picking up the language with alarming speed, the way you pick up a tune; she read it aloud, slowly, translating:

"'By order of the Warden of the Quiet Country: the traitor Jonah Reyes, author of the false prophecies, thief of the unfinished Song, son of the singer who broke her oath. Wanted for the crime of existing ahead of schedule.'" She lowered the paper. "That last part might be my translation. The idiom's tricky."

"Son of the singer who broke her oath," Jonah repeated. He sat down heavily on a kitchen stool that seemed to have been waiting for him. "Nana Reyes. Who taught me to whistle. Who made jam. Who told me—" his voice caught— "she told me stories about a country that didn't exist, and I thought they were *stories*, Theo. I thought she was just being a grandma."

"She was being a grandma," Maren said firmly, shaping loaves with quick, sure hands. "And being a singer. And being, I'd wager, a woman carrying the worst secret in the world and trying to hand it to somebody gently. People do terrible-looking things out of love, boy. Wait until you've read the whole letter before you burn it."

Outside, the morning light came down the terraces in long gold bars, catching the empty bell-tower in the middle of town. From here it looked like a throat waiting to speak.

Theo was drying dishes — she had insisted; Maren had pretended to resist for exactly four seconds — when she heard it. Not with her ears first. With the older way. A scratching at the back door, and then a voice, low and rough and familiar:

*The gray man is on the road,* said the fox — the same fox, Theo was nearly certain, that had warned her about the paper man a lifetime ago in another valley. *The gray man is on the road, and he is not riding fast, because he does not need to. He knows where the bells sleep.*

Her hands went still in the dishwater.

"Maren," she said. "How long does it take to bake bread?"

"An hour, why?"

"How long does it take to run?"
