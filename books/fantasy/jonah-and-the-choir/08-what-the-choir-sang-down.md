## 8. What the Choir Sang Down

Maren's house sat at the crook of the river where it bent around the terraces like a hand cupped to hold something precious. It was small, stone-walled, and its roof was thatched with a flowering grass Theo didn't recognize—grass that chimed faintly in the evening wind, as though the whole roof were a struck instrument someone had forgotten to stop playing.

"You'll want to sit," Maren said, shouldering open the door. "And you'll want bread. And you'll want both before I tell you what I have to tell you, because nobody hears this on an empty stomach. That's not superstition. That's just kindness."

The inside of the house smelled of woodsmoke and rosemary and something sweeter underneath, like honey left too long in the sun. There were bells everywhere. Not large ones—Maren had said her family kept the great bells of Vessany, but there wasn't a single one here—only small ones: door-bells and table-bells and a whole shelf of tiny silver things no bigger than thimbles, each with a slip of paper curled inside it like a message in a bottle.

"What are those?" Theo asked.

"Letters." Maren set down a tray of bread, hard cheese, and figs so ripe they had split their own skins. "When the country went, people wrote to each other. Wrote to the future. Wrote to whoever came after. A bell holds a sound better than a box holds paper—the paper rots, but a bell remembers its ringing. So they folded their words small and put them where the sound lives." She touched one of the little bells without ringing it, the way you'd touch a sleeping child's head. "Most of them have never been opened. Nobody's been born here in a hundred years who didn't already know what they say."

Jonah stood very still by the hearth. He hadn't taken off his coat. The leather book was still tucked under his arm, and he held it the way Theo had seen him hold nothing else—not casually, not proudly, but like a man holding a verdict he hadn't read yet.

"Maren," he said. "The records you showed us. The execution order. It had my face on it."

"It did."

"And the date."

"Next week," Maren said, matter-of-fact, and cut more bread. "Eat first."

Theo ate. It seemed important to obey. The bread was dense and dark and tasted faintly of the same chiming grass that grew on the roof, and she found that eating it steadied her in a way that made no sense whatsoever, and she decided not to question it. She'd spent her whole life deciding not to question things. She was getting very good at it.

* * *

Afterward, Maren banked the fire and sat across from them with her hands wrapped around a clay mug she didn't drink from, and told them.

"You know the shape of the story already," she began. "Every country has one like it buried somewhere. Ours just happens to be true. A hundred and twenty years ago, Vessany was the orchard of the world—or we thought we were. Terraces from the river to the snowline, every family keeping bees and bells, our fruit on tables in nine capitals. We were rich in the way that makes neighbors hungry." She turned the mug slowly in her hands. "There was an empire coming up out of the south. You wouldn't know its name. It erased itself too, eventually—erasure is contagious—but back then it was very thorough about other people's names. It sent an envoy first. The envoy looked at our terraces and our bells and said, very politely, that we could keep our orchards if we gave up our songs. Every song. Lullabies, work chants, the naming-songs for children. All of it surrendered, all of it burned, and in exchange we could go on being farmers in a country that no longer knew how to say its own name."

Theo felt Jonah go rigid beside her. She reached over without thinking and put her hand on his knee, and he covered it with his own, and neither of them looked at each other, because looking would have made it real in a way that stopped the story.

"Some towns took the offer," Maren went on. "You can't hate them. They had children. But most of Vessany gathered in the amphitheater at Corvel—that's the old capital, upriver—and the Choir stood on the stage where the best singers had always stood, and they sang the country out of existence."

"Out of—" Theo started.

"Not destroyed. Erased. There's a difference, and the difference is everything." Maren's voice was steady, but her thumb had gone white against the mug. "A map is a kind of agreement. So is a law, a ledger, a memory. The Choir sang the agreement away. Not the land—the land stayed, folded over like a letter. Not the people—they stayed too, folded in with it. But every treaty, every record, every mind outside the fold forgot Vessany had ever existed. The empire arrived at a valley full of terraced hills and empty villages and could not make itself care. Its maps showed wilderness. Its generals felt nothing worth conquering. And the world went on, complete and contented, minus one country."

The fire popped. Somewhere on the shelf, one of the tiny bells shivered in its sleep, though there was no wind indoors.

"The singers paid for it," Maren said quietly. "That's the part the story always gets wrong when it gets told at all. The song needed voices freely given—all of them, all the way down, holding the note until the fold closed. Nine hundred and six singers. When the country finished folding, the song didn't end. It paused. Mid-note. Because a thing sung out of existence can be sung back, and the Choir wanted the last word to be theirs." She looked up. "They're still holding it. Or some of them are. The ones who didn't die of old age inside the pause—which is its own strange mercy, or curse; time moves strangely where a note is being held."

Jonah's voice came out cracked. "My grandmother."

"Alba Reyes." Maren nodded slowly. "Second voice from the left, if the old portraits tell true. She was young then. Younger than you are now."

"That's impossible," Jonah said, but he said it the way people say impossible things when they've already stopped believing in impossibility—wearily, like a man arguing with weather. "She died. I was twelve. I carried the casserole dish to the wake myself. My mother cried into it."

"People die inside the fold all the time," Maren said gently. "But Alba Reyes was a singer, and singers inside a held note don't spend time the way we do. She stepped through the door once, years ago—to buy thread, of all things, thread for mending the choir robes—and she lived a whole life out there, married, raised a son, grew old, and then walked back through and returned to the amphitheater younger than she'd left it, because the note was still holding her place." She smiled, and it was a sad smile, the kind that has done a lot of work. "Your grandmother has died twice, Jonah Reyes. Once out there, in your world, at the end of a long and ordinary life. And once in here, in advance, in ink, on a warrant that says her grandson will hang for treason next week."

"Why?" Theo asked. It came out sharper than she meant. "Why would she write that? Why would anyone write that?"

"I don't know," Maren said. "But I know this much: the book doesn't predict. It remembers forward. Everything written in it has already happened somewhere, sometime, in some version of the fold—and the fold has more versions than we have days. Which means whatever's written can be unwritten. It's happened before." She set down the mug at last. "It's why I'm telling you all this instead of running you out of town with a broom, which, frankly, my grandmother would have done. Because a dead man walked into my kitchen carrying his own death warrant, and dead men don't come back unless something in the story has changed."

* * *

Later, when Maren had gone out to check the goat-shed—she kept three goats, and Theo had spent twenty minutes pretending not to understand every word they said, which was the hardest acting of her life—Jonah sat by the fire with the book open on his knees and didn't read it.

"My grandmother sang my country away," he said finally. "Or helped. Nine hundred and six people, and one of them was the woman who taught me to whistle."

"She also bought thread," Theo said.

He huffed something that was almost a laugh. "She did. She was always buying thread. I used to think she was just... frugal." He turned a page with one finger, not looking at it. "Theo, what if the warrant's right? What if I'm supposed to die here? What if the book isn't a draft—it's a sentence?"

"Then we appeal," Theo said. "I've read enough fantasy novels to know the rules. Prophecies are contracts, and contracts have loopholes, and loopholes favor people who read the fine print." She hesitated, then said the truer thing underneath: "Also, I've spent my whole life hiding half of who I am so the world stays comfortable. I'm not doing it again. Not even to fate."

Jonah looked at her then—really looked, the way he had across the tavern table when the dog growled at the book and he'd laughed and she'd pretended not to notice that the laugh didn't reach his eyes. Now his eyes were steady, and warm, and terrified, and she liked him better for all three.

"The animals on the other side of the door," he said. "They begged you not to come. Do you think they knew? About any of this?"

"They usually know first," Theo said. "They just don't get believed."

Outside, the chiming grass had gone quiet, and in the silence Theo heard something else—far off, downriver, faint as a memory of a memory: dogs. Many dogs. And beneath them, almost too low to catch, the slow rhythm of hooves on pale stone.

Maren came back in fast, without her lantern, her face lit only by the fire.

"They've crossed the ford," she said. "The Warden's hounds don't smell blood, girl. They smell magic. And this house—" she glanced at the shelf of little bells, at the singing roof, at the two strangers by her hearth, "—this house reeks of it."
