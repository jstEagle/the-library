## 9. Prophecy Is a Draft

The book had followed Jonah through three dreams before he understood that dreams could leave residue.

Not the ordinary kind—the vague, damp feeling of having been somewhere you couldn't name—but actual residue. The first morning, he'd woken with a splinter of pine in his thumb that hadn't been there when he fell asleep. The second morning, his boots were muddy to the ankle, though he'd left them by the door, and the mud smelled of bells. The third morning, he woke with the taste of ink in his mouth, sharp and metallic, like he'd been chewing a pen in his sleep, and when he spat into the sink it came out black.

Theo had found him staring at the sink like it had personally betrayed him.

"You look," she'd said, "like a man who's losing an argument with his own saliva."

"It tastes like the book," he said.

"Everything tastes like the book lately." She'd said it lightly, but her hand had gone to her pocket where she kept the leather volume, the way other people touched talismans or worry stones. "Come on. Maren's making breakfast, and if we're late she'll take it personally."

That had been two days ago, in Maren's crooked little house at the edge of the terraces, where the bread was dense and good and the walls hung with small brass bells that never rang no matter how the wind tried. Now they were back at the mill—because Jonah couldn't stop thinking about it, because the backward water itched in his brain like a song heard once and half-remembered—and Theo had gone down to the millrace to talk to whatever animals happened to be listening, which was all of them, always, everywhere, a fact that still made Jonah feel like he was dating someone slightly famous.

Which left him alone with the book.

He sat on the fallen beam near the door-that-wasn't-a-door, the one they'd come through, the one that stood in the middle of the air like a held breath. Sunlight came sideways through the broken slats of the roof and lay across the open pages in warm bars. He hadn't meant to open it. He'd told himself he was just going to hold it, the way you check on a sleeping animal.

But the book was already open. It had been open when he picked it up. And the page facing him was written in his grandmother's hand—he knew her handwriting the way you know a face: the way her *r*'s leaned like tired people against a wall, the way her *t*'s were crossed with a kind of impatient flourish, as if even her letters were always halfway out the door.

*And on the third day,* the page read, *the boy who carries me will stand at the water that runs wrong, and he will read aloud what is written here, and the valley will hear its own name spoken by a stranger's mouth.*

Jonah's mouth went dry.

"That's not—" he started, and then stopped, because arguing with a century-old prophecy felt like arguing with weather. But the words sat there, patient as a cat, and the sunlight moved, and the millrace murmured its backwards murmur below, and eventually he cleared his throat and did the only reasonable thing, which was the thing the book said he would do, because the alternative was sitting there knowing he was defying a dead woman's handwriting, which somehow felt worse.

"The boy who carries me," he read aloud. His voice sounded strange in the empty mill, too loud and too young. "Will stand at the water that runs wrong."

Below him, the millrace gurgled upstream.

"And he will read aloud what is written here."

His voice caught. Not from fear—from something stranger. As he spoke the words, the ink seemed to settle deeper into the paper, the way footprints settle into snow. Like the words had been waiting to be *said*, and saying them completed some circuit.

"And the valley will hear its own name spoken by a stranger's mouth."

Silence. Then, distantly, from somewhere across the terraced orchards, a single bell tolled—one note, low and wondering, though Maren had told him every bell in Vessany had been silent for a hundred years.

His hands shook as he turned the page.

The next page stopped him cold.

It was the same entry. Nearly. The same words, in the same leaning, impatient hand—but crossed out. All of it. Every line struck through with a single decisive stroke, and beneath the wreckage, in fresher ink, a new version:

*On the third day the boy will hesitate at the water that runs wrong, because he is his grandmother's grandson, and hesitation is the family trade.*

Jonah laughed. It came out of him startled, a bark of sound that echoed off the broken rafters. Because that was true. That was so precisely, specifically true that for a moment he looked around the mill expecting to find his grandmother hiding behind a beam, delighted with herself.

He turned another page. Another draft. Lines struck out, revised, annotated in margins that grew more crowded as the pages went on—little notes in different inks, some brown with age, some still dark:

*"Too dramatic. Cut the bell."*

*"He won't say it like that. He'll make a joke first. He always makes a joke first."*

*"Try again after supper."*

"After supper," Jonah whispered. His grandmother's marginalia read like someone working on a crossword puzzle, not scripture. Not fate. *Drafts.* These weren't prophecies handed down from on high—they were drafts. Attempts. A woman with a pen and a hundred years and apparently endless patience, trying again and again to get the wording right.

He thought of how he'd found the book—claimed he'd found it—in his late grandmother's attic, in a box marked *MISC. DO NOT OPEN (SERIOUSLY)*. He'd assumed the prophecies were old things, fixed things, history that had already decided what he would do. He'd walked around for weeks feeling like a character in someone else's story, reading ahead in his own script, flinching at lines that described him too well.

But if these were drafts—

He flipped faster now, pages whispering, and there it was, halfway through: an entry dated—for a moment his eyes refused to focus—dated *tonight*. Yesterday, actually, when he checked the strange slow calendar Maren used. And the entry described this exact hour, this exact mill, a boy reading aloud at the water that runs wrong while a girl talked to eels downstream.

Except the entry didn't end the way he remembered it ending when he'd skimmed it days ago, terrified, in Theo's kitchen. He remembered it ending badly. He remembered something about *the water rising* and *the door closing forever*. He'd carried those words around like stones in his shoes.

Now the page read differently. The last line had changed.

*...and the boy will understand at last that nothing here is written until it is lived, and the living can argue back.*

"I can argue back," Jonah said slowly, testing the idea the way you test ice.

The ink on the page shimmered—not dramatically, not like magic in a story, just the faintest tremor, like heat off summer asphalt—as if the book itself were considering the point.

"Oh, you have got to be kidding me." He was grinning now, wildly, stupidly, alone in a haunted mill in a country that didn't exist, grinning at a book. "You're not prophecy. You're a *first novel*."

The bell tolled again in the distance, and this time it was joined by another, then a third, and Jonah realized the bells weren't tolling randomly. They were tolling *toward* something—or away from it. And the direction they pointed, if bells could be said to point, was back the way they'd come. Back toward the millrace. Back toward the door.

Toward home.

He scrambled up the fallen beam to where Theo crouched at the water's edge, her sleeves rolled, her voice low and running, mid-conversation with something in the shallows.

"—no, I'm telling you, the whole *concept* of a mill is rude, you're basically a river doing unpaid labor—"

"Theo."

She glanced up. Whatever the eel had said made her snort, and she patted the water once, twice—a goodbye gesture, he was learning—and climbed up beside him, shaking water off her fingers. "The eels think your book is pretentious. Their word. I didn't know eels knew the word 'pretentious,' but apparently Vessany's eels are critics."

"Theo, listen." He held up the book, open to the crossed-out page. "These are drafts. She wrote them like drafts. Look—" he showed her the strikethroughs, the marginalia, the notes that said *try again after supper*. "She wasn't recording the future. She was *writing* it. Trying versions. Which means none of it's fixed. Which means—" his voice cracked on the word and he pushed through it, "which means the bad endings aren't set. We can argue with them."

Theo took the book carefully, the way she handled everything—goats, injured birds, him—with a competence that pretended to be casual. Her eyes moved over the crossed-out lines, and he watched something shift in her face, something tight that had been tight since the scarecrow with his face and the word TRAITOR.

"So when it said the valley opens," she said quietly, "that was just... a version."

"A rough draft."

"And the version where we die in a mill fire?"

"Haven't gotten there yet." He paused. "Wait. Is there a version where we die in a mill fire?"

Theo turned pages, faster, her lips moving. Then she stopped. Her face went very still in a way he was starting to recognize as her version of shouting.

"Jonah." She turned the book toward him. "There's a version where the Warden burns the mill."

* * *

They smelled the smoke before they saw it.

It came rolling along the millrace, thick and gray-green, smelling wrong—smelling of wet ash and cold iron, of something burned that had never quite been alive. Theo grabbed his arm and pointed, and Jonah turned to see the far bank of the race shimmering with heat-haze that had no sun to cause it.

"He's burning the *other side*," Theo breathed. "The home side."

"No." Jonah's stomach dropped through the floor of the world. "No, he's burning the *door*."

Because that was what the Warden did. That was the job, Maren had explained it once, in her matter-of-fact way, while slicing bread: the Warden didn't rule Vessany; he *guarded the erasure*. Kept the country quiet, kept the door shut, kept anything that might remember itself from remembering. And a door that opened rarely—

A door that opened rarely would burn beautifully.

They ran. Down the length of the mill, through the gap where the wall had given up sometime in the last century, along the race where the water ran the wrong way and threw spray upward instead of down. The heat came at them in waves now, and through the smoke Jonah could see the doorway—that impossible rectangle of standing air, the held breath, the way home—still there, still open, but the world around it was not. The grass on the far bank was curling. The old millstones were cracking in the heat with sounds like distant gunfire.

And standing in the smoke, unhurried, gray as the ash he made, was the Warden.

He wasn't looking at them. He was looking at the door, head tilted, like a man assessing a job nearly done. On his pale horse beside him, the hounds milled restlessly, and even from here Jonah could see they were pulling toward Theo rather than away from her, straining at nothing, moored to her like boats to a dock.

"Theo." His voice came out thin. "Theo, the book said—"

"I know what the book said." She was already running, already pulling ahead of him, and she shouted back over her shoulder with a wildness he'd never heard from her before, a wildness that sounded almost like joy: "But the book's just a *draft*, Reyes!"

Behind them, the mill groaned, settled, and began to burn in earnest.

Ahead of them, the door stood open—and beyond it, faintly, impossibly, through the smoke and the heat-haze, Jonah could see the valley. Home. Theo's goats, the tavern, the Oath of Quiet. And between them and it, the gray figure turned at last, unhurried, and looked directly at Theo with an expression Jonah couldn't read—pity, maybe, or recognition—and raised one hand.

The hounds broke toward them like a wave deciding.
