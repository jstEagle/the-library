## 10. The Market of Small Magics

The door to the market was a wardrobe.

Not metaphorically, not poetically—an actual wardrobe, standing alone in the middle of an orchard terrace as if it had walked there and gotten tired. Its doors hung open on darkness that smelled, improbably, of cedar and warm bread.

"I want it noted," Theo said, "that I have stopped asking questions."

"Noted," Maren said, "and disbelieved." She was walking ahead with a lantern she hadn't lit yet, which Theo had learned was a Vessany habit—carrying light you weren't ready to show, in case showing it cost something. "You've asked eleven since we left my house. I counted."

"That's restraint," Jonah said. He had the book strapped across his chest like a breastplate, which he'd started doing after the mill burned. "She used to ask me questions on our first date. Dozens. I found it charming."

"You found it *flattering*," Theo said. "There's a difference."

The wardrobe's darkness breathed out at them, cool and green, like the inside of a hill. Maren stepped through without ceremony and vanished to the knees, then entirely.

Theo hesitated. Behind her, down the terraces, the evening was doing its ordinary work—the light going amber, the empty bells of the town below holding their silence the way they always did, patient as held breath. Somewhere out there, a gray figure on a pale horse was doing whatever gray figures did between hunts. She thought about the hounds, the way their voices had folded toward her like paper toward flame. *Follow her*, they'd said, when she'd only meant *stop*. Words were heavier here. Everything was heavier here.

"Goat," she said quietly, to no one, because the habit of a lifetime doesn't break just because you've fallen through a door. "We're going in."

No goat answered. But something in the dark of the wardrobe shifted, attentive, and she chose to take that as encouragement.

She went through.

* * *

The market existed in the space where an orchard should have been, except the orchard had been politely asked to step aside and had agreed.

Lanterns hung from branches that grew sideways out of nothing, burning colors Theo didn't have names for—a green like the moment before rain, a gold like the inside of a honey jar. Stalls lined crooked lanes: some were tables, some were boats beached on cobbles that appeared only underfoot, one was simply a blanket with a very old woman sitting cross-legged at its center, radiating the specific authority of someone who has never once moved for anybody.

And the people—or the near-people. A man whose shadow arrived a half-second before he did. Twin sisters who shared one reflection between them and took turns using it. A child selling bottled weather off a tray: *fog, morning kind; fog, regretful; one thunderstorm, barely used.*

Nobody stared at Theo. That was the thing that undid her, more than the lanterns or the bottled lightning or the boat-stall selling what looked suspiciously like secondhand dreams by the pound. Nobody stared. A woman with moss growing in her hair brushed past her carrying a basket of eggs that hummed lullabies to each other, and did not look twice, and Theo felt her chest go tight with a longing so sharp it was almost grief.

Here, her whole life would have been ordinary. Here, she could have been *boring*.

"Oh no," Jonah said softly beside her. "I know that face."

"What face?"

"The face of someone realizing they've been living in the wrong country their entire life." He bumped her shoulder with his. "Careful. That way lies staying forever, and I have a date to get home from. Eventually. Technically it's still the same date."

"It has been four days."

"A long first date. Memorable, though."

Maren was already three stalls deep, greeting people in low Vessany, and the greetings came back warm but quick, eyes flicking past them toward the lane mouths. Word traveled in a market like this. Word about a stranger with a traitor's face traveled faster.

"We need passage," Maren said, catching up. "The Choir's sanctuary is in the old amphitheater, up past the high terraces, but the ways up are watched now. The Warden's people can't find the market—it's not *findable*—but they watch who leaves it. So we need someone to walk us out a back way. And back ways here aren't free. Nothing here is free. You don't pay with coin."

"With what?" Theo asked, though she was beginning to dread the answer.

Maren looked at her with real sympathy. "With truth."

* * *

They tried Jonah first.

He stood before a stall run by a broad woman with a beard braided into three plaits and rings on every finger, each ring set with a stone that was visibly listening. The stall sold passage—maps that rearranged themselves, keys that fit doors that hadn't been built yet, and, in a locked case at the back, what appeared to be a single boot, priced at *one honest apology, loudly given*.

Jonah offered his secret with the confidence of a man who had spent a century writing other people's fates: "My grandmother is alive, and I'm angry with her."

The bearded woman's stones all turned toward him at once, interested. Then one by one they turned away, bored, until the last stone—the smallest, a dull gray pebble—gave a little rattle that Theo, with her gift, translated effortlessly as *honey, that's not a secret, that's a Tuesday*.

"That's history, love," the woman said kindly. "History isn't currency. It's already spent. Next."

Jonah tried again—"I talk to the book in my sleep"—and got the same result, the pebble rattling its contempt. The market wanted secrets still warm. Secrets with weight on them. Secrets a person was actively carrying.

Which meant, Theo realized with a slow drop of her stomach, there was only one merchant in their party with inventory.

"Don't," Jonah said, seeing her face.

"It's fine."

"Theo."

"It's just a name," she said, and heard how thin it sounded even as she said it.

Maren put a hand on her arm. "Names aren't small things here. But—" she hesitated, choosing honesty over comfort, which seemed to be a regional specialty—"a secret freely traded buys more than one pried loose. If you're going to spend it, spend it well."

* * *

The vendor she chose was the old woman on the blanket, because the old woman was the only seller in the market whose goods Theo couldn't identify, and that felt right somehow. You didn't sell your dearest coin to a stall that sold bottled fog. You sold it to mystery.

The old woman's wares were arranged on scraps of cloth: a keyhole with no key and no door. A feather that fell upward. A small glass ball containing, unmistakably, a Tuesday afternoon from Theo's childhood—she could see the kitchen window, the dust motes, her mother's hands kneading bread. A jar labeled, in handwriting like a spider's farewell, *the sound of a decision not yet made*.

"Passage," Theo said. "Up to the high terraces. A way the Warden's watchers won't see."

The old woman looked at her. Her eyes were the color of tea gone cold in the cup, and they did not blink often enough.

"Payment," she said.

Theo knelt on the blanket's edge. Around them the market went on—laughter somewhere, the egg-basket humming, the child hawking weather—but it seemed to quiet around this corner of it, the way rooms do when something true is about to happen.

"I have a goat," Theo said. "Had. Back home. Her name is Brindle."

The old woman waited. The waiting had texture to it, like a hand held out.

"She's not just a goat. None of them are, but her especially. I named her when I was nine and I've never told anyone the full name, because the full name isn't for people." Theo's throat tightened. This was the ridiculous part, the part that would sound like nothing to anyone else: "Her full name is Brindlemarch Eveningstar, and I call her that every night before bed, and it's the only place I say the truest version of myself out loud, because she's the only one who hears it and she loves me anyway. It's the only sentence in my life that has never once lied or been lied about."

Silence. The market noise rushed back in, indifferent.

Then the old woman reached beneath her shawl and brought out a small clay whistle, plain as a button, and set it on the cloth between them.

"For the passage," she said. "Give this to the ferryman at the cistern gate. Blow it once; he'll know the buyer." She paused. "And for the change you gave me over the price—"

"There's no change, I don't even know the price—"

"—take this too." From the same fold of shawl: a single dried apple slice, brown and wrinkled, utterly ordinary. "Eat it when you're afraid and remember that fear is just love with nowhere to go yet."

"That's not worth anything," Theo said automatically.

"No," the old woman agreed placidly. "It's free. Those are different."

Theo opened her mouth to ask—and then understood, with a lurch, that the question was already being paid for, that curiosity here had a till and a clerk. She closed her mouth. The old woman smiled for the first time, and it transformed her face into someone's grandmother, someone's lost aunt, somebody's favorite.

"And a gift," the old woman said, leaning close, her voice dropping to almost nothing, "because you paid in the only coin I truly love. Lean in, girl. Free advice is worthless, but gossip—that's priceless."

Theo leaned in.

"The Warden," the old woman whispered, "was a singer once."

The market seemed to tilt. "That's not possible. Maren said—"

"Maren said what everyone says. That he silenced himself, that he cut his own voice out to hunt the ones who sang the country away." The old woman sat back, folding her hands. "But I was at the singing, child. I stood in that amphitheater with the rest of them, a hundred years and a paused breath ago. And I will tell you what nobody says, because nobody stayed to see." Her cold-tea eyes fixed on Theo. "He wasn't the one who silenced himself. The Choir took his voice *from* him. He voted against the song—he wanted to fight the conquest, to bleed for Vessany rather than vanish—and when the vote went against him, they took his voice so he couldn't sing against them. Made him a warning. And a warning, kept long enough, becomes a warden."

Theo's skin prickled. "Why tell me?"

"Because you have a voice the land has never heard," the old woman said simply. "And because someone should know, before you meet him, that you are not looking at a monster. You are looking at what happens when a country takes a man's song and hands him a leash instead." She began gathering her impossible wares, folding cloth over the upward-falling feather, the unmade decision. "Monsters you can kill. A stolen voice—you have to give it back. Think on which task your heart would rather attempt."

* * *

They found the cistern gate at the market's far edge, where the lanterns thinned and the dark between them grew honest again. The ferryman was a long, sad-looking man poling a flat barge along a channel of water that flowed uphill, patiently, like it had somewhere better to be.

Theo blew the whistle. It made no sound she could hear—but the water flinched, and the ferryman tipped his cap.

"Three for the high terraces," he said. "Off the record, if you take my meaning. Mind the third bend. There's a watcher at the third bend who owes me money, so he'll be looking elsewhere."

They boarded. The barge slid upward through black water, terraces rising on either side like the ribs of a sleeping animal, and for a while nobody spoke. Jonah held the book against his chest. Maren watched the banks with her unlit lantern in her hand.

Theo ate the apple slice.

It tasted like nothing much—dry, faintly sweet, faintly of cinnamon. But halfway through it, she realized her hands had stopped shaking, and that she was thinking about the Warden not as a shape of gray doom on a pale horse but as a man standing in an amphitheater a hundred years ago, voting no, being unwound for it. Fear with somewhere to go. Love, maybe, pointed the wrong direction for a century.

"Your goat," Jonah said quietly, beside her. "Brindlemarch Eveningstar."

"Don't."

"I'm not making fun." He was looking at the water. "I think it might be the best name I've ever heard. I think—I think that's the thing, isn't it? This whole country ran on names. The Choir sang one down. The Warden lost his. And you've been keeping one safe your whole life in a valley that punishes exactly that." He glanced at her. "When we get home—if we get home—you should say it out loud more. To people, even. Starting with me."

Theo looked at him for a long moment. On a first date, four days and a lifetime ago, she'd have filed that away as charm. Now she recognized it as something sturdier.

"Thank you," she said.

The barge rounded the second bend. Ahead, the third bend curved out of sight behind a wall of terraced apple trees gone wild, heavy with fruit nobody harvested, and the ferryman's pole slowed, just slightly, just enough.

Theo heard it before any of them saw it—not with her ears, but with the older sense, the buried one: hooves on stone, and beneath the hooves, the low mutter of hounds. Many hounds. And threaded through their voices, familiar as a scar, the pale-horse rhythm of the Warden himself.

At the third bend. Where the watcher owed money.

"He's here," she breathed. "Jonah—Maren—he's *here*."

Below them, far down the channel, torchlight bloomed. And the hounds began to sing.
