## 11. Hunted Across the Terraces

The trouble with orchard country, Theo decided somewhere around the fourth terrace down, was that it was built for people who were not running for their lives.

Every path was a neat switchback designed for farmers carrying baskets of pears at a contemplative stroll. Every wall was waist-high and decorative. There was nowhere to hide except behind trees that had been pruned into polite, open shapes, like guests at a party who wanted you to see exactly what they were wearing.

"Left," she gasped, because the hounds' voices came to her the way weather comes—before you can see it, you feel it in your teeth. "No, wait. Right. They've circled."

"They said left two seconds ago," Jonah panted beside her, one hand pressed to his ribs, the leather book strapped under his coat where it thumped against him like a second heartbeat. "You're getting contradictory intelligence."

"The hounds are not a monolith, Jonah. They have opinions."

Behind them, up on the ridge, the opinions in question bayed—a sound like bells being dragged through mud, wrong in a way that made the pear trees shiver even though there was no wind. The Warden's hounds did not smell like dogs. They smelled like magic going stale, like a song left out in the rain, and when they spoke in Theo's head their voices were layered, three or four of them sharing each throat, all of them hungry.

Except lately they weren't hungry for her.

That was the part she couldn't say out loud yet, not with Maren ahead of them on the path, waving them onward with the brisk authority of someone who had grown up ringing bells and considered emergency evacuation a scheduling matter.

"Faster," Maren called over her shoulder. She ran the way she did everything—efficiently, sleeves rolled, dark braid swinging, entirely unbothered by the fact that the world was ending behind them. "There's a drainage cut past the walnut terrace. If we get below the frost line before they crest the ridge, their noses go stupid. Cold air confuses them."

"You know a lot about hunting hounds," Jonah wheezed.

"I know a lot about *not being found*." Maren's grin flashed back at them, quick and bright. "It's a family tradition. We ring the bells so everyone else can hide."

Theo's chest did something complicated and painful. Three days ago this woman had been a stranger feeding them stew in a house full of silenced bells. Now she was leading them through her own country's bones, and every hour she spent doing it made her easier to lose.

*Don't think about that,* Theo told herself, and thought about it anyway, the whole way down the third terrace, hopping a low wall, ducking under a branch heavy with fruit nobody had picked in a hundred years because there had been nobody to pick it.

The fruit was perfect. That was somehow the saddest thing about Vessany—the orchards hadn't stopped. They'd just kept making sweetness for an empty country, year after year, patient as anything.

A hound spoke directly into her skull, close now, closer than it should have been: *She is here. She is here. She smells of doors.*

*Not doors,* Theo answered without meaning to, the way you answer someone who calls your name. *I'm right here. Yes.*

And then—this was the part that made her stomach drop through the bottom of the world—the baying stopped.

All of it. All six voices, at once, mid-bay, like a choir losing its place.

"Theo?" Jonah had caught her arm. "Why did they stop? Stopping is bad, right? Stopping means planning?"

"I don't—" She swallowed. "I don't know."

But she did know. She knew exactly why, and the knowing sat in her throat like a swallowed stone, and she looked at Jonah's worried face and Maren's waiting silhouette against the sky, and she said nothing, because saying it would change everything, and she wasn't ready. She'd spent twenty-six years not ready.

They ran.

* * *

The drainage cut was real, a stone channel where meltwater once tumbled from terrace to terrace, now dry and shadowed and mercifully narrow. They went down it single file, Maren first, then Jonah, then Theo last, which meant Theo heard it happen.

Boots on stone above. Not hound-paws—boots. The Warden traveled with men, gray-cloaked and quiet, though the quiet ones were almost worse than the hounds, because the hounds at least told you what they were thinking.

Maren held up a fist. Everyone froze. Above them, at the lip of the channel, a gray shape leaned over the edge, and Theo felt the Warden's attention pass across them like a searchlight made of cold water.

Then the hounds spoke again, and this time they didn't bay.

This time they argued.

*Where is she? Where is the door-smelling one?*

*Down. Down and left. But—*

*But she does not run from us anymore. Why does she not run?*

*We do not want to bite her. We remember her voice. Her voice was warm.*

Theo closed her eyes. Below her, Jonah was watching her face in the dim light with the expression of a man assembling a very unwelcome puzzle, and she could practically hear the pieces clicking together: the hounds stopping, the hounds circling wrong, the hounds arguing about warmth.

*Oh,* he mouthed. No sound. Just *oh.*

The Warden's voice came down the channel then, and it was worse than the hounds, because it was human and ruined, a voice like a bell with a crack through it, every word arriving slightly broken.

"She is turning them." A pause. The cracked voice seemed almost puzzled, and that was the most frightening thing yet—that the hunter could be surprised. "One girl, and my hounds forget their orders. Find her. Take the traitor and the bell-girl if she interferes. The speaker I want breathing."

*Take the bell-girl.* The words went through Theo like ice water.

Because Maren wasn't supposed to be part of this. Maren had fed them stew. Maren had shown them the execution records with Jonah's face in them and laughed until she cried at Jonah's impression of a Vessany tax collector, and none of that was worth a gray cloak's attention, except that she was here, helping, interfering, being exactly the kind of person who got taken.

"We need to move," Theo whispered. "Now. Quietly."

Maren, bless her, was already moving, quick and silent as water finding its level. They went down another terrace, and another, the orchard closing around them, and for maybe ten minutes Theo let herself believe the cold air trick had worked, that the frost line was real, that they might actually—

That was when Maren stopped dead at the edge of the fifth terrace and said, conversationally, "Hm. That's inconvenient."

Below them, spread across the next three levels of orchard like spilled ink, stood the gray cloaks. Twelve of them. Maybe fifteen. And rising up the far slope, pale horse first, came the Warden.

"There's a path," Maren said quickly, already turning, already recalculating, her mind visibly working the problem the way other people worked jigsaws. "West wall, through the quince rows, down to the old cistern—it connects to the market tunnels, we could be at the sanctuary by—"

"Maren." Jonah's voice cracked. "They'll see us cross the open ground."

"Then we cross it fast."

"And if they don't miss?"

Maren looked at him. Then at Theo. And Theo watched, with a horrible slow clarity, as Maren arrived at the same arithmetic she herself had been avoiding for three days: someone had to be loud while the others were quiet. Someone had to draw eyes. And Maren was the only one of them who knew this country, who could find her way back, who had any business being caught.

"No," Theo said.

"I haven't said anything yet," Maren said.

"You're about to say 'I'll lead them off,' and I'm telling you no."

"You're telling me no?" Maren raised an eyebrow. In the middle of a manhunt. On a hillside. Theo would have laughed if her whole body weren't clenched around dread. "Theo. Sweetheart. You've known me four days. I've known these hills my whole life. If anyone gets to sacrifice themselves dramatically on this particular hillside, it's me. It's practically zoning law."

"It's not dramatic if it works," Jonah said desperately. "Can't we all just—run in different directions and feel bad about it later?"

"That's your plan?" Maren asked.

"It's a draft," Jonah said, with the ghost of a grin. "Prophecy is a draft. So are escape plans."

Above them, the hounds began to sing.

Not bay—sing, a long rising harmonic that made the pear trees drop their fruit, and Theo felt it land in her chest and *answer*, felt her own throat tighten with the urge to harmonize, and the hounds felt her answering, and their song wavered, split, turned toward her like compass needles.

Toward her. Away from Maren. Away from Jonah.

*Follow her,* the voices murmured, overlapping, confused, tender. *Follow the warm voice. Follow the door-voice home.*

And the Warden, on the pale horse on the ridge, saw it too. Theo couldn't see the face under the gray hood, but she saw the hood tilt, the way a person tilts their head when a suspicion becomes a certainty.

"Speaker," the cracked voice called down the hillside, conversational, almost kind. "Your gift is showing."

* * *

They ran west anyway. Of course they did. Maren led, Jonah stumbled after, Theo brought up the rear listening to six hound-minds trail her like devoted, terrible sheepdogs, and the guilt rode her shoulders the whole way, heavier than the book, heavier than anything.

Because here was the truth she'd been carrying since the hounds first stopped baying: they weren't hunting her anymore. They were *herding* her. Gently. Lovingly, insofar as things made of stolen magic could love. Every time the gray cloaks closed in, the hounds drifted wide, opened a gap, whined *this way, this way, safe this way* — and every gap they opened was a trap the Warden was patiently building around her, because he had understood something in the last hour that Theo's whole valley had spent a century trying to suppress:

A beast-speaker doesn't hide. A beast-speaker accumulates.

And the fastest way to catch a beast-speaker is to follow the animals that adore her.

"They're clearing a path for me," she blurted, because Jonah deserved to stop assembling the puzzle himself. "Jonah. The hounds. They won't hunt me. They're—they're *following* me. They like me. And the Warden knows, and he's using it, every gap they leave is a trap, and I led us straight into—"

"I know," Jonah said.

"You *know*?"

"I've known since the channel." He grabbed her hand as they plunged down through the quince rows, thorns raking their sleeves. "Theo. Look at me. This isn't a failure of hiding. This is the opposite of hiding. Your whole life you've buried this thing, and the first time it comes out in daylight, it *saves* us—repeatedly—and the cost is that you can't bury it again. That's not you failing. That's you being *found*."

"That's very romantic," Theo gasped, "and we're still trapped in an orchard."

"Everything's a draft," Jonah said. "Even traps."

Ahead, Maren had stopped at a low stone arch half-swallowed by ivy—the cistern mouth, black and breathing cool air. And she had stopped for a reason, because standing between them and the arch, patient as Sunday, were three gray cloaks.

And behind those, coming down the slope unhurried, hooves clicking on stone, the Warden.

Maren did the math again. Theo watched her do it. Watched her shoulders set, watched her roll her sleeves the way she rolled them for everything, watched her arrive at the only answer her brave, stubborn, four-days-known heart was ever going to arrive at.

"Well," Maren said cheerfully, "I did tell you it was practically zoning law."

"Don't," Theo said.

"Somebody has to be loud." Maren was already pulling the little handbell from her belt—the one she'd carried since her father's day, the last unbroken bell in her family's keeping. "And I am *extremely* loud. It's genetic."

"Maren—"

"Go down the cistern. Follow the water marks. Left at the dry fountain, and whatever you hear, don't come back for me." She smiled, and it was the same bright efficient smile she'd given them over stew, and it broke Theo's heart cleanly in two. "I'm a bell-ringer's daughter. I know exactly how long a distraction needs to ring."

She stepped out onto the open terrace, lifted the bell, and rang it—one clear silver note rolling down the hillsides, the first bell Vessany had heard in a hundred years, and every gray cloak turned toward the sound like iron filings snapping to a magnet.

Even the Warden turned.

Even the hounds hesitated, torn between the warm voice and the ringing one.

"GO," Maren shouted, and rang the bell again, and walked toward fifteen enemies ringing it a third time, and Theo stood frozen at the cistern mouth with Jonah's hand crushing hers and the choice tearing her in half—go, follow, survive, or—

Jonah pulled her into the dark.

* * *

The cistern swallowed them. Cold air, dripping stone, the smell of a century of still water. Their footsteps echoed too loudly. Somewhere above, muffled by earth, the bell rang on—steady, metronomic, brave—and then, between one note and the next, it stopped.

Theo's knees nearly gave.

"She told us not to come back," Jonah whispered.

"I heard her."

"Theo—"

"I *heard her*, Jonah." Her voice came out strange and thick. In the darkness she could hear the cistern's rats stirring in the walls, could hear bats overhead shifting, could hear—far above, faint as memory—the hounds, sitting down in a row on some terrace, keening softly toward the hole in the ground where the warm voice had gone.

They weren't hunting her. They never would again. They were waiting for her like dogs wait at a door.

And that was the moment the last of it came loose, the secret she'd been rationing out in pieces since the millrace, because rescue—real rescue, the kind that meant going back up into the teeth of the Warden's men—was going to require everything she had, openly, in front of Jonah, in front of whoever waited at the end of these tunnels, with no burying and no pretending and no more drafts.

"Jonah," she said. "I need to tell you what I am. All of it. Before we go back up there."

He was quiet a moment. Water dripped. Then, gently: "Theo, I've seen you argue with hounds. I'm braced."

"It's not—I don't just *talk* to them." She wrapped her arms around herself. "When I speak, they listen. Really listen. Obey. And I've spent my whole life treating that like a disease, and now Maren is up there in a cage because I wouldn't say the words out loud, and the Warden wants me *breathing*, and I finally understand why." She looked up at him in the dark. "Because a voice like mine doesn't just open doors. It turns armies. And I'm done apologizing for it."

Somewhere ahead in the tunnel, faint but unmistakable, something answered her.

Not a rat. Not a bat.

Something large, with wings folded and a voice like gravel and honey, speaking words Theo had only ever read in the margins of Jonah's impossible book:

*At last,* it said. *At last, a speaker. Little sister—we have been waiting a hundred years for someone to talk to.*

"Jonah," Theo whispered, "I think the tunnels know we're coming."

And the griffin, patient in the dark, began to laugh.
