## 12. The Warden's Offer

Theo had always thought of herself as a person who could not be ambushed, because ambush required surprise and she had spent her whole life expecting the worst. Goats had cured her of optimism early. A goat will find the one gap in a fence you didn't know existed and stand in it, chewing, looking at you with an expression that says *I told you so* in a language older than fences.

So when the gray figure stepped out from between the olive trees on the upper terrace — no horse, no hounds, just the Warden himself, hands folded like a man waiting at a bus stop — Theo was not surprised.

She was, however, deeply annoyed.

"You," she said.

"Me," said the Warden.

He looked different without the pale horse. Smaller, somehow, though he was still tall in the way that lampposts are tall — vertical, patient, impossible to argue with. Up close his grayness resolved into details: a coat the color of river fog, hair gone silver at the temples, and eyes that were not cold at all but tired, the way her grandfather's eyes had been tired after long shifts at the cannery. The kind of tired that comes from doing a job for too many years because someone has to.

The hounds were nowhere in sight. That should have frightened her more than their presence did.

"Maren is in your citadel," Theo said. "Jonah is—"

"Jonah Reyes is currently climbing down the aqueduct wall behind the tannery with all the grace of a man who has never once considered that gravity applies to him." The Warden's mouth twitched. It might have been a smile. It might have been indigestion. "You have until he reaches the bottom to hear me out. He moves slowly when he's being brave. They always do."

Theo's heart did something complicated. She kept her face still. Years of hiding conversations with goats gave you a good poker face; there was nothing quite like maintaining composure while a nanny named Duchess explained, in detail, why your haircut was a personal insult.

"I'm not going anywhere with you."

"I'm not asking you to." He gestured to a low stone wall beside the terrace path, worn smooth by generations of orchard workers sitting down to rest. "Sit if you like. Stand if you prefer. Scream, if it would help — though I'd advise against it. My hounds are resting, and they're cranky when woken. One of them bit me Tuesday."

Despite herself, despite everything, Theo felt a laugh climb up her throat and had to strangle it. "Your own dog bit you?"

"He wasn't my dog. He was following you. There's a difference, and it's the entire reason we're having this conversation." The Warden sat down on the wall himself, which was somehow more disarming than anything else he could have done. A man who hunted you across terraces did not sit down and look at his boots. "The girl — Maren — is fed. She's warm. She has complained twice about the bread and once about the company, which tells me she's recovering her spirits. No harm will come to her. Or to Jonah. That is the offer."

"There's always a price."

"Yes." He said it simply, without shame. "There's always a price. I've never understood people who pretend otherwise. Everything costs. Bread costs. Rest costs. Love costs enormously and everyone signs up anyway." He looked up at her then, and his tired eyes were very direct. "Your gift, Theo Dunmore. Give it up, freely, and your friends walk free. And before you refuse on principle, let me tell you what you'd be refusing, because I don't think anyone has ever told you the truth about what you carry."

* * *

The wind moved through the olive trees with a sound like pages turning. Somewhere below, faintly, Theo could hear Jonah swearing at the aqueduct in two languages.

"I grew up here," the Warden said. "Before. In Vessany, when Vessany was a country and not a rumor with orchards attached. My mother sold figs at the market. My father tuned bells — not rang them, mind, tuned them. There's a difference. Ringers get the glory; tuners get the headaches." He paused. "I had a voice, back then. Not beast-speech. Something smaller. I could make things *listen*. Doors would open for me. Storms would go around. Once, when I was nine, I asked a wasp to leave the kitchen, and it did, and it told its friends, and we had no wasps for three summers."

"That's not small," Theo said quietly.

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. Nothing about us is small. That's rather the problem." He turned his hands over, palms up, and Theo saw it: a thin white scar across each throat-side of his wrists, old and deliberate, the kind of mark made on purpose by someone who knew exactly what they were doing. But that wasn't the thing. The thing was that when he spoke, his voice came out flat. Not hoarse, not damaged. Flat. Like a bell with the clapper removed — shaped perfectly to ring, and ringing nothing.

"I gave it up," he said. "Ninety-one years ago. Freely, the way you'd give it. And I want to be honest with you, girl, because everyone else in this country will lie to you out of hope or fear, and I've found that honesty from an enemy is worth more than hope from a friend."

He stood, and for a moment he let something loosen in his throat, some held-back shape of sound, and what came out was—

Nothing.

Not silence exactly. Silence has texture. This was absence, a hole in the air where a voice should have been, and Theo felt it against her skin like standing near an open freezer. The olive leaves nearest him went still. Even the wind seemed to step aside.

Then it closed, and he was speaking again in that flat, clapperless tone.

"That's what's left," he said. "I can speak words. I can give orders. I can sing the national anthem on feast days, badly, like everyone else. But the *listening* is gone. Do you understand? Your gift isn't that animals talk to you. Anyone can hear an animal make noise. Your gift is that you *hear*. The world talks constantly — doors, storms, wasps, dogs, rivers, the whole chattering creation — and almost nobody listens. You listen. When you give that up, you don't lose a trick. You lose the world."

Theo's hands had curled into fists without her permission. "Then why—" Her voice cracked and she hated it. "Why are you offering this to me? If you know what it costs?"

"Because I know what it costs," he said, "and I also know what refusing costs, and I have spent ninety-one years weighing those two sums, and I am telling you, from inside the arithmetic: pay mine." He began to walk, slow, along the terrace edge, and she fell into step beside him because her legs decided before she did. "Listen to me. The Choir is going to sing again. Maybe this year, maybe in fifty years — but they'll try, because old women who pause mid-note cannot bear to leave a song unfinished, it's against their religion. And when they finish it, Vessany comes back. All of it. The borders, the taxes, the wars we hid from — the conquest we erased ourselves to escape is still out there, still hungry, still keeping our maps in a drawer somewhere. Rebirth sounds like a blessing. It is also a door opening onto everything we died to avoid."

"And the Warden stops that?"

"The Warden *was* that. Once. A whole order of us — singers who regretted, keepers who chose erasure and meant it. We hold the country quiet. We hold the door mostly shut. We catch the ones who slip through, like you and your Jonah, and we send them home before they start hoping." He stopped walking. Below them, the terraced orchards fell away in green steps toward the town, where the empty bell tower stood against the sky like a held breath. "But something has gone wrong with my order, Theo. The hounds won't hunt you. Did you notice? They circle you. They follow you. Hounds bred for ninety years to smell magic and drag its carriers to me, and they've decided you're not prey." His flat voice did something almost gentle. "They're not broken. They're right. You're not what we hunt. You're what we were."

The words landed in her chest and sat there, heavy and warm and terrible.

"I'm not joining you," she said.

"No," he agreed. "You're not. You have a stubborn chin. I've arrested enough people to recognize one." He reached into his coat and drew out a folded paper, cream-colored, sealed with gray wax. He did not hand it to her. He simply held it where she could see the seal. "This is a pardon. Both of your friends, safe passage to any door, any time, no conditions except the one. You say yes, Maren walks out of her bell tonight, Jonah walks out of whatever ridiculous place he's breaking into tonight, and you all go home to your goats and your taverns and your dates that were going badly anyway — don't look shocked, he ordered the cheapest wine on the list and then pretended it was his favorite, I read the incident report from your valley, we're thorough."

"He was being charming," Theo said automatically, and then wanted to bite her own tongue off.

"He was being nervous," said the Warden. "It's worse, honestly. Nervous men order cheap wine and call it taste. Charming men order expensive wine and apologize." He tucked the paper away. "You don't have to answer now. You have until the Choir sings, which given their age and their knees gives you some months at least. But hear the last part, because no one else will say it plainly."

He turned to face her fully, and for a moment the gray coat, the gray eyes, the flat voice all aligned into something that was not a hunter at all but a man standing at the edge of his own long mistake, showing her the view.

"The door home stays shut either way," he said. "Until the Choir sings, you cannot leave. Not through the millrace, not through any crack. So you will live here, in a country that remembers you as a traitor's companion, hunted by my order, hiding in cellars, eating Maren's terrible bread. Months of it. Perhaps years. Every day, your gift grows louder here — you've noticed, haven't you? The stones talk to you now. The orchard roots. This land is soaked in listening; it's why the Choir chose it. And every day it gets harder to imagine giving it up, and every day giving it up becomes the only way to save the people you love. That is not an accident. That is the design. I designed it." A breath. "I built the trap I am now standing in, warning you about. Ninety-one years, girl. That's how long I've been sorry."

* * *

They stood there while the light went gold and long across the terraces.

Theo thought about her goats. That was the strange thing — in the middle of everything, exiled in a vanished country with a pardon in a gray man's pocket, what rose in her was Duchess's offended commentary and the stray dog from chapter one of her life, the first creature who'd ever told her a secret, and how lonely she had been her whole life in a valley full of people, and how the only cure had been conversations nobody knew she was having.

And she thought about the hounds. Circling. Following. Choosing.

"If I say yes," she said slowly, "you free them. Truly free. Not followed, not watched."

"On my name."

"What is your name?"

Something flickered across his face — surprise, then a pain so old it had fossilized into habit. "I haven't been asked that in sixty years," he said. "The order retires names. We become the office." He looked out at the bell tower. "Tomas. My name was Tomas. My father wanted me to tune bells after him. I was better at listening than tuning, so they took me instead, and I told myself it was an honor, and then I told myself it was necessary, and then I stopped telling myself things altogether, because telling yourself things requires a voice that believes itself."

"Tomas," Theo repeated.

"Don't," he said, and his flat voice cracked around the edges of the word, which should have been impossible and wasn't. "Don't make it a person. It's easier for both of us if I stay a gray coat."

"It was already too late," Theo said gently. "You told me about the wasp."

For a moment — just a moment — the Warden, the terror of a hidden nation, the man on the pale horse, looked like a boy whose father had wanted him to tune bells.

Then he straightened, and the coat was gray again, and the office was back. "Think on it. The pardon keeps. But I'll tell you what I told the last one who stood where you're standing, ninety years ago, before she ran off to join the Choir and became somebody's grandmother." He began to walk away, down the terrace path, and his parting words drifted back through the olive trees like something the wind was reluctant to carry:

"She asked me what I'd do, if I could choose again. I told her I honestly didn't know anymore. That's the cost, Theo Dunmore. Not the silence. The not-knowing. Don't let anyone take your not-knowing from you."

* * *

She found Jonah at the bottom of the aqueduct, sitting in the mud, breathing hard, one shoe missing, holding a length of rope he had clearly lost an argument with.

"You're filthy," Theo said.

"I'm *infiltrating*," Jonah said with dignity. "Infiltration is dirty work. Also I think I broke a finger. Also — Theo, I found it. The citadel. I found the records room, I saw it through the grate, and there's a ledger with our names in it, and Theo — there's a death warrant in there with my face on it. Signed—" His voice dropped, and all the bravado drained out of him at once, leaving something young and scared. "Signed by my grandmother. Dated next week."

Theo looked at him — muddy, brave, holding a rope like a defeated enemy — and then up at the citadel rising black against the last light, and then at her own two hands, which could hear the stones humming under the road like a choir tuning up.

She had until the Choir sang. Tomas had said so.

But Jonah had until next week.

"We're getting Maren tonight," Theo said. "Both of them. Before your grandmother's ink dries."

Jonah stared at her. "How?"

Theo smiled, and somewhere on the darkening hillside, forty hounds lifted their heads as one, and began, softly, to follow her.
