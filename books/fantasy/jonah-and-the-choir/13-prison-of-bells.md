## 13. Prison of Bells

The Warden's citadel had been a bell tower once. Jonah could tell by the shape of it—a tall stone finger rising out of the terraced hills, wider at the shoulders, tapering toward a crown that no longer held bells but something grayer. The Vessany he'd seen so far was all orchards and honeyed light and terraces stitched into the hillsides like a grandmother's quilting. The citadel was what happened when someone took that quilt and turned it over to look at the ugly back of the stitching.

He crouched in the ditch below the outer wall, mud soaking through his jeans, and thought—not for the first time—that his grandmother had a lot to answer for.

"You could have left me a map," he muttered to her memory. "Or a warning. Or literally anything other than a book that writes my name in century-old ink."

The book sat against his ribs inside his jacket, warm as a sleeping animal. It had been quiet since the mill burned. Quiet in the way a dog is quiet when it's watching a door.

* * *

Getting in was insultingly easy, which Jonah distrusted immediately.

The gate guards were not men but shapes—gray figures like the Warden himself, faceless under hoods, holding pikes they never shifted on their shoulders. They stood at the gate the way furniture stands in a room. When Jonah approached along the wall's shadow, book pressed flat against his chest, the nearest gray head tilted—listening—and then swung away again, as if he'd been filed under a category that didn't require attention.

*Unimportant,* Jonah translated. *Not worth noticing.*

It stung more than it should have. He'd spent his whole life being charming precisely so people would notice him. Apparently in Vessany, charm was not a recognized currency.

Inside, the courtyard was swept clean as a parlor. Ranks of gray figures drilled in silence—no shouted commands, no drum, just hundreds of boots moving in perfect unison without one sound. It was the quietest army Jonah had ever seen, and somehow that made it worse. An army that shouts is an army that wants you afraid. An army that says nothing has already decided about you.

He slipped along the inner wall, following the only guidance he had: Maren's voice in his memory, from weeks ago now, or days—the time here slid around like water in a cupped hand.

*"They'll put me somewhere sound can't reach,"* she'd said once, when they'd talked about what capture would mean. She'd said it lightly, the way she said most hard things, buttering bread while she did it. *"Bell-ringers' daughters know how these things go. If they take me, don't come looking. But if you're stupid enough to come looking anyway—"*

She'd smiled then.

*"—then find the bell that doesn't ring."*

* * *

The citadel's interior was a maze of staircases that went up and up, past floors of offices where gray clerks copied gray documents in gray ink, none of them making a mark of noise. Jonah passed a doorway and heard—no, didn't hear—the absence of a quill scratching. His skin crawled.

On the seventh floor, he found the archive.

He hadn't meant to find it. He'd meant to keep climbing, but a door stood ajar, breathing cold air, and inside were shelves upon shelves of ledgers, and one ledger lay open on a reading table as if waiting for him.

Jonah Reyes had been raised by his mother on stories of his grandmother: Alma Reyes, who died before he was born, who kept bees, who sang off-key and didn't care. He knew her handwriting from birthday cards preserved in a shoebox. Round, confident letters, the kind that leaned forward like they were going somewhere.

The ledger's entry was dated ninety-nine years ago. The handwriting leaned forward like it was going somewhere.

*Subject: J. Reyes. Status: traitor to the Silence. Sentence: death by the long fall. Warrant signed and sealed.*

And beneath it, the signature: *Alma Reyes.*

The floor did not tilt, though Jonah gripped the table anyway. The room stayed exactly where it was, indifferent, well-organized, and insane.

"That's not possible," he told the ledger conversationally, because talking to objects was apparently a family trait he'd inherited along with the cursed stationery. "You're dead. You've been dead since before I existed. I have your birthday cards. They say things like 'bee well.' You signed a death warrant for a man who wasn't born yet?"

The book against his ribs grew warmer.

He should have left it there. He knew that even as his hands opened the jacket and drew the leather volume out and laid it beside the ledger, old pages next to old pages, grandmother's drafts next to grandmother's verdicts.

The book fell open on its own—to a page he'd never seen, near the end, where the ink was freshest. New lines were surfacing even as he watched, rising through the paper like fish coming up to feed:

*The boy goes into the tower. The tower remembers him. He finds the warrant and does not run, because running is what the draft expected, and he is tired of being written.*

Jonah laughed—one short, cracked note in the silent building, and flinched at the sound of it, because in this place sound felt like blood in the water.

"Okay," he said softly. "Okay. You want to play it that way."

He understood it suddenly, standing there with his own death warrant and his own prophecy side by side: the book wasn't predicting him. It was *drafting* him. And somewhere in this tower was the pen that had signed the first draft—a woman with round, leaning-forward handwriting who had chosen, ninety-nine years ago, to condemn a grandson she would never meet rather than let the song finish.

*Why?*

That was the question that got its hooks in him and pulled him onward, up the stairs, past the drilling silences, deeper into the gray heart of the citadel. Not fear. Not anymore. Fear had curdled into something more useful.

His grandmother had helped build this trap. Which meant his grandmother—or whatever echo of her lived in the book, in the ink, in the machinery of this place—had also built the door out of it. Drafts had revisions. He was going to find the revision.

* * *

He found the bell on the twelfth floor.

Not by sight—the corridor there was featureless stone—but by wrongness. Every other part of the citadel hummed faintly with suppressed sound, like a throat holding a cough. This corridor swallowed. Sound went in and did not come out. Jonah tested it: he dropped a coin, and the coin hit the stones with all the drama of snowfall.

At the corridor's end hung the bell.

It was enormous—taller than Theo, wide as a wagon wheel, bronze gone green-black with age—and it hung from a beam with no rope, no clapper, no crack in it anywhere. Its surface was smooth as a closed eye. Carved around its crown ran words in old Vessany script that Jonah couldn't read, though the book in his jacket tugged toward them like a compass needle.

And inside the bell—if you pressed your ear to the cold metal—you could hear her.

Maren. Not her voice; her voice had been taken the way the bell took everything, poured in and sealed. What remained was rhythm: the pattern of her breathing, quick and stubborn, and beneath it, fainter, the ghost of a tune she was tapping out against the bronze from within—some bell-ringer's exercise, patient as rain, counting the hours.

She was alive. She was in there. She was *keeping time*, because she was her father's daughter and her grandmother's granddaughter, and if Maren Bell-Ringer's-Daughter was going to be imprisoned in a bell, she was damned well going to practice.

Jonah pressed both palms flat against the bronze and felt the answering tap-tap-tap stutter, then quicken. She'd felt him. She couldn't hear him—he was sure of it—but she could feel the vibration of his hands, and she tapped back a question, urgent now, rapid.

"I'm here," he said, knowing it wouldn't carry, saying it anyway. "I'm getting you out. Don't ask me how yet. I don't know how. But I'm—"

Tap. Tap-tap. Tap.

He didn't speak bell-code. But he was starting to learn the grammar of it: three quick taps meant *hurry*. Two slow ones meant *careful*. And the long dragging scrape she made now, fingernail or knuckle drawn down the inside of the bell, meant something he understood in his bones:

*You are being an idiot, and I am frightened, and do it anyway.*

"Yeah," he whispered. "That's about the size of it."

* * *

The problem, as ever, was the Warden.

Jonah found him—or found his workroom—on the floor above the bell. The door was unlocked, which in a fortress of locked silences was either arrogance or invitation, and Jonah had learned enough in Vessany to suspect both.

Inside was a room that made his stomach drop.

Hooks lined the walls, and from the hooks hung voices. There was no better word for it. Small glass vessels, clay jars, lead-sealed boxes, each one faintly trembling, each one holding something that strained against its container like a moth in a cupped hand. A jar labeled in clerk's-gray ink hummed a lullaby fragment. A box rattled with what sounded like laughter, muffled and desperate. Dozens of them. Hundreds. A whole country's worth of confiscated sound, catalogued and shelved.

And on the great desk in the center, under a weight of gray glass, lay a single sheet of paper, covered in fresh writing.

*The beast-speaker will come for the bell-ringer. Let her come. The city must see what she is. The gift must be shown to be dangerous, so that its surrender will look like mercy.*

Beneath it, another line, in different ink, older, rounder, leaning forward:

*Unless the boy rewrites the ending first.*

Jonah stared at the two lines of handwriting—his grandmother's underneath, the Warden's on top—and felt the whole shape of his situation snap into focus with an almost audible click. He wasn't sneaking into a trap. He was standing inside a *draft*, and the Warden was an editor too, and the two of them had been annotating the same manuscript for a century while the story bled.

"Grandmother," he said to the empty room, to the jars of stolen voices, to the woman whose birthday cards said *bee well*, "we need to talk about your editorial process."

The book against his ribs pulsed, warm, insistent. New ink was rising. He didn't look down at it. For once, he didn't want to read ahead.

Because here was what he knew, standing in the Warden's workroom with Maren's heartbeat tapping patiently one floor below: the Warden wanted Theo to come. Wanted the whole gray city to watch a beast-speaker revealed, so her gift could be framed as threat, so her sacrifice could be staged as mercy. Everything Jonah did next had to happen outside that draft.

Which meant doing the one thing neither author expected.

He took the sheet from the desk—the Warden's plan, the trap in writing—and folded it into his jacket beside the book. Then he looked around the room, at the hooks and jars and boxes, at a hundred years of stolen sound, and did the most reckless thing available to him.

He picked up the nearest jar—the one humming the lullaby—and unstopped it.

Sound came out like water from a broken dam. A woman's voice, singing half a verse of something old and sweet, filled the room, spilled into the corridor, rolled down seven floors of silent stairwell like a lit match falling through a dark house.

Every gray figure in the citadel turned its hooded head at once.

Jonah was already running—down the stairs, four at a time, toward the bell, toward Maren, toward the sound of his own name being written and rewritten somewhere in the ink of his veins. Behind him the lullaby echoed and echoed, refusing to be shelved, and above him, somewhere in the high gray places, the Warden finally made a sound of his own.

It was not a shout. It was worse.

It was a single, clear, ringing note—like a bell struck once—and every light in the citadel went out.

In the sudden dark, against his chest, the book wrote one final line before he reached the stairs' bottom, and this time he read it, because he had no choice, because it blazed through the leather like fire through paper:

*The boy frees the bell. The bell remembers everything.*

One floor below, in the swallowing dark, Maren's tapping stopped.

Then, slow and deliberate, she began to ring.
