## 14. The Rescue That Costs a Song

The citadel of the Warden had been built by people who believed that beauty was a form of disobedience.

Theo saw it that way from the first moment she crouched in the shadow of its outer wall: every stone squared, every window narrow as a slit, every surface scrubbed of anything that might have been mistaken for ornament. Even the banners were gray. Not white, not black—gray, the color of a word spoken too quietly to mean anything.

Beside her, Jonah pressed his back to the cold masonry and pulled his hood lower. In the three days since Maren had been taken on the terraces, he had aged what felt like a decade and a half, most of it around the eyes.

"Say it again," he whispered. "The plan. Slowly. I want to hear how insane it sounds one more time before we die."

"It's not insane," Theo said. "It's simple."

"Simple things can be insane."

"The griffin is kept in the east tower. The bells are kept in the west. Maren is—" Theo's throat tightened, and she made herself finish, "—Maren is being held in a bell. A bell that swallows sound. So no one hears her. No one hears *anything* from her cell."

"And your part is to walk up to a griffin—a creature that could take my head off with a sneeze—and have a chat."

"A conversation," Theo corrected. "Chats are casual. This will be sincere."

Jonah looked at her for a long moment. In the gray half-light his face did something complicated—fear and faith fighting over the same territory—and then he huffed a laugh through his nose, which was, Theo had learned, how Jonah Reyes said *I love you* when saying it out loud would be inconvenient.

"Sincere," he repeated. "Right. And my part is the death warrant."

"Your part is the death warrant," Theo agreed. "Which you found in the records office, signed by your grandmother, which means somewhere in this building there's paper with your name on it and an execution date. Which means the guards expect you. Which means they'll be looking at doors, not at a girl talking to a bird."

"They'll be looking at a girl talking to a *griffin*."

"That's the point," Theo said. "By the time they understand what they're looking at, we'll already be gone."

She wished she believed it as firmly as she said it. But there was no more time for believing things comfortably. Somewhere above them, in a tower full of swallowed sound, Maren was sitting in silence so complete it must have felt like being buried alive with the dirt politely refusing to touch her. Maren, who had fed them stew like they'd always belonged. Maren, who had bought them thirty seconds on the terraces with her own freedom and hadn't even made it dramatic about it—she'd just turned around, lifted her chin, and said, *Run, then,* as if it were the most obvious instruction in the world.

Theo was done running from things. She'd done it her whole life, quietly, expertly, the way you learn to do anything you practice daily.

"Go," she told Jonah. "West tower. I'll make noise in the east."

"What kind of noise?"

"The kind," Theo said, "that has feathers."

* * *

The east tower smelled of straw and copper and something older underneath—the smell of a large animal kept too long in too small a place, which is a smell Theo knew from her worst days tending neglected flocks, and which had always made her want to commit small crimes on behalf of goats.

The griffin stood in the center of the round chamber, chained at the ankle to an iron ring set deep in the floor. It was smaller than the stories promised and sadder than the stories dared: feathers the color of storm-light gone dull, wings folded tight against its body like a person trying to take up less room. Its eagle-half was turned toward the slit of a window, where a strip of gray sky showed. Its lion-half was turned toward nothing at all.

When the door closed behind Theo, the griffin's head came around. Its eyes were gold and furious and, beneath the fury, exhausted in a way Theo recognized down to the bone.

It screamed at her. The sound filled the tower like water filling a cup.

Theo stood very still and let it finish.

"I know," she said, when the echo died. "I know exactly. You've been screaming at walls for years, and the walls don't answer, and neither do the people, because people who chain things don't want conversations. They want quiet." She took one slow step forward. "I'm not going to tell you to be quiet."

The griffin's crest rose. Its beak opened—not to scream this time, but on the edge of it, deciding.

"You can scream at me all night if you want," Theo said. "I'll listen to every note. That's the whole reason I'm here. Everyone else in this building wants something from you. I just want to talk."

Silence. The long, assessing silence of an animal deciding whether a human is a person or weather.

Then, low, rough as gravel turning over: ***Small voice. Strange voice.***

Theo's breath caught. She had spent twenty-six years hearing animals speak, and it had never once stopped feeling like a door opening in a wall she'd been told was solid.

***You hear me,*** the griffin said. It wasn't a question. It was the question. ***The gray man says hearing is sickness.***

"The gray man says a lot of things," Theo said. "What's your name?"

A pause. Then, almost shyly, in a voice far too large for shyness: ***Kestrel-of-the-Long-Wind. But the gray man calls me Guard.***

"He would." Theo crouched down, arms loose, the way she did with frightened ewes. "Kestrel-of-the-Long-Wind. How did they get you?"

***Sky-net over the pass. My wing-mate tore free. I did not.*** The gold eyes flicked to the window, to the strip of sky. ***She will think I fell.***

"She'll think you fell until tonight," Theo said. "Because tonight you're going to fly home and scream at her from a great height until she believes you."

Something moved through the griffin's feathered shoulders—a shiver that might have been hope trying to remember its shape. ***The chain.***

"I know about the chain." Theo had spent two days learning about the chain—from Maren's cousin's old maps, from a stable cat who traded gossip for scratches, from Jonah's increasingly unhinged sketches of guard rotations. "Iron ring, iron links, and the lock is on the inner side, facing the wall, because whoever built this place never imagined anyone would stand where I'm standing now and ask you nicely."

***And if asking nicely fails?***

"Then we improvise," Theo said. "But Kestrel—may I call you Kestrel?—the nice asking comes first. Because here's the thing nobody in this gray building understands: I don't want to free you *from* something. I want to free you *to* something. There's a country outside these walls that's about to wake up. Bells that haven't rung in a hundred years are going to ring. Orchards that haven't fruited are going to fruit. And there is going to be a truly embarrassing amount of open sky, and I would like you to see it."

The griffin stared at her.

***You speak like a singer,*** it said at last. ***The old ones spoke like that. Before the gray.***

"I've been told I talk too much," Theo said. "It's nice to finally have it called singing."

She crossed to the wall, found the lock where the stable cat had promised it would be, and set to work with the bent nail Jonah had stolen from the armory, whispering apologies to every god of locksmiths she'd ever heard of and several she invented on the spot. Behind her, the griffin watched with its head tilted, and after a while it began, very softly, to make a sound Theo had never heard a griffin make.

It was laughing.

***The nail,*** it said. ***They keep me with war-iron and warding-sigils and a warden who smells fear three valleys off. And you come with a nail.***

"Borrowed," Theo said, working it deeper into the lock. "From a guard who's currently asleep. Well—asleep soon. He yawned at me earlier; I'm counting it."

The lock was old and bitter and fought her for eleven minutes. On the twelfth, something inside it gave up with a clunk that sounded, in the silent tower, like a gunshot.

The chain slid loose across the straw.

For a moment, Kestrel-of-the-Long-Wind didn't move. She looked down at her unchained ankle as though the ground had betrayed her by letting go. Then she spread her wings—all the way, for the first time in years, feathers rasping out to their full storm-colored width until they brushed both walls—and the sound she made was not a scream and not a laugh but something between, something that had been waiting years in a cramped throat for exactly this much room.

***Small voice,*** she said, and her voice was not small at all anymore. ***What do you need of me?***

"Maren," Theo said. "There's a girl in a bell in the west tower. We're going to go get her. And then we're going to introduce you to some friends of mine who sing."

* * *

Getting Maren out was, in the end, almost insultingly easy, which should have warned them.

The west tower's guards had been drawn east by the sound of a griffin screaming—which Kestrel had provided generously, on Theo's request, as a diversion she described afterward as *the finest performance of my life.* Jonah had already reached the bell chamber by the time they arrived, standing pale and determined among rows of hanging bells, holding the key he'd lifted from the records office along with his own death warrant.

The bell that held Maren was easy to find. It was the only one that made no sound at all—not when wind touched it, not when rain struck it, not when Jonah rapped it gently with his knuckles. Sound went in and did not come out. Standing next to it, Theo felt the silence press against her eardrums like deep water.

Jonah unlocked the clapper-cage, swung the great bell aside, and there was Maren—crumpled, blinking, lips cracked, eyes wild with a hundred hours of nothing.

She looked at the three of them—at Jonah, at Theo, at the enormous storm-feathered griffin folded awkwardly in a stairwell built for humans—and her mouth worked twice before any sound came out.

"Well," she croaked, in a voice rusted from disuse. "Took you long enough. Did you bring food?"

Theo laughed until she cried, and wasn't entirely sure which one she was doing.

* * *

They nearly made it. That was the thing Theo would turn over and over in the months afterward—they *nearly made it*, clean as a story, out the postern gate and into the olive terraces before the city woke.

It was the bells that undid them. Of course it was.

Because Vessany's citadel sat above a city of bell-towers, and a freed griffin does not fly quietly past a hundred bells, and when Kestrel launched herself from the wall into the pre-dawn dark, wings thundering, one startled watchman looked up and saw her—one saw her, and saw the girl on her back clinging to her neck feathers, and understood in a single horrified heartbeat what he was seeing.

"*Beast-speaker!*" he shouted, and his voice cracked across the sleeping city like a whip. "*A beast-speaker rides the griffin! Wake! Wake! The beast-speaker—*"

Below, in the shadow of the gate, Theo felt the words land on her like cold water. All her life she had hidden this. Her whole self, folded up small, carried silently, defended with lies and careful silences. And now a stranger had named it, loudly, in public, and the name was running through the streets faster than any pursuit.

Doors banged open. Lamps bloomed in windows. Voices took up the cry and passed it along, street to street, tower to tower, until the whole gray city rang with it: *beast-speaker, beast-speaker, the beast-speaker walks.*

Jonah grabbed her hand. Maren, riding double behind Theo with her arms locked around her waist, leaned close and shouted over the wind:

"They're afraid of you!"

"I know!" Theo shouted back.

"No—" Maren's grip tightened. "Listen! Some of them are afraid. But some—" Below them, in a doorway, an old woman had come out in her nightclothes, and she wasn't shouting or hiding. She was looking up at Theo with her hand pressed flat over her heart, and her mouth was moving, and though Theo couldn't hear the words over the wingbeats, she could read them plain as print: *thank you. Thank you. Someone finally.*

"Some of them have been waiting," Maren finished.

Behind them, the citadel's horns began to sound, and far below, at the head of the waking streets, a gray figure on a pale horse emerged from the gates and did not shout at all. The Warden simply looked up—looked straight at Theo, across all that dark air, with an expression she couldn't name—and raised one hand. Not commanding the hunt.

Pointing. Marking her. *That one.*

Kestrel banked hard south, toward the hills, toward the ruined amphitheater where nine singers waited with an unfinished song, and Theo pressed low against the warm storm-feathered neck and let the wind take her tears sideways off her face.

They had Maren. They had their lives. They had, wheeling joyfully overhead, a griffin who kept shouting ***FREE! FREE! FREE!*** at the moon like a drunkard at a wedding.

And by sunrise, every soul in Vessany would know what Theo had spent her whole life burying: that a beast-speaker walked among them, and that she was headed for the Choir.

There was no more hiding. There was only what came next.

"Hold on," Kestrel called back to them, wings beating toward the hills. "***Hold on, small voice. The singers will want to meet the girl who talks like springtime. And the gray man—***"

She glanced back once, at the distant rider on the distant road, keeping pace below them like a shadow that had learned patience.

"***—the gray man is coming too.***"
