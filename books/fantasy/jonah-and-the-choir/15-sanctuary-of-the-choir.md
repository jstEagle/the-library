## 15. Sanctuary of the Choir

The amphitheater had been hiding in plain sight for a hundred years, which Theo decided was very Vessany of it.

They came down through a fold in the terraces where the olive trees grew crooked and old, their trunks twisted like hands wringing themselves, and there it was: a bowl of stone sunk into the hillside, half-swallowed by ivy and wild fig. The seats were pale limestone, worn smooth at the edges by generations of sitting people who no longer existed. Grass grew up between the rows in soft green aisles. At the center lay an orchestra pit flooded with two feet of clear water, and in that water, lily pads floated in a perfect circle, as if someone had arranged them.

Or as if they'd arranged themselves, Theo thought, because nothing here did anything as ordinary as simply growing.

"It's beautiful," Jonah said quietly.

"It's empty," Maren said. "That's the point."

She had barely spoken since the terraces—since the soldiers took her father's whistle, since the hounds turned and followed Theo like ducklings instead of hunting them—and when she did speak her voice had a scraped, careful quality, as though she were rationing it. But now she walked down into the bowl with the confidence of someone coming home, past the third row, to a seat near the center where the stone was darker than the rest.

She sat down on it.

Nothing happened.

Then everything happened, slowly, the way dawn happens: the lily circles in the orchestra pit began to turn, gently, against no current at all. The ivy along the far wall shivered. And out of the hillside itself—out of seams Theo had taken for cracks in the rock—came light. Thin, warm, honey-colored, spilling from a dozen hidden apertures until the whole amphitheater glowed like the inside of a lantern.

"Oh," said Jonah.

"Oh," agreed Maren, and for the first time since the orchards she smiled, small and tired and real. "They always did love a dramatic entrance. You'd think after a century of hiding they'd have learned subtlety." She raised her voice toward the glowing seams. "It's me. It's Maren Bell. I've brought the grandson."

Silence. The particular silence of many people holding their breath.

Then a door that was not a door opened in the hillside behind the stage—a seam of shadow widening into a passage—and nine figures came out into the light.

Theo had expected ancient things. She didn't know why; perhaps because the country itself felt ancient, or because a century of erasure seemed like it ought to leave people dusty and gray. But the nine who walked down into the amphitheater were simply old the way trees are old—bent in places, straight in others, wearing clothes of undyed wool in colors the valley of Merrow's Hollow would have called drab and that here, in this light, looked like every color at once. A tall man with a farmer's shoulders. A woman whose hands were stained blue to the elbow, as if she'd been dyeing something for a hundred years without stopping. A pair of twins so identical they might have been one person reflected. And others, moving carefully, leaning on each other, blinking in the light of their own concealed lamps.

Nine. Theo counted twice. Nine singers, when the Choir had been hundreds.

And then she saw the tenth person, standing slightly apart at the back of the little procession, and forgot how counting worked entirely.

She was small. That was Theo's first thought, absurdly. *She's so small.* Silver hair cut short and practical, a wool shawl the color of oatmeal, hands folded in front of her like a woman waiting in a queue. She could have been anyone's grandmother at any market anywhere. She could have been nobody at all.

Except that Jonah made a sound Theo had never heard a human being make before—not a word, not a sob, something from underneath both—and sat down hard on the nearest stone step.

"Abuela?"

The woman looked at him for a long moment. Her face did something complicated—hope and fear wrestling to a draw—and then she said, in a voice roughened by disuse but unmistakably alive:

"Hello, mijo. You've gotten so tall. Though I suppose you would have."

Jonah got up. Sat down again. Got up. Walked toward her three steps and stopped, as if the air between them were deep water he wasn't sure would hold him.

"You're dead," he said. "You died. My mother has your funeral card. I've seen it. There were *lilies*."

"There usually are," said his grandmother. "Lilies are traditional. I'm sorry about the funeral. I imagine it was lovely."

"It was *rainy*," Jonah said, and his voice cracked clean through, and then he crossed the distance and held her, and the small woman held him back, and around the amphitheater the eight other singers looked away with the practiced courtesy of people who had waited a long time for this and intended to let it happen properly.

Theo found she was crying too, which annoyed her, because she hadn't decided to. She wiped her face on her sleeve and watched Jonah hold his dead grandmother under the honey-colored light of a country that didn't exist, and thought: *this is what it costs, and this is what it's for.*

* * *

Her name was Alma Reyes, and she made tea.

This struck Theo as the single most impossible thing that had happened all day, which was saying something, given the day. But there was a kitchen carved into the hillside behind the stage—a real kitchen, with a stove, and copper pots gone green at the edges, and shelves of jars labeled in a looping hand—and Alma moved through it with the ease of long habit, setting water to boil, finding cups, and talking the whole time in a low, tired, wonderfully ordinary voice.

"The song paused mid-note," she said, handing them cups of something that smelled like thyme and honey. "That's the whole trick of it, if you want the truth. Everyone thinks we sang Vessany away, finished, done, a curtain falling. We didn't. We sang up to the last line and stopped. Held it. Like a breath." She pressed her own chest briefly, as if demonstrating. "A country mid-syllable isn't gone. It's *unspoken*. There's a difference, and the difference is why any of us are still breathing."

"But you died," Jonah said. He had not let go of his teacup since receiving it, as if it were evidence. "There was a funeral. In Merrow's Hollow. Nineteen sixty-one."

"Mm." Alma sat down across from him, folding her hands the same way she'd been folding them when she first appeared, and Theo realized it wasn't patience—it was pain. Her hands hurt. Everything about her, Theo thought suddenly, hurt, in the quiet chronic way of a body that had been holding one enormous note for sixty years. "I left a body. Or rather, the song left a body. When you pause a country, you can't pause in your own skin—the note needs somewhere to live. So it lives in us, and we—" she gestured vaguely at herself, at the other elders visible through the doorway, playing some kind of slow board game by lamplight, "—we live in it. Round and round. No aging worth mentioning. No leaving, either. The door opens rarely, and never for us. We are the hinge, mijo. Hinges don't walk."

"You knew," Jonah said. "In the attic. You knew what the book was. You wrote it, and you let me find it, and you didn't say a word."

"I couldn't say a word. Literally. Anything I told you outside would have unspooled the moment you crossed over—that's the Warden's doing, most likely, or the song's own caution, I've never determined which. Prophecies written down survive. Spoken ones don't." She smiled, and it was a tired smile, and it reached all the way to her eyes anyway. "So I wrote drafts. Forty years of drafts. And I hid the book where my granddaughter-in-law would eventually go looking for Christmas decorations, because I know her, she reorganizes every December, and I knew you'd be the one to find it, because you were always the child who read the endings first."

"I read endings first because I don't like surprises," Jonah said.

"I know," said his grandmother. "I'm sorry about the surprises."

Theo, who had been sitting very still in the corner nursing her tea and feeling like a chaperone at a reunion across the boundary of death, cleared her throat. Alma turned to her immediately, and Theo felt the full weight of the woman's attention land on her like a warm coat—sudden, heavy, oddly comforting.

"And you must be the beast-speaker," Alma said.

Every head in the kitchen turned. Through the doorway, the board game stopped.

"We heard the hounds turn," Alma said, before Theo could decide whether to be alarmed. "Eighty years I've listened to those hounds hunt. They sing while they run, did you know? A sort of counter-melody to the Warden's orders. Today they changed key." Her eyes were bright and fierce and very, very tired. "Today they sang *harmony*. So. You'll be wanting to know what we're asking of you."

"I—" Theo began.

"No," Alma said gently. "First you eat. Nobody makes bargains hungry, that's how the Warden got half of his converts. Marta!" she called through the doorway. "Bring the stew, and the good bread, and stop letting Osip win, he cheats when he thinks we're not watching."

* * *

The stew was lamb and white beans and something herbaceous Theo couldn't name, and it was the best thing she had ever eaten, and she told the cook—a round, cheerful woman named Marta with flour on her forearms—as much, and Marta looked so pleased that Theo understood in one flash why Maren had risked everything to lead them here. This was what belonging smelled like. Bread and thyme and woodsmoke, in a hole in a hillside at the end of the world.

They ate at a long table in the main hall, all twelve of them, and the elders asked questions the way people do when they've been starved of news: not about politics or war, but small things. What did the valley's bells sound like now. Had the millrace ever been repaired. Did children still dare each other to touch the Oath-stone. Maren answered most of them, her voice loosening spoonful by spoonful, and once she laughed—an actual laugh, startled out of her—and the tall farmer, Osip, looked at her like a man watching the sun come up somewhere he'd been told it never would.

Theo mostly listened. But she noticed things. She noticed that the blue-handed woman, whose name was Sefa, kept glancing at Theo's hands whenever Theo gestured, with an expression of professional interest, the way a locksmith looks at a lock. She noticed that the twins kept finishing each other's sentences and that nobody else seemed to find this remarkable. And she noticed that Alma watched Jonah eat with an expression Theo recognized from her own mother: the look of someone memorizing a face against future loss.

Afterward, while Marta and Osip argued about dishes, Alma drew Theo aside into the passage behind the kitchen, where the honey-light came through a seam in the rock and fell across the floor in a bright bar.

"I owe you an apology," Alma said.

"For what?"

"For what we're going to ask you. For what the book already asked you, in ink, before you ever consented." She looked at the floor, at the bar of light, anywhere but at Theo. "I wrote your name into a prophecy, girl. I dressed it up as fate because fate asks nothing of anyone. But prophecy is a draft—I taught Jonah's grandmother that, and Jonah's grandmother is me, and I keep having to learn it again." She finally met Theo's eyes. "Whatever the book says. Whatever the Choir says. Whatever *I* say. You will always have the right to put the pen down."

Theo thought of the goats. Of the dog who had warned her, of the hounds running beside her like friends, of a griffin she hadn't freed yet but felt waiting somewhere ahead like a debt. Of a voice she'd spent her whole life burying, offered now as a key.

"What exactly does the song need?" she asked. "To finish?"

Alma's face went carefully, terribly neutral. "Tomorrow," she said. "Tomorrow I'll show you the score, and the door, and all of it, honestly, I promise you. Tonight—" and here the great prophet of Vessany, the woman who had sung a nation out of existence, yawned enormously and without any dignity whatsoever, "—tonight you sleep. Prophecy keeps. It always has. That's rather its problem."

* * *

They gave them a room carved from the same honey-lit stone, two narrow beds with quilts that smelled of lavender and decades. Jonah lay on his bed staring at the ceiling, fully clothed, and Theo lay on hers staring at the same ceiling, and for a while neither of them spoke.

"My grandmother is alive," Jonah said finally.

"Yes."

"She's been alive this whole time. While my mom lit candles every year. While—" His voice caught. "While I told her stories about Abuela's cooking like it was family lore. Dead people lore."

"She couldn't tell you."

"I know *why* she couldn't tell you. Knowing why doesn't fix it." He rolled onto his side, facing Theo across the dark. "Can I ask you something strange?"

"You've asked me several strange things this week. One more won't tip the scale."

"When you talk to animals. What does it feel like? Honestly. Not the valley's version—sin, shame, hide it. What's it actually like?"

Theo considered. The honest answer rose in her like water finding its level.

"Like being less alone," she said. "Always. Even when it's just a goat complaining about rain. Especially then." She pulled the quilt up. "Your grandmother asked me tonight what the song needs. I didn't answer her. But I think I know, and I think she knows I know, and tomorrow she's going to show me a door and a price, and I'm going to have to decide whether I'm the kind of person who pays it."

"You don't have to decide anything tonight."

"No," Theo agreed. "But I'm going to anyway. I always do. I lie awake deciding things. It's my second-worst habit."

"What's the first?"

"Talking to goats."

Jonah laughed—really laughed, the first time since the scarecrow with his face—and the sound went up through the honey-lit stone, and somewhere deeper in the hillside, faint as memory, something answered. Not words. A hum. Nine voices' worth of a held note, resonating through the rock like a tuning fork the size of a country, and Theo felt it pass through her ribs and settle somewhere behind her sternum, patient as a seed.

*Jonah,* she almost said. *I think your grandmother's song has been waiting for me my whole life.*

She didn't say it. She slept instead, and dreamed of bells.

* * *

She woke to shouting.

Not frightened shouting—astonished shouting, the shouting of people who have seen something impossible before breakfast. Theo was up and out of the room in seconds, following the sound down the passage to the main hall, where all nine elders stood at the mouth of the amphitheater, looking out into the morning light.

Maren was already there, and Jonah, and beyond them, at the top rim of the bowl, silhouetted against the sunrise—

Hounds. Eight of them, sitting in a row along the amphitheater's edge like a jury, like a congregation, like dogs waiting to be told they're good. And behind them, on the ridge, gray against the gold, a pale horse stood riderless, cropping grass.

"They followed us," Maren whispered. "All night. They—" She turned to Theo, and her face was white. "Theo. They're not hunting. They're *waiting*. On you."

Alma Reyes stepped forward to stand beside her grandson, and the oldest singer among them, the blue-handed Sefa, said softly, in the voice of a woman reading a score she'd hoped never to conduct:

"The Warden has let them off the leash. Which means either he's given up—"

"He hasn't," said Alma.

"—or he wants to see," Sefa finished, "what they do when the beast-speaker decides."

Eight hounds looked down into the amphitheater. Theo looked up at eight hounds. Behind her sternum, the held note of a paused country hummed, and waited, and the morning held its breath along with everyone in it.

One of the hounds stood, stretched elaborately, and began to trot down the aisle toward her, tail high, like a dog coming home.
