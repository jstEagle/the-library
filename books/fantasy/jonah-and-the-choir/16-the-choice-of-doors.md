## 16. The Choice of Doors

The amphitheater smelled of rain that had fallen a hundred years ago and never quite dried.

Theo felt it in the stones under her palms as she climbed the last row of seats—damp, patient, the way a cellar smells, or a grave that someone still visits. Nine elders sat scattered through the tiers like old coins dropped in a fountain, none of them together, each wrapped in their own shawl and silence. Above them, where a stage should have been, there was only a circle of scorched earth, and above *that*, a sky that Theo was beginning to suspect wasn't entirely Vessany's own.

And at the center of it all, small and upright on the lowest step with her hands folded like a woman waiting for bread to rise, sat Jonah's grandmother.

"You're taller than your pictures," Alma Reyes said. "Both of you. Sit down before you fall down."

Jonah made a sound Theo had never heard from him before—not a word, not quite a laugh, something between a sob and a hiccup—and then he was down the steps three at a time, and the old woman was up to meet him, and they collided in the middle like weather fronts.

"I don't—" Jonah's voice cracked against her shoulder. "You're dead. You've been dead for six years. I carried you out of the hospice garden in my arms, I *buried* you, we put a stone—"

"And very nice it is too," said Alma, patting his back briskly. "Granite. You always had taste. Now hush, you're getting my shawl wet, and it's the only one I've liked in ninety years."

Theo hung back at the top of the rows, hands jammed in her pockets, watching the reunion with the peculiar ache of someone standing outside a lit window in winter. She knew this woman. Not personally—but she knew what she'd done. Maren had told them on the road, in the flat voice she used for hard things: *Alma Reyes was one of the nine. She wrote the song down so it could be finished someday. Then she walked out through the door and let the world believe she'd died.*

A century-old book of prophecies. A grandmother who faked her death to carry it into the world. A grandson who'd found it in an attic and dreamed his way to a door.

*The book writes back,* Theo thought. *Because she wrote it forward.*

Alma looked up over Jonah's shoulder, directly at her, and her eyes were sharp and brown and utterly unfooled.

"Well," she said. "Don't lurk, girl. Lurking is for people with something to hide, and you gave that up in the citadel. The whole city knows what you are by now. Come sit where I can see you."

*

They fed them first. Of course they did; they were Vessanyan, or had been, and Theo was learning that this country treated grief and supper as two courses of the same meal. The elders produced barley soup and dark bread and a honey so old its jar had been sealed with wax and prayer, and they ate in the amphitheater's shadowed gallery while the light went amber through the broken roof.

Maren sat pressed against Theo's side the whole time, still smelling faintly of the citadel's iron, her wrists raw where the bell-chains had been. She didn't talk much. But when Theo's hand shook lifting the spoon, Maren reached over and steadied it without comment, and that was somehow worth more than anything anyone said aloud.

"So," Jonah said finally, setting down his bowl. "You're going to tell us why. Why the book, why me, why—" he gestured at everything, the scorched stage, the silent bells, the whole erased country, "—*this*. And don't give me the gentle version. I've had six years of grieving you. I'd like to know if it was necessary."

Alma set down her spoon with a small click, and Theo saw the warmth go out of her face—not into coldness exactly, but into something older and more careful. The face of a woman about to do arithmetic with lives.

"Necessary," Alma repeated. "That's a dangerous word in this country. We used it in 1904, when the armies were coming over the pass. Conquest, conscription, our language banned within a generation—that was the future we could see. So the Choir sang the country out of existence. Every soul, every field, every bell, folded into a pause between one note and the next. The world forgot us. The maps forgot us. Even the conquerors forgot what they'd come to take." She smiled, and it was a terrible smile, thin as paper. "It worked beautifully. That's the tragedy. It worked so well that nobody remembers how to stop."

"Stop?" Theo said quietly.

"A song paused mid-note isn't finished," Alma said. "It's *held*. Like a breath. And a held breath, girl, however long you hold it, must eventually be either released or died holding." She turned to face Theo fully, and Theo felt the weight of it settle onto her shoulders like a yoke. "We are one hundred and twenty years into a held breath. The singers are dying—one by one, these eight behind me are what's left, and I am the youngest. When the last of us goes, the note fails. Vessany doesn't wake up. Vessany simply stops, everywhere at once, and no one will ever have known it existed. No grief. No record. Nothing."

The gallery was very quiet. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the empty bell tower and made a sound like a throat clearing.

"But it can be finished," Jonah said. "The song can be completed. That's what the book is—the score, the drafts—"

"The book is the argument," Alma corrected gently. "I wrote every version of the ending I could imagine, hoping one of them would prove survivable. They aren't. Here is the truth, and I'll say it once, plainly, because you deserve plainness: to release the note, the song must be sung complete, by the Choir, in this place. And the final verse requires a voice the land has never heard. A living voice, freely given—not performed, not coerced, not traded. *Given.* The land takes nothing it isn't offered, because offering is the whole point. We saved ourselves by giving everything. To bring us back, someone must give something irreplaceable." Her eyes did not move from Theo's face. "Your gift would do."

The words landed in Theo's chest like stones dropped down a well.

She had known. She realized, sitting there with the honey-sweetness still on her tongue, that some part of her had known since the market, since the vendor whispered about the Warden, maybe since the first hound laid its head against her knee on the terraces. Everything in Vessany ran toward this. The door hadn't opened for Jonah. It had opened for *her*.

"My beast-speech," she said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "You want me to sing away the thing I've hidden my entire life. The only part of me that ever felt true."

"I want you to *choose* it," Alma said. "Which is not the same thing, and I will not pretend otherwise. If you refuse, we will not force you—we couldn't, and wouldn't. But understand what refusal means." She counted it off on her fingers, an old teacher's habit, and Theo hated how much it reminded her of home. "The door you came through closes when the last singer dies. Weeks, perhaps. Months if we're lucky. There is no reopening it. You would live out your lives here—in a country of orchards and ghosts, among people who would welcome you, feed you, love you even. It is not a prison. But it is an ending. Everyone you knew would never learn what happened to you. Your valley would forget you inside a year. The Oath of Quiet would see to that."

"The Oath of Quiet," Theo repeated, and heard her own bitterness come back at her in the acoustics of the ruined place. "Of course. My whole life I've been taught to bury the gift. Sworn oaths, elders watching, goats I wasn't allowed to answer. And now the price of going home is the same as the price of staying honest. Either way I lose it."

"No," Alma said sharply, and for a moment she sounded less like a prophet and more like a grandmother scolding at a kitchen table. "Either way you *spend* it. Losing is what happens to you. Spending is what you do. I have had a hundred and twenty years to learn the difference, and I am telling you it matters."

*

Later—after the bowls were cleared, after the elders had drifted off to their separate shadows, after Maren fell asleep against Theo's shoulder mid-sentence and had to be carried to a pallet in the gallery—Theo stood alone at the top of the amphitheater, looking down at the scorched circle.

Jonah found her there. He always did; it was becoming a habit she suspected she'd miss.

"She's right, you know," he said, leaning on the rail beside her. "About spending versus losing. She's been right about things my whole life and I hated it every time."

"Your grandmother faked her death," Theo said. "Let you bury an empty box. Let you grieve for six years. All to courier a book to a stranger's granddaughter."

"To *you*," Jonah said. "Not a stranger's granddaughter. Just—you." He was quiet a moment. "The night I found the book, the ink was still wet on your name. I thought I was losing my mind. I nearly didn't ask you out at all—I kept thinking, what kind of man brings a haunted ledger to a first date? But the dog liked you. And the dog clearly knew more than I did."

Despite everything, Theo laughed—a short, cracked sound that echoed strangely off the stones. Below them, in the dark, one of the elders stirred in her sleep and murmured half a bar of something.

"If I do it," Theo said, "I won't hear them anymore. Any of them. Goats, dogs, hounds, griffins. The whole conversation I've been having since I was four years old, gone. Do you know what that is? That's not a talent. That's been my only friend on a lot of bad nights." She wrapped her arms around herself. "And if I don't do it, everyone in this country—the bell-ringers, the orchard-keepers, Maren, your grandmother—they just... stop. Quietly. Politely. While I walk around a beautiful ghost country eating soup."

Jonah didn't answer right away. When he did, his voice was rough.

"I keep trying to find the version where I offer instead of you," he said. "I've been composing the speech since the citadel. I've got three drafts. One of them has a metaphor about bridges."

"Jonah—"

"But it doesn't work," he went on, stubbornly, hoarsely, "because it has to be a voice the land has never heard, and the land has heard mine since before I was born—it sang to my grandmother while she carried me in her belly, apparently, which I found out tonight and which I am *not* over. So the speech is useless. So all I can do is stand here next to you and refuse to make this easy, and tell you that whatever you choose, I choose the same thing. That's the offer. It's a bad offer. It's the only one I've got."

Theo looked at him—at this strange, charming man who had walked into her carefully silenced life carrying a book that wrote back, and who now stood in a dead country offering to share any fate she picked, as if love were a load-bearing wall.

Below them, Alma Reyes climbed the steps slowly, one hand on the rail, and stopped a few rows down. She didn't come closer. She just looked up at them both, and her face in the starlight was tired beyond measuring, and hopeful beyond decency.

"There's one more thing you should know before you decide," she said. "The final verse begins at dawn tomorrow. Whether or not we have a voice for it. Because the Choir is dying, and I would rather fail singing than succeed in silence." She gripped the rail. "So sleep, if you can. And Theo Dunmore—when the sun comes up over those terraces, the Choir will stand on that stage and open their mouths, and they will sing until they cannot. Whatever you choose, you will have to choose it *then*. In front of everyone. With the whole held breath of a nation waiting on your tongue."

Far below, in the scorched circle, the first gray hint of dawn was already touching the edge of the stage.

It was going to be a very short night.
