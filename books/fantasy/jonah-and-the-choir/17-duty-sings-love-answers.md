## 17. Duty Sings, Love Answers

Jonah had never thought much about his voice before Vessany. It was just a thing he had, like his elbows or his terrible sense of direction. He used it to order coffee, to argue with landlords, to sing badly in the shower with the door locked so no one could testify. In the valley of Merrow's Hollow it had gotten him exactly one compliment in twenty-nine years—a barista who said he had "a radio voice," which he'd privately treasured for a week.

Now he stood in the ruined amphitheater of a country that had sung itself out of existence, and his voice was the only coin he had.

The nine elders sat in a crescent of broken stone seats, wrapped in shawls against the mountain evening. His grandmother sat at the center—Grandmother Reyes, who was supposed to be dead, who had been dead for six years as far as Jonah's whole family knew, and who was currently looking at him with an expression he recognized from every childhood disaster: the patient face she wore when she already knew how the argument would end but was willing to let him have it anyway.

"You're asking her to give up the only part of herself she's ever kept secret," Jonah said. "You're asking her to go home *mute*."

"I am asking nothing," Grandmother Reyes said. "The song asks. There is a difference."

"There really isn't, from where she's standing."

"Jonah." Theo's voice came quiet from beside him. She'd been silent since the revelation—an hour now, maybe more, sitting very still on the cold stone with her hands folded like a woman at a funeral. "It's okay."

"It is not okay. Nothing about this is okay." He turned back to the crescent of elders. "Find another way. You're nine of the greatest singers your country ever produced. You've had a hundred years to workshop this. Surely somewhere in a century of drafts there's a verse that doesn't cost my—" He stopped himself, because he'd almost said *my girlfriend* to a woman he'd met three dates ago, and even in extremis that felt like a lot. "*—the person I came here with*," he finished, with dignity.

One of the elders, a small round woman with laugh lines carved deep as riverbeds, made a sound that might have been a snort. "He negotiates," she said to the others, delighted. "Like a fishmonger."

"He gets it from me," said Grandmother Reyes, and there was pride in it, which somehow made everything worse.

Jonah took a breath and did what he did best, which was commit fully to a bit until it became sincere. "Then let's talk terms. You need a voice freely given, right? A gift the land has never heard?" He spread his arms. "Here I am. I have a voice. The land has never heard it. I checked—I asked the orchards on the way in, they were very clear that no Reyes has ever contributed anything to Vessany except scandal and a treason charge."

"Theo's voice speaks to beasts," said the round elder. "Yours speaks to landlords."

"That's a real skill. Ask anyone I've rented from."

"Jonah." This time it was his grandmother, and the gentleness in it stopped him mid-flourish. She rose from her seat, slowly, one hand pressed to her hip the way she had after the winter she fell on the ice, and came down the steps to stand in front of him. Up close she smelled the same—cedar and black tea—and that nearly undid him more than anything else. Six years dead. Six years of his mother setting out flowers every November. And here she was, warm and tired and real.

"Child," she said, "do you know why the song must be given, not taken?"

"Because magic is dramatic?"

"Because Vessany died of taking." Her voice dropped, and the amphitheater seemed to lean in. "Conquest comes to take land, take language, take children. We could have fought. We would have lost. So instead we sang ourselves into the space between—one great act of giving, the whole country at once, everyone surrendering something they loved so that nothing could be seized. The song holds because it was *given*. If we finish it with a voice that was bargained for, or pressured, or sacrificed by someone else's decision—" she touched his chest, lightly, over his heart, "—then we build the new country on the same theft that killed the old one. It will not hold. It will not hold for a day."

"So Theo has to want it."

"Theo has to *give* it. Freely. Which means you cannot make it easy for her, and you cannot make it hard. You can only love her while she decides." His grandmother smiled, and it was the saddest smile Jonah had ever seen on a living face. "That is the hardest work there is. Harder than singing."

"I'm offering," Jonah said stubbornly. "I'm standing right here offering. That's not making it hard, that's making it *possible*. Maybe the land would rather have mine. Maybe the land is tired of beast-speakers—no offense, Theo—maybe what Vessany actually needs is someone who can talk a miller down from a bad price. Let the land choose. Put us both up there. Auction rules."

The round elder laughed outright. "Auction rules," she repeated, wiping her eye. "Oh, the Choir should have had him a century ago. We'd have been conquered in a week but we'd have died *entertained*."

But Grandmother Reyes didn't laugh. She looked at her grandson for a long moment, and then past him, at Theo, and something passed between the two women that Jonah couldn't read and didn't like.

"The land does choose," she said quietly. "That is what none of you understand yet. The land has been choosing all along. Why do you think the book found its way to *you*, Jonah? Why do you think the door opened at the millrace when you two stood together? Vessany has been reading you both for months." She reached up and cupped his cheek, her thumb rough as bark. "And I am sorry, little one, because I know what you are doing. I know you think if you shout loud enough, you can carry the cost yourself and spare her. My husband tried the same thing, the night before the first singing. He offered his voice too. The Choir refused him, and he sulked for a week, and then he gave something else instead—he gave up his name, so that our son would be born outside the song, safe in another country, carrying nothing but a surname." Her hand fell away. "You are his grandson. The stubbornness is inherited. But the choice is not yours to inherit."

Jonah's throat hurt. "So that's it. We just sit here while she—"

"We sit here while she *decides*," his grandmother corrected. "There is a world of difference, and you will learn it tonight whether you wish to or not."

* * *

They were given a cell in the singers' warren beneath the amphitheater—a low stone room with two pallets, a candle, and a window slit that showed a slice of darkening sky. Theo hadn't spoken since the crescent. She sat on the edge of her pallet, elbows on knees, staring at the candle flame.

Jonah paced. Four steps, turn, four steps. The room was too small for pacing and he did it anyway.

"Say something," he finally said. "Yell at me. Tell me I'm being patronizing. Something."

"What would that change?"

"Nothing! Probably nothing! But silence is worse." He stopped pacing. "Theo. Look, I keep replaying it—the Warden, the offer he made you, all of it. Everyone keeps handing you these impossible choices like you're the only one with anything to lose. And I'm sick of it. I'm sick of watching you get drafted into sacrifices. So yeah, I argued with your grandmother for an hour. Yes, I proposed auctioning my voice to a country. I have no plan and less dignity left. But I needed you to see someone fight for you out loud, in front of witnesses, even if it changed nothing. Especially if it changed nothing."

Theo looked up at him then, and in the candlelight her eyes were wet but steady. "It didn't change nothing," she said softly. "It changed me."

"Well. Good. That was—wait, really?"

"Jonah Reyes." She almost smiled. "You stood in front of nine ancient singers and negotiated with them like a fishmonger. For me. The goats never did that for me. The goats mostly just judge."

"They do seem judgy."

"They're extremely judgy." And then the smile cracked, and she pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes, and her voice came out small and raw. "But Jonah, I don't *want* to give it up. That's the horrible truth nobody wants to hear. Everyone keeps treating me like a saint-in-waiting and I'm not. I love talking to them. The goats, the dog, the hounds—even the griffin, God, especially the griffin. It's the only part of my life that ever felt like *mine*. And tomorrow I'm supposed to hand it over like a coat check ticket, and everyone will call it beautiful, and I'll spend the rest of my life smiling at animals I can't hear, and—" Her breath hitched. "I don't want to be a story. I want to be a person."

Jonah crossed the room and sat down beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched. He didn't say *it will be okay*, because he was done lying for comfort. He didn't say *be brave*, because she was the bravest person he'd ever met and bravery wasn't the problem.

What he said was: "Then don't decide tonight. Decide ugly. Decide angry. Take as long as the song allows. And whatever you choose—give it or keep it, walk through the door or stay—I'll be standing wherever you are, arguing with whoever needs arguing with. That's my whole offer. No poetry. Just stubbornness."

Theo leaned into him, her head finding the hollow of his shoulder like it had always belonged there. "Stubbornness," she repeated. "That's the romantic speech? After everything?"

"I told you. Radio voice, fishmonger soul."

She laughed—wet, cracked, real—and the sound filled the little stone room better than any bell.

Outside, far off across the terraces, a horn sounded once, then again. Then the dogs began to bellow—not hunting-howl, but alarm—and the candle flame bent sideways though no wind came through the slit.

Jonah was on his feet before Theo moved. Down the corridor, running footsteps, and Maren's voice, hoarse with terror: "The Warden! He's found us—he's brought the hounds—all of them—"

Theo stood, and Jonah saw her square her shoulders the way she did before walking into any pasture full of large opinionated animals.

"Hounds," she said quietly. "Hounds I can talk to."

And she went up toward the light, and Jonah—hoarse, terrified, hopelessly hers—went after her.
