## 18. The Warden's Last March

The hounds came at dawn, and Theo heard them coming the way you hear a storm—not as sound exactly, but as a pressure behind the ears, a gathering weight in the air.

She was standing on the highest terrace of the amphitheater, where the stone seats curved around a stage of pale grass, when the first of them crested the eastern ridge. There were nine of them. She knew that without counting, the way she knew her own goats by the shape of their silhouettes against a hillside. Nine hounds, gray as river fog, moving down through the terraced orchards in a loose crescent with the Warden's pale horse at its center.

"They're early," Jonah said beside her. He had his grandmother's book clutched to his chest like a shield, which Theo thought was optimistic. Books were not shields. She'd learned that the hard way, back when a book had dragged her through a door between worlds.

"The sun isn't even properly up," she said.

"Neither are we." He tried for a smile and mostly managed it. "I've never been besieged before. Is there a protocol? Do we write strongly worded letters?"

"Maren would know."

Maren was below, in the amphitheater's heart, helping the nine elders of the Choir arrange themselves on the stage. They moved slowly, these singers who had held one note for a hundred years—old women and old men with hands like paper and eyes like banked coals—and yet when they settled into their places, something in the air changed pitch. The grass seemed to lean toward them. Even the morning birds went quiet out of what Theo could only describe as respect.

Grandmother Reyes stood among them, small and straight-backed, wearing a shawl Maren had knitted her overnight because apparently that was the kind of thing bell-ringers' daughters did when the world was ending: they knit.

Theo looked east again. The hounds were closer now, flowing down the terraces like water finding its level, and the Warden rode behind them on his pale horse, gray cloak stirring in a wind that didn't touch anything else.

*They smell magic,* she thought, and then corrected herself. *No. They smell voices.*

Because that was the truth she'd been circling since the night she'd first heard them baying across an orchard, since the impossible moment they had stopped mid-hunt and turned to her like compass needles finding north. The hounds hunted magic, everyone said so. But Theo had listened to them, really listened, and what she'd heard underneath the snarl wasn't hunger.

It was longing.

* * *

"You should go inside," Grandmother Reyes said, appearing at her elbow with the silent suddenness of very old women everywhere. "The amphitheater has good acoustics but terrible sightlines for spectators."

"I'm not a spectator."

"No," the old woman said, studying her with eyes the same dark brown as Jonah's. "I don't suppose you are." She folded her hands. "My grandson tells me you speak to beasts. He says it the way other men say *she's beautiful*—like it explains everything about him."

"He talks too much."

"He gets it from me." A pause. "The hounds, child. What will you do?"

"I don't know yet." Theo watched the gray shapes pour through the last orchard row. "When I talked to them before—the second time, after Maren—they didn't obey me. I asked them to hunt and they just... looked at me. Like I'd asked them to do something embarrassing at a dinner party."

"And what did you ask them this time? In your head, I mean. You're asking right now; don't look so surprised, I raised a boy who hums when he lies."

Theo flushed. It was true. Ever since the ridge, some part of her had been reaching toward the hounds, the way you press your tongue against a sore tooth. *What do you want?* she'd been asking, over and over, into the gray morning. *What do you actually want?*

"They haven't answered," she said.

"They will." Grandmother Reyes patted her arm once, briskly, and descended toward the stage. "Everything answers eventually. That's rather the whole point of Vessany."

* * *

The Choir began to sing at full sunrise.

It was not, Theo realized within the first minute, a song in any sense she understood. There were no words. There was barely melody. It was more like nine people breathing together in perfect agreement—a long, low tone that started below hearing and climbed slowly, patiently, like water rising in a lock. The air itself thickened. The pale grass on the stage began to shimmer.

And Vessany began to wake up.

Theo felt it in her teeth first, then her chest: the country remembering itself. The empty bells in the far-off towns gave a single ghost-note each, though no wind touched them. Somewhere beyond the terraces, an orchard that had stood fruitless for a century put out blossom all at once, a white detonation of flowers. The door home—the millrace door, the way back to Merrow's Hollow and goats and Oaths of Quiet and a world that had never heard of any of this—trembled open at the edge of her senses like an eye unclenching.

*That's it,* she thought. *That's the sound that erased a country. And they're singing it backward.*

"It's working," Jonah breathed. He had come to stand beside her on the high terrace, and his face was wet without his seeming to notice. "Theo, it's actually—"

Then the hounds howled.

It rolled up the terraces like a landslide given voice, nine throats in horrible harmony, and Theo flinched—but even as she flinched, she *heard*, truly heard, what she'd been straining toward all morning. Underneath the hunting-cry, under the command-smell of the Warden's will laid over theirs like a hand pressing down on nine necks—

Grief. Longing. A want so old and so starved it had curdled into ferocity.

"They don't want to kill us," she said aloud, astonished at her own certainty. "Jonah. Jonah, listen. They want—"

The Warden rode into the amphitheater.

He came through the western arch alone, having left the hounds fanned out along the rim of the bowl, and he sat his pale horse in the center of the stage with the unhurried confidence of a man who had done this many times and been interrupted none of them. Up close he was less a figure than a absence—a gray man-shaped quiet, his face smooth as an unfired pot, his throat wrapped in old scar tissue that caught the light wrong.

The Choir's note wavered. Held.

"Warden," said Grandmother Reyes, from the front row of singers. Her voice was dry as autumn leaves. "You're late. You missed a hundred years."

"I missed nothing," the Warden said. His voice was gravel poured over glass. "I have kept the silence you bought. I have kept it perfectly. And now you propose to break it—for what? So the conquerors can come back? So the maps can find us again?" He turned his smooth face across the rows of singers. "I was a singer. I know the cost of the song better than any of you. I paid it first."

"You paid nothing," said an old man three seats down, trembling with rage or grief or both. "You *took*. When the verse demanded your voice, you fled, and the silence took your throat as forfeit, and you have spent a century making certain no one else got to choose differently."

"I spent a century keeping us *safe*."

"You spent a century keeping us *empty*."

The note wavered again. Below it, faintly, Theo could hear the hounds shifting along the rim of the bowl, restless as weather.

Jonah stepped forward. "There's another way," he called out, hoarse from yesterday's arguing, which had gone on until dawn and accomplished nothing except proving that he loved her, loudly, in front of nine immortals. "The verse needs a voice freely given. Mine's right here. Take mine."

"We have discussed this," Grandmother Reyes said, with the weary finality of a woman who had been discussing it for approximately eleven hours.

"Then let's discuss it again—"

"*Jonah.*"

But the Warden had gone very still on his horse. And then, slowly, he laughed—or made the sound that a laugh makes when it has to climb over ruined throat to get out, a grinding, awful almost-music.

"A voice freely given," he rasped. "Yes. That is the flaw in my design, isn't it. That is why the silence always frays at the edges. Because the song only takes what is *offered*." His blank face turned, and found Theo on the high terrace. Found her and stayed. "So the land has already chosen its price. The beast-speaker. Of course. The valley that bred her sent her here the way a body sends blood to a wound."

Every eye in the amphitheater followed his.

Theo's mouth went dry. She had known this moment was coming—had felt it approaching for days, patient as frost—but knowing did nothing against the actual weight of nine hundred years of a country's hope settling onto her shoulders.

"I haven't decided," she said. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted.

"You will." The Warden dismounted. He moved like a man assembled from hinges. "Or you won't, and I will finish what I came to finish, and the silence will hold another century, and another, until there is no one left who remembers wanting anything else. That is mercy too, girl. The kindest kind. The kind that outlasts the wish to be anything at all."

He raised one gray hand.

Along the rim of the bowl, nine pairs of hound-eyes ignited like coals blown alight.

* * *

Later, Theo would try to explain what happened next, and she would fail, because there was no explaining it—only the fact of it, plain as bread.

The Warden pointed at the Choir, and the hounds came down into the amphitheater like a gray flood, and Theo Dunmore walked off the high terrace, down the stone steps, out onto the pale grass, and stood directly between them.

"Theo!" Jonah's shout cracked across the bowl.

She didn't stop. Her heart was slamming against her ribs hard enough to bruise, and every sensible cell in her body was screaming about teeth, and she kept walking, because she had spent her whole life hiding from exactly this—from being seen, being known, being *loud*—and she was done. Done with the Oath of Quiet. Done with smallness. If she died on a stage in a country that didn't exist, she was going to die having asked one honest question out loud.

The lead hound reached her in four strides.

It was enormous up close—shoulder-high, gray as ash, breath like winter—and it skidded to a halt so suddenly that the two behind it piled into its flanks. Its lips peeled back from teeth the length of her fingers. Its coal-bright eyes fixed on hers.

And Theo opened her mouth, and spoke—not in words, not in the language of the valley, but in the other voice, the buried one, the gift she had fed in secret her whole life. The voice that was not human and not animal but the narrow bridge between.

"*What do you want?*"

The amphitheater went silent. Even the Choir's great note bent around her question like a river around a stone.

The hound stared at her. Its ears, which she had never once seen move, moved—tilting forward, hungry, *listening*.

And then, in a chorus of growl and whine and the subsonic rumble that hounds used when they spoke to each other in the dark, all nine of them answered at once:

*"To sing."*

Theo's knees nearly buckled. Not fear. Something worse than fear—recognition.

"You've been listening to the song for a hundred years," she whispered, translating as she went, her human voice stumbling after her true one. "From outside. From the edges. Every time the door opened, every time the note leaked through—you heard it. And you were made to hunt it. Made to smother it. Your whole purpose is the opposite of the thing you—" She stopped. Started again. "You were built from the silence, weren't you? All of you. The Warden couldn't sing, so he made hunters out of the not-singing. And he never once asked you what you—"

"*We asked him*," said the lead hound, and its voice broke on the word, actually broke, splintering into harmonics that no hunting beast should own. "*In the beginning. Before we were only teeth. We asked to join the verse and he said the verse was finished, the verse was dangerous, the verse was death—and we believed him, because he was our singer, our broken singer, and who else did we have?*"

On the stage, the Warden had gone rigid. "Hounds," he said, and his gravel voice had a crack running through it now, hairline but spreading. "*Heel.*"

Nine hounds did not heel.

Nine hounds sat down. All at once, in a perfect ring around Theo, folding their gray bodies onto the pale grass like congregants taking pews, and lifted their heads, and opened their mouths—and waited. Not to bite. To *be sung to*.

"Oh," said Grandmother Reyes softly, from the front row. "Oh, you dear, dreadful creatures."

"They want to join the verse," Theo said. Her voice shook. She let it. "They've always wanted to join the verse. Warden—your weapon is asking for asylum."

"That is not possible." But the crack in his voice had widened. "They are constructs. Silence given shape. They cannot—"

"They can," said the oldest of the Choir, a woman whose hands shook so badly she held her own wrists to steady them. "Anything made of silence remembers the sound it was cut from, Warden. You would know. You are made of it too."

The Warden stood in the center of his own defeat, and for a long moment the only sound in the amphitheater was the Choir's patient rising note and the ragged, hopeful breathing of nine hounds.

Then he reached for the gray cloak at his throat, and Theo saw—everyone saw—that beneath the wrap of scar tissue, something was glowing. Faint. Flickering. Like a coal that had been buried for a hundred years under ash and had never, quite, gone out.

"One note," the Warden rasped. It was not clear whether he was speaking to them or to himself. "If I remember one note. If the silence lets me keep even one—"

"Then you'll have to fight us for the rest," said Grandmother Reyes, and held out her hand.

* * *

And that was when the sky tore.

Not the door—that was still opening gently at the edge of everything, patient as sunrise. This was different. This was a sound like the world's largest sheet of paper being ripped lengthwise, and through the rip poured cold, and through the cold poured *hunger*—the thing on the other side of the silence, the conquerors the Choir had fled, arriving at last at the country that had hidden from them for a century, drawn like sharks to the one sound they had waited a hundred years to hear.

The Choir's note faltered.

The hounds rose as one, hackles up, turning toward the tear.

And Theo felt her gift stir in her chest—her spent, promised, not-yet-given gift—and understood, with terrible clarity, that the verse was not finished demanding things.

It was only getting started.
