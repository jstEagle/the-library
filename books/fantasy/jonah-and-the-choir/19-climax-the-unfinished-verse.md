## 19. Climax: The Unfinished Verse

**Theo**

The Warden came at dawn, and the dawn came up gray as his coat.

Theo stood on the highest terrace of the amphitheater with the morning cold biting through her sleeves, and she counted what they had against what was coming. What they had: nine old singers, one hoarse boy who would not stop arguing, a griffin with an opinion about everything, and her. What was coming: the pale horse, the gray figure, and every hound in Vessany moving like a tide through the orchard rows below.

"He's early," Maren said beside her. Her wrists were still ringed with bruises from the bell that swallowed sound, and she kept touching them the way you touch a bruise—half to hurt it, half to prove it's yours.

"Of course he's early," Theo said. "He's been waiting a hundred years to be late for something."

Below them, in the bowl of the ruined amphitheater, the Choir had arranged themselves on the broken tiers like nine stones that had decided, after long deliberation, to be people. Grandmother Reyes sat among them as if she'd never sat anywhere else. She had braided her white hair back off her face, and her hands were folded, and she looked—Theo thought—not like a woman about to attempt the impossible, but like a woman about to do the dishes. There is a particular courage that looks exactly like that.

Jonah came up the steps two at a time, which he did whenever he was frightened, because walking slowly gave fear too much room. He stopped next to Theo and did not take her hand, which meant he was very frightened indeed, because taking her hand was the first thing he'd ever done right.

"The hounds," he said. "Are they still—"

"They're mine." Theo said it quietly, testing the truth of it again, the way you test ice. "They've been mine since the terraces. But they're *his* too. That's the problem. You can't order someone to want two things."

"You could try," Jonah said. "I hear you're good at wanting."

She elbowed him. It was important, right then, to elbow someone. The alternative was to look down at the orchards and let the size of the morning swallow her.

The hounds came up through the terraces in silence, which was the worst thing about them. Real dogs announce themselves; these moved like weather. They poured along the stone walls and pooled at the amphitheater's rim, dozens of them, gray-eyed and wrong-jawed, and above them all rode the Warden on his pale horse, and the horse's breath made no fog in the cold air, which told Theo everything about how long ago it had stopped being entirely alive.

The Warden looked up at the tiers. At the singers. At Jonah. Last of all—at her—and there was something in his face that Theo had spent chapters of her life learning to recognize, because she wore it herself most mornings: the look of a person standing guard over their own grave.

"Singers," the Warden said. His voice was ash and gravel, a voice that had been taken apart and reassembled by someone in a hurry. "You know the law. Nothing sung may be unsung. Nothing erased may return. I am the last verse of Vessany, and my verse is *quiet*."

Grandmother Reyes rose from her tier. She was small; from where Theo stood she looked like a child standing up in church.

"Amadeo," she said.

The name landed on the amphitheater floor like a dropped glass. Even the hounds flinched.

"You remember your name," Grandmother Reyes went on, gentle as anything, "because we sang it into the erasure with everything else. We could not bear to lose *you*, dear heart. So we hid you inside the quiet and called it duty." She spread her hands. "We were cowards, Amadeo. We saved our country by making you carry its silence. And now you've carried it so long you think the silence is yours."

"Don't," the Warden said.

"We're going to sing," she said. "And you are going to remember one note. Just one. It's been in your throat this whole time, sleeping. I put it there myself, the night before we began, so that somewhere in Vessany there would always be one voice that couldn't be taken—only forgotten."

"I said *don't*—"

"Sing," said Grandmother Reyes, to her Choir, in the tone of a woman saying grace.

* * *

**Jonah**

Later—much later, in another country, on ordinary mornings—Jonah would try to describe the final verse of Vessany, and he would fail, and he would forgive himself for failing, because some things are doors and not windows. You can't show a person through a wall.

But he remembered the beginning perfectly. He remembered that it started with his grandmother humming.

That was all. A hum, low and unhurried, the kind of sound a grandmother makes over a stove. Then the singer beside her caught it and turned it into a note, and the note became a line, and the line became the verse that had hung unfinished for a hundred years—the verse they had stopped singing mid-breath when the conquest armies crossed the border, the verse that had held an entire country in suspension like a note held under water.

*Vessany, Vessany, seed under snow—*

The amphitheater stones woke first. Jonah felt it through his boots, a shiver like a horse shaking off rain. Moss that had been gray went green. Somewhere below, in the dark under the tiers, a spring that had been dry made a sound like a throat clearing.

*Sleeping, sleeping, waiting to grow—*

The hounds surged forward.

They came over the rim in a gray wave, and Theo stepped off the terrace edge without hesitating—which Jonah would have nightmares about later, the sheer casualness of it—and stood on the lowest tier directly in their path, a small woman in a borrowed coat with her arms slightly out from her sides, and she spoke to them.

Not in words. Jonah heard nothing, or almost nothing—a pressure, a shape, the way you can feel music through a wall. But the hounds heard it. The whole gray wave broke around her like water around a post, and then, horribly and wonderfully, they *sat*. Dozens of them, sitting in the dust of a country that didn't exist, looking up at her with their wrong-jawed faces, waiting.

"What did you say?" Jonah called down, because he couldn't help himself.

"They asked me what happens to them," Theo called back, "if Vessany comes back. Nobody ever asks the dogs." Her voice cracked in the middle and she kept going anyway. "I told them the truth. I don't know. I told them they get to find out."

*Roots in the dark and a heart below—*

The Warden spurred his horse forward, and the horse—for the first time in a century, Jonah was sure of it—hesitated. It planted its feet. It looked at the singing tiers, and then at the hounds sitting in the dust like a congregation, and it made a sound that was not quite a whinny and not quite a sob.

"Stop them," the Warden said. "Hounds. *Stop them.*"

The hounds did not move. One of them, the largest, a female with frost-colored eyes, leaned her head against Theo's knee.

"She says," Theo announced, loudly, to everyone, "that you should come down and listen. She says you've been so quiet for so long that you've forgotten quiet isn't the same as peace." She paused, listening to something Jonah couldn't hear. "She says—" and here Theo's voice did break, fully, and she let it, "—she says she remembers being a real dog. Before. She remembers grass."

The Warden raised his hand, and light gathered in it, gray light, the color of erased things, and Jonah understood with terrible clarity that the man was going to unmake the hounds rather than let them choose.

"No!" Jonah shouted, uselessly, humanly, from the top of the stairs—

* * *

**Theo**

—and Theo, who had spent her whole life being quiet at the exact moments it mattered, opened her mouth and did the thing she had never once done in front of another human being.

She sang.

It was not a beautiful sound. Beast-speech was never beautiful; it was true, which is different and better. It came out of her as a rising, layered cry, goat-song and dog-song and the deep bell-note of the griffin behind her, and it wove itself into the Choir's verse the way a river weaves into the sea—not joining it, becoming it.

*Water remembers. Stone remembers. Every voice the valley buried—*

The amphitheater answered. That was the only word for it. The stones themselves took up the verse, a hum in the masonry, and the terraced orchards beyond took it from the stones, and the whole hidden country began to sound like a struck bell that had been waiting a hundred years for permission to finish ringing.

The Warden's hand shook. The gray light guttered.

"Come down," Theo said to him, out loud, in her own cracked human voice, because some things must be said in the plain tongue. "Amadeo. Come down and listen. Your grandmother left you a note in your own throat. Open it."

"I have nothing," the Warden said, and his voice broke apart on the word *nothing*, and underneath the breaking, faint as a coin at the bottom of a well, Theo heard it.

One note.

It was small and it was rough and it was the most beautiful thing she had ever heard, because it was the sound of a hundred years of silence ending. The Warden's mouth opened and the note came out of him whether he willed it or not—one raw, trembling, human tone—and the gray light in his hand went out like a candle pinched between wet fingers, and the pale horse beneath him lowered its head and wept, actually wept, great silent horse-tears into the dust.

The Choir caught his note and folded it into the verse.

*Every voice the valley buried—*

Theo threw her head back and gave them everything. The goats of Merrow's Hollow, whose names she had traded and guarded and loved. The dog who had warned her about the paper man. The griffin, wheeling overhead now, screaming joy in a register that cracked two roof tiles clean off the old citadel. Every conversation she had ever hidden, every secret she had ever swallowed, all of it pouring out of her at last into a song that had room for exactly that—all of it, everyone, everything suppressed sounding at once.

*Rise, rise, rise.*

* * *

**Jonah**

The world blazed.

There was no gentler way to put it, and Jonah had never been interested in gentle ways when true ones existed. Light came up out of the ground itself, gold and green, and ran along the terraces like fire running along a fuse, and everywhere it touched, Vessany remembered itself.

Orchards that had been gray sketches of trees filled in—bark, leaf, blossom, fruit—in a single rushing breath, until the hillsides bent under apples and plums and things Jonah had no names for, heavy branches groaning with a century of stored growing. Wells filled. Roads uncracked. Far away, across the valleys, he saw towers stand up straight and windows catch the sun, thousands of them, a whole country exhaling.

And the bells rang.

Every bell in Vessany, all at once, with no hands on them—the empty bells of empty steeples, the bells in the deep of the citadel, the little cracked bell in Maren's kitchen that had hung silent since 1904—they all rang together, and the sound was not noise. It was the verse, finished. The song and the ringing were the same thing, and the country sang itself back into existence with its own bronze voice, and Jonah stood on the amphitheater steps and cried like a child and did not care who saw.

Beside him, Maren was laughing and sobbing and holding her father's old bell-rope, which she had carried wrapped around her waist for years without quite admitting why.

"It's rung," she kept saying. "It's rung. It's *rung*."

The Warden—Amadeo, he was Amadeo now, there was no putting that name back in the box—had slid from his horse. He knelt in the dust with both hands pressed over his mouth, and the hounds crowded around him, and instead of guarding him they leaned on him, all of them at once, a gray pile of forgiveness, and through his fingers Jonah could see that the man was making sounds. Small ones. Broken ones. Practicing.

The door tore open at the top of the amphitheater, where no wall was.

It did not open so much as *unhappen*—the air splitting along a seam that had always been there, spilling through it the smell of cut hay and diesel and the specific damp of a millrace in Merrow's Hollow, and beyond it, impossibly ordinary, the green banks of home.

Grandmother Reyes turned around. She looked at the door. She looked at Jonah.

"Well," she said, over the bells. "Don't just stand there, sweetheart. Someone has to carry the seeds."

Theo reached for Jonah's hand, and this time he got there first.

* * *

**Theo**

The last thing Theo heard in Vessany was the smallest voice in the whole ringing country.

The frost-eyed hound pressed her forehead against Theo's palm one more time, and Theo listened, really listened, one last time, and what the hound said was not goodbye. It was:

*Tell them about us. Where you're going. Tell them dogs can be songs too.*

And then the door took them, bells roaring behind, orchards blazing, a reborn country shouting its first morning into the sky—

—and the seam closed, and Merrow's Hollow was suddenly, overwhelmingly quiet.

Just the millrace. Just the birds. Just Jonah beside her, breathing hard, holding a leather book that was, at that very moment, rewriting its final page in fresh ink.

Theo opened her mouth to say something—to say *anything*, she had a century of a country to tell him about—and no sound came out.

The silence was total. And it was clean. And it was hers.

Jonah looked at her, stricken, and she smiled, and pointed at the book in his hands, and raised her eyebrows.

Because the book was writing. Fast. And the ink was not hers, and not his grandmother's, and not the Warden's.

It was a new hand entirely—round, careful, hopeful—and the first legible line read:

*Chapter One: In which a country learns to write itself down.*
