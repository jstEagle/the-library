## 20. What Was Given

Theo woke to silence.

Not the ordinary silence of early morning—goats shifting in their pens, wind combing the orchard terraces, the small negotiations of birds overhead. She had lived her whole life inside that noise, translating it, answering it, hiding it. This was different. This was a silence with walls and a floor and a roof over it, total and clean, like snow.

She lay on her back in the grass of the amphitheater and listened to nothing.

The bells were ringing. She could see them doing it—Maren's great bronze bell swinging in its frame at the edge of the ruined tiers, the smaller bells down in the town catching fire one after another like a sentence being read aloud—but they made no sound she could hear. Or rather, they made sound; she simply had no way in. It was like watching someone speak through glass. The whole reborn country was talking, and Theo Dunmore stood outside the conversation for the first time since she was four years old.

She should have wept. She kept waiting for it, the way you wait for a sneeze. Instead what came was something stranger: lightness. Her chest felt scoured. For twenty-six years she had carried two lives in one skull—the girl who said *good morning* to her mother, and the girl who said *the east fence is broken, mind the third goat, she's been lying about her limp*—and now there was only the one life left, and it fit her like a coat cut to her actual shoulders.

*You gave it away,* she reminded herself, testing the thought for regret the way you press a bruise. *Freely. On purpose.*

The bruise didn't hurt. That was the miracle of it. Somewhere in the last hour of the song, when the Warden's hounds had thrown back their heads and the old singers' voices had cracked and Jonah's grandmother had held the final note so long the air itself seemed to be straining, Theo had opened her hands around her gift and let the land take it—and the land had taken it gently, the way you'd take a bird from a child's cupped palms, and given back bells.

"Hey." Jonah's shadow fell across her. He was filthy, hoarse, half-carrying his grandmother's shawl over one arm like a trophy he wasn't sure he'd earned. He lowered himself into the grass beside her with the graceless flop of a man whose legs had finally filed a formal complaint. "You're awake. How do you feel?"

Theo opened her mouth. Closed it. Opened it again and produced—nothing. Not even a rasp. The silence where her voice should have been was so complete it was almost polite.

Jonah's face did something complicated. "Right," he said softly. "I keep forgetting. I'm sorry—I keep forgetting, and then I remember, and it's—" He stopped, dragged a hand through his hair. "I argued with nine elders until I was hoarse trying to stop this, and it worked out exactly the way they said it would, and I don't know whether to be furious or grateful."

Theo reached over and patted his knee. Then, because she had spent a lifetime communicating without words and was very good at it, she pointed at him, then at the ground, then made an exaggerated face of awe, then pointed at the ringing bell, then shrugged with her whole body, palms up, as if to say: *look what happened while we weren't looking.*

"You're going to be insufferable at charades," Jonah said, and laughed, and the laugh cracked in the middle, and then he was crying, quietly, the way men cry when they've been brave in public all day and have finally found a patch of grass where nobody needs them to be anything. Theo put her arm around him and let him lean. It was, she reflected, the first fully honest conversation they'd ever had, and neither of them had said a word.

Above them, Vessany went on existing.

It was doing it loudly, from what she could gather by sight alone. The orchards on their terraces had gone heavy overnight—oranges and quinces and things she had no name for hanging so thick the branches bowed. Smoke rose from chimneys that had been cold for a century. People were in the streets below the amphitheater—not many, not yet, the singers and the freed prisoners and Maren's neighbors from the bell-house, but enough that the lanes had the feel of a place rather than a memory. Doors stood open. Nobody was checking the horizon.

And in the middle of it all, on his knees in the trampled grass before the amphitheater steps, was the Warden.

* * *

Theo saw him properly for the first time when they came down the tiers an hour later.

He had not moved far. He knelt where the battle had ended, gray coat gray with dust, pale horse standing loose-reined behind him like a servant who no longer knew his duties. His hounds sat in a ring around him—not guarding him, Theo understood now, but *with* him, the way family sits with you at a bedside. They had chosen. All of them had chosen, and the choosing had unmade something in the man, or perhaps finally finished making it.

His head was bowed. When the singers passed him—and they did pass him, deliberately, Grandmother Reyes at their center walking with a cane and an expression Theo could only describe as *accounted for*—he did not look up. But his shoulders shook.

"He can't believe it either," Jonah murmured. "A hundred years he kept us erased. Kept himself erased. And now the country came back anyway, and it didn't even need his permission."

Grandmother Reyes stopped in front of him. Up close, Theo could see what the battle had hidden: the Warden was not old the way the singers were old. He was old the way a locked room is old—preserved, airless, untouched by any year since 1904. His face under the gray hood was young and terrible and exhausted, a face that had been holding its breath for a century.

"Sera," Grandmother Reyes said. She used a name, which meant once someone had loved him enough to give him one. "Look at me."

Slowly, joints cracking like green wood, the Warden raised his head.

"I sang with you," Grandmother Reyes said. "Third verse, second harmony. You had the loveliest low line in the Choir. When they took your voice for the warding, I marched on the Council myself. I was told it was necessary." Her voice frayed. "I believed them. That is my shame, Sera, and I am saying it in front of the whole country because you deserve to hear it said."

The Warden's mouth worked. What came out was not words. It was a single note—a cracked, rusted, wobbling note, the ghost of a bass line, more breath than music—but it rose, and it held, and every bell in Vessany caught it and rang it back to him until the air trembled with it.

Then he bent forward and pressed his forehead to the ground of the country he had spent a hundred years keeping dead, and stayed there, and his hounds pressed their noses against his shoulders, and no one moved to help him up, because some kneelings are not meant to be interrupted.

Theo watched until her eyes blurred. She had hated him. She wanted to go on hating him—it would have been simpler, and she had earned her simplicity. But she had heard the hounds, back on the terraces, when she'd asked them what they wanted. *We want to sing too.* He had built his whole self out of that same hunger, starved past all recognition. He was what happened to a singer when you took the song away and left the duty. He was a warning written in a person instead of a book.

She walked over. Jonah made a small alarmed noise. She ignored him.

Up close, the Warden smelled of dust and horse and, faintly, incredibly, of beeswax—old candle smoke, the smell of a chapel. Theo crouched down in front of him, the way you'd crouch for a frightened animal, which was after all her native language even if the words had been taken out of it. She put her hand flat on the grass between them.

Then she waited.

The nearest hound lifted its head. It looked at her—she looked back—and though no voice crossed between them anymore, something else did. Recognition, maybe. Gratitude. The wordless understanding of two creatures who had both been used as tools and had both, in the end, refused. The hound leaned its great head against her knee. Its weight was enormous and warm and absolutely trusting.

Behind her, she heard Jonah say, wetly, "She always does this. Don't ask. Just accept it."

When Theo finally stood, the Warden was looking at her. His eyes were red-rimmed and human. He touched two fingers to his own throat, then to the ground, then to her—an odd little grammar, but she read it easily enough. *My voice bought this silence. Yours paid for the bells. We are the same ledger.*

"No," Theo said—or tried to say, and got nothing, and so instead she shook her head firmly and tapped her own chest. *Different.* Because it was true. He had lost his voice to a bargain struck over his head. She had given hers with both hands, eyes open, to a country that asked. The difference was everything. The difference was the whole difference between a cage and a door.

The Warden studied her for a long moment. Then, slowly, he nodded—the first nod anyone had seen him give that wasn't an order—and turned his face back to the earth, and let the bells wash over him.

* * *

They fed her that evening at a long table in the bell-house yard, and Theo discovered that losing one sense sharpens the others in ways no philosopher warns you about.

She couldn't hear the laughter, but she could see it—Maren's head thrown back, the singers' shoulders shaking, Jonah snorting wine through his nose at something his grandmother had said. She couldn't hear the toast, but she watched forty cups rise together and understood it perfectly. She ate bread still warm from an oven that had been cold since before her grandmother was born, and tasted it more completely than she had ever tasted anything, because there was nothing else in her head. No goat commentary. No dog weather reports. No second channel running underneath everything, whispering the secret lives of beasts. Just bread, and firelight, and people.

Maren sat down beside her partway through the meal and put a plate of honeyed figs in front of her with the firmness of a woman who would brook no refusal. "You," Maren announced, "are going to learn to write very fast, because I have questions. Forty years of questions and now suddenly everyone's alive and nobody has time for me, so you're it. My question-asker. Starting tomorrow at dawn."

Theo raised an eyebrow.

"Yes, dawn. Bell-ringers keep dawn hours, it's in the job description, which I now officially have." Maren tore her bread in half and pushed the bigger piece onto Theo's plate without appearing to notice she'd done it. "Also I've decided you're staying at least through the planting. Grandmother Reyes agrees. She says the land likes you, whatever that means, and honestly after everything I've decided not to question the old woman anymore, it's exhausting."

Theo smiled. It felt strange on her face—not the guarded smile she wore in Merrow's Hollow, the one that said *nothing to see here*, but a full, unguarded, slightly crooked smile that arrived without permission. She pointed at herself, then at the ground, then mimed digging, then shrugged: *we'll see.*

"'We'll see,'" Maren translated instantly. "That's what my father used to say too. Fine. We'll see." But she was smiling too, and she refilled Theo's cup, and the two of them sat shoulder to shoulder in the noise they could only half share, and it was enough. More than enough.

Later, when the fire had burned down to its red arithmetic, Jonah found her at the yard wall looking out over the dark terraces. He handed her a scrap of paper and the stub of a pencil without a word.

*Well?* she wrote.

"Well," he said, and leaned on the wall next to her, close enough that their elbows touched. "Grandmother wants to talk to us tomorrow. Both of us. She says—" he exhaled— "she says the song isn't entirely finished. Something about the verse needing to be *carried*, not just sung. She wouldn't explain. She said we'd understand better if we slept on it, which is exactly the kind of thing a woman who faked her own death for a century would say."

Theo wrote: *Your grandmother is enjoying this.*

"She's enjoying it immensely. A hundred years of solemnity and now she gets to be mysterious again." He was quiet a moment. Below them, somewhere in the dark orchards, something moved—deer, maybe, or the wild goats of the high terraces—and Theo felt the old reflex rise, the tilt of the head, the listening. Nothing came. Just night, and leaves, and her own heartbeat.

"It doesn't hurt?" Jonah asked carefully. "The quiet?"

Theo considered. She took the pencil and wrote slowly, wanting to get it right:

*It hurts like a scar hurts. Only when I press on it. And I keep pressing on it to check, which I should stop doing.*

Jonah read it twice. "Yeah," he said. "Okay. That's honest, at least." He hesitated, then added, quieter: "For what it's worth—I heard the hounds singing today. After. When the country came back. Real singing, Theo. Terrible, off-key, wonderful singing. And I thought—if that's what your gift turned into, then it didn't die. It just changed shape. Like everything else here."

Theo looked at the paper in her hand. Then she folded it carefully and put it in her pocket, because she suspected she would want to reread it on bad nights, and there would be bad nights, and honesty was easier to carry in writing than in memory.

Above them, Maren's bell rang the hour—and for the first time, Theo didn't just see it. She felt it. The bronze voice came up through the wall, through the stones, through the soles of her feet and the bones of her spine, a great slow pulse rolling through the whole country like a heart deciding to keep beating. The land itself was speaking now. It had plenty of voices. It had never needed hers the way she'd feared, and it hadn't needed it the way she'd hoped, either—it had needed it exactly the way it had gotten it: freely, once, completely.

She pressed her palm flat against the wall and let the bell ring through her, and mourned, and was at peace, and both of those things were true at once, and she was learning that this was allowed.

Tomorrow, Grandmother Reyes would tell them about the unfinished verse. Tomorrow there would be questions about doors—about one door in particular, a backward millrace in a valley that forgot, and what it meant to leave a way open. But tonight there was only the bell, and the warmth of Jonah's elbow against hers, and the long clean silence where a hundred small voices used to live.

Theo closed her eyes and listened to nothing, and for the first time in her life, nothing sounded like home.
