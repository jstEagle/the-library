## 21. The Door Stays Open

The voting took three days, which everyone agreed was remarkably fast for a country that had spent a century unable to vote on anything at all.

They gathered in the amphitheater, because the amphitheater had become the place where Vessany did its thinking. Nine elders sat in the front row like a panel of skeptical owls. Behind them, several hundred returned citizens—some aged, some restored to the ages they'd been when the song paused mid-note, all of them blinking at sunlight as if it were a rumor they'd only half believed—filled the stone seats. Theo and Jonah sat near the top, where they could see everything and, more importantly, where nobody could ask them anything without climbing.

The question on the table was simple: what to do with the door.

"Seal it," said an orchard-keeper named Bram, whose family had been mid-harvest in 1904 and had apparently never forgiven the interruption. "We sang ourselves out of the world once. We can do it again. The world outside forgot us once; it will forget us again."

"The world outside," said Maren, "has my mother's grave in it. And a tavern with terrible stew that I have developed opinions about."

A murmur of laughter ran through the seats. Theo felt Jonah relax beside her by a single degree, which for Jonah was the equivalent of most people weeping with relief.

It went back and forth like that all morning. Seal it, open it, seal it, open it. An old woman stood up and said that doors were how the Warden had gotten in, a hundred years ago, and half the crowd hissed before remembering that the Warden was currently kneeling in the east field planting turnips, which was somehow both justice and the strangest thing any of them had ever seen.

Then Grandmother Reyes stood up.

Theo had been trying, for two weeks now, to think of her as anything other than terrifying. It wasn't working. Ilsa Reyes was small and round-shouldered and had flour permanently under her fingernails from baking bread for half the valley, and when she looked at you, you felt audited.

"I wrote the draft," she said, "in which we sealed every door." Her voice carried without effort; a century of singing will do that. "I also wrote the draft in which we opened all of them. And the one where Jonah dies, which I am pleased to report he has been ignoring." She looked up at them in the high seats, and her eyes crinkled. "My grandson has always been bad at following instructions. It's the best thing about him."

"Mamá," Jonah said, mortified.

"So I will not tell you which door to choose," she went on. "I will only say this: a song held forever is not a song. It is a held breath. And I—" here her voice did something complicated, and the whole amphitheater leaned forward— "I have been holding my breath for a hundred and twenty years. I would like very much to exhale. But I would like to do it *toward* something. Not into a locked room."

She sat down. The silence lasted long enough that Theo heard a bee working the rosemary along the amphitheater's edge, and then someone in the middle rows began, quietly, to hum—and the hum spread, seat to seat, until the whole of Vessany was humming the first bar of the rebirth-song, the one they'd finished with Theo's voice braided through it, and when the humming finally settled, the vote was taken by raised hands and there was no real doubt left at all.

One door. Ajar. Never wide, never barred. A door you could knock on, and be answered.

* * *

The door itself was the old millrace door, naturally—the first one, the one that had pulled them through with backward water and a book's worth of insistence. Now it stood in a frame of new-cut timber, set into the mill wall, and it did not look like much: a plain wooden door with an iron latch, propped open exactly the width of a determined cat.

"It should be wider," Bram grumbled, supervising the carpenters. "What if someone important needs to come through?"

"Then they can be less important," said Maren, "or thinner."

There was a ceremony, of course. Vessany had discovered ceremonies the way a starving man discovers bread. There was singing, and there was a ribbon, and there was a speech by the eldest singer that went on long enough that Theo caught Jonah checking whether the door would stay ajar if he leaned on it. (It would. They tested. He got elbowed.)

But the moment Theo remembered afterward—the moment she knew she'd carry home, folded flat against her heart like a pressed flower—came after the crowds had gone. Grandmother Reyes stood alone at the threshold, one hand on the frame, looking out through the gap at the millrace and the valley beyond: Merrow's Hollow, green and ordinary and utterly unaware.

"You could come with us," Jonah said softly. "Both of us want you to. You know that."

"I know it." She smiled. "And I love you for offering, and I am going to say no, and you are going to pretend you expected it, because you are your grandfather's grandson and he was a terrible liar too."

"Why stay?" Theo asked. "After everything—you waited so long to come back to the world—"

"I didn't wait to come back to the world, child." Grandmother Reyes turned and looked back over her shoulder at Vessany: the terraces going gold with late fruit, the bells—Maren's bells—catching the light. "I waited to come back to *this*. I helped sing a country out of existence to save it. Do you know what it does to a person, to spend a century wondering if you saved something or just buried it? Well." Her mouth twitched. "You know exactly what it does. You gave your gift to find out."

Theo's throat tightened. Even now, weeks later, the silence where the beast-speech had been was a strange clean space, like a room after the furniture's gone. She still turned her head sometimes when goats bleated, out of habit, and heard only goats.

"I'm staying to help build it," Grandmother Reyes said simply. "A country reborn needs people who remember what it cost. And besides—" the audit-look softened into something purely, dangerously fond— "somebody has to write down what actually happened, and my grandson's book writes *him*, not the other way around. I've seen it try. It'll have him writing prophecy about next Tuesday's breakfast."

"It did that once," Jonah admitted. "The porridge burned. The book called it 'a trial by fire.'"

Grandmother Reyes laughed—a real laugh, unguarded, the laugh of a woman who had finally put down something very heavy—and then she embraced them both, hard, flour and all, and smelled of bread and cedar and a hundred years of held breath finally let out.

"Letters," she said into Theo's hair. "I expect letters. Real ones, ink ones, carried through the door by hand, because I refuse to trust anything in this family that arrives by magic."

"You'll get letters," Theo promised.

"Weeks of them," Jonah added. "You'll regret asking."

"No," she said, pulling back, wiping her eyes briskly as though the tears were someone else's and she was doing them a favor by handling them. "I won't."

* * *

Maren's naming happened at dusk, in the bell tower above the town square, and it was the first time since the rebirth that anyone had climbed those stairs with permission instead of desperation.

The bells hung silent in their frames—all but one. The great bell, the one that had swallowed sound during the dark years, the prison-bell whose sister-bells had rung the country back into being, had been recast over three furious weeks while its old bronze was melted down and mixed with metal from the Warden's citadel chains. Nobody had asked the Warden's opinion. The Warden had said, quietly, that it seemed fitting, and then had gone back to his turnips.

Now the new bell hung gleaming, and beneath it stood Maren, in her mother's old coat let out at the seams, looking terrified in a way Theo recognized completely: the terror of getting exactly what you wanted.

"Bell-ringer of a rung bell," the eldest singer pronounced. "Not the last daughter of a silenced tower. Say what you'll ring, and when."

Maren opened her mouth. Closed it. Looked at Theo and Jonah in the doorway, and at the crowd below filling the square like spilled light, and at the rope in her own hands.

"Everything," she said. "Whenever it deserves it."

Which was, everyone agreed later, the correct answer, and also the reason the town got no sleep whatsoever that night, because Maren rang the bell at midnight for the sheer joy of hearing it answer, and again at dawn because the sunrise deserved it, and once more mid-morning because Bram's harvest cart lost a wheel and she felt the occasion warranted acknowledgment.

"She's going to be insufferable," Jonah said happily, watching her ring.

"She's going to be *magnificent*," Theo corrected, "and insufferable, and those are the same thing in the best people."

Before they left, Maren pressed a bundle into Theo's arms: apple cuttings from the terraces, wrapped in damp cloth. "From the trees that were fruiting when the song paused. They've been holding their breath a hundred and twenty years too. See if they'll grow in your valley." Then, quieter, gripping Theo's wrist: "You gave your voice for this. So I'm going to ring loud enough for both of us. That's the trade. Don't argue with a bell-ringer, we're famously stubborn."

"I wouldn't dream of it," Theo said, and meant the promise underneath it, which was: *I'll hear it. Every time. Even from the other side of the door.*

* * *

They left on a morning so golden it felt staged.

The pack between them held letters—dozens, sealed with wax, addressed in a dozen hands to cousins and grandchildren and one extremely confused great-nephew in a city that had never heard of Vessany and was about to receive mail from it anyway. It held seeds. It held a jar of honey from Bram's restored hives, and a loaf from Grandmother Reyes ("for the road, and don't share it with the dog, dogs have no gratitude"), and the book.

The book rode at the bottom, quiet now. Its pages had stopped writing themselves since the final verse; the last entry remained the one line Theo had read a lifetime ago in her kitchen—*and the valley opens*—followed by nothing but clean paper, as if the book were resting. Or waiting. Jonah checked it every morning anyway, the way you check on a sleeping animal that has bitten you before.

At the threshold, they paused.

Through the gap—wider now than a cat, narrower than a cart, exactly as voted—the millrace murmured forward, in the correct direction, which still struck Theo as slightly show-offy. Beyond it lay Merrow's Hollow: the tavern, the goats, the Oath of Quiet, the whole small careful world where she had spent her life folding herself smaller and smaller to fit.

Except she wasn't small anymore. She was carrying a country's mail.

"After you," Jonah said.

"After *you*," Theo said. "You're the traitor. Let history record you went first."

He laughed and stepped through, and the air changed around him—the particular hush of Merrow's Hollow, the silence-with-rules—and then Theo followed, and the door swung gently at her heels and came to rest ajar, breathing.

* * *

The walk down from the mill took an hour, and they talked the entire way, the way people talk when they've been somewhere enormous and need to keep saying ordinary things or risk floating off the earth.

"—and when we get back, I'm telling no one," Jonah was saying. "That's my position. Officially, you found me interesting at a tavern and it spiraled."

"Officially," Theo agreed, "we had a lovely date. There was stew. It was mediocre."

"There was a second date. Also mediocre. We're very boring people."

"The most boring. The valley elders will be relieved."

The dog was waiting for them at the edge of town—the stray from chapter one of everything, gray-muzzled and self-important—and it fell into step beside them, tail going, and Theo knelt down in the dust out of pure habit before she remembered.

The silence rose in her, total and clean, the silence she'd bought with her gift. She heard the dog's tail thump. She heard its breath. She heard nothing else, and would hear nothing else, ever, from anything with four legs and fur.

But the dog looked up at her anyway—looked the way it had looked that first morning, when it had told her about the paper man—and its eyes were bright and certain, and it made a small sound low in its chest, and Theo found, to her surprise, that she understood it perfectly.

Some things, it seemed, had never needed words.

"Oh," she said, and her voice cracked, and Jonah crouched beside her and put his arm around her shoulders and didn't ask, because he knew, because he'd watched her give the gift away and had never once treated her like something broken by it.

"What's it saying?" he asked gently.

"That we took our time," Theo managed, laughing wetly. "And that it kept my goat company. And that—" she listened with her whole body, this new way, this old way— "that there's someone waiting at the tavern who's been asking after us for days."

Jonah went very still. "Asking *how*?"

"With signs," Theo said slowly. "Paper. Drawn pictures." She looked up at him, and the evening light came gold through the trees, and somewhere far behind them—impossibly far, through a door ajar in another country—a bell began to ring, and she chose to believe she heard it. "Jonah. Someone in this valley knows we went somewhere. And they're leaving messages for us at the tavern."

The dog barked once, urgent, and trotted toward town, and looked back at them the way dogs do when they are quite sure you should already be following.
