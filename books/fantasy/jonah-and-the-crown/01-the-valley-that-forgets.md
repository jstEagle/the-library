## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The goats knew before he did that the weather would turn. They always did — some animal wisdom older than any magic Noor Ravensworth had ever been taught, and far more reliable. By the time the first grey bruise of cloud crested the western ridge, the whole herd had drifted down from the high pasture and stood clustered at the gate of the lower fold, chewing in that slow, deliberate way goats had, as if to say: *well? Are you going to stand there all afternoon, or are you going to do something useful?*

Noor did something useful. He always did.

He counted them twice — thirty-one heads, one lame ewe named Sorrel who had a talent for hiding herself in the bracken when it suited her — and found thirty-one, which meant Sorrel had decided, for once, not to be difficult. He latched the gate with a loop of tarred rope, checked the water trough for the green scum that gathered on warm days, and only then allowed himself to look up at the sky.

It was going to rain. Not storm-rain. Just rain, the ordinary kind, the kind that fell on Ashenmere the way it fell everywhere else in the world: without malice, without memory, without anyone's name attached to it.

Noor had lived in the valley long enough now that ordinary weather no longer made his hands shake. That had taken years. The first spring, when a thunderhead built itself over the eastern peaks and began to mutter, he had run halfway up the sheep track before his legs gave out, kneeling in the wet grass with his palms pressed flat against the earth as though he could hold the world down by main force. Old Maren, the widow who had taken him in because her sons were dead and her knees were bad and a valley needed hands more than it needed questions, had found him there and said nothing at all. She had simply sat down beside him in the mud, in the rain, until the thunder passed over and went elsewhere to be somebody else's grief.

That was the thing about Ashenmere that no one told you, the thing you could only learn by living inside it: the valley did not merely suppress magic. It forgot it. Cross the ridgeline and whatever you carried — the gift, the curse, the blood-debt, call it what you liked — simply stopped being true. Not bound. Not sleeping. *Gone*, the way a word goes out of your mouth and is gone, irrecoverable, belonging now to the air and not to you.

For most people that would have been nothing. For Noor it was the closest thing to mercy the world had ever shown him.

He walked back down through the lower pasture as the first drops came, fat and unhurried, spotting the dust. The cottage Maren had left him — left him outright, two winters past, along with the herd and the debt-free title to eleven scrubby acres — sat where the pasture met the alder brake, low-roofed and smoke-stained, its stone walls furred with moss on the north side. It was not a beautiful house. It was not a beautiful life. But it was a life that could be held in two hands, and Noor had learned the hard way what happened to lives that couldn't.

Inside, he banked the fire, set barley to soak for tomorrow's porridge, and ate standing up at the window: bread, hard cheese, an apple gone soft on one side that he cut around rather than waste. Rain ticked against the shutters. Somewhere up-valley a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

This was the whole of it. This was everything. Count the goats. Mend the fence. Split the wood before the cold came. Watch the sky and know — *know*, in the marrow-deep way that had taken five years to believe — that the sky could do nothing to him that he could not also do nothing about.

There were worse prayers than powerlessness. He had made most of them.

* * *

He had been twenty-two the night the storm killed Veylan Hollow.

People in the lowlands still called it a freak weather event, when they called it anything at all; the war had chewed up so many villages in those years that one more drowned hamlet in the hill country barely merited a line in the courier reports. But Noor remembered every hour of it, the way you remember a wound you gave yourself: precisely, uselessly, forever.

His father had been away — that was the detail that gnawed, that had gnawed for five years like a rat behind a wall. Aldric Ravensworth was always away in those last months, riding some errand or other for people he never named, coming home smelling of road dust and other men's politics, kissing Noor's mother on the forehead with a distracted tenderness that looked, even then, like goodbye. So it was Noor in the house that night, twenty-two and furious about it, furious about the silence his father's absence had spread over everything, furious about the war news from the south, furious about being young and strong and full of a gift that everyone in the family pretended was a family trait rather than a weapon.

And then the thunderheads had come down off the mountains, and something in Noor had come up to meet them.

He had not meant to. That was the truth he clung to in the worst years, and the truth he had finally stopped clinging to, because both versions hurt. He had been standing on the rise above the village with his arms at his sides, and the storm had leaned down toward him like a great blind animal scenting its own kind, and he had reached up — one hand, half-open, hardly a gesture at all — and the sky had answered.

Not a little answer. The sky had poured itself into him the way water pours into a cracked jar, faster than he could hold, faster than he could want, and for one impossible moment he had felt enormous, felt *chosen*, felt the whole roiling weight of the heavens balanced on his palm like a coin —

— and then he had dropped it.

The lightning took the granary first. Then the mill. Then the row of houses along the beck, where the Tallowmans lived, where old Sefa lived, where his mother's cousin Brenna lived with her three children. The rain that followed came down so hard it seemed less like weather than like apology, a drowning attempt to put out what it had fed. Eleven dead. His mother among them — she had run toward the burning granary because Brenna's children were inside, and the second strike had come down between her and the door, and after that there was no door, and no granary, and no mother.

Noor had knelt in the flood-mud at the edge of it all, hands still raised, empty, smoking faintly at the fingertips, and understood that he had not been chosen by the storm at all. He had been used by it. Or worse: he had used himself.

The storm-born elders came within the week. They were not cruel about it — that was somehow the unbearable part. They wrapped him in a grey cloak, the color of mourning among the high clans, and told him gently that a gift that size needed training, discipline, a holder's hand, and that since his father was unreachable, the clan would provide. They spoke of sanctuaries, of structured service, of the honor of the bloodline.

They spoke, in short, of making him a weapon again, but a *managed* one this time, kept in an armory, pointed by other people.

He ran instead. South, then west, following rumors of a valley where the gifted went to die quietly, until he crossed the ridgeline into Ashenmere and felt his own magic leave him like a fever breaking — sudden, total, leaving him shaking and weeping in a stranger's turnip field, lighter than he had ever been in his life.

Old Maren had found him there too. She had a genius for finding him at the bottom of things.

"You'll work for your bread," she'd said, looking down at this soaked, hollow-eyed young man from nowhere, "and you'll keep your history to yourself unless I ask. I won't ask."

She never had. She'd died in her sleep two winters ago with her hands folded and the fire burned down to ash, and Noor had buried her next to her sons on the south slope, and inherited thirty-one goats, eleven acres, and the only peace he had ever known.

Five years. Five years of counting goats under a sky that wanted nothing from him.

He should have known it wouldn't hold. Nothing that quiet ever does.

* * *

Three days later, near dusk, with the first real cold of autumn breathing down off the peaks, Noor was mending the hinge on the byre door when he saw the figure come through the pass.

The pass was the valley's single throat — a notch in the western ridge where the old drovers' road climbed out of the lowlands, half a day's walk from the cottage, visible from the pasture on clear days as a pale thread stitched across the rock. In five years, Noor had watched perhaps two dozen travelers cross it: a tinker, a pair of pilgrims, a tax-man from the lowland lord whom everyone fed and flattered and sent on his way. They always came down the same way, small with distance, growing slowly human as they approached, and Noor always felt the same small tightening in his chest as he watched them — not fear exactly, and not longing either, but something adjacent to both. The tightening of a man watching the outside world remember that his valley existed.

This figure did not walk down the pass. It fell down it.

Even at that distance Noor could see something was wrong — the lurching, catching gait, the way the man (it must be a man, broad-shouldered even hunched) used the rock face like a thing he hated and needed in equal measure. Twice he went down fully and rose again, and the second time he stayed down long enough that Noor, setting down the hinge-pin, heard himself say aloud, in the flat voice he used for talking to goats: "Don't."

But the man rose. And came on.

By the time the traveler reached the alder brake the light was nearly gone, and Noor could see him properly for the first time, and wished he couldn't. The man was maybe fifty, heavyset, dressed for riding in clothes that had once been good — good leather, good wool, the kind of quality that got men robbed on the lowland roads — except that everything he wore was dark. Not dyed dark. *Soaked* dark. His left side was black to the hip, and the hand pressed against it was black to the wrist, and he left a print on the gatepost when he gripped it that Noor would still be able to point to, months later, when people asked where it had all begun.

"Please," the man said. His voice was wrecked, gravel over broken glass. "Water. Only — water, and I'll go."

Noor had a bucket in his hand. He didn't remember picking it up. He stood in the gateway of his small, hard-won, carefully forgettable life and looked at this bleeding piece of the outside world, and every instinct he had spent five years sharpening told him the same thing: *shut the gate. Let the valley handle him. The valley forgets everything; let it forget this too.*

Instead he heard himself say, "Come inside before you fall down. Falling down costs extra."

The man almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, gray-lipped, more reflex than feeling, but there was something underneath it — some flicker of a person who had once laughed easily — and it undid Noor more than the blood had.

Inside, Noor put him in Maren's old chair by the fire, because it was the only chair deep enough to slump in, and gave him water, and then, because the man's eyes had begun to wander in a way that Noor recognized from the deathbeds of animals, a cup of broth with a beaten egg stirred into it. The man drank the water like a man who had been rationing himself for days. The broth he held in both hands without drinking, warming his fingers, staring into it.

"I need to tell you," he said.

"You need to drink that."

"I need to tell you." His eyes came up, and they were clear — horribly, urgently clear, the clarity of a man spending his last coins. "I've been carrying something a long way. Further than I thought I could. And I don't think—" A breath, wet and ragged. "I don't think I'm going to carry it much further."

"You're going to carry it to a healer in the morning. There's a woman up-valley—"

"No." The word came out flat and final, and the man set the broth down with exaggerated care, as if his hands could no longer be trusted with delicate things. "Listen to me. Listen. You live in the forgetting valley. Good. Good place to hide a thing. But I didn't come here to hide it. I came here to give it away, and the giving can't wait for morning, because the men behind me don't wait for mornings."

Noor went very still. Outside, the wind had begun to move through the alders, and somewhere far up the western ridge, too far to matter, too far to mean anything, thunder muttered once and thought better of it.

"What is it?" Noor asked. And then, because some older, deeper part of him already knew the shape of the answer, already felt the old dread rising in him like floodwater: "Who are you?"

The man fumbled at his collar, at something tucked inside it, and his breath caught on what might have been pain or might have been laughter. "My sister will come looking," he said. "When I don't send word. Wren. Her name is Wren. She'll find you whether you want finding or not — she's like that. Tell her—" He stopped. Pressed his lips together. Started again, quieter. "Tell her I finished the delivery."

Then he pulled the bundle from inside his shirt — oilcloth, wrapped tight, tied with cord, stained dark along one edge — and pressed it into Noor's hands with both of his, and the weight of it was nothing, less than nothing, a few folds of cloth and paper, and yet Noor's arms nearly buckled, because beneath the oilcloth and the blood he could feel it: a hum. Faint. Familiar. A frequency he had not felt in five years, that could not possibly exist here, past the ridgeline, in the valley where magic died —

Except that it wasn't dying. It was *waiting*. Whatever slept in that wrapping had crossed the ridge and gone on humming, patient as a seed, and Noor's skin prickled with the first cold touch of a thing he had sworn never to feel again.

"The thirteenth turn," the man whispered. He was slumping now, sliding, his eyes fixed on Noor with terrible urgency. "It has to be before the thirteenth turn. Your father—"

The fire popped. The wind moved in the alders.

"—your father would have known what to do with it. Ask yourself why a Ravensworth courier rode three hundred miles to find a Ravensworth son."

The world went narrow and ringing. Noor heard himself say, "My father is dead."

Something moved behind the man's eyes — pity, or knowledge, or both. "Yes," he said softly. "I expect he is. Now. He wasn't when I started this journey." His grip on Noor's wrists tightened, then loosened, finger by finger, like a tide going out. "Before the thirteenth turn. Don't let them make you choose sides. There aren't any sides. There's only—"

He never finished it. His head tipped back against the chair, and his breathing went long and rattling and then, between one gust of wind and the next, stopped altogether, and the fire burned on, and the rain began again against the shutters, ordinary rain, harmless rain, while Noor Ravensworth sat on the floor of his kitchen holding a dead man's bundle and listening to the sky mutter over the ridge like something waking up after a very long sleep.

Outside, far to the west, beyond the pass, beyond the ridgeline, the clouds were gathering — and for the first time in five years, Noor felt them gathering *toward* him.
