## 2. A Stranger at the Gate

The goats knew before Noor did.

He was mending a fence post at the far edge of the paddock when they began to bleat — not their ordinary complaining, which was constant and meaningless as weather, but something sharper, a rising chorus of alarm that sent them crowding against the eastern wall of their pen. Noor straightened, hammer in hand, and looked toward the pass.

Ashenmere had one road in and one road out: a grey thread of stone that climbed through the notch between Mount Ceren and its nameless twin, then dropped into the valley like a rope let down from the sky. In the six years since Noor had come to live there, he had watched that road with the patience of a man watching water boil. Merchants came twice a season. A tinker came more often, if the mood took him. Shepherds from the high pastures drifted down like leaves. Nothing else. The valley had a way of discouraging visitors; even the merchants cut their stays short, complaining of headaches they couldn't name, of dreams that left them tired, of a feeling — so several of them said, rubbing their temples — *of being forgotten*.

That was the magic dying at the ridgeline. That was what Noor had paid everything to find.

But the goats were staring at the pass, and the pass was empty, and the evening light was going long and copper across the water, and Noor told himself it was a fox. It was always a fox. He went back to his fence post, and the goats went on screaming at nothing, and somewhere above them, on the road Noor could not see from where he knelt, a man was bleeding out into the dust.

* * *

Noor heard him before he saw him — a scrabbling, a wet dragging sound, and then a fist against his door, three blows that had no strength behind them at all, more like a bird throwing itself at glass than a man asking entry.

It was full dark by then. Noor set aside his bowl of barley soup, and stood, and did not move for the space of ten heartbeats. Six years of habit held him still. Strangers meant questions. Questions meant names. Names meant the ridgeline losing its meaning, the valley losing its silence, the whole careful architecture of his exile coming down around his ears because some traveler had a tale to tell in the next town over: *there's a man living alone in Ashenmere, keeps goats, doesn't talk much, and I'll tell you what, there's something wrong about his eyes.*

The fist struck the door again. Weaker.

"Please," said a voice, cracked down to the grain. "Please, I was told — the keeper — I was told there'd be a keeper—"

There was no keeper in Ashenmere. There never had been. Noor crossed the room anyway.

He opened the door on a man who fell forward into his arms.

He was younger than the voice suggested — thirty perhaps, maybe less, though starvation and fear had aged him badly — dressed for travel in clothes that had once been fine and were now ruined past all repair. Mud to the knee. Blood everywhere else. His left side was soaked dark, and when Noor eased him down onto the floorboards, the man's hand pressed flat against his ribs and came away glistening.

"Lock the door," the stranger rasped. "Lock it. They'll follow. They always follow."

Noor looked out past the threshold at the blue-dark valley, the flat silver sheet of the mere, the black shoulder of the ridge. Nothing moved. Wind moved. He shut the door all the same, and slid the bolt, and turned back to find the man watching him with an expression Noor recognized and hated: recognition.

"You're him," the stranger whispered. "You're Ravensworth's boy."

The words landed in Noor's chest like stones dropped down a well. He heard them fall and fall without reaching bottom.

"I don't know what you mean," he said. "You're hurt. Let me—"

"Don't." The man caught his wrist with shocking speed, fingers slick and cold. "Don't lie to me. I've bled for this. I carried it over the Spine in winter. I buried two friends in the snowmelt. Don't you dare stand there and lie to me with your father's face on."

"My father is dead," Noor said. His voice came out level, which surprised him. "Everyone I come from is dead."

"Not dead enough," the stranger said, and laughed, and the laugh turned into coughing, and the coughing brought up blood. "Not hidden enough. Not *forgotten* enough. Gods, boy, do you think a valley makes you safe? Do you think the sky forgets what you are?"

Noor crouched beside him. Up close the man smelled of horse sweat and iron and something else beneath it, something burnt, like lightning had passed close enough to singe. His eyes were fever-bright and fixed on Noor's face as if he were reading a letter written there long ago.

"The storm-calling," Noor said quietly. "Is that what this is about?"

"It's about the map." The word came out of him like a tooth being pulled. "It's about the map and the thirteenth turn and every fool in this world who thinks they can hold what should be holding them. Listen to me. Listen." He dragged himself up against the wall, leaving a smear of red on the plaster, and his grip found Noor's collar. "They're coming. Both of them. The sun-courts have riders on the north road and the storm-born have runners in every port from here to the Salt Reach, and neither of them knows yet that the courier died in a goat-herder's house at the end of the world, but they will know. They always know. So you take it, and you run, and you don't stop running until—"

"I don't want whatever you're carrying."

"Nobody wants it." The man's grin was terrible. "That's the whole trick of it. Nobody *wants* it. Everybody *needs* it. There's a difference, boy, and the difference is graves."

Then his eyes rolled back, and for a moment Noor thought he was gone, and felt — absurdly, shamefully — relief. But the man rallied, the way the dying do, gathering himself for one last expenditure. His hand went inside his coat, moving with the slow deliberation of a man underwater, and came out holding a scroll case wrapped in oilcloth, stained nearly black.

He pressed it into Noor's hands and folded Noor's fingers closed over it, both of his own hands wrapped around Noor's fists, as if in prayer, as if in benediction.

"Keep it dry," he breathed. "Keep it hidden. Keep it away from the thirteenth moon. When she comes — when my sister comes — tell her—" His voice failed. He tried again, and what came out was barely sound at all, shaped more by breath than voice, and Noor bent close to catch it.

A name.

One word. One name, spoken with the last of a dead man's air, and it went through Noor like a spear of ice:

"*Ravensworth.*"

Not his own name. His father's.

The man's hands slipped away. His head tipped back against the wall. And Noor knelt there on his own floorboards with a dead stranger's blood cooling on his hands and his father's name ringing in his skull, while outside, far off across the mere, a light appeared on the northern road — small, steady, deliberate — and began to move toward the valley.

* * *

He worked until dawn, because work was the only language he had left for grief or fear or anything else too large to hold.

He dug the grave himself, up on the rise behind the house where the soil was thin and stony, and laid the stranger in it with his boots on, because whoever he had been, he had died walking. No marker. Noor told himself he would carve one later, and knew he wouldn't, because markers were questions and questions were dangerous, and besides — the man had asked to be forgotten. Hadn't he? Noor wasn't sure anymore. Dying men said many things. He chose to believe this one.

The light on the north road had vanished sometime in the night, or turned aside, or been snuffed out. Noor had spent the hours between burying and dawn doing what he always did when the past knocked: nothing. Sitting. Watching the door. Telling himself the man had been mad, feverish, mistaken. That *Ravensworth* was a common name. That his father — quiet, careful, weatherwise Aldous Ravensworth, who could read clouds the way priests read scripture and had never once, in all Noor's childhood, raised his voice — that his father had no enemies worth sending a bleeding courier across a mountain range to find.

Except.

Except that his father *had* died strangely. That had always been the shape of the wound Noor refused to press. The fire, they said. The fever, said others. But Noor had been seventeen, and he remembered his father in the last weeks standing at the window during thunderstorms, watching the lightning with an expression Noor had never understood until this very night, kneeling here, holding a dead courier's oilcloth bundle: the expression of a man listening for footsteps.

And except that his father had taught him storm-script. Not openly — never openly — but in the way of fathers, in fragments, disguised as games. Scratches in frost on the window. Marks drawn in spilled flour. *What does this say, little storm?* And Noor, laughing, reading them without knowing he was reading, the way children read the faces of people they love.

The bundle sat on his table where the stranger had left it. All night it sat there, patient as a grave.

When the first grey light came up over the ridge, Noor washed the blood from his hands in the trough, dried them, and unwrapped the oilcloth.

Inside was a map.

Or the bones of one — vellum gone soft with handling, creased white along fold-lines, marked with rivers and ranges he half-knew and coastlines he didn't know at all, and threaded through all of it, dense as frost on glass, a script he had not seen in nine years.

Storm-script. Every line of annotation, every margin note, every winding path marked in that spidery, angular hand — written in a language only a storm-caller could read.

Written, Noor realized, his mouth going dry, in a language only *he* might be left alive to read.

The ink seemed to shift as he looked at it. He told himself it was the dawn light, the tiredness in his eyes. He told himself a great many things. Then, at the map's lower edge, in a fresher hand, in ordinary trade-common anyone could read, someone had written four words:

*Before the thirteenth moon.*

Outside, the goats had begun again — that sharp, rising chorus of alarm, heads all turned toward the pass.

Noor looked up from the map. Down the length of the valley, on the road that climbed toward the notch, two riders had appeared out of the morning mist, moving at an easy canter, unhurried, the way men move when they are certain the thing they hunt has nowhere left to go.

Noor wrapped the map back in its oilcloth with hands that almost didn't shake, slid it beneath a loose floorboard under the hearthstone, put the poker back in its place, and opened his door to meet the day.

The riders were still a mile off. He had time to count his lies and find them wanting.

*They always follow,* the dead man had said. *They always know.*

The past had found him. Now Noor had to decide what he was willing to become to keep it from taking everything back.
