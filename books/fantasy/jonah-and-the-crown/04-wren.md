## 4. Wren

Wren had been walking for eleven days when the valley finally let her in.

That was how it felt — a letting-in, like passing through a door that only opened for people with nothing left to lose. The pass narrowed above her, walls of grey stone pressing close, and then, all at once, the world went quiet. Not the ordinary quiet of high country. A deeper quiet, as if someone had drawn a curtain between her and everything she had ever known. The wind still moved through the grass; she could see it bending in silver waves down the slope below. But she couldn't *feel* it anymore.

She stopped at the ridgeline and stood there a long moment, one boot on each side of the invisible line, and understood what her brother had meant in his last letter, the one written in his cramped, hurried hand: *There is a place where the sky forgets you. If I don't come home, look for me there.*

The sky had never spoken to Wren the way it spoke to storm-born. She was plain-blooded, road-born, a smuggler's daughter from a family whose name had been scraped off every ledger in three provinces. But even she felt the absence now, like a tooth pulled from the jaw — the ghost-ache of something gone. Whatever lived in this valley ate magic the way sand ate water. No wonder Tam had come here to die.

"No," she said aloud, to the valley, to herself. "He came here to hide. Someone else made him die."

She adjusted the strap of her pack, checked the knife at her hip out of habit rather than fear, and started down into Ashenmere.

* * *

The village was smaller than she'd expected. A scatter of stone houses with turf roofs, a crooked mill leaning over a stream the color of old pewter, goats on the hillside like dropped stones. Smoke rose thin and straight from a dozen chimneys. It looked like a place where time had been poured thick as honey and left to set. She hated it immediately, the way you hate anything that has kept what you've lost.

She did what travelers do. She bought bread with the last of her honest coin, sat on the bench outside the bakery, and listened.

Ashenmere folk were not unfriendly, exactly. They were careful, the way animals are careful near a snare they can smell but not see. When she asked about newcomers — a man, wounded, arrived within the fortnight — the baker's wife wiped her hands on her apron three times before answering.

"Strangers don't stay long here," the woman said. "Valley doesn't suit them."

"There was one. Tall, dark-haired, keeps to himself. Herds goats up past the north field."

Something flickered across the woman's face — recognition, and beneath it, a kind of pity. "Ravensworth. He keeps to himself, right enough." She said the name the way you'd say the name of a disease that had passed through town years ago and might come back. Then she caught herself, and her eyes went sharp. "Why do you ask?"

"Family friend," Wren said, and smiled the smile she'd learned before she'd learned to read — open, guileless, entirely false. "I've come a long way."

The baker's wife looked at her for a moment longer than was comfortable. Then she wrapped a second roll, unasked, and pushed it across the counter. "Take him this. He forgets to eat, that one. Forgets he's allowed to."

Wren carried the rolls up the hillside wondering what kind of exile forgot he was allowed to eat.

* * *

She found him at dusk, at the edge of a stony pasture where the grass gave up trying. He was kneeling in the dirt with his arms around a goat's neck, steadying it while it struggled against something — a thorn in its hoof, by the way it kept wrenching its leg. He worked without hurry, murmuring to the animal in a low voice, and there was such unguarded gentleness in the set of his shoulders that for a moment Wren almost turned back.

Almost.

Then she remembered Tam's face the last time she'd seen it — not dead, because she'd never seen him dead, thank every god that didn't answer, but leaving. Leaning in the doorway with his pack half-packed and his eyes already somewhere else, saying, *One more job, Ren. One more and we buy the name back.* Everyone said one more job. The one-more-jobs were how orphans got made.

She stepped off the wall she'd been leaning on and let her boots crunch deliberately on the gravel.

He turned fast — faster than a goatherd should, with a coiled readiness underneath the calm that told her more than any introduction could have. His eyes were dark and level and very tired, and they went from her face to her knife to her pack and back again, cataloguing, the way Tam used to catalogue a room.

"You're not from the valley," he said.

"No one who matters is." She held up the paper-wrapped rolls. "Brought you dinner. The baker's wife says you forget to eat."

"I don't know you."

"No," Wren agreed. "But you knew my brother. Ten days ago a man bled out on your floor. Tall. Red hair going grey at the temples. Talked too much even when he was dying, especially when he was dying. That was Tam." Her voice stayed flat and steady, which cost her more than shouting would have. "Tam Corvane. And you're standing in a pasture feeding goats, so either you buried my brother and stole whatever he was carrying, or you buried my brother and lost it. Either way, I've walked eleven days, and I'm not leaving this hillside until I know which."

The goat, freed, trotted off with an air of grievance. Noor Ravensworth rose slowly, dusting his knees, and looked at her the way a man looks at weather he'd hoped would pass him by.

"He didn't suffer long," he said quietly. "If that helps."

It didn't. It cracked something in her chest that she'd soldered shut somewhere on the road south of the Ashen, and she had to stand very still and breathe until the crack sealed over. "It doesn't," she said. "Nothing about it helps. What did he give you?"

"Why does everyone assume he gave me something?"

"Because Tam never traveled light. Because he wrote to me six weeks ago talking about a map and a thirteenth turn like they were scripture. Because—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter. "Because he was my brother, and I know what he looked like when he was carrying something worth killing for. So. Which is it? Stole it, or lost it?"

For a long moment he just watched her. The light was failing; the first stars were pricking through over the ridge, and down in the valley the mill wheel turned with its slow wooden groan. Somewhere behind those tired dark eyes, Wren could practically hear the arithmetic running — *who is she, who sent her, what does she know, what will it cost.*

She recognized the arithmetic. She ran it herself, every day, in every room. Two liars, she thought, looking at each other across a dead man's grave neither had dug yet.

"Come inside," he said finally. "You shouldn't be on the hillside after dark."

"That an invitation or a warning?"

"With me? Neither. With the valley — both."

* * *

His house was small and painfully clean, the way homes are clean when no one in them expects joy. One room downstairs, a hearth swept to ash, two chairs, a table scrubbed white. Dried herbs hung from the beams in tidy bundles. On the wall, a single shelf holding perhaps thirty books, their spines cracked and re-cracked, read to death and read again. There was a cot in the corner with a folded blanket, and beside the hearth, a patch of floorboards slightly paler than the rest.

Wren looked at that pale patch and knew. Blood had been spilled there, and scrubbed, and scrubbed, and the wood had never quite forgotten.

She sat down in one of the chairs without being asked and put the rolls on the table between them like a toll paid. "Talk."

He didn't sit. He stood with his back to the hearth, arms crossed, and studied her with that same exhausting thoroughness. "Who told you he died here?"

"A courier out of Marrowgate. Paid him a month's wages to tell me, which tells you how badly Tam wanted it kept quiet." She leaned forward. "You're doing the thing where you decide how much truth I can carry. I've watched men do it my whole life. Do it once more and I'll walk out of here and take the valley watch with me, and you can explain to them why there's blood under your floorboards and a stranger's horse tied outside your gate."

"There's no horse."

"There will be by morning if you keep wasting my time."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile — the ghost of one, surprised at itself. He sat down opposite her, slowly, like a man lowering himself into cold water.

"He gave me a map," he said.

Wren's breath went out of her all at once. She hadn't realized she'd been holding it since the ridgeline. "And?"

"And it's not ink on vellum the way you're imagining. It's storm-script. Written in a language only a storm-caller can read — the letters move, they change, they—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening, as though he'd said more than he meant to. "It was meant for someone like me. Which means Tam knew what I was when he climbed that pass. Which means he came looking for me specifically, dying, to hand me a map I haven't finished reading."

"What's on it?"

"I don't know yet. All of it. None of it. The script shifts." He rubbed his eyes with the heels of his hands, and for a moment the exhaustion broke through the caution and he looked young, and wrecked, and terribly alone. "Every time I look at it, I understand a little more, and what I understand frightens me. Thirteen moons. A crown. Something about a war — the old war, the one they don't teach children anymore except as a story to make them behave."

"The Crown of Thirteen Moons," Wren said.

His hands came down. He stared at her. "You know that name."

"Everyone south of the Salt Road knows that name right now, whether they admit it or not. Sun-court soldiers asking questions in every market town. Storm-born moving in the hills like smoke. Travelers telling stories over fires about an artifact that ends wars or starts them, depending on who you're afraid of." She ticked them off on her fingers. "Tam believed it could restore our family's name. Our grandfather held lands under the sun-courts before the purges. The Corvanes were somebody, once. Tam spent his whole life trying to buy back a word that was taken from us before either of us was born." Her voice caught on the last part and she let it, deliberately, because grief was the one coin she had that couldn't be counterfeited. "And now he's dead in your kitchen, and you're sitting on the thing he died carrying, and you want me to believe you don't know what it is."

"I believe it's a seal," he said.

The words dropped into the room like a stone into a well. Wren waited for the splash and heard none.

"Not a prize," he went on, slowly, watching her face as he said it, testing her. "Not a weapon. A seal. Something that's keeping a war shut — the real war, the first war, the one between the storm-born and the sun-courts that predates every kingdom currently pretending it invented the sky. And whoever claims it doesn't win anything. They *open* it." He shook his head. "That's what I've read so far. I could be wrong. The script lies sometimes, or maybe I'm the one lying, because the alternative is that my brother—" He stopped. Corrected himself, and Wren saw the correction cost him. "—that *Tam* died carrying a door-key to the end of the world."

Wren sat very still. Outside, the wind moved through the valley grass, and she couldn't feel it, and the not-feeling suddenly seemed like the most important fact in the world. Magic couldn't follow past the ridge. Which meant whatever was coming for that map — soldiers, rebels, things worse than either — would eventually have to climb down into this valley to get it, and the valley would strip them bare as it had stripped her.

But it wouldn't stop them walking. Nothing stopped people from walking.

"Here's what I know," she said. "I know roads. All of them. The salt routes, the smuggler paths, the passes the maps pretend don't exist. I can get two people across three provinces without a single checkpoint seeing our faces. I've done it since I was nine years old." She met his eyes. "What I can't do is read a map that rearranges itself. And you can't cross a hundred leagues of hunted country without being taken apart by the first patrol that looks at you sideways, because you lie like a man who's never needed to practice."

"I lied to the riders fine."

"You lied to the riders *badly*. I talked to them yesterday in Marrowford. They described you to me — tall, dark, nervous, kept touching his left sleeve where he'd hidden something. If I could pick you out of a crowd from their description alone, so can the next ones, and the next ones won't ask questions in a market square." She leaned back. "So. You know what the map says. I know how to survive the road it points down. My brother died getting that map to you, which means either he trusted you with his life's work, or he had no one else left. Both of those make you mine to deal with, Ravensworth. The question is whether we deal with each other or spend the whole journey watching our backs."

Silence. The fire ticked in the hearth. Down in the valley a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"You want the Crown," he said at last. "To restore your name."

"I want what my brother wanted. He bled out ten feet from where you're standing believing in it. That's not nothing. That's a debt." She tilted her head. "And you? What do you want, goat-herd? Because nobody crosses a ridgeline out of a valley like this for a map they're afraid of. You'll come because the map chose you, or because you think running is safer than staying, or because—" she paused, and watched him flinch, and knew she'd found it — "because someone with your father's name is written into this somewhere, and you need to know if it's true."

The silence that followed had weight. He looked at the pale patch of floorboard by the hearth, and Wren understood that she'd struck closer than she'd planned, and that whatever she'd hit, he'd been bleeding from it for years.

"My father's name was on the man's lips when he died," Noor said quietly. "That's all I'll say tonight."

"It's more than you said an hour ago."

"Yes." He exhaled, long and slow, and some of the wariness went out of his shoulders — not trust, never that, but the exhaustion of a man deciding to postpone a war he couldn't afford. "We leave before dawn. There are riders on the southern pass, and others I can't account for. If we're doing this, we do it tonight."

"We?" Wren raised an eyebrow. "Just like that?"

"Just like that." He almost smiled again, that startled ghost of it. "You said it yourself. You lie badly enough to be honest with, and I read maps that rearrange themselves. Between us we make nearly one trustworthy person."

"Don't flatter yourself. I'm coming for Tam, not for you."

"Good," he said. "Keep it that way. It's cleaner."

They shook on it — her grip hard, his careful, both of them measuring the other's hand like card players counting a deck. Wren took the roll she'd carried eleven days up a mountain and tore it in half and handed him his share, because a bargain wasn't real until you'd broken bread on it, and because she wanted to watch whether he'd eat.

He ate. Slowly, like a man remembering how.

Outside, the stars wheeled over the ridge, and far to the south — though neither of them saw it yet — the thirteenth moon was climbing toward its fullness, and the map hidden beneath Noor's floorboards had begun, very faintly, to glow.
