## 6. The Road of Hanged Men

The road south was called the King's Way, once. Now the signposts had been stripped of their letters, and what remained were posts — bare, weathered, leaning at angles like men too tired to stand straight — and Wren thought that was honest, in its way. A road with no name couldn't pretend it led anywhere good.

She walked three paces ahead of Noor Ravensworth and did not look back at him. She'd learned the trick of it on the first morning: if you looked back, you started talking, and if you started talking, you started asking questions, and she wasn't ready for his answers yet. Or her own.

Three days out of Ashenmere, and the valley's hush still clung to them like river silt. She kept glancing at the sky without meaning to — a habit from childhood, when her brother Tam had taught her to read weather off the clouds the way other children learned letters. The sky over the lowlands was bruise-colored at the horizon, clearing to a hard pale blue overhead, and every time the wind shifted she felt something prickle along her arms, some animal instinct telling her the air was *wrong*, though wrong how, she couldn't say.

Noor felt it too. She could tell by the way he held himself, shoulders drawn up around his ears like a man walking through a room full of sleeping dogs. He hadn't called so much as a spark since the ridge — she'd seen him try, once, alone at the stream while he thought she slept, fingers spread against the wind, face tight with concentration and then, worse, with nothing at all. Like a man speaking into a well and hearing no echo.

Good, she told herself. Better for both of them if the storm-caller stayed broken. But the thought sat poorly in her chest, because Tam had died pressing a map into this man's hands, and whatever else Tam had been — fool, dreamer, courier, liar — he hadn't wasted his last breath on a broken thing.

The first village appeared just past midday: a cluster of stone houses around a dry well, roofs caved in, doors hanging open on darkness. No smoke. No dogs. In the doorway of the largest house, someone had hung a bundle of dried herbs — old magic, border-folk magic, the kind meant to keep out grief or ghosts, Wren couldn't remember which.

"Emptied," Noor said quietly, behind her. "Not burned."

"No." She picked her way through the square, boots crunching on glass gone green and dull with age. "The sun-courts emptied these. After the last war. They moved whole villages inland, resettled them where they could be watched. Anyone who wouldn't go—" She nodded toward the far end of the street, where a gallows stood in the weeds, its rope long since rotted to a frayed gray tail. "They left the frame standing. As a lesson."

"You know this road."

"I grew up two days east of here." She said it flatly, before she could decide not to. "Before my family lost everything. Before the name was taken."

He didn't ask what name. She noticed that — noticed the care of it, the deliberate not-asking, and hated him a little for being kind about it. Kindness from a man who was lying to her by the hour; she could feel the lies on him like heat off a hearthstone. He knew more about Tam than he'd said. He knew more about the map. And she needed him anyway, because the map was written in storm-script, and storm-script was written in lightning, and lightning answered to exactly one living soul she knew of.

They watered at the village well — dry, but there was a spring behind the shrine, sweet and cold — and pressed on.

* * *

The travelers began appearing that evening, and that was how Wren knew they were truly out of the dead country and into the living one: the living traveled in fear, in clusters, in silence.

First came a tinker with a handcart full of dented pots, who fell in beside them for an hour because the road was safer shared. He talked constantly, the way frightened men do, filling the air so nothing else could fill it. He spoke of tolls on the southern bridges, of soldiers wearing white-and-gold who took boys off the roads for questioning and returned half of them. He spoke of the thirteenth moon.

"It's coming early this year," he said, squinting up. "Old folk say when the thirteenth turns full out of season, the Crown wakes. My grandmother saw the last turning. Said the bells rang in Vaelmoor, all of them, under the water. Rang for a day and a night." He crossed himself with a pot-mender's dirty thumb. "Course, Vaelmoor's under the sea now, so maybe it's all stories."

"Most stories are," Wren said.

"Aye, but which ones?" The tinker grinned, gap-toothed, and wheeled his cart onward south, and Wren watched him go and felt the question lodge under her ribs like a fishhook.

They made camp that night in the shell of a waystation, one wall standing, the roof open to the stars. Noor built the fire with the grim competence of a man who'd tended goats alone for years, and Wren ate hard bread and watched him across the flames and finally let herself ask the thing she'd been circling for three days.

"Your father," she said. "The name Tam whispered to you. You went white as milk. So you knew it. Which means your father isn't just some farmer whose son got struck by lightning's fancy." She turned the bread in her hands. "Who was he, Noor Ravensworth?"

The fire cracked. Somewhere down the valley a nightbird called, twice, and went quiet.

"I don't know," Noor said at last. "That's the truth, Wren, however little it's worth. I knew him as a quiet man who mended nets and never swam. I knew he flinched at thunder. I knew the storm-born came for him once, when I was small, and my mother hid me in the flour chest, and when they left, my father sat by the fire until dawn and never said a word about it." His jaw worked. "I exiled myself before I ever thought to ask him anything. And then the storm—" He stopped. Started again. "There's no one left to ask."

Wren studied him across the fire, hunting the seam in the lie, and found — infuriatingly — that she believed him. It was the worst answer he could have given her, because it meant Tam had died carrying a secret that had died with him, unless—

Unless the map. Unless the letters. Tam had carried letters south for years, letters Wren had never been allowed to read, sealed with wax and a sigil she'd only ever seen once: a crescent crossed by a bolt. She'd assumed they were love letters. She'd been fifteen and romantic and stupid.

"The thirteenth moon," she said instead, because her mouth was braver than her judgment. "You heard the tinker. Do you believe it? That the Crown wakes?"

"Do you?"

"I believe Tam believed it." She pulled her knees up, wrapped her arms around them. The firelight made the ruined waystation almost gentle, shadows softening the scorch marks on the standing wall. "My brother spent six years working couriers' roads, carrying things for people whose names he never gave me, and every coin he sent home he sent with the same note: *soon*. Soon we'll buy back the house. Soon the name will be restored. Soon, soon, soon." Her voice came out harder than she intended. "Our family held land under the old kings. Before the sun-courts rewrote the rolls, we were Vaelmoor's stewards — did you know that? There are records. There are *witnesses*. And the courts took it all with a signature and called it reform, and my mother scrubbed floors in the houses that used to be ours until her hands split, and my father drank himself into the ground rather than look at us, and Tam—"

She stopped. Breathed. The fire popped and settled.

"Tam believed there was one thing in the world big enough to make the courts give back what they stole. Not gold. Not armies. One thing." She looked up at Noor, and let him see it, all of it, the naked wanting she'd kept wrapped since Ashenmere. "The Crown of Thirteen Moons. He used to say it like a prayer. *One crown to unmake every false king.* If we found it — if our family brought it back into the world — no court in the south could refuse us restitution. They'd kneel for it. They'd have to."

Noor was silent for a long moment. The firelight moved on his face, and she watched something complicated pass through it — not greed, she realized. Fear. Real fear, the deep kind, the kind that has already imagined the worst.

"And if it's not a treasure?" he said quietly. "If it's something else?"

"Then it's something else worth more than my mother's split hands." She lay back against her pack and closed her eyes, ending the conversation the way she ended most of hers. "Sleep, storm-caller. Tomorrow we reach the hanged men, and you'll want your rest for that."

"What hanged men?"

But she didn't answer, and eventually his breathing slowed, and she lay awake under the hole in the roof watching the thirteenth moon climb — swollen, silver, a few days shy of full and burning out of season, exactly as the tinker said — and she made herself a promise in the dark, quiet and cold and absolute:

Whatever the Crown was, she would carry it home. For Tam. For the name. For every floor her mother had ever scrubbed.

* * *

The Road of Hanged Men earned its name honestly.

They came upon it in the gray of the second morning: a stretch of the King's Way two miles long, lined on both sides with gallows — dozens of them, hundreds, oak frames black with decades of rain, most empty, ropes rotted or cut, but not all. Here and there a body still swung, tanned to leather by sun and wind, dressed in the rags of soldiers or farmers or, twice, in the white-and-gold of sun-court officers. The old war had hanged everyone, it seemed. Victory had only decided which corpses got taken down.

Noor walked the whole stretch without speaking. Wren watched him do it — watched him count the frames, read the rot on the ropes, cataloguing the road the way she'd seen him catalogue everything since the ridge, like a man memorizing exits from a burning house.

At the halfway mark, a wayside shrine had been built from gallows-timber, and an old woman knelt before it, laying out little offerings: bread crusts, a copper bit, a child's carved bird. She looked up as they passed, and her eyes were milky and calm.

"Pilgrims?" she asked.

"Travelers," Wren said.

"Everyone's traveling south now." The woman smiled, gapless gums and terrible serenity. "The moon's turning early. You feel it, don't you? In the teeth. In the blood." She patted the shrine. "I hang flowers for them. All of them. Sun and storm alike. Because when the Crown comes back, none of it will matter who hanged whom. Only who's standing near it when it wakes." She turned her blind eyes on Noor, and Wren saw him go rigid. "You're one of theirs, aren't you, boy? Storm walks in you like a lodger. I can smell the thunder on your breath."

"We should go," Wren said sharply, and pressed a copper into the old woman's hand, and drew Noor away down the road of the dead.

Only when the gallows thinned did he speak, and his voice was strange, scraped thin.

"She said the Crown wakes. The tinker said it. Tam believed it. Everyone on this road is walking toward the same word." He stopped walking. "Wren. What happens if it's real? What happens if we find it and it's — what your brother hoped?"

"Then my family gets its name back," she said, "and yours gets its answer."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have."

He looked at her then, really looked, and she saw him weighing something — whether to tell her, whether to trust her, whether she was worth the truth — and she watched him decide against it, watched the door close behind his eyes, and she filed that away with all the other evidence, cold and patient, the way she'd learned to file things in the houses where her mother scrubbed.

Because here was what Wren knew that Noor didn't: Tam hadn't only carried letters. On his last visit home, drunk on festival wine and grief for their mother's grave, he'd told her one true thing about his work. *There's a keeper,* he'd said. *There's always been a keeper. The Crown doesn't sit in a vault waiting to be found — it sits in a family, waiting to be failed.* And he'd wept after he said it, and refused to explain, and in the morning he'd begged her to forget it, and she had pretended to.

She hadn't forgotten a single word.

And watching Noor Ravensworth walk the road of hanged men with his shoulders hunched against a sky that no longer answered him, Wren felt the shape of it assembling itself in her mind, terrible and inevitable, piece fitting into piece: the map in storm-script, the name that whitened Noor's face, the keeper's family, the courier who died delivering his message home.

The Crown wasn't waiting to be found.

It was waiting to come home to him.

Ahead, past the last gallows, the road bent south toward the river country, and on the bend, dust rising, movement — riders, six or eight, moving fast, banners snapping white and gold above them like teeth.

Wren put her hand on Noor's arm. "Down," she breathed. "Sun-court livery. *Now.*"
