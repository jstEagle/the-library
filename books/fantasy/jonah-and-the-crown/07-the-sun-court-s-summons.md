## 7. The Sun-Court's Summons

They came out of the wheat at midday, when the road was a ribbon of white dust and the heat made the horizon tremble like water.

Noor saw them first — he had learned to see soldiers before Wren did, some old animal instinct that had survived the exile of everything else about him. Six riders where there had been empty fields. Then eight. Then a dozen, fanning out with the unhurried precision of men who had never once needed to hurry.

"Wren." He said it quietly, not turning his head. "Company."

She was already counting. He could feel her doing it beside him, the way her breathing changed, the small shift of her weight off her bad heel. "Twelve," she murmured. "Lances. Sun-court colors under the dust — see the gold thread on the standards? That's not militia. That's a household troop." A pause. "Somebody's household."

"Somebody important."

"Somebody who can afford to be wrong about us." She laughed softly, without any humor in it at all. "Walk slower. Men who run look guilty. Men who walk look stupid, and stupid men get searched and released."

"And if they're not looking for stupid men?"

"Then we're dead either way," she said cheerfully, "so we may as well be comfortable."

The wheat ended and the road took them, and the riders came on until the sound of hooves filled the whole hot afternoon. Noor kept his eyes down and his hands loose and thought, with a strange detached clarity, *I have done this before.* Not this exactly — never this — but the posture of it. The smallness. Standing very still while power circled him, waiting to learn what shape his life would take by nightfall.

He had been fourteen the last time. The storm had still been in his blood then, and the men circling had been storm-born elders come to pass judgment on a boy who had called down lightning onto his own village. They had looked at him the way farmers look at a field after a flood: measuring what was ruined, calculating what might yet be saved.

These men would not measure ruin. These men would measure value.

The riders halted around them in a loose crescent, close enough that he could smell the horses' sweat and the oiled leather of their harness. Their armor was pale enameled steel, chased with gold at the edges, and every piece of it gleamed as though sunlight had been poured into it and sealed there. In the flat glare of noon it hurt to look at them directly.

The one who rode forward wore no helmet. He was perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, with a face like a coin that had passed through too many hands — all the sharpness worn away, only the impression of a profile remaining. His eyes were the exception. They were pale, almost colorless, and they moved over Noor the way an assessor's eyes move over livestock.

"Travelers," the man said. It was not a question. It was a category.

"Pilgrims," Wren said, before Noor could open his mouth. Her voice had changed entirely — softer now, rounder, carrying the faint rural burr she slipped into like a borrowed coat. "Bound for the shrine at Ellow Ford, lord, if the roads are safe enough for the likes of us."

"The roads are as safe as the people on them." The officer smiled. It did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "And you'd be brother and sister, I think. There's a family look about you both. The same stubborn jaw."

"We get that from our mother," Wren said.

"No," the officer said pleasantly. "You get it from each other. You've been traveling together less than a month — your boots don't match your story, girl. Hers are new. Yours have walked a war road." He let that sit in the silence. "But then, lying is a kind of pilgrimage too. Everyone's walking toward something they want to believe."

Noor felt the trap close by degrees, elegant and unhurried, and understood that this man had done this many times, to many travelers, and enjoyed it the way some men enjoy carving.

*Catalogue them,* his father's voice said in his memory, absurdly calm, a lesson given over maps long burned. *A man who talks is a man who is telling you what he fears.*

So Noor catalogued. The officer: patient, theatrical, confident enough to waste time — which meant he had orders not to kill them, or believed he didn't need to. The sergeant behind his left shoulder: hand resting on his sword but thumb outside the guard, a man not expecting violence. Two riders at the crescent's ends had crossbows, unspanned. Unspanned meant theater. Theater meant recruitment.

And one rider, at the back, whose horse stood apart and whose armor bore no gold at all — a clerk, or a questioner, watching Noor specifically. Watching his hands.

His hands. Of course. Storm-callers were known by their hands; the gift lived in the palms, in the old scar-lines that lightning left like signatures. Noor had spent seven years keeping gloves on even in summer, and today, in the heat, he had gone bare-handed like a fool, like a man who had forgotten what he was because the valley had let him forget.

The clerk nudged his horse forward. "Ser Vaelin," he said, and his voice was dry paper. "If I may."

"You may, Quillmaster."

The clerk did not look at Noor's face. He looked at his hands, openly, the way one reads a document. And then he smiled — a thin, satisfied expression, the smile of a man confirming a sum.

"There was a courier," the Quillmaster said, "who passed through Corvane eleven days ago. Wounded. Riding hard. He carried something belonging to a traitor house — Ravensworth, if the old registers are to be trusted. The courier died. But things travel. Things find hands." His gaze lifted at last, and settled on Noor with the gentle weight of a net settling over a bird. "Storm-script has been seen again in the world, traveler. Ink that moves. Ink that burns cold. Only one kind of man can read it."

The road was very quiet. Somewhere in the wheat a lark went up, singing, indifferent.

"I don't know anything about ink," Noor said.

It was a bad lie. He heard it be a bad lie even as it left him — flat, too quick, the words of a man reciting rather than speaking. Wren's elbow found his ribs, feather-light, a punctuation mark meaning *amateur.*

The officer, Ser Vaelin, sighed like a disappointed tutor. "No. You wouldn't. Storm-born never do — that's the tragedy of your kind. You were given the sky's own voice and taught nothing but how to scream with it." He dismounted, slowly, joints cracking, and walked toward Noor with his hands clasped behind his back like a man strolling his own gardens. "Do you know what I was doing before this commission, pilgrim? I was governor of a salt province. Boring work. Taxes, tides, disputes about eel-weirs. And then the Crown of Thirteen Moons woke up, after three hundred years of sleeping, and my emperor remembered that the world has edges, and I was sent to its edges to look for—" he stopped, two paces away, and tilted his head, "—*travelers.*"

"We're just—"

"Do you know what happens to a storm-caller in sun-court territory?" Ser Vaelin asked, conversational, almost kind. "Nothing, usually. We're not barbarians. But there are laws older than courtesy. A storm-caller who cannot prove allegiance is a weapon unregistered. And unregistered weapons are confiscated." He glanced back at the Quillmaster. "Or registered. Registration requires... cooperation."

There it was. Noor felt the shape of it settle over him, familiar as an old coat that had never once fit: *not kill, but keep. Not destroy, but use.* The storm-born had wanted him to be their fury. The sun-court wanted him to be their instrument. Between the two of them, they had divided the whole of what he was, and neither side had ever once asked what he wanted, because instruments do not want.

"Lord," Wren said, and her voice cracked just enough, artistry in the crack, "my brother's simple. He's got a fever of the mind since childhood, he doesn't understand half of what folk say to him—"

"Your brother," Ser Vaelin said, without looking at her, "has the hands of a storm-caller and the eyes of a man reading a battlefield right now. Simple brothers don't count exits." He turned fully to Noor at last, and lowered his voice, and the performance dropped away like a mask slipping — and what was underneath was worse, because it was sincere. "Boy. I am going to say a thing once, plainly, because I am tired and it is hot and I have buried enough clever men this season. We know a storm-caller carries the map. We know the map moves. We know the thirteenth moon wanes, and when it turns full, the Crown can be taken — by whoever reaches it first. My emperor wants it reached by *us*. You want—" he gestured vaguely at Noor's worn boots, his patched coat, the whole visible poverty of him, "—apparently nothing much. So here is the arithmetic. Come with us. Read the map for us. Find the Crown for the sun-court, and be paid in whatever currency a man like you values. Land. Pardon. A name that isn't hunted." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "Refuse, and I arrest you as an unregistered weapon, and the Quillmaster asks his questions, and the Quillmaster's questions have a way of going on for years."

The lark was still singing. Noor found that obscene, somehow, and held onto the obscenity because it kept him steady.

*Everyone wants the same thing,* he thought. *The map. The Crown. Me, as the key that turns the lock. Nobody wants the man holding the key.*

He thought of the dying courier, the weight of him against the doorframe, the blood going tacky between their hands. *Tell my sister,* the man had rasped, and then, instead, had pressed the map into a stranger's grip and whispered a name — *Ravensworth* — and died with his father's name in his mouth like a curse or a blessing, Noor still didn't know which.

His father. Who had sent a courier out into a world full of men like Ser Vaelin. Who had known, apparently, what was coming. What had the old man set in motion? What had he been protecting, all those silent years, that it required couriers and storm-script and death?

"I can't read any map," Noor said again, and this time he made it slow, made it dull, made his eyes go slightly unfocused the way Wren had coached him on the second night of their road. "Ink scares me. Letters swim. My sister does the reading."

Ser Vaelin studied him for a long moment. The colorless eyes were patient as stone under rain.

"Yes," the officer said finally. "I imagine she does." He turned. "Quillmaster. The sister travels with us as well. Guests of the crown." The word *guests* was pronounced with the exact intonation of *hostages*, and everyone present understood it, including, presumably, the horses.

Wren's hand found Noor's sleeve — a warning, or a comfort, or both. Under her breath, barely a breath at all: "Saltmarsh is two days south. Smugglers' country. Sun-court writ dies at the waterline."

He almost smiled. Even now, even here, she was counting exits aloud, telling him the walls had doors.

They bound his wrists anyway. Soft cord, almost courteous, the kind used on honored prisoners — but cord is cord, and the Quillmaster tied the knots himself, and his fingers lingered on Noor's bare palm a moment longer than knots require, reading it like scripture.

"Thirteen moons," the Quillmaster murmured, so quietly only Noor could hear, "and the sky keeps its own ledger, storm-caller. Whatever you're pretending to be — the sky knows."

They put him on a horse. They put Wren on another, close enough that their stirrups nearly touched. And the column turned south, twelve lances and two captives and one smiling officer, moving through the golden wheat like a blade drawn slowly through bread.

Noor rode with his bound hands in his lap and catalogued, because cataloguing was all he had left. Six hours to the river crossing. Two days, if Wren's geography held, to the saltmarsh. Twelve soldiers, one of whom — the youngest, the one who kept glancing at the horizon with longing rather than vigilance — would sleep badly tonight and wake later than the rest.

He did not know yet what he would do with that knowledge. But he folded it away carefully, the way his father had folded maps, and waited for the sun to move.

Behind them, far to the north, a cloud was building over the mountains — tall, white-shouldered, utterly ordinary.

Noor watched it for a long time. It watched him back, in the way that clouds sometimes seemed to, and he could not hear it, could not feel it, could not call so much as a shiver of wind — and yet something in his chest leaned toward it like a dog toward a whistled name.

Two days to the saltmarsh. If Wren was right. If the walls had doors.

The column rode south, and the thirteenth moon's shadow lengthened across the road ahead of them, though the moon itself would not rise for hours yet.
