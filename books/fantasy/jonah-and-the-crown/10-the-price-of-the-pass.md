## 10. The Price of the Pass

The rebels gave them until moonrise.

Wren knew it the way she knew weather in her bones — not from any announcement, but from the small, telling cruelties of men who had already decided. The storm-born camp on the shoulder of the pass had stopped pretending to be hospitable around midday. The stew they'd been offered at dawn was not repeated. Their escorts, once loose-jawed and curious, now stood in pairs with their spears unslung, watching Noor the way farmers watch a sky that has gone the wrong color. And their leader — a broad woman with lightning-scarred cheeks and a voice like gravel rolled in honey, called Sera Vane by everyone, addressed as nothing by anyone — had smiled at Noor over the cookfire and said, *Sleep well, storm-caller. Tomorrow you walk with us.*

Not a request. Wren had heard enough requests in her life to know the difference.

Now she sat apart from the fire, in the lee of a fallen pine, with her brother's satchel in her lap and her hands flat upon it like a woman keeping a lid on something boiling. Around her the camp murmured and clinked. Above the pass the peaks held the last light, and somewhere beyond them, south and east and down, lay the drowned cathedral and everything her brother had died carrying toward.

She opened the satchel.

Tam's letters were wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, tied with the frayed blue ribbon their mother had used for sewing kits and, in the end, for Tam's hair when they laid him out. Wren had read them so many times the folds had gone soft as cloth. There were eleven. She counted them again anyway, the way you touch your own teeth after a blow to the jaw — not because the count might change, but because counting is what hands do when the heart is running.

Eleven letters. Ten of them ordinary: complaints about roads, jokes about a mule named Bishop, dry observations about the sun-court's tax collectors that would have gotten him hanged if anyone official had ever bothered to read them. Wren could recite them. She had, some nights, walking alone on watch, just to hear his voice.

The eleventh was different.

She didn't take it out. She only pressed her palm against the place where it sat, third from the bottom, and felt its shape through the oilcloth — thinner than the others, written on better paper, sealed twice. Tam had told her about it once, drunk on plum wine in a room above a stable in Corrow Ford, laughing and then not laughing. *If anything happens to me, Wrennie, burn that one. Don't read it. Burn it.* He'd said it like a joke. Then he'd said, quieter, *There are names in it that get people killed. Good people. Our name.*

Their family name had been stolen nine years ago — struck from the guild rolls of Corrow Ford on a magistrate's whim and a rival's bribe, so that the Ashvales went from respected cartographers to persons of no account in a single afternoon. Tam had spent every year since trying to buy the name back. That was why he carried maps for a man like Ravensworth. That was why he'd been crossing war-country alone with a bloodstained chart sewn into his coat. Not loyalty. Hope. The desperate, expensive hope that one great delivery could purchase back everything a signature had erased.

And the eleventh letter — Wren had broken her promise within a week of his death, because grief makes liars of everyone and she had never been good at obedience — the eleventh letter said, in a hand she recognized from the map itself:

*To the keeper's son, should the keeper fall: your father did not hide the Crown from the world. He hid the world from the Crown. I was his courier because I asked to be, knowing what I carried. If you read this, then the seal weakens, and someone must choose again. Do not let them choose for you.*

Signed, in ink gone brown: *Aldous Ravensworth, Keeper of Thirteen Moons.*

Noor's father. Noor's father had kept the thing. Had hidden it. Had died — or worse, had simply stopped being findable — rather than use it. And his son was walking beside her right now, twenty paces away across the firelight, sitting up straight and sleepless because he could feel the rebels' intention the way animals feel earthquakes, and he did not know. He did not know that the map in his coat was drawn by his own father's hand, or that the woman who had promised to guide him had known for three weeks and said nothing.

Wren closed her eyes.

*You're protecting him,* said the voice in her head that always sounded reasonable, that always sounded a little like Tam. *He's a boy playing at a man's errand. If he knew his father kept the Crown, he'd want to understand it. He'd start asking questions. Questions travel. Questions reach ears. And there are two armies between here and Vaelmoor who would peel him open like fruit to hear the answers.*

*Or,* said the other voice, the one that sounded like her mother, *you're protecting yourself. Because if he knows you knew, he'll never forgive you, and you've grown accustomed to having him beside you, and you are ashamed of how much you need that.*

She hated that voice. She hated more that it wasn't wrong.

* * *

Sera Vane found her an hour before moonrise.

Wren heard her coming — the woman walked like weather too, all pressure and no wasted motion — and had the satchel's strap back over her shoulder and her face arranged into wary neutrality before the rebel leader rounded the pine.

"Can't sleep either?" Vane said. She lowered herself onto the fallen trunk without invitation, close enough that Wren could smell woodsmoke and iron on her. Up close, the lightning scars on her cheeks caught the firelight and gleamed faintly, like rivers seen from very high up. "Your storm-caller sits very straight by that fire. Men who sit straight are listening to something."

"He's not my storm-caller."

"No." Vane studied her with eyes the gray of old ice. "He isn't, is he? You're the sister of the courier. The dead one. Ravensworth's man." She said the surname lightly, tasting it. "You know, when my scouts brought word that a storm-caller had come down out of Ashenmere with a woman and a chart, I thought: the son. Old Aldous's boy, come home at last. The cause has waited nine years for that boy."

Wren kept her breathing even. "And when you met him?"

"When I met him," Vane said, "I saw a young man flinching from his own shadow, wearing power like a coat two sizes too large. And I thought: *of course*. Of course Aldous hid him in a valley where magic dies. That's what keepers do. They bury the dangerous things and call it love." She turned her head, and the firelight moved across her scars. "You've read his letters, haven't you. Your brother's."

Wren said nothing.

"You may as well admit it. I searched Tam Ashvale's effects when we took him off the road at Fennick's Bridge. He was dying; he made it hard; I respect that. But I found the satchel empty and the map gone, and a man doesn't give away his most precious cargo to strangers." Her voice stayed pleasant, which was somehow worse than menace. "So here is my offer, cartographer's daughter, since you strike me as a woman who prefers honest transactions to whatever theater we'd otherwise perform."

"Say it."

"The pass south of here is watched. Sun-court outriders, forty strong, holding the narrows since the moon began to wane. My people can get your storm-caller past them — through the old miner's cut, guided, provisioned. Without us, he walks into those outriders with a glowing piece of parchment in his coat and dies stupidly inside a week." Vane picked up a twig, snapped it, dropped the halves. "In exchange, I want the letters. All of them. Tam Ashvale wrote to Aldous Ravensworth for six years; those letters are the only record outside the keeper's own head of where the Crown sleeps and how the seal works. My cause needs them more than you need souvenirs of a dead man."

"They're mine," Wren said. "They're all I have of him."

"I know." For a moment — a single, unguarded moment — something moved behind the ice-gray eyes, something old and tired. "I lost brothers too. More than I can put a number to without drinking first. So I'll tell you plainly, woman to woman: I will take them if I must. But I'd rather you gave them. A gift buys passage. A theft buys a knife in the dark, someday, from someone like you."

Wren looked across the fire. Noor sat exactly as Vane had described — spine straight, hands still, staring into the flames as though the flames might answer a question he hadn't dared ask aloud. Twenty paces. She had crossed a kingdom at this man's side. She had slept back-to-back with him in ditches. She had lied to him every single day of it, and each lie had weighed less than the last, which frightened her more than any army.

*Protecting him means controlling him,* said Tam's voice, smooth as plum wine. *It always has. It always will. Choose.*

"What guarantee," Wren said, "that you see us safely past the outriders?"

"My word."

"Your word is worth what any rebel's word is worth."

Vane laughed — a short, genuine bark that startled a nearby sentry. "Fair. Then collateral: I send my own nephew with you. Dain. He's seventeen and insufferable and he worships me, which means he'll die before he fails me. If we betray you, you kill the boy, and I live with that forever. Better?" She stood, brushing pine needles from her coat. "Moonrise soon. Decide."

Wren decided.

Later she would try to remember doing it — try to locate the exact moment the ribbon came untied, the moment her hands lifted the oilcloth bundle and held it out — but memory refused to cooperate. It presented the transaction instead as something that happened to her body while she watched from a distance: her arms extending, Vane's scarred fingers closing around the letters, the weight leaving her hands so completely and so finally that her fingers curled shut around the absence and stayed that way.

"The thin one," Wren heard herself say. "Third from the bottom. It's — it's just accounting notes. Keep it or burn it, I don't care."

The lie came out smooth as river stone. That was the worst part. Not the giving — grief had already taught her she could survive giving things away. The worst part was discovering how easily her mouth now produced falsehoods, how practiced the muscle had become, how little it cost. Three weeks ago she had been a woman who told the truth badly and often. Now lies fit her like gloves.

Vane weighed the bundle in one hand, and her gaze lingered on Wren's face a beat too long — long enough that Wren understood, with cold certainty, that the rebel leader knew exactly which letter was which, and had perhaps known all along, and had simply wanted to watch whether Wren would lie.

"Third from the bottom," Vane repeated mildly. "Accounting notes." She tucked the bundle inside her coat, over her heart, which Wren suspected was where the woman kept everything she intended to weaponize. "Dain will wake you both an hour before dawn. Sleep, Ashvale. You've bought your road."

She walked back toward the fire, toward Noor, and Wren watched her go and felt the guilt arrive on schedule — not all at once but in installments, the way pain does, the way debt does. First the hands, still curled around nothing. Then the throat. Then behind the eyes, hot and pressurized, refusing quite to become tears because tears would mean admitting what the tears were for.

*Burn it,* Tam had said. *Don't read it. Burn it.* She had neither burned it nor given it freely. She had traded her brother's last words for a mountain pass and then lied about which words they were, and somewhere south of here, in a drowned cathedral under a waning thirteenth moon, a reckoning was already walking toward her wearing Noor Ravensworth's face.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She packed.

By the time the eastern sky went the gray of dirty wool, Wren had repacked both their bags twice, eaten nothing, and memorized the shape of her own guilt so thoroughly she could navigate by it. When Dain came — a lanky, freckled boy with his aunt's gray eyes and none of her gravity, chattering about the miner's cut and the quality of rebel hardtack — she woke Noor with a hand on his shoulder and watched him surface from dreams she suspected were not kind.

They left the camp in the blue hour, single file, Dain leading. The pass swallowed them. Behind them, faintly, she heard Sera Vane's voice giving orders, and beneath it, imagined or real, the sound of paper unfolding.

Noor fell into step beside her as the trail narrowed. He looked terrible — hollowed, wrung out, the way he always looked after a night among the storm-born, as though their hunger for him leaked through his skin. But he walked, and he kept pace, and after a mile of switchbacks he spoke without looking at her.

"She took them, didn't she. The letters."

Wren's heart performed a slow, deliberate stumble. "How—"

"I saw her reading something by the fire, after. She reads the way my father taught me to read charts — left to right, top to bottom, like a man defusing something." He still wasn't looking at her. The path dropped steeply ahead, down into cloud. "It doesn't matter. They were yours to trade. I'd have done the same."

*Would you,* Wren thought. *If you knew which letter she's holding right now. If you knew what it says about your father. About you.*

"She offered the pass," Wren said aloud, and her voice came out steady, and she marveled at herself, at the craftsmanship of it. "Outriders hold the narrows. We'd have died on the road without her people."

"I know. I heard." He paused at the trail's edge where the clouds began, and for a moment the wind coming up from below moved through his hair and around his hands, and Wren saw — actually saw — the air respond to him, a faint pressure, a gathering, like the sky leaning closer to listen. Then he mastered it, or it mastered him, and he exhaled. "One thing, Wren. Before we go down into whatever's down there."

"Anything."

"If there's something in those letters about my father — about what he was, what he did — I'd want to hear it from you. Not from an army. Not from a rebel with scars on her face reading my family's mail by firelight." He turned and looked at her then, fully, and his eyes in the pre-dawn gray were the exact color of the sky before hail. "Whatever it costs you to tell me. I'd rather pay it with you than learn it alone."

The clouds waited below like a held breath. Somewhere in them, forty sun-court outriders held the narrows, and a drowned cathedral held a crown, and a dead man's letter held a sentence that would unmake everything she had built between them.

Wren smiled — she felt it happen, felt the muscles obey, another glove fitting perfectly — and said, "It's accounting notes, Noor. Nothing about your father. I promise."

He held her gaze a moment longer. Then he nodded, once, and stepped down into the cloud, and she followed, and the mountain closed over them both.

*Promise,* said her mother's voice, one last time, from very far away.

The thirteenth moon, fat and low above the southern peaks, watched them descend, and waned.
