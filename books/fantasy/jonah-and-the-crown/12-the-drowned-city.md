## 12. The Drowned City

The tide began to fall an hour before dawn, and with it, the sea gave up its dead.

Noor stood on the shingle above Vaelmoor and watched the water withdraw like a sheet being pulled from a corpse. It did not retreat so much as recede — reluctantly, in long silver sheets that left the mud gleaming, and beneath the mud, shapes. A spire first, black and crooked as a burnt finger. Then a wall. Then rooftops, dozens of them, their tiles furred with weed and barnacle, emerging one by one from the grey water as though the city were surfacing to breathe.

"Gods," Wren said softly beside him. She had her hood up against the sea-wind, and her knuckles were white around the strap of her pack. "It's bigger than I thought."

"It's not bigger," Noor said. "It's taller. You're only seeing what the water hasn't taken." He had read about Vaelmoor in his father's books, back when there had been books, back when there had been a father who kept them. *The sea took it in a single night,* one account claimed. *The bells rang until the water closed over them, and some say they never stopped.* He had thought, as a boy, that this was a story meant to frighten children away from the shore.

Now he stood on the shore, and the map in his breast pocket was warm against his skin — warmer than it should have been, warmer than blood or body heat could explain — and the ink beneath the leather was shifting again, crawling into new configurations every time he glanced down at it, as if the map itself were eager.

"The thirteenth moon rises tomorrow night," Wren said. "Low tide won't come again for a month after this one. It's now or never."

"It's always now or never with you."

"One of us has to say it." She looked at him sideways, and in the pre-dawn greyness her face was all sharp planes and exhaustion. They had been running for days — from the sun-court soldiers, from the storm-born rebels, from the converging roads that carried both armies toward this same drowned place. Her brother's letters were gone, traded away in a bargain she still hadn't explained fully, and something in her had closed since then, a door pulled shut in a house he'd thought he knew. "Are you ready?"

No one had asked him that in eleven years. In Ashenmere, readiness was irrelevant; nothing happened there, and no one needed you to be anything. *I don't know* would have been the honest answer. What he said instead was: "The tide's falling. That's enough."

They went down onto the mud.

* * *

Walking through Vaelmoor was like walking through the inside of a throat.

The mud sucked at their boots with every step, thick and grey-brown and stinking of rot and salt, and everywhere around them rose the architecture of the drowned city — streets become canyons between walls crusted white with salt-bloom, doorways opening onto darkness filled with drifting silt, staircases climbing out of the muck toward windows that hadn't seen sky in eighty years. Fish moved through the upper stories. On a balcony overhead, a fishing net hung draped like a funeral shroud, though whether some fisherman had left it or the sea itself had woven it there, Noor couldn't say.

And the bells.

He heard the first one before he saw it — a low, vast tone rolling through the waterlogged air, so deep he felt it in his teeth before his ears caught it. He stopped. Wren stopped a few paces ahead, turning. "What?"

"You didn't hear that?"

"Hear what?" She listened. The wind moved through the broken streets; somewhere distant, a gull cried. "There's nothing, Noor."

But there was. The sound came again, and this time he traced it: a cathedral tower rising from the mud perhaps two hundred yards east, its belfry open to the sky, and inside it a bell the size of a house, green-black with age, hanging motionless in its frame. Motionless — and yet ringing. Not with sound exactly. With something older than sound. The bell was singing the way a struck glass sings, the way a wire sings when the wind finds its note, and the note it had found was *him.*

His magic answered before he could stop it.

It rose in him like floodwater — sudden, total, the way it had risen at the ridgeline crossing weeks ago, except that then it had been a trickle compared to this. Every drop of power he had spent years strangling out of himself came awake at once, reaching toward the bell, resonating with it, and the bell resonated back, and between them the air began to tremble. Salt spray lifted off the mud in fine veils. The puddles in the sunken street shivered into rings.

"Noor." Wren had grabbed his arm. Her grip was iron. "*Noor.*"

"I can't—" His voice came out strange to him, doubled, as though someone else were speaking through his mouth. "It knows me. Wren, it *knows me.*"

"Then make it stop knowing!"

He didn't know how. That was the truth he had never spoken aloud, not to her, not to anyone, maybe not even honestly to himself: that he had never learned to control his magic, only to suppress it, and suppression was not control any more than drowning is swimming. For eleven years in Ashenmere the valley had done the work for him. Magic died at the ridgeline; therefore magic stayed dead; therefore Noor Ravensworth could be a goatherd, a quiet man, a harmless man. He had called that peace. Standing in the drowned streets of Vaelmoor with a dead bell singing his name, he understood at last that it had never been peace at all.

It had been a tourniquet.

The memory came the way the tide came — not all at once but in waves, each one higher than the last.

He was nine years old. There had been a storm coming in off the coast for three days, a great slow bruise of cloud that the village elders said would pass them by, the way storms always passed them by. And Noor had climbed the watchtower because he wanted to see it, because even then the sky called to him the way it called to him now, and from the top of the tower he had done the thing that storm-born children do without being taught, the thing that came as naturally as flinching from a flame: he had reached into the storm and *pulled.*

He hadn't meant to pull hard. He hadn't known there was a hard. The storm had been a living thing above him, vast and wheeling, and he had touched it the way you touch a horse's neck, and it had turned its whole attention on him — a child on a tower with his hands full of lightning — and it had *obeyed.*

What happened next took four seconds. He had counted them afterward, in the years of sleeplessness, in the long grey hours before dawn when the counting was the only prayer available to him. One: the clouds broke open directly over the village. Two: the rain came down like a verdict. Three: the lightning — not one bolt but a web of them, a lattice of white fire descending on the houses below the tower. Four: silence, and under the silence, screaming.

Seventeen people. He had learned the number later, from a survivor who spat it at him across a courtroom. Seventeen, including the miller's twin daughters, including old Maren who had once given him honeycakes, including — though the court records listed it separately, as though the category made it worse — his own aunt and cousins, who had been visiting from the coast.

They had not executed him. He was nine, and storm-born, and killing a storm-born child was a political act, and no one wanted the political act. So they exiled him instead, which was execution stretched thin across a lifetime. *The valley of Ashenmere,* the magistrate had said. *Where your kind cannot follow. Where you will harm no one ever again.* And everyone had nodded as though this were mercy, and his father had signed the order with a hand that did not shake, and Noor had been taken through the pass in a cart with his face covered, and the valley had closed behind him like a door.

Eleven years. Eleven years of goats and quiet and the particular numbness of a man who has decided he deserves nothing, which is a decision that looks, from the outside, exactly like contentment. He had believed — he had needed to believe — that the valley had healed him. That the absence of magic was a bandage, and under the bandage the wound was closing.

The bell sang, and the wound opened, and he saw that it had never closed at all. It had only been pressed shut, year after year, by the weight of a valley that forgot things. That was what Ashenmere did — that was its gift and its cruelty both. It didn't cure you. It made you forget you were sick, and forgetting is not the same as healing, and someday the tide goes out and shows you everything the water took.

"Noor!" Wren was shaking him with both hands now, her face inches from his, and her voice cut through the resonance like a knife through rope. "Look at me. Look at *me.* Breathe. You're doing the thing where you leave. Come back."

He came back. Slowly, painfully, the way a man surfaces from deep water — and as he surfaced, he did the only thing he could think to do, the thing his body remembered from a childhood of clamping down: he pushed the magic *down*, hard, all of it, cramming it back into whatever sealed room he kept it in. The bell's note bent, wavered, and faded. The trembling air went still. Spray drifted down out of nowhere, settling on their shoulders like ash.

Wren held his arms until his legs stopped shaking.

"What was that," she said. Not a question. An accusation wearing a question's clothes.

"A bell," he said. "A bell that remembers."

"Bells don't remember."

"This one does. This whole city does." He looked past her, down the flooded avenue, and felt the words rise in him with terrible certainty. "Vaelmoor didn't drown in a night, Wren. It drowned in a war. My war. Our war — the storm-born and the sun-courts, the one they keep saying ended. The sea didn't take this city. *We* did. Someone called the ocean up over it, or someone called a storm down into it, and either way—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Either way, I know what that feels like. From the inside."

Wren was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had lost its edge, which frightened him more than the edge ever had. "Your storm. The one they exiled you for. Was it like this? Here?"

"Worse. Smaller, but worse." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Seventeen people, Wren. I was nine years old, and I killed seventeen people, and my own father signed the order that sent me away, and I have spent eleven years telling myself the valley made me safe. Made everyone safe. From me." He looked up at the cathedral tower, at the motionless bell in its open belfry, and his chest ached with something that was not quite grief and not quite longing. "But it wasn't safety. It was just... a room with the door held shut. And now the door's open, and everything I buried is standing in the doorway asking to be let in."

Wren did something he did not expect. She stepped forward and put her forehead against his chest, briefly, hard, the way you might lean against a door to hold it closed. Then she stepped back and became practical again, brisk, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand as though the gesture were merely clearing salt spray.

"Then we finish this," she said. "We find the Crown, we do whatever your map says to do, and then you never have to open that door again. Whatever it takes."

*Whatever it takes.* He heard the words and felt them snag on something — the shape of her silence these past days, the letters traded away, the door pulled shut inside her — but the bell was still humming faintly in his bones, and his hands were still unsteady, and he was too tired and too hollowed-out to press. Later, he told himself. There would be time for later.

"Come on," he said. "The map wants the cathedral."

* * *

They waded the last quarter-mile through water that rose from ankle to knee to waist, cold as a grave, clear as grief. The cathedral grew around them as they approached — buttresses like ribs, doors thrown wide, the whole vast skeleton of it rising out of the sea with its nave flooded to the height of a man's chest. Inside, light fell through the shattered roof in pale columns, and in those columns drifted things that might have been fish and might have been souls.

The map was burning against his chest now. Literally — he could feel heat through the oilcloth, and when he finally drew it out, holding it above the waterline, the storm-script was glowing a soft furious blue, the lines rearranging themselves even as he watched, drawing him onward. Down the nave. Past the drowned pews, their wood bleached bone-white. Toward the far end, where the altar should have been.

And the bells rang for him the whole way.

Not one bell now but many — he could feel them all through the water, through the stone, through the soles of his boots, a chord sustained across eighty years, and every note in the chord was tuned to him. To his blood. To the storm-caller's inheritance that ran in his veins like a second pulse. *You are one of ours,* the bells seemed to say. *You are the storm's child, and the storm remembers what was done here, and the storm has been waiting.*

Waiting for what?

He was afraid he knew. He was afraid the answer was *you.*

At the foot of the altar steps, where the water was deepest and darkest, the map's glow suddenly shifted — the lines collapsing inward, spiraling, pointing *down*. Beneath the cathedral. Beneath the mud and the bones and the eighty years of patient sea.

"There's a vault," Noor said. His voice echoed strangely in the flooded nave, doubled by the bells' resonance, so that it sounded as though the drowned city itself were speaking. "Under the altar. The map ends here — or rather, it starts here. Everything else was just directions to this one spot."

Wren stared at the dark water at the base of the steps. Her reflection looked back at her, wavering. "Then we've found it," she whispered. "After everything. We've actually found it."

Above them, through the broken roof, the sky was beginning to change. The thirteenth moon would rise tonight — the last night, the map had promised, the final turn before the seal weakened past saving. And far to the south, Noor could feel it now, the way you feel weather coming in your bad tooth: armies. Sun-courts and storm-born both, marching on the same drowned city, drawn by the same dying star of a moon.

The bells rang on, patient as the tide.

And beneath the water at the altar's foot, something answered — a faint, cold light, rising.
