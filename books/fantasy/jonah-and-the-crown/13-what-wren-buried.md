## 13. What Wren Buried

The light beneath the altar was not gold. Wren had expected gold — sun-court treasure, storm-born glory, the whole gaudy mythology of the Crown of Thirteen Moons that her brother had carried in his chest like a second heart for three years. But what rose through the black water at the cathedral's foot was cold and silver and patient, a moonlight that had been waiting underwater for a generation, and it made her skin crawl in a way no campfire ghost story ever had.

"Noor." She said his name quietly, because the drowned bells were still ringing somewhere above them, faint as memory, and she did not want to disturb whatever was listening. "Noor, look at it."

He was already looking. He had been looking since they waded into the nave at low tide, since the water gave way to stone steps slick with weed, since the great doors of the antechamber had swung open — or rather, fallen open, their hinges long since surrendered to the sea. His face in the silver light was the face of a man standing at the edge of something he had spent his whole life pretending wasn't there.

"It's answering him," Wren thought, and hated the thought. The light pulsed when he moved. It dimmed when he stood still. Whatever slept beneath the altar knew the blood walking toward it.

They climbed the last steps together, boots squelching, and came out into the antechamber — a smaller vault behind the altar, dry where everything else was drowned, sealed by some craft older than either of their peoples. Salt crust furred the walls like frost. And there, on a stone plinth at the center, sat a coffer of black wood banded in tarnished silver, unremarkable as a merchant's strongbox, except that the air around it hummed with the same note the bells rang.

Noor stopped three paces away. Wren watched him stop — watched the way his hand rose halfway to the coffer and then fell, as if the air itself had refused him.

"Thirteen moons," he said softly. "It's really here."

"You sound surprised."

"I sound afraid. There's a difference." He turned to look at her then, and in the moonlight from the broken roof his eyes were pale as shallow water. "Wren. Before I open anything. Tell me again why your brother died carrying this map. Tell me the truth this time."

The question landed in her stomach like a swallowed stone. Because she had told him truths — carefully selected ones, arranged like flowers in a vase so that the stems never showed. *My brother worked as a courier during the war.* True. *He believed the Crown could restore our family's name.* Also true. *He trusted the man who sent him.* Truest of all, and the one she had wrapped in so many layers of vagueness that Noor had never once seen its face.

"He died doing his job," she said. "That's the truth."

"That's a sentence, not a truth." Noor's voice didn't rise. That was the worst part, later — how quiet he stayed. "You've been circling something since the saltmarsh. Every time I asked about my father, you found something else to look at. The road. The fire. Your knives." He took a breath. "I'm asking you now, with armies marching on this city and the thirteenth moon rising tonight. If you know something about Aldric Ravensworth, tell me before I touch that box."

And Wren — who had lied to customs men and sun-court officers and her own grief for months without her pulse so much as stuttering — found that she could not make her mouth form the lie. Not here. Not with the Crown humming ten feet away and her brother's blood dried somewhere on a road behind them.

So she did the only thing left. She reached into her coat, drew out the oilskin packet she had kept against her ribs since the Road of Hanged Men, and held it out.

Noor stared at it. "What is that?"

"The letters," she said. "The ones I traded to get us past the rebels at the pass."

His face went very still. "You told me you burned them."

"I told you what you needed to hear so we'd both live." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she despised herself for it. "There's one letter I didn't trade. One I couldn't. It's addressed to whoever finds the vault. It has your father's seal."

For a moment Noor didn't move at all. Then he crossed the space between them and took the packet from her hands — took it gently, which was somehow more frightening than if he had torn it — and broke the seal that Wren had last seen on her brother's table two years ago, before everything.

* * *

The letter was written in a firm, careful hand, the ink browned with age but legible, the storm-script flowing beside common tongue in twin columns as though the writer had wanted no ambiguity to survive him. Noor read it aloud, because Wren thinks — later, always later — that he needed to hear it in a voice, needed it to be real in the way things said aloud become real.

*"To the one who reaches this place: know first that I did not come here by accident, and neither will you.*

*"My name is Aldric Ravensworth, and I am — was — the Keeper of the Crown of Thirteen Moons. Not its owner. There are no owners. Only keepers, and only for a time, and the time is paid for in full.*

*"You have been told the Crown grants victory. This is a lie told by people who want soldiers. You have been told it grants peace. This is a lie told by people who want thrones. The Crown is a seal upon the oldest wound in the world — the war between the storm-born and the sun-courts was not ended; it was bound, thirteen moons deep, and the Crown is the knot that holds the binding. Whoever claims it does not win the war. Whoever claims it unbinds it. All of it. Every debt, every death, every hatred our grandparents buried comes back up out of the ground the moment a hand closes around that circlet.*

*"I write this because I am dying, and because I could not carry it further. The seal weakens each turn of the thirteenth moon, and the factions smell the weakness like wolves smell blood. I chose to hide the Crown rather than use it, knowing that hiding would cost me everything — my name, my family, my son. Especially my son. I sent him away to a valley where magic cannot follow, and I let him believe himself exiled, because an exile is safe in ways a keeper's heir can never be. If you are reading this and your name is Noor — I am sorry. I chose your safety over your forgiveness, and I would choose it again, though God knows it has cost me every night since.*

*"There is one way to reseal the Crown once it is claimed: a keeper must give it back willingly, with his life. That is the price written into the binding. I could not pay it and leave my son alone in the world, so I hid instead, and hid, and hid, until hiding killed me on a road far from home with a courier's hand holding mine.*

*"If you hold this letter, the choice is yours now. Claim it and unmake the world's long truce. Or find another way. Or run, as I ran, and let someone else's son read this letter someday.*

*"Forgive me. Or don't. But do not say no one warned you.*

*"— A.R., Keeper, last of his line, hoping to be the last of any line ever to bear it."*

* * *

The silence afterward lasted longer than any silence Wren had ever endured. The bells rang above them. Water dripped somewhere in the dark of the nave. And Noor stood holding his father's handwriting in both hands, reading it a second time, a third, as though the words might rearrange themselves into something kinder.

"He was the Keeper," Noor said finally. His voice sounded scraped hollow. "All this time. All those years I hated him for exiling me — for the valley, for the silence, for leaving me nothing but a name and a curse —"

"Noor —"

"He was protecting me." The letter trembled in his grip. "He was dying alone on a road to keep this thing buried, and I was tending goats and calling myself abandoned." Then his head came up, and the pale eyes found hers, and everything in them had gone cold and clear as deep water. "And you knew."

"I didn't know all of it —"

"You knew he was the Keeper." He held up the letter. "This is dated two years old, Wren. Tam has carried it since the day my father died. You've known since the saltmarsh — maybe since the beginning — that my father kept the Crown, that he chose exile for me, that the Crown isn't a prize at all. And you let me walk across half the world believing we were chasing salvation."

"I let you walk toward it because the alternative was letting you die ignorant!" Her own voice broke loose now, all the guilt curdling into anger because anger was easier to carry. "Yes. I knew. Tam told me everything before he died — the Keeper, the seal, the sacrifice, all of it. And I made a choice, same as your father made a choice. I decided that if you knew the Crown could only be resealed by dying, you'd either refuse to come — and the armies would find it anyway, and the war would come to Ashenmere, to the valley, to every place like it — or you'd come and throw yourself into that box the first chance you got. So I lied. I lied to keep you alive long enough to reach this room and see it with your own eyes, because I thought — I *hoped* — that seeing it would change the arithmetic."

"And did it?" Noor stepped toward her, and the silver light pulsed brighter with each step, as though feeding on his fury. "Has it changed your arithmetic? Because here's mine: everyone I have ever trusted has decided what I'm allowed to know. My father. The storm-born. The sun-courts. And you. You're all the same. You look at me and you don't see a person — you see a key, a weapon, a keeper, a sacrifice. Something to be managed."

"That's not true."

"Isn't it? You bargained away Tam's letters to save our skins and never told me. You kept this—" he shook the letter "—against your ribs for weeks, next to your heart, and never once reached for it. You controlled me, Wren. Kindly. Carefully. The way you'd gentle a horse you needed to ride."

The words landed harder than any blow she had taken on the road, because they were true, or true enough, and the truest wounds are the ones you cannot parry. Wren stood in the humming dark with her brother's killer-map and her own lies piled around her like the drowned city's stones, and understood that she had done exactly what she had sworn never to do: become another person deciding Noor Ravensworth's life for him.

"I was trying to protect you," she said, and heard how small it sounded.

"I know." And that was the cruelest thing — that he did know, that his fury had no cruelty in it, only exhaustion. "Everyone's always trying to protect me. My father protected me into exile. The rebels tried to protect their cause with me. You protected me straight into the one room in the world I should never have entered." He looked down at the letter one last time, folded it along his father's creases with terrible care, and tucked it inside his coat, over his heart, where hers had lived. "From this moment, you don't decide anything for me. Not one thing. Do you understand?"

"Yes," Wren said.

"Good." He turned toward the coffer on its plinth, and the light rose to meet him like a tide coming in. "Because the thirteenth moon rises in hours, and the armies will be here by dark, and I have a decision to make that apparently my father couldn't make and my mother's people couldn't make and no one in a hundred years has survived making."

Wren's hand went to her knife out of pure habit, then fell away. There was nothing left to cut. "Noor. Wait. Whatever you're going to do —"

"What I'm going to do," he said quietly, "is think. Alone. For the first time in my life, with the whole truth in front of me." He glanced back at her once, and the look was worse than anger — it was the look of a man closing a door. "Go watch the waterline. Tell me when the tide turns."

She went. What else was there to do?

* * *

From the antechamber's threshold Wren watched him stand before the coffer, a lone figure in the silver gloom, his father's letter over his heart and thirteen moons' worth of borrowed power singing in the stone around him. Above, through the broken roof, the sky was bruising toward evening, and far off — closer than it had been that morning — she could hear it now, faint under the bells: drums. Two sets of them, converging. Sun-courts from the south. Storm-born from the north.

The tide was turning. Within the hour, the sea would begin crawling back up the streets of Vaelmoor, and with it, whatever came riding on it.

And Noor Ravensworth, last son of the last Keeper, reached out his hand toward the black-wood coffer — and paused, fingers trembling in the cold light, as somewhere overhead the first bell of the thirteenth moon began, very slowly, to ring.
