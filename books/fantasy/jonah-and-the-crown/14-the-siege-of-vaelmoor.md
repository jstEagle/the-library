## 14. The Siege of Vaelmoor

The sea gave them one hour of low tide, and the armies spent it like coin.

Wren heard the sun-court horns first — three long notes, silver-bright, the sound her brother had once described to her as *the sound of being owned* — and then, rolling in from the west where the drowned causeway broke the waterline, the storm-born answer: no horn at all, only thunder with no cloud behind it, a drumbeat made of pressure and old anger.

She stood on the roof of what had been a counting-house two hundred years ago, its slate bones furred with salt-crust and barnacle, and watched two wars arrive at once.

The sun-courts came down the eastern road in column, gold-and-white banners snapping in the wind off the water, their vanguard wading already through the shin-deep flood that covered the lower streets of Vaelmoor. Their armor caught the light of the setting thirteenth moon — nearly full now, swollen and wrong-colored, a bruised pearl hanging over the drowned city — and threw it back in fragments. Behind them came the war-priests in their gilded cages, carried on the shoulders of soldiers so they would not sully their feet with seawater.

And from the west, the storm-born came like weather. No columns. No banners. They moved through the flooded streets the way water moves through a broken hull, silent until they weren't, and when the first lightning walked across the rooftops ahead of them — blue-white, hungry, answering some call Wren couldn't hear — she understood that they had brought callers with them. Real ones. Not exiles hiding in goat valleys. The kind who had spent the whole long peace sharpening themselves for exactly this morning.

*They're going to kill each other over a room,* she thought. *A room under a church at the bottom of the sea.*

And somewhere inside that room, or close to it, was Noor.

She had lost him in the antechamber — in the white-hot moment after he'd read the letter she'd buried, after his face had done something she wasn't ready to look at directly, after he'd said her name in a voice she'd never heard him use before, flat and cold as a frozen lake, and then the horns had sounded and the world had come apart. She'd been shoved one way by fleeing smugglers; he'd gone another, toward the vault stairs, alone, and she had let him go because there had been no time to do anything else and because — she could admit it now, in the privacy of her own skull, where lying was harder — some cowardly part of her had been glad of the interruption.

He knew. He knew what she'd traded away, and why, and what it meant about his father, and she had no speech prepared for it. She never had. That was the whole shape of her life: she acted first and explained never, and she had told herself it was strength right up until the moment it cost her the only person left whose opinion mattered.

Now the cost was coming due with trumpets.

* * *

The first true clash happened below her, in the Street of Chandeliers — so named, according to Tam, because the glassblowers' guild had once hung a thousand lamps across it every midwinter. The lamps were gone. The street was a canal of grey-green water, hip-deep, littered with the skeletons of boats, and into it the sun-court vanguard marched in perfect formation while the storm-born poured in from the flanking alleys like ink dropped in milk.

What followed was not a battle so much as a drowning with weapons.

Lightning hit the flooded street and the water itself became the weapon — Wren saw an entire rank of gold-armored soldiers convulse and drop at once, thirty men folded by a single strike, and the scream that went up was not courage or fury but simple animal agony, and it was answered from the sun-court lines by something worse: fire-priests, their hands wrapped in white gauze, hurling flame that did not flicker even where it struck the sea. Steam exploded upward in blinding clouds. The air turned to scalding fog. Somewhere a horse was shrieking, and somewhere else a storm-born woman was laughing as she fought, high and wild, and Wren pressed herself flat against the counting-house roof and thought of Tam's letters, all of them describing this, all of them written in his careful courier's hand: *the courts are rehearsing, sister. They have been rehearsing for sixty years. When it comes, it will come fast, and it will look exactly like the stories, and the stories were always lies.*

He'd been right. He'd been right about everything except the part where he lived through it.

She got off the roof the way she'd gotten onto it — by routes no soldier would think to watch, the smugglers' ways, the gaps and gutters and drowned window-frames that Tam had mapped for her in childhood letters because he'd always believed she'd need them someday. She dropped into an alley above the floodline, waded to her thighs through water the color of tarnished pewter, and started moving east, toward the cathedral, toward the vault, toward —

Toward which thing? That was the question, and it got heavier with every step.

Because the Crown was *right there*. That was the obscene truth of it. After all the miles, all the marshes and passes and hanged-men roads, after all of Tam's blood dried into a map she could no longer read — the vault was beneath her feet, reachable, and if she reached it first, if she took the Crown and ran, she could sell it. To either side. Both sides were bidding already; she'd heard it in the last hour like weather reports. A sun-court captain had grabbed her by the collar in the fog and said, *woman, if you know the way to the relic, name your price, the court will pay it in land,* and later a storm-born runner with frost in her eyelashes had pressed a token into her hand and said, *bring it to us and your family's name is restored, sworn by the Moot itself.* Her family's name. Ravensworth had taken everything from the Zhou line — lands, title, honor, Tam's whole future — and here were both great powers falling over themselves to offer it back, like coins tossed to a beggar they needed to carry something for her.

Every faction offered her something. None of them offered truth. She'd learned to price that particular currency years ago, and she knew exactly what it bought: nothing, forever, with interest.

But the Crown itself — the Crown was real. The Crown didn't lie. It just sat in its vault being the most dangerous object in the world, and whoever held it held the war's leash, and hadn't that been the whole argument she'd made to herself in the saltmarsh, in the dark, when she'd decided that protecting Noor meant controlling him? *Someone sane should hold it. Someone who knows what it costs.*

It had been a lie then, too. She'd known it was a lie even as she'd bargained away Tam's letters, and she'd done it anyway, because grief makes liars of everyone and she was more grieving than most.

So: the Crown, or Noor.

She was still asking the question when the second wave hit.

* * *

They came through the fog at her out of nowhere — four storm-born, moving low and fast along a drowned colonnade, and the leader put a knife against her throat before she'd finished turning around.

Then the knife-hand froze. "Wait." A woman's voice, young, astonished. "Wait — I know you. You were with the caller. On the pass road, at the ambush." The knife withdrew. In the steam-light Wren could see her clearly now: maybe twenty, storm-grey eyes, a scar splitting one eyebrow, the restless crackle of held lightning playing along her knuckles like a cat's tail. "You're the courier's sister."

"And you're the one who put a blade at my throat twice in one week," Wren said. "We really must stop meeting like this."

"The caller," the woman said, ignoring her. "Noor. Where is he?" And around her the other three shifted, and Wren saw it happen in their faces — the hunger, the reverence, the way men look at a banner rather than a person. They'd felt his magic on the pass. They'd marked him as theirs. A storm-caller born, walking free, carrying the map to the Crown — to them he wasn't a man, he was a sign, an omen, a weapon finally coming home to be aimed.

"He's not yours," Wren said. "Whatever you think he is."

"Everything is ours," the young one said simply, without malice, and that was the worst part — there was no cruelty in it, only certainty, the same serene certainty Wren had seen on the faces of sun-court priests. Two religions, one god, and the god was victory. "The Moot offers him a place. A home. His people, his power, his name restored — you think we don't know what the courts did to his family? He could stand at the front of the host when we take the Crown, and no one would ever exile him again. Tell him that. Tell him we wait for him." Her eyes flicked past Wren, toward the cathedral's black silhouette rising from the flood, its drowned bell-tower leaning like a drunk against the sky. "Or don't tell him. It changes nothing. He'll choose us. People always choose belonging."

She melted back into the fog with her three, and Wren stood thigh-deep in a dead city's streets with the words stuck in her chest like fishhooks.

*A place. A home. His people.*

That was the offer. That was what both sides were selling, and they were selling it because they'd looked at Noor Ravensworth and seen the loneliest man alive. Exiled from the storm-born for a storm he hadn't meant to call. Stranger to the sun-courts by blood and by nature. A man who had spent seven years in a valley that ate magic, tending goats, making himself small, making himself nothing — and now two empires were competing to tell him he could be *something*, could be *someone's*, and the price was only his hands.

They'd offered Wren her family's name. They'd offered Noor a family at all. And both offers were built on the same foundation stone, the one Tam had died trying to deliver: *the Crown doesn't end the war. It unseals it.* Whoever lifted it wouldn't be holding a prize. They'd be holding the first domino.

Unless Noor reached the vault first. Unless he read his father's testament with his own eyes. Unless someone told him the truth before the temptation got there wearing the face of everything he'd ever wanted.

*Go get the Crown yourself,* whispered the old Wren, the practical one, the survivor. *Take the choice out of his hands. Take it out of everyone's hands. Hide it, sink it, sell it, whatever — just don't let him touch it, because if he touches it he'll carry it, and if he carries it he becomes the war, and you've already lost one person you loved to this thing.*

*Go find Noor,* whispered the other voice, newer, quieter, the one that had started up in the saltmarsh and refused to shut up since. *Because he's down there alone with his father's ghost and two armies' worth of promises, and because when he learns what you did — and he already knows — the only thing that can save what's left between you is you standing in front of him with nothing hidden. No strategy. No leverage. Just the ugly truth, delivered by hand.*

The old Wren pointed out, reasonably, that finding Noor might accomplish nothing. That he might turn his back on her. That the Crown, unguarded, would fall into the hands of whichever butcher arrived first, and then everything Tam bled for would be for nothing.

The new voice pointed out, unreasonably, gently, that Tam hadn't bled for the Crown. Tam had bled for the message. *There was never a home on the other side of it.* And messages don't deliver themselves, and couriers die, and sisters inherit the route.

Below her, the fighting surged along the cathedral steps — sun-court shields locking into a wall, storm-born lightning crawling over it like ivy, the drowned bells in the tower beginning to hum with all the power loose in the air, a low mournful note that no one was ringing, the dead city itself groaning in its sleep. The tide would turn within the hour. When it did, the streets would fill, the vault would drown again, and whatever chance existed to reach the Crown — or the man — would be gone for a hundred years.

Wren took the token the storm-born girl had given her, looked at it once, and threw it into the flood. Then she took the sun-court captain's promised reward, which existed only as words, and let the water have that too.

She went down into the cathedral.

Not for the Crown. She told herself it plainly, the way she wished she'd told him everything plainly: not for the Crown. For the courier's last delivery, which had fallen to her, and for the man standing at the end of it, whom she had lied to, and used, and — somewhere between the saltmarsh and the siege, without noticing the exact moment it happened — loved.

The water rose around her knees as she waded through the nave of the drowned cathedral, past pews furred green with a century of weed, toward the vault stairs where the thirteenth moon's light fell through a broken dome in a single silver column. Lightning flashed through the high windows, painting everything white, and in the flash she saw footprints in the silt, fresh ones, leading down.

And beside them, half-buried, glinting — a scrap of parchment she recognized with a sickness that went through her like cold iron.

Tam's handwriting. Another letter. One she had never seen.

It was addressed to her.
