## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The mare was dying, and no one in Hollowmere would say her name.

Astrid Kavanagh crouched in the straw-dark corner of the miller's stable, her knees pressed into old hay gone soft and gray with damp, and listened to the animal breathe. It was a bad sound. A wet, dragging sound, like water pulled through a broken reed. The miller's boy had named the mare Clover when she was a foal, but names were dangerous things in Hollowmere — they stuck to a creature, made it real, made its loss a wound instead of an inconvenience — so the miller called her *the bay*, and his boy had learned not to correct him, and now the mare lay on her side in the straw with her ribs rising and falling like something struggling up from deep water, and nobody would say her name at all.

"I'll say it," Astrid whispered. "Clover."

The mare's ear twitched. Turned toward her. That small motion was worth more to Astrid than every word spoken in the village below, where the evening bells were ringing the hour home and lamplight was blooming yellow in windows one by one, and where Astrid Kavanagh was known as a quiet girl, a dutiful girl, a girl who kept her eyes down and her mouth shut and gave no one any reason to look twice at her.

Clover's breath came again, that terrible wet rattle, and beneath it — faint as a moth against glass — Astrid felt the other thing. The thing that was not sound. The pressure of another mind, dim with pain, turning toward hers like a candle flame bending toward a draft.

*Cold,* the mare said. Not in words. In the shape of cold, in the memory of frost on water troughs, in the ache of a body that could not get warm. *So cold. And dark coming.*

"I know." Astrid eased herself closer, careful of the great barrel of the mare's ribs, and laid her palm flat against the sweat-stiff neck. Her hand was small against all that trembling warmth. "I know you're cold. I'm sorry. I'm sorry we let it go this long."

*Let what go?* There was no bitterness in it. Animals did not do bitterness; that was a human invention, like locks and ledgers and wards. The mare only wanted to understand the shape of her dying, the way a traveler wants to see the road ahead even when the road ends badly.

"The sickness," Astrid said softly. "It's in your lungs. It's been there since the first hard rain, and Old Tammas bled you wrong, and the miller wouldn't pay for the herb-woman because—" She stopped. Because the herb-woman's craft sat too close to the line. Because in Hollowmere you did not pay for anything that smelled of magic, not even the harmless kind, not even the kind that was really just knowing which moss stops bleeding. "Because he was afraid," she finished. "People here are afraid of the wrong things."

*You are not afraid,* the mare said, and it was not a question, and it undid Astrid a little, the way it always did — how plainly animals saw you, without any of the mercy humans used on each other, without any of the lies.

"I am," she said. "I'm afraid all the time. Just not of you."

She worked as she talked, because talking kept the fear quiet and working kept the guilt quieter still. From the satchel under her cloak she took the honey-and-thyme brew she'd steeped over three nights on her mother's hearth, blamed on a cough remedy for Astrid herself, and a cloth to strain it warm, and the little horn spoon she'd bought off a tinker two summers ago and kept hidden inside a hollowed-out prayer book, which was the sort of joke Hollowmere would not have found funny. She fed the mare slow spoonfuls, tilting the great head up, feeling the throat work, counting breaths between.

And all the while, beneath the words, she listened.

That was the gift. That was the crime. Not potions, not poultices — those were Maren-the-herb-woman's sins, and Maren paid her tithe of suspicion every season and survived it. What Astrid had was older and stranger: she could hear the minds of beasts the way other people heard music. Had been able to since before she had words for it, back when she was four and told her father the neighbor's dog grieved for its dead littermate, and her father had gone white as flour and carried her to the ward-stones at the valley's mouth and made her touch them, made her swear she'd never — never *say* such things again, never listen, never be what she was, because the wards of Hollowmere were carved to keep magic out, and a child who spoke with beasts was magic that had gotten in.

*Alone,* the mare said, and Astrid couldn't tell if the mare meant herself or Astrid, or both of them, or every creature in the world that had ever been talked to in secret by someone who couldn't afford to be known.

"Both of us, then," Astrid said aloud, and her voice cracked on it, and she was glad the stable was empty and the bells were loud and no one ever came to the miller's stable after dark except her.

* * *

Hollowmere forgot things. That was its whole genius, and its whole cruelty.

Astrid had understood this slowly, the way you understand a wall by walking alongside it your whole childhood until one day you measure it with your eyes and realize how tall it is. The valley sat folded into the northern hills like a cupped hand, green and sheltered and unremarkable, and the folk who lived there were good folk — honest farmers, a smith, a miller, a carter, a priest who served a god whose name Astrid's father had never quite been able to explain. And around the whole valley, set into the earth at intervals like the teeth of some enormous buried jaw, stood the ward-stones: gray pillars taller than a man, older than the village, older than the king in his far southern capital, older than any history anyone in Hollowmere bothered to keep. They bore no runes that anyone could read. They needed none. Their work was silence.

Magic did not cross them. Magic did not *want* to cross them; the stones turned it aside the way a stone in a stream turns water, gently, endlessly, without effort or malice. Inside the wards, prayers went unanswered. Omens stayed omens. The hedge-cunning that thrived in every other corner of the world — charms against toothache, blessings on the seed-barley, the little bargains country people struck with the small spirits of field and hearth — all of it simply failed at the valley's edge, and the people of Hollowmere had built their pride on that failure. *We are clean,* they said. *We are safe. Nothing unhallowed touches us.* When travelers came through with tales of wonders — saints who healed with a touch, rivers that ran backward on holy days, beasts that spoke — the villagers smiled the way you smile at children describing dreams.

Astrid had spent nineteen years learning exactly what that smile cost.

Because the wards kept magic out, yes. But magic born inside — magic that arrived in the blood of a baby born between the stones, as Astrid had been, as perhaps others had been before her — the wards did not remove. They only silenced. They pressed down on it like snow on a roof, year after year, and everyone pretended the roof would hold forever, and the ones who carried it learned to hunch beneath the weight and speak of it to no one.

Her father had died believing she'd stopped listening. Her mother believed she'd grown out of it, the way children grew out of night-terrors. Only Astrid knew that the gift had never left her — that it lived in her like a second heartbeat, that every bird at the window and every dog behind a fence and every ox in every furrow had a voice, and that she had spent her whole life holding hers over her own ears, pretending deafness, dying of loneliness in a valley full of neighbors.

Except at night. Except in stables and byres and the reed-beds where the herons stood like patient monks. Except in stolen hours like this one, when the need in some suffering creature grew louder than her fear, and she crept out with her satchel and her hidden spoon and let herself, just for a little while, be what she was.

The mare swallowed the last of the brew. Her breathing eased — not healed, nothing could heal her now, but eased, the rattle thinning to something less like drowning. Her mind, pressed against Astrid's, lost some of its gray terror.

*Warm now,* she said. *Where you touch. Warm.*

Astrid bowed her head over the great gentle skull and did not trust herself to answer.

* * *

She was halfway home along the mill-race path when the hawk fell out of the sky.

It happened the way disasters happen — sideways, too fast, without dignity. One moment the dusk sky over the eastern ridge held nothing but the first stars; the next, a shape tumbled down through the dark like a thrown stone, struck the alder branches above the path, crashed through them, and landed in the bracken three strides from Astrid's boots with a sound she felt in her teeth.

She froze. Then, because her body had never once in nineteen years obeyed the rules her mind set it, she ran toward it.

The hawk was a marsh harrier, or had been — a female, big across the shoulder, and she had flown through something. That was Astrid's first thought, absurd and certain: *through*. The bird's wings were wrong, the flight feathers scorched to quills, the skin of her breast bare and blistered in a pattern that looked almost like writing. Her talons were clenched around something. Her eye — one eye, the other crusted shut — rolled up and found Astrid's face, and the mind behind that eye hit Astrid's like a wave hitting a seawall.

Pain first. Pain past pain, pain that had burned away everything else. But beneath it, threaded through it like wire through wax:

*Running. So fast, so long. Carrying. Must carry. Must—*

"You're hurt," Astrid said stupidly, kneeling in the bracken, her hands hovering over the ruined wings. "Oh, you poor thing, you're—"

*LISTEN.*

The word was not a word. It was a blow. Astrid had touched the minds of hundreds of creatures — mice and horses, adders and owls, a dying salmon once, a bear that had crossed into the valley's edge and turned back confused — and none of them, none, had ever *shouted*. Beasts did not shout. Beasts thought in weather and hunger and the shapes of things. This bird was thinking in language, in human-shaped meaning, and the effort of it was tearing her apart from the inside, and she did it anyway.

*They are waking. The old ones. The buried ones. The ones your stones were raised against. The wards are cracking — I have seen them crack — I flew the length of the north range and the stones are weeping light, the high passes are full of singing, the gods are WAKING—*

"That's not—" Astrid's voice came out thin. "The wards don't crack. The wards have held for—"

*Everything holds,* said the hawk, *until it doesn't.*

Her body shuddered. Blood, black in the starlight, seeped from beneath her breast feathers, and Astrid saw now what the talons had been clenching: a feather of her own, but changed — stiffened, glazed, shot through with veins of dull ember-light, like a coal wrapped in skin. The hawk worked it loose with the last strength in her ruined foot and pushed it toward Astrid across the frozen bracken.

*Carry it. You're the Listener. We looked for you — the stones sang your name — carry it south, carry it to—*

"To who?" Astrid's hands closed around the feather and it was warm, warm as a living thing, warm as the mare's neck, and it pulsed against her palm with a rhythm that was almost — that was exactly — a heartbeat. "To whom? Who's looking?"

But the hawk's eye had already begun to fix. The great mind guttered like a lamp out of oil, and the last thing it sent was not language at all but an image, raw and enormous: a valley seen from very high, cupped in dark hills, and around it a ring of standing stones — and one of them, on the eastern ridge, split from crown to root with a seam of light like a crack in a kiln door.

Then the hawk was only a body in the bracken, and the night was only the night, and Astrid knelt alone on the mill-race path holding a burning feather and hearing, for the first time in her life, the wards of Hollowmere make a sound.

It was very faint. It was almost nothing. It was the sound a house makes in winter, settling — except it came from the ridge, from the direction of the eastern stone, and it did not stop, and somewhere beneath it, deeper than her ears, deeper than the valley, something vast and patient turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to wake.

Behind her, down in the village, the evening bell rang out the hour. Ahead of her, at the valley's mouth, a boy was stumbling through the dark toward the ward-stones with fire in his chest — though Astrid Kavanagh could not yet know that, could not yet know how completely her life had already ended and begun again.

She only knew the feather was warm, and the stone was cracking, and the gods were waking, and there was no one in all of Hollowmere she could tell.
