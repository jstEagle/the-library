## 2. The Boy Who Fell From the Road

The ember had been burning in Jonah's chest for eleven days, and he had learned to count its moods the way sailors counted weather.

Some hours it was a coal banked in ash — a dull warmth beneath his sternum that let him almost forget it was there, let him walk with something like a normal gait, let him eat the hard black bread he'd stolen from a shrine offering-box without tasting anything at all. Other hours it woke. It flared behind his ribs like a swallowed star, and his skin went luminous at the throat and wrists, veins showing gold through the flesh as though he were a lantern made of paper, and then he had to throw himself into ditches, into bracken, into the cold mud of drainage cuts, and lie there shaking until it dimmed again.

It had woken an hour past dawn, on the last morning of his running.

He was on the downs above the southern rim of Hollowmere valley by then. He'd chosen the valley deliberately, or told himself he had: Father Ellery's maps showed it as a blank, a smudge of nothing between the hill roads and the northern fells, annotated in the old priest's cramped hand — *warded; dead to magic; nothing enters, nothing leaves unmarked.* A place where a burning thing in a boy's chest might finally be smothered. A place where whatever had marked him back in Vaelmarrow might lose his scent entirely.

He hadn't known, then, what it meant for a valley to be *dead* to magic when you were carrying a piece of it inside you like a stolen coal.

Jonah crested the ridge as the sun cleared the eastern fells, and the whole of Hollowmere opened below him — a long green bowl of fields and hedgerows, a river stitched through its middle like a silver seam, smoke rising straight from a dozen chimneys in the windless morning. It should have been beautiful. It was beautiful. But even from the ridge he could feel the wrongness of it, the way you can feel a silence in a house where a clock has just stopped. The air over the valley lay flat and dense, like the surface of a pond no one had ever thrown a stone into.

And the ember knew it too. He felt it recoil against the inside of his ribs, a live thing scenting a trap, and he pressed his hand flat against his chest and whispered to it — because by now he whispered to it, because there was no one else to whisper to —

"Not yet. Almost. We'll rest there. Just rest."

He was so tired. That was the truth under everything else, the plain ugly truth he kept trying to bury under plans and prayers: he had not slept more than two hours at a stretch in eleven days, because sleep was when the ember dreamed, and its dreams were not his own. He had eaten perhaps nine meals. His feet had gone past blistered into some numb country beyond pain. And somewhere behind him — south, always south, he'd been running south — the bells of Vaelmarrow had stopped ringing the hour he left, which meant they were ringing for something else now.

He started down.

The slope was long and sheep-cropped, and halfway down it the ember flared so violently that he lost his footing and went the last thirty yards in a tumbling rush of bracken and thorn, fetching up against a drystone wall with a crack he felt in his shoulder rather than heard. He lay in the wet grass gasping. Gold light leaked between his fingers where he clutched his chest. Above him, the sky seemed to lean closer, interested.

"No," he said aloud, to the ember, to the sky, to whatever had put the thing in him in the crypt under Vaelmarrow three nights after the old god's altar-stone cracked. "No, not here, be quiet, be *quiet*—"

But the wards had already felt him coming.

He didn't understand it then — wouldn't understand it until much later, until a girl with storm-grey eyes explained it to him in words he never forgot — but the boundary of Hollowmere was not a wall. It was a *judgment*. It hung over the valley rim like heat-haze made solid, invisible until you were inside its reach, and when Jonah dragged himself upright and took one stumbling step across that unseen line, every hair on his body stood on end and the world said *no.*

There was no other word for it. The air itself said *no.* Pressure closed around him like a fist of cold water, and the ember screamed — not in his ears, nowhere so kind as his ears, but in the marrow of him, a soundless shriek of a flame being smothered — and Jonah went down onto his knees on the near side of the ward-line as if the ground itself had refused to hold him.

He tried again. Of course he tried again. He was eleven days past the point of good sense.

He got one boot across the threshold, felt the ward take hold of the ember and *squeeze*, felt the fire lash out in answer, and the collision of the two went through him like a struck bell. He was thrown backward — actually thrown, lifted off his feet by nothing visible — and landed hard in the bracken ten feet down the slope, and when he tried to breathe afterward the breath came in as a whistling sob, and light poured off his skin in waves now, gold and desperate, his whole body strobing like a signal fire on a dark coast.

*They'll see me,* was his last coherent thought. *Whoever watches this valley, they'll see me burning on their doorstep, and they'll know exactly what I am, and Father Ellery is dead and no one is coming, no one has ever been coming—*

Then the world tilted, and the grass came up very slowly to meet his cheek, and everything went soft and far away.

* * *

He surfaced once, briefly, to the sound of footsteps.

Not many. One set. Light, quick, stopping abruptly some yards away — the particular stop of someone who has come around a hedgerow expecting sheep and found something else entirely.

He couldn't open his eyes. He managed a sound, half word, half animal hurt, and the footsteps came closer — hesitated — came closer again. He felt a presence crouch beside him, felt a hand hover near his blazing chest without touching, and heard a voice, low and wary and younger than he expected:

"You're burning." A pause. "You're actually — what did you *do*?"

He tried to answer. What came out was: "Didn't... do anything. It was done to me."

Silence. Then, quieter, almost to herself: "That's what I'd say. If anyone ever caught me."

He felt her weigh it. He was lucid enough, in that flickering way of the half-conscious, to feel her weighing it — the fear of discovery sitting on one side of the scale, and on the other side a boy who was dying on her doorstep in broad daylight. He felt her stand up. He felt her walk three steps away.

His heart broke, a little, without surprise. Eleven days of running had taught him exactly how much strangers could be relied upon.

But the footsteps came back.

"Idiot," the voice muttered — whether at him or at herself he never learned. "Absolute idiot. If the elders ask, I found you already through. Already through, do you hear me? You fell through before I got here."

Hands gripped his shoulders. And Jonah, drifting, felt the strangest thing: felt the girl brace herself against the ward as though against a current, felt her drag him bodily toward that invisible judgment, felt the pressure close over them both—

—and part.

It parted for her like water around a stone. It did not part for him; it *argued* with him, the ember shrieking and the ward bearing down, and for one endless moment Jonah hung between them like a wishbone about to snap. Then her grip tightened, and she said through her teeth — to him, or to the ward, or possibly to both — "*In,*" and the resistance gave all at once, and they tumbled through together onto the soft inside grass of Hollowmere, and the terrible pressure vanished.

The ember, smothered no longer, sank down gratefully into banked coals. The light died off his skin. And Jonah, lying in the deep green silence of a valley that hated everything he was, looked up into the face of the girl who had saved him despite herself.

She had storm-grey eyes and hay caught in her dark braid, and she was looking at him with an expression he would come to know very well in the years after: exasperation and wonder fighting to a draw.

"You're a mess," she said.

"I'm sorry," Jonah said, because it was true, and because he was too exhausted to say anything cleverer.

"You will be," she said grimly, hauling his arm across her shoulders. "If anyone sees us, you're my cousin from the fells, you're sick, and you fell off your horse. There's no horse. Don't ask about the horse."

* * *

Consciousness came and went after that, the way tide comes and goes.

Once he woke to the smell of hay and liniment and realized he was in a loft, tucked into a corner behind stacked bales, hidden with a deliberateness that frightened him more than the hiding itself. Once he woke to voices below — a man's voice, heavy with worry, saying *the mare's no better, Astrid, I don't hold with your ways but if anyone could—* and cutting off sharply, guiltily, as though the speaker had said too much. Once he woke in full dark and heard the girl climbing the ladder, and lay still, pretending, while she sat nearby in the straw and watched him with those grey eyes and murmured, so quietly he was nearly sure he'd imagined it:

"I don't know what you are. But whatever burned you in like that — the wards have never fought anything that hard. Not in living memory." A pause. "Whatever's in you, it doesn't belong outside either."

And once — the last time, the time that mattered — he woke properly, in late afternoon light slanting gold through the loft boards, with the ember quiet as a sleeping cat and a cup of something bitter steaming at his elbow, and the girl sitting cross-legged in the straw a careful arm's length away, watching him with her arms folded, as though she'd been waiting for him to wake and resented having waited.

"You've been asleep a day and a half," she said.

"I'm sorry," Jonah said again, and this time it made her mouth twitch, almost against her will.

"Stop apologizing. Drink that. It's willow and something Maren sent up years ago that I'm not supposed to have." She watched him drink. Her eyes kept going, he noticed, to his chest — to the place where, he realized with a lurch, his shirt hung open and the skin over his sternum showed a faint tracery of gold, like lightning frozen in glass, like a scar of light. She looked at it the way he had once looked at the locked cabinet in Father Ellery's study: wanting to understand, afraid of understanding.

"What's your name?" she asked.

"Jonah." His voice cracked; he drank again. "Jonah Vale. I was — I was apprenticed at the temple in Vaelmarrow. Before."

"Before what?"

He looked at her. She had risked exile — worse than exile, if half of what he'd overheard about the valley's law was true — to drag a stranger through a ward that wanted him dead. She deserved, he thought, at least the shape of the truth, if not the whole burning weight of it.

"Before the old altar cracked," he said. "Before something got loose down in the crypt. Before it—" His hand rose without his permission and pressed flat against the golden tracery, and the ember stirred, warm and drowsy, like a thing dreaming. "Before this. It was a fragment of something. Of someone. And now it's in me, and things are following it, and I ran, and I ran, and your valley was the only place on any map that said *nothing magical enters.*"

The girl was silent a long moment. Outside, far off, a dog barked twice and went quiet.

"Astrid," she said at last. "Astrid Kavanagh." And then, lower, with the wariness of someone laying down a card she can never take back: "You should know something too, since we're trading. This valley isn't empty of magic because it hates magic. It's empty because it's *hiding* from it. Everyone here agreed, generations ago, to be blind. And people like me—" her jaw worked, "—people like me aren't supposed to exist. So whatever you are, whatever's in you — you're not the only secret in this loft. Understood?"

Jonah understood. Two fugitives, sitting in straw, each holding a lit match and pretending not to see the other's.

"The elders," he said. "Will they find me?"

"They'll find *something*," Astrid said. "Ward-scar like yours doesn't fade in a day. When they do — we'll deal with it. One disaster at a time." She stood, brushing straw from her skirt, and paused at the ladder's edge, not quite looking at him. "Sleep. And Jonah?" Now she looked at him, and the exasperation had gone out of her face, leaving only something tired and fierce underneath. "Whatever followed you here. Whatever's coming. You don't face it alone. Not anymore. That's not — that's not how I was raised, whatever else Hollowmere taught me."

She went down the ladder. Jonah lay back in the straw, hand over the banked fire in his chest, and stared up through the gap in the boards at the first stars.

The ember pulsed once, softly, contentedly — and in the moment before sleep took him, he could have sworn he felt it turn toward the farmhouse below, toward the girl walking back through the dusk, with something that felt horribly, unmistakably like *recognition.*

As if it had known her name long before either of them had.
