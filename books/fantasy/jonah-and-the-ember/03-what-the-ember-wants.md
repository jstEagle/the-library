## 3. What the Ember Wants

Jonah dreamed of a door.

It stood alone in a field of ash-grey grass, no wall around it, no house behind it — just a door, oak and iron, standing upright in the windless dark like a tooth pulled from some enormous jaw. He knew it was a dream because the grass did not bend and the sky had no stars in it, only a faint bruised glow along the horizon, as if something vast were burning just below the edge of the world.

*Open it,* said the ember.

It spoke from inside his chest, which should have been terrifying and was not, quite. It had been speaking to him for three nights now, ever since Astrid Kavanagh dragged him half-dead through the valley's wards, and each night its voice grew less like a whisper and more like a hand on his shoulder. Warm. Patient. Certain.

*I don't want to,* Jonah said, in the way one says things in dreams, without mouth or breath.

*You do. You simply don't know it yet.* The ember pulsed, and he felt it against his ribs like a second heartbeat, slightly out of time with his own. *The door is not locked, little wick. It never was.*

"I'm not a wick," Jonah said aloud, and his voice in the dream was a boy's voice, thin and young, and he hated it.

*You carry fire. What else would I call you?*

He woke before he could answer, gasping, in the hayloft above the Kavanagh stable, sweat freezing on his skin in the pre-dawn cold. Below him the horses shifted and sighed, and — this was the part that still unsettled him, four days after Astrid had hauled him over the threshold and hidden him among the straw — one of them answered him back. A low nicker, questioning. As though it had heard him cry out and wanted to know if he was well.

Jonah pressed his palm flat against his sternum. Beneath the skin, beneath the bone, something glowed. He could feel it when he breathed: a coal-sized heat that never spread, never cooled, never slept. Father Aldous at the seminary in Corvane had taught him that divine fire was a metaphor — the light of understanding, the warmth of grace. Father Aldous had never had an actual piece of it lodged under his breastbone like a swallowed star.

"Go back to sleep," he whispered to himself, and the mare below snorted as if in agreement, and Jonah lay staring at the rafters and did not sleep at all.

* * *

Astrid knew the boy was lying about something long before she knew what.

She'd gotten good at reading lies — living in Hollowmere taught you that. A valley full of people pretending not to notice things bred a particular kind of silence, and Astrid had spent nineteen years learning its grammar. Her father lied about the state of the fences. Old Tam lied about how much he drank. The whole village lied, gently and constantly, about what had happened to the Kavanagh name after her grandmother was exiled for the crime of *conversing*, and everyone pretended the lie was true, and that was how you survived in a place where magic was a wound in the world.

But Jonah lied differently. He lied the way a burned man flinches from a candleflame — not to deceive, but because touching the truth hurt.

"You didn't sleep," she said, dropping a bundle of feed sacks beside the loft ladder and looking up at him where he sat on the edge of the hay, legs dangling, looking like a scarecrow someone had dressed too optimistically. "I heard you talking."

"To myself." Too fast. Too smooth. A rehearsed lie, then, which meant the truth underneath was load-bearing.

"Mm." Astrid crossed her arms. "Talking to yourself in a voice that isn't yours."

That landed. She watched it land — watched the color leave his face in stages, like water draining. His hand came up to his chest, an unconscious gesture she'd catalogued the first day: he touched his sternum the way other people touched old scars, or locket chains, or the place where a tooth had been.

"It talks," he said finally. Very quietly. "In dreams. It doesn't — it's not words, exactly. Not at first. But last night it was words."

"What does it say?"

"That depends on whether you believe me." He laughed, and there was nothing funny in it. "Because what it says is insane."

"I live in a valley where the fences are warded against miracles," Astrid said. "Insane is my native language."

Something in his shoulders loosened — a fraction, barely — and he looked at her properly for the first time that morning. He had grey eyes, she'd noticed. Grey as slate, grey as the sky before snow, and they went wide and strange when the thing inside him stirred, which it did now; she saw him stiffen, saw his breath catch.

"It wants me to open a door," he said. "And it keeps calling you something. A title." He swallowed. "It calls you the Listener."

The word went through Astrid like a fishhook.

She stood very still. Around them the stable went on being a stable — the horses shifting, wind worrying the loose shingle on the south roof, morning birds quarreling in the eaves — but all of it seemed suddenly far away, sounds arriving from another country. *Listener.* Her whole life she had thought of her gift as a sickness, a deformity, the Kavanagh curse: hearing the beasts, answering them, carrying on conversations no human ear could follow. Her grandmother had been cast out for it. Her mother had taught her to hide it before she'd taught her letters. Nobody had ever called it anything but a crime.

Nobody had ever given it a name that sounded like a *calling*.

"How does it know that?" she asked, and her voice came out steadier than she felt.

"I don't know. It knows things." Jonah slid down from the loft, landing awkwardly, favoring the ankle the wards had nearly broken when they spat him back onto the road. Up close, in the grey light slanting through the door, she could see the marks again — the ones she'd tried not to stare at while she stitched his forehead. Thin lines across his forearms and throat, silver-white, branching like frost ferns or river deltas. Ward-scars. The valley's defenses had burned him trying to keep him out, and where they'd burned him, they'd left their signature. "It knew your name before I told it. It knew about the hawk."

"The hawk?"

"The dying one. That brought you the warning." He hesitated. "It says — it says the hawk wasn't wrong. And it says I'm sorry, whatever that means. It says *sorry* a lot, actually. Like it's apologizing for something enormous."

Astrid looked at him for a long moment. Then she did something she had never done in her life, not once, not with anyone: she told the truth first.

"When I was seven," she said, "a fox got into the henhouse. My father set traps. I went out at night and talked the fox out from under the floorboards and walked it to the far wood, and my mother caught me coming back, and she made me swear on grandmother's grave that I would never do it again. And I swore. And I've done it every week since, because I can't *not*." She heard her own voice shaking and hated it and kept going. "I have spent my entire life being fluent in a language that gets you exiled. So when something in your chest says I'm the Listener — I need to know if it's mocking me. Because if it is, I will drag you back to the ward-line myself and let the valley finish what it started."

The ember flared. She couldn't see it, but she felt it — felt it the way you feel a hearth across a dark room, warmth arriving on your face before you understand why. Jonah made a small sound and pressed both hands to his chest, and through the gaps between his fingers, for one heartbeat, one impossible heartbeat, light leaked out. Gold-white. Steady as a pulse.

And every animal in the stable went silent at once, and then — softly, in voices only she could hear — they all began, very quietly, to sing.

* * *

Elder Maren Osgood — no relation to any Osgood worth mentioning, a fact she corrected cheerfully and often — had held her seat on Hollowmere's council for thirty-one years, and in that time she had learned that wards were like teeth: you checked them constantly, and you worried most about the ones that didn't hurt.

So when Tam Whitlock's boy came running up the lane shouting that the miller's mare had gone mad in the night, kicking her stall to splinters and singing — *singing*, he said, actual notes — Maren put down her tea, put on her boots, and went to see for herself.

What she found was a horse standing calm as Sunday in a wrecked stall, and a crowd gathering, and — this was the interesting part — a stranger's scent on the air. Faint. Burnt. The smell of a ward doing its work.

She found the boy that afternoon, because boys who fell off roads and got patched up in secret always made exactly the same mistake eventually: they got hungry. He was in the Kavanagh's back garden, eating bread like it owed him money, and when he saw her his face did the thing faces did — the flicker, the calculation, the decision about which lie to spend.

Maren smiled her council smile and held out her hand. "Shake," she said. "And mind you give me the right arm."

He gave her the right arm. Of course he did. They always did.

Through the thin weave of his sleeve she saw them: pale branches, silver-fern patterns, climbing from wrist to elbow. Ward-scars. Fresh ones, days old at most, and shaped in a pattern she recognized down to her bones, because she had helped renew those wards every midwinter for three decades and knew their geometry the way a weaver knows her loom.

The valley's defenses had marked this boy. Which meant the valley's defenses had *tried* to stop him. Which meant he had come through anyway, or been brought, or —

Or carried something the wards had tasted and feared.

"Nice evening," Maren said pleasantly, releasing his arm. "Cold coming, I think. Early winter. The geese are flying wrong this year — too high, too quiet. You'll want to be somewhere settled before the first hard frost."

"I — yes, ma'am. Thank you. I'm leaving soon."

"Oh, I imagine so." She turned to go, then paused, and let the smile drop for just a moment — let him see what lived underneath it, thirty-one years of council meetings and buried grandmothers and a valley holding its breath. "Boy. Whatever you're carrying — and you are carrying something, don't insult us both by denying it — you should know that the wards cracked the night you arrived. First time in two hundred years. Cracked like ice on a spring pond, all at once, all around the valley, and nobody here can explain it." She watched his face go white as the frost-ferns on his arms. "So whatever it is, it's already awake. And things that are already awake don't stay quiet in haylofts forever."

She walked away whistling, an old woman with bad knees and good instincts, and behind her she heard the boy's breath come out in a long, shaking rush, and heard — faintly, unmistakably, in a voice that was almost but not quite his own — something in him answer:

*Too late. Too late. We are already so late.*

* * *

That night, Jonah dreamed of the door again.

This time it was open. Just a crack — a sliver of gold light falling across the ash-grey grass — and through the gap he could hear something breathing on the other side. Vast. Slow. Patient as stone.

*Not yet,* the ember murmured, and for the first time its voice sounded afraid. *Not yet, little wick. Close your eyes. If it sees you before you're ready—*

Across the field, at the edge of the light, a figure stood watching. Tall. Crowned. Its face was a brightness with no features in it, and when it spoke, Jonah felt the words in his chest beside the ember, twin fires calling to each other across the dark:

*"Listener,"* it said, tender as a lullaby. *"I know you're listening. I have waited so long to be heard."*

In the hayloft, Astrid woke with her hand pressed over her own mouth, silencing a scream she hadn't decided to make — and outside, in the darkened valley, every beast in Hollowmere lifted its head and stared north, toward the mountains, toward something coming down out of the high passes with the dawn.

The wards had cracked. And now something on the other side of them had begun, gently, to knock.
