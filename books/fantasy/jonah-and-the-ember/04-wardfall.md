## 4. Wardfall

Astrid woke to silence.

Not the ordinary silence of Hollowmere, which was never truly silent at all — the valley hummed beneath its hush like a struck bell held muffled in wool. She had lived inside that hum her whole life without knowing it was there, the way a fish does not know water, and so when she woke in the grey hour before dawn to a quiet with no texture underneath it, she lay still in her narrow bed and felt the absence the way one feels a missing tooth: with the tongue of the mind probing again and again at a gap that should not exist.

The mare's voice was gone.

She hadn't realized she could hear it even through the walls, through the wards, through half a mile of sleeping village — a low, patient presence at the edge of her mind, like a candle burning in another room. Old Sorrel, the miller's mare, whom she'd been nursing in secret for three weeks now, whispering comfort into the dark of the stable while the miller slept above. *The pain is less today, Listener. The pain is less.* Astrid had never told anyone what the beasts called her. She wasn't sure she wanted to know how they knew.

She sat up. The window showed her the valley floor wrapped in river mist, the mill wheel still, the fields lying grey-gold under a sky the color of unpolished pewter. Everything looked exactly as it always looked. Everything sounded wrong — hollowed out, as if someone had scooped the world's marrow from its bones in the night.

She dressed quickly, not bothering with her boots laced properly, and went down the stairs past her father's snoring and her mother's early-banked fire, out into air that bit with autumn's first real teeth.

The wards were dead.

She knew it before she reached the boundary stones, before she saw old Tam Wicker standing at the edge of his turnip field staring up at the sky with his mouth open like a landed fish. She knew it in her skin, which had spent twenty-two years pressed against an invisible wall she'd never been allowed to acknowledge. The wards of Hollowmere were not magic — that was the joke of them, the bitter irony her grandmother used to mutter over her tea. They were the *absence* of magic, a great hollow bell of nothing cast over the valley generations ago by founders who believed that what could not be felt could not take root. Inside the wards, no spell could be cast, no godling could hear a prayer, no gift could wake. The valley was a place where things like Astrid went to sleep forever, or learned to pretend they had.

And now the bell had cracked, and everything it had kept out came pouring through the hole like cold water into a boot.

She felt it first as pressure. Then as sound — not sound in the ears but sound in the blood, a vast low chord rising from the ground itself, from the stones, from the roots of the hills. The wild magic of the world, the old current that ran everywhere except here, rushing into the vacuum left behind. It came the way floodwater comes: not gently, not gradually, but all at once, finding every crack, filling every cellar, drowning every dry thing.

Astrid stood in the lane with her breath smoking and listened to the world turn on.

It was unbearable. It was glorious. It was every sense she'd ever been told to smother suddenly opened wide, and the land itself began to speak to her — not in words, not yet, but in pressures and hungers and slow green thoughts, the soil dreaming its long mineral dreams, the hedgerows flexing their thorned opinions about the frost. And threaded through it all, faint and far away and getting louder, something else. Something that felt like being watched by a very large eye.

*Oh,* she thought, with a clarity that felt like falling. *This is what I am. This is what I've been all along, and I couldn't hear it because they walled me off from myself.*

Then Tam Wicker screamed, and the morning stopped being beautiful.

* * *

The prayer had gone up sometime in the night. That was what the elders would piece together later, in the shaking hours after, when they gathered in the meetinghouse with the shutters closed as if closing shutters against this were possible. Tam's wife had been dying since midsummer — the wasting sickness, the kind that takes a woman slowly while her husband watches and can do nothing but count what remains. And Tam, who had never been devout, who had laughed at the traveling preachers and their stories of gods who answered, had gone out into his own field in the dark and knelt down in the wet dirt among his turnips and prayed.

He had prayed because for the first time in living memory, someone could have heard him.

And something did.

Astrid reached the field as the sun came up, and what she saw she would spend the rest of her life trying not to see. The godling stood in the middle of Tam's turnips, and it was beautiful, and it was wrong, and those two facts were the same fact. It had grown overnight out of the answered prayer the way a mushroom grows out of buried wood — sudden, fruiting, fed on rot. It wore a shape like a man's shape but taller, too tall, its proportions sliding whenever she tried to fix them, its skin the deep gold of ripe wheat and its hair a tangle of living stalks that moved in no wind Astrid could feel. Where its face should have been there was a suggestion of features, the way a face appears in smoke, and it was smiling. It was always smiling. Later she would understand that the smile was not cruelty. It was simply what devotion looked like when you peeled away everything else.

"Tam," she said, and her voice cracked on the name.

Tam Wicker knelt in the dirt before it. He was weeping. His hands were raised, palms up, in the oldest posture of supplication there is, and he was saying thank you, thank you, thank you, over and over, in a voice worn down to a thread. Because his wife had sat up that morning. Because the wasting had left her body between one breath and the next, and she was downstairs asking for porridge, confused and hungry and alive. Because his prayer had been answered, exactly, completely, in the way prayers are only answered by things that do not understand what they are giving or what they are taking.

Because the godling had eaten the sickness. And it was still hungry.

"Astrid." Tam turned his head toward her without lowering his hands. His eyes were shining, wet, luminous with joy, and it was the joy that frightened her more than the godling did. "Astrid, it heard me. Do you understand? All my life, nothing — and then last night I knelt down and *it heard me.*"

"I know," she said. "Tam, get up. Get up and come away from it."

"It gave her back." He laughed, and the laugh broke in the middle. "Forty years I farmed this dirt and it never gave me back anything."

The godling's head turned. Slowly, with the smoothness of something that has never needed joints. The suggestion of its face found Astrid, and she felt its attention settle on her like a hand on the back of her neck — vast, mild, incurious, the attention of a thing regarding a smaller thing and wondering idly whether it was food. Through the newly-opened channels of her gift she heard it, and what she heard made her stomach drop through the floor of the world.

It was listening to Tam the way she listened to beasts. With love. With genuine, bottomless, uncomplicated love — the love of a hearth for the fire that feeds it.

*More,* it said, in a voice like grain pouring into a silo. *He is open. He is open all the way down. Oh, he prayed so beautifully.*

"Tam," Astrid said, very quietly, the way she spoke to hurt animals. "Listen to me. You prayed for your wife. You didn't pray for this. Whatever it took from you — whatever it's taking right now — it isn't the price. There's always more price. Come away."

But Tam Wicker had forty years of unanswered kneeling in his knees, and he did not come away. He bowed lower. And the godling reached for him with both golden hands, tenderly, the way you cup water in your palms to drink, and Astrid saw — she *heard*, in the terrible new clarity of the morning — the moment the harvest began.

It did not eat his body. Not at first. It ate the prayer. It drank the devotion out of him like sap out of a tapped tree, and Tam sighed as it did, a long shuddering sigh of relief, of emptiness, of a man finally setting down a weight he had carried so long he'd forgotten it was heavy. He sagged against the godling's knee. His raised hands fell. And where he had knelt, when the golden shape finished and straightened and turned its smiling absence of a face toward the rest of the valley, there was nothing left but a fine drift of chaff, already scattering on the wind across the stubbled field.

The godling looked at Astrid. It smiled wider, if a smile can widen when it was never attached to anything.

*Listener,* it said, and the word went through her like a hook through a fish. *You hear us waking. Good. Tell them to pray.*

Then it poured itself down into the earth, into the roots, into the seed-rows, and was gone, leaving behind a field of turnips swollen grotesquely fat and glistening, ripe beyond any season, and a ring of trampled dirt where a man had knelt, and nothing else at all.

* * *

By noon the whole valley knew. By evening, the meetinghouse was full, and Astrid stood at the back of it with her arms crossed tight over her chest, watching Elder Maren's cousin — no, not Maren, that was another life; watching Elder Corwin Hale stand at the front with the ward-stone fragment in his hands and try to explain the end of the world to two hundred frightened people.

"The wards failed in the night," Corwin said. His voice was steady. His hands were not; the black shard of ward-stone trembled against his palm. "Failed, or were broken — we don't know which. What we know is that the valley is open. What we know is that Tam Wicker is dead, and Marta Wicker is alive and asking for her husband, and there is no honest way to tell her why."

Someone sobbed. Someone else said, loudly, "Burn the field. Burn the field and salt it," and a murmur of agreement ran through the room like wind through wheat, and Astrid thought of the swollen turnips glistening in their rows and felt sick.

"And what of the cause?" That was Elder Brandt, from the far side, and his eyes, when they swept the room, snagged on Astrid the way a hook snags on wool. "Wards don't fail, Corwin. They're tended. They're renewed. Unless—" and he paused, delicately, horribly, "—unless something inside the valley weakened them. Something that was never supposed to be here at all."

The murmur changed key. Astrid felt it turn toward her like weather.

She had spent her whole life being careful. She had spent her whole life making sure that no one ever caught her lips moving in an empty stable, that no one ever noticed how the dogs of Hollowmere went quiet and attentive when she passed, that no one ever marked the way injured birds found her windowsill and crows left bright things on her fence post like payment. Twenty-two years of careful, and it had bought her nothing but loneliness, and now, in the space of one day, it was worth nothing anyway — because the valley was open, and the magic was in, and everyone in this room could feel it pressing against their teeth, and they needed someone to blame for the fact that home had become a place where men were harvested in their own fields.

"There was a boy," said a voice near the door, and Astrid's heart stopped. "Came through two nights back. Half-dead. The Kavanagh girl pulled him past the stones — I saw her do it, saw the wards flare and fight him. Saw her *carry* him." It was the shepherd, Dawes, and he would not meet her eyes as he said it. "Ward-scarred, he was. Marked all down one side. If anything tore the wards—"

"He was dying," Astrid said, and her voice came out louder than she meant, cracking across the meetinghouse. "He was a child dying on the stones and I pulled him through. That's what I saw fit to do. And if any of you had been there, you'd have done the same, and if you say otherwise you're liars."

Silence. Two hundred people looking at her, and for the first time in her life she did not look away.

"You admit it, then," Elder Brandt said softly. "You admit the gift."

"I admit I talk to horses," Astrid said. "I admit I've done it since I was six years old and I've hidden it since I was seven, because I knew exactly what you'd do, and look — look at yourselves. Look at your faces. I was right."

"The wards are down," Brandt said. "A man is dead. A godling walked our fields this morning and ate him, and the same night a marked boy crossed our boundary, and you ask us to believe these things are strangers to each other?"

"They are strangers to each other," Astrid said. "But they won't stay that way. None of us will. The valley's open, Corwin. The wards aren't coming back — I stood on the stones this afternoon and I listened, and there's nothing left to listen *to*. Whatever comes next comes whether we exile him or not, whether you exile *me* or not. Burning the boy won't rehang the bell."

Corwin Hale looked down at the ward-shard in his trembling hands. When he spoke, his voice was very tired. "Exile," he said. "For both of them. At dawn. It's the law, and the law is all we have left that still works."

* * *

She packed by candlelight. Her mother stood in the doorway and did not speak, and her father sat on the bed with his head in his hands, and between the three of them there was nothing that words could carry, so Astrid folded shirts and wrapped bread and cheese and her grandmother's bone-handled knife, and let the silence be what it was.

At the door, her mother caught her wrist. "Marta Wicker came to the house," she whispered. "Asking after you. Asking if you'd sit with her tonight, because she can't bear the quiet." Her mother's face was grey. "What am I supposed to tell her, Astrid? About her husband?"

"Tell her he saved her," Astrid said. "Tell her the truth that matters."

Outside, the night was loud. That was the worst of it, she thought, walking down through the sleeping village toward the cottage where the ward-scarred boy lay fevered behind bolted doors — the night was *loud*, full of the voices of everything she'd spent her life pretending she couldn't hear. The dogs murmuring their watch-dreams. The hedgerows grumbling about frost. The river running its endless silver sentence. And beneath it all, far away, that vast watching attention she'd felt at dawn, patient as winter, growing.

The boy's door opened before she knocked. He stood swaying in the frame, fever-bright, one hand pressed to the scarred side of his chest where something under his skin glowed faintly through the cloth like a coal breathing.

"You heard it too," he said. It wasn't a question. "Last night. When the wards broke. You heard it."

"Yes," Astrid said.

"So did this." He touched his chest, and his young face — he was barely more than a child, she saw clearly now, a runaway apprentice with a stolen god's spark lodged in his ribs — twisted with something between terror and awe. "It's been talking to me all day. It says they're coming. All of them. It says the valley was the last quiet place, and now there isn't one." He swallowed. "It says we have to go north. It says *she'll know the road*, and I think—" his voice dropped, feverish, certain, "—I think it means you."

Astrid looked north, past the rooftops of the only home she had ever known, toward the dark line of the hills and everything beyond them. Somewhere out there, the thing that had eaten Tam Wicker was feeding, and growing, and learning the shape of its hunger. Somewhere out there was a road she had never walked, leading away from everything she had ever loved.

Behind her, the valley hummed its new and terrible song. Ahead, the dark waited, open-mouthed.

"Well," she said at last, to the boy, to the ember, to the listening night. "We'd best pack light."
