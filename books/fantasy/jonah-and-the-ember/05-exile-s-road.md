## 5. Exile's Road

The brand was still smoking when they crossed the northern fence-line.

Jonah could smell it — his own skin, seared in the shape of a broken circle, the mark Hollowmere's elders pressed into his forearm with an iron rod heated in the ward-fire. *Cause of the Falling*, they'd called it. He hadn't even cried out. He'd been too busy watching Astrid Kavanagh stand in front of him with her arms spread and her chin up and her whole life burning down around her, telling three grown men with staves that if they wanted to touch the boy, they'd have to go through her first.

They hadn't touched him. They'd branded them both instead — hers on the wrist, his on the arm — and told them to be gone by dawn or be gone by force.

Now it was dawn, gray and drizzling, and the valley of Hollowmere was a green smudge behind them, shrinking with every step like something drowning.

"You're limping," Astrid said, without looking at him.

"I'm walking."

"You're walking wrong. That's different." She stopped at the crest of the hill where the north road bent toward the fells, and finally turned. Her braid had come half undone overnight; there was ash on her cheek from the burned field, and she hadn't wiped it away. "Sit down before you fall down."

"I don't want to sit down."

"Of course you don't. Nobody ever wants to sit down until their legs stop being their legs." She pointed at a flat stone beside the road, one of the old waymarkers with worn runes nobody in the valley could read anymore. "Sit."

Jonah sat. It was humiliating how good it felt. His whole body seemed to sigh at once, and the ember in his chest shifted with the breath — a warm coal behind his sternum, pulsing faintly, like a second heart that had opinions about things. It had been quiet since the wardfall. Quiet in the way a held breath is quiet.

Astrid crouched in front of him, produced a strip of linen from her pack, and held out her hand for his arm. He gave it. The brand had blistered along its lower curve, and she dressed it with a competence that surprised him — her father was the miller, he remembered, but someone had taught her field-mending somewhere, and taught her well.

"You didn't have to do that," he said. "Stand up for me. You could have let them take me. They only wanted me gone after I came through. Before that, you were just the miller's strange daughter who talked to horses."

"I was never just anything," Astrid said, tying off the bandage with a sharp little tug that made him wince. "And neither are you. That's rather the problem we're both having, isn't it?"

* * *

They walked north through the morning, and the valley let them go the way a fist lets go of sand.

Jonah had never been north of Hollowmere. Nobody had, not really — the wards had made the outside world a rumor, a place of vague dangers and vaguer freedoms, spoken of the way sailors speak of deep water. Now the wards were broken shards humming under the turf, and the outside was simply *there*: wet hills, black pines, a road of packed mud rutted by cart wheels, and a sky so wide it made his eyes ache. He kept catching himself flinching at the horizon. There was nothing to stop at. Nothing to hold the world in.

The ember pulsed when he thought about that. *Open,* it seemed to say. Not in words — never quite in words — but as a warmth that spread up his throat and settled behind his teeth. *It's all open now.*

"Don't," Astrid said.

He blinked. "Don't what?"

"You've got your listening face on. The one where you're hearing something I can't hear and deciding whether to tell me about it." She trudged ahead, hood up against the drizzle. "If the fire in your chest has thoughts, I'd rather know them sooner than later. We learned that lesson from the godling already, thank you very much. I'd prefer not to learn it twice."

"It doesn't have thoughts. Not exactly." Jonah flexed his branded arm inside his sleeve. "It's more like... weather. Moods. This morning it feels — hungry? No. Not hungry. Homesick."

"Homesick." Astrid snorted. "Your chest-fire is homesick. For where? It fell out of a murdered god into a priest's orphanage, according to you. Which home is it missing?"

"That's just it. I don't know. And neither does it, maybe. Or—" He hesitated. The next part felt dangerous to say aloud, though he couldn't have said why. "Or it knows, and it's not telling me."

Ahead of them, a crow lifted off a fencepost and flew north along the road, keeping pace, and Jonah would have sworn — would have sworn on whatever was left of his faith — that it glanced back at them. Twice.

Astrid saw it too. She said nothing, but her hand drifted to the whistle cord at her neck, the little bone whistle she'd carried since childhood, the one that wasn't for calling dogs.

* * *

They made twelve miles the first day, which Astrid declared disgraceful and Jonah declared miraculous, given that one of them was branded, feverish, and carrying a piece of a dead god, and the other was seventeen and had never slept further than a mile from her family's mill.

They argued about it the whole time. It was, Jonah was beginning to understand, how Astrid prayed.

"—and anyway, if you'd let me try the deer trail—"

"The deer trail goes over Harrow Fell. Harrow Fell is above the snowline in *summer*, Astrid, and it is currently not summer."

"It's barely autumn. And I've been up Harrow Fell. There's a shelter hut—"

"There is a *ruin* with a roof-shaped suggestion, and you know it, because you told me you and your cousins used to dare each other to sleep there and Tam Kavanagh cried—"

"Tam was *six*—"

They stopped arguing abruptly because the road did.

It bent around a shoulder of gorse and dropped into a hollow where an old tollhouse squatted beside a stone bridge — and the bridge was blocked. A cart lay on its side across the span, one wheel still turning lazily in the wind, and three men rose from behind it with the unhurried ease of people who had done this many times and enjoyed it.

"Well then," said the tallest, who had a crossbow held low and friendly and a smile that wasn't. "Travelers. On *our* road. How lovely."

Jonah's whole body went cold and then hot, and the ember surged against his ribs like a dog hitting the end of a chain. Heat crawled up his throat. He felt it in his palms, that prickling pressure, the sense that something enormous was leaning on the inside of his skin wanting out — and he knew, with terrible clarity, that if it got out here, in this hollow, these three men would not be robbed. They would be *harvested*. He'd seen what answered prayer looked like. He'd watched a farmer become a stain in a wheatfield.

"No," he whispered, to the ember, to himself. "No. Not like that."

The tall man's eyes flicked to him and narrowed. "What's he muttering at? Boy's got a look about him. Got a *glow* about him, near enough." The crossbow came up six inches. "Ward-scarred, too. I can see it on his arm. You're the ones from the fallen valley, aren't you? The ones what broke the wards. There's talk on the roads. Talk says there's a bounty."

"There's always talk on the roads," Astrid said. Her voice had changed. Jonah had heard it change once before — in the stable, the night the hawk died, when she'd spoken to something no one else could hear. It went low and even and utterly unafraid, and it was somehow the most frightening voice he'd ever heard, because it was the voice of someone standing in two worlds at once. "And talk is cheap. So is your crossbow string, friend. It's frayed. Look at it. When did you last wax it?"

The tall man glanced down at his crossbow — an instinctive flicker, the mistake of a man addressed with authority — and in that flicker, Astrid whistled.

It wasn't loud. It was three notes, high and thin, almost nothing, swallowed immediately by the gorse and the drizzle. Jonah felt foolishly certain it had done nothing at all.

Then the hillside moved.

It wasn't an army. It was better than an army. It was *everything* — a boiling gray-black surge out of the bracken and the rocks and the culverts under the road: badgers, three of them, moving like boulders with intentions; a red kite dropping out of the sky with a scream that scattered the bandits' nerve before it scattered their bodies; a fox; a pair of feral goats with the flat yellow-eyed stare of creatures that had never once been sorry about anything. The kite hit the tall man's raised forearm talons-first, going for the frayed bowstring and the hand holding it, and the man's shriek joined the bird's. The badgers did not shriek. Badgers do not believe in it. They simply arrived at the nearest bandit's ankles with the patient inevitability of winter.

"Go," Astrid said to Jonah, grabbing his sleeve. "*Run.*"

They ran. Behind them the hollow dissolved into cursing and flapping and the particular clatter of a crossbow being dropped into a stream. One bandit made it three steps in pursuit before a goat — a *goat* — caught him in the hip and sent him into the gorse, where the gorse expressed its opinion of him at length.

They didn't stop running until the road climbed out of the hollow and the sounds faded behind them, and then Jonah put his hands on his knees and laughed, helplessly, breathlessly, the mad bright laughter of a person who has recently expected to die and instead witnessed a goat commit assault.

"You—" he gasped. "You *whistled*. You whistled and the entire countryside committed crimes on our behalf."

"They weren't crimes. They were pest control." But Astrid was grinning too, breathless, wild-eyed, and for a moment the mask of the miller's hard-bitten daughter slipped entirely and Jonah saw what was underneath: a girl who had spent her whole life hiding the best thing about herself, and had just, for the first time, used it to save someone. Both of them. "Three notes. High-low-high. My grandmother taught me. Said every creature within a mile owes you one favor if you ask right." Her grin dimmed a shade. "She also said never use it for anything you wouldn't die for, because the asking costs. I never understood what she meant until today. I can feel it. Like I spent something."

"What did it cost?"

"I don't know yet. That's the worst part." She rolled her shoulders, wincing. "Come on. Daylight's dying and I refuse to sleep in the open with three angry bandits behind us and whatever's ahead of us."

"Ahead of us," Jonah repeated. "You mean there's more?"

"Astrid," she said, pointing north, "there is *always* more."

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a drystone wall, sharing a fire the size of two cupped hands, because Astrid insisted small fires drew fewer eyes and Jonah insisted any fire at all was a risk worth taking because the ember ran colder at night and cold made it *loud*. It hummed against his spine as he sat, a low note like a struck bell that never finished ringing, and he'd learned that if he breathed slow and thought warm thoughts it settled. Warm thoughts were harder to come by lately.

Astrid sat across the flames, cleaning her nails with a knife, watching him with the frank stare she probably thought was subtle.

"Ask," Jonah said.

"I wasn't going to ask."

"You've been constructing the question for an hour. It's practically built. It has a chimney."

That startled a laugh out of her — short, reluctant, real. Then she sheathed the knife and asked it. "When it surges. Back on the road, in the hollow. What would have happened if you'd let it?"

Jonah looked into the coals for a long time. "I don't know exactly. I know it wanted to answer them." He tapped his own chest, over the ember. "This thing is a piece of something that eats devotion. Worship. Prayer. Those men looked at me and saw a bounty and a story to tell, and there was this — *appetite* — waking up in my ribs, and it wanted to give them what they were looking at. Make me into something worth worshiping. And then..." He swallowed. "I think it would have taken everything they had. Their faith, their fear, their — selves. All of it. The way the godling took Farmer Odell. I don't think there'd have been bodies left to bury. Just empty clothes and a very satisfied coal."

Astrid was quiet for a moment. The fire popped. Somewhere upslope, an owl called, and Jonah noticed Astrid tilt her head toward it slightly, automatically, the way a person turns toward a familiar voice — and then catch herself, jaw tightening, because the owl's call was just a call now, or should be.

"You're afraid of it," she said. Not cruelly. Carefully.

"Every day. Every hour." He hugged his knees. "In the seminary they taught us the gods were distant. Beautiful. Safe, because far away. And now I've got a splinter of one lodged in my chest and it *knows my name*, and the worst part — the truly worst part, Astrid — is that some nights it's the only thing that's ever felt like it was listening to me back."

The words hung there between them, more honest than he'd meant to be. He waited for her to flinch, or mock, or do the thing people did when you showed them the soft underside of yourself — the gentle, horrible pat-pat of pity.

Instead Astrid reached across the tiny fire and took his branded arm, thumb resting light on the edge of the bandage, and held on.

"My whole life," she said, "the animals were the only ones listening to me. And now I'm on a road where using the only true thing about me might get us killed, and you're carrying the only true thing about you like a lit lamp in a dry field, and we're both exiles, and we're both liars, and we're both—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "We're both going to have to trust each other eventually. I'd rather start tonight than the night something makes us."

Jonah turned his hand over and gripped hers. Her palm was rough from stable work and warm as a banked hearth, and the ember in his chest went suddenly, profoundly quiet — not silent, but *settled*, the way a child goes still when it hears a voice it knows.

"Allies, then," he said.

"Allies," Astrid agreed. "Bickering allies. Don't get sentimental. Tomorrow we walk, and tomorrow you tell me everything the ember's ever said to you, starting with why it keeps calling me *Listener* in your dreams, because I've decided I'm allowed to be frightened about that now."

Above the drystone wall, unnoticed by either of them, the crow from the morning's road landed on a post. It folded its wings. It cocked one glossy eye at the firelight, at the two figures huddled against the dark, and it did not leave.

It had been following them since the valley.

It intended to keep doing so.
