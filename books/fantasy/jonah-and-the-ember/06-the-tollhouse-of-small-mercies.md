## 6. The Tollhouse of Small Mercies

The rain found them an hour past dusk, and by the time the road bent toward the river it had stopped being weather and become a verdict.

Astrid walked with her shoulders hunched against it, her cloak long since soaked through to a weight like wet clay, and told herself for the hundredth time that the shivering was only cold. It wasn't only cold. Since Wardfall she had been hearing things she had no wish to hear — the low continuous groan of the land where the wards had been torn out of it, the thin animal distress of hedgerows and field-mice and the river itself, all of it suddenly *loud*, the way a room goes loud when a door is kicked open. For nineteen years Hollowmere's silence had been a prison. She had not understood until it broke that it had also been a kindness.

Jonah stumbled beside her, caught himself on the air, kept walking. He had been doing that for two days — the small stagger, the recovery, the stubborn refusal to fall — and every time he did it, the ember under his breastbone flared faintly visible through his shirt, a coal-glow like a lantern behind linen.

"You should rest," she said.

"I'm resting *while* I walk." His teeth chattered around the joke. "It's very efficient."

"You're about to fall face-first into a puddle."

"Then I'll be resting face-down. Even better."

She almost smiled. That was the trouble with him, she was learning. Two days ago he'd been a stranger she'd dragged through a broken ward because leaving him at the valley's edge to die had been more than her stomach could bear, and now he was making jokes in the rain, and she was *almost smiling*, and that felt more dangerous than any elder's brand.

Ahead, where the road met the river, there was a light.

Not torchlight — torchlight moved, guttered, breathed. This light sat still and yellow and patient in the dark, spilling from the windows of a squat stone building crouched at the crossing like a toad. A tollhouse, or what had been one, before the roads went quiet and the tolls went unpaid. A sign swung above its door on rusted brackets, too far off to read, creaking in a rhythm that sounded almost like words.

Astrid stopped. So did everything else, in a way — the frogs along the bank, the wind in the reeds, even the rain seemed to lean aside.

"What is it?" Jonah whispered. He'd learned already what it meant when she went still like that.

"There's something living here," she said slowly. "Something old. The birds won't go near the roof." She tilted her head, listening past the rain, past the land's ache, into the small crowded chorus of the night. "And there's a crow on the chimney who is *swearing at us*. Not warning us. Swearing. He's using words I don't know and some I do, and three of them are about your mother."

Jonah blinked water out of his eyes. "My mother?"

"He seems to feel you've been walking too loudly."

"I have been walking *normally*—"

The door of the tollhouse opened.

* * *

The woman who stood in the doorway was perhaps fifty, broad-shouldered, gray-streaked hair hacked short with what looked like a knife and no particular regret. She wore an apron over a man's coat, and she held neither weapon nor lamp, just a wooden spoon, as though she had been interrupted mid-cooking and resented it.

"Well," she said, looking them up and down with the flat appraisal of someone pricing livestock. "Two drowned rats, one of them glowing. Come in before the river decides it wants you instead."

Astrid's hand found Jonah's sleeve. "How did you know he's—"

"The glow, girl. It's not subtle." The woman stepped back from the doorway. "I'm Maren. I was a hedge-witch before hedge-witching became a hanging offense, which means I know exactly what the two of you are running from, and I promise you my stew is better than whatever answer you were about to lie to me with. In. Boots off. There's a cat and she'll judge your feet."

Inside, the tollhouse was warm in a way that seemed to argue with the world outside. A fire burned in a hearth big enough to roast a sheep whole; copper pots hung from beams; bundles of dried herbs crowded the rafters, and among them — Astrid noticed, and made herself not notice — hung sprigs of plants she knew only from her grandmother's whispered warnings. Rowan. Wolfsbane. A dried starflower, silver-petaled, that had no business growing anywhere within a hundred miles.

A tabby cat regarded them from atop a dresser with the weary contempt of customs officials everywhere.

"Sit," Maren said, pointing the spoon at a table scarred by decades of knives and cups. "Talk when you're warm. Not before. Warm people tell the truth; cold people tell stories."

So they sat, and steamed, and a bowl was put in front of each of them — barley and root vegetables and something darkly savory that Astrid could not identify and did not ask about — and it was the best thing she had ever eaten, or her body simply thought so, being nine parts rainwater by then.

Maren watched them eat without eating herself, arms folded, and Astrid felt the scrutiny like a hand between her shoulder blades. It was the same look the elders used to give her across the market square, except the elders' looks had been suspicion wearing politeness, and this woman's was recognition wearing none.

"You're from Hollowmere," Maren said at last. Not a question.

"How could you possibly—" Jonah began.

"The ward-scar." Maren nodded at Jonah's forearm, where the skin showed a pale branching mark like frost-flower or lightning frozen mid-fall. "Wards leave a signature when they spit someone out. Hollowmere's wards were woven by the Vael line, four hundred years back, and they always burn in that pattern. Like a fern. Like a *hand*." Her eyes moved to Astrid. "You pulled him through. Through a live ward. Which means either you're dead and don't know it yet, or you've got a gift the wards decided to ignore."

Astrid set down her spoon. Every instinct she owned — nineteen years of instincts, bred careful and quiet in the valley's hush — said *deny it, deny it, deny it*. But the fire was warm, and the stew was in her belly, and the land outside was screaming, and she was so tired of the shape of lies.

"She talks to animals," Jonah said.

Astrid turned to stare at him. "*Jonah.*"

"What? You were going to take an hour getting to it and she already knows. Look at her face. She knows more than we do." He shrugged, unrepentant, steam still rising off his shoulders. "Besides, you saved my life twice. I can spend a little of your secret if it buys us supper."

"It bought you supper before you spent anything," Maren said dryly. "But your instinct's right, boy. Secrets are currency out here now. Spend them wisely and they buy shelter." She leaned forward, elbows on the table, and for the first time her voice lost its transactional edge and gained something underneath it — something tired and old and careful. "Now I'll spend one of mine, so we're square. Three nights ago, before the sky over the south went the color of a bruise, every bird within ten miles of this house flew north. All at once. At midnight. And the river ran backward for the space of nine heartbeats, and then a prayer got answered down in Ferrow's Bend, and the man who prayed it is a grease-stain and a story now." She looked at Jonah's chest, at the faint coal-light breathing there beneath his damp shirt. "And you walked into my yard carrying the reason why."

The fire popped. The cat, on her dresser, yawned with enormous insolence.

"That's not—" Jonah's hand rose halfway to his sternum and stopped. "It's not a *reason*. It's just — it's mine. It's been mine since the temple burned. I don't even know what it *is*."

"No," Maren agreed. "But I know someone who does. Or claims to. Whether you believe him is between you and your sanity." She raised her voice slightly, addressing the rafters. "Bramble! Get down here and stop eavesdropping. Yes, I know you've been on the beam since they came in. You weigh nothing and you think that makes you invisible."

There was a scrabble of claws, a whir of wings, and a crow dropped out of the smoky dark onto the table between the stew bowls.

Astrid had heard crows all her life. Crows had opinions, mostly about bread and hawks and other crows; their minds were bright little knives, quick and shallow. She had braced herself for that familiar chatter.

What came out of the black bird's beak was a voice. A real one, dry as old paper, sardonic as a judge.

"*Well,*" it said. "*At last. I was beginning to think the girl was going to pretend she couldn't hear me all evening, which would have been tedious, because I have been working on my opening remarks since the bridge.*"

Jonah knocked his stool over backward.

Astrid didn't move. Couldn't. Because the voice was wrong in a way that made the fine hairs on her arms stand up — not the bright shallow knife-sound of crow-thought but something layered beneath it, deep and slow, like a bell heard through a wall. The crow's mind, she realized, was a window, and behind the window was a room, and the room was vast, and mostly empty, and very, very old.

"You," she whispered. "You're not a crow."

"*No,*" said the crow, arranging its feathers with the air of a man settling into a chair. "*I am a crow the way a glove is a hand. The hand that used to fill me is — elsewhere. Dead, probably. Murdered, certainly. But the shape of me remembers, and the shape of me has opinions.*" One glossy eye fixed on Jonah, then on the coal-glow at his chest, and the sardonic dryness fell away from the voice like a mask slipping. When it spoke again it was quieter, and older, and it made the fire seem smaller. "*And I know that ember, boy. I knew it when it was part of something much larger. I sang at its forging, back when there were songs. You are carrying a piece of a murdered god in your chest, and you have been feeding it on fear and road-dust, and it is starving, and starving gods make poor neighbors.*"

The silence after that lasted long enough for the rain to change its mind twice.

Jonah sat down again, slowly, on the fallen stool, because standing had apparently stopped being an option. His hand was pressed flat over his heart now, over the glow. "Murdered," he repeated. "Gods don't get murdered. Gods get worshipped, or forgotten, and the ones everyone forgot just fade. Everyone knows that."

"*Everyone knows a great many comforting things,*" Bramble said. "*Most of them were written by the murderer.*"

Maren had gone to the hearth while the crow spoke. Now she came back and set a cup of something steaming in front of Jonah — not stew this time; it smelled of bitter herbs and honey — and her face, which had been all brisk practicality, was grim.

"Drink that. It'll steady you." She looked at Astrid. "He says that every time. 'Everyone knows.' As if the world were a fair tale with a moral at the end." She pulled out a chair and sat, heavily, like a woman setting down a load carried too far. "Here's what I know, girl, and I'll give it to you plain because plain is all I've got left. I was hedge-witch to six villages on this river. Small work. Birthings and fevers and blessings on the boats. And eleven years ago the king's new church came through — the Crowned God's church, gold sun and iron crown, very handsome — and they asked me to swear that my craft was nothing, that the old powers were nothing, that there was one god and he wore a crown and answered prayers and wanted tithes. And I swore it, because I had a daughter." Her jaw tightened. "And then they burned my daughter anyway, two years later, for a charm she wore under her collar. A *charm*, girl. A knotted thread. And I learned what I should have known at the start: a god that demands you swear other gods never existed is not a god. It's a thief wearing a crown, and it's stealing something, and I have spent nine years trying to find out what."

"And?" Astrid asked quietly.

"And last week," Maren said, "the birds flew north at midnight, the river ran backward, and a farmer in Ferrow Bend prayed for a good harvest and got eaten by the answer. Whatever the Crowned God is stealing, girl, it's nearly done stealing it. The wards are down. The old seals are cracking. Everything that was locked away is waking up hungry." She nodded at Jonah's chest, at the ember's slow coal-breath. "Including that."

Jonah looked down at his own chest as if it belonged to a stranger. "It doesn't feel hungry. It feels — warm. It feels like being *held*."

"*That,*" said Bramble, hopping closer, head cocked, "*is because it loves you. That's the horror of it, boy. The devourer wears love the way I wear feathers. Your ember loves you, and it is starving, and one day soon it will be hungry enough that loving you and eating you will start to look like the same act. Unless we find it somewhere better to put its hunger. Unless we finish what was started the night it was torn out of its murderer's hands.*"

"Torn out," Astrid said. Her mouth had gone dry. "By whom? Torn out how?"

The crow was silent a moment. Behind the window of its mind, in that vast empty room, something shifted — grief, maybe, or the memory of grief, worn smooth as a river stone by nine years of polishing.

"*By the ones who remained,*" it said finally. "*The old faith. What's left of us. We tore our own god apart to keep half of it from the thing that wears the crown — hid the pieces in vessels, in bloodlines, in stones and wells and boys running from burning temples. And we have been waiting eleven years for the pieces to wake up and come home.*" Its eye glittered, wet-black and ancient. "*Which raises the question, Listener, of what you are doing sitting at my table.*"

The word went through her like a fishhook.

"I'm sorry," Astrid said. "What did you call me?"

"*Listener.*" The crow said it the way other creatures said names — carelessly, a label. But the room behind its mind stirred when it spoke the word, and the stirring had weight, had *recognition*, and Astrid felt it settle over her like a mantle she'd been fitted for without ever having seen. "*Don't play modest. You heard me before I spoke. You heard the river run backward. You've been hearing the land scream since the wards fell, and you haven't once thought to wonder why the gift chose you, of all the mouths in the world, to sit inside. Gifts don't choose randomly, girl. They choose their offices. And yours is an office older than the valley that buried you.*"

"I'm not — I'm a miller's daughter. I talk to horses. Badly. The mare at home liked me better than most people, that's all—"

"*The mare at home,*" said Bramble gently, and the gentleness was worse than the mockery, "*told the sparrows you were the first human voice that ever sounded like telling the truth. The sparrows told the wrens. The wrens told the river. The river has been waiting for you a long time, Astrid Kavanagh.*"

Her name. Her full name, spoken by a bird she'd met four minutes ago, in a voice like a bell through a wall, and somewhere beneath her terror Astrid registered the absurdity of it — that she had spent her whole life hiding a secret so large, and the world had known all along, had *always* known, had merely been polite enough to wait until she ran away from home to tell her.

Jonah's hand found hers on the table. She hadn't seen him move. His fingers were warm — warmer than they should be, ember-warm — and they closed around hers with a steadiness that belied the fact that he'd knocked over a stool twenty minutes ago.

"They knew me too," he said quietly. "Before the temple burned. Before I even understood what was in me. Didn't they, Bramble? That's why I ran south. That's why I ended up at the valley's edge. It wasn't chance."

"*Nothing about you two is chance,*" the crow said. "*Chance died with the old gods. What's left is design, and debt, and very little time.*"

Maren stood, and began clearing the bowls, and the ordinary clatter of crockery was somehow the kindest thing anyone had done all night. "Enough," she said. "Prophecies keep till morning; stew doesn't. Boy, there's a pallet by the hearth. Girl, the loft, mind the third rung, it's rotten. Cat sleeps wherever she likes; don't negotiate." She paused at the hearth, a bowl in each hand, and looked at the two of them sitting in the firelight with their hands still linked on the scarred table, and something in her hard face eased by a single degree. "Nine years I've kept this tollhouse for travelers who never come. Tonight it earned its name back. Small mercies. Take yours and sleep."

* * *

Astrid did not sleep.

She lay in the loft under a quilt that smelled of lavender and woodsmoke, staring up through the dark at the underside of the roof, and listened. It was what she did; it was all she had ever done. Below her, Maren moved through her closing-up rituals — banking the fire, latching the shutters, murmuring to the cat in the tone people use when they've given up pretending the animal doesn't understand. On the roof ridge, Bramble sat in the rain he apparently didn't mind, and hummed. That was the strangest thing yet: the crow *hummed*, tuneless and low, fragments of melodies that sounded worn away, like steps in an abandoned stair. Songs with most of themselves missing.

And below everything, beneath the floorboards and the foundations and the river mud, the land went on speaking. The wards of Hollowmere had muffled all of it for nineteen years, and now that the muffling was gone Astrid could hear the whole wounded choir of the world — the river counting its troubles, the old oaks holding their ground like veterans, the small frantic prayers of voles and moths. And threaded through it all, from the south, a pull. A gravity. Something vast drawing every loose thread of wildness toward itself, patient as a drain.

She should have been terrified. She was, partly. But underneath the terror was another thing, newer and stranger, and it took her a long time in the dark to name it.

She was not alone.

For nineteen years her gift had been a solitary cell — a secret shared with horses, hidden from every human voice, a self amputated daily so the visible self could pass. And now, in one night, she had been handed a witch who fed strangers and grieved loudly, a boy whose warmth reached up through the floorboards like a second hearth, and a crow who knew her name and her office and the sound of her truth. A ragged, impossible, half-broken *we*. It was not safety. Safety was a valley with walls, and walls were what had nearly killed them both. It was something else, something she had no word for yet, and she lay in the dark turning it over like a stone found on a beach, feeling its weight, deciding to keep it.

Near dawn, footsteps crossed the room below, and Jonah's voice drifted up, low, pitched not to wake her — which meant, of course, that she heard every word.

"—and if it's starving, what happens when it's done starving?"

Bramble's answer came soft as ash falling. "*Then it eats, boy. Or it's eaten. Or — if the Listener truly is what the river says she is — then someone finds a third road. Sleep now. Roads look shorter in the morning. Lies, too.*"

Astrid lay still until the sounds below went quiet, and then she lay stiller, watching the first gray light seep through the shutter-crack, and thought about the pull in the south, and the ember's slow breath, and the word *Listener* sitting on her shoulders like a yoke she hadn't agreed to carry.

Three days later, she would remember this dawn as the last easy morning they had. Because it was on this morning, as the sun came up red over the eastern hills, that the bells of Vael Crossing began to ring — thirty miles away, impossibly clear, every bell in every tower ringing at once — and Bramble dropped from the roof ridge through the loft hatch, feathers slicked with rain, and said:

"*They've announced a feast. The Feast of Answered Prayers. The crowned thing is going to feed in public, girl, and it has sent riders out with a description of a boy who glows.*"
