## 7. Prayers With Teeth

The city announced itself long before they saw it.

Jonah felt it first — a pressure behind his sternum, like a thumb pressed against the ember in his chest. It had been quiet since the tollhouse, since Maren's salves and Bramble's croaking commentary and the strange, fragile peace of three days on the road with people who didn't flinch from him. But now the ember stirred, restless as a dog before a storm, and when he pressed his palm flat against his breastbone he could feel it turning over, tasting the air.

"What is it?" Astrid asked. She walked half a step ahead of him, as she always did, her hood up against the drizzle, her eyes doing that thing they did in open country — flicking to every bird, every hedgerow, reading the world in a language no one else could hear.

"I don't know." He did know, a little. "It's like... standing outside a bakery. When you're hungry."

"That's a terrible thing for a holy fire to feel like."

"Holy fires have terrible appetites," said Bramble from Maren's shoulder, preening one ragged wing feather with the air of a gentleman inspecting his cuff. "I knew a god once who ate nothing but the last words of dying sailors. Very refined palate. Terrible table manners."

Maren snorted. The hedge-witch had been quieter these past two days, her sharp humor worn thin at the edges, and Jonah had noticed her glancing north whenever she thought no one was looking — toward Vael Crossing, and whatever waited there. But she only adjusted her pack straps and said, "City's close. We should decide how we're walking in. Together we look like a caravan of trouble. Apart we look like four separate kinds of trouble."

"Optimist," said Bramble.

They came over the last ridge as the rain thinned to mist, and Vael Crossing opened below them like a wound in the green world.

Jonah had seen cities before — he'd grown up in the shadow of the cathedral at Saint Verren's, had memorized its buttresses and its bells, had believed until very recently that stone could hold holiness the way a cup holds water. Vael Crossing was bigger than anything he'd imagined. It sprawled across the meeting of two rivers, a sprawl of slate roofs and smoke, and rising out of the middle of it, taller than any cathedral, was a spire of white stone crowned in gold.

Gold, shaped like a crown.

Even at this distance, even through the mist, it caught the grey light and threw it back burning.

"There," Maren said quietly. "That's new. That wasn't there when I—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "When I was young, that hill held a grove. Old oaks. People left offerings, but nobody built on it. You didn't build on holy ground you hadn't earned."

"Someone earned it," Bramble said, and for once the crow's voice carried no joke at all.

Jonah's hand was still pressed to his chest. The ember had gone from restless to eager, straining toward the city like a plant toward light, and the sensation frightened him more than bandits or ward-scarred skin or any of the thousand small terrors of the road. Because he recognized eagerness. He had spent six years in a priest's household learning to name his own hungers so they couldn't rule him, and what he felt now was hunger — vast, directionless, and not entirely his own.

"It wants to go there," he said.

Astrid looked at him. Under her hood her eyes were the grey-green of river shallows, and they missed nothing. "Then we don't go."

"We need supplies. Maren needs a physiker's shop for her herbs. And we can't walk around a city the size of that without adding weeks." He heard himself arguing and hated the sound of it, because he wasn't sure which side of the argument he was on. "We go in careful. We keep our heads down. We leave before dark."

"You said that about the last town," Astrid said.

"And we did leave before dark."

"After you set a mule loose in the market square."

"The mule was unhappy. I fixed the mule."

"You *talked* to the mule. In front of people."

"It was a very understanding crowd."

Bramble made a sound that was either laughter or a coughing fit; with crows it was hard to tell. Maren just sighed the sigh of a woman who had adopted children late in life and already regretted it, and started down the hill, and the others followed, because that was how decisions got made among them — someone walked, and the rest fell in line, and the argument continued the whole way down like a second heartbeat.

* * *

Inside the walls, Vael Crossing smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and, underneath it all, something sweeter. Incense. It drifted from every street corner, from braziers set on iron posts, from the doorways of shops where little shrines had been built into the lintels — niches holding candles and small golden crowns, some no bigger than a thumbnail.

And everywhere, everywhere, people were praying.

Not the way Jonah had grown up seeing prayer — the muttered grace, the distracted genuflection, the old women crossing themselves out of habit older than memory. This was different. This was *fervent*. A fishwife knelt in the gutter beside her baskets, hands clasped, lips moving, tears cutting tracks through the fish scales on her cheeks. Two merchants paused mid-transaction to bow toward the white spire, their bargain suspended, their faces slack with devotion. Children sang in doorways — a skipping-rope rhyme that, when Jonah listened, resolved into words:

*Crown above and crown within,*
*tell Him all the state you're in,*
*ask Him sweetly, ask Him true,*
*and everything He'll give to you.*

The ember in his chest thrummed along with the tune.

"Stop humming," Astrid murmured.

"I'm not—"

"You were. In your chest. I can't hear it, but I can see it. Your whole face changes." She pulled him into the shadow of an awning, out of the flow of pilgrims. They had fallen in with a stream of them somewhere near the gate — travelers in rough cloaks, families carrying bundled children, an old man on a cart drawn by two white oxen, all flowing toward the spire like water finding its level. "What does it feel like? Honestly."

Jonah considered lying. He was getting better at honesty around her, the way a muscle gets stronger with use, but it still cost him. "Like being called home," he said finally. "Like hearing my mother's voice in a crowd. Except I never knew my mother, so I don't actually know what that would be like, and that's the point, isn't it? It feels like the thing I've wanted my whole life without knowing its name."

Astrid was silent for a moment. Rain dripped off the awning between them.

"When the wards cracked," she said, "and magic came into the valley like cold water — for about a minute, before the screaming started, it felt like that. Like putting on a coat I'd been told all my life didn't exist." Her jaw tightened. "Then the godling ate Tomas Hedley's field, and Tomas with it, because he prayed for a good harvest and something answered *literally*. So I don't trust things that feel like coming home anymore."

"No," Jonah agreed quietly. "Neither do I."

But his hand stayed on his chest the whole way through the pilgrim quarter, and the ember stayed warm, warm, warm, like a coal refusing to die.

* * *

They found Maren haggling in a physiker's row off Cordwainer Street, and Bramble — hooded, smuggled, complaining — perched inside her basket while she bought willow bark and dried nettle and a jar of rendered goose fat that she swore by and everyone else swore at. It was while they waited, sheltering under the striped awning of a candlemaker's shop, that Jonah saw the procession.

It began with bells.

Every church bell in Vael Crossing had been recast, he realized — the peals rolling over the rooftops were wrong for the old gods' liturgies, too bright, too many notes, arranged in patterns that snagged in the ear like hooks. And under the bells came the crowd: hundreds strong, moving up the processional way toward the spire, carrying candles. Thousands of candles. The flame-light turned the rain to drifting gold.

At the head of the procession walked priests in white and gold, and behind them, borne on a litter by eight men, a great gilded crown the size of a cartwheel, blazing with candlelight.

"The Reliquary Crown," said a woman beside them, to her neighbor, in the hushed tone of gossip that wants to be sacred. "They say a piece of the Crowned God himself lives in it. Touch it and your prayers fly straight to Him. My sister touched it at springtide and her lame son walked by Lammas. Walked!"

"And the tithe?" the neighbor asked, lower.

"Oh, well. Faith asks faith, doesn't it. One part in ten of whatever you're given. It's only fair."

"One part in ten of *what*, though?" the neighbor pressed. "My cousin gave at the shrine in Marlow Ford and after, he said he couldn't remember his wedding day. Not the faces. Nothing. Just that he'd been happy once, and paid for it."

The first woman crossed herself — then, catching herself, bowed toward the spire instead, awkwardly correcting her gesture mid-motion. "He gives so much," she said, with the slight desperation of belief defending itself. "Is it so much to ask?"

Jonah found he had stopped breathing.

Beside him, Astrid had gone very still, in the particular way she went still when she was listening to something no one else could hear. Her face, always guarded in cities, had drained of color.

"Astrid?"

"The land here," she whispered. "Under the streets. It's — Jonah, it's *thin*. Like a pasture grazed down to mud. Everything growing here is growing on top of something eaten." Her hand found his sleeve and gripped it. "I heard it in Hollowmere when the wards broke, the land screaming. This isn't screaming. This is worse. This is the silence after."

Maren had come back with her basket and her purchases and a look on her face like curdled milk. "Cordwainer Street used to have six shrines," she said flatly. "Old ones. Moss-grown, harmless, half-forgotten. All six are stripped bare. Chiseled clean. There are men going door to door with chisels right now, I watched them. And the candlemaker—" she nodded at the shop whose awning sheltered them, at the window display of little golden crowns, each with a slip of paper beneath it bearing a supplicant's written prayer, waiting to be burned. "That man's father sold charms against the evil eye. Now he sells *this*."

"Faith moves with the times," said the candlemaker himself, appearing in the doorway, a round cheerful man with flour-white hands and wax-burned fingers. "You'll be wanting crowns for the evening rite, goodfolk? The Answering begins at dusk. Best places fill early."

"The Answering," Maren repeated.

"The Crowning of Prayers!" The candlemaker beamed as though they'd asked about a festival rather than pronounced a word that made the hair on Jonah's arms rise. "Once a week, the priests carry the Reliquary Crown to the Steps of Grace, and anyone may come and pray aloud, and the God *answers*. Truly answers! Not like the old days, when you prayed to a stick in a bog and maybe, if the stick felt like it, something happened a season later. Now! Now you ask and you receive, before the whole city, praise His golden name." He leaned in, confiding, delighted. "I'm saving up my tithe myself. Come springtide I mean to ask Him to cure my daughter's lung-fever. Worth every memory, the priests say. What's a memory against a life?"

"What do you mean, memories?" Jonah asked. His voice came out strange.

The candlemaker blinked at him, as if the question were obscene. "The tithe, lad. One part in ten of what you receive. If He grants you a healing, you give Him a tenth part of your remembering. If He grants you fortune, a tenth of your years. Everyone knows this." He smiled, and the smile was serene and bottomless and wrong. "It's not a price. It's *gratitude*."

* * *

They should have left. All four of them knew it later, in the way you know the exact step where the bridge rotted through. Maren said it aloud — "We've seen enough, we've heard enough, let's buy our salt and go" — but dusk was falling, and the bells had changed again, and the crowd was moving like a single creature toward the Steps of Grace below the spire, and Jonah's feet were moving with it before his mind agreed to anything.

Because the ember was *singing*.

Not words. Something older than words, a resonance in his ribs, in his teeth, in the roots of his hair, and it rose as the crowd rose, swelled as the candlelight swelled, until Jonah stood in the great square beneath the white spire with thousands of souls packed around him and the ember in his chest burning like a second heart.

The Steps of Grace were a broad stair of white stone climbing to the spire's doors. Upon them, priests arranged themselves in ranks, gold and white, and the Reliquary Crown was set upon an altar at their center, and when the last bell faded, a hush fell over the square so complete that Jonah could hear candleflames breathing.

An old man was brought forward.

Jonah saw him clearly, though the crowd pressed — a farmer by his hands, a grandfather by the bent shoulders, dressed in his best coat with the collar frayed, helped up the steps by two acolytes who handled him gently, tenderly, the way you handle something precious. The crowd murmured approval. Someone near Jonah whispered, "It's Aldous Fen. His granddaughter has the wasting sickness. He's been tithing all year for this."

The high priest raised his arms. His voice, amplified by some art or other, rolled across the square like a benevolent landslide.

"Beloved of the Crown! Tonight a father of the faithful comes to ask. Aldous Fen, what do you seek of the God Who Answers?"

The old man's voice shook, then steadied. "My granddaughter, Your Holiness. Lira. She's nine. The physicians say the winter will take her." He knelt before the crown, and laid his forehead against the altar's edge, and Jonah heard the whole city inhale. "I ask for her life. Take anything of mine. Anything."

"Then pray aloud," said the high priest, "and be answered."

And the old man prayed.

It was not eloquent. It was a grandfather's prayer, stumbling and plain and utterly naked — he named the girl, named her crooked smile, named the doll she kept though its arm was lost, named the way she laughed through her nose like her dead mother. He prayed with his whole heart pouring out of him like blood, and the crowd wept around Jonah, and Jonah himself felt his throat close, because whatever else this thing was, the love in that prayer was real, human, unmanufactured, worth more than all the gold in the spire.

The Reliquary Crown began to glow.

Light poured from it like dawn through a keyhole, and the crowd gasped and sank to its knees in waves, and the ember in Jonah's chest *roared* — and in that instant, with the ember wide open and his senses flung wide, Jonah saw what none of the kneeling thousands could see.

He saw the prayer.

It rose off the old man like heat-shimmer, visible to whatever the ember had made of his sight: a ribbon of pale gold light, woven through with images — the girl, the doll, the laugh — a thread of pure devotion climbing the air toward the crown. And Jonah saw the crown open. Not the metal. *Behind* the metal. A mouth that was not a mouth, a patience that was not patience, a vast attention bending down through the gilded circle like a depth of black water.

The prayer touched it.

And the devourer drank.

Jonah watched the ribbon of light unravel into the dark, thread by thread, image by image — and watched the images *go out*. The doll. The laugh. The crooked smile, dimming, dimming, consumed. And the drinking did not stop at the prayer. It followed the prayer back down, down into the old man kneeling there, and Jonah saw the light of him — the warm human amber of him — begin to flow upward, slow at first, then faster, a stream, a torrent, a lifetime being siphoned through his open, adoring mouth.

The old man was still praying. He didn't stop. He couldn't. His face was radiant with joy.

"He's answering," the old man whispered. "Oh — oh, He's answering, I can feel her getting well, I can feel it—"

His coat sleeves collapsed around arms that were becoming thinner than arms should be. His white hair went greyer, then white again, then translucent. The acolytes stepped in on either side of him, supporting, smiling, guiding his hands to remain clasped in prayer as the light poured out of him faster and faster, and the crowd was cheering now, cheering the miracle, and the high priest's voice rang out —

"Behold! The God provides! The child will live! Rejoice, beloved of the Crown, rejoice—"

The old man's joy was the last thing to burn.

Jonah saw it go — saw the final bright thread of that enormous, foolish, beautiful love drawn up into the dark behind the gold — and then Aldous Fen folded, gently, like laundry, into the arms of the acolytes, who bore him away with practiced tenderness while the crowd sang a hymn of thanksgiving.

Somewhere a woman was sobbing with happiness. "Lira will live. Lira will live!"

*She would,* Jonah thought, with a horror that had passed beyond shaking and become something cold and crystalline. *That's the teeth of it. The girl lives. The god eats the grandfather whole and calls it mercy, and the city sings.*

The ember in his chest had gone silent. Utterly, terribly silent — the silence of a creature pressing itself small in the dark, hoping not to be noticed. And Jonah understood, with absolute clarity, that the thing wearing King Aldric's crown had felt the ember's flare across the square, was perhaps feeling it even now, a foreign warmth in the flock, a wolf-smell among the sheep—

Across the square, on the highest tier of the Steps of Grace, the high priest turned his head.

Slowly. Precisely. Like a compass needle.

And looked directly at Jonah.

Beside him, Astrid's grip closed on his arm hard enough to bruise. "Jonah," she breathed. "Your chest. It's *glowing*."

He looked down. Through the weave of his shirt, through his clenched fist, light was leaking — faint, gold-edged, unmistakable — the ember answering the call of its murderer across a square full of kneeling witnesses.

The high priest began to descend the steps.
