## 8. The Listener's Burden

The screaming started three days out of Vael Crossing.

Not the screaming of people — Astrid had heard enough of that in the city, enough to last her a lifetime — but the screaming of the land itself. It came up through the soles of her boots and settled behind her teeth like a stone she couldn't spit out. The road north from Vael Crossing ran through country that had once been green: orchards gone silver-brittle, hedgerows bleached to bone, fields where the wheat had died standing upright as if it hadn't wanted to kneel before it fell. And everywhere, threaded through everything like rot through fruit, that low, endless cry.

*Empty. Empty. Empty.*

She had learned to hear it in Hollowmere without knowing what it was — the faint hum beneath the millstone's turning, the grumble of roots in winter, the mare's fevered dreams bleeding into her own sleep. But this was different. This was not the land murmuring to itself. This was the land being *drained*, pulled taut like a hide stretched over a drum, and something vast and patient drinking at the far end of the pull.

"You've stopped walking again." Jonah's voice came from behind her, careful the way he'd been careful for days now, ever since Vael Crossing, ever since the man who had burned alive mid-prayer with his arms flung wide and his face lit up like a bridegroom's.

Astrid didn't turn around. She crouched instead, pressing her bare palm flat against the dead field's crust. The soil was cold in a way soil shouldn't be in late summer. Beneath the cold, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, something whimpered.

"There's nothing here," Jonah said. He'd come up beside her; she could see his boots at the edge of her vision, patched leather, one lace trailing. "The whole valley's dead. It's been dead since we crossed the ridge."

"It's not dead." Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "It's *screaming*. There's a difference."

He went quiet. When she finally looked up at him, he was watching her with that expression she'd begun to dread and crave in equal measure — the one that said he was cataloguing her, filing away another piece of evidence toward some conclusion he wouldn't share.

"The wheat," she said, because she was tired, and tired people tell truths. "When the godling took Harrow's field back home, you remember how I knew which stalks were still alive? How I told Maren which rows to save? I didn't guess. I *heard* them. The ones still living were crying for water, and I could tell them apart from the ones that had already given up." She stood, wiping her palm on her skirt as if she could wipe away the sound too. "I can hear this whole countryside dying, Jonah. Every root. Every worm. Every bird that flew over and decided there was nothing worth landing for. It doesn't stop. It hasn't stopped since Vael Crossing."

The wind moved through the dead wheat, and it made no sound at all, which was somehow worse than any sound.

"That's why you haven't slept," Jonah said quietly.

"That's why I haven't slept."

They walked on. The road bent north along a dry riverbed, following the ghost of water that had been pulled elsewhere or simply drunk. Bramble rode on Jonah's shoulder, uncharacteristically silent, his feathers dull in the grey light. Even the crow, who talked enough for three gods and twice as many fools, had gone quiet in this country. Once, near midday, he opened his beak as if to say something clever about the weather, thought better of it, and closed it again.

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen wayshrine — an old thing, pre-Crowned, its stone worn smooth by centuries of hands that had touched it not in worship but in greeting, the way you'd touch a neighbor's shoulder passing in the lane. Astrid ran her fingers over the worn hollow and felt, very faintly, an echo. A memory of being loved without being fed upon.

Jonah built a small fire, shielding the flame with his body out of habit. They ate hard bread and harder cheese and pretended not to watch each other across the flames.

It was Jonah who broke first. He always did.

"You have to tell me," he said. "What it's doing to you. All of it. Because I've watched you go grey around the eyes for three days, and I've watched you flinch every time we pass a living tree, and I've been patient, Astrid, I have been *so patient*—"

"Have you?" She laughed, and it came out ugly. "Three days. You call that patience?"

"I grew up in a house where my father confessed his sins to a wall every night and called it faith," Jonah said. "I know what silence costs. I'm asking you not to pay it."

The fire cracked. Somewhere far off, a fox screamed — a real scream, animal and ordinary, and Astrid clung to the ordinariness of it like a rope thrown to a drowning woman.

"My mother knew," she said. "Before anyone else did. Before the elders, before the ward-wardens, before I understood it wasn't normal. I was four years old, and I told her the cat was angry because the dog next door had eaten its kill, and she looked at me the way you look at a door you're not sure is a door." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "She taught me to hide it. Not cruelly. She loved me. But she taught me that if the valley ever heard my voice answering theirs, they'd put me out past the wards, and the wards are all that keeps the wolves and worse from coming down into Hollowmere. So I learned to whisper. I learned to listen with my mouth shut. Twenty years of listening with my mouth shut, Jonah, and now the whole world has opened its mouth and started screaming and I can't make it stop and I can't stop hearing it."

Jonah had gone very still. In the firelight his ember-scar showed faintly through his shirt collar, that tracery of gold like veins of ore in dark stone. It pulsed when he was frightened. It was pulsing now.

"I never told anyone," Astrid went on, and found, to her horror, that she couldn't stop. "Not even Maren, not fully. She guessed — hedge-witches guess — but I never said it aloud. Saying it aloud makes it real. Real things can be taken away." She pressed her forehead against her knees. "There. Now you know. Now it's real. Take it, if you want. Everyone always does, eventually."

The silence stretched. Then Jonah moved — not away, as half of her had braced for, but closer, settling onto the cold ground beside her until their shoulders touched.

"I'm not going to take it," he said.

"You don't know that."

"I know it the way I know the ember burns." He turned his head; she felt his breath stir her hair. "Astrid. Look at me."

She looked. His eyes were brown and ordinary and utterly sincere, and behind them, deep down, something golden flickered — the ember looking out through him, the way it sometimes did, like a tenant peering from a window.

"When I collapsed at your valley's edge," he said, "the wards tried to kill me. You know that. You dragged me through anyway, and I've never asked you why, because I was afraid the answer would be pity, and I couldn't stand pity from you. But I'll tell you something I've never told anyone either." He swallowed. "The ember spoke to me before I ever reached Hollowmere. The night I ran from the seminary. It said: *go south, go down, find the Listener.*"

The words landed like stones dropped into still water.

"I thought it meant a priest," Jonah said. "Some hidden hierophant, some old woman in a cave. I walked two hundred miles looking for a title. And then I crawled to your valley half-dead, and the wards spat me back three times, and then a girl came out of the barley with dirt on her hands and fury on her face and *pulled me through* — and the ember went quiet for the first time in weeks. Quiet and warm. Like a dog that's found its person." His voice roughened. "It wasn't looking for a priest, Astrid. It was looking for you. It chose you before I ever saw your face. Maybe before I was born."

"That's—" Astrid's throat worked. "That's madness. I'm a miller's daughter who talks to horses. I'm nobody's Listener."

"You heard the land screaming today," Jonah said. "You heard dying wheat crying for water in a warded valley where magic isn't supposed to work at all. You pulled a god-bearer through wards that kill god-bearers, and the wards let you, because—" he paused, choosing, "—because I think the wards recognized you too. Everything old recognizes you, Astrid. The wards. The ember. That wayshrine you keep touching like it's a friend's shoulder. You've spent twenty years thinking your gift was a disease you caught. What if it's a calling you answered before you could walk?"

She wanted to laugh at him. She wanted to hit him. She did neither, because underneath the wanting was a third thing, vast and terrifying: relief. The relief of a splint coming off a healed break — agony and freedom in the same motion.

"If that's true," she whispered, "then it's not just a trick I have. It's a *duty*. And duties can demand things. My mother hid me to keep me safe, and you'd hand me a burden instead—"

"No." He took her hand. His palm was hot — ember-hot, always a degree too warm, a fact she'd stopped noticing and now noticed all at once. "I'd never hand you anything. I'm telling you what I see because you asked me not to let you drown in silence, and I won't do the same to you. Listen—" a broken laugh escaped him, "—listen. That's all you do. That's all you've done since I met you. You listened to me raving about the seminary. You listened to Maren's regrets. You listened to Bramble's stories even though half of them are lies. You are the only person I have ever met who treats listening as if it were holy work. And maybe it is. Maybe that's exactly what it is."

Bramble shifted on his perch, feathers rustling. "For what it's worth," the crow said, in the tone of one conceding a point under duress, "the old crow-god kept a Listener too. Long ago. Before the Crowned Thing ate the sky's share of prayer. The Listener was the one who heard the world's grief and carried it to the gods' ears, because gods are proud and deaf and will not stoop to hear a sparrow beg." He clicked his beak. "The position has been vacant some time. The benefits are terrible. The mortality rate is worse."

"Bramble," Jonah said warningly.

"What? Truth-telling night, isn't it? I can smell it. Humans reek of it when they finally say the true thing. Like cut onions and courage." He ruffled himself. "Carry on. Pretend I said nothing wise."

But the words had already rooted. Astrid sat with Jonah's hand around hers and the fire burning low and the land's screaming rising around them like floodwater, and for the first time since Vael Crossing she did not try to dam it. She let it in. The dead wheat. The dry riverbed mourning its own bed. The wayshrine's memory of gentler hands. The fox, far off, denning down with kits that would need more than this stripped country could give. All of it, pouring through her, and she stopped fighting and simply *held* it, the way you hold a grieving friend — not fixing, not silencing, just bearing witness.

And the screaming changed.

It didn't stop. But somewhere in the middle of it, as if the land itself had noticed someone finally paying attention, the raw animal terror thinned into something else — something almost like speech. Grief with grammar. Sorrow shaped into words she couldn't parse yet but could feel the edges of, the way you feel the shape of a letter through sealed paper.

"Astrid?" Jonah's voice seemed to come from very far away. "Your eyes—"

She couldn't answer. The pull was stronger here than anywhere she'd felt it, and beneath the pull, beneath the draining, she could suddenly feel the *direction* of it — not vague and everywhere anymore but specific, a line drawn through the world, taut as a bowstring. North. Farther north than they'd yet come. Toward the capital, yes, toward the Crowned God's seat — but not quite. Off-axis. Somewhere between the throne and the wild places, a knot in the bowstring where all these threads of stolen devotion gathered and were drawn down into—

Into something buried. Something old. Something that hurt.

The vision snapped. Astrid came back to herself gasping, on her hands and knees in the cold dirt, Jonah's arm around her shoulders, Bramble cawing alarm into the dark, the fire nearly dead.

"I saw it," she rasped. "I saw where it drinks. Not the palace — deeper, older. There's a place north of here where the pull knots together, like a wound the whole country's been bleeding into. Jonah, the land wasn't just screaming. It was trying to tell me *where it hurts*."

Jonah stared at her, and the ember beneath his skin flared gold through his collar, bright as a struck match, pulsing in rhythm — she realized with a chill — with her own hammering heart.

"It knows you," he breathed. "Astrid — the ember's glowing. It's never done that when you—"

And then, from the darkness beyond the dead fields, from the direction she had just seen in her mind's eye, came a sound that froze them both: distant, vast, and unmistakably delighted — something laughing at having been found.

* * *

They didn't sleep after that. They walked through the small hours with the fire doused and Bramble aloft as scout, and the laughter did not come again, which was somehow worse than if it had. Dawn found them on a ridge overlooking a river valley that still lived — green, startlingly green after the dead country, willows trailing in actual water — and at the valley's heart, half-hidden in morning mist, the ruins of something enormous.

"Tollhouse of Small Mercies is two days east," Jonah said, consulting Maren's scratched map. "If we cut through this valley—"

"We're going down there first." Astrid heard her own voice and barely recognized it: steady, certain, carrying the residue of the land's grammar. "Whatever that knot is, whatever it's feeding — the land led me to it. Or led it to me. Either way, hiding from it hasn't worked, and I'm done running blind."

Below them, mist curled off the living water, and beneath the green and the birdsong, faint but persistent, the land's voice rose to meet her — no longer screaming now, but waiting, attentive, like a crowd gone silent before a speaker steps forward.

At the edge of the ruins, half-drowned in ivy, stood a weathered statue of a crowned figure whose face had been chiseled away long ago — and fresh, at its feet, a ring of scorched earth where hundreds of pilgrims had recently knelt.

Someone had been praying here. Recently. In numbers.

And the ground beneath the ruin was still warm.
