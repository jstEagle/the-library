## 9. A Court of Crows

Maren had been thrown out of better places than this.

She stood at the mouth of a disused quarry three days north of Vael Crossing, watching the rain pool in the hollows of her own cupped hands, and told herself — as she had told herself on every threshold of every hidden thing she'd ever entered — that whatever waited below could not possibly be worse than the Synod's tribunal that had stripped her of her license and her name in the same breath. Hedge-witch. That was what they'd called her when they were being polite. Heretic, when they weren't. She had survived both words. She would survive a quarry full of old-faith survivors who probably hated the Church even more than she did, which was saying something, because Maren's hatred for the Church was the one fire she had never let go out.

"You're doing the face," Bramble said from her shoulder.

"What face?"

"The one where you decide everyone in a room is an idiot before you've met them." The crow shifted his weight, claws finding the seam of her coat. "It's a good face. Very efficient. But maybe save it until after we're inside."

"Maybe save your opinions until after you've explained why a god's remnant is leading us into a hole in the ground."

"Because holes in the ground are where gods go to hide," Bramble said, and there was something under the flippancy then, something old and tired that made Maren glance sideways at him. "You'd know that if you'd ever been one."

Behind them, Astrid and Jonah waited with the particular stillness of people trying not to be nervous. Jonah had his hand pressed flat against his chest again — he did that more often now, Maren had noticed, as though holding a door shut from the inside — and Astrid stood close enough to him that their shoulders nearly touched, which was its own kind of telling. Three weeks on the road together and neither of them had said anything aloud, but Maren had been reading people since before either of them was born, and those two read like a broadsheet printed in very large letters.

"Well?" Jonah called softly. "Is it — is there a way in? I can't see anything."

"That's rather the point of a secret conclave, dear boy."

The quarry had been abandoned for a generation, by the look of it — flooded at the bottom, its terraced walls green with moss, its rusted winch-houses slumped like drunkards. Nothing moved except the rain. But Maren had spent thirty years learning to feel the seams of things: wards, charms, the small dishonesties woven into ordinary places. And this place was *seamed*. The air above the quarry floor shivered faintly, the way heat shivers off a summer road, and beneath the smell of wet stone there was something else — incense, old tallow, feathers.

She found the seam where the illusion met the world and pressed her thumb against it, murmuring the counter-phrase — not a spell, exactly; spells required licenses, and licenses required institutions, and she had burned hers in a brazier on the tribunal steps, which had been either the most defiant or the most foolish act of her life, depending on the day.

The quarry opened like an eye.

* * *

The stairs went down farther than any quarry had any right to be deep, cut into the living rock and lit at intervals by lamps that burned without oil — hedge-craft, or something older, something Maren eyed with professional envy. Bramble rode her shoulder in silence now, and she could feel him gathering himself the way a performer gathers before a stage.

At the bottom, the rock gave way to a vaulted hall that had clearly once served some other purpose — a shrine, perhaps, or a tithe-barn for a god no one remembered — and it was full of crows.

Not only crows. People, too: perhaps sixty souls scattered through the gloom, sitting on benches salvaged from a dozen ruined churches, wrapped in cloaks of every color that gray could be. Farmers with the callused hands of men who had prayed to field-gods their grandfathers' grandfathers had known. A woman whose eyes were milk-white and who turned her face toward each newcomer as they descended, listening to something beneath the sound of footsteps. Two children asleep against an old man's ribs. And everywhere, everywhere, the crows — perched along the vaulting, on the lamp-brackets, on shoulders and knees and the backs of chairs, hundreds of black eyes reflecting lamplight, all of them, Maren realized with a slow crawl up her spine, blinking in unison.

"Oh," she said quietly. "Oh, you absolute dramatic little feathered—"

"They like the effect," said a voice from the far end of the hall.

He sat on what had clearly once been an altar — a crow the size of a hunting dog, larger than any corvid had any right to be, his feathers shot through with threads of silver like frost through black water. One eye was bright and quick. The other was a pale ruin, seamed with an old scar that ran down past his beak into his breast. When he spoke, his voice came out layered, crow-scrape and something deeper underneath, something that made the lamps gutter.

"I am what remains of Corvael," he said. "Once called the God of Small Thieves and Necessary Lies. Once worshipped in six kingdoms. Now—" he spread one wing, showing the pinion feathers missing, the scarred hollow where worship should have kept him whole, "—now I am this. And you are the witch Maren, the Listener Astrid Kavanagh, and the boy carrying a piece of my murderer in his chest."

The hall went very quiet. Sixty faces turned toward them. Somewhere overhead, a hundred crows shifted on their perches with a sound like wind through winter branches.

Jonah's hand went to his chest. "Your murderer."

"The thing your priests taught you to call the Crowned God." Corvael's good eye fixed on Jonah, and Maren saw the boy flinch as though the gaze had weight. "Come and sit, god-bearer. We have much to tell you, and less time than any of us would like."

* * *

They gave them a bench near the front, which Maren suspected was equal parts honor and surveillance. An old woman pressed a bowl of barley soup into her hands without a word, and Maren ate it because she had learned long ago that hospitality in hidden places was a currency you did not refuse.

Corvael spoke, and the hall listened, and Maren found herself leaning forward despite herself, because the crow-god told the story the way no priest ever had: plainly, bitterly, without ornament.

"Before the Crowned God, there were many of us. Not gods as your Church describes them — no thrones, no trumpets. Small divinities. Local ones. A god of the mill-race. A goddess of first frost. Me." He paced the length of the altar, favoring his scarred leg. "We asked little. A bowl of cream. A lie told kindly. A coin left in a wall. In return we kept the fields sweet and the roads safe and the dead quiet in their graves.

"And then came the hunger." His voice dropped, and the layered quality of it thickened until Maren felt it in her back teeth. "We never learned where it came from — whether it crawled up out of the space between worlds or fell down from somewhere worse. It devours devotion. Not faith — faith is a human thing, private, free. Devotion. Worship given outward, prayer aimed at a receiver. It drinks what we drink. And unlike us, it gives nothing back. It cannot make. It can only take, and grow, and take again.

"The old gods bound it. All of us together, in the last act of cooperation our kind ever managed. We tore a fragment from it — the piece that held its binding-knot, its true name, the lock of it — and we sealed the rest beneath the world with oaths written in our own substance." He stopped pacing. "Most of us died doing it. The rest diminished. And the fragment — the key — we hid it inside a mortal line, so that no power could gather it up and use it to open what we had closed."

Maren felt it before she understood it: the silence spreading through the hall, sixty people arriving at the same conclusion at slightly different speeds. Beside her, Jonah had gone white to the lips.

"The ember," he said. His voice cracked on the second word. "The thing in my chest. It's the — it's the lock."

"It is the key," Corvael corrected gently. "And yes, boy. You have been carrying the door out of hell against your heart since before you could walk. Your grandmother's grandmother made a bargain with a dying god, and the price has come due in you."

Jonah stood up. Sat back down. Stood up again, and this time Astrid caught his wrist, and he let himself be caught, and Maren looked away from the two of them because some griefs deserved privacy even in a crowded room.

"And the devourer," Astrid said, steady, always steady, though Maren had heard her voice shake exactly twice on the road and knew the steadiness was load-bearing. "It's awake. It's answering prayers in Vael Crossing. People are burning alive mid-prayer. If it's still sealed—"

"It is not still sealed." Corvael's ruined eye seemed to fix on her as well, though it could see nothing. "A seal is only as strong as the belief that maintains it. For eight hundred years, the Church of the Radiant Flame taught mankind to pour every scrap of devotion into a single distant, silent sky-father — harmless enough, a god-shaped absence, drinking nothing. But hunger is patient, and hunger whispers. Somewhere — we do not know when, we do not know how — it found a crack. A single unanswered prayer, perhaps. A priest who prayed desperately enough to hear something answer back." The great crow's feathers rose along his neck. "It has spent decades cultivating that crack. Building itself a church within the church. Teaching the faithful to pray *to it*, by name, by crown, by throne. Every answered miracle feeds it. Every harvest it grants, every plague it lifts, every king it blesses — devotion, devotion, devotion, poured straight down its open throat.

"And now it has done the one thing we never imagined it clever enough to attempt."

He looked, then, at Bramble.

"You tell it," Corvael said. "You were there."

Bramble hopped from Maren's shoulder to the altar, and Maren watched him draw himself up, and she realized with a strange lurch that the irreverent, sarcastic bird who had eaten half her rations and mocked her cooking for three weeks was frightened.

"It wears a king," Bramble said. His voice was small and flat and carried anyway. "King Aldric of the Ninefold Throne. The most beloved monarch in living memory. The healer of the succession wars. The man whose portrait hangs in every tavern from here to the sea. It slid into him the way rot slides into timber — slowly, from the inside, wearing him out and wearing him as armor. Because who would strike at the Crowned God when the Crowned God wears the face of the one man everyone loves? Who would question miracles performed by a king so holy?" He paused. "I served Aldric's grandfather, in the years when I still had enough divinity left to be worth serving. I watched the boy grow up. He used to feed my flock crumbs on the palace steps and apologize to each bird individually."

The hall was absolutely silent now. Even the children had woken and gone still.

"Aldric is still alive," Bramble finished. "That's the worst of it. The thing doesn't kill its hosts — killing ends the sympathy between them, breaks the disguise. It keeps him. Hollows him out room by room and lives behind his eyes. There are moments — my flock in the capital tell me — moments when the king surfaces. Moments when he weeps without knowing why, or begs his servants in a whisper to run, to fetch someone, anyone, before he forgets how to want to be saved." Bramble's head drooped. "And then the hunger closes over him again, and the king smiles, and blesses the crowds, and eats their love."

* * *

Afterward there was chaos, briefly — questions shouted from the benches, the blind woman rising to speak in a voice that trembled with fury, an old farmer weeping openly because he had prayed to the Crowned God for his blighted orchard and the orchard had indeed bloomed, overnight, impossibly, and he had thanked it. Thanked *it*. Maren watched him grieve and thought: there it is. That's the trap entire. They don't just feed it. They love it. You cannot fight a thing that people love without breaking something in the people too.

Corvael let the noise crest and fall, and then struck the altar once with his beak, a sound like a bell cracking, and the hall stilled.

"There is a rite," he said. "Older than me. The unbinding. What tore the fragment from the devourer can tear the devourer from its host — sever god from king, lock from door, and cast the hunger back into the dark with nothing left to wear and no name left to answer to. It requires three things." He raised one talon. "The fragment itself, freely surrendered by its bearer — the key must consent to turn. It requires the rite spoken at the seat of the host's power, on holy ground the devourer has claimed as its own — the palace chapel, during the Feast of Answered Prayers, when its attention will be spread thinnest across the greatest number of throats. And it requires—"

He stopped.

The stopping lasted three full seconds, and Maren, who had spent a lifetime watching powerful creatures decide how much truth the room could hold, felt her stomach drop.

"It requires a soul," Corvael said finally. "Freely given. A life offered willingly, without coercion, without trickery, by someone present at the rite. The binding was sealed with divine blood. Only a willing death can unseal it. That is the price. That is the toll. Hope has a cost, friends, and I am sorry — I am sorry — that the cost is this."

The hall erupted again, but Maren didn't hear most of it. She was watching Jonah.

She was watching Jonah's face do something terrible and quiet — watching the horror arrive and then, worse, watching it *settle*, watching acceptance move in behind it like a tenant who intended to stay. His hand was flat against his chest. His lips were moving, barely, and she was close enough, and she had spent thirty years eavesdropping on prayers not meant for her ears, and she caught it:

*It wants to go home,* the ember-silence seemed to say, or perhaps Jonah said it, or perhaps there was no difference anymore. *Let me carry it home.*

Beside him, Astrid had gone rigid, her eyes fixed on the middle distance with the expression of a woman hearing something no one else could hear — and Maren remembered, with a cold slide of understanding, exactly what the girl could hear, and what the ember might be saying, and how much of this conversation the two of them were having in a language the rest of the room couldn't touch.

*Astrid,* Maren thought fiercely, willing the girl to look at her. *Whatever it's telling him. Whatever he's deciding. Don't you dare let him decide it alone.*

Astrid's jaw tightened. She didn't look away from Jonah. But her hand found his on the bench between them, and gripped it, hard, and did not let go.

Corvael descended from the altar, limping, and stopped before the three of them, and his one bright eye moved from face to face with the weary patience of a creature who had watched mortals volunteer for noble deaths for longer than most nations had existed.

"So," the last god of small thieves and necessary lies said softly. "Now you know its name — the Hunger Beneath the Crown, the Unmaker of Altars, the thing the old tongue called *Vhalorath*. Now you know where it sleeps and what it wears. And now you know the price." He folded his scarred wings. "The Feast of Answered Prayers falls in nineteen days. The palace gates will open to pilgrims. Decide quickly, children, and decide carefully — because the devourer already knows the ember walks the world again, and it has wanted its key back for eight hundred years."

Above them, unhurried, unblinking, a thousand crows opened their wings at once, and the lamplight went out like a held breath released.

"It isn't waiting for you to come to it," Corvael said in the dark. "It's already coming."
