## 10. The King Who Is Not Home

The palace of Vael Crossing did not look like a place where a god lived. It looked like a place where a god had been *eaten*.

Jonah saw it first from the roof of the tannery across the river, where Maren's contact — a one-eyed woman who smelled permanently of lime and ash — had promised them a view worth the climb. The palace rose from the heart of the city in pale tiers, white stone gone gray at the joints, its spires crowned with gilded suns that caught the morning light and threw it back in hard, blind flashes. Banners hung from every window: white on gold, gold on white, the sigil of the Crowned God stitched so many times over that the original royal arms beneath had been all but devoured by embroidery. Even the stone gargoyles along the cornices had been chiseled off and replaced with carved doves, their beaks open in perpetual song.

Everywhere Jonah looked, he saw the same gesture repeated: something older scraped away, and something newer laid over it like skin grown too tight.

"It used to be the seat of the Aldric line," the one-eyed woman said, following his gaze. She'd given her name as Sef, and nothing else, and she spoke the way people speak when they have decided not to care whether they live. "Three hundred years of kings. Now there's just the one, and half of him's missing."

"Half of him," Astrid repeated flatly. Beside her, Bramble shifted on her shoulder, his feathers lying flat and uncharacteristically silent.

"You'll see." Sef spat over the roof's edge. "They parade him every Feast day. He smiles at the crowds. He blesses the children. And if you stand close enough — I stood close enough, once, when I still thought prayer was a ladder instead of a hook — you can hear it."

"Hear what?" Jonah asked.

Sef turned her single eye on him, and whatever she saw in his face made her answer honestly. "Weeping," she said. "Underneath the smile. Like a man singing while he drowns."

* * *

They went in through the undercroft, because that was where the conclave said the way in lay — though Jonah would remember later, with a bitterness that never quite dulled, exactly how easily that door had opened for them, and wonder.

The undercroft ran beneath the palace's eastern wing like a buried vein. It had been a wine cellar once, then a crypt, then — according to the crow-god's remnant, who remembered such things the way old men remember slights — a temple to gods whose names the Crowned God had eaten along with everything else. Now it was storage. Crates of candle wax. Bales of incense stacked to the ceiling, their sweetness thick enough to chew. And everywhere, everywhere, the white-and-gold: painted on crates, stamped into wax seals, branded into the wood itself as though even the furniture needed to declare its faith or be thrown out.

Jonah moved through the dark with Maren ahead of him and Astrid at his back, and he could feel the ember in his chest doing something it almost never did.

It was *quiet*.

Not calm — he knew calm, the ember's low banked warmth on ordinary days, the way it hummed against his ribs like a cat too dignified to purr. This was different. This was the silence of an animal that has caught a scent it knows, and is deciding whether to run or fight. The ember pulled inward, contracting around itself, and Jonah felt it the way you feel your own heartbeat change when a door opens somewhere in the house at night.

"It doesn't like this place," he murmured.

"Neither do I," Astrid whispered back. In the gloom her eyes were wide and dark, and Jonah knew without asking that she was hearing things he couldn't — the undercroft's stones were old enough to remember being holy, and old stones, she'd told him once, held their memories like teeth hold marrow. "There's grief down here. Old grief. It's soaked into the mortar."

Maren held up a hand and they stopped. Ahead, a stair climbed out of the dark toward a thread of lamplight, and above them — muffled by stone but unmistakable — came the sound of voices raised in unison. Not speech. Song. Hundreds of voices, maybe thousands, all moving together in the slow, rolling cadence of liturgy, rising and falling like tide against a seawall.

"The Feast preparations," Maren breathed. "Three days early and already they're singing. Listen to them. They sound—" She paused, hunting for the word, and Jonah heard it before she found it.

*Hungry.*

That was the thing about the devotion in Vael Crossing that had been gnawing at him since they'd entered the city walls. Prayer, where Jonah came from, had been a small country thing — mumbled graces, harvest blessings, a priest droning through the litany while the congregation's eyes wandered to the windows. Here, the prayers had *teeth*. People prayed the way starving men eat. He'd watched a woman in the market square press her forehead to the cobblestones until they bloodied, whispering thanks for a son cured of fever, and the gratitude coming off her had been so dense, so *flavored*, that Jonah's ember had stirred toward it involuntarily — the way his own mouth watered at the smell of bread.

He had made himself walk away, and hated himself for how much he'd wanted to stay.

"Up here," Maren whispered, and they climbed.

* * *

The passage let out behind the great gallery, in a servant corridor walled with whitewash and hung with framed portraits of dead Aldrics — kings and queens and stern-eyed children, each face systematically defaced. Not scratched out. That would have been simpler. Instead, someone had painted over each set of eyes with tiny golden suns, so that the whole dynasty stared down the corridor in radiant, identical blindness.

"They don't erase the bloodline," Astrid whispered, tilting her head at the portraits. "They *replace* it."

"So the king stays king," Jonah said slowly, working it through, "but he stops being *Aldric*. He becomes—"

"A glove," Bramble said from Astrid's shoulder. The crow's voice was low and stripped of its usual theatrical flourish, and somehow that was worse than any shriek. "I wore a king once myself, boy. Long ago. A small king, a frightened king, in a country whose name has rotted off the maps. He gave me his mouth because he couldn't bear to speak his own grief anymore. And I wore him until there wasn't enough of him left to take off." The crow's head turned, one black eye catching the lamplight. "What waits up those stairs is worse than anything I ever was. Because I only borrowed. What wears Aldric intends to *keep*."

Maren pressed her ear to the door at the corridor's end, then eased it open a finger's width. Beyond lay the gallery itself — a vaulted hall running the length of the eastern wing, its far end opening through tall arches onto a balcony overlooking the Grand Court, where the singing came from. And between the arches, framed in light, they could see the throne.

And the king.

* * *

King Aldric sat alone.

That was the first wrongness, and it took Jonah a moment to understand why it was wrong. There should have been guards. There should have been priests, attendants, petitioners, the whole apparatus of a court that had made worship into government. But the gallery was empty except for the king, and the emptiness around him had a curated quality, like a stage cleared for the second act.

He sat on a throne of white stone, and he was weeping.

He did not move otherwise. His hands lay open on the arms of the throne, palms up, in the posture of a man receiving — and Jonah understood with a lurch that this was not a posture of rest. This was a *function*. The hands were cupped the way you cup them under a tap. The king was receiving something, constantly, invisibly, from the air itself, and his body was shaped around that reception the way a riverbed is shaped around water.

And he wept. Silently, steadily, tears tracking down a face that was — Jonah strained to see — still handsome, still young-ish, perhaps forty, with the fair hair and strong jaw that the coins bore. But the face was slack in the wrong places and taut in the wrong places, like a mask worn slightly askew, and the eyes—

The eyes were open. And they were not crying.

That was the detail that would follow Jonah into sleep for weeks afterward: the eyes dry and clear and faintly luminous, gold-shot, *attentive*, while the face below them wept and wept. Two creatures sharing one skull. The thing behind the eyes watching the world with vast, patient hunger; the man trapped underneath it, leaking grief through every seam the thing hadn't thought to seal.

"Oh," Astrid breathed beside him. Her hand had found Jonah's sleeve without either of them noticing. "Oh, Jonah. He's still *in there*."

"I know."

"No — you don't — I can *hear* him." Her voice cracked, dropped to almost nothing. "Through the stone, through the floor, it's faint, but he's praying. He's been praying for years. The same prayer, over and over, and no one hears it because the thing wearing him is the thing he's praying *to*." Her hand tightened on his sleeve hard enough to hurt. "*Let me out. Let me out. Let me out.*"

The singing from the court below swelled, and King Aldric rose.

It was not the way a man rises. It was the way a puppet rises when the hand above remembers it exists — a smooth, jointless unfolding, too graceful, the robes settling around him like poured wax. He walked to the balcony rail. Below, Jonah could see the edge of the crowd now, thousands of upturned faces, thousands of open mouths pouring devotion upward like smoke. And the king lifted his hands — palms still upturned, always upturned — and the light changed.

It came down out of the air and into him. Jonah felt it pass *through* the building, through the stone under his feet, a vast slow current drawn from ten thousand faithful hearts and funneled into one standing figure, and the figure drank, and drank, and the gold in its eyes brightened, and somewhere inside that borrowed body a man screamed without sound, and kept praying, *let me out, let me out.*

And the ember in Jonah's chest recognized it.

That was the worst part. That was the part he would carry like a stone in his shoe for the rest of his life. Because the hunger pouring off the throne — huge, bottomless, patient, the hunger of a thing that had been starved for centuries and had learned to feed on love itself — that hunger was not alien to him. It was *familiar*. It was the ember's voice in his dreams, the wanting that coiled through him on bad nights, the pull toward Astrid's gift that he had never once spoken aloud because he was ashamed of it. The devourer's hunger and the ember's hunger were the same hunger. One of them had simply been given a kingdom to eat.

"We're the same as it," he whispered, and his stomach turned on the words. "That's what nobody says. That's what the conclave won't say. It's not some other kind of thing than us. It's what we are, with no one to stop us."

Bramble ruffled his feathers. For once, the crow didn't argue.

Below, the king finished drinking. The crowd's song broke into cheers, into sobbing, into the ecstatic chaos of ten thousand people who had touched divinity and wanted more. And for one moment — one moment only — the golden light in the king's eyes guttered, flickered, dimmed, and the face beneath looked out.

Jonah saw him. Really saw him. A tired man with tear-salt on his cheeks, looking out over his adoring people with an expression Jonah had last seen on his own face in a mirror, the night before he ran: the expression of someone standing in a burning house, smiling at the neighbors through the window, because it was easier than screaming.

Then the light surged back, the mask settled, and King Aldric smiled down at his people with a warmth that reached all the way to his borrowed eyes, and turned, and walked back into the deeps of the palace, alone.

* * *

They did not speak until they were three streets away, in the lime-smelling dark of Sef's back room, with the shutters barred and a single candle burned down to a stub.

"He's alive," Astrid said. She was sitting very straight, very still, the way she sat when she was holding herself together by main force. "The king. He's alive in there. He's been alive the whole time, praying, and every prayer gets swallowed by the thing he's praying to, and it *feeds on that too*, Jonah, it feeds on his despair the same as their devotion, it's got him plugged in at both ends—"

"Astrid."

"—and everyone in this city loves him, they *love* him, and none of them know they're loving a cage—"

"*Astrid.*" He took her hands. They were cold. "I know. I saw him too."

She looked at him, and something in his face must have told her, because she stopped, and her breath came out shaky, and she said, quietly: "You know what he feels like. From the inside."

Jonah let go of her hands and sat down on the floor with his back against the wall, because his legs had opinions about standing. "When it drank today — the crowd's faith, all of it funneling into him — my ember *ached*. Not fear. Ache. Like hearing your own name called in a stranger's voice." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "All my life I've been afraid of what's in me. The priests said it was a splinter of the divine, a blessing, a burden, pick your word. And today I finally saw what it looks like when a thing like that grows up. It looks like a king on a throne, weeping behind eyes that aren't his, eating a country one prayer at a time."

"That's not going to be you," Astrid said.

"You don't know that."

"I know *you*." She crouched in front of him, fierce and close. "You ran. Do you understand? The thing in Aldric never had anyone run from it. You've spent your whole life running from your own hunger, and I used to think that was sad, and now I think it might be the only reason any of us are alive. You know the shape of the cage because you've lived in it. Which means you're the only one of us who knows how to spring it."

From the shadows by the shutter, Bramble made a sound Jonah had never heard from him before — a low, hollow croak, almost like mourning. "The unbinding," the crow said. "You're thinking about the unbinding."

Jonah said nothing.

"Boy." Bramble hopped closer, head cocked, one eye fixed on him with terrible gentleness. "Whatever you're turning over in that clever, guilty skull of yours — the rite demands a soul freely given. Freely. Not coerced, not calculated, not smuggled past the gods on a technicality. If you go to that altar planning your own martyrdom, the rite will know, and it will fail, and it will take you anyway. Do you hear me? A lie of generosity is still a lie."

"I hear you," Jonah said, which was true, and meant nothing, which was also true.

Astrid searched his face for a long moment. He watched her decide — he actually watched the moment happen behind her eyes — that he was grieving, not scheming, and he hated himself for how relieved he was to be underestimated.

Outside, the bells began. Evening service, ten thousand voices rising again, the whole city exhaling its love toward the palace where a man knelt in a golden cage, praying *let me out* to the thing that ate his prayers. Jonah closed his eyes and felt the ember turn over in his chest like a sleeper troubled by a dream, and thought, with a clarity that felt almost peaceful:

*I know how to open the cage. I just have to make sure the key isn't me.*

He was still thinking it when the knock came at the door — three soft raps, the conclave's signal, and Sef rose to answer it, and Jonah did not yet know, could not yet know, that trust was about to become the most expensive thing any of them owned.
