## 12. Hunted Through Holy Ground

The bells began at dawn.

Astrid heard them before she was fully awake — a deep, rolling peal that came out of the east and did not stop, one village's tower answering the next like a line of beacons made of sound. She sat up in the bracken with her heart already hammering, and beside her Jonah stirred, his hand flying to his chest where the ember slept beneath his ribs.

"They know," he said. Not a question. His voice was rough with sleep and something older than fear.

"They've known since Vael Crossing." Astrid stood, brushing dew from her knees. Three days since the undercroft, three days since Maren had turned to face the templars so they could run, and every mile north had been a mile of watching the roads for white-and-gold cloaks. "But bells mean they've stopped guessing. Bells mean they're *certain*."

Bramble dropped out of a rowan tree onto her shoulder, feathers slicked flat with displeasure. "Certain," the crow repeated sourly. "That's a church word. Church words are never good. In my day, when a god wanted you found, it sent ravens. Ravens at least had the decency to be bribable."

"Can you see them?" Astrid asked.

"I can see everything, which is a curse of my position." He lifted off, circled once above the treeline, and came back down with his voice gone quiet in the way that frightened her more than shouting would have. "Riders on the road behind us. Twelve. Maybe fifteen. And ahead—" he hesitated, which Bramble never did— "ahead there are more bells. The whole pilgrim road to Saint Ellowen's shrine is ringing. They're not chasing us, Listener. They're herding us."

Jonah got to his feet slowly, one hand pressed to the ember. When he spoke, it was half to himself, half to the fire inside him. "Consecrated ground. Every field between here and the shrine has been blessed and re-blessed for a hundred years. The Crowned God's faithful walk these roads barefoot." He looked up, and Astrid saw the terrible clarity in his face. "My gift doesn't work here. The ember can't hide itself on holy ground. Every step we take on their roads, I'm a lit torch in a dark field."

"Then we don't take their roads," Astrid said.

She closed her eyes and did the thing she had spent her whole life hiding, the thing that in Hollowmere would have seen her cast out past the wards with nothing but the clothes she wore. She opened herself to the land.

It came in like a tide. The hedge-sparrows first, small bright chattering minds full of seed and territory. Then the foxes, cool and sly and half-asleep in their earths. Then deeper things: a badger's patient dark, a hare's wire-strung panic, the slow green attention of an old oak's rookery. And underneath all of it, faint but unmistakable, the ache she had been hearing for weeks now — the land itself, groaning under the pull of the devourer's hunger, its wild places thinning like ice in spring.

*Run,* she said into all of it, the way her mother had taught her to say it without words, shapeless and wide. *Something hunts us. Show me the ways that don't have roads.*

The answer came back as a tugging, a leaning, the way water finds a downhill. A deer path threading the western wood. A ford where the river went shallow over stone, too stony for horses. A hollow way sunk below the fields, ancient and sunken, where drovers had walked cattle before any king was crowned.

"There," she said, opening her eyes. "West. The beasts will keep us ahead of them."

Jonah stared at her — not with the fear she'd grown up seeing on faces in Hollowmere, when someone guessed what she could do, but with that open, wondering grief he always wore when her gift showed itself. As if each time it hurt him a little, that anyone had ever made her ashamed of it.

"You hear them," he said. "All of them."

"All the ones who'll listen."

"Then let's give them something worth listening to," said Bramble, and lifted off into the grey morning.

* * *

They ran all day.

The hollow way took them west beneath banks hung with honeysuckle, and Astrid walked with her senses flung wide like a net, reading the country through ten thousand small minds. It was exhausting in a way no one who didn't carry the gift could understand — being everywhere at once, feeling the hare's terror when a stoat found its form, the rooks' bickering politics, the slow cold dreams of fish in the ford. But it kept them alive. Twice the deer paths bent away from clearings seconds before riders crossed them; once a vixen screamed from a hedgerow on purpose, drawing two templars' horses toward a bog that swallowed them to the girths.

By late afternoon they had put six miles of broken country between themselves and the road, and Astrid dared to hope.

Then the bells changed.

Not the rolling peal of search anymore — this was a single tolling, slow and deliberate, from the shrine ahead of them, and every beast within three miles felt it the way she did. She felt it as a wrongness in the sparrows, a flattening; the songbirds went silent in patches, spreading outward from the shrine like frost.

"What is it?" Jonah caught her arm as she staggered.

"The bell." Her mouth had gone dry. "It's not calling men. It's calling *faith*. There's a rite at the shrine — they're pouring devotion into the ground, and the ground is—" she pressed a hand to her temple— "the ground is turning against everything wild in it. The hedges are going quiet. Jonah, if they bless the whole countryside, there'll be nowhere left for anything with a gift to stand."

The ember answered before Jonah could. Heat bloomed through his shirt, and he gasped, doubling over, and when he straightened his eyes held a light that wasn't entirely his own. When he spoke, the voice was layered, his and another beneath it, old and burning:

*"She is right. This is how it ate the last world. Not with armies. With altars. Every prayer is a fence-post; every hymn, a wire. Until the wild things have no ground left to stand on, and then the god walks in and harvests what cannot flee."*

"Bramble," Astrid whispered. "Is that—"

"My old master's voice, or what's left of him," the crow said grimly from her shoulder. "And he'd know. He watched it done before." He ruffled his feathers. "Well. If they want a war of consecration, we'll give them a war of crows and foxes. Listener — how many voices can you hold at once?"

"As many as will listen."

"Then listen well, because I count thirty riders coming down the pilgrim road, and they've brought dogs."

* * *

The dogs were the worst part.

Templars on horseback she could manage — horses were sensible creatures, easily persuaded that adders lay in the road ahead, that the bridge was unsound, that home was a better idea than duty. But the hunting dogs were another matter. They were bred to the chase, their minds narrow and blazing and utterly given over to the scent, and when Astrid pushed at them she met not malice but joy, a pure and terrible happiness in the finding, and it broke something in her to twist such gladness into fear.

Still, she did it.

She reached into the pack as it crossed the ford and gave them the river's voice: deep water, cold water, the smell of drowned things. Half the pack balked on the stones, hackles up, and their handlers cursed and dragged them. The other half came on.

So she gave them the boar's voice instead — a big old boar she could feel rooting in the beech-mast two valleys over, and she poured his musk and his anger into the hounds' narrow world until they believed a wounded boar lay in the fern brake ahead. The pack swung toward it, joyful, and the templars followed their dogs into country where the deer paths she'd been shown looped and doubled and led nowhere a man on horseback wanted to be.

"It won't hold them long," she panted. They were climbing now, up through birch scrub toward a ridgeline, Jonah hauling himself along by root and stone, the ember pulsing with every toll of that distant bell. "Dogs aren't stupid. They'll sort the lie from the truth eventually, and then they'll be angry."

"Everything's angry eventually," Bramble said. "It's the natural condition. What matters is being somewhere else when it arrives."

They crested the ridge as the sun went down red behind them, and Astrid looked back once at the valley they'd crossed. She saw the pilgrim road strung with torches, the shrine glowing like a coal, and rising above it all — faint, only visible because she knew how to look — a darkness that fed. The answered prayers going up from the shrine like smoke, and something vast drinking them.

Her gift, the thing she'd hidden her whole life, the thing that had made her small and strange and alone in Hollowmere — it had bought them a day. A day of life, purchased with a hundred small borrowed voices.

"Is it worth it?" Jonah asked quietly beside her. He was watching her face, not the valley. "Using it like this. It costs you. I can see it costs you."

She thought about lying to him. She found she didn't want to.

"When I was twelve," she said, "I told a neighbor's cow her calf had died in the birth, because no one else would, and she needed to hear it from someone. Someone saw me standing in the byre talking to nothing. My father made me swear on the wards that I'd stop. And I did stop — outwardly. But I never stopped listening. I used to lie awake at night hearing everything, all the small lives of the valley, and I thought, *this is the loneliest thing in the world, to be the only one who hears.*" She turned to look at him, at the faint glow under his skin, at the crow on her shoulder. "Today I wasn't lonely. Today every voice I touched was fighting on our side. So yes. It's worth it. Whatever it costs."

Jonah didn't say anything. But the ember flared gently, warm rather than burning, and she felt — absurdly, impossibly — that it had been listening too. That it approved.

Then Bramble exploded off her shoulder, screaming a warning, and the night came apart.

* * *

They came out of the dark on the ridgeline — not templars this time but something worse, shapes that moved wrong, too smooth, too fast, white cloaks that didn't ripple in the wind because there was no wind. Consecrated wraiths, Maren had called them once, when she'd been drunk enough to talk about the church's inner workings. Prayers given legs. Devotion with teeth.

There were four of them, and they did not breathe, and they did not tire, and the beasts could not help her because they were not beasts at all.

"Astrid!" Jonah shoved her behind him, and the ember tore free of restraint, light pouring through his skin like a lantern through a shutter. The wraiths stopped. Turned. Focused on him with a hunger she recognized — the same hunger she'd felt pouring off the devourer's shrine, vast and starving, drawn to the one thing in the world it could not digest.

"The ember," one of them said, in a voice like a hymn played backward. "Return what was stolen."

"Come and get it," Jonah said, and his voice shook, and he did not move aside.

Astrid did the only thing left. She reached out — not to beasts this time, but to everything else. To the rooks settling in the ridge-top trees, to the foxes in the scree, to the moths, the mice, the slow worms, the whole small nation of the wild night. *Not to fight,* she told them. *To witness. To crowd. To be.*

They came. Rooks streaming out of the trees in a black ragged storm, foxes flowing up the slope, moths rising in clouds around the wraiths' blank faces, thousands of small lives suddenly present and loud and *alive*, and the consecrated things faltered — because they were made of prayer, and prayer is meant to rise from living throats, and here was a wall of living throats screaming wordlessly at them, drowning their hymn in sheer unnumbered breath.

The wraiths shrieked — a sound like tearing linen — and broke apart into motes of pale light that drifted upward like reversed snow.

For a moment the ridge was silent except for breathing. Rooks settled back into the trees. A fox looked at Astrid for a long moment with bright amber eyes, then trotted away into the dark.

Jonah sank down onto a rock, shaking, the ember dimming to a coal. "You drowned their prayer," he said wonderingly. "With *life*."

"They were made of dead words," Astrid said. Her hands were trembling. "Words need mouths. I just... gave them other mouths to compete with."

Bramble landed on the rock beside Jonah, and for once the crow had nothing sardonic to say. He smoothed his feathers twice, three times, and then, quietly: "My master would have liked you, Listener. He had a softness for small loud things."

Ahead of them, far to the north, past the ringing shrine and the torch-lit roads, the land rose into mountains — and in the mountains, somewhere, the conclave waited, and Maren waited, captive, and answers waited. The bells behind them tolled on, patient, certain.

But between Astrid and the peaks, a new sound was rising. Not bells. Drums. Slow, deep, rhythmic, coming up the pilgrim road from the south — from the direction of the capital.

From the direction of the Feast of Answered Prayers.

"They're not herding us anymore," Jonah said softly, the ember-light catching the dread in his eyes. "They're summoning something. And whatever it is, it's walking faster than we are."

Astrid looked north at the dark mountains, and for the first time since Hollowmere fell, she was afraid not of being caught — but of arriving too late.
