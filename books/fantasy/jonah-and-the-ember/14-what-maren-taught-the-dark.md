## 14. What Maren Taught the Dark

The cell smelled of old incense and older blood.

Maren had been counting the hours by the drips. There was a crack in the ceiling above her, some flaw in the consecrated stone, and every so often a bead of water gathered there, swelled, and fell into a puddle that had long since stopped growing. She counted them because counting was something she could do. Counting kept her from thinking about her hands.

They had taken her rings first — the silver ones, the ones with the charmed settings she'd spent thirty years engraving with hedge-craft and half-remembered god-tongues. Then they'd taken the skin of her palms, or most of it, pressing her fingers to a hot iron plate until the flesh lifted away like wet parchment. The high priest had watched the whole time with an expression of mild scholarly interest, as though Maren were a text he was translating rather than a woman he was unmaking.

"Again," he had said, at the end of the second day. "But slower this time. She's stubborn, not stupid. Stubbornness is just stupidity that's found a purpose."

Maren had laughed at that. It had cost her two teeth — one of the acolytes had a temper and a cudgel — but it had been worth it, because the laugh had made the priest pause, and pausing meant she was still a person to him, and being a person meant she could still be negotiated with, lied to, misled. A thing was only ever broken. A person could be *steered*.

She sat now against the far wall of her cell, knees drawn up, wrists chained loosely enough that she could cradle them against her chest. The burns had begun to scab. Scabs were good. Scabs meant the body hadn't given up on her even if her mind wandered sometimes, drifting toward the door, toward the road south, toward a tollhouse where a crow named Bramble was probably eating everything in her pantry and complaining about the quality of her tea.

*Don't think about the tollhouse.* Thinking about the tollhouse made her eyes sting, and stinging eyes were a weakness, and weaknesses were things the dark drank.

Because that was the truth of this place, the thing she'd understood by the end of the first night: the Crowned God didn't only eat faith. It ate *certainty*. Every prayer answered here in the capital fed it, yes, but so did every doubt crushed, every question silenced, every soul that looked at the burning supplicant in the temple square and decided not to wonder why. The devourer gorged itself on the moment a believer stopped believing anything else. And so Maren did the only thing a hedge-witch of her particular talents could do.

She wondered. Loudly. In the privacy of her own skull, she wondered magnificently, extravagantly, with the full theatrical flourish of a woman who had once talked her way out of a hanging by convincing three village elders that the rope was cursed.

She wondered whether the god in the throne room was really a god at all, or just hunger wearing divinity like a stolen coat. She wondered whether King Aldric wept behind those glassy eyes because some sliver of him was still home. She wondered what a creature would have to have lost, to need worship the way a drowning man needs air — and whether a thing that needy could ever truly be strong.

Doubt, Maren had learned across sixty years of small mercies, was not the opposite of faith. Doubt was the bone inside faith, the thing that kept it from being swallowed whole. And bones, she knew better than anyone, could be set. Could be broken. Could be used as weapons.

The lock turned.

Maren straightened, arranging her burned hands in her lap with the studied casualness of a queen receiving visitors. The door swung open on candlelight and the tall grey silhouette of High Priest Corvane — though his name had not been Corvane originally; she'd heard the guards call him something else, something older, before the temple records had been purged. Names, like gods, could be worn.

"You're awake," he said. "Good. Sleep is a mercy I can withdraw."

"You say the most romantic things." Her voice came out rougher than she'd have liked. "Is this a social call? You should have brought wine. Prisoners judge hosts harshly."

Corvane closed the door behind him and settled onto the stool across from her, folding his long hands. Up close, in the candlelight, Maren could see the fine tremor in his fingers — barely there, a hummingbird's flutter, gone when he noticed her noticing. Interesting. Interesting and possibly useful.

"They took the others," he said. "Your companions. The witch-boy, the Listener, the god-bearer. They crossed the river at Wrenford two nights ago."

"I wouldn't know anything about that," Maren said. "I've been very busy being tortured."

"The conclave member who sold you to us says otherwise. He says you were headed for the palace. That the ember is a fragment of the imprisoned god, and that your ragged little family means to use it in some rite." He leaned forward slightly. "I am offering you the chance to confirm this. Freely. Before I am required to stop asking nicely."

"And what does 'not nicely' look like?" Maren glanced down at her ruined palms. "Oh wait. I've seen the brochure."

Something flickered across Corvane's face — annoyance, yes, but beneath it, briefly, something that might have been respect. Or recognition. He was, she suspected, a man who had once asked questions himself, back before the questions got him reassigned to the priesthood of a hungry god.

"Maren of the Tollhouse," he said. "Disgraced hedge-witch. Expelled from the Vael Crossing circle for — let me recall the charge — 'teaching smallfolk charms the church reserved for the ordained.' You were never a great sinner. You were an inconvenience. There is a difference, and the difference is that inconveniences can be rehabilitated."

"Oh, I *like* that word. Rehabilitated. It sounds so gentle. Like being put through a mangle."

"It could be gentle." His voice dropped, went soft, went sincere — and Maren felt the trap in the sincerity, the way she'd feel a snare under leaves. "You are old. Your hands will never hold a needle properly again. Your circle cast you out decades ago. No one is coming for you. But the church has infirmaries, Maren. Healers. Warm rooms. And all you must do is tell me where the god-bearer is going, and what the rite requires."

There it was. The freely offered lie wrapped in a freely offered kindness. Maren let the silence stretch, let her shoulders sag a fraction, let her eyes go distant and tired — performed the broken old woman down to the last trembling breath, because performance was the oldest magic she knew, older than charms, older than wards, older than the names of gods.

And then, quietly, she began to lie.

"The boy," she said. "Jonah. He doesn't know what the ember wants. None of us do, fully. But the rite..." She paused, licked cracked lips, watched Corvane lean in. "The rite requires a vessel. A living body the god can pour itself into, once it's cut free of the king. Fresh. Young. Already carrying divine fire, so the seams don't show."

Corvane had gone very still.

"They mean to give the devourer Jonah," Maren continued, letting horror bleed into her voice, letting it crack. "That's why they took him north. To a place where the wards are old and the ground remembers how to hold a god. And if you want to stop them, you'll need to move fast, because—" she faltered, played the reluctant traitor to its final inch— "because the conclave has already sent word ahead. There are people in Hollowmere preparing. Old-faith people. They know you're coming."

Every word of it false. Every word of it a stone rolled into the enemy's road. If Corvane believed her, he'd split his forces — send hunters racing north to a valley where nothing waited but sheep and suspicion, while Astrid and Jonah slipped east toward the coast, toward whatever the conclave planned next. And if he didn't believe her—

Well. Then she'd bought time the expensive way.

Corvane rose slowly. In the candlelight his shadow climbed the wall behind him, thin and crooked, and for a moment Maren thought she saw something else in the shadow too — a shape too large for the man casting it, a suggestion of vast wings folded wrong, of a hunger with a silhouette.

The god listened through him sometimes. She'd felt it in the interrogations, that pressure against the inside of her skull, patient and enormous, tasting her thoughts for certainty to strip away. She'd learned to keep her true purposes buried under layers of trivial memory: recipes, hymn fragments, the price of wool in Vael Crossing the year of the floods. Let it dig. Let it find nothing but turnips and thread counts.

"You are lying to me," Corvane said.

"Obviously," Maren said. "But isn't it a lovely lie? So much effort went into it."

He stared at her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed — a dry, rusted sound, like a hinge that hadn't been oiled in years. "You know what the god offers its faithful, Maren? Answers. Every question resolved. Every doubt devoured. Do you know what that's like? To finally be *certain*?"

"I imagine it's like being dead," Maren said, "but with better singing."

The blow, when it came, was open-handed and almost gentle — a backhand that rocked her head against the stone and left her ears ringing. Almost gentle. That was the worst part. Even the violence here was administered with the weary patience of a teacher correcting a slow pupil.

"The Feast of Answered Prayers is in nine days," Corvane said from the doorway. "On that day, the god will walk among the faithful openly. Whatever your friends intend, they will fail, because on that day there will be nowhere in this city that is not His." He paused. "Unless, of course, someone gives me a reason to doubt my own certainty. But no one has ever managed that."

The door closed. The lock turned. Maren sat in the dark, cheek throbbing, hands burning, and smiled until her split lip bled.

Nine days. He'd told her nine days. Either he was arrogant enough to hand a prisoner the calendar, or — and this possibility warmed her more than any hearth — some small corner of Corvane wanted to be doubted. Wanted, desperately, to lose.

Either way: nine days. She pressed the number into the deepest vault of her memory, beneath the turnips and the hymns, and held it there like a coal cupped in burned hands.

*Hold on, children,* she thought at the ceiling, at the walls, at the whole indifferent city above her. *Grandmother's still teaching.*

* * *

They moved her on the fourth day.

Not to another cell — to a chamber above the temple proper, with a real bed and a window barred but glazed, and a bath, and clean bandages for her hands applied by an acolyte whose touch was careful and whose eyes wouldn't meet hers. Rehabilitation, Corvane had called it. Maren recognized the shape of the strategy immediately: comfort as a lever. Pain softened the body; kindness went after the will. She'd used both herself, over the years, on stubborn fevers and stubborn men.

She also recognized the acolyte.

Young. Sixteen, perhaps seventeen, with the raw-knuckled hands of a farm boy and a tonsure grown out unevenly, as though he'd done it himself with a kitchen knife. He changed her bandages every morning and every evening, and he never spoke except to murmur the liturgy of dressing wounds, which wasn't the temple's liturgy at all but something older, something his grandmother might have taught him, words that belonged to hearth-craft and not to the Crowned God.

On the sixth evening, as he wound linen around her palm, Maren said, "That's a mending-charm you're muttering."

The boy flinched. "It's a prayer."

"It's a charm with a prayer's clothes on. My grandmother used the same one. Different words up in the hill country, but the same bones." She watched his ears go red. "Who taught you?"

Silence. The linen whispered. Then, very quietly: "My mother. Before the temple took me for tithe." He tied off the bandage with a knot that was pure farmyard, pure habit, nothing of the seminary in it. "She said the words worked better if you meant them."

"And do you mean them?"

Another silence, longer. When he finally looked up, his eyes were wet and furious and terrified, all three at once, and Maren felt something in her chest twist tight — because she knew that look. She'd worn it herself at fourteen, when the Vael Crossing circle had found her charming pigs and told her the gift was sin.

"I mean them," he whispered. "I mean them and I hate that I mean them and I pray every night for the god to take the meaning out of me and He won't, He *won't*, and Father Corvane says doubt is the devil's seed but I can't stop—"

"Hush." Maren covered his knotted work with her bandaged hand. "Listen to me, boy. What's your name?"

"Tobin."

"Tobin. Doubt isn't the devil's seed. Doubt is the *root*. Nothing grows without it — not wheat, not faith, not love. A man who never doubts loves nothing, he only consumes. Do you understand? The god you serve eats certainty because certainty is easy to swallow. But you—" she tapped his chest, right over the heart, gently, with two scorched fingers— "you keep asking questions. Guard that. Water it. It's the holiest thing in this building."

Tobin stared at her. Somewhere below them, the evening bells began, and the sound crawled up through the floor like something swimming.

"He answers prayers," Tobin said miserably. "Real ones. My friend Anselm prayed for his mother's lung-rot and she woke the next morning well. And then at the Feast of the First Snow, a man in the square prayed too hard and—" He stopped. Swallowed. "We're told that's what happens to liars. To those who pray falsely. But Anselm's mother wasn't lying. So why her husband, then? Why did *he* burn?"

Maren's blood went cold and stayed cold. "His name was Anselm's father?"

"He prayed for the same thing. For the lung-rot to leave. Same words, same sincerity. And he burned alive in front of four hundred people, and the priests said his faith was flawed, and Anselm believes them because believing them is easier than—" the boy's voice splintered— "than believing the god ate him."

"There it is," Maren said softly. "There's the question. Hold onto it, Tobin. Feed it. And when the day comes — and it's coming, sooner than the priests think — when someone asks you to choose between the answer and the question, choose the question. Questions are how the trapped get free."

She saw it land. Saw it settle somewhere behind his eyes like a seed in turned earth. She couldn't water it herself — she had, at best, three more days before Corvane escalated, before the comfort became a cage with softer bars, before they either broke her or grew bored and gave her to the god outright at the Feast. But seeds didn't need gardeners forever. Seeds needed only soil and one honest rain.

Tobin gathered his kit with shaking hands. At the door he paused, back still turned, and said: "The northern roads. The ones you lied to Father Corvane about. Were they lies?"

"All of it," Maren said cheerfully. "Except the parts that weren't."

He frowned at her, utterly lost, and left, and Maren lay back against the pillows and laughed silently at the ceiling until her ribs ached, because somewhere in that boy's head, a question had just been born with her fingerprints all over it, and no torture in the world could take that back.

* * *

On the eighth day, Corvane brought the mirror.

It was hand-sized, silver-backed, the kind peddlers sold at fairs, and he carried it into her room wrapped in velvet as though it were a relic. Perhaps it was. He set it on the table beside her untouched supper and did not sit, which Maren noted with professional interest: standing men were performing authority; sitting men were negotiating.

"Do you know what this is?"

"A mirror."

"It is a window." He turned it toward her. The glass showed only her own face — gaunt, grey-streaked, one eye still puffed from the backhand — and then, as the candlelight shifted, showed other faces behind it, faint as fish under ice. "The god sees through silver. Through polished steel, through still water, through any surface that returns an image. Every mirror in this city is His eye. And tomorrow, at the Feast, there will be ten thousand of them, and every faithful soul who prays before their reflection will be seen, and heard, and *answered*."

"That sounds exhausting," Maren said. "For Him, I mean. Ten thousand mirrors. Doesn't the poor dear sleep?"

"The god does not sleep." Corvane's voice had gone strange, flat, recitational. "The god hungers. The god waits. The god wears the king as the king wears his crown, and on the morrow He will wear the city, and after the city, the world, and there will be no wild magic left anywhere, no beasts that speak, no gifts that stray, only devotion, perfect and total, forever."

Maren went still.

Not because of the words — she'd known the shape of the devourer's ambition since the conclave, since Bramble's frantic croaking about the old imprisonment. She went still because of the *voice*. That hadn't been Corvane speaking. That had been the litany, the creed, rolling through a borrowed mouth, and beneath the flat recitation she had heard — faintly, horribly — the sound of a vast attention turning toward her, like the shadow of a hawk crossing a field.

It knew she was listening. It was listening back.

So Maren did the bravest thing she had ever done, which was also the stupidest, which was also, she suspected, exactly the kind of thing her ragged absent family would have done: she looked directly into the mirror, into that cold fish-scale shimmer, and she smiled.

"Well," she said clearly, to the glass, to the god, to whatever vast starving thing wore a king like a coat. "Aren't you a big one. All those mirrors, all those eyes, all that worship — and you still need to borrow a dying old woman's conversation to feel less alone. That's not power, dear. That's *hunger*. And hunger, I happen to know, can be starved."

The temperature in the room dropped. The candles guttered. In the mirror, the fish-scale faces writhed, and something behind them — something with too many angles to be a face — pressed close against the silver, and Maren felt her mind gripped, delicately, enormously, the way a fist might hold a moth without crushing it. It poured into her, that attention, searching for certainty to devour, for faith to strip, for the exact shape of her soul so it could wear her next.

And Maren opened herself wide and gave it — nothing.

Turnips. Thread counts. The smell of her grandmother's kitchen. The mending-charm's words in three regional variants. The price of wool. The recipe for barley soup, the one with too much thyme, the one nobody liked. Layer upon layer upon layer of magnificent, weaponized triviality, a haystack with no needle, and beneath it all, at the very bottom, wrapped in seven kinds of misdirection, a single true thing:

*Nine days. The Feast. Tomorrow. They're coming.*

Let it chew on that. Let it spend its vast attention sorting grain from chaff while somewhere across the city, in whatever flooded ruin or crow-haunted garret hid her family, Jonah was learning the rite and Astrid was sharpening her gift into a blade and Bramble was — knowing Bramble — insulting someone important.

The pressure withdrew. The candles steadied. Corvane blinked, surfaced, and looked down at his own hands as though surprised to find them attached to him.

"What did I say?" he asked. His voice was hoarse. Frightened. For one unguarded moment, the high priest of the Crowned God sounded like a man waking from a dream he didn't remember having — and hating that some part of him had enjoyed it.

"You said the god doesn't sleep," Maren said gently. "You said it loud enough that He heard you saying it. Funny thing, doubt. Gets everywhere. Even into pulpits."

Corvane stared at her. And then he did something no high priest should ever do in front of a prisoner: he looked at the mirror on the table, quickly, guiltily, the way a man looks at a creditor, and snatched it up, and wrapped it in its velvet, and left without another word.

Maren exhaled, long and slow, and let herself shake. Only a little. Only where no one could see.

* * *

Feast-day dawn came grey and cold, with bells.

They dressed her in white — the acolytes, brisk and impersonal, robes of unbleached linen over her shift, her burned hands wrapped in ceremonial gauze. An offering, she understood. A display. The captured witch paraded before ten thousand faithful as proof that the old powers failed, that hedge-craft bent the knee, that doubt itself could be bound in pretty cloth and led on a chain.

They were going to make her kneel before the throne. Before the king who was not home. And then, if the liturgy followed the pattern she'd pried out of Tobin over three careful conversations disguised as bandage-changes — the boy came to her now willingly, hungrily, carrying questions the way other boys carried contraband — they would offer her to the god. Publicly. As a demonstration of mercy. *Repent, and be filled.*

Tobin fastened the last clasp at her shoulder, and his fingers trembled, and his eyes were red-rimmed and blazing.

"Last night," he whispered, "Anselm came to me. He found his father's prayer-book. The real one, hidden under the floorboards. And in the margin, in his handwriting — Father wrote, *'If the god takes me, it was never answering. It was only eating.'*" The boy's jaw shook. "Anselm hasn't reported for morning bells. Three others haven't either. And Father Corvane — he was in the reliquary at midnight, alone, talking to no one, asking the empty air *why*."

Maren closed her eyes. Beneath the fear, beneath the pain, something bloomed — fierce and improbable and absolutely indestructible.

"There's my harvest," she breathed. "Sixty years of planting, and the crop comes in the day I'm due for the sickle." She opened her eyes and gripped Tobin's wrist with gauze-wrapped fingers. "Now listen carefully, because I'll only say this once. Today, when they bring me before the throne, something is going to go wrong. I don't know what. I don't know when. But my family is coming — a girl who speaks with beasts, a boy with fire in his chest, a crow who used to be somebody important — and when it goes wrong, you're going to have exactly one chance to choose. The answer, or the question."

"And if choosing the question gets me killed?"

"Then you'll die the only way worth dying," Maren said, "which is *unconvinced*." She squeezed his wrist once, hard, and released him. "Go on, then. Find your place in the procession. And Tobin — whatever you see today, whatever the god shows you, whatever miracle it performs with ten thousand mirrors and a stolen king — remember: a thing that needs this much audience is afraid of something."

Procession horns sounded below the window, brazen and bright, and the bells of Vael's capital began to ring out the Feast of Answered Prayers, and Maren of the Tollhouse — disgraced, branded, burned, and utterly undefeated — lifted her chin, squared her bandaged shoulders, and walked toward the throne room with the unhurried stride of a woman who had absolutely no intention of kneeling.

Behind her, unheard by any of the faithful, a single crow dropped from the cathedral eaves and followed the procession at rooftop height, keeping pace, black eyes fixed on the small white figure below.

Bramble had found her.

Which meant the others were already inside.
