## 15. The Ember's Confession

The drowned chapel had become, over the days of their hiding, something almost like a home. Astrid would not have dared to name it that aloud — names invited loss, and loss had been following them north like a patient wolf — but she felt the shape of it anyway. The flooded nave had drained in the weeks since they arrived, leaving a floor of pale silt and fallen stone. Maren's salvaged herbs hung drying from the broken ribs of the roof. Bramble had claimed a rotted rafter as his own and defended it from pigeons with the ferocity of a much larger bird. And Jonah slept each night beneath the shattered rose window, where the last light of evening came through colored glass and laid its pieces across him like a quilt.

It was there, in that light, that Astrid first heard the ember speak.

Not through Jonah. That was what unsettled her. She had grown used to the way his dreams leaked — the murmured words in no language she knew, the heat rolling off him at midnight, the way the horses refused to graze within ten paces of where he lay. But this was different. This was the ember speaking *past* him, speaking to her directly, the way a beast might speak when it finally decided you were worth the trouble.

*Listener.*

She was alone in the chapel when it came, kneeling by the cold fire pit, sorting dried yarrow by lamplight. The word arrived not in her ears but in her chest, in the same place where she had always felt the animals' voices — a pressure, a warmth, a meaning wearing the skin of sound.

"I hear you," she said quietly, because she had learned long ago that it never helped to pretend otherwise. The beasts had taught her that. Truth, offered plainly, disarmed more than any blade.

The warmth pulsed. It was pleased. She could feel its pleasure the way she could feel a dog's pleasure at a scratched ear, and that familiarity frightened her more than anything else had — because this was not a dog, not a hawk, not anything with fur or feathers or honest teeth. This was a piece of a god, and it was glad that she listened.

"What do you want?"

*Home,* said the ember, and the word was so vast, so old, so worn smooth with longing that Astrid set down the yarrow and pressed both hands flat against the floor as though the chapel itself might tip and spill her into some darker country. *They tore me out. They locked the rest away. I have been small for so long.*

"Who tore you out? Who locked you away?" She knew, of course. She had known since the conclave of crows, since the crow-god's remnant had spoken the devourer's true name in a voice like ash falling. But knowing a thing secondhand was different from hearing it confess itself. "You were part of it. Part of the thing that wears the king."

Silence. Not denial — she knew denial too, from foxes caught in traps and children caught stealing eggs. This silence was older and sadder than that. The silence of a creature that has done a thing it cannot undo and has stopped pretending otherwise.

*I was hunger once,* the ember said at last. *Before they cut me free, I was the hunger. When the old gods bound my greater self beneath the mountain of the world, they took this piece — the wanting, the reaching, the part that says MORE — and they hid it in mortal flesh, generation after generation, priest to apprentice, so that the devourer would starve forever incomplete.* A flicker, almost wry. *They did not count on mortals praying so loudly. They did not count on a king who would kneel.*

Astrid's hands had gone cold on the warm stone. "And now it's found you. Through Aldric. Through the prayers."

*Through the faith. Every prayer answered feeds it, and every feeding brings it closer to whole, and when it is whole it will come for me, Listener, and take me back, and then nothing under the sky will be able to say no to it again.* The ember's warmth banked suddenly, like a fire drawn down to coals. *Unless I am unmade. Unless the binding is renewed with a price it cannot refuse.*

"The unbinding." Her mouth had gone dry. "Maren told us. A soul freely given."

*A soul freely given,* the ember repeated, gentle as a hand smoothing a blanket over a sleeping child, and Astrid understood, in one terrible vertical drop of knowing, why it sounded so gentle. Why it had waited until Jonah slept deep and dreamless before speaking to her. Why it had chosen this hour, this lamplight, this solitude.

"You've already asked him," she whispered. "Haven't you. You've already asked him, and he's already said yes."

The ember did not answer. It didn't need to. She had spent her whole life reading creatures, and the shape of a secret kept out of love was one she knew better than any other — she had carried one herself for twenty years behind the miller's fence, whispering to sick mares in the dark.

*He offered before I asked,* the ember said finally. *In the drowned dark, three nights past, he spoke to me while you all thought him asleep. He said: if it must be someone, let it be me. I am already half god-stuff. I burn already. Take what is closest to the flame.* A pause, and then, softer: *He made me promise not to tell you. I am breaking that promise now, Listener, because I have watched you since the valley's edge, and I know what you are. You do not let the ones you love walk into fires alone. You pull them through. You pulled him through the wards when the wards said no. You will want to pull him through this too.*

"Damn you," Astrid said, and her voice cracked on it, and she found she didn't care. "Damn you, you burning scrap of a murdered god, you should have kept his secret—"

*Should I?* For the first time the ember flared, and the lamp guttered, and every animal-sound in the night outside went silent at once, hawks and owls and field-mice all holding their breath together. *Or should I tell you what I have seen in his heart while he sleeps? He does not offer himself because he wishes to die, Listener. He offers himself because he believes his life is the only coin he has ever held that anyone wanted. A priest's apprentice, runaway, branded, marked. He thinks he is spending himself wisely at last. He thinks it is the first useful thing he will ever have done.*

Astrid pressed her knuckles against her mouth. Above her, the rose window held its shards of dying color: blue, and red, and a gold that might have been fire or might have been evening.

"He's wrong," she said thickly. "He's wrong about what he's worth."

*Yes,* said the ember. *But he will not believe it from me. I am the thing that wants to go home, and home is his death. He knows I need him. He cannot trust my grief.* The warmth gathered itself, and Astrid felt the ember turning its attention fully upon her for the first time, the way a great bird turns its eye. *But you — you he trusts. You he loves. And there is another thing you must know, Listener, before you decide what to do with this night.*

"Tell me."

*The unbinding demands a life freely given. It does not demand a life. There is a difference, and the devourer knows the difference, and fears it.* The words came slowly now, deliberately, like a creature choosing its footing on scree. *I was torn from it. What was torn can be given back — or given away. A gift freely surrendered, of equal weight to a soul. The gods wrote the rite loosely, Listener, because even they could not bear to demand blood outright. They left a door. They hoped someone would find it.*

Astrid went very still. Outside, the night sounds crept back one by one, an owl resuming its question, the small rustle of things in walls.

"What gift," she said, though she already knew. She had known since the word *Listener*, since the ember had named her across a darkened chapel. Some part of her had known since Hollowmere, since the first time a dying hawk had spoken to her of waking gods, since the moment she had pulled a burning boy through wards that rejected him — as if the valley itself had known what she was carrying, what she would one day be asked to pay.

*Your voice,* the ember said. *The speech of beasts. The oldest listening there is. It is wild magic, pure and unbent, the last of what the devourer cannot make and therefore covets above all things. Give it freely — not to me, not to the devourer, but to the rite itself, to the door the gods left open — and the binding will close on it forever. No god will ever wear it. No prayer will ever harvest it. And the devourer, severed from worship and denied its prize, will collapse inward and starve.*

"And me?" Her voice sounded far away to herself. "What happens to me?"

*You will live,* the ember said. *You will live, and you will never again hear a horse's greeting or a crow's complaint or a hawk's last warning. The world will go quiet around you, Listener, quiet as your valley pretended to be all those years. You will keep your human voice. Your songs, your arguments, your laughter. Only the other voices — the true ones, the old ones — will be gone.*

Astrid sat back on her heels. The lamp had steadied. Somewhere above, Bramble shifted on his rafter and muttered something in his sleep that was either a curse or a psalm.

She thought of the miller's mare, feverish and afraid, and how her whisper had been the only kindness in the animal's whole gray world. She thought of the bandits' horses wheeling at her command. She thought of the wolves of holy ground, the falcons overhead, the whole chattering congregation of the wilds that had become, without her ever deciding it, her people. Twenty years of hiding a self, and now she was being asked to give that self away — not stolen, not punished, not exiled for it, but handed over, freely, which was somehow both worse and better than everything else she had ever feared.

And Jonah would live.

That was the arithmetic that undid her. That was the sum at the bottom of the page. She could hate the cost with her whole body and still see, clear as the lamplight, that she would pay it twice over rather than stand at the Feast of Answered Prayers and watch a boy who believed himself worthless step into a god's hunger because no one told him he was worth more.

"He'll fight me on it," she said at last. Her voice was steady now. Dread had passed through her like a storm front, and on its far side was something harder and calmer, something with edges. "When I tell him. He'll fight me. He'll say it has to be him, he'll say I don't understand, he'll—" She stopped. Breathed. "I need you to help me. Not to deceive him. I won't lie to him. But when the time comes, when we're standing in front of that thing wearing a king — I need you to be ready. Both rites. His and mine. Because I intend to win the argument, and I intend to win it fast, and there won't be time for hesitation."

*You would surrender the listening,* the ember said. It was not quite a question. It was the tone of a creature checking a wound to see if the bleeding had stopped. *Forever. Knowing what it is to you.*

"It's not more than he is." She reached up and touched the rose window's cold stone sill, the place where the gold light fell. "Nothing is more than he is. He just doesn't know it yet. So I'll trade the part of me that tells him — and spend the rest of my life showing him instead."

The ember burned long and low in the silence that followed, and Astrid felt it considering her, the way the old hawk had once considered her from a fencepost before delivering its warning — measuring, weighing, deciding whether she could carry what it was about to hand her.

*Then listen well, Listener,* it said at last, *for these are the last instructions I will ever give you, and I would have them perfect. The rite requires three things: the freely given gift, spoken aloud in the devourer's hearing; the true name, which the crows will sing; and a vessel willing to hold the god while the severing is done — for something must contain the devourer's attention in the moment it is torn loose, something it desires, something it will turn to watch. That is why I was made bait. That is why I was hidden in boys. In the throne room, Jonah will be the vessel. You will be the voice. And the king—*

The ember paused, and its warmth trembled, and for the first time Astrid heard fear in it — genuine, ancient fear, from a fragment of divinity that had endured an eternity of imprisonment.

*The king must wake on his own. No rite can do that part. Aldric must choose, in the last instant, to open his eyes inside his own skull and cast the thing out — and no one can do it for him, and no one can promise he will. If he does not, the severing fails, and it takes us all.*

"So we're betting a kingdom," Astrid said quietly, "on a man none of us has ever met, weeping behind eyes that aren't his own."

*We are betting everything,* the ember agreed, *on the hope that somewhere inside that hollow crown, the king is still home.*

Far off to the south, beyond the hills, beyond the drowned chapel's broken walls, a bell began to ring — not the alarm of pursuit, but the slow, ceremonial tolling of a city preparing a feast. The Feast of Answered Prayers was seven days away. The roads would fill with pilgrims. The gates would open for the faithful.

And somewhere in the capital, a boy was going to wake at dawn and try to slip away alone, believing his secret still intact.

Astrid stood, brushed the silt from her knees, and climbed the chapel steps toward the loft where Jonah slept, rehearsing as she went the hardest sentence she would ever say:

*Jonah. Sit down. We need to talk about who dies at this feast — and I have already decided it isn't going to be you.*
