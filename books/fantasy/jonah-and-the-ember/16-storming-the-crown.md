## 16. Storming the Crown

The capital smelled of incense and burning fat, and beneath both — faint as a lie told well — of something older and hungrier.

Astrid felt it before she saw the walls: a pull in her chest like a hook set behind her ribs, dragging toward the palace at the city's heart. The beasts she'd gathered along the road felt it too. The wolves pacing in the drainage culverts had stopped howling three nights ago; they only pressed their ears flat and trembled when the bells rang. Even the crows overhead flew in patterns that looked less like foraging and more like mourning.

"They're feeding it today," Jonah said beside her. He stood on the ridge above the river road, hood up, hands buried in his sleeves to hide the ember-light bleeding through his knuckles. "The Feast of Answered Prayers. Every soul in that city is going to kneel at once."

"Then we walk in with them," Astrid said. "Kneeling is easy. It's standing back up that costs."

Below them, the pilgrim road poured into Vael's Gate like a river of candles — thousands of them, ten thousand, farmers and merchants and weeping grandmothers, all come to see the Crowned God answer prayers with their own eyes. All come, though they didn't know it, to be eaten.

Maren stood at her other shoulder, thin as a winter branch after her captivity, her wrists still ringed with scorch-marks from the priest's irons. She'd refused a day's rest. "I spent six weeks listening to that thing's faithful," she said now, her voice dry as old paper. "You learn their liturgy whether you want to or not. Feast-day processions enter through the Gate of Bells. Guards don't check pilgrims who are already praying." Her mouth twisted. "They think prayer is armor. Let them keep thinking it."

Bramble dropped out of the grey sky and landed on Astrid's shoulder, his claws finding the familiar grooves he'd worn into her cloak over a hundred leagues. *The conclave is inside,* he said. *Old tunnels under the tannery quarter. Forty-two of them, what's left. They've got fire-oil, crow-sight, and exactly one plan between them.*

"One plan is more than we had yesterday," Jonah said.

*It's a bad plan,* Bramble said. *But it's ours.* He ruffled his feathers, and for a moment his voice went quieter, almost gentle — as gentle as a crow-god's remnant ever got. *Listen, girl. When you get inside those walls, the pull will be worse than anything on the road. It will offer you things. It knows your name now.*

"It knew my name before I did," Astrid said. "Listener. That's what it called me in my dreams, back when the wards still held. It's been calling me since I was a child."

*Yes,* Bramble said. *And that's why it fears you more than the boy with the fire in his chest. Hunger can't imagine being full. But a Listener — a Listener could hear every prayer in that city and tell them the truth about where their prayers go.* He clicked his beak. *Don't give it the chance to make you doubt. Doubt is just hunger wearing your face.*

Astrid looked down the pilgrim road, at the candle-river flowing toward its own devouring, and thought of Hollowmere — of the miller's mare, of the hawk dying in her hands with a god's warning in its throat. She had spent her whole life hiding her voice because the valley taught her that gifts were crimes. Now the whole world was Hollowmere, warded against wonder by a thing that ate it.

"No more doubting," she said. "Let's go answer some prayers."

* * *

Jonah had never seen so many people kneel at once.

The Gate of Bells swallowed the procession into streets paved white with chalk-dust and petals, and everywhere — everywhere — the crowned sigil stared down from banners and doorposts and the foreheads of the devout, painted in ash and honey. Priests in gold moved through the crowd like fish through water, touching brows, murmuring blessings, harvesting nothing yet. Today was the feast itself. Today the god would feed openly, and the whole city would cheer while it happened.

The ember burned high under Jonah's sternum, and he kept his palms pressed flat against it the way Maren had taught him — grounding, banking, holding the fire down to coals. But the closer they came to the palace hill, the more the ember strained toward something. Not fear. Recognition.

*It knows we're coming,* Jonah said silently, the way he'd learned to speak to the fragment without words. *It's glad.*

*Of course it is,* the ember answered, in a voice like a struck bell heard underwater. *We were cut from the same flesh. When you walk toward it, you are a limb remembering the body. Be careful, little bearer. It will not fight you. It will welcome you.*

*I know,* Jonah said. *That's why I'm bringing friends.*

He glanced sideways at Astrid. She walked among the pilgrims with her head bowed like any penitent, but her eyes never stopped moving, and twice he saw her lips move slightly — speaking to sparrows on a lintel, to a lame dog nosing through the crowd. *Third archer on the granary roof is drunk,* she'd murmured to him an hour ago. *The dog by the chandler's says the guard rotation changes at dusk-bell.* Her gift, stitched invisibly through the whole city, a web no priest could see.

Maren walked ahead of them both, transformed. In her captive weeks she'd worn rags; now she wore the stolen vestments of a minor feast-priestess, gold thread at the hem, and she carried herself with such serene authority that lesser clergy bowed to *her*. Twice she'd redirected a patrol with a word. Once she'd quietly unlocked a gate with a hedge-charm disguised as a blessing-sign. Watching her, Jonah understood something he hadn't before: defiance wasn't always a shout. Sometimes it was a woman smiling at the people who tortured her while she picked their locks.

At the foot of the palace hill, the crowd compressed into the great Processional Stair, ten thousand bodies funneled upward toward the Basilica of the Answered Word. And there, at the stair's base, the priests had built a small altar — a harvest table, really — where supplicants could leave written prayers before ascending. A bronze brazier beside it received the folded papers, and the smoke rose straight up, unnaturally straight, threading into the sky like a rope.

Jonah watched a woman kneel at the altar. Grey-haired, sunburnt, a farmer's widow by her hands. She wrote her prayer slowly, kissed the paper, fed it to the flame. For a moment her face went slack with peace — and then, so briefly that Jonah might have imagined it, her outline *flickered*, like a candle guttering, and she swayed, and the priest beside her steadied her kindly and said something comforting, and she smiled and climbed the stairs.

"She lost years," Jonah breathed. "Just then. That smoke took years off her life and she thanked it."

"That's the arithmetic of the feast," Maren said grimly. "Devotion in, divinity out. The god eats the difference." She adjusted her stole. "Tannery tunnel opens at dusk-bell. The conclave fires the oil stores under the basilica's west foundation at the same moment the bells ring — chaos at the front, us through the undercroft at the back. Everyone remember where they're supposed to be terrified?"

"I'm already practicing," Astrid said, and even now, even here, her crooked smile made something in Jonah's chest unclench that had been clenched since Wardfall.

Dusk-bell came like a verdict.

* * *

The first bell note had not finished ringing when the west of the city bloomed orange.

From the undercroft stair, Jonah heard rather than saw the conclave's work: a whump of ignited fire-oil, the roar of flame climbing the basilica's western face, the scream of ten thousand pilgrims discovering in one breath that their god did not protect them. The bells kept ringing — automaton-priests cranked them, he realized, brass figures wound like clocks — but the sound was drowned under the human noise, terror layered on terror layered on faith refusing to die.

"Their god will answer," Maren shouted over the din, shoving open the undercroft door. "Watch. It has to. It can't let them see it fail."

She was right, and it was worse than being wrong. As they sprinted the vaulted corridor beneath the basilica, the flames above them *bent* — visibly bent, like wheat before wind — and poured backward into the building, sucked into the throne room above like breath into lungs. The devourer was eating its own temple's fire. Somewhere overhead, the crowd's screams turned to gasps, then to weeping, then — horribly — to *cheering*. The god had saved them. The god had answered. The feast would continue.

"They'll love it more now," Astrid said, horror flattening her voice. "It burns their church and they'll love it more."

"Hunger always mistakes consumption for care," said a new voice, rough and many-voiced, and out of the shadows of the cross-corridor came the conclave — forty-odd figures in ash-grey, faces masked with crow-feather, led by a tall woman whose mask bore a single silver eye. Corvessa, the remnant's speaker. Behind her, bound and stumbling but upright, walked two conclave scouts freed from the palace cells, and behind *them*, carrying a stolen censer like a weapon, came a face Jonah had feared he'd never see again.

"Maren?" he said stupidly, and then caught himself — the woman beside him was Maren. The one walking forward was—

"You're seeing double," Astrid said, and laughed once, sharp and disbelieving. Because the figure pulling back her hood was Maren too — thinner, greyer, one eye swollen half-shut, but unmistakably, impossibly her.

"The priests interrogated a hedge-witch for six weeks," the Maren in vestments said, and pulled off her own face — wax, glamoured, a hedge-craft mask peeling away to reveal a young man with a conclave scar on his jaw. "They never thought to ask if they'd caught the right witch."

"The real one," Corvessa rasped, nodding to the battered original, "has been in our keeping since the betrayal. Your friend sold them a decoy and bought herself a beating. She is either the bravest woman I have known or the most foolish, and I have known both kinds extensively."

"I contain multitudes," Maren said hoarsely, and Astrid crossed the distance and hugged her hard enough to make the old woman grunt.

"There'll be time for reunions when the god is dead," Corvessa snapped, but her silver-eyed mask lingered on the embrace a heartbeat longer than necessary. "The undercroft runs beneath the throne room. The devourer will be at its feeding — the feast peaks at full dark, when every prayer in the city rises at once. That is when Aldric's body is weakest. That is when we strike."

"And that," said the ember, aloud now, through Jonah's mouth, in a voice that made every conclave member flinch and reach for iron, "is when *I* am strongest. Bearer. Are you ready to go home?"

Jonah put his hand over his sternum, over the heat, and found — to his own surprise — that he wasn't afraid. Or rather, that the fear and the readiness had become the same thing, the way grief and love do.

"Yeah," he said. "Take me home."

* * *

Astrid heard the throne room before they reached it.

Not with ears. With the gift — the vast, unwilling, magnificent gift that had carried her across a kingdom and was about to be spent like a coin. From thirty feet below the basilica floor, she could hear the prayer-smoke rising through the stone like sap through a tree, and threaded through it, the voices of everything alive in Vael Crossing: rats in the granaries, horses in the royal stables, pigeons roosting in the crown of the bell-tower, all of them keening the same animal distress. The land itself was screaming, low and constant, the way a beast screams when it cannot escape the trap.

And beneath the screaming, vast and patient, something *listening back*. Drinking.

*It knows we're here,* she told the others, pressing her hand to the cold undercroft wall. *It's known since the gate. It's letting us come.*

"Good," said Jonah.

"Good?" the young conclave scout whispered.

"It means it's arrogant," Maren said. "Arrogant things make mistakes. Come on."

The stair to the throne room was black marble veined with gold, and as they climbed it the pull on Astrid's chest became a hand, then a fist, then a gravity. The ember-light leaked through Jonah's sleeves now, unhidden, painting the marble in shifting amber. Above them, through the narrowing gap of the great doors, light pulsed — not torchlight, not firelight, but the slow luminous throb of something digesting.

Corvessa raised a fist. The conclave fanned out along the walls, fire-oil satchels ready, crow-feather masks glinting. Maren stood at the stair's head with her stolen stole and her swollen eye and her spine straight as a lance. Bramble settled onto Astrid's shoulder, and she felt the old god's weight there, feather-light and mountain-heavy at once.

*Whatever happens,* Bramble said, *whatever it offers you — remember the mare. Remember the hawk. You were never a crime, girl. You were the antidote.*

Astrid laid her hand briefly on Jonah's arm. He looked at her, and in the ember-light his eyes were steady, and she thought — with the terrible clarity that comes before catastrophe — that she had never told him, not in plain words, what he'd become to her. That there might not be time now. That she might be about to spend her voice on a god and leave this unsaid forever.

"After," she said. "There's something I'm going to say to you. After."

Jonah smiled — that crooked, exhausted, stubborn smile. "After," he agreed.

Together, they pushed open the doors of the throne room, and the light of the Feast of Answered Prayers poured over them like drowning —

— and the thing on the throne opened King Aldric's eyes, and smiled, and said, in a voice like ten thousand prayers spoken at once:

"*Listener. Bearer. You brought my heart home to me. Come — let us be whole.*"

Behind Astrid, very softly, Bramble began to sing.
