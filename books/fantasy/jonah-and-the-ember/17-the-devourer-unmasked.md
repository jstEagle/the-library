## 17. The Devourer Unmasked

The Feast of Answered Prayers had turned the capital into a candle.

From the servants' stair that Maren's stolen key opened, Jonah could see it all: ten thousand votive flames guttering in the great hall below, ten thousand faces upturned toward the dais where King Aldric sat upon the Ember Throne — a name that had once been a joke of history and was now, Jonah understood, a prophecy no one had thought to read aloud.

He pressed his palm flat against his own chest. Beneath his ribs, the ember pulsed in time with the chanting below, like a second heart trying to climb out through his sternum.

*It knows its sibling,* the ember had said, in the drowned chapel, in dreams, in the voice that was sometimes a woman's and sometimes a bell and sometimes nothing but heat. *It will know you too.*

Below, the high priest raised the Crowned God's sigil — a circlet of gold wrought to look like thorns — and the crowd's prayer rose like smoke with nowhere to go.

"They're not praying," Astrid whispered at his shoulder. Her voice was very quiet; she had learned, on the road north, how small she could make herself when the world grew too loud for her gift. Even now, even here, he could see her flinching from the noise of ten thousand souls wanting things. "Listen. It's not words anymore. They've stopped *asking*. They're just... pouring."

"I know." He did know. The ember drank devotion the way a dry field drinks rain, and what the faithful below were feeling — that warm surrender, that abdication of self — came off them like steam off a slaughterhouse floor. "That's the harvest. This whole feast is a harvest festival."

Bramble shifted on his shoulder, feathers flat against his neck. The crow had been silent since they'd entered the palace, which from Bramble was more frightening than any scream. Now he said only: *"Throne room's warded against old faith. I can't hear my god in there. Whatever you do in there, you do alone."*

"We're never alone," Maren said grimly. She stood behind them both, hedge-witch's hands stained green to the wrist with the salves that had gotten them past three checkpoints of consecrated iron. Her eyes were still bruised-looking from the undercroft, from the priest's questions, from whatever she had endured and refused to speak of. But her spine was straight as a lance. "That's rather the point of us."

Astrid looked at Jonah. In the candlelight her face was pale and set, and there was something in her expression he had seen only twice before — once when she'd pulled him through the wardline with the valley's magic tearing at his skin, and once when she'd found the farmer's body folded into the devoured field like a letter someone had given up on finishing.

It was the look of a woman who has already decided, and is only waiting for the world to catch up.

"Don't," he said softly.

"Don't what?"

"Whatever you're planning. Don't."

She almost smiled. "You first."

* * *

They went down into the light.

There was no clever way past the last hundred feet of open marble between the servants' door and the dais; the conclave's maps had made that plain. The plan had been chaos — Bramble and the crows firing the granaries, Maren loosing the palace's chained griffin-hounds, the old-faith survivors flooding in through the crypts — and chaos was indeed unfolding somewhere beyond the walls, bells clanging wrong, screams starting and stopping. But the last approach was simply this: walk into the sight of ten thousand worshippers and the thing that fed on them, and keep walking.

Jonah walked.

The ember burned so hot now that he could feel sweat rolling down inside his shirt, could feel the air around him shimmering faintly, the way roads shimmer in high summer. Worshippers turned as he passed. Some frowned. One woman reached out and touched his sleeve and gasped — not at him, he realized, but at whatever she felt through the contact, some echo of true fire passing near its counterfeit.

And on the throne, King Aldric turned his head.

Jonah had seen the king once before, through a grate in the royal chapel, and had told himself the memory exaggerated. It had not. Aldric sat with the terrible stillness of a man who has forgotten how his own body works — hands resting on the armrests like borrowed tools, chest rising and falling slightly out of rhythm, as though breathing were a courtesy he performed for observers. His face was handsome the way a portrait is handsome. And his eyes —

His eyes were weeping.

Tears ran steadily down the king's cheeks, cutting clean lines through the ceremonial gold dust painted there, and the king's mouth smiled, and the two things happened together without contradiction, because they belonged to different owners.

*Oh,* Jonah thought, and the pity hit him like a physical blow, buckling something in his chest that had nothing to do with the ember. *Oh, you poor man. You're still in there.*

The devourer wore its host the way a hand wears a glove, and the glove was screaming.

"You brought it home."

The voice came from everywhere — from the king's mouth, yes, but also from the ten thousand candles, which all leaned their flames toward the dais at once; from the marble, from the rafters, from the prayer-smoke itself. It was a beautiful voice. That was the worst of it. It was the voice of every kind thing anyone had ever said to Jonah, braided together and pitched just low enough to sound like confession.

"The little ember," the Crowned God said through Aldric's smiling mouth, while the king's eyes wept and wept. "My missing piece. My stolen heart. Did you think you were carrying a weapon, child? You were carrying an invitation."

Guards moved — or tried to. The first two got within ten feet of Jonah and simply sat down where they stood, weapons clattering, faces going slack with sudden bliss, their devotion siphoned upward like wine through a straw. The devourer did not even look at them. It was looking at Jonah, and then —

— and then it was looking at Astrid.

Jonah saw the change come over the vast attention like weather. The hunger sharpened, focused, became something almost tender.

"And the Listener," the god breathed. Ten thousand candles guttered. Somewhere above, glass burst. "Oh. Oh, you sweet ruined valley. Do you know how long I have listened for one who listens?"

* * *

What happened next, Jonah would spend the rest of his life failing to describe accurately.

The devourer shed Aldric.

Not all at once — not fully — but the way a snake loosens the first coils of an old skin. The king's body slumped forward on the throne, still smiling, still weeping, and out of him, out of the gold and the shadow and the accumulated weight of ten thousand answered prayers, rose the shape beneath.

It did not have a shape, exactly. It had a *hunger* that had learned to imitate one. Jonah's mind kept trying to render it — wings, a crown, a mouth — and kept failing, because each attempt was true for half a heartbeat before the thing was larger than the image. It filled the hall the way night fills a valley. It was vast and starving and patient, patient the way famine is patient, and every candle flame in the palace bent toward it like grass under wind.

The crowd did not flee. That was the horror Jonah would carry longest. The crowd *knelt*, sighing, blissful, offering themselves up like plates passed at a table.

"There," the devourer said, and its voice was gentler now, gentler than anything that large had any right to be. It coiled down toward Astrid, and though it had no face, Jonah felt it *regarding* her, the way a miser regards a coin of perfect weight. "Little Listener. They cut your valley off from me. They warded away the wildness and called it safety, and they taught you to be ashamed of the one true thing about you. I heard you, all those years. Every word you spoke to beasts in secret. Every lonely night. I heard, and I could not reach you, and it *grieved* me."

"Liar," Jonah said.

"Am I?" The vastness rippled — amusement, or the memory of amusement worn like a costume. "Ask her if I lie. Ask her whether the mare lived. Ask her whether the hawk's warning was heard by anything else in all the world." The god drifted closer, and the kneeling crowd sighed in unison, drunk on proximity. "I am not your enemy, Listener. I am the answer. Every prayer ever prayed was prayed to *me* — they only used smaller names. And I give answers. Look at my feast. Look at my faithful. No one here is hungry. No one here is afraid. No one here is *alone*."

Its attention narrowed to a point, and the point was Astrid, and Jonah watched temptation walk into the throne room wearing everything she had ever wanted.

"Give me your hand," the devourer said, "and I will give you back your voice — not as it was. As it should have been. You will hear the speech of every living thing, and every living thing will hear *you*, and there will be no wards anywhere in the world to make you small again. Your valley — I will unmake its forgetting. Hollowmere will remember itself. Green fields, little Listener. Open gates. A village that loves you instead of fearing you. Your mother's grave tended by hands that know her name."

Jonah heard Astrid's breath catch.

He knew — he knew with sick certainty — that the offer was not even a lie. That was the devourer's genius, the thing Maren had tried to warn them of in the tollhouse, over stew, in her rough practical voice: *it doesn't tempt you with falsehoods, it tempts you with invoices.* Everything it promised, it could pay. It had eaten kingdoms' worth of devotion. It could rebuild a valley the way a man flicks crumbs from his coat.

And Astrid stood in the ruin of the feast with her chin lifted, and Jonah saw her actually consider it — saw her go somewhere inside herself, to the miller's stable, to the dying hawk, to twenty years of whispering to horses in the dark and calling it a sickness.

"Astrid," he said. Just her name. It was all he had.

Her eyes didn't leave the god. "It's real," she said quietly. "Jonah. It's all real. I can feel it — the valley, the offer, all of it. It's not lying."

"It doesn't have to."

"No." Something moved through her face then — grief, and underneath the grief, a bedrock steadiness that he had fallen in love with somewhere on the exile road and never found the courage to name. "No, it doesn't. That's how I know what it is." She took one step forward, into the shadow of the vastness, small as a sparrow under a storm front. "Everything true about me, you'd keep. And everything else — everyone else — you'd eat. That's not a gift. That's a *price*."

The devourer went very still.

Around them, the ten thousand candles began, one by one, to go out.

* * *

"You mistake patience for permission," the god said, and the gentleness was gone, and what remained was the sound of a stomach the size of the sky. "I offered. I do not often offer. The ember I must take gently — it is kin, and kinship has rules. But *you*, Listener. You are not kin. You are harvest."

The vastness lunged.

And Jonah stepped between them.

He did not decide to. Later, turning it over, he would find no decision anywhere in the memory — only the ember blazing up through his ribs like dawn through a cracked shutter, and his own body moving, arms spread, a boy of nineteen standing in front of a hunger older than the word for hunger.

The devourer stopped.

Stopped as though it had struck glass. Stopped as though the whole enormous engine of its attention had snagged on one small burning point and could not look away.

"*There* you are," it whispered, and the whisper filled the dead hall, and every kneeling worshipper shuddered at once. "There is my heart. There is the piece they tore from me when the world was young and the gods were many. Do you know what it has been doing in you, little vessel? All these years? It has been learning to love. I can taste it. It has learned to love *her*, and to love the witch, and the bird, and the road, and the rain." The vastness trembled — with longing or with laughter, Jonah could not tell. "It has gone soft. It has gone *mortal*. And soft things, broken things, loving things —"

The god's attention swung back to Astrid, and now the temptation curdled into something worse, something intimate:

"— soft things I can eat *slowly*. Give me my heart, Listener, and I will let you watch it live. Refuse, and I will take it, and take you, and take the crow and the witch and the weeping king, and I will begin again with what remains of the world. I have eaten gods before. I am very good at endings."

The last candle died.

In the dark, Jonah felt the ember speak — not in words now, but in warmth, in a pressure against the inside of his breastbone like a hand pressing back against his own. It had learned to love. It had learned it from him, from all of them, from stew in a tollhouse and a crow's bad jokes and a girl who talked to horses.

And it was afraid.

Beside him, in the blackness, he heard Astrid draw one long breath — the breath of a woman standing at the edge of something, weighing it, measuring the drop.

Then she said, clear as a bell across the ruined feast:

"I have a better offer."

The dark went utterly, listeningly silent.
