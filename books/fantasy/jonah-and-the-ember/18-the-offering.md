## 18. The Offering

The throne room of Vaelhold had been built to make people feel small.

Astrid felt it now, standing in the wreckage of the Feast of Answered Prayers — shattered glass underfoot like frost that would never melt, banners of the Crowned God hanging in scorched ribbons from the rafters, the great rose window bleeding colored light across a floor slick with spilled wine and worse. Somewhere beyond the doors, the conclave's fighters were holding the line against the palace guard, and Maren's voice was still ringing in her ears from the moment they'd burst through: *Go. I'll hold the stair. Go.*

So they had gone. And now here they were, at the end of everything, and the devourer wore the king like a coat it had grown fond of.

Aldric sat upon the throne — or rather, the thing inside Aldric sat, and let the body drape over the armrest with the boneless patience of a spider. His crown had melted into his hair somewhere in the last century of wearing; Astrid could not tell where gold ended and gray began. His eyes were wrong. She had known they would be wrong since the conclave's crows first described them, but knowing did nothing against the reality: eyes like wells with no bottom, eyes that reflected light without receiving it.

And beneath all of it — beneath the wrongness and the hunger and the vast attention now turning toward them like a lighthouse beam — a man wept. Quietly. Continuously. The tears had worn channels down his cheeks, Astrid realized. Old channels. He had been crying for years.

*Jonah,* she thought, and reached for his hand.

He wasn't there.

He was three steps ahead of her, walking toward the throne with the ember blazing through his shirt like a second heart, and he was smiling, and Astrid had known him long enough — through ambush and tollhouse and drowned chapel, through every small dark hour — to know exactly what that smile meant.

"No," she said.

The word came out too loud in the ruined hall. The devourer's attention swung to Jonah like a compass needle finding north, and Astrid saw its expression change — saw hunger arrange itself into something almost tender, the way a wolf might look at a lamb that walked willingly into its jaws.

"There you are," the thing said. Its voice came from everywhere at once — from Aldric's throat, from the walls, from the bells still trembling faintly in their towers across the city. It was a voice made of answered prayers, warm as candlewax and just as easily snuffed. "My little splinter. My stolen piece. You've come home."

"I've come to give myself back." Jonah's voice was steady. That was the worst part. He had rehearsed this. In the drowned chapel, in the quiet hours she'd thought he spent sleeping, he had stood in black water and practiced dying. "Freely offered. A soul freely given — that's the price of the unbinding. You taught us that yourself, through every mouth you ever spoke through. So take mine. Let the king go. Let the world keep what wild magic it has left."

"Jonah—" Astrid started forward.

Bramble dropped out of the rafters and landed on her shoulder, claws biting through her cloak, and the crow's voice cracked against her ear: *"Wait. Wait, girl. Look at it. LOOK at it."*

She looked. And she understood.

The devourer was leaning forward on the throne. The whole vast weight of it — the thing that had eaten a harvest godling whole, that had burned a supplicant alive mid-prayer in Vael Crossing, that had pulled the land itself screaming toward its gullet — was leaning toward Jonah with an eagerness it could not disguise. Not because it wanted to be unbound. Because it wanted the ember. The fragment torn from it when the old gods had chained it beneath the world, the piece that had been bait and key both, the piece that had *wanted to go home* —

— because home was a stomach.

"It's not going to release anything," Bramble hissed. "It's going to swallow him and call it mercy."

Jonah had stopped walking. Ten feet from the throne, close enough that the light of his own ember lit the hollow king's face from below, and Astrid watched him hesitate — watched doubt flicker across his face like a shadow of birds — and then watched him crush it, because that was who Jonah was. He had carried a god's fire in his chest since he was a child fleeing a burning temple. He had never once put it down. Of course he would carry it all the way into the dark.

"A freely given offering," the devourer purred, "is accepted. Come, little flame. Come home, and I will loose this poor king. I will let your friends walk out. I will even leave the wild places alone — for a time. See how generous I am? See how much better I am than the gods who broke you off from me and left you to burn in a boy's chest?"

"You're lying," Astrid said.

Everyone turned to look at her. Aldric's ruined eyes found hers, and behind the hunger, something sharpened with interest.

"The Listener," the devourer said softly. "The one who hears. Do you know how long I have wanted to taste that gift? Every prayer in the world comes to me screaming, but you — you hear the *answers*. You hear the land itself. You hear what no god has heard since the first silence." It smiled with Aldric's mouth, and the smile did not fit. "I offered you everything once. I offer again. Your voice returned tenfold. Your valley green again, your mother's grave marked, your name cleared. All of it yours, if you step aside and let the boy pay his debt."

Astrid's throat closed.

Because that was the cruelty of it — the offer was real. She could feel the truth of it humming in the air the way she felt truth in animals, in the wind, in the roots of things. The devourer would pay. It could afford to pay. It simply knew, the way all predators know, that she would never take the bargain while Jonah stood between it and its meal.

"Step aside, Listener," it whispered. "Or don't, and watch. Either way, I eat tonight."

Jonah turned back to look at her then. And his face — gods, his face. He wasn't afraid for himself. He was afraid *for her*, standing there in the broken glass with a crow on her shoulder, and his eyes said what they always said: *I'm sorry. This is the only way. Take care of them.*

Take care of them. As if she could survive a world where she'd watched him walk into a god's mouth and done nothing.

As if love were a debt someone else got to pay for you.

"No," Astrid said again, and this time her voice didn't shake.

She walked forward. Past Jonah. Through the colored light falling from the broken window — red and gold and a deep wounded blue — until she stood between the boy with the ember in his chest and the god on the throne, and she looked up into the well-eyes of the thing that ate devotion, and she said:

"You want a gift freely given? Then hear me. You want the one thing in this world you cannot take by force. You've tried. You've pulled the land apart trying to rip my gift out of me, and it won't come, because it isn't *yours* to take. It only leaves if I give it. Whole. Willing. Forever."

The hall went very quiet. Even the fighting beyond the doors seemed to hold its breath.

"So I give it."

* * *

Later — in the hours and years after, in every retelling, in the silence where the songs used to be — Astrid would try to describe what it felt like to reach into herself and take hold of her own voice.

There was no gesture required. No rite, no words, no blood. Maren had told her once, drunk on nettle wine in the tollhouse, that the old faith held all true sacrifices were made in the space between two heartbeats — that the body bargained longer, but the soul decided fast. She understood it now. Between one beat and the next, she found the place inside her where the listening lived. It was not a sound. It was a door. It had been open her entire life — open to the miller's sick mare breathing her fear into Astrid's palms, open to the dying hawk's warning, open to the scream of the land under the Crowned God's pull, open to every small voice the world had ever tried to drown out.

She wrapped her hands around that open door, the way she had once wrapped her arms around a stranger collapsing at the valley's edge, and she pulled it shut.

"You can't," the devourer said. For the first time, its voice cracked. "Your gift is the oldest thing left in you. It is older than your name. It is tied into the root-system of the world —"

"Then you know I'm telling the truth," Astrid said. "You can hear it. You can hear *everything* I mean. So listen."

And she gave it.

Not the way she'd imagined giving things — not the clean severance of a cut, but a *pouring*, like emptying a jug, like a river giving itself to the sea. Every conversation she'd ever had with a beast went out of her: the mare's last grateful breath, the wolves she'd turned into shields on holy ground, the horses' thundering hearts beneath her during the flight from Hollowmere, the crows' gossip, the hawks' cold arithmetic of distance. The language without words. The hearing without ears. The self she had hidden her whole life, shamed for, exiled for, and finally — finally — fought beside.

It poured out of her and into the air between her and the throne, and the devourer *lunged* for it.

That was the mistake. That was the moment everything turned.

Because the gift did not go to the devourer. It couldn't. A freely given sacrifice belonged to the rite, and the rite was already turning — the unbinding, the great tearing-loose that the conclave had described in whispers, that the drowned chapel's murals had shown in fading paint: god from king, chain from anchor, fire from flesh. Her offering struck the rite like a spark striking oil, and the whole throne room ignited with light that cast no shadows.

Jonah screamed her name.

The ember tore free of his chest.

She saw it happen — saw the little flame rise out of him trailing light like a comet's tail, saw him drop to his knees with his hands pressed to the wound in his sternum, saw the devourer strain forward out of Aldric's body with a shape that should not have fit inside a man, vast and starving and many-mouthed, reaching for the ember and the offering and *her*, reaching for everything —

—and the old gods' chains, buried in the fabric of the world, woke up.

They came down through the ceiling of heaven like harpoons. Astrid felt them more than saw them: a vibration in her teeth, in the floor, in the last flickering edge of her dying gift. The devourer shrieked — a sound that peeled paint from the walls and burst the remaining windows and sent every bird in the city up screaming into the night — and the ember, the key, the bait, the stolen piece, drove itself into the center of that vast hungry shape like a nail into a coffin lid.

The light went white.

Astrid threw herself over Jonah. The world roared. Glass rained. Somewhere Bramble was cursing in three dead languages. And beneath all of it, beneath the roar and the rain and the ringing, Astrid heard — with the last thread of the hearing she had just given away — the sound of a god coming apart.

It sounded, she thought wildly, like a valley forgetting.

* * *

When the light faded, the throne room was silent.

Snow was falling through the broken roof. Actual snow, soft and impossible, in a season too early for it — the world exhaling after holding its breath. It settled on the shattered glass, on the scorched banners, on the two figures crumpled before the empty throne.

Astrid crawled to Jonah first. He was pale as the snow, both hands still pressed over his chest, and when she pulled his fingers away she found unbroken skin — smooth, unscarred, bearing only a faint tracery of silver like frost ferns where the ember had lived. He was breathing. He was staring at the ceiling with the expression of a man who has set down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying.

"It's quiet," he whispered. "Astrid. It's so quiet. I didn't know it was ever — I thought that was just what existing sounded like—"

"I know," she said.

And then she stopped, because she had said *I know*, and there had been nothing behind it. No answering hum of assent from the rafters. No crow-thought brushing her mind. No sense of the building's old bones murmuring around her, of the city's dogs reporting in from every street, of the land's deep slow voice underneath it all.

Silence. Total, ordinary, human silence.

She knelt there in the snow with her hand pressed flat against her own chest, searching for a door that was no longer there, and grief hit her like cold water — sudden, total, filling every space the gift had occupied. Twenty-three years of a second language, gone. The mare. The hawk. The wolves. All of it paid out, poured away, spent in the space between two heartbeats.

*"Astrid."*

Jonah had sat up. He was looking past her, at the throne, and his face was doing something complicated — horror and wonder fighting for the same ground.

She turned.

King Aldric lay at the foot of his own throne, in the snow, in his own body. Just his body. Thin, gray, shaking, crowned with nothing but his own matted hair. His eyes were closed, and his chest rose and fell, and the channels of old tears on his cheeks were finally dry.

As they watched, his eyelids fluttered. His hand twitched against the stone. And King Aldric — hollow man, puppet, prison, hostage — opened his eyes for the first time in living memory, and looked up at the two strangers kneeling over him, and wept.

Not the terrible mechanical weeping of the thing that had worn him. Human weeping. Ugly, gasping, disbelieving — the sound of a man waking from a nightmare decades long, in a room full of broken glass, in a kingdom he no longer recognized.

"Is it—" His voice was rust and gravel. His hand found Jonah's sleeve and gripped it with shocking strength. "Is it gone? Is it *gone*?"

"Gone," Jonah said hoarsely. "You're free. You're — you're free, your Majesty."

Aldric laughed. It was a broken sound, half sob, and he pulled his hand back and covered his face, and the King of Vaelhold cried in the snow like a child while the last of the devourer's light guttered out in the high windows above.

Astrid knelt beside him and felt — nothing. No whisper of his heartbeat. No reading of his terror or his relief. Just a man crying, legible only by his face, by his shoulders, by the thousand human signals she had spent a lifetime ignoring because she'd never needed them.

She had traded omniscience for this: the hard, plain work of understanding people the ordinary way.

*Love given up is still love kept.* The thought arrived from nowhere — from the ember, maybe, in its final moment, or from whatever part of her had made the choice and would have to live with it. She held onto it like a rope over a cliff.

Bramble landed in the snow in front of her. The crow looked rumpled, singed, and profoundly rattled — which, for a former god's servant, was saying something. He opened his beak.

And Astrid heard nothing.

She stared at him. He stared at her. His head tilted, that quick mechanical bird-tilt, and his beak worked again, and the words came out as pure sound — noise, chatter, meaning locked away behind a wall she could no longer climb through.

"Bramble," she whispered. "I can't—"

The crow went still. Something moved through his black eyes — realization, then something fierce and unbearably gentle. He hopped closer across the snow, right up to her knee, and pressed his warm feathered side against her hand, and stayed there, saying nothing, needing to say nothing, being present in the only language left to either of them.

Beyond the doors, boots were pounding up the stairs — Maren's voice, ragged and triumphant, calling their names. The snow kept falling through the broken roof. And on the far side of the room, half-buried in drifts, something small and bright pulsed once, twice, in the shadow of the empty throne.

Like an ember. Like a heart. Like a door, very slightly, opening.
