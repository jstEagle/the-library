## 19. Unbinding

**JONAH**

The throne room of Vaelhold had been built to make a person feel small.

Jonah had heard that once, from a scholar in his master's library — that the architects of the old kings had raised those vaulted ceilings and hung those banners not for beauty but for awe, so that any soul who walked the length of that marble floor would arrive at the throne already kneeling in their heart. He understood it now from the inside. The ceiling arched away into shadow like the ribs of some enormous sleeping thing. Banners of gold thread caught the light of a hundred candles and threw it back down in fragments. And at the far end, on a dais of white stone, sat the Crowned God wearing King Aldric like a coat it had grown too fond of taking off.

The Feast of Answered Prayers raged beyond the walls — ten thousand voices singing the god's name, bells hammering out joy, the whole city drunk on devotion. That was the point. Maren had explained it with blood still crusted at the corner of her mouth: *You cannot cut a root while the tree is flowering. You must do it in the dark between songs.* The conclave had bought them that darkness by shattering the great bell of the Basilica of the Answered, and for eleven minutes — Bramble had counted, smug even now, perched on Astrid's shoulder — the devourer would be deaf to its own worship.

Eleven minutes. Jonah's chest burned where the ember lived, hotter than it had ever been, as if it knew it was going home and could not decide whether home was salvation or a grave.

They crossed the marble floor together, the five of them and what remained of the crow-conclave flowing behind them like a black river. Maren walked with a limp she refused to acknowledge. Bramble rode on Astrid's shoulder, silent — truly silent, which frightened Jonah more than anything else this night, because Bramble silent meant Bramble afraid. And Astrid walked beside him with her chin up and her hands open at her sides, the way she did when she was listening to something no one else could hear.

She had tried, one last time, in the drowned chapel three nights ago, to talk him out of what he meant to do. *A life freely given,* the ember had told him through his dreams, over and over, patient as water wearing stone. *The lock turns only on a willing key.* And Jonah had made his peace with it the way you make peace with winter — not gladly, but knowing it was coming, knowing there was no bargaining with it. He had written no letters. There was no one to write them to who wasn't standing beside him.

But he had seen Astrid's face when she finally understood, and her face had undone every careful arrangement of his heart. She hadn't wept. She had gone very quiet, the way the valley went quiet before the wards broke, and then she'd said, *We'll see*, and Jonah had been fool enough to find hope in it.

The god saw them coming long before they reached the dais.

It turned King Aldric's head — a slow, wrong movement, like a puppet remembering how necks worked — and smiled with the king's mouth, and the smile did not reach eyes that were molten gold from edge to edge. No pupils. No whites. Just hunger, given a human shape and a crown.

"Little ember," it said. The voice filled the vaulted dark the way smoke fills a room, from the floor up. "You've brought yourself home. I wondered how long you'd crawl."

"I'm not here for me," Jonah said. His voice cracked on the second word and he hated it, and kept walking anyway.

"No," the devourer agreed pleasantly. It rose from the throne, and the king's body moved with it, and Jonah saw — as he had seen through the undercroft grate weeks ago — that the body was only mostly a body now. Light leaked from the seams of it. Gold bled from beneath the fingernails, from the corners of the eyes, from the hollow of the throat, as if the man were a lantern burning through. "You're here for the girl. So is everything I am." The golden gaze slid past Jonah and fixed on Astrid, and Jonah felt the attention of it the way you feel a hand on the back of your neck. "The Listener. Do you know how long I have starved for a voice like yours? Every prayer in this kingdom comes to me secondhand, strained through priests and desperation. But you — you hear the world itself asking. Give yourself to me, child, and I will give you back your valley whole. Your wards unbroken. Your gift multiplied until every living throat in creation speaks *to* you and *for* you. You will never be lonely again."

Jonah heard Astrid's breath catch. He didn't blame her. It was a beautiful offer. It was designed to be beautiful; that was the horror of the thing, that it knew exactly what loneliness weighed and named its price accordingly.

And then Astrid laughed — short, sharp, almost disbelieving — and said, "You're offering me company by eating everyone I'd want to be company with. You don't even see the flaw, do you?"

The god's smile thinned.

"You've never listened," Astrid said, and her voice shook but held, "only swallowed. That's why you need an ember to get home to yourself. That's why you wear a man instead of being anything. Because nothing that only takes can ever hold onto what it catches." She stepped forward, ahead of all of them, small and furious on that vast marble floor. "I know what the rite costs. I've known since the chapel. And you can't have it."

"Astrid—" Jonah started.

"Shut up, Jonah," she said, not looking at him, and something in her tone made Bramble shift his weight on her shoulder, made Maren go still. "Ember. Come out. We do this now, before the bell rings again."

The devourer lunged.

There was no other word for it. The king's body came apart mid-motion, seams splitting wide, and what poured out was not light but the absence of it — a shape of hunger unfolding upward toward the vaulted ceiling, vast and jointless and *starving*, wearing the crown on nothing at all. Candles guttered. Marble frosted. Jonah felt the cold of it enter his bones and recognized it instantly, intimately, because he had carried a splinter of that same hunger in his chest for two years and knew its temperature the way a man knows his own fever.

"Now!" Maren shouted, and began the words of the rite, the old-faith words, the ones that predated the Crowned God and every church built over its grave.

The ember answered before Jonah chose to answer for it.

Heat bloomed in his chest — not pain, not yet, but a terrible brightness, a door opening inward. He felt it rise toward his throat, toward his hands, gathering itself to leap the gap between his body and the devourer's, carrying him with it. A soul freely given. His. Offered. He had planned for this. He had *made peace* with this. He opened his mouth to speak the assent that would complete the circuit—

Astrid stepped into the space between him and the fire.

Later, Jonah would try to reconstruct that moment and fail. There was no time in it, or too much time; the world seemed to hang like a drop of water about to fall. He saw her turn her head just enough to look at him, and her eyes were wet and absolutely steady, and she said — clearly, deliberately, in the voice she used for frightened animals and for him —

"*Freely given.* You taught it that rule, Jonah. Well. Here's my freedom."

And she opened her gift like a hand opening, and gave it away.

He felt it happen. Everyone felt it happen. The air of the throne room rang — actually rang, a note too low for ears and too high for bone, a chord struck on an instrument the size of the world — and every beast-song, every whispering green thing, every murmuring current of wild speech that Astrid Kavanagh had spent her whole hidden life translating poured out of her in a single silver torrent and struck the devourer full in its starving center.

The god screamed.

Not with Aldric's voice. Not with any voice. It screamed the way a mountain screams when it splits, the way ice screams on a freezing lake, and the sound tore through the throne room and through the palace and through the stunned singing city beyond, and in that scream Jonah heard something he understood down to his marrow: the sound of a thing that had fed on devotion for a century discovering what it felt like to be *given to* without being asked for anything back. Love, freely surrendered, undiluted by fear or bargain. It had never once tasted that. It had no teeth for it. It had no stomach for it.

Maren's voice cracked like a whip through the chaos: "*Unbind!*"

Jonah spoke the final word of the rite with the last breath he thought he owned.

The world went white.

**ASTRID**

She heard the tearing.

That was the cruelty of it — the gift left last of all, lingering just long enough to let her hear her own sacrifice finish its work. She heard the devourer come apart from King Aldric like ivy ripped from a wall, root by grasping root, a century of threads snapping in sequence: the prayer-threads, the fear-threads, the fat silken cords of worship it had wound through a kingdom's spine. Each one sang as it parted. Each one screamed.

She heard the god collapse inward, denied its prize — the Listener's voice slipping through its fingers even as the offering-soul it had been promised failed to arrive, both doors slammed shut in the same instant — folding down and down into the ruin of its own hunger, smaller and smaller and colder, until with a sound like a cathedral exhaling, it was gone. Not dead. Gods did not die, Bramble had always said. But empty. Scattered. A famine where a feast had been.

Then the silver torrent ran dry, and the ringing faded, and Astrid stood on the scorched marble of the dais steps in a silence so total it pressed against her eardrums like deep water.

She reached, out of habit older than thought, for the world's voice — the way she had reached every day of her life since she was six years old and first heard the miller's dog say *good morning, small human.*

Nothing.

No crows gossiping over the rooftops. No horses stamping their opinions into the courtyard below. No rats in the walls muttering their small gray prayers. Nothing. The world had closed its mouth, and Astrid Kavanagh stood inside the closing of it, deaf in a way ears had nothing to do with, and she did not know yet whether she was going to scream or simply stand there forever.

Something heavy fell into someone's arms nearby. Someone shouted her name — Maren, hoarse. Jonah.

She turned.

King Aldric lay crumpled at the foot of his own throne, and Jonah was on his knees holding him, and the king was *weeping* — great, ugly, human sobs, the tears of a man surfacing from years underwater, gasping at the weight of his own body, his own grief, his own hands. His eyes were brown. Ordinary brown, red-rimmed, terrified, alive. The crown had rolled away across the marble and lay spinning slowly to rest like any piece of metal, like nothing.

"He's — he's breathing — Astrid, he's *breathing*—" Jonah looked up at her, and his face was streaming with sweat and soot and something that might have been tears, and he was laughing, actually laughing, the broken helpless laugh of a man who had braced for death and been handed a morning instead. "You *fool*. You absolute — you *gave it away*, you weren't supposed to—"

"I know what I was supposed to do," Astrid said.

Her voice sounded strange to her. Flat. Unaccompanied. For twenty years every word she spoke had been layered under birdsong and hoofbeats and the low green murmur of growing things, a chorus she had stopped hearing as separate because it had never once stopped. Now her words fell into silence and just — landed there. Alone.

She knelt beside Jonah and put her hands on the king's shoulders, because that was the work in front of her, and work was the only thing keeping her upright. Aldric stared up at her with the raw, uncomprehending eyes of a man waking from a nightmare into a worse one. His lips moved.

"The prayers," he whispered. "Where — where did they *go*? I heard them, all of them, every one, for years, I couldn't stop hearing—" His face crumpled. "What did it make me do?"

"Nothing you'll remember wanting to," Maren said gently, lowering herself down beside them with a grimace. She touched the king's brow with two fingers, some hedge-witch's blessing, quick and unsentimental. "That's the mercy of it. Mostly."

Bramble hopped down onto the dais, feathers scorched, one eye swollen half-shut. He looked at Astrid for a long moment. She braced herself for the crow's commentary, the endless irreverent stream of it, and realized with a fresh, dull ache that she would not hear it even if he spoke — that whatever came out of his beak now would be just sound, just noise, a bird making noises at her.

But Bramble only pressed his forehead, briefly, against her cheek. Wordless. Warm. Then he hopped over to the king and said, in his croaking, ancient, insufferable voice:

"Well. You're shorter than your propaganda."

Aldric flinched, stared at the crow, stared harder. "That bird just—"

"Talks, yes, welcome to the actual world, your majesty, sorry about the rent." Bramble ruffled his singed feathers. "Now sit up. There are ten thousand people outside who think you're a god, and roughly four hundred crows upstairs who are about to explain to them, at length, that they aren't."

Through the shattered windows, Jonah could see it beginning: the crowd in the holy square faltering mid-hymn, faces tilting up toward the palace where the devourer's scream had torn across the sky. Somewhere a bell tolled — not the great basilica bell, still in pieces, but a small chapel bell, uncertain, ringing out of habit and finding nothing to ring *for*. The Feast of Answered Prayers was ending. What came after it, no one in the history of the kingdom had ever needed to imagine.

Aldric got his feet under him, swaying, leaning hard on Jonah's shoulder. At the top of the dais steps he turned and looked at the four of them — the hedge-witch, the crow, the boy with the fading scar of ember-light over his heart, and the girl who stood very still with her hands curled shut like things protecting something already gone.

"There was a hawk," the king said suddenly, hoarsely. "Years ago. On the wall of a valley called Hollowmere. I was young. I heard it *say my name*." His voice shook. "My father had the village watched after that. Warded. Contained." He looked at Astrid, and the shame in his face was bottomless and entirely his own, which meant it belonged to him, which meant he was, at last, only a man. "I owe a debt I don't know how to count."

"You'll figure it out," Astrid said.

Her voice held steady all the way through it. It was only afterward, when Jonah took her hand — carefully, the way you take the hand of someone standing at the edge of something — that her fingers trembled, and would not stop, and she understood that the silence had only begun to settle, and the mourning had miles yet to go.

Above them, through the broken roof, the first grey light of a godless dawn crept across the rafters of the throne room.

And far to the south, unseen, unheard, in a valley whose wards had cracked and never mended, something small stirred in the roots of the hills — listening, in its slow green way, for a voice that no longer answered, and beginning, patiently, to grieve.
