## 20. Silence Where the Songs Were

The morning after the world was saved, Astrid woke to silence.

Not the ordinary silence of a room at dawn — she had known that all her life in Hollowmere, the hush of wards pressing down like snowfall, the muffled quiet of a valley that refused to listen back. This was different. This was the silence of a door she had opened every day of her life, now bricked over from the inside.

She lay on her pallet in the ruined chapel where they'd made camp and reached for it the way she had reached for it every morning since she was six years old: outward, with her whole attention, the way other people reached for light. The horses grazing in the flooded meadow below should have been a murmur at the edge of her mind — contentment, wariness, the slow green thoughts of animals chewing. The crows in the yew trees should have been gossip. The land itself, scarred and singing under the devourer's pull for so long, should have been a chorus now, freed, rejoicing.

Nothing.

She reached until her chest ached with reaching, until tears slid sideways into her hair, and there was only the sound of her own breathing and, faintly, Jonah's, two cots away, still deep in the sleep of someone who had almost died and been permitted not to.

*This is what you bought*, she told herself. *This is the price. You knew the price.*

Knowing a price and paying it, it turned out, were separated by a country as wide as the one they'd crossed to get here.

She got up quietly, dressed, and went out into the grey-gold morning alone, because she could not bear to be witnessed yet.

* * *

The capital was waking around the drowned chapel's hill like a body remembering itself after fever. Bells rang — real bells, rung by human hands, announcing nothing holy, just the hour — and people streamed toward the palace square, where King Aldric had been carried three days ago, hollow-eyed and weeping, a man returned from a long captivity inside his own skin. They called it a miracle. Priests who had served the Crowned God were being pulled from their temples; some were beaten, some fled, some stood in the doorways of their gutted churches looking like men who had woken to find the house they'd kept was built on graves.

Astrid walked through it all and heard none of the underneath.

That was the thing no one warned you about losing a gift like hers. It wasn't the grand conversations she mourned first — the hawk's dying warning, Bramble's sardonic commentary, the ember's terrible confessions. It was the small weather of it. The constant low hum of the living world, the sense that she was never entirely alone, never the only mind awake under the sky. She had spent twenty years hiding that hum, resenting it, terrified of it — and now that it was gone she understood it had been the floor she stood on. Without it she felt suspended, a woman hanging over an abyss of ordinary quiet, holding onto nothing.

She passed a carter whose mule had gone lame, and heard him curse it, and heard the mule's ears flatten, and could not tell the man — could not even know, truly know, whether the animal was in pain or pride or simple stubbornness. She passed dogs straining at leads and read them now the way she'd read them before Hollowmere taught her shame: by guesswork. Tail up, probably friendly. Hackles raised, probably not. A child's arithmetic where she had once spoken fluent grammar.

At the edge of the meadow below the chapel, the horses they'd ridden north grazed among the ruins of a shrine. The dappled mare, Wren, lifted her head as Astrid approached, and Astrid's heart did the old treacherous lurch — *say something, she'll say something back* — and then the mare only looked at her, dark eyes reflecting the morning, and blew air through her nostrils, and went back to the grass.

It shouldn't have broken her. It broke her anyway. She sat down in the wet grass among the fallen stones of the shrine and put her arms around her knees and cried the way she hadn't let herself cry during any of it — not at Wardfall, not at Maren's capture, not in the throne room when the devourer had offered her everything and Jonah had stepped forward to pay with his life. She cried like a child, ugly and helpless, mourning something the rest of the world would call a small thing. A voice. Only a voice. Only the whole architecture of who she had been.

Because that was the truth she kept circling: the gift hadn't been a tool she'd used. It had been the shape of her solitude. Every year of hiding it had carved her hollow in its exact outline, and now the carving was filled with silence, and she did not know what shape remained.

"You're doing it wrong," said a voice above her. "Grief, I mean. You're supposed to do it dramatically. Wailing. Tearing of garments. I've seen professionals."

Bramble dropped from the yew branch onto the shrine stone beside her, feathers ruffled, one eye fixed on her with the flat gold appraisal he reserved for things he was deciding whether to be kind about.

"Go away," Astrid said.

"Can't. Sworn service. Terrible terms." He hopped closer. "Also you've been out here an hour and Jonah has noticed, and when Jonah notices he does that face, and frankly I'd rather sit through your weeping than his face."

"I'm fine."

"Liar. Magnificent liar, actually, one of the best I've known, and I served a god, so my standards were high." He tilted his head. "You gave up the one thing you never wanted anyone to know you had, to save a boy you met falling off a road, and now you're sitting in wet grass pretending the sky isn't empty. That's not fine. That's a wound."

"It's done," she said. Her voice came out scraped raw. "The rite is done. Aldric is free. The devourer is—" she couldn't finish it, the memory of that vast shape folding inward, starving, screaming without sound as worship drained out from under it. "It's done. Everyone keeps saying it's done, like done means over."

"Done means done," Bramble said. "Over is a different country. You don't get there by marching. You get there by sitting in wet grass for a while." He settled his wings, and for once said nothing more.

They sat together while the sun climbed. Astrid cried again, less catastrophically. At some point she found herself talking — to Bramble, because he was the only creature left she could talk *to* rather than merely talk near — about Hollowmere, about the miller's mare she'd healed in secret at fourteen, about the hawk that had died delivering its warning, about how she had hated the silence of the valley her whole life and had walked straight into a larger one voluntarily.

"The worst part," she said, "is that I chose it. If it had been taken from me I could hate someone. I did this."

"Yes," Bramble said. "And?"

"And what?"

"And that's what love is, most of the time. Choosing the loss yourself instead of letting it choose you. You didn't lose your voice, girl. You spent it. There's a difference, and the difference is everything." He preened a feather flat with great deliberation. "I watched a god die once, you know. My god. The crow-father. He didn't go quietly either. And afterward I spent forty years telling myself I'd lost him. Took me thirty-nine of those years to understand I'd spent him — that everything I am, every clever thing I've ever said, is him, spent well." He looked at her sidelong. "You're spending well. It doesn't feel like it. It won't for years. Do it anyway."

She wanted to argue. She found she didn't have the material. She put out her hand, half expecting him to hop away — he was not, as a rule, a creature that tolerated touch — but he only regarded her finger with suspicion and stayed exactly where he was, which from Bramble was practically an embrace.

* * *

The days that followed taught her the geography of her new country.

She learned that grief for a sense comes in tides, not waves. Some hours she forgot — genuinely forgot — and turned to speak to Wren or to the chapel cats or to the wind, and the forgetting was worse than the remembering, because each time the silence caught her anew, like stepping onto a stair that wasn't there.

She learned that the world was full of people trying to comfort her badly. Maren, recovered from her captivity with new scars and a permanent hitch in her left hand, tried hexes to restore what the rite had taken, and grew furious when none worked, and finally sat down across from Astrid and said, "It can't be undone because it wasn't stolen. It was given. Given things root where they're planted. Even here." She said it gently, for Maren, which meant she said it while aggressively chopping herbs.

Jonah tried nothing at all. He simply was there — carrying water she could carry, sitting beside her in the evenings, saying nothing with a fluency she was beginning to suspect was its own kind of gift. Once, late, he said: "The ember chose you before we ever met. It named you Listener. And you listened us all the way here." He paused. "You're still listening. It's just quieter now. You hear people the way you used to hear beasts. I've watched you do it. You heard what Aldric needed before he knew it himself."

"That's not the same."

"No," Jonah agreed. "It's not the same. But it's not nothing."

She learned, too, the strange arithmetic of what remained. When a rider came pounding up the hill with news, the horses' terror told her things — she could still *read* them, if not speak with them, the way a deaf woman reads lips, the way she had read all animals before the gift woke fully. Body language was a language too; she had simply forgotten she'd ever been fluent in both. It was like being given back a tenth of a fortune and discovering a tenth could still buy bread.

But the nights were hardest. In Hollowmere, sleep had always come with the valley's murmur beneath it, the dream-adjacent chatter of foxes and owls, the deep slow voice of the earth itself turning over in its sleep. Now the nights were pure black water, and she swam in them alone.

On the fifth night she dreamed of the valley anyway — not hearing it, but seeing it, the way memory preserves what magic cannot: the mill wheel turning, the wards' old silver shimmer, the mare in her stall lifting her head. In the dream she understood something she hadn't managed awake: that the gift had never been the point. The gift had been how a lonely child survived. The loneliness was the original wound; the listening had been the bandage. And bandages, when the flesh beneath has finally healed, are meant to come off.

She woke with wet eyes but a steadier heart, and went out before dawn to the meadow, and stood among the horses, and did not reach for anything. She only stood. Wren came over and pushed her nose against Astrid's shoulder, wanting nothing, saying nothing, and Astrid put her hand on the warm neck and breathed, and it was enough. It was not what it had been. It was enough.

* * *

It was on the seventh day that the crow landed on her shoulder and stayed.

Not Bramble — Bramble announced himself at volume and expected tribute. This was another crow, one of the wild ones that had gathered in the capital since the devourer's fall, drawn perhaps by whatever residue of divinity clung to the places it had fed. It came down from the yew tree while she sat on the chapel steps watching the city smoke its last funeral pyres, and it landed on her shoulder with the particular weightlessness crows have, deliberate and light at once, and it did not speak.

Of course it did not speak. Crows did not speak. Only Bramble spoke, and gods, and — once — her.

But it stayed. It settled its feet in the wool of her cloak, arranged itself against the wind coming off the river, and stayed, and after a while it began, very softly, to make the sound crows make when they are content: a low, rolling, rattling purr, half croak, half song, felt in the collarbone more than heard.

Astrid went very still.

It meant nothing. It was a bird being comfortable. It was warmth and proximity and nothing else, and she would be a fool to read prophecy into a bird choosing a warm shoulder on a cold morning.

It stayed anyway. Through the morning. Through Jonah coming out with tea and stopping dead at the sight of it, his face doing the thing Bramble had described. Through noon. When she finally rose, it lifted, circled once, and resettled on her other shoulder as though the first had been merely a draft position.

"Bramble," she said that evening, "there's a crow following me."

"There are four hundred crows in this city following everyone. It's what crows do. We're thorough."

"This one sits on my shoulder."

"Ah." Bramble was quiet a moment, and when he spoke again his voice had lost its irony, which from Bramble was rarer than gold. "Yes. They do that, sometimes. After. The wild ones can feel where a god has been — where one died, where one was unmade, where one was *spent*. It reads like a burned field to them. Fertile, eventually, but burned." He hopped along the beam to look at her directly. "They gather at those places. Some of them stay. Not because they expect anything. Because staying is what you do at a grave that might grow something."

"I'm not a grave."

"No," Bramble agreed. "You're a field. But you'll forgive them for taking a season to be sure."

The crow — she had begun, helplessly, to think of it as *hers*, the way you think of a stray — rode her shoulder through the next days like a small dark conscience. It never spoke. It would never speak. And slowly, in a way she could not have explained to anyone, including herself, the wordlessness stopped hurting. It became, instead, a kind of companionship she recognized from the far side of her life: the companionship of presence without demand. The mare in the meadow. The wind in the yews. All the things she had loved before she knew she could love them better, and which, it turned out, had never required her voice at all. Only her attention. And her attention, unlike her gift, was still entirely her own.

One evening Jonah sat down beside her on the steps, close enough that their shoulders touched below the crow's feet, and said nothing, and the crow purred its rattling song, and the sun went down over a city learning to be afraid of different things, and Astrid thought: *this is what remains. Not what I lost. What stayed.*

She was almost — almost — at peace with it when the horns sounded from the palace.

Three notes, repeated: the summons-call, the one heralds used for matters of state that could not wait. A runner was already climbing the chapel hill, waving a folded paper bearing the royal seal, shouting for the god-bearer and his company before he'd even reached the gate.

Jonah took the letter, broke the seal, and read it twice. When he looked up, his expression was the complicated one — hope and dread sharing a face.

"It's Aldric," he said. "He wants to see us. All of us. Tomorrow." He hesitated. "He says he owes us a debt. And he says—" Jonah glanced at the paper again, and his voice dropped, "—he says he remembers everything the devourer did while wearing him. Everything it heard. Including prayers it answered and never told anyone about." He looked at her, and then at the crow on her shoulder, and something in his face said the letter held more than he'd read aloud. "He says one of those prayers was made in Hollowmere. The night the wards fell. And he thinks we should hear who prayed it — and what they asked for."

The crow on Astrid's shoulder went silent, head cocked toward the palace, gold eye catching the last of the light.

Astrid touched its feathers once, lightly, and stood.
