## 21. The King's Debt

The throne room still smelled of smoke.

Three days had passed since the devourer collapsed inward like a star dying in reverse, and the masons had not yet finished clearing the rubble from the eastern wall where its true shape had burst through Aldric's body and into the world. Sunlight came through the gap now, falling in a long pale blade across the marble floor, and Jonah kept finding his eyes drawn to it — to the ordinary gold of morning where a god's hunger had been.

He stood at the foot of the dais with Astrid on his left and Maren on his right, Bramble a dark weight on Maren's shoulder, and waited for the king of Vaelmar to speak.

Aldric did not look like a king. He looked like a man who had been drowned for eleven years and only just remembered how to breathe. He sat the throne as though it were a horse that might bolt under him, his hands — long hands, scholar's hands, with ink stains still ghosting the knuckles beneath the court physicians' scrubbing — resting on the armrests as if he'd forgotten they were his own. His crown lay on a cushion beside him. He had not touched it since they'd carried him from the throne room floor.

Jonah knew why. He'd held the man while he wept, while the last threads of the devourer's presence tore out of him, and what Aldric had sobbed into Jonah's shoulder was not *thank you* but *what did I do*, over and over, a litany of every prayer he'd ever spoken aloud, every blessing he'd given in the thing's name, every face in every crowd that had burned a little brighter as he passed. Eleven years of devotion harvested through his mouth, his hands, his smile. He remembered all of it. That was the cruelty of the unbinding: it left the host whole enough to grieve.

"Master Jonah." Aldric's voice was rough, half-repaired. "You're staring at the wall."

"Forgive me, Majesty."

"Don't." A ghost of something crossed the king's face — not quite a smile, but the place where one used to live. "I've had enough forgiveness to last several lifetimes, and I haven't earned a grain of it. Look at whatever you like."

Bramble shifted on Maren's shoulder and croaked, softly, "*Told you he'd be decent, once you scraped the god off.*"

"Bramble," Maren murmured.

"What? He can't hear me. Mortal ears." But the crow said it quieter anyway, and Jonah caught Astrid's mouth twitching at the corner — the first almost-smile she'd managed in three days, and it went through him like sunlight through a cracked window.

Aldric rose. It took him two tries; his legs had been someone else's legs for a decade, and they were learning their old work slowly. When he stood straight, he was taller than Jonah had expected, and thinner, and there was a tremor in his left hand that he hid by closing it around the throne's armrest.

"I have spent three days reading," the king said, "the laws I signed."

The hall was nearly empty — a handful of guards, the chamberlain, a scribe hunched over his desk in the corner trying very hard to be furniture. But Aldric spoke as though addressing a nation.

"There is a statute," he went on, "from my fourth year on the throne. It declares that any person found to bear an unlicensed gift — beast-speech, hedge-craft, fire-hearth, storm-calling, any communion with the wild magic — shall be taken to a consecrated house for 'correction.' I remember signing it. I remember believing it was mercy, because the alternative proposed was burning." His jaw worked. "I remember the devourer whispering through my own thoughts that correction was kindness. That gifted folk hoarded what belonged to the gods. That every unlicensed wonder was a theft from the crown." He looked down at his trembling hand. "It was never my voice. And I let it wear mine for eleven years, and people were taken from their homes because of it. People died in those consecrated houses. I have read the ledgers."

Maren's breath went out slow and controlled. Jonah knew what she was doing with it — she'd told them, the night after the battle, sitting in the infirmary with her wrists bandaged where the irons had bitten: *they asked me questions for nine days. I want to hear him say it wrong even once, so I know what to do with my anger.* So far he hadn't said it wrong.

"The statute is repealed," Aldric said. "Not amended. Repealed. Every record of 'correction' — every name in those ledgers — will be copied, verified, and returned to the families of the taken, with formal apology under seal. The consecrated houses are being emptied today. Whatever they hold will be released." He paused, and when he continued his voice dropped, went quieter, more human. "It isn't enough. I know it isn't enough. Nothing I do will be enough. But it is a beginning, and beginnings are the only currency I have left."

"*Beginning,*" Bramble repeated, tasting the word. "*Hm. Kings usually start with gold.*"

"Gold is the easiest thing to give," Aldric said, "and therefore worth the least." He descended the dais steps — slowly, gripping the rail, refusing the chamberlain's offered arm with a small shake of the head that Jonah understood completely. Some things you had to do on your own legs or not at all. The king stopped three paces away, close enough that Jonah could see the fine scarring at his temples where the devourer's presence had rooted deepest, thin silver lines like frost-fern.

"So I ask you plainly," Aldric said. "You five — the witch they tortured in my name, the crow who served a murdered god, the boy who carried divine fire in his chest for a season and gave it back, and—" his gaze moved to Astrid, and softened, and broke a little, "—and the woman who paid for all our lives with the dearest thing she had. Name your price. Land, title, pardon, gold, a ship, an army. Whatever the crown has, ask, and if it is within my power to give, it is yours."

Silence stretched. Somewhere above, through the broken wall, a gull cried — one of the few voices Astrid could no longer translate, though Jonah had seen her tilt her head toward bird-song twice already, an old habit running up against a new silence, and each time the little flinch that followed had made him want to break something.

Astrid spoke first. Her voice — her human voice, the only one she had now — was steady. "We don't want anything *from* you, Majesty."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the beginning of one." She glanced at Jonah. *Your turn,* the look said. *You're the one who talks to kings.*

Jonah stepped forward. His heart was hammering, which was absurd — he'd faced a devourer wearing a crown four days ago, and here he was nervous over a petition. But this mattered more. Battles ended things; this was a beginning, and beginnings could be botched.

"When I was eight years old," he said, "my mother took me to see the standing stones at Carn Duvach. Before the persecutions. There were hedge-witches there, and a storm-caller, and an old man who could grow wheat through frost just by singing to it. Nobody was afraid of anybody. The magic wasn't licensed or corrected or burned. It was just — part of the valley. Part of the harvest. People brought the storm-caller bread." He swallowed. "Then the edicts came, and the stones went quiet, and everyone who could hide, hid. My master the priest used to say the old faith died of neglect. It didn't. It was murdered politely, with paperwork."

Aldric flinched. Jonah hadn't meant it as a blow, but he didn't take it back either.

"Hollowmere," Jonah said. "That's where we're asking you to look. A valley in the southern reaches, warded against magic for three hundred years. The wards were built by people who were afraid — genuinely afraid, I'll grant them that, the old wars were real — and so they built a home where nothing wild could live, and then they made their children swear never to miss what couldn't come inside." He felt Astrid go still beside him, the way she always did when Hollowmere was named, a whole country of grief folded into the line of her shoulders. "The wards broke when the old gods woke. What's left of the valley is emptying out. And the crown could ward it again — properly, this time. Not to keep magic *out*."

"To keep it *in*," Maren finished quietly. "Shelter instead of exile." She stepped forward, wrists still wrapped, chin up. "A sanctuary, Majesty. Wards with a door in them. A place where a child who talks to horses doesn't have to whisper, and a hedge-witch can hang her charms in the window without checking the road first. Not a prison. Not a reservation. A *home* — with roads open at both ends, for anyone who wants to come or go."

"And the crown funds it?" Bramble said skeptically. "*Feeds it? Guards it? For how long? Until you die and some other poor soul gets a hungry god whispered into his ear?*"

"Maren," Astrid said again, but Aldric raised a hand.

"The crow may speak freely. I find I prefer honest insolence to loyal flattery; I got eleven years of the latter." He turned and walked — slowly, deliberately — to the broken eastern wall, and stood looking out at the city below, the rooftops steaming in the morning sun, the bells silent because half of them had cracked during the Feast of Answered Prayers and the rest had been ordered quiet until the priests could agree on what, exactly, they'd been praying to.

"You should know what you're asking for," the king said, without turning. "A re-warded valley is not a signature. It's a generation of signatures. Warders of the old school — and there are perhaps six left alive who remember the craft — will need to be found, persuaded, fed, housed. The neighboring lords will call it a den of witches a mile from their borders and petition me annually to burn it. The new faith — the one that grew fat feeding the devourer without knowing what it fed — will preach against it from every surviving pulpit." He looked back over his shoulder. "You are asking me to spend political blood for twenty years on behalf of people who will mostly never thank me, some of whom will actively hate me, and all of whom have excellent historical reasons to distrust the crown. Do you understand that?"

"Yes," Jonah said.

"And you ask anyway."

"We ask anyway."

"Why?"

It wasn't a king's question. It was a tired man's question, a drowning man's, and Jonah realized with a jolt that Aldric wasn't testing them. He wanted to know if hope was worth the labor. He'd spent eleven years watching devotion get eaten, and he needed to hear, in plain words, what devotion was *for*.

Jonah thought about the miller's sick mare, and a girl kneeling in a stable straw whispering comfort to her when the whole valley would have exiled her for it. He thought about a crow who'd served a dead god and chose, instead of despair, to be insufferable. About a hedge-witch who lied to torturers for nine days to buy strangers time. About an ember that had chosen its bearer before he was born and burned itself out keeping him alive.

"Because hiding doesn't work," Jonah said. "Believe me, I've tried it. You can build walls against the wild magic, and the wild magic waits, and meanwhile the people who carry it drown quietly inside the walls, and the fear grows in the dark until something comes along and offers to eat it for you." He nodded at the broken wall, at the sky beyond it. "Something did. The only cure for that is light. Rooms with doors. Valleys with gates. Let people be what they are somewhere safe, and the fear starves."

Aldric was quiet a long moment. Then he laughed — short, startled, rusty, as if the sound had been locked in a chest for a decade and he'd just found the key.

"A priest's apprentice," he said, "arguing theology better than my chaplains." He came back from the wall, and there was something different in his bearing now — not the hollow puppet, not the shattered penitent, but a third thing, newer: a man deciding who he would be with the years he had left. "Very well. Hollowmere will be re-warded. Crown-funded, crown-guarded, crown-*answered-for*. I'll send for the warders myself — all six, if I have to ride to their doors. And I'll stand at the valley gate when the first family arrives, if they'll suffer my presence, so that the first face they see in their new home is the face of the man who apologizes."

"*Oh,*" said Bramble, and for once the crow had nothing sardonic to add.

"There's more," Aldric said. "You said roads open at both ends. Then hear the other half of my debt. The ledgers of the taken — I will have them read aloud, every name, in every market square in Vaelmar, until the country knows what was done in its name. And any gifted soul who wishes passage to your valley will be escorted, provisioned, and protected by crown soldiers on the road. Let the lords watch the caravans pass. Let them count the faces. Fear shrinks best in daylight — your priest's apprentice taught me that just now." A pause. "And Master Jonah — the ember. What remains of it. Where is it kept?"

Jonah's hand went, without thinking, to his chest. Four days ago there had been a god's heart burning behind his sternum; now there was only the memory of warmth, and a small lead casket in his pack where the last coal of it slept — a fragment of the murdered deity the crow-god's conclave had begged to inter, and which Jonah had not yet been able to give up.

"It sleeps," he said. "It's going home. To the conclave. Soon." *Soon,* his mind whispered, and he heard Astrid shift beside him, heard the concern she didn't say aloud, because he'd been saying *soon* for four days and the casket stayed in his pack.

Aldric studied him with eyes that had learned, painfully, to see clearly. "When it goes," he said gently, "you will grieve it. That's not weakness. I grieve the thing that wore me — do you understand? Eleven years, and some traitor corner of me misses being *held*." His voice roughened. "Grief for a god is a heavy debt too. If the crown can lighten yours in any way—"

"You already have," Jonah said, and meant it.

They walked out of the throne room together, the five of them, past guards who saluted — actually saluted, Maren noted with disbelieving satisfaction — and down the great stair into the sunlit courtyard where a crown carriage waited to bear them to the conclave's embassy house. Bramble rode Maren's shoulder with his feathers preened into unusual dignity. Astrid walked close, her shoulder brushing Jonah's, and neither of them said anything, because everything that needed saying had been said in a flooded chapel and a burning throne room and a hundred small moments between.

But at the carriage door, Astrid stopped.

"Jonah," she said quietly. "The casket. Tonight. We'll take it to the conclave together. I promised you I wouldn't carry it for you." Her hand found his — the hand that had held hers in the dark of the rite, that had pressed the ember's warmth against her palm when her voice was being torn away. "But I'm holding you to *soon*."

Above them, on the palace roof, the gulls wheeled and cried in a language she could no longer read — and yet she watched them a moment anyway, head tilted, as if listening with something deeper than ears.

And far to the south, though none of them could know it yet, in a half-empty valley between grey hills, the first of the warders had opened an old book, licked her thumb, and turned to the page marked *Hollowmere*.

* * *

That night, Jonah dreamed of the valley.

Not the Hollowmere of wards and whispers, but the one that was coming: lamplight in windows that had been dark for a generation, a storm-caller teaching children to whistle up rain over the barley fields, horses turning their heads to listen to a girl who no longer answered them — and a crow, perched on the gatepost, translating.

He woke before dawn with the casket warm against his ribs, warmer than lead had any right to be, and a single word hanging in the dark of the room like a struck bell:

*Soon.*
