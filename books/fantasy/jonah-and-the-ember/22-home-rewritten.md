## 22. Home, Rewritten

The road into Hollowmere smelled the same.

That was the thing that undid her first — not the sight of the valley, which she had braced for the way one braces for a wound, but the smell of it: wet loam and cut hay and the cold mineral breath of the millrace, all of it rising up to meet her on the last hill like a dog that remembered her name. Astrid stopped at the crest of the ridge with her pack straps biting into her shoulders and simply breathed, and found that she was crying without any drama at all, the way rain comes, evenly and everywhere.

"You're leaking," Bramble observed from his perch atop her pack. "Should I be concerned? I've seen humans leak before. Usually something's about to break or something's about to begin."

"Both," she managed.

"Hm." He sidled down her arm — she felt the familiar tickle of his feet through her sleeve, a sensation that no longer required translation, that was just *Bramble* now, the way a hand on the shoulder is just a hand — and settled on her forearm, feathers fluffed against the wind off the moor. "Well. Try to do the beginning part louder."

Below them, Hollowmere lay in its bowl of hills like a coin at the bottom of a green purse. The mill turned. Smoke stood up straight from a dozen chimneys in the windless morning. The fields were stubbled and dark with autumn plowing, and along the valley floor the new wards caught the light — and there it was, the difference, the thing she would never stop seeing now that she'd seen it once.

The old wards had been a wall. She'd grown up inside them without knowing what they were, only that the world beyond the valley felt muffled, dreamlike, wrong; only that when she spoke to animals she did it in whispers behind closed doors, because whatever made the valley safe also made her gift a crime. The wards her mother's generation had inherited were built of fear, laid down by people who had decided that magic was a flood and the only answer was to keep the water out entirely, even if it meant drowning everything alive inside the walls instead.

The new wards were a roof.

Maren had explained it a dozen times on the road south, drawing diagrams in campfire ash with a charred stick while Bramble heckled her geometry. *You don't keep the magic out,* she'd said. *You give it somewhere to fall gently. A ward isn't a wall, girl, it's a promise — you're telling the wild things: here, this ground is held. You may pass through. You will be changed by nothing against your will.* The conclave's oldest survivors had come down from the fells to lay it, crow-feathers and river-stones and songs so old they predated the names of gods, and where the old wards had hummed with prohibition, the new ones sang — quietly, constantly, a note below hearing, a note Astrid could almost feel in her back teeth if she stood very still.

She could not hear what the wards said. That was the other thing, the thing under everything, the stone in her boot that she carried with every step home.

* * *

They came down into the valley in the late afternoon, four travelers and a king's warrant folded carefully in oilcloth at the bottom of Jonah's pack, though none of them expected to need it. Aldric had sent word ahead by fast rider — the first act of his restored reign that had actually reached Hollowmere before they did — and so it was not torches and pitchforks waiting at the mill bridge but a knot of villagers, maybe thirty of them, standing in the road with the awkward, shuffling posture of people who have rehearsed an apology and are terrified they'll botch it.

Astrid recognized faces. The tanner's widow. Old Garrick, who had once threatened to report her for lingering too long near the stables. The miller himself, hat in his hands, wringing it like a neck.

She had imagined this moment on a hundred nights of the road. In most of those imaginings she had been magnificent — cold, distant, above their contrition, delivering some speech about forgiveness that Maren would later tease her for having rehearsed. In a few of them she had been cruel, and in one or two, shamefully, she had dreamed of making them kneel.

What actually happened was that the miller stepped forward, opened his mouth, and said, "Your mam kept your room," and then burst into tears, and Astrid discovered that there was no magnificence available to her at all. She crossed the bridge and let him grip her hands with his flour-roughened ones and said, "I know. I know," in a voice that shook, and behind her she heard Jonah say, gently, to no one in particular, "It's all right. We're just glad to be home," as if he had lived here all his life, as if the valley hadn't tried to spit him out at its border two seasons ago hard enough to crack bone.

Bramble flew a slow circuit over the crowd and landed on the well-head, which was, Astrid suspected, deliberate theater. "For those who don't know me," he announced, in the carrying voice he saved for occasions, "I am Bramble, formerly in service to a god whose name you burned out of your records, currently in service to nobody, and I have opinions about all of you. I'll share them gradually. Don't crowd."

The tension broke the way ice breaks — all at once, with a sound like laughter that half the crowd didn't know they were making. Someone brought out cider. The tanner's widow pressed a heel of bread into Jonah's hands and told him he was too thin, and Jonah, who had been too thin since the day Astrid pulled him half-dead through the ward-line, accepted it with the bewildered gratitude of a boy who had spent his whole life being told he was a burden and was only now learning the grammar of being welcomed.

Her mother was waiting at the house.

Astrid had written ahead too — short letters, sent from Vael Crossing and again from the capital, letters in which she had tried and failed to explain everything: the devourer, the ember, the throne room, the choice. Her mother had written back exactly once, a letter three lines long, which was more frightening than any length of silence could have been. *Come home. Bring them all. There is soup.*

Now her mother stood in the doorway with flour on her apron and her hands doing the same wringing thing the miller's had done, and for a long moment neither of them moved, and then her mother crossed the yard in six strides and held her so tightly that Astrid's ribs ached, and Astrid put her face in her mother's hair and breathed in bread and woodsmoke and home, and did not make a single magnificent speech.

"I lost something," she whispered, when she could speak. "You should know that before— before anything else. I can't hear them anymore, Mam. The animals. It's gone. I gave it."

Her mother pulled back and took her daughter's face in both floury hands and looked at her the way she used to look at her across the supper table when Astrid was small and had broken something precious and was waiting to be punished. "You gave it to save a life," her mother said. "That's not losing, love. That's spending. And I didn't raise you to hoard."

Then she looked past Astrid's shoulder at the ragged procession coming up the lane — Jonah with his bread, Maren leaning on her staff with her grey braid full of road-dust, Bramble riding Maren's pack now because he believed in distributing his favors — and raised her voice in the tone that had governed kitchens and, apparently, kingdoms' worth of found family without any difficulty at all.

"Well, don't stand in the lane. Soup's on."

* * *

The first weeks were work, and the work was good.

Hollowmere had a great deal to unlearn. The village elders who had once enforced the old prohibitions — the whisper-networks, the watched doors, the exile sentences handed down for the crime of a girl talking to horses — sat through the conclave's teaching circles with the grim diligence of students catching up on lessons skipped for a generation. Some of them wept. Some of them argued. One of them, Garrick, stood up in the third circle and confessed, in front of everyone, that he had known about Astrid for years and had said nothing, not out of kindness but out of cowardice, and that his silence had made him complicit in her loneliness, and Astrid had to leave the room because her eyes were burning and she refused to let him see it.

Jonah found his place faster than anyone. The temple in Hollowmere had been empty since its priest fled during Wardfall — fled south, toward the Crowned God's light, and no one had mourned him — and it turned out that a boy who had carried a fragment of murdered divinity in his chest for two years had certain thoughts about what worship ought to be. He reopened the chapel himself, swept it, mended the roof with his own hands, and preached his first sermon to eleven villagers and a crow, and the sermon was five minutes long and consisted mostly of the sentence *you were never required to be afraid*, repeated until it stopped sounding like a sentence and started sounding like weather.

"He's going to be insufferable," Maren predicted, watching him through the chapel window with her arms crossed and her expression betraying her completely. "Give it a year. He'll have opinions about incense."

"He already has opinions about incense," Astrid said.

"Gods help us."

Maren herself took the cottage at the valley's edge — the one with the herb garden gone to seed, the one that had stood empty since the last hedge-witch had been driven out before Astrid was born. She planted it again. She hung her charms in the window where the neighbors could see them, which was the point; the seeing was the mending. Within a month she was delivering babies and setting bones and selling fever-tinctures, and children followed her around the lanes in a crocodile asking questions she answered with theatrical reluctance, and Bramble perched on her fence post most mornings holding court, dispensing gossip and corrections in equal measure.

And Astrid — Astrid went to the stables.

She went the first morning without deciding to, drawn by habit older than thought, and stood in the doorway of the miller's stable with her heart going hard, looking at the mare. The mare was old now, grey-faced, the sickness long past her but leaving her thin. She lifted her head at Astrid's step and blew a greeting through her nose, warm air against Astrid's palm, and Astrid waited for the voice — the soft pressure of animal meaning against her mind, the wordless conversation she had carried like a second heartbeat her entire life —

And heard nothing. Just breath. Just horse.

She had known it would be like this. She had chosen it with her eyes open, had spoken the words of the unbinding with the taste of Jonah's terror in her mouth, had felt her gift leave her like a tide going out — and she would choose it again, a thousand times, because the alternative had been Jonah's heart stopping in her arms, and there was no arithmetic in which that sum came out acceptable. But knowing a grief is true does not make it small, and every morning for the first month she stood in that doorway and waited for a voice that did not come, and grieved, plainly and privately, the way you grieve a limb.

On the thirty-first morning, Bramble was already there when she arrived, sitting on the manger rail.

"She says you look tired," he reported.

Astrid stared at him. The mare snorted and stamped, and Bramble cocked his head at her, listening with the ordinary excellent ears of a very clever bird, reading the swivel of her ears and the set of her neck and forty small signals that Astrid had always received directly and now could only witness from outside, like a woman watching a conversation through glass.

"She also says the new hay is inferior," Bramble continued, "and that she has been patient about it, and that patience is not the same as approval. She says—" he paused, head tilting further, and when he spoke again his voice had gentled into something rare for him, something almost careful, "—she says she knew. All those years. She knew what you were saying to her, girl. Horses aren't fools. You think a creature that remembers every path it's ever walked can't remember kindness? She didn't need your gift to know you loved her. She needed you to keep showing up. So. Show up."

Astrid leaned her forehead against the mare's warm neck and cried, briefly and without shame, and the mare stood still and let her, and if there was no voice in Astrid's mind there was something else — breath and warmth and weight, the plain physical fact of another creature choosing to stay — and it was not the same, and it was enough, and she began learning, slowly, the way everyone in Hollowmere was learning, to live in the country of enough.

After that, Bramble came to the stable most mornings. He claimed it was for the company. He translated anyway — the dogs, the goats, the crows passing overhead with news of the wider world, the fat and self-satisfied barn cat whose opinions on the mice situation Bramble rendered with visible disgust. "He speaks for all of us," the conclave had joked, when they granted Bramble some formal title Astrid never bothered to learn, and Bramble had puffed up so thoroughly he nearly fell off his perch. But it was true, and it was not a joke, and Astrid understood, in the quiet of the stable with the mare's breath on her hands, that her gift had not died. It had been *shared*. Scattered among the ones who loved her. Love given up, she had learned on the worst day of her life, is still love kept — and sometimes it comes back wearing feathers and an attitude.

* * *

Winter came early that year, the first winter of the new wards, and Hollowmere discovered what a roof made of promises felt like when the snow fell.

Magic moved through the valley openly now, gentle and domesticated by consent. Wisps rose off the millpond on cold mornings like steam given ideas. The hedgerows bloomed out of season in small defiant patches where old faith lingered in the roots. Children born that spring would carry small talents — Maren predicted it, watching the pregnant women at market with a professional squint — and for the first time in living memory, no one in Hollowmere planned to nail the shutters against them.

On the night of the first real snow, the whole improbable family ended up at Astrid's mother's table, because her mother's table had become the center of gravity of the household and no one had the power to resist it. Soup, then bread, then Maren's plum brandy, which was less a drink than an event. Garrick came, and the miller, and the tanner's widow with her new baby, who was passed hand to hand down the table and who gripped Jonah's finger with astonishing force and refused to release it, to his evident delight and everyone else's teasing.

Later, when the table had thinned and the fire had burned to its red bones, Astrid stepped out into the yard to check the sky, an old habit from nights when the stars had been the only witnesses to her secrets.

Snow was falling in big slow flakes, hissing softly where it met the embers of the forge next door. The new wards sang their low subterranean note, and the snow seemed to fall *through* the song, unhurried, unafraid, each flake landing where it chose. Beyond the valley's rim the fells stood white and vast under a torn-open sky of stars, and somewhere out there the world was still wounded — the devourer's cults did not vanish with its death, only scattered; the wild magic was still diminished; there were valleys like this one all across the north, walled in fear, waiting for someone to come and teach them how to build roofs instead. There would be roads again. Work again. Maren already talked of it, and Bramble of flying ahead as scout, and Jonah — Jonah had begun, shyly, to speak of traveling east in spring, to the places where the old temples still rang bells for a god who wasn't there anymore, to tell them what he'd learned about what worship was for.

But that was spring. This was snow, and firelight spilling yellow across the yard, and the door opening behind her.

Jonah came out with a blanket over his shoulders and a second one folded in his hands, and he draped it around her without asking, the way he had learned to do, and stood beside her in the falling snow. For a while neither of them said anything. They had gotten good at that, the two of them — good at the silences that hold things instead of hiding them.

"The mare says you're brooding," he said finally.

"Bramble says the mare says I'm brooding."

"Bramble says a lot of things." He smiled, and the ember-light that still lived faintly behind his eyes — dimmer now, domesticated, a coal banked rather than a blaze — caught the smile and warmed it. "He told me this afternoon that I'm 'tolerable, for a priest,' which from him is practically a knighthood."

"He called me 'adequately stubborn' last week. I've decided to have it engraved."

Their laughter steamed in the cold. Then it faded, and the snow filled the space it left, and Jonah turned to face her, and his hand found hers in the dark between them — found it easily, without searching, the way a hand finds a thing it has held ten thousand times and intends to hold ten thousand more.

"Astrid," he said. "When spring comes. When we go east. I want—" He stopped, laughed at himself, started over. "Two years ago I fell out of the sky at your border and you dragged me through a wall that was trying to kill me, and I have been trying to find a way to thank you ever since, and everything I come up with is too small. So I'm not going to thank you. I'm going to ask you something instead, and you can take as long as you want answering, and the answer can change, and I'll still—" His thumb moved over her knuckles, once. "I'll still be here. Whatever you say. But I'm asking."

The snow fell. The wards sang. Somewhere behind the kitchen window Bramble's voice rose in indignant negotiation over the last of the plum brandy, and Maren's laugh rolled out under it like a bass line, and the whole warm lit weight of the found family pressed against the glass at their backs.

"Yes," Astrid said.

"I haven't asked yet."

"I know. Yes anyway. Ask me properly tomorrow, in daylight, where Bramble can ruin it, and I'll say yes again." She squeezed his hand, and felt, in the place where her gift used to live, not emptiness exactly — not anymore — but space. Room. The kind of room a roof makes. "Ask me every year if you want. I'll keep saying it. That's the whole trick I learned this year, Jonah. The things you give away keep coming back. You just have to leave the door open."

He kissed her then, snow settling in both their hair, and behind them the door banged open and Bramble's voice cut across the yard like a knife through pudding:

"If you two are quite finished, Maren's attempting a toast and Garrick is crying again, and frankly the entertainment value in there exceeds anything the night sky has to offer. Also it's freezing. Also I called dibs on the corner seat by the fire in *autumn*, which none of you respected, and I am prepared to escalate."

They went in together, hand in hand, out of the cold and into the noise and the light, and the door closed behind them — but not latched, never latched again, not in that house — and the snow went on falling gently through the singing wards, covering the valley like a blessing, like a promise kept, like a home rewritten at last in a hand that had room in it for everything they'd become.

The cost was real. It would always be real; she would feel it every morning of her life, standing in a stable doorway, listening to a silence where a voice used to be. But survival had bought this — the soup and the snow, the crying elder and the laughing witch, the boy whose hand fit hers, the crow who spoke for all of them — and standing in the firelight with her family loud around her, Astrid Kavanagh knew, with a certainty deeper than any gift she had ever possessed, that what remained was worth keeping.

Outside, far off over the fells, a hawk cried once — a wild, wordless, beautiful sound — and Astrid smiled at the window, and did not need to know what it said.
