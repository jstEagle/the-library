## 1. The Smuggler's Ledger

The goats were going to kill them, and Tamsin Thornbury had known it since before dawn.

She stood on the shelf of rock above the Greywater Pass with the wind pulling at her hood, watching the herd move along the far slope the way water moves—unhurried, inevitable, forty-odd bighorn ewes and their lambs trickling down toward the scree field that sat directly above the smugglers' route. The scree was old, stacked a thousand years deep, held together by frost and habit and nothing else. One startled hoof in the wrong place and two hundred tons of stone would come down on the caravan like the mountain's own judgment.

Below her, eight men and a boy were loading pack-mules by lantern light, their breath steaming, their voices kept low because she'd told them to. They thought her silence was professional caution. It was, mostly.

"Thornbury." Doss Rennick, the caravan master, climbed up to her ledge with the rolling gait of a man who'd spent thirty years trusting his feet more than his eyes. He was broad as a door and twice as hard to move, and he'd hired her four times now because she'd never lost so much as a crate of him. "You've got that look."

"What look?"

"The one where you're about to tell me something I don't want to hear."

Tamsin didn't answer right away. She was watching the lead ewe—a big grey female with a torn ear, one she knew, one who had grazed these slopes for nine years and remembered every winter they'd survived together. The ewe had stopped. Her head was up. She was looking not at the pass but at the sky above it, at something Tamsin couldn't see yet: weather coming down out of the north, cold enough to crack stone.

"They'll be moving through the narrows within the hour," Tamsin said. "Forty head, maybe fifty, coming down to water before the storm hits. If we're in the narrows when they come through—"

"We go around."

"You can't go around. The only other route adds three days, and you don't have three days of feed for the mules, and you told me yourself the crown's toll-men are watching the low road this month." She turned to face him. "We go through. But we go through *now*, fast and quiet, before the herd commits to the descent. And nobody cracks a whip. Whips echo."

Doss studied her the way he always did when she gave orders that sounded like prophecy—which was often, which was why he paid her triple what other guides asked and never asked why. "And if we're wrong?"

"Then you'll wish we'd gone around."

He grunted, which from Doss was agreement, and went down to hurry the loading.

Tamsin stayed on the ledge a moment longer. Then she did what she always did when no one could see her face: she closed her eyes, found the thread of attention that connected her to the grey ewe, and pulled it gently, the way you'd tug a sleeve.

*Old mother. Storm's coming down the north face. Take your lambs to the east meadow instead. There's sweet grass there, and no men.*

The reply came back the way it always did—not words, exactly, but shapes of meaning, impressions worn smooth like river stones. *Grass is grass. Men are men.* A flicker of wariness. The ewes had been moved off their routes by men before, and never for their benefit.

*These men won't touch you. I'll see to it. East meadow. Go now, before the ice.*

A long pause. Then the herd shifted, all forty-odd heads turning east at once, moving with the sudden collective purpose of animals who have received an instruction they cannot explain wanting to follow. The grey ewe looked back once—Tamsin felt the look, dry and skeptical, an old woman's glance over her shoulder—and then led them away along the contour of the slope, away from the scree, away from the narrows, away from the caravan that would have killed half of them without ever knowing they were there.

Tamsin let out a breath she hadn't realized she was holding.

That was the whole of it, really. That was the gift. Not thunder, not command, not the wild sorceries the old stories promised. Just a voice, quiet as snowfall, that beasts heard and answered—if she asked politely, if she asked soon enough, if no human being was anywhere near to watch her do it.

Speaking to beasts had been outlawed in Thornfell for twenty-three years. She knew the number precisely, because her grandmother had died in the first winter of the Edict, and Tamsin had been seven years old, and she had learned arithmetic from counting the years her grandmother hadn't come back.

She went down to the caravan.

* * *

They made the narrows by midmorning, and the narrows were empty except for wind.

Tamsin walked point with her staff, reading the mountain the way Doss read ledgers: closely, constantly, with the assumption that somewhere in the fine print something was waiting to ruin them. She called the holds before anyone saw them—"loose rock left side, hug the wall"—and the men obeyed without question, because in four runs she had never once been wrong, and smugglers are the most religious men alive when it comes to a guide who keeps them alive.

The boy, Fen, walked behind her. He was fourteen, Doss's sister's son, clever-handed and incurious in the specific way of boys raised around secrets: he knew better than to ask questions whose answers might be dangerous. But he watched her. She'd noticed it on the last run too—the way his eyes followed her when she paused at trail junctions, when she crouched to study a track, when she stood very still with her head slightly tilted, listening to something no one else could hear.

She made sure never to tilt her head when he was close.

At noon they cleared the narrows and came out onto the high shelf where the route bent south toward the border country, and Doss called a halt in the lee of a granite spine, out of the wind. The men ate hard bread and harder cheese. The mules dozed. Somewhere below, a hawk rode a thermal, and Tamsin felt its attention brush past hers like a stranger's hand in a crowd, and she carefully did not reach back.

"So," Doss said, settling beside her against the stone. He offered bread; she took it. "Rumor from the lowlands. You hear anything?"

"I don't hear rumors. Rumors cost money."

"This one's free." He chewed for a while. "Crown's posted new edicts in Millbrook. Something about beasts. Talking to them, or some such nonsense." He said it lightly, the way men say things they think are nonsense, and Tamsin kept her face still and her breathing even and bit her bread with the unhurried confidence of a woman who had never once broken a law in her life.

"Nonsense," she agreed.

"Herald's supposed to read it in the square next market day. Some new relic the king's scholars dug up. Bell of some kind." He shrugged. "Doesn't matter to us. We deal in wool and untaxed brandy, not livestock."

"No," Tamsin said. "It doesn't matter to us."

But her hands had gone cold around the bread, and she finished eating anyway, because Doss was watching, and because twenty-three years of practice had taught her that fear is a thing you swallow whole or not at all.

* * *

They camped that night in a hollow below the tree line, a bowl of pine and shadow that Tamsin had used a dozen times because it held no echoes and showed no fires. The men slept in shifts. Fen took second watch, sitting up against a log with his crossbow across his knees, and Tamsin waited until his breathing went long and slow—boys always fought sleep and always lost—and then she rose, silent, and slipped out of the camp into the dark.

This was the other life. The real one, if she was honest, though she tried not to be honest with herself about much.

She climbed the slope behind the hollow until the firelight was a faint orange smudge below her, then stopped among the pines and whistled—three notes, low, a sound she'd invented herself years ago and had never once made within earshot of a human being. It wasn't a signal any beast needed. It was just habit. Just the shape her loneliness had worn into a ritual.

The hawk came first.

She felt him before she saw him, a descending spiral of recognition, and then he dropped out of the black sky onto the branch above her head—scarred across the chest where a snare had nearly taken him six winters ago, one eye clouded but fierce, feathers ruffled against the cold. He was wild. He had never been tame and never would be, and that was the arrangement between them: he came when he chose, left when he chose, and in exchange she told him true things, because hawks despise nothing so much as a lie.

*You're late,* he said, in the wordless way of him.

*I was working.*

*You're always working.* He shuffled down the branch closer, talons clicking on bark. *There are men in the valleys. More than usual. Crown colors.*

Her stomach tightened. "How many?"

*Many. Moving slow. Carrying cages.*

"Cages?"

*One cage. Big. It screams sometimes.* The hawk's feathers flattened. *Not a man screaming. Not a beast either. Something else. The men flinch when it cries out.*

Cold walked up Tamsin's spine, vertebra by vertebra. "Where?"

*South. Coming north. Up the valley roads, village to village.* He cocked his clouded eye at her. *You should fly away, small-voice. You can't. So you should hide deeper.*

"I hide fine."

*You hide like a stone hides. Stones are found.* He ruffled, dismissive, and she almost smiled despite everything, because the hawk had been insulting her competence since the day she'd pulled the snare off him, and there was a strange comfort in being known that well by something that would eat her if she were small enough.

"There's a bell," she said. "In Millbrook. Do you know it?"

*I know bells. Bells mean men gather. I stay high.* A pause, and then, reluctantly, the way he gave true things: *The small beasts say the bell makes them want to answer it. Dogs especially. They hate it and they love it. They say it sounds like their names.*

Tamsin stood very still among the pines.

*A bell that sounds like your name.* She thought of the neighbor's dog when she was a girl, before the Edict, before everything—no. Before the Edict there had been no need to hide. She thought of the stories her grandmother had told instead, of the purges in the southern kingdoms, of how they'd always started the same way: not with soldiers, but with a tool. Something that turned the hunted into their own hunters.

"If it calls," she said slowly, "and a beast answers—what happens to the beast?"

*Nothing,* the hawk said. *That's what frightens them. Nothing happens to the beast. The men watch the beast, and then they watch whoever comes for the beast.*

The wind moved through the pines. Below her, the camp fire had burned to embers, and eight sleeping smugglers and one dreaming boy lay between her and the morning, and none of them knew that the woman they trusted with their lives broke the oldest law in Thornfell every night of her life, alone, on hillsides, in a language with no words.

"When does it ring?" she asked. "The bell in Millbrook."

*Market day,* the hawk said. *Three days.*

Three days. She'd be back in the valley country by then. She could avoid the town entirely—she'd done it before, gone seasons without setting foot on cobbled ground. Or she could walk into the square with the rest of them, anonymous, unremarkable, and watch a relic of the old war force her neighbors' dogs to betray everyone who'd ever scratched their ears.

*Tamsin.* The hawk's voice, gentler than he ever let it be in daylight. *Come away from the edge of this. Go high. Stay gone until the men pass.*

"And leave the valley beasts to answer it alone?"

*They answered bells before you were born.*

"Not this bell."

The hawk said nothing, because there was nothing to say to that, and after a while he dropped from the branch and took the darkness in one long silent glide, leaving her alone with the wind and the ember-smell of the fire below.

She stayed out another hour, doing the thing she never let herself do in daylight: talking. To the pines about the weather. To a fox she flushed from a den, apologizing for the intrusion. To the mountain itself, in the shapeless way she did, not expecting answers, just spending the silence she'd been saving all day. This was the tax her life levied—that everything true in her had to be paid out in the dark, to an audience of animals, and banked again before sunrise.

When she finally came back down into the hollow, Fen was asleep at his post, crossbow sagging. She eased it from his fingers, set it safely aside, and covered him with her own blanket, and stood a moment looking down at him with an expression she would have denied under torture.

Then she got out her ledger—the guide's ledger, the professional one, ink and figures and routes—and by ember-light she entered the day's distances in her small precise hand. At the bottom of the page, below the figures, she wrote the thing she wrote every night of a run, the private line that meant nothing to anyone but her:

*All quiet. All accounted for.*

She was blowing out the candle stub when the hair on her arms lifted.

Out in the dark beyond the hollow, something howled. Long, low, ragged at the edges—an old voice, a familiar voice, a voice she knew the way you know a lullaby. The blind wolf. Her wolf, who had not come down from the high country in two full seasons, who had no business this far south, who did not howl for greetings and did not howl for hunger.

He howled the way he howled for warnings.

Tamsin stood in the dark with her ledger in her hand, listening, and every animal within a mile of that hollow went silent at once—all of them, the owls, the mice, the mules, the whole breathing world holding its breath—and in that enormous silence she understood, with the calm certainty of a woman who has spent her whole life learning to read the fine print, that the mountain had just changed its mind about her.

She packed her go-bag before dawn.
