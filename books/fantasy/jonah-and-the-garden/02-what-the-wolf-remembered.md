## 2. What the Wolf Remembered

The wolf came at dusk, the way she always did, and Tamsin knew it before she heard it—knew it in the way the birds went quiet along the treeline, in the way the wind off the high crags seemed to hold its breath.

She was mending a snare on her porch when the hens stopped their scratching all at once. That was the first sign. Chickens were fools about most things but honest about danger, and hers had gone still as painted birds, heads cocked toward the pines.

Then the smell came down through the trees: wet fur, old meat, the particular musk of an animal that had lived long enough to stop caring what it smelled like.

"Evening, Grey," Tamsin said, not looking up from her work.

The blind wolf emerged from the treeline like a ghost deciding to be solid. She was enormous—half again the size of any wolf that had a right to exist this far south—and gray as river fog, with a face gone silver at the muzzle and eyes gone white as milk. She moved without hesitation despite the blindness, because she had memorized this clearing years ago, every root and stone, the way Tamsin had memorized every pass and switchback of Thornfell. Two creatures who knew their ground by heart.

Grey stopped ten feet away and sat, which was her way of announcing that this was not a social call.

Tamsin set down the snare. "You're early."

In summer the old wolf usually came under full dark, ghosting up from the high country where she'd once run with a pack that no longer existed, taking whatever scraps Tamsin left on the flat stone by the woodpile, staying just long enough for the silence between them to feel like conversation. But tonight she'd come in the last light, and she didn't go to the feeding stone. She sat in the middle of the clearing and stared at Tamsin with those blind white eyes, and her ears were pinned forward, and her breath came fast and shallow.

"Well," Tamsin said slowly. "That's new."

* * *

People who didn't know better thought beast-speaking worked like talking. Words going back and forth, a conversation you could write down. The ballads made it sound that way—shepherds chatting with their flocks, heroes bargaining with dragons in full sentences.

It wasn't like that. Not with wild things, anyway. A dog raised among people might manage something close to words, and the wyrmlings—if you were mad enough to deal with them—spoke in broken fragments of human speech they'd stolen from miners and smugglers over centuries. But a wolf? A wolf spoke in pictures. In pressure. In the language underneath language: hunger, territory, fear, the memory of blood.

And right now Grey was pushing pictures at her so hard and so fast that Tamsin's head ached with them.

She closed her eyes and let them come.

Firelight. No—not firelight. Lanterns. Dozens of them, strung along a column of men moving up a valley floor, and the valley was wrong, flattened and strange the way images got when an animal remembered a place it had never been, built out of pieces of other places. The men wore colors. That detail came through sharp and hard, repeated three times, four times, the way wolves repeated the things that mattered: red and black. Red and black. Red and black.

Crown colors.

Behind the men, dragged on a wagon, a cage. And in the cage—

Grey's memory shuddered there. The image splintered, refused to resolve, and what came through instead was pure sensation: a sound. A screaming sound, high and wrong, a sound that scraped against the inside of a wolf's skull like a claw against bone, a sound that had sent every living thing in three valleys fleeing uphill or into the deep earth, a sound that was still ringing in the old wolf's memory days after she'd heard it, would ring for seasons, maybe forever.

The caged thing screamed, and everything that heard it understood one thing with animal certainty:

*it is calling, and you must answer.*

Tamsin opened her eyes. Her hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against her thighs.

"That's not real," she said aloud, to the clearing, to herself. "Wolves dream. Wolves remember wrong. You heard a mountain lion in a trap somewhere, some cat gone mad in a pit, and you've been carrying it around—"

Grey made a sound low in her chest. Not a growl. Something more patient than a growl. The sound of an old creature correcting a foolish young one, and Tamsin was forty-one years old and being corrected by a wolf older than she was, and the worst part was that the wolf was right and they both knew it.

Because here was the thing Tamsin knew and had spent twenty years refusing to think about: animals did not invent. They exaggerated, they misremembered distances and numbers, they confused one man for another—but they did not invent crown colors. They did not invent cages that screamed. Those details had the weight of things seen. Things smelled. Things heard across miles of mountain air, which carried sound farther than any animal could see.

Men in red and black, moving up the valleys. With something caged that called to beasts the way a bell calls sheep.

Her grandmother's voice rose unbidden from thirty years down: *If the crown ever comes for the speakers, girl, they won't come with swords first. Swords make martyrs. They'll come with something that makes the beasts do the finding for them.*

Tamsin had been eleven. She'd asked what could possibly make a beast betray the hand that fed it, and her grandmother had looked at her for a long moment with those pale Thornbury eyes and said, "Nothing yet. Pray nothing ever." And then she'd gone back to her ledger and written something Tamsin hadn't been allowed to read, and burned the page a week later in the stove, and Tamsin had watched the paper curl and thought it was just another of the old woman's superstitions, like salting the doorstep and never whistling indoors.

Grey was still watching her. Waiting. Old wolves were patient the way glaciers were patient.

"I hear you," Tamsin said finally. "I hear you."

But hearing wasn't believing. She stood, joints cracking in the evening cold, and made herself do the ordinary things: split kindling for the morning, check the smokehouse latch, count the hens into their coop. Ordinary things, done with deliberate slowness, while behind her eyes the images kept replaying. Red and black. The wagon. The scream that wasn't a scream but a summons.

*It is calling, and you must answer.*

Against what? Against whom?

She fed Grey at the stone—dried venison, the good strips she'd been saving—and the wolf ate without hurry, which meant she wasn't starving, which meant she'd come for another reason entirely. Wolves traveled far for food. They traveled farther for warning.

When the last light failed, Grey rose, shook the dusk off her coat, and stood facing Tamsin one last time. And what she pushed across the dark then was not an image but something simpler and older, something that needed no translation at all:

*Run.*

"No," Tamsin said.

The word came out harder than she intended, and Grey's head tilted, and Tamsin realized she was gripping the porch rail hard enough to hurt her palms.

"This is my ground," she said, quieter now. "Twenty years I've kept this cabin, kept these passes, kept my mouth shut and my head down and my gift buried so deep even I forget it's there most days. Nobody knows. Nobody *can* know. I'm careful, Grey. I'm the most careful woman in Thornfell. Some column of soldiers chasing shadows in the low valleys doesn't change that."

The wolf regarded her with those blind eyes that saw more than sighted ones ever had.

"They're not looking for me," Tamsin said. "There's nothing to find."

Grey turned and ghosted back into the trees, and the forest swallowed her without a sound, and Tamsin stood alone in her clearing telling herself the same sentence over and over until it wore smooth as river stone:

*There's nothing to find.*

* * *

She lasted until midnight.

Not because she believed the wolf. Because her body believed the wolf, and bodies were traitors that way. She lay in her bed listening to the ordinary night sounds—the wind, the settling timbers, a fox working the scree below the cabin—and heard, underneath all of it, a screaming that wasn't there. By the second hour she couldn't tell whether she was remembering Grey's memory or generating it herself, and that was the moment she gave up on sleep, lit a candle, and pulled the loose stone out of the chimney breast.

The cavity behind it held everything she owned that mattered, packed for a life she'd spent two decades pretending she'd never have to live.

The go-bag came out first: oiled canvas, waterproofed twice, straps replaced every spring. Inside, dried food for six days, flint and steel, a fishing line and hooks small enough to pass for a woman's sewing kit if anyone ever searched the bag and lived to report it. A knife with no maker's mark. Thirty crowns in old coin, worn smooth. A folded map of Thornfell in her own hand—every pass, every cave, every spring—drawn from memory because her grandmother had taught her never to keep maps, and Tamsin had obeyed the letter of that law while breaking its spirit, because a guide without a map in her head was just a woman lost with confidence.

Under the bag, wrapped in oilcloth: her grandmother's ledger.

She didn't open it. She never opened it. It was a smuggler's route-book, coded in the old woman's cramped hand, routes and tolls and safe-houses for a trade that had mostly died with her, and Tamsin kept it the way you keep a dead parent's wedding ring—because throwing it away was unthinkable and reading it felt like eavesdropping on grief. She weighed it in her hands, that small dense block of paper and leather, and slid it into the bottom of the bag.

Last, the stone cavity gave up a bundle of feathers and bone: hawk talons, shed years ago by a bird that had since died, kept for reasons Tamsin had never examined too closely. Superstition, probably. Or maybe just the habit of holding on to pieces of the wild that had chosen her, back when choosing her was still safe.

She repacked the wall. She set the bag by the door.

And then she stood in the middle of her cabin, in her shift, in the cold, feeling profoundly stupid.

Look at yourself, she told her reflection in the dark window. One bad dream off a senile wolf and you're packed to run like the Edict's already signed. This is how they catch women like us, you know. Not with bells and soldiers—with our own nerves. We scare ourselves into the open. We run where nobody's chasing, and running leaves tracks.

The reflection didn't argue. Reflections never did.

She unpacked the bag. Put the food back in the wall cavity. The coin. The knife. The map. The ledger, last of all, weighed in her hands a second time, and this time she hesitated over it a heartbeat longer than the others before burying it beneath the stones.

She banked the fire. She went back to bed.

She lay awake until the sky went gray at the edges, and then she got up, and took the bag back out of the wall, and packed it again.

This time she left it packed.

* * *

Dawn found her on the porch with tea she wasn't drinking, watching the eastern passes for dust.

That was the thing about living alone in the high country: you learned to read distance. A caravan on the shepherd's road threw up dust you could see five miles off in dry weather. Soldiers moved slower than smugglers and kicked up more of it. If Grey's vision was true—if there was a column coming up the valleys—she'd have a day's warning, maybe two, before it reached anywhere near her ground.

No dust. Just morning mist burning off the lower slopes, and a hawk turning slow circles over the far ridge, and the ordinary business of a mountain waking up.

See? she told herself. Nothing.

The hawk dropped, and rose, and dropped again, hunting the scree, and Tamsin watched it longer than she needed to because watching hawks was easier than thinking. Then it lifted, crossed the valley in a long diagonal, and landed on the dead pine at the edge of her clearing—an old scarred bird, missing an eye, feathers gone brown at the edges like autumn come early.

Sorrel. Who was supposed to be hunting the western slopes, eight miles west, where the carrion was good and the wind suited him.

He'd flown eight miles out of his way to sit in her tree and stare at her with his one good eye, and what he pushed down at her, unasked, unwanted, was the same picture Grey had given her, but sharper, closer, seen from above:

The column. Real. Moving up the Shepherd's Road valley at marching pace, lanterns doused now in daylight, red and black uniforms, forty men at least, and behind them the wagon with the cage, and around the cage a clear circle in the grass where nothing walked, nothing perched, nothing grew—a circle of absence half a mile wide, because every living thing that could flee had fled, and the thing in the cage had not stopped calling since dawn.

Sorrel had gotten close enough to feel it pull at him. He showed her that too: the terrible sweet tug in his chest, the almost-answer rising in his throat, and how he'd beaten his wings and fought it and climbed until the pull broke against altitude, and how his heart was still hammering hours later.

*It asks,* Sorrel pushed at her, fierce and frightened. *It asks and asks and asks.*

Tamsin set down her tea very carefully, so as not to spill it, because her hands were not steady, and looked out at her quiet clearing, her smokehouse, her coop, her woodpile, twenty years of careful invisible life stacked neatly against a cabin wall.

One wolf's dream could be doubted. One old bird's panic could be excused.

Two witnesses, agreeing in every particular, could not.

"All right," she said softly. "All right."

She went inside and dressed for travel—wool, leather, boots broken in past the point of blisters—and took down the go-bag, and this time she added things to it instead of arguing with herself about it. The ledger went in first. Then the coin, the knife, six days of food, the flint, the line and hooks. She stood a long moment at the chimney breast, looking at the empty cavity, at the shape her whole life had left in the stone.

Then she closed the wall, wrote no note—for whom?—and stepped off her porch into the morning.

She made it exactly forty yards before she stopped.

Because the hens were making noise. Not alarm—hens had one note for alarm and this wasn't it—but the indignant clatter of a hen whose routine has been violated, and Tamsin turned, and there was Grey standing at the treeline in broad daylight, which the old wolf had not done in fifteen years, staring blindly at the cabin she could no longer see.

Tamsin walked back. The wolf fell in beside her—not ahead, beside, at her knee, the way a packmate walks—and together, without a word passing between them, because no words were needed, they went around the side of the cabin to the feeding stone.

Where Sorrel waited on the rail, one eye bright, feathers tight with purpose.

Three of them. The blind wolf who remembered, the scarred hawk who had seen, and the woman who had spent her whole life making sure no one would ever look for her—standing in her own yard, packed and ready, with a column of crown soldiers a day's march up the valleys and closing.

Tamsin looked east, toward the low country, toward Millbrook and the market road and whatever the crown was bringing up into her mountains.

"We go the other way," she said. "High passes, north fork, stay ahead of them. And first—" her jaw tightened— "first I'm going down to Millbrook, because I need salt and I need to know what's being proclaimed, and because if the crown is ringing bells in the market squares, I'd rather hear it myself than from a bird."

Grey's ear flicked. Sorrel shifted on the rail. Neither of them argued, which was its own kind of warning.

Tamsin shouldered the bag. The strap bit into her shoulder, familiar as guilt.

"Dread's just information arriving early," her grandmother used to say. "Most people throw it away unread."

Tamsin had read hers. Twice. And still she caught herself, halfway down the trail, listening—not for soldiers, not for wagons, but for a sound she'd never heard and already dreaded: something caged, calling her name in a language she'd sworn never to speak again.

The mountain held its breath around her, and began, quietly, to move.
