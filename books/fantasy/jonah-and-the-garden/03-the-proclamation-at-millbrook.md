## 3. The Proclamation at Millbrook

Millbrook smelled the same as it always did on market day: woodsmoke, wet wool, and the sweet rot of apples nobody had gotten to in time. Tamsin came down off the ridge with her hood up and her face arranged into the particular blankness that had kept her alive for twenty-six years—the look of a woman thinking about nothing, going nowhere, worth no one's trouble.

She'd left Grey and Sorrel above the treeline. That was the rule, and she'd never once broken it: no beast of hers came within a mile of town. But this morning they'd both tried. Grey had planted himself in front of the cabin door like a wall with fur, and Sorrel had screamed at her from the rail until Tamsin finally said, out loud, in words, *I have to go or we starve*, and the hawk had gone silent in a way that was worse than screaming.

Animals knew things before people did. She'd built a whole life on that fact. And this morning every creature between her cabin and Millbrook had been wrong-footed—deer standing in open meadow at midday, staring east; crows flying in tight, purposeful lines instead of their usual drunken scatter; a fox trotting down the middle of the shepherd's road without a care for who saw it.

Something was coming up the valleys. The wolf's warning hadn't been fear. It had been a schedule.

*Don't think about it. Buy salt. Buy lamp oil. Be unremarkable.*

The market square was fuller than usual, which was the first wrong thing. Market days ran thin in late autumn; most folk had already sold what they had and were saving coin against winter. But today the square was packed shoulder to shoulder, and the crowd wasn't shopping. It was waiting, the way crowds wait when someone has told them to.

Tamsin drifted toward the edge, near the cooper's shed, where she could see the whole square and be seen by almost none of it. Old habits. Old architecture.

At the far end, on the steps of the tithe-house, someone had raised a scaffold draped in blue-and-silver. Crown colors. Two soldiers stood at either side with halberds, and between them, on a small iron stand, sat something covered in grey cloth.

A cage, Tamsin thought first. Her heart went hard and quick. Then she looked again. Not a cage. A shape. Round-shouldered, like a bell lying on its side, and even from thirty yards she could feel it—not hear it, *feel* it—a faint pressure behind her eyes, like the moment before thunder.

Beside it stood a herald in a tabard too clean for Thornfell, holding a scroll sealed with enough wax to buy a horse.

"Edict," the herald called, and his voice carried wrong, too far, too flat, as if the square itself were helping him. "By decree of the Crown, let all within the borders of Thornfell attend."

The crowd hushed. Somewhere behind Tamsin a baby fussed and was shushed harder.

"The Quiet Tongue is hereby proclaimed." The herald unrolled the scroll with ceremony. "Let it be known that the practice of the beast-speech—the making of common cause with dumb creatures by voice or sign or other unnatural means—is declared treason against the Crown and the gods who set man above beast."

The words went through the crowd like cold water through cloth. Nobody gasped. Nobody moved. That was the thing about Thornfell—people here had learned, a generation ago, how to be still. Their grandparents had watched neighbors burn. Stillness was inheritance.

But Tamsin felt the stillness differently than they did. To them it was obedience. To her it was a hundred held breaths, and somewhere among them, maybe, another held breath like her own.

"Any person found to hold the Quiet Tongue," the herald read on, "shall surrender themselves to crown authority within thirty days, submit to the Rite of Severance, and enter the Crown's service—" a pause, the paper turning, "—or be taken."

*Rite of Severance.* The words meant nothing and everything. She filed them away the way she filed away trail conditions and toll schedules, some part of her mind already working while the rest of her stood blank-faced by the cooper's shed.

"And so that none may hide," the herald said, and here his voice changed—warmer, almost kind, the voice of a man delivering good news, "the Crown brings you mercy. No more suspicion. No more accusation. No neighbor betraying neighbor on rumor alone."

He gestured to the covered shape.

"The Hollow Bell answers only truth. When it rings, beasts will turn to their masters—those masters being any who speak to them unnaturally. Let the Bell ring in every square of Thornfell, and the guilty will name themselves."

He pulled the cloth away.

It was a bell, but wrong. Cast in dull grey metal that drank the morning light instead of returning it, its surface pitted like something dredged from deep water, hung from a frame of black iron carved all over with script Tamsin didn't recognize—and recognized anyway, in the way you recognize a scar pattern. Old work. Older than the crown using it.

Her grandmother's voice, unbidden, from years back, low and urgent over the fire: *If ever you see a bell that makes your teeth ache, you run, girl. You don't walk. You run.*

Tamsin's teeth ached.

The crowd murmured now, uneasy, curious. A few folk edged backward. Most pressed forward. Fear and spectacle pull the same way.

The herald raised a small hammer.

"Now," he said pleasantly, "let us see who among you has been honest."

He struck the bell.

There was no clang. That was the horror of it—the hammer fell, metal met metal, and the sound that came out wasn't sound at all. It was a pressure, a rolling wave Tamsin felt in her chest and her back teeth and the roots of her hair, a note pitched below hearing that seemed to pass through the stone under the square and come up through the soles of her boots.

Every dog in Millbrook began to bark.

No—that wasn't right either. They weren't barking. They were *turning*. Every dog in the square swung its head at once, in perfect unison, toward the scaffold, ears forward, bodies rigid, like compass needles finding north. And then, one by one, they turned again—away from the bell, sweeping across the crowd like searchlights, hunting.

Tamsin understood one full second too late what she was watching.

The Bell didn't call speakers. The Bell called *beasts*. It reached into every animal within its range and turned them into instruments—pointed them like fingers at the person their hearts answered to. It didn't detect the Quiet Tongue.

It made the beasts testify.

*Run,* said her grandmother's voice. *You don't walk.*

But running was impossible. Running was confession. The square was packed solid around her, and the soldiers were watching not the animals but the animals' owners, reading faces, and Tamsin stood frozen by the cooper's shed with blood draining out of her own, doing the only thing she could do: nothing. Being nobody. Praying to whatever old god still listened in Thornfell that neither Grey nor Sorrel was close enough for the Bell to find.

They weren't. She'd left them above the treeline. She'd followed her own rule for the first time in years, and the rule had saved her, and she wanted to be sick.

Across the square, a dog stopped searching.

It was a big brown mastiff-bitch, grey about the muzzle, and Tamsin knew her—knew her the way you know the animals of a place you've lived half your life. Bramble. Marta Ostler's dog. Marta who sold cheese and eggs at the corner stall, whose husband had died in the slate quarries, who'd once given Tamsin a heel of bread on a bad day and asked no questions about why her pack was heavy or why she never stayed long.

Bramble turned, swept past a dozen faces, and stopped.

She looked at Marta.

Not at the bell. Not at the soldiers. At Marta Ostler, standing behind her stall with her hands flat on the boards, and the dog's tail began—slowly, helplessly, the way a tail wags when the body can't lie—to move.

"Oh no," Marta said. Just that. Barely aloud. "Oh, no, no—"

"Bramble," she said then, sharper, the way you speak to a dog doing something wrong. "Home. *Home.*"

The dog took one step toward her, tail wagging, eyes bright with a love that had just become evidence.

"Hold there," the herald called out, mild as milk. "Let her come."

Two soldiers detached from the scaffold. They didn't hurry. They walked through the crowd, which parted for them the way water parts, and everyone in that square understood simultaneously and silently that the space opening around Marta Ostler's stall was not an accident of crowd-flow.

Marta grabbed the edge of her stall. Her knuckles went white. "She's just a dog," she said, louder. "She's fifteen years old, she's deaf in one ear, she doesn't know which way she's pointing—"

"Then you have nothing to fear," the herald said, and smiled, and it was the smile that did it, the terrible reasonable smile of a man who believes he is being merciful. "The Bell does not err. If the beast turns to you, the beast knows you. If the beast knows you—" he spread his hands, "—then the Crown offers you the Rite. Thirty days. Come willingly, keep your family, keep your home. Refuse, and we take you, and your family watches."

"I'm not a speaker," Marta said. Her voice cracked down the middle. "I talk to her. Everyone talks to their dog. I tell her to sit, I tell her—"

"You tell her," the herald agreed gently, "and she understands you. Don't she, though."

The soldiers reached the stall. One took Marta's arm—not roughly, that was the worst part, courteously, the way you escort an honored guest—and Marta looked at the crowd. At her neighbors. At the faces she'd known for twenty years, faces that looked everywhere except at her, because looking was complicity and not-looking was survival and everyone in that square had learned the arithmetic young.

Tamsin looked too. Away. At the ground. At her own boots. Doing the thing she had done ten thousand times, the thing that had kept her breathing since she was nine years old and her grandmother pressed a finger to her lips and said *never where anyone can hear, never, never*—

And then Bramble howled.

It wasn't grief. Tamsin knew animal grief, knew it better than her own name, and this wasn't it. It was the Bell, still humming in the stones, still holding the dog's throat in its fist, dragging the sound out of her—a long rising note that hurt to hear, that made three other dogs in the square answer it, that made Tamsin's own chest seize with the awful recognition that the sound was aimed. The dog was calling to her person the way a lost lamb calls, and the Bell was amplifying it, broadcasting it, turning love itself into a siren that marked its owner for the whole valley to witness.

Marta Ostler was led through her own village to the wagon at the square's edge, and her dog strained after her on the rope a soldier had somehow gotten on her, and the crowd watched its boots, and the herald thanked Millbrook for its cooperation, and said the Bell would return, and said thirty days was a generous term, and said the Crown was merciful.

Tamsin bought her salt. That was the discipline of it, the ugly necessary discipline: she joined the queue at the chandler's with her hood up and her face empty, and she counted out exact coin, and she said *thank you* in a flat voice, and her hands did not shake until afterward, alone, when shaking cost nothing.

She bought lamp oil too. She bought dried fish and a new flint. She did everything a woman with nothing to hide would do, and inside she was screaming, and the scream had a very specific shape: *that could have been me. That was me, one mile of distance ago. If I'd brought Grey down to the market like always, if Sorrel had been on my shoulder—*

One mile. Her life had hung on one mile of habit.

* * *

She was nearly at the west gate when the boy caught up with her.

Tam'sin—no one called her anything in town, that was the point—but this boy, Jory Cooper's lad, maybe twelve, came pelting after her with his cap in his hand and terror written all over him, and she almost walked faster. Almost. But he was a child, and children were the one category of human her rules made room for.

"Mistress Thornbury." He was panting. "Mistress—they're asking at the houses. After. The soldiers. Going door to door, asking who keeps animals, who feeds strays, who's got birds coming to their window—" He swallowed. "Da says you live up past the high meadow alone, and they'll ask, and Da says to warn you 'cause—" He faltered, embarrassed. "'Cause you fixed our goat's leg that time. In the night. And never said nothing."

Tamsin looked at him. The boy's eyes were wide and honest and frightened, and behind him Millbrook went on with its market day, stalls closing early, the wagon with Marta Ostler in it already a rattle of wheels on the south road.

"Thank Jory for me," she said. "Tell him I'm just a guide. Tell them all I'm just a guide."

"Yes'm." He hesitated. "Is it true? What the herald said—that the Bell finds them true?"

"It found a cheese-seller today," Tamsin said. "What do you think?"

The boy went pale and ran.

She stood a moment longer at the gate, looking back at the square, at the scaffold where the Hollow Bell sat under its grey cloth again, drinking light. Thirty days, the herald had said. Thirty days of grace, and bells in every square, and door-to-door questions, and a mountain full of creatures who trusted her, and a cabin with a blind wolf in it who couldn't run fast anymore and wouldn't leave anyway.

Thirty days. Less, really, by the time word traveled and panic traveled faster.

Tamsin pulled her hood lower and started up the west road, walking, not running, because a woman with nothing to hide walks, and every step of it was a lie, and behind her the Bell's silence followed like a hand between her shoulders.

Above the treeline, she knew, Grey would be waiting. And tonight, for the first time in her life, she was going to break her oldest rule—she was going to sit down with the wolf and the hawk and every wild thing that would listen, and she was going to do the unthinkable.

She was going to make a plan.
