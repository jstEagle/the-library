## 4. The Bell's First Song

The road home from Millbrook was a wound she kept walking into.

Tamsin had taken the high track instead of the valley road, the one that clung to the shoulder of Cragmoor and gave her three days of stone and scree and no neighbors, no dogs, no eyes. It was the long way. It was the safe way. She told herself both of those things every time her feet chose it without asking, and by the second morning she had stopped believing either one.

Three days since the herald's voice. Three days since the neighbor's dog had lifted its head and answered the Hollow Bell with a sound like a child crying its own name, and Master Aldren had been folded into the crown's wagon like a sack of meal while his wife stood in the crowd and did not scream, because screaming was what they wanted, screaming was how they found the rest of you.

Tamsin had not screamed. Tamsin had not moved. Tamsin had stood in that square with a basket of dried fish she never bought and let her face go smooth as river ice, and inside that ice something had been clawing the whole time, saying *run*, saying *they know, they'll always know now*, and she had walked out of Millbrook at a farmer's pace with her heart going like a rabbit's and her face saying nothing at all.

That was the trade. That was always the trade. You could have your gift or your life, and if you were careful you could have neither, just the space between them where a person lived.

She was halfway up the Cragmoor switchbacks when the hawk found her.

It came down off the thermals in a long slanting stoop and pulled up ten feet above her head, wings wide, and Tamsin felt the words arrive the way they always did—not sound, not quite, but pressure, meaning pressed against the inside of her skull like a thumb against an eggshell.

*Smoke. Many men. The small bell walks with them.*

Her stomach dropped through the mountain.

"Where," she said aloud, because there was no one for forty miles to hear, and because speaking made the fear take shape, and shape was something you could fight.

*Behind. Below. Coming up the shepherd's road.*

The shepherd's road. Her road. The road that led, in six miles, past the place where the blind wolf slept under the leaning stone, and beyond that to her cabin, and beyond that to everything she had spent thirty-one years building out of silence.

"How many men?"

*More than stars on a cloudy night.* Hawk had no numbers. Hawk had impressions, and the impression was a river of iron and torch-smell moving slow up the valley floor, patient as winter.

"And the bell?"

Hawk's feathers flattened along its neck. Even at distance, even secondhand, the thing frightened the wild things, and that fright came through the bond like cold water.

*It is hungry,* the hawk said. *It sings and things must answer.*

Tamsin stood on the switchback with the wind pulling at her hood and did the arithmetic she'd been doing since Millbrook, the arithmetic that never came out any different. Run north, and the hunting parties were already there. Run south, and the borders would be sealed within the month—Wren Alcott's maps had shown her that much before he'd vanished into the rockslide country. Stay, and be found. There was no direction on the compass that led away from this. There was only through.

"Go up," she told the hawk. "Stay high. Don't listen to it. Whatever it sings, don't answer."

*I do not answer bells,* said the hawk, with the flat contempt only raptors can manage, and climbed until it was a fleck against the cloud.

Tamsin shouldered her pack and started down toward the wolf.

* * *

She found him where she'd known he'd be, in the hollow beneath the leaning stone where the snow lingered latest and the wind couldn't reach. He was old now, older than their friendship, older than the edict, maybe older than the crown itself as far as Tamsin knew. His muzzle had gone white as frost. His eyes had gone dark years ago, filmed over like river stones, and still he turned his great gray head toward her footsteps when she was fifty yards out, because he had never needed eyes to find her. He had her smell, and he had her voice, and between those two things he had built a picture of the world more accurate than most people ever managed with theirs.

"You heard," she said.

He made a sound low in his chest, not quite a growl, not quite speech. Old wolves spoke in weather. What came to her was overcast, pressure dropping, the smell of a storm two valleys off.

"I know." She sat down in the snow beside him, which was foolish, which soaked her trousers through in a minute, and she did it anyway because some comforts were worth wet wool. "I know they're coming. I'm working on it."

He leaned his weight against her shoulder, all sixty pounds of him, and she took it, the way she'd been taking his weight since he'd wandered half-starved into her life nine winters back with his eyes already going and his pride still enormous.

Here was the thing Tamsin had never said to anyone, because saying it would have been suicide: she loved this animal more than she had ever loved a person. Here was the other thing: if the Bell rang within a mile of this hollow, he would answer it. Not because he was weak. Because he was loyal, and the Bell sang a master's voice, and old blind creatures who had lost their packs answered masters. That was the whole filthy trick of it. It didn't hunt speakers. It hunted the love speakers had planted in the world, and it turned that love into a leash.

She'd understood it in fragments before. Standing on the Cragmoor switchbacks with the hawk's warning still ringing in her, she understood it whole.

They weren't coming to find her. They were coming to make everything that loved her point at her like a drawn bow.

"I have to move you," she said. "High camp. The cave behind the falls. You remember it."

*Cold,* said the wolf, in the tone of an elder being asked to relocate on principle.

"Yes. Cold. And alive."

He grumbled, but he rose, stiff in the forelegs, and stood swaying while she gathered herself. And that was when the wind changed.

It came up the valley in a single gust, warm and wrong, carrying woodsmoke and horse and oiled leather and underneath it all a note that wasn't a smell at all but a sound remembered in the body, a resonance, a hum, like standing too close to a struck bell that hadn't finished dying.

The wolf's head snapped toward the valley mouth. Every hair along his spine stood up.

"It's here," Tamsin breathed. "It's already here."

* * *

Later, she would try to reconstruct those next minutes and find gaps in them, places where fear had simply eaten the record. What remained was this:

She was on the lower slope, wolf beside her, moving fast for the tree line, when the Bell sang.

It did not ring the way bells ring. There was no strike, no clang. It was as if someone had reached into the air and pulled a single thread of it taut and plucked, and the note went out across the valley and through the valley and through her, and every living thing within the reach of that note turned toward it.

She saw it happen. That was the horror of it—that she saw it from outside, the way the crown's men must have seen it, the way Serah Vane or whoever commanded this party saw it from their position on the shepherd's road below. The ravens went up off the pines in a black sheet and wheeled, all together, all facing the same point. A red fox broke cover two hundred yards east and sat down in the open, ears forward, gazing down-valley like a dog waiting for its owner's hand. Somewhere upslope a herd of elk stopped mid-flight and stood in the snow, breathing steam, heads turned.

And the wolf.

The wolf turned.

Not all the way. Halfway. His blind face swung toward the sound and his body followed it a step, two steps, and then he stopped, and Tamsin watched him fight it, watched the old muscles lock, watched his claws dig furrows in the frozen ground, and the sound that came out of him was the worst thing she had ever heard—an animal being torn between two loves, the pack-voice calling and the woman holding his scruff, and he was too old for this war and it was happening inside him anyway.

"Tamsin," she said to him, using her own name because names were anchors, because her name was the first word he'd ever learned from her. "Look at me. You're mine. I'm yours. Nobody else. *Nobody else.*"

His head trembled between the two calls.

And the Bell's note went through *her.*

She had thought—she had actually believed, in the arrogance of thirty-one years of successful hiding—that the Bell would pass over her. That it was made for beasts, tuned for beasts, that a human speaker would be invisible to it the way she'd been invisible to everyone her whole life. She had staked everything on that belief, and the note went through her chest like a spear of ice and she felt it *grip.*

It didn't hurt. That was the terrible part. It felt like being known. It felt like the moment after years alone when someone finally says your name in a crowd—and it wanted her to turn, wanted her to walk down that slope with her chin up and her secret shining out of her like a lamp, and part of her, some starved buried part of her, *wanted to.*

She bit down.

She bit down on her own lip, hard, harder, past the point of pain into the meat of it, and the pain came up red and animal and hers, and it was the ugliest magic she ever worked: she used the body's oldest voice, the voice under all voices, the one that says *danger, bleed, flee*, and she drowned the Bell's song in her own blood.

The grip loosened. The note went on singing, patient, vast, indifferent, but she was no longer facing it. She was facing the wolf, both hands fisted in his ruff, her lip pouring warmth down her chin, and she was growling at him through the torn flesh, actual words, human words, ugly ones:

"*Mine. You're mine. Come home.*"

And the wolf—the brave, blind, stubborn ruin of a wolf—came home. He turned his back on the Bell's song with his whole body shaking, and pressed his bleeding-hearted head against her knees, and stayed there while the note rolled over them both and found nothing it could hold.

Above them, the ravens scattered. The fox fled. The elk ran. The Bell's first song ended the way songs end, in silence, and in the silence Tamsin knelt in the snow with blood dripping off her jaw and understood, at last, completely, what she was running from.

They didn't need to catch speakers. They didn't need proof, or informants, or trials. They had built a machine that made the whole living world into witnesses. Every hound, every horse, every milk-cow and barnyard hen in Thornfell was now a spy that didn't know it was spying. The Bell didn't detect the hidden tongue. It conscripted everything that could hear it, and somewhere down that valley a man in crown colors was reading the answers off the animals like a hunter reading tracks.

Aldren's dog hadn't betrayed him by choice. Nothing in Thornfell would ever betray anyone by choice again.

"Up," she rasped to the wolf, and her voice came out wrecked. "We're going up. High camp. Now. And we're not stopping."

* * *

They made the falls by full dark, and the cave behind them by moonrise, and Tamsin built no fire because fire was visible and her lip needed no help finding attention. She sat with her back against cold stone and a strip of linen pressed to her mouth and listened to the valley below, where the hunting party had made its own camp, and their fires burned cheerful and orange, and once, faintly, carried up on the night wind, she heard laughter.

They laughed. They'd rung their bell and read their animals and written their lists, and tonight somewhere a farm wife was learning why her chickens had screamed her name.

The wolf lay against her side, still trembling at intervals, chasing the song out of his dreams. The hawk had not come down. High roosts were safe roosts; she'd taught it that herself, years ago, without knowing she was rehearsing for this.

*Tomorrow,* she thought, and the thought tasted of copper. *Tomorrow they work up-valley. Toward my cabin. Toward Pip's hollow, if Wren's map is right and the girl hasn't moved. Toward everything.*

She had spent her whole life believing silence was armor. Tonight, with her own blood drying on her chin, she understood that silence was just a slower way to die—one beast, one neighbor, one market square at a time. The Bell had sung once and the entire valley had answered, and the only reason she was free was that she'd bitten through her own mouth rather than sing with it.

How long could she keep doing that? How long could any of them?

In the dark behind the waterfall, the wolf dreamed and whimpered, and Tamsin put her hand on his neck and stared out at the moonlit snow and began, for the first time in her life, to think about fighting instead of hiding.

Far below, at the edge of the firelight, a sentry shouted—a short, sharp call—and pointed up-slope, toward the high passes.

Toward the cave country.

Toward the girl in the hollow.
