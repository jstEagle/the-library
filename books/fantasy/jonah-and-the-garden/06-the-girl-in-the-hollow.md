## 6. The Girl in the Hollow

The map Wren Alcott carried was a lie drawn beautifully.

Tamsin had known that within the first hour of following it—known by the way he kept touching the parchment with two fingers, the way a man touches a wound he's pretending isn't there. But the lie had a shape to it, and the shape led them up out of Millbrook's valley, away from the Bell-song still ringing in her teeth like a struck bone, and into country she knew better than her own hands.

"Here," Wren said, stopping at the mouth of a ravine so narrow the noon light never touched its floor. He held the map flat against his knee and pointed at a blank space where a mountain should have been. "There's a cave system through here. Old mining tunnels from before the Edict. If anyone was running, this is where they'd run."

"You're reading that off a piece of paper you drew yourself," Tamsin said.

"I'm reading it off a piece of paper I *un*drew myself." He said it lightly, but his eyes weren't light at all. "There are places on the crown's maps that don't exist because I made them not exist. Hollows. Gaps. Rooms I built into the kingdom for people to disappear into." He folded the map away. "I've been waiting eleven years for someone to use one."

The hawk circled once overhead—her scarred hawk, come down from the high crags when she'd whistled nothing at all, just lifted her arm the way she did—and screamed once, a sound like tearing tin.

"She says something's already using it," Tamsin translated, and didn't say how she knew.

Wren looked at her for a long moment. He hadn't asked, in three days of hard walking, what exactly she was. She suspected he'd worked it out anyway. Men who lied professionally recognized other people's silences for what they were.

"Then we go carefully," he said.

* * *

The ravine narrowed until they walked sideways, packs scraping stone, the stream under their boots running cold and quick over slate. Tamsin went first. That wasn't negotiable; she'd told Wren as much on the first morning, and he'd had the good sense not to argue with a woman who could read a mountainside the way he read ink.

Halfway in, she stopped.

Not because of anything she saw. Because of what she felt—a pressure behind her sternum, faint as a finger tapped against glass. The old feeling. The one she'd spent thirty years pretending was indigestion, or nerves, or nothing.

Something nearby was speaking without words.

She crouched, laid her palm flat on the wet stone, and let herself listen the way she only ever listened alone: fully, shamelessly, with the part of her she kept locked in a cellar most days.

*Small. Small-small-small. Hurt-hurt. Hungry-long. Fox says hide-hide-hide but fox is tired too.*

Her breath went out of her slow and controlled, the way she breathed when a rockslide started, because if she let it out any other way it would have come out as a sound.

A child.

"There's someone ahead," she said, standing. Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "In the caves."

"Refugees?"

"Maybe." She turned back toward him. In the dim green light of the ravine, Wren's face was all angles and shadow. "Listen to me. Whatever we find—if it's a child, if it's armed, if it's both—you follow my lead. You don't call out. You don't make promises. People who've been hiding a long time will believe any promise, and then they'll hate you for it later."

"And what will you do?" he asked.

It was a fair question. She didn't have an answer, so she didn't give him one. She turned and kept walking.

* * *

The cave mouth was hidden behind a curtain of ivy gone bronze with autumn, and inside, the old mine shafts ran back into the dark like veins. Tamsin counted her steps the way her grandmother had taught her—left foot, breath, right foot, breath—and at forty paces she stopped again, because the pressure behind her sternum had become a ringing.

And under the ringing, small and cracked and human:

*"Don't come. Don't come don't come don't come—"*

Not spoken aloud. Spoken the way Tamsin spoke to beasts, the way no human being should be able to speak, spilling out of the dark unguarded, undisciplined, loud as a bonfire.

Tamsin stood very still in the blackness and felt thirty years of careful, practiced invisibility tear straight down the middle.

Because the child wasn't hiding. The child thought she was hiding, the way a bird thinks a window is sky—but every animal in half a mile knew exactly where she was, and so did every Bell within range of whatever that voice touched. The girl was a beacon. She was a lit candle walking around inside a powder store.

"Tamsin?" Wren whispered behind her.

"There's a girl," she said. "Eight years old, maybe nine. And she's—" How to explain it? "She speaks the way I do. Except she doesn't know how to stop."

Wren was quiet a moment. "The Bell—"

"Would find her in a day. Maybe less." Tamsin closed her eyes. In the dark she could feel the child's fear radiating outward, and threaded through it, steadier, older, a second presence: the fox. *Fox says hide. Fox says fox will bite first.* A scarred little sentinel, running itself ragged around a child it couldn't protect from the thing that actually endangered her—which was the child herself.

She had a choice. She understood that with terrible clarity, standing in the cold dark with her hand on the stone. She could turn around. She could walk Wren back out of the ravine and lose themselves in the high passes and tell herself, for the rest of her life, that there had been nothing she could do. She'd gotten very good at telling herself things like that. It was practically a craft, like tanning hides or reading weather. Thirty years of practice.

Or.

"Wait here," she said.

"That's not—"

"*Wait here.*" And then, because he deserved more than an order: "If I'm not back in a quarter hour, go back down the ravine and don't stop until Millbrook is a memory."

He didn't answer, but she heard him settle against the rock, and she heard him not follow her, which for a man like Wren Alcott was probably an act of considerable discipline.

She went forward alone.

* * *

She didn't announce herself. Announcing yourself to a frightened child with a fox was how you got bitten. Instead she sat down ten feet from the inner chamber, where the tunnel widened into an old ore room, and she spoke into the dark the only safe way she knew how—not with her mouth.

*I'm not going to hurt you. My name is Tamsin. I'm like you.*

The silence that followed had a texture to it—the silence of something small holding very, very still.

*Liar,* said the fox's voice, sharp and close now, somewhere to her left. *Liar liar liar. Big ones always lie. Big ones took the mother. Big ones took the father. Fox bit one and the big one kicked fox into the wall and took them both away in a cart with bars.*

Tamsin's throat tightened. She let the silence sit, and then she said, aloud, quietly, in the plain human voice she used for prices and directions and everything else that didn't matter: "My name is Tamsin Thornbury. I guide smugglers through these mountains. I've lived alone in a cabin above Shepherd's Road for eleven years. I have a blind wolf who eats from my hand and a hawk with a torn wing who sleeps on my roof beam. I speak to beasts. I've done it my whole life, and I've never told a living soul until three days ago, and the only reason I'm telling you now is that you already know it's true, because you can hear it in me the way I can hear it in you."

Silence.

Then, from the darkness, a voice—small, hoarse, unused. Not the mind-voice. The real one, rusty as a gate left shut all winter.

"How do I know you're not wearing a Bell?"

Tamsin almost laughed. Almost. It caught in her chest like a fishbone, because the question was so practical, so exactly the question a survivor asks, and it came out of a child.

"No Bell," she said. "You'd know. Bells feel like hooks. Like something pulling your insides toward it. Have you felt that?"

A long pause. Then, smaller: "Once. In the valley. Papa said run. Mama said run. Fox said run. I ran." A sniff. "They didn't."

Tamsin closed her eyes. *There it is*, she thought. *The whole story in four sentences.* Somewhere down-valley, a market square, a Bell rung, parents who couldn't run fast enough or hadn't been warned or had stayed to buy their daughter time. It was the story the whole kingdom was writing now, thousands of times over, in ash and cart-bars.

"I'm coming closer," Tamsin said. "Slowly. Your fox can bite me if it wants. Fair warning—I'll probably yell, and I apologize in advance for the language."

Something in the dark made a sound that might have been a laugh, or might have been a sob, or might have been both wearing each other's clothes.

She crawled forward into the ore room, and the fox hit her.

It came off a shelf of rock like a thrown torch—all red fur and fury, teeth finding her forearm, and she did exactly what she'd promised: yelled, swore with genuine artistry, and held still. The fox was starving. She could feel its ribs under her grip like a bundle of kindling. It bit her and shook and bit again, and then, gradually, it registered what she was letting it do—that she wasn't fighting, wasn't reaching for it, was taking its pain with her free hand pressed flat against its heaving side.

*You smell like wolf,* the fox said at last, bewildered, teeth still in her sleeve. *Old wolf. Blind one. You feed him.*

"Every third night," Tamsin said. "He prefers the left side of his face scratched. Don't tell him I told you."

The fox let go. It backed up two steps, hackles still up, and sat, and stared at her with amber eyes that were far too old for the body carrying them.

Behind it, wedged into the gap between the ore room wall and a fallen timber, was a girl.

She was thin in the way that made Tamsin's chest ache—thin the way winter-thin livestock got, all edges and hollows. Hair cut raggedly, maybe by her own hand. Eyes enormous in a smudged face. She wore a coat sized for a much bigger child, sleeves rolled six times, and clutched in one fist a wooden fox carved badly and lovingly, worn smooth at the ears from handling.

"Hello," Tamsin said.

The girl said nothing. But Tamsin felt her, the way you feel a fire through a wall—this roiling, unguarded broadcast of want and terror and hope, hope so big it was dangerous, hope saying *maybe-maybe-maybe* while terror said *liar-liar-liar*, and underneath it all a question the girl didn't even know she was asking, aimed at Tamsin like an arrow:

*Are you real?*

"I'm real," Tamsin said aloud, and then realized the girl hadn't asked aloud either, and that they were already doing it—already speaking the forbidden way, face to face in the dark, and it felt like setting down a weight she'd forgotten she was carrying. It felt like sin. It felt like the first honest breath she'd taken in years.

"What's your name?"

"Pip." The word came out cracked. "Papa called me Pip. On account of I was small even when I was borned."

"Pip," Tamsin repeated. And then, because the silence was filling up with things too large for either of them: "Your fox has been keeping you alive. Do you know that? Out here, alone, this long. That's not luck. That's love."

"He talks too much," Pip whispered. A ghost of something crossed her face—not quite a smile. "He worries."

"Foxes worry," Tamsin agreed gravely. "It's their whole profession."

*She likes you,* the fox said suspiciously, from beside Pip's knee. *Do not make her cry. If she cries I will bite you again.*

"Noted," Tamsin said.

* * *

Wren appeared at the tunnel mouth twenty minutes later, having clearly decided that a quarter hour was a suggestion rather than a term. He stopped dead when he saw the scene: Tamsin sitting cross-legged on the mine floor sharing strips of dried venison with a feral child and a skeletal fox, the child watching him with the flat wariness of a wild thing deciding whether he was weather or threat.

"This is Wren," Tamsin said. "He draws maps. Bad ones, on purpose."

"They're excellent maps," Wren said automatically, and then caught himself. "That is—they're lies. Excellent lies. Hello."

Pip studied him. Then, aloud, in that rusty voice: "Is he like us?"

"No," Tamsin said. "But he hid people like us for eleven years, in the spaces he left in the king's own maps. So he's ours anyway."

Something shifted in the girl's face at the word *ours*. Tamsin saw it land and wished immediately that she could take it back, because it was a promise, and she'd warned Wren not to make promises, and here she was making the largest one of her life with a strip of venison halfway to her mouth.

She chewed. Swallowed. Made herself keep going, because retreat now would cost more than the promise.

"Here's what happens next," she said. "We can't stay. This hollow is safe from men, but it isn't safe from the Bell. Every time you speak—with your mouth or otherwise—it's like lighting a signal fire. You understand?"

Pip's chin tucked down. "Mama was teaching me to stop. Before." Her voice wobbled. "She said, *hold it in your teeth, Pip, hold it like a secret.* But I'm bad at secrets. I always was. Papa said I'd tell Santa Claus his own presents early."

The laugh that escaped her was wet and sudden, and then she was crying, silently, the way children cry who have learned crying brings attention, and the fox pressed against her side and glared at Tamsin as though the tears were her doing, which, fairly, they were.

Tamsin moved. She didn't decide to; her body decided, crossing the distance and sitting down beside the girl, awkward as a fence post, one hand hovering and then landing, feather-light, on a ragged shoulder.

"I'm bad at secrets too," she said. "Thirty years, and I never got good at it. I just got good at being alone. And I'm going to teach you the first thing I know, and then we're going to argue about whether it's the right thing, because honestly I don't know anymore."

Pip hiccupped. Looked up. "What's the first thing?"

"The beasts aren't witnesses," Tamsin said. "Everyone else in the world will look at you and see a crime waiting to happen. The crown, the neighbors, the Bells. But the beasts—" her voice caught, and she pushed through it, "—the beasts aren't witnesses, Pip. They're family. The fox isn't evidence against you. He's the only witness *for* you. Learn the difference and you'll survive. Forget it and you'll end up hiding from the only ones who love you, and that's a lonelier death than the Bell."

The fox made a small sound. When Tamsin glanced over, it had put its chin on Pip's knee and was looking at her with an expression she could only describe as reluctant approval—the same expression, she realized, that she could currently feel on her own face.

Above them, muffled by a hundred feet of slate, the hawk screamed once.

Tamsin went still. She reached out along the thread of it, the old thread, and what came back chilled her worse than the cave cold.

Riders. Four of them, maybe five, on the shepherd's road below the ravine. Crown colors. And with them, wrapped in canvas on a pack frame, something that hummed.

The Bell. Moving. Hunting.

"It found us," Pip whispered. She'd felt it too—of course she'd felt it; she was a lantern in a cellar. Her small hand found Tamsin's sleeve and gripped it. "Didn't it. It found me."

Tamsin looked at the child. At the fox. At Wren, whose face had gone the color of the slate around them. At the dark tunnel leading deeper into the mountain, and the long road beyond it, and the name Wren had given her three nights ago by firelight—*the Verdant Door*—which had sounded, then, like a rumor. Like a fairy tale. Now it sounded like the only word in the language.

"No," Tamsin said, and was surprised to find her voice steady. "It found a signal. Signals can move."

She stood, and rolled her bitten sleeve down, and began counting their supplies in her head.

"We leave tonight."
