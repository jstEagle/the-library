## 7. Hollow Bell Rising

The rockslide had gone exactly as planned. That was the problem with plans, Wren Alcott thought as he lay face-down in the scree with a boot on his neck: they worked for everyone.

He'd spent three days watching the hunting party work its way up the Shepherd's Road—eleven soldiers, two mule-drawn cages, and a wagon carrying something under oilcloth that made every bird within a half-mile go silent. He'd watched them from ridgelines he'd drawn himself, back when drawing maps for the crown had still felt like a neutral trade. He knew their route better than they did. He'd known which slope above the Narrows was rotten shale held together by nothing but habit.

Tamsin Thornbury had known too. She'd stood beside him at dawn, arms crossed, and said only, "Bring it down when the wagons are in the throat of it. Not before." Then she'd walked off to do whatever she did with her animals, and Wren had climbed up alone into the cold and pried loose the one stone that mattered.

The slide had taken the rear wagon, two mules, and split the column in half. In the chaos, Tamsin was supposed to slip past with Pip and the fox and be three valleys gone before anyone regrouped.

Instead, Wren had run straight down into the dust cloud and directly into the arms of the six soldiers who hadn't been buried.

"Cartographer," said a voice above him. Not angry. Almost amused. "Wren Alcott. The crown's been looking for you since spring."

The boot lifted from his neck. A hand took his collar instead and hauled him upright so fast his vision swam. When it cleared, he was looking at a woman in a captain's coat, gray-streaked hair bound back tight, a scar running from her left ear down into her collar like a seam someone had started sewing and abandoned.

She was not what he'd expected. He'd expected Vane to be a butcher. Everyone said Serah Vane was a butcher.

What he saw instead was tiredness. Deep, structural tiredness, the kind that lives in a face the way load-bearing walls live in a house.

"You're the one who falsified the border surveys," she said. "Forty miles of refugee routes, erased. Clever work. I've studied your maps." She turned a sheet of paper in her gloved fingers—his own work, the false survey of the Kestrel Gap, the one that had moved a mountain pass onto paper where no pass existed. "You draw lies beautifully."

"I draw accurately," Wren said. "I just choose which truths to include."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. Recognition, maybe—the look one professional gives another across an unbridgeable difference of employer.

"Tie him," she said. "Loosely. He'll behave." She looked at Wren as she said it, and he understood it wasn't a threat. It was a test of whether he'd make her wrong.

* * *

They marched him up the Shepherd's Road behind the surviving wagon, hands bound in front, which told him either Vane was careless or confident, and he suspected neither. The soldiers were quiet in the way of men who'd seen the thing under the oilcloth uncovered once and preferred not to think about it. One of them kept glancing at the trees. Another crossed himself whenever a crow called.

The Hollow Bell rode in the wagon under its canvas, and even covered, Wren could feel it. Not hear it—feel it, the way you feel a storm building behind a ridge. A pressure in the teeth. A wrongness in the air like the moment before lightning.

At midday they halted in a hollow below a burned-out shepherd's hut, and Vane sat across a small fire from him while her men saw to the mules.

"You ran toward the rockslide," she said. "Everyone else runs away from them. You came down into the dust on purpose. Why?"

"To steal your bell," Wren said. "Obviously."

"With your hands tied and no horse?" She shook her head slowly. "No. You came down because you wanted to see if we survived. You wanted to know how many of us there were, and whether the Bell was damaged, and whether we'd turn north or south after it." She fed a stick to the fire. "That's cartographer's thinking. Information first. Always information first."

"And what does a beast-hunter think first?" Wren asked. It came out more bitter than he intended.

Vane didn't flinch. "Where the speakers are," she said simply. "Always where the speakers are. Because every hour I don't find them is an hour they're walking toward something worse than me."

"That's a strange thing for a hunter to say."

"It's a strange job." She studied him across the fire, and Wren had the uncomfortable sense of being surveyed himself, contour lines read off his face. "You've heard stories about me, I imagine. That I burn villages. That I enjoy it."

"The stories mostly agree you enjoy it."

"The stories are written by people who've never had to do it." She reached up and, without ceremony, pulled open the collar of her shirt.

The scar was there too—on her throat, just below the jaw. But it wasn't a scar. It was a brand. A circle bisected by a line, seared into the skin years ago, healed thick and pale. The mark of a surrendered speaker. Wren had seen the symbol before, stamped on crown documents he'd been ordered to file and had never dared ask about.

"They don't tell you that part either," Vane said, watching him look. "That the Edict has a mercy clause. A speaker can surrender the gift. There's a rite. It hurts—it hurt worse than anything I've ever felt—but afterward you're clean. Silent. Free." She closed her collar. "I was eleven years old when my mother made that choice for me. Our farm dog answered the first Bell ever rung in our valley, and it led the king's men straight to our door, and my mother knelt in the yard and gave up the only thing she loved more than us so that we wouldn't hang. She never spoke another word to anything but people for the rest of her life. She died quiet. She died free." 

The fire cracked. Somewhere upslope, a crow called again, and one of the soldiers flinched.

"So when they handed me this commission," Vane went on, "I didn't take it because I hate speakers. I took it because I'm the only commander in this army who knows what the Bell actually does—and what it doesn't."

"And what's that?" Wren asked, though his mouth had gone dry.

"Everyone thinks it detects speakers. It doesn't. It calls beasts, and beasts answer, and frightened people watch which animals come running and call those animals' owners traitors." Her voice stayed level, but Wren heard the iron underneath it. "A lie like that gets innocent families killed. So here is what I intend, cartographer, and you may carry it back to whatever smuggler's guide you've been leading around my passes: I am going to ring the great Bell at the mountain's heart, once, properly, with the crown's full authority—and then I am going to use it. Not to find speakers. To command the beasts. Every animal in Thornfell answering to the crown means no mob ever burns a farmhouse because a dog barked at the wrong moment. No child is ever dragged out of bed because her goat betrayed her. Control isn't cruelty. Control is the cage that keeps the mob outside."

Wren stared at her. He had prepared arguments on the march—arguments about tyranny, about the Edict, about the burning villages he'd heard rumor of. None of them fit. You couldn't argue with a woman who was genuinely trying to build a smaller horror than the one coming.

"You'd enslave every creature in the mountains," he said finally.

"I'd leash them," she corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference is who else holds the knife. Right now every farmer with a torch thinks he's doing the crown's work. Give the crown the beasts outright, and the mobs have nothing left to fear, and nothing left to hunt." She leaned forward. "I watched my mother kneel in the dirt, Alcott. I have spent thirty years making sure no other child watches that. If the price is that the wolves stop singing at night, I will pay it, and I will sleep."

"And the speakers who won't surrender? The ones like—" He stopped himself.

"Like the guide," Vane said, and there it was: the flicker of the professional's recognition again, colder now. "Yes. We know about her. The blind wolf that follows her trails. The hawk with the old wound. The girl." Something moved in her face at the word *girl*—a hairline crack, quickly plastered. "Pip Marsh. Eight years old. Discovered in Lowmill when her family's cow spoke her name to a neighbor. Her parents surrendered rather than watch her be branded. They're in custody. Cooperating fully." She paused. "Which is why I know she's alive, and why I know someone is moving her through my mountains, and why I will find her before winter. Not to hurt her, Alcott. To ring the Bell, and end this, and put her somewhere safe where nobody will ever ask her to speak again."

*Safe*, Wren thought. *Safe as a bird in a cage. Safe as your mother, kneeling in the yard.*

"You call it protection," he said. "It sounds like erasure with better manners."

"It sounds like survival," Vane said. "You've never had anything taken from you except your commission, and you earned that. Come talk to me after you've watched a brand heal on someone you love."

* * *

They kept him overnight in the hollow, bound to a wagon wheel, and it was there, in the dark, that Wren learned the last piece—the piece that changed everything.

He heard it first as weather. A pressure rolling down the valley, the same tooth-ache wrongness he'd felt all day, but stronger now, closer. Then the soldiers were on their feet, and the oilcloth came off the Bell, and Wren saw it for the first time: a bronze bell the size of a man's torso, hanging from a frame of black iron, and it was *wrong*—its surface crawled faintly, like water remembering it used to be ice, and inside its mouth there was no clapper. Only a darkness that breathed.

Vane stood before it with her hand flat against the bronze, eyes closed, and Wren realized with a lurch that she was listening to it. Communing with it. The way—

The way Tamsin listened to beasts.

The Bell sang one note. Softly. Barely a sound at all, more a permission than a noise.

And the night answered.

From the treeline above the hollow, eyes opened. Dozens of them. Foxes, deer, a badger, owls dropping soundless from the pines, a she-bear ambling down with her cubs trailing behind her—all of them walking with the smooth, unhurried gait of sleepwalkers, all of them turning their faces toward the Bell with expressions of perfect, terrible peace.

The soldiers pressed back against the wagons. Even Vane's veterans feared this.

But Vane herself stepped forward among the gathered animals, unhurried, unafraid, and raised her hand, and the bear stopped three feet from her and sat down like a hound at heel.

"Do you see?" she said quietly—not to the soldiers, not entirely to Wren either, but to the night itself. "No blood. No fire. No hunting. They come, and they rest, and at dawn they go back to their dens, and no one dies tonight." She looked over her shoulder at Wren, bound to the wheel, and her expression was almost gentle. "This is what I'm offering your mountains, cartographer. Not slaughter. Order. Ask yourself what your smuggler's guide is offering them. Ask yourself what happens to every beast in Thornfell the day the crown decides negotiation is over—and it will decide that, soon, because commanders gentler and stupider than me are already asking for the great Bell."

The animals melted back into the dark when she lowered her hand. The Bell went silent. The pressure lifted, leaving Wren's ears ringing and his heart hammering against his ribs.

He should have been afraid. He was afraid. But underneath the fear, the cartographer in him was working, cold and fast, the way it always worked when the map tore.

Because he'd seen the animals' faces. And he'd spent enough time near Tamsin Thornbury to know the difference between a beast that obeys and a beast that chooses—and those creatures in the firelight had not chosen anything. Their eyes had been empty. Lamps with the flame blown out.

Vane thought she was building a sanctuary. Wren had seen enough of the crown's sanctuaries to know what they became.

Near midnight, when the sentries' fire had burned low, a shadow crossed the edge of the hollow—a shape low to the ground, gray-muzzled, moving with the careful economy of an old animal that had learned every kind of danger. The blind wolf. It paused at the rim of the light, nostrils working, reading the camp the way Wren read terrain.

Then its head turned, and found Wren, and held there.

It couldn't see him. It didn't need to. And in the morning, when Vane's column broke camp and marched him north with them toward Thornhold, Wren understood that the wolf had gotten what it came for—and that somewhere in the high country, Tamsin Thornbury now knew exactly where he was, and exactly what the Bell could do.

Which meant she would come for him.

Which meant, he realized with a sinking, vertiginous clarity, that she would walk straight into the hands of the one commander in Thornfell who understood speakers best—because she had been one, and had cut it out of herself, and had never once looked back.

"Comfortable, cartographer?" Vane asked, falling into step beside him on the road.

"Delighted," Wren said. And began, very quietly, to plan.
