## 8. The Wyrmling's Bargain

The pass closed at dawn.

Tamsin knew it before she saw it—the way the birds went quiet, the way the wind changed its mind mid-gust and came back down the valley smelling of hot stone and blood. She stopped on the shepherd's road where it narrowed between two shoulders of granite, and held up a hand, and Pip walked into her back like a small warm door slamming.

"What is it?" Wren whispered. He'd learned to whisper in three days on the mountain, which was faster than most courtiers.

"Something's hurt," Tamsin said. "Something big."

The wyrmling lay across the pass like a fallen roof beam.

It was smaller than the stories made them—stories being mostly lies told by men who'd never seen one and needed an excuse for their fear—but it was still the length of a hay wagon, scaled in slate and rust, wings folded tight against its flanks like a beggar folding a coat against winter. One wing was wrong. The membrane had been torn and badly healed, or not healed at all, and along the leading edge ran a stripe of raw flesh that smoked faintly in the cold morning air.

Burned. Not by fire. By something colder than fire.

Tamsin had seen that mark once before, on a hawk her father had kept caged—a ring of dead tissue, precise as a brand. The Hollow Bell left wounds like that on anything with ears that mattered.

The wyrmling's head came up.

Its eyes were old. That was the thing no story ever got right: dragons were not terrible because they were fierce, they were terrible because they were *old*, and this one had eyes like tarnished coins, like riverbed stones, like every winter Tamsin had ever walked through stacked one atop another. It looked at her the way a magistrate looks at a ledger.

"You," it said.

Its voice was broken speech, words worn smooth and misshapen like pebbles tumbled in a stream. Human words, but few of them, each one costing it visible effort—its throat was built for other sounds entirely.

"You are Thorn-blood," it said. "Small Thorn-blood. Grand-Thorn-blood's get."

Pip made a sound behind her, half wonder, half terror, and buried her face in Tamsin's coat. The fox pressed close against the girl's legs, hackles up, and said nothing—which from a speaking fox meant a great deal.

"I am," Tamsin said. She kept her voice level. Her heart was doing something undignified. "My grandmother was Maren Thornbury."

"Maren." The wyrmling tasted the name. Its nostrils flared, and the smoke that curled out was gray and thin. "Maren owed."

*Here it comes*, Tamsin thought. Every smuggler's child knew debts crossed generations; half the mountain's feuds were grandfathers' quarrels wearing new coats. But she'd never heard of her grandmother owing a dragon.

"She paid what she promised," Tamsin said carefully. "Whatever it was."

The wyrmling laughed. There was no better word for the sound, though it was closer to rockfall than laughter, and it made Wren take an involuntary step backward and put his hand on Pip's shoulder as if he could shield a child from geology.

"Maren paid," the wyrmling agreed. "Maren paid *once*. Debts of passage do not pay once. Debts of passage run down the blood." It shifted, and even that small movement made the pass tremble, made grit hiss down the rockface, made the torn wing shudder—and when it shuddered, the wyrmling made a sound it clearly did not want to make, high and thin, and Tamsin's chest clenched around it like a fist.

It was in pain. Terrible, grinding, endless pain, the kind that wore patience down to the quick.

"The Bell," she said. "It rang near you."

The wyrmling's eyes fixed on her. "The Bell rang. I flew to make it quiet. Flying things cannot stop their ears." A pause. Smoke. "It called me *down*. Three days falling. Three days fighting my own wings." Its head lowered until those coin-old eyes were level with hers, close enough that she could see herself reflected twice, small and bent. "I am done flying. So now I sit in your road, small Thorn-blood. And we bargain."

"We could go around," Wren murmured at her shoulder. "There's a route through the scree fields—"

"There isn't," Tamsin said without turning. "Not with Pip. Not in this season. This pass is the way, and you know it, because you drew the map." She felt him go still beside her. Yes. He'd known. They'd all known since the scree fields began to narrow, and none of them had said it aloud: sooner or later, the mountain would present its bill.

She stepped forward.

The fox growled low. Pip grabbed at her coat and Tamsin gently unwound the small fingers, one by one, and hated herself for how much steadier her hands were than her heart.

"What do you want?" she asked the wyrmling.

"Passage," it said simply. "You walk to the Door. I smell it on you—all three of you, but you most. You smell of the Door the way bread smells of the oven before it's lit." Its tongue flickered out, black and forked, tasting the air over her head. "I want to stand before it. Once. Before I die in this pass like a stuck pig while the Bell-thing hunts me by my own heartbeat."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange—" The wyrmling's ruined wing twitched. "In exchange, truth. Grand-Maren bought passage with a truth. I give you the same coin, same weight. Fair trade. Old trade."

Tamsin's mouth had gone dry. "What truth?"

The wyrmling smiled. Dragons should not smile. Everything about the expression was wrong—the jaw hinging too far, the teeth too many, the ancient eyes glittering with something that was almost kindness and therefore worse than cruelty.

"The Door's price," it said. "You think you know it. Everyone thinks they know it. 'A secret,' the songs say. 'Tell the Door your deepest secret and walk free.'" It shook its great head slowly, and loose scales clicked together like coins in a miser's purse. "Songs lie. Songs are what people tell themselves so they can sleep after they've sent someone else through."

Wren had come forward despite himself. Tamsin heard his boots on the gravel, heard his breath going shallow. "Then what is it?" he demanded. His voice cracked on the second word. He was thinking of his maps, Tamsin realized. Of the routes he'd falsified, the refugees he'd hidden in blank spaces, the lie that had cost him his name and nearly his neck. He was thinking: *I have already given my secret away. What if it wasn't enough?*

"The Door does not want your secret," the wyrmling said. "Secrets are cheap. Secrets can be told to a stranger in a tavern and forgotten by morning." It drew breath, and the whole pass filled with the smell of hot stone. "The Door wants the *self*. The part you hide so deep you've stopped calling it hiding. The thing you would be, if no one was watching and you finally stopped pretending otherwise. You carry it to the Door, and you set it down, and you do not pick it up again. And what walks out the far side—" the coin-eyes glittered, "—is not quite what walked in."

Silence, except the wind and the small crackle of the burned wing.

"That's not surrender," Wren said hoarsely. "That's—"

"A price," said the wyrmling. "Prices are never fair. That is what makes them prices."

Tamsin stood very still.

She thought about her grandmother's ledger, sewn into the lining of her pack these eleven years, the pages of coded routes and toll-marks she'd never fully deciphered. She thought about the last entry, the one in different ink, different hand: *Paid in full. Do not come back.* She'd always read it as a smuggler's sign-off. Now it sounded like a warning nailed to a door.

And beneath that, quieter, in the cellar of herself where she kept the things she didn't look at: a small voice asking, *what would I set down? What have I been carrying so long I've mistaken it for my spine?*

She didn't let the voice finish. Some doors you don't open standing in front of a dragon.

"Passage," she said instead, briskly, guide's voice, ledger voice. "You want us to lead you to the Door. Fine. But look at yourself—you'll never fit the high route, and the low route floods with meltwater by noon. We go the goat tracks above the treeline, single file, slow. You keep to the rear and you keep your fire in your throat unless I say otherwise. Agreed?"

The wyrmling regarded her for a long moment. Then, incredibly, it inclined its head—an inch, no more, but from a dragon an inch might as well be a bow.

"Grand-Maren talked like that," it said. "Like the mountain belonged to her and everything on it was slightly late for an appointment." Smoke curled from its nostrils, and Tamsin could have sworn the smoke smelled almost like amusement. "Yes, small Thorn-blood. Agreed."

"It has a name," Pip said suddenly.

Everyone turned. The girl had crept forward during the bargaining, fox at her heel, and was looking up at the wyrmling with the frank, unafraid stare that was either Pip's greatest gift or the thing that would eventually get her killed.

"It has a name," Pip repeated, "and it's sad about it. It hasn't said it out loud in a hundred years because there wasn't anyone to say it to." She tilted her head, listening to something Tamsin couldn't hear—or could, maybe, in the place where her own gift lived, the place she kept locked. "It's lonely," Pip said, matter-of-fact as weather. "It's been lonely longer than the kingdom's been a kingdom."

"Pip," Tamsin started—

But the wyrmling made a sound. Not rockfall this time. Something smaller, rougher, dragged up from wherever it kept the things it never spent.

"Cinderveil," it said. Quietly. Like setting down something heavy. "Once. When the valleys were green and the two-legs brought offerings instead of Bells." The coin-eyes blinked, slow. "Cinderveil," it said again, as if checking whether the shape of it still fit.

Pip smiled up at it, gap-toothed and certain. "That's a good name."

The wyrmling—Cinderveil—looked at the child for a long time. Then it looked at Tamsin, and there was something new in the old eyes, something that made her deeply uneasy, because it looked almost exactly like hope.

"Your grand-maren traded truths," it rumbled. "This one trades names. Watch her, small Thorn-blood. Children who hear what others hide grow up to open doors that should stay shut." A pause. "Or doors that have been waiting."

They moved within the hour.

It was slow going, as Tamsin had known it would be—the wyrmling's bulk on the goat tracks was a study in patience, its taloned feet placed with surprising delicacy among the stones, its ruined wing held close whenever the path narrowed. But it knew the mountain. That was the strange gift inside the ordeal: Cinderveil had flown these ridges before the crown had names for them, and twice it corrected Tamsin's route with a grunt and a jerk of its scarred head, steering them away from cornices that looked solid and weren't.

Wren walked beside Tamsin through the middle stretch, map case clutched to his chest like a breastplate.

"The price," he said quietly, when Pip had ranged ahead with the fox and the dragon's footsteps were a steady grind behind them. "What it said. Is it true?"

"Dragons don't lie," Tamsin said. "They just don't tell you everything."

"But the Door—it really takes something? Not a confession. A piece of you."

"So it says."

He was silent for twenty paces. Thirty. Then: "I gave up everything already, Tamsin. My name, my post, my—there's not much left to set down. If the Door looks at me and finds an empty table—" He stopped. Laughed, without any humor in it. "Listen to me. Worried the escape hatch won't accept me."

"Maybe that's the trick of it," Tamsin said. "Maybe the Door only opens for people who still have something left to lose."

"Is that wisdom or fear talking?"

"With me, those share a bed."

Ahead, Pip had stopped on a shelf of stone and was pointing back the way they'd come. Tamsin followed the line of her arm to the southern horizon, where the sky above the low country sat wrong—not clouded, not smoky, but *stained*, a bruise-colored haze pressing down on the valleys like a lid.

And then, faint as a memory, faint as a bell heard through six feet of earth and remembered rather than heard at all, came the sound.

Not loud. It didn't need to be. The Hollow Bell carried the way grief carries—not through air but through everything, stone and blood and bone. On the ridge above them, every bird went up at once. In the scree below, something small and many-footed fled in a single rippling wave. And behind her, Cinderveil stopped walking altogether, and the sound the wyrmling made then was not pain and not rage but both braided into one long note, and Tamsin understood:

The Bell was moving into the high passes. The Bell was climbing.

"They're not chasing us anymore," she said. The words came out very calm, which was how she knew they were the worst words she'd ever said. "They're herding us. Up. Toward the Door." She turned to Wren, and saw her own dread reflected in his face. "Because someone in a crown uniform has figured out that if every speaker in Thornfell runs to the same exit—"

"—you don't need to hunt them," Wren finished. "You just need to wait at the door."

Behind them, Cinderveil spread its torn wing as wide as it would go, shadowing them all, and spoke in its broken, ancient voice:

"Then walk faster, small Thorn-blood. And decide, before the top—what you will set down. Because the Door does not bargain twice."

* * *

They climbed through the afternoon with the stain on the horizon growing darker, and Tamsin caught herself, more than once, with her hand pressed flat against the ledger through the canvas of her pack—her grandmother's last entry running through her head like a pulse.

*Paid in full. Do not come back.*

She was starting to understand that her grandmother hadn't been signing off.

She'd been leaving instructions.
