## 9. Ash on the Shepherd's Road

Pip knew the village was dead before she saw it, because the fox stopped talking.

Sorrel had talked the whole way down from the Hollow—talked about everything, really, in his quick rusted-hinge voice: about the taste of voles versus mice (voles, obviously, they were sweeter), about how Wren's boots smelled like ink and fear, about whether clouds had bones. He talked so much that Pip mostly didn't have to talk at all, which was good, because when Pip talked too long her words came out wrong-shaped, with other words tucked inside them like seeds, words that weren't hers, words that belonged to crows and goats and once, terribly, to a mule who had repeated what she said back to a drover in a voice like gravel, and that was why she lived in a cave now.

But on the shepherd's road, going down out of the high country with Tamsin walking ahead and Wren limping behind, Sorrel went quiet. He pressed against Pip's leg, all his weight, which he never did unless he wanted something or was scared, and he said only one thing:

*Don't smell it. Don't look at it. Keep walking.*

Pip looked anyway.

The village sat in the crook of the valley like something dropped there and broken. She'd seen villages before—she'd lived in one until the mule—and this one had been small, maybe twenty houses, stone-walled, with sheep pens stitched up the hillside above it. Except the houses weren't houses anymore. They were black shapes, hollow ones, with their roofs fallen in like cakes somebody had punched. The pens were empty. The well had a bucket lying beside it, spilled.

And there were animals everywhere.

That was the part Pip couldn't stop looking at. Dogs, three of them, sitting in the ash of what had probably been the square. A flock of geese standing in a row along a fence line, not moving. A cat on a doorstep, washing its paw, slow and careful, over and over, though there was nothing left of the house behind it to be clean for.

They weren't dead. That was the worst thing. They were just *sitting there*, all facing the same direction, toward the road, toward where men would have come from, waiting.

"Sorrel," Pip whispered. "Why are they doing that?"

The fox didn't answer. His ears were flat as river stones.

Tamsin had gone still ahead of them, one hand up—the stop sign, the one that meant shut your mouth and breathe through your nose. She stood in the middle of the road for a long moment, and Pip watched her face do something complicated, the way it did when she was listening to things nobody else could hear. Then her shoulders dropped, just a little, and she walked forward.

"Stay close," she said. "Touch nothing. Talk to nothing."

"I didn't—" Pip started.

"Not you." Tamsin glanced back, and her eyes were the gray of slate under rain. "Them. The ones here. If anything here speaks to you, you don't answer. Not even inside your head. Some doors, once you open them, don't close."

Wren caught up, breathing hard, his bandaged side making him walk crooked. He took one look at the village and put his hand over his mouth. "Gods," he said through his fingers. "This is Harrow's Bend. I mapped it. Two years ago. There was a cheese market."

"There's no market now," Tamsin said.

She walked into the ash and Pip followed because following Tamsin was the safest place in any world, even a world that smelled like this—burnt thatch and burnt wool underneath it, and something else, something sweetish and rotten that Pip decided very firmly not to think about.

Up close, the dogs weren't sitting anymore. They were lying down now, flat, chins on their paws, but still facing the road. As Pip passed the nearest one, an old brown bitch with a grizzled muzzle, the dog's eyes rolled up to follow her.

And the dog spoke.

Not out loud. It came the way Sorrel's words came, straight into the space behind Pip's eyes, except this voice was slow and heavy and broken, like furniture dragged across a floor:

*We told them where she slept.*

Pip stopped walking. Her whole body went cold, colder than the cave in winter.

*The girl. The little one who fed us scraps. We told the men in red where she slept, because the Bell rang and rang and rang and we could not make our legs go the other way. We told them, and they took her, and then they burned the house so we would remember what telling costs. And now we sit here because we cannot leave, because leaving would mean leaving the place where she is not.*

Pip's eyes were burning. "I'm sorry," she whispered, and then clapped both hands over her mouth, because Tamsin had said don't answer, don't answer—

But the dog had already heard. Its tail moved, once, a tiny sweep through the ash, and its voice came again, softer, almost grateful:

*You answered. Nobody has answered us since. Thank you, small speaker. Go quickly. The Bell comes back this way. It always comes back.*

Pip ran. She didn't decide to run; her legs just did it, and she caught up to Tamsin and grabbed her coat and held on, and Tamsin let her hold on, which she almost never allowed, and that told Pip more than anything how bad it was.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow under a leaning rock, off the road, with no fire because Tamsin said fire was a shout in the dark. Wren spread his maps and pretended to study them, but Pip noticed he kept looking up at her, quick little glances, like she might break.

She felt like she might break.

She sat with her knees pulled up and Sorrel curled in her lap, and finally she said it, because it was pushing against her teeth and if she didn't say it she thought it might come out sideways into some animal's head by accident, and she couldn't bear for anyone else to carry it.

"The dog talked to me. In the village. I wasn't supposed to listen but it talked anyway." Her voice came out small. "It said they told the men where the girl slept. Their own girl. The one who fed them. Because the Bell rang and they couldn't stop themselves." She swallowed. "Did the men burn the girl too?"

Nobody answered fast enough, and the slowness was the answer.

"Tamsin. Did they?"

Tamsin was sharpening her knife, the way she did when her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking. She didn't look up. "The Edict doesn't kill speakers, Pip. It takes them. To Thornhold. For the crown's use." A pause, the knife whispering against the stone. "Sometimes taking is worse than burning. Sometimes it isn't. I don't know which it was for that girl."

"But the animals *told on her.*" Pip's voice cracked in the middle. "Their own person. How? If you love someone, how do you tell?"

"Because the Bell doesn't ask them." Now Tamsin looked up, and her face in the dark was hard to read, all shadow and edge. "It reaches into them and moves their mouths. Like a hand in a puppet. They watched themselves do it. They'll remember doing it for the rest of their lives. That's not betrayal, Pip. That's theft."

"It felt like betrayal," Pip said. "To me it did. And to them."

"Yes." Tamsin set the knife down. "It did. That's the cruelest part. The crown figured out a long time ago that you don't need to catch a speaker. You just need her own dog to point at her bed. Every beast in Thornfell is a witness now, every cow and crow and barn cat, and every speaker knows it, so every speaker stops trusting the only friends they ever had." She shook her head slowly. "That's what the Bell really does. It doesn't find us. It makes us alone."

Pip looked down at Sorrel. Sorrel looked up at her, one ear cocked, and his voice came warm and certain into her head: *I would bite the Bell. I would bite the man holding it. I would bite them both until my teeth broke, and then I would bite them with my gums.*

Despite everything, something hiccupped out of Pip that was almost a laugh. "He says he'd bite the Bell."

"He would, too," Tamsin said. "He's an idiot. A loyal idiot." And then she did something she hadn't done before: she moved over next to Pip, close enough that their shoulders touched, and she was quiet for a while, and when she spoke again her voice had changed, gone low and even, the voice she used for important things.

"Pip. I need to teach you the first rule. I should have taught it sooner and I'm sorry I didn't. Listen to me now, because this is the rule that keeps speakers alive, and it's not the rule you think."

Pip nodded. "Don't talk where people can hear."

"No. Everyone teaches that one, and it's wrong, or half wrong. Hiding is a tool, not a life. Here's the real rule." Tamsin held up one finger. "*The beasts are not witnesses. They are family.*"

Pip frowned. "But the dog—"

"The dog was stolen, not turned. There's a difference, and your life depends on knowing it. The day you start looking at an animal and seeing a thing that might betray you—that's the day the crown wins without ringing a single bell. Because then you're alone, and alone is exactly what they want us. Do you understand? Fear will tell you every eye in the forest is reporting to Thornhold. Fear lies. The beasts are on our side of the wall, Pip. All of them. Even the ones the Bell has used. *Especially* those."

"How do you know?" Pip asked. "How do you know they're all on our side?"

Tamsin was quiet a moment. Out in the dark, somewhere far off, an owl called, one long low note, and Pip saw Tamsin tilt her head toward it, listening, and something in her face eased.

"Because I've spent my whole life asking them," she said, "and not one of them—not one—has ever given me up. Not for gold, not for threats, not for a Bell. Men have sold me. Beasts never have." She looked at Pip straight on. "So here's what family means, the first law of it: you don't hide from your family. You hide *with* them. You warn them, and they warn you. You feed them, and they feed you. When the Bell comes, you don't run from the animals—you run *to* them, because a hundred throats lying at once can drown out one throat telling the truth. That's how we survive. Together, and loud, and lying our hearts out."

"Lying?" Pip blinked. "Animals can lie?"

"Sorrel, lie to me," Tamsin said.

Sorrel lifted his head and said, in perfect bored innocence, *There is no fox here. This is a rock. Rocks cannot speak.*

"See? Terrible at it. But he tries." Tamsin almost smiled, and Pip felt something loosen in her chest, just a little, the first loosening since the dog's voice. "The point is, Pip: the crown wants you to believe every animal is a spy, so that you'll silence yourself, cut off your own gift, live half a life in a hole in the ground. I did that for thirty years. I'm telling you it's not living. It's just a slower way of dying."

Pip looked at her hands. "In the cave," she said quietly, "before you found me, I was starting to try to stop. Talking, I mean. I thought if I never talked to them again, maybe I'd be safe. Maybe I could be normal. Sorrel got scared. He thought I was dying." Her throat went tight. "Maybe I was."

"You were," Tamsin said simply. "Cutting the gift off is like cutting off a hand and wondering why you keep dropping things." She reached over and tapped Pip's chest, right over the heart, one light knock. "It lives here. You can starve it but you can't dig it out. So don't starve it. Feed it. Feed it the right way. That's what I'm teaching you, and someday, if we get through this, you'll teach someone else."

"Like you're teaching me?"

"Exactly like that. Somebody taught me once, too." Something crossed Tamsin's face then, old and private, and Pip knew better than to ask. Grandmother-shaped, that look. Everything about Tamsin had grandmother-shapes hidden in it.

From across the camp, Wren cleared his throat. "Forgive me," he said, "but I've been listening, and as a professional liar I feel obligated to point out a flaw in the plan. A hundred animals lying at once works beautifully—until the Bell rings. Then none of them are lying. They're all pointing. You can't out-lie a puppet master."

"You can't," Tamsin agreed. "Which is why the plan was never to outrun the Bell forever." She looked east, toward the black bulk of the mountains, toward something Pip couldn't see. "There's a Door, Wren. You know it. That's where we're going. And past it—"

"Past it, if it exists, is a land with no crown in it," Wren finished. "If it exists."

"It exists." Tamsin said it flat and sure, the way she said true things. "My grandmother wrote about it. My grandmother *kept* it." She stood, brushing ash-colored dust off her knees. "Sleep now. We move at first light, wide around Harrow's Bend. Pip—wake me if anything speaks in the night. Anything at all. Family watches family's sleep."

Pip lay down with Sorrel curled against her stomach, his heartbeat quick and small against hers, and she thought about the dog in the ash, facing the road, waiting for a girl who wasn't coming home. She thought about how the dog had said *thank you* just because Pip had answered. Just because someone had spoken back.

Before she closed her eyes, she sent one thread of thought out into the dark, careful and quiet, the way Tamsin had shown her—outward, gentle, to everything with ears in the night:

*I'm here. I'm with you. You're not witnesses. You're family.*

And the dark answered her—a bat's squeak, a badger's grumble, the owl's long note again, closer now, watchful—dozens of small voices saying in their wordless way, *we hear you, we hear you, we hear you,* and Pip fell asleep inside that sound like falling into a warm coat.

She woke once, deep in the night, because Sorrel had gone rigid against her.

*Lights,* he breathed. *On the road. Below us. Lanterns, many, and something big being carried between poles, wrapped in canvas, and the men around it are singing. Low. Like a funeral.*

Pip crawled to the lip of the hollow. Far below, strung out along the shepherd's road like glowing beads, the lanterns swayed. And beneath the men's footsteps, faint but unmistakable, rising up through the ground itself into the soles of her feet, came a sound she felt more than heard—a slow, patient, bronze hum.

The Bell, coming back up the valley. Coming back to Harrow's Bend, to check on its dogs.

Tamsin was already awake beside her, already watching, and in the lantern-light from below Pip saw her face, and it wasn't afraid.

It was furious.
