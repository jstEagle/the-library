## 10. The Pass That No Longer Exists

Wren Alcott had spent eleven years learning how to lie with ink.

That was the trade, in the end. Cartography looked like honesty—here is the world, measured, gridded, true—but every map the Crown ever issued was an argument. A mountain range drawn too steep to cross was a border no one would test. A river drawn wide was a war deferred. A pass left off a map entirely was a door that did not exist, and Wren had erased more doors than he could count before they put him in irons for it.

He had told himself, once, that erasure was mercy. That leaving the Verdant Door off the royal charts kept it safe from soldiers and surveyors and men like him. He had believed it right up until the night the Edict of the Quiet Tongue was read aloud in the palace courtyard, and he understood that a thing hidden from the Crown belonged to whoever found it next—and that the Crown had a way of finding everything eventually.

Now he sat on a flat stone above a fire that Tamsin had built small and screened, watching her feed strips of dried venison to a fox that was not a pet, while a child slept against the fox's flank as though the world were a kind place, and he prepared to confess to the worst lie of his career.

"You're doing the face," Tamsin said without looking up.

"What face?"

"The one where you're rehearsing something and hoping I won't notice." She tore another strip from the meat. "I guide smugglers, Wren. I know what rehearsal looks like."

The wyrmling lay coiled at the edge of the light, its wounded wing bound in moss and linen, one amber eye open and fixed on him with the patience of a creature that had already lived through whatever came next. It had told them the Door's price. It had not told them where the Door was. That, apparently, was his part of the bargain—the debt he'd offered in her cabin two nights ago, when he'd said *I know where the Verdant Door is* and watched hope move across her face like weather.

He owed her the truth now. All of it.

"The pass doesn't exist," he said.

Tamsin's hand stopped halfway to the fox's ear. "Which pass?"

"Any of them." He pulled his satchel into his lap and drew out the rolled oilskin he had guarded through prison, through the rockslide, through three days of walking with ribs that clicked when he breathed. "Here. Look."

She wiped her hands on her trousers and took it. Unrolled it by firelight: the western reaches of Thornfell, drawn in his own careful hand, the ranges rendered in the fine stippled shading the Royal Cartographic Office required, the rivers in blue-black ink, the smuggler routes he'd falsified marked in a cipher only he could read.

"This is your master chart," she said. "The one you went to prison over."

"It's the one I went to prison *for*." He leaned forward and touched the blank space west of the Grey Teeth, where the mountains rose into a wall of nothing—no passes, no valleys, just clean white paper and the cartographer's conventional hatching that meant *impassable*. "This region. The Door is here."

She stared at the emptiness. "There's nothing here."

"There's everything here. That's the point." His voice came out flatter than he intended. "Tamsin, I didn't falsify the refugee routes out of ignorance. I falsified them because I knew exactly what I was hiding. And the biggest thing I ever hid wasn't a route. It was a whole valley system. A pass that runs through the heart of the Grey Teeth—a pass so old the first surveyors called it the Shepherd's Throat, because the pre-Edict shepherds drove their flocks through it to summer pasture on the far side. My grandmother's generation used it. Then the Edict came, and the Crown ordered every crossing west of the Teeth struck from record, and I—" He stopped. Started again. "I didn't strike it. I did worse. I made sure no one could ever find it again."

"How do you erase a pass?"

"You don't erase it. You bury it under better lies." He took the chart back and turned it toward the firelight, angling the paper so the light raked across the surface. "Ink has texture. If you scrape a mark away, anyone holding the sheet at the right angle sees the ghost of it. So instead I drew over it. A false scree field. A rockfall dated forty years back, annotated as surveyed and confirmed. I gave the pass a history of being closed, and then I gave the closure a history of being checked. Three separate confirmations, three different surveyors' initials—all mine, all forged. Any royal officer who went looking would find a pass that had been dead since before he was born, verified dead by three men who never existed."

Tamsin was quiet for a long moment. The fire popped. Somewhere up-slope, an owl asked its question of the dark, and the fox's ears swiveled toward it, and Pip murmured in her sleep and burrowed closer to the warm fur.

"And the real pass?" Tamsin said finally. "Is it still there? Physically?"

"Oh, it's there. Rock doesn't care about paperwork." He almost smiled; it hurt his split lip. "But finding it is another matter. The Shepherd's Throat doesn't look like a pass from below. The entrance is a slot canyon barely wide enough for a loaded mule, hidden behind a waterfall that runs only in spring melt, and the approach winds through six miles of terrain that looks identical in every direction. No cairns—the Keepers forbade them. No trail markers. The old route was passed down orally, shepherd to shepherd, keeper to keeper, for four hundred years. There was exactly one written record of the approach. Distances, bearings, the sequence of landmarks, which side of the waterfall, where the slot opens, the order of the turning stones in the high corridor." He met her eyes. "One written record. And I'm the man who filed it in the restricted archive at Thornhold, under seal, twenty years ago, because sealed meant safe."

The word hung between them. Tamsin set down the venison strip. The fox ate it.

"Thornhold," she said.

"The crown archive. Yes."

"That's the fortress we just escaped the shadow of. The fortress where Serah Vane keeps her Bells and her hunters and her cells." Her voice stayed level, but he heard what moved underneath it, the way he'd learned to hear what moved underneath a smuggler's cheerful report of clear roads. "You're telling me the only way to find the Door that saves us from the Crown is to break into the Crown's own house."

"I'm telling you worse than that." He made himself keep going, because he had decided, somewhere on the glacier road, that he was done rationing truth. "It's not just the route document. When I was taken, they inventoried my effects. Everything I carried went into the Thornhold evidence vault—including my field notes. Twenty years of field notes. Every erased pass, every buried route, every door I ever hid, annotated in my own hand. Including—" he exhaled, "—including a page describing the Shepherd's Throat in enough detail that a competent surveyor could reconstruct the approach without the original document. I wrote it as insurance, back when I still believed I might need to find the Door myself someday."

"So the Crown may already have a map to the last way out of Thornfell."

"They have my handwriting and a locked box. They don't know what they have. Vane's people catalogued it as 'miscellaneous drafts.'" He rubbed his eyes. "But the moment anyone reads those notes carefully—and someone will, because the Bell campaign means the Crown is suddenly very interested in every rumor of a western passage—they'll have the Door. And then there won't be a Door. There'll be a garrison."

The wyrmling shifted, scales whispering against stone. Its voice, when it chose to use human words, always sounded like something learned secondhand, syllables handled by a creature that thought in older shapes. "*Then the papers must come out of the stone house,*" it said. "*Before their eyes learn to read them.*"

"Yes," Wren said. "Thank you. That's the entire strategic situation, summarized by a dragon."

Tamsin laughed—one short, sharp note, gone as fast as it came. She stood and walked to the edge of the firelight, arms crossed, looking north toward the black mass of the Grey Teeth. He let her think. He'd learned that about her in three days: she thought on her feet, moving, and if you filled the silence you'd get less truth, not more.

Pip stirred. The fox licked the girl's hair once, gently, and Pip settled again.

"We can't go around," Tamsin said at last. "South, the border garrisons are doubling every week—I've seen the supply trains. North is the ice fields, and even if we crossed them, the far side is Crown territory all the way to the coast. East is where we came from." She turned back to him. "And the Bell gets louder every day. You feel it too, don't you? Even non-speakers feel it now. Like a toothache in the air."

He did feel it. Since the great Bell had been rung at the mountain's heart—or rather, since Vane had *not* rung it, though none of them yet knew why the expected shockwave had never come—the hunting parties had spread through the lowlands like spilled water, and the portable Bells sang nightly from the valley floors, and every night fewer birds answered the dawn.

"There's one more problem," he said. "The worst one."

"Of course there is."

"The original route document isn't just in the archive. It's in the *restricted* archive. The Keeper's vault, under the old chapel. Which requires a Keeper's seal to access." He hesitated. "Or a royal warrant. Or—"

"Or?" 

"Or someone who's already inside." He watched her face change. "Tamsin. Your grandmother's ledger. The smuggler's ledger you told me about—the one with the marginalia, the route notes, the things she wrote that you never understood. Where is it?"

Her jaw tightened. "Sold. Years ago. After she died, I sold half her things to pay the land tax. A book dealer in Millbrook took the ledger. I was seventeen and I needed coin, and it was just an old account book full of numbers I couldn't read."

"Numbers you couldn't read." Wren felt the cold arrive in his stomach, the specific cold of a man watching a map assemble itself into terrible shape. "Tamsin, what did the dealer look like? Was he tall, grey beard, soft voice, called himself Master Ellery?"

She went very still. "How do you know that name?"

"Because Master Ellery is a purchasing agent. For the Crown. He travels the market towns buying documents—old ledgers, letters, anything with route information—and he ships everything to a single destination." Wren closed his eyes briefly. "The Thornhold archive. Your grandmother's ledger has been sitting in the same building as my field notes for probably fifteen years. Two halves of the same secret, shelved thirty feet apart, waiting."

"*Waiting,*" the wyrmling repeated softly, and the word sounded different in its mouth. Older. Like the creature found it funny in a way that had teeth.

Tamsin sat down slowly on the stone beside him. For a moment she looked younger than Pip—not in years but in structure, the way a person looks when a load they've carried so long it became their spine is suddenly lifted and they discover they don't know how to stand straight without it.

"She was the Keeper," Tamsin said quietly. "Wasn't she. That's what the wyrmling meant. That's what the ledger is."

"I think your grandmother was the last Keeper of the Verdant Door," Wren said. "And I think the ledger isn't a smuggler's account book at all. I think it's the key to the Shepherd's Throat, written in a cipher only a Keeper could read—which is why you couldn't read it, and why the Crown's archivists haven't cracked it either. Numbers, you said. To them it's inventory records. To us—" he gestured at the darkness beyond the fire, toward the impassable white space on his beautiful lying map, "—it's the way through."

The fire burned down a notch. Above them the stars came out hard and cold, the way they did at altitude, close enough to wound.

"So," Tamsin said. Her voice had changed. He'd heard it change this way once before, on the glacier road, when she'd turned to face the hunters instead of running. It was the voice of a woman finishing a calculation she hated. "We break into Thornhold. Into the most fortified stronghold in the northern marches. We steal a dead woman's account book and a disgraced cartographer's confiscated notes out of a sealed royal archive, past a garrison, past Serah Vane, past whatever Bells they've got mounted on those walls. And then we walk six miles of unmarked wilderness guided by a cipher nobody alive can read, to find a pass that officially does not exist, behind a waterfall, in early winter."

"When you say it like that—"

"It sounds exactly like what it is." But she was almost smiling now, and the smile scared him more than the plan did. "Wren. I've spent my whole life being invisible. Invisible doesn't work anymore—the Bell saw to that. So the question isn't whether it's desperate." She poked the fire, sending sparks up toward the indifferent stars. "The question is whether we're cleverer than they are. And I'll tell you one thing I know that they don't."

"What's that?"

"Thornhold's walls are built against men." She glanced at the fox, at the sleeping child, at the wounded wyrmling whose amber eye had not blinked once through the entire conversation. "They are not built against *us*. Rats run free in the granaries. Owls roost in the chapel rafters—right over the Keeper's vault, if your chapel is like every other chapel in these mountains. And wolves," she said, and her voice softened into something that was almost grief, almost love, "wolves go wherever they decide to go. You want a plan? Here's a plan. We don't break into Thornhold. We let the small things walk in through the cracks, and we ask them nicely to bring our paper home."

Wren looked at her—at this woman who had spent thirty years pretending to be ordinary, proposing to conduct a burglary of the Crown's greatest fortress by correspondence with rats—and felt, absurdly, the first real hope he'd felt since the night of his arrest.

"It's insane," he said.

"Yes."

"The ledger might not even be there. Ellery might have resold it. The notes might be misfiled. The vault might be warded—"

"Might." She stood, and pulled her pack toward her, and began checking straps with the brisk economy of a woman who had already decided. "Might is a smuggler's favorite word. Might means maybe, and maybe means worth trying. Get some sleep, cartographer. Tomorrow we scout the service roads." She paused, and something flickered across her face—doubt, quickly mastered. "Wren. One thing. If we do this, and it goes wrong—if Vane takes me again—you get Pip to the Door. Cipher or no cipher. Promise me."

He thought about lying to her. He was good at it; it was his profession.

"No," he said instead. "Nobody gets left. That's the deal. That's been the deal since the rockslide, and you know it."

For a long moment she just looked at him. Then she nodded once, sharply, the way you nod at a price you've agreed to pay, and lay down with her back to the fire.

Above the treeline, far to the north, a light appeared on the shoulder of the Grey Teeth. Small. Orange. Deliberate. Not a campfire—a signal, answered a minute later by a second light further east, and a third beyond that.

Hunting parties, closing the net, lighting the dark between them and the fortress they would have to enter.

The wyrmling watched the lights with its one open eye and said, in its borrowed human words, "*Stone houses keep paper badly.*"

Wren hoped, with everything he had left, that the creature was right.
