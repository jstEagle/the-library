## 11. Thornhold by Night

The plan came to Tamsin the way most of her plans did: sideways, in the dark, from something with more sense than she had.

They were camped in a hollow below the shepherd's road, three days out from Thornhold, and Wren was drawing the fortress from memory on a scrap of birchbark. He drew it the way he drew everything—precisely, lovingly, with little annotations that meant nothing to anyone but him. *Gatehouse, rebuilt 402. Curtain wall, patched. Kennels here. Archive here.*

"The archive is in the old chapel," he said, tapping it. "Ground floor. The crown moved the records when they converted the nave. Two guards on the door at night, one clerk by day. The ledger will be filed under confiscated goods—Thornbury, M., seizure dated—" he paused, "—the year after you were born."

"My grandmother's been dead eleven years."

"Paper lives longer than people." He said it without bitterness, which was somehow worse.

Tamsin looked at the birchbark. Looked at the walls he'd drawn, high and pale in the moonlight, patrolled every quarter hour. Looked at herself—a woman whose face was known now, whose name was on lists, who could not walk through a market or a checkpoint without someone remembering the Edict had teeth.

"We can't get in," she said. "Not me. Not any of us."

"No," Wren agreed cheerfully. "We can't."

Pip was asleep against the scarred hawk's warm side, the fox curled at her feet like a sentry. The wyrmling lay apart from all of them, as it always did, its wounded wing folded wrong, its amber eyes open and fixed on the fire with an expression Tamsin had learned to read as patience. It was waiting for something. It had been waiting since the pass.

And the blind wolf—

The blind wolf was sitting at the edge of the firelight, facing her, the way it always faced her, though it hadn't been able to see her in years. Its muzzle was gone silver. Its hips were stiff. When it stood, it stood like an old soldier deciding whether the war was worth one more march.

Tamsin looked at it, and the plan arrived.

*Beasts move freely where speakers cannot.*

That was the whole of it. That was the thing the crown had never understood about their own Bell—the Hollow Bell didn't hunt speakers, it enslaved beasts, turned every animal within earshot into a witness, a traitor, a leash around a hidden tongue. Vane rang it and the mountain betrayed its own children.

But a leash pulls both ways.

"They ring the Bell," Tamsin said slowly, "and every rat in Thornhold comes when called. Every owl. Every dog in the kennels." She turned the thought over, feeling its edges. "Which means the Bell has already told every beast in that fortress exactly what obedience sounds like. Which means I already know the voice they answer to."

Wren frowned. "You're saying you can counterfeit a Bell?"

"I'm saying the Bell doesn't speak beast. It speaks *command*, and command is just a voice with weight behind it." She'd spent her whole life whispering so no one would hear. She had never once tried to be loud. "I can't ring a Bell. But I can call rats. And no one guards against rats."

*

She worked through the night, and for the first time in her life she did not work quietly.

She sat at the mouth of the hollow and she opened herself the way she'd only ever opened herself in fragments—in the dark, alone, half-ashamed—and she called. Not with words, exactly. Words were the smallest part of it. She called with need, with shape, with the particular hunger of *come here, I have grain, I have warmth, I have a purpose for your clever hands.*

The rats came first. They always did. They poured out of the scree like spilled water, dozens of them, eyes catching the firelight, whiskers working. Behind them, softer, an owl dropped from the pines without a sound and settled on a stone to listen. A fox trotted down from the ridge. Somewhere far off, a badger grumbled its way toward her voice like a man answering a knock.

Pip woke and watched with her mouth open. Wren watched with his hand near his knife until the hawk clicked at him and he put it away.

"You've never done this," Wren said. "Called like this. Out loud. All of them at once."

"I've never needed anything before," Tamsin said. "I've only ever needed to hide."

She gave them the plan in images, because that was how beasts understood best: the fortress drawn in scent and stone, the chapel with its two doors, the box of paper that smelled of her grandmother's hands—because Tamsin made them smell it, made each rat press its nose to the scrap of ledger binding Wren had salvaged years ago, until the scent was carved into them. *Find this. Bring this.*

The owl she sent ahead as eyes. The rats she sent as hands. And the wolf—

The wolf she asked last, alone, away from the fire, the way you ask an old friend for something you're afraid they'll give.

"I need you inside those walls," she said. "I need you where I can't go. And if it goes wrong—if they ring the Bell—you'll hear it, and it will pull at you, and you'll have to fight it harder than anything has ever asked you to fight."

The wolf's blind face turned up toward her. It made a sound low in its chest, not quite a word, and pressed its head against her knee, and Tamsin understood: *I have been yours since before you could walk. Ask me for anything except staying behind.*

She buried her face in its ruff and did not let herself cry, because crying was a luxury and the plan was a knife and knives don't weep.

*

Thornhold at night was a skull with torches in its eye sockets.

Tamsin saw it first from the ridge above the valley road, the owl's sight layered over her own—the curtain wall, the gatehouse, the kennels where hounds slept in rows, the archive chapel squatting against the inner wall like a toad. And in the central yard, on an iron frame built tall enough to be seen from every corner of the fortress, hung the great Hollow Bell. Not the portable ones the hunting parties carried. The mother Bell. The one the heralds had promised would be rung in every square in Thornfell until no quiet tongue went uncounted.

It hung there silent, and even silent it seemed to lean on the world.

"She'll be there," Wren said beside her. He'd drawn the fortress again, better this time, and marked the commander's quarters. "Vane sleeps light. She'll know within minutes if something's wrong."

"Then something has to be wrong," Tamsin said. "Loudly, and immediately, and nowhere near the chapel."

Wren looked at her. "What are you planning?"

"Something stupid," she said. "Something brave. They look identical from the outside."

*

Here is what Tamsin Thornbury did, on the third night of the waning moon, at the gates of Thornhold:

She walked down the valley road with her hood back and her hands empty and visible, and when the gate guards challenged her she said, in a clear voice that carried, "I'm the one you're looking for. Tamsin Thornbury. Take me to Serah Vane."

Because that was the part the plan required that no beast could do. The Bell had taught the crown that speakers hid—that they ran, that they lied, that they could be flushed out with noise and fear. Nothing in the Edict's logic accounted for a speaker who walked up to the front door and knocked.

The guards stared. One of them laughed, then stopped laughing when the second guard didn't. They bound her wrists—not well; she'd been tied by professionals before and these men were frightened, and frightened men tie knots like apologies—and marched her into the yard, shouting the alarm ahead of them, and Tamsin walked into the skull's mouth with her chin up and her heart going like a drum.

Above her, unseen, the owl crossed the wall on silent wings.

Below, in the drains and the granary rafters, the rats began to move.

* *

They took her to a cell in the gatehouse, which was a mistake, and then to Vane, which was a bigger one.

Serah Vane looked worse than Tamsin remembered—or perhaps Tamsin was simply seeing her clearly for the first time, without the distance of fear. There were new lines around her mouth. Her armor was immaculate but her eyes were red-rimmed, and when she dismissed the guards and stood alone across the cell bars from the woman she'd hunted across half of Thornfell, she looked less like a hunter than like a woman reading a letter she already knew the contents of.

"You surrendered," Vane said. "No one surrenders. In eleven months, no speaker has ever just—walked in."

"I'm told I do everything wrong," Tamsin said. "Ask my grandmother. Oh—you burned her papers, so you can't."

"Why?"

"Because I'm tired of running." It was even mostly true, and the true parts were the best bait. "Because you're going to ring that Bell eventually, and I'd rather talk to you before you do than after."

Vane studied her for a long moment. Then she unlocked the cell door herself and stepped inside, alone, unarmed, which told Tamsin two things: the woman was either very confident or very desperate, and both were useful.

"You want to talk," Vane said. "Talk."

"Every beast in this fortress answers to your Bell," Tamsin said. "Did you ever wonder what happens when something else calls them?"

For the first time since Millbrook, Serah Vane looked uncertain. And upstairs, in the rafters of the archive chapel, forty rats began to chew through the leather straps of a confiscation crate, while in the belfry above the yard, an owl settled onto the iron frame of the great Hollow Bell and waited, patient as stone, for the signal it had been given:

*If they try to ring it—scream.*

*

It nearly went wrong twice.

The first time was the kennels. The hounds woke at midnight for their watch rotation, caught the strange new scent-thread in the air—*her*, the caller, the voice with weight—and began to bay, and for four endless minutes Tamsin stood in the cell with Vane talking maps and mercy at her while half her mind wrestled six war-hounds into confusion, layering her own call over their handlers' whistles until the dogs circled, whining, unable to decide whom they served. Vane cocked her head at the sound.

"Your dogs are restless," Tamsin said.

"It's the season."

"It's me," Tamsin almost said, and didn't.

The second time was the clerk. He came to the chapel early—insomnia, or duty, or some clerkish instinct—and found the crate straps chewed through and the confiscation box open and a small grey shape flowing out the window with a leather-bound ledger in its teeth, and he shouted, and the shout became bells, the alarm bells, and the yard filled with soldiers holding torches, and somewhere in the chaos a lieutenant screamed the order everyone feared:

*"Ring the Bell! Ring the Bell!"*

Two soldiers ran for the belfry ladder.

The owl fell on them like a piece of night with opinions.

It didn't kill them—it wasn't asked to kill—but it hit the first man's face and the second man's eyes, wings and talons and shrieking, and both soldiers went off the ladder in a tangle, and by the time they'd untangled themselves the owl was gone into the dark and the Bell hung untouched, swaying slightly, as if it had flinched.

In the cell, Tamsin felt all of it—the rats' triumph, the owl's fury, the hounds' confusion—as pressure behind her eyes, a hundred small hearts beating in rhythm with hers. She had spent thirty years being a secret. Tonight she was a general, and the army was made of everything the kingdom had decided didn't matter.

Vane grabbed the cell bars and shouted for her guards, and when she turned back her face had changed. Not softness. Calculation collapsing into something rawer.

"What did you do?" Vane whispered. "There are no animals in this fortress that obey you. We rang the Bell here ourselves, three weeks ago. Everything inside these walls is *ours*—"

"That's the flaw in your arithmetic," Tamsin said. "You counted the beasts you own. You never counted the ones that chose."

Then the wall of the gatehouse shuddered, dust sifting from the ceiling, and both women heard it come up the valley road together: a sound like rockfall, like thunder with intent, like something enormous and furious and half-tame arriving at a dead run with a debt to collect.

The wyrmling had heard the alarm too.

And it had never agreed to be subtle.
