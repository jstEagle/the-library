## 12. Two Voices in a Cell

Serah Vane had conducted forty-one interrogations since the Edict, and she had a system for all of them.

The system was simple: take away the dark, because speakers loved the dark. Take away the quiet, because they thought in it. Give them light and noise and no schedule for either, and within three days most of them were begging to confess just to make the world stop shifting. She had learned it from the Bell itself. The Bell worked by taking silence away from beasts; she worked by taking it away from people.

The woman in cell nine of Thornhold's undercroft had been given neither light nor noise, which was against Vane's own standing order, and when Vane descended the stairs at the fourth bell of the night and found the guard dozing with his boots up, she understood why. The prisoner had asked him for a story. He'd been telling her one about his mother's sheep for two hours.

"Out," Vane said.

The guard scrambled. The prisoner didn't move at all.

She sat on the cot with her back to the wall, hands loose in her lap, ankles crossed, as if she'd been waiting for company and mildly pleased it had finally come. Her lip was split—Vane had heard about that, the hunting party's report, the woman who'd stood in the open with blood running down her chin while every animal for a mile turned its head toward the Bell like flowers toward sun—and the scab had cracked again from her talking, or her smiling, or whatever it was she did instead of being afraid.

"Tamsin Thornbury," Vane said. "Guide. Smuggler's daughter. Granddaughter." She let that last word sit. "You've been walking crown roads without a license for eleven years."

"Have I?" The woman's voice was low and unhurried, a voice made for saying things quietly to animals in the dark. "I mostly walk goat paths. I don't think those belong to anyone."

"They belong to Thornfell. Everything does. That's what the Edict means."

"Mm," said Tamsin Thornbury, in exactly the tone Vane's mother had used when Serah, at nine years old, had tried to explain why the neighbor's dog had told her where the lost lambs were. *Mm.* The sound of a person deciding you weren't worth arguing with yet.

Vane pulled the stool from the corner and set it in the middle of the cell, deliberately inside the lantern's circle, deliberately facing the door rather than the cot. Interrogations were theater. She had learned that too. You never sat across from them; you sat where they had to turn their heads to watch you, so that watching you became work, so that silence became something they had to fill.

She sat. She opened the folder she'd brought, though she didn't need it. She knew the file by heart: no registered gift, no Bell-response on record, taken at the pass with a disgraced cartographer and a child whose fox had answered the Bell in full sentences. The child was upstairs. The cartographer was in surgery, or what passed for surgery in a fortress this far from anywhere. The guide was here, in the dark she'd been given illegally, looking comfortable.

"You understand your position," Vane said.

"I understand you gave me a lamp and a listener. That's more courtesy than your herald showed Millbrook."

Something moved behind Vane's ribs, quick and unwelcome. She kept her face still. "Millbrook was lawful."

"Was it." Tamsin turned her head at last—the first movement she'd made, and Vane noted with professional displeasure that she'd waited until it cost Vane something to have waited. "I watched it. From the ridge above the square. Your man rang the Bell and old Marta's dog stood up and spoke. Just once. Said *home now, home now, storm coming,* the way he'd said it to her for fifteen years, because she'd gone deaf and he'd learned to say it loud enough for her ears." The woman's eyes came around, gray and level. "Then your soldiers took Marta too. For listening. Is that the lawful part?"

"The dog was conscripted. It serves the crown now."

"It's a dog."

"It's an asset that can find speakers. Every beast that answers the Bell is an asset. That's not cruelty, Thornbury. It's the only mercy anyone's designed yet." Vane heard her own voice flatten into the register she used for recruits, and hated it slightly. "You've spent your life among beasts. Tell me I'm wrong. Tell me what happens to a speaker we *don't* find, when the border seals and the purges start in the villages. Tell me what happened at Shepherd's Road."

"I know what happened at Shepherd's Road. I walked through it two days after."

"Then you know the alternative to the Bell." Vane leaned forward. This was the argument, the whole architecture of it, the thing she'd built out of the wreckage of her own life and carried like a spine: *the Bell is not the fire, the Bell is the fence before the fire.* "A village finds out its shepherd talks to his flock. The village burns him, because fear makes neighbors into soldiers faster than any edict. Or the crown finds him first, rings the Bell, and takes his gift under law, under record, under protection—he lives. His family lives. He loses a voice he was never allowed to use anyway. Which is the kinder arithmetic?"

Tamsin looked at her for a long moment. Not hostile. Worse than hostile—*interested*, the way you look at a snare you've seen before and are deciding whether the trapper knows it's sprung on himself.

"That's a real question," Tamsin said. "So I'll give you a real answer. Ask your dog-assets how they feel about serving."

"They're beasts. They don't feel about it."

"There it is." Something almost gentle entered the woman's voice, and Vane realized with a cold slide of the stomach that she'd been waiting for it, had angled the whole conversation toward it like a guide angling a caravan toward a pass. "There's the crack in your arithmetic, Commander. You've decided the beasts aren't anybody. So taking their voices costs nothing, and giving ours costs little, and everyone lives. But you were a speaker. You know they're somebody. You know what it is, hearing a hawk say your name." A pause. "You know what you actually surrendered. And you've spent every day since telling yourself it was small, because if it wasn't small, then the Bell isn't a fence. It's just a quieter fire."

The lantern hissed. Somewhere above, boots crossed stone.

"My family is alive," Vane said.

"Yes," said Tamsin. "Mine's dead. My grandmother died in a cell smaller than this one, under an earlier edict, and my mother died free and silent and half-starved of everything except fear. So I'll grant you've done better by yours." She unfolded her hands at last, and Vane saw the calluses, the rope-wear, the guide's hands, competent even at rest. "But answer me one thing honestly, and I'll answer anything you ask. One honest thing, Commander to whatever I am."

"What?"

"When the Bell rang at the pass, and every animal turned toward it—" Tamsin's voice didn't change at all, which was how Vane knew the sentence had cost her more than any other she'd spoken. "When the hawk turned. When the wolf turned. Did you watch their faces? Because I did. And they weren't answering a call, Commander. They were screaming. The Bell doesn't summon. It *takes*. And you've built your whole mercy on top of a machine that works by theft, and called the theft protection."

"You bit through your own lip rather than answer it," Vane said. "That's in the report. That's the behavior of someone with something to hide."

"It's the behavior of someone who's heard what the Bell does to the things she loves." Tamsin tilted her head. "You'd know. You've rung it yourself. Haven't you? Small ones. Portable ones. On the road, in the squares. And afterward, haven't you ever found one of them—the beasts, your assets—sitting somewhere alone, making a sound you couldn't name? Haven't you ever heard one calling for something it couldn't remember wanting?"

Vane did not answer.

Because she had.

Three winters ago, outside Harrow Ford, a stag the Bell had taken—a great gray stag that had spoken to a poacher's daughter in whole sentences, that had named her *little wren* the way her dead mother must have—stood in the corral for six days after the girl was taken south, pressing its skull against the fence rail, making a sound like wind through a burned house. Vane had ordered it fed. She had ordered it exercised. She had not, on the seventh night, ordered anything at all; she had stood in the snow and listened until she couldn't stand it, and then she had signed the transfer papers herself and sent it to the breeding program at Cael Morath, where the taken beasts went to serve, and she had told herself the sound meant nothing because it could not mean anything, because if it meant anything then—

Then the fence was the fire.

"You were a speaker," Tamsin said softly. "What did they take? What animal was yours?"

"That's not relevant."

"What was *its* name?"

"*Stop.*"

The word came out of her like something breaking, and the cell went very quiet, and Vane sat in the lantern light with her hands flat on her knees and understood that she had just handed this woman the exact thing interrogators were trained never to hand anyone: the shape of the wound.

Tamsin didn't press it. That was the worst part. She simply looked at Vane with a terrible patience, the patience of someone who had hidden for thirty years and knew precisely how long a person could survive inside a lie, and said, "You don't have to tell me. But you should tell yourself. Because the Bell will ring at the mountain's heart soon—I've seen the wagons coming up the valleys, I've heard the big one being assembled—and when it does, it won't take gifts one at a time under law and record. It will take *everything*. Every beast in Thornfell, permanently, all at once. There won't be assets after that, Commander. There won't be a wild left to protect anyone from. Just a kingdom full of hollow animals and a crown that owns the silence." She rose, unhurried, and Vane realized she'd been sitting in a cell for four days and still moved like the mountains belonged to her. "You keep saying the choice is the Bell or the fire. But there was always a third thing. We were living in it. Quietly. Badly, sometimes—but alive, and *ourselves*, and the beasts were our family and not your witnesses. You didn't save us from the fire. You just taught the fire to whistle."

At the door she stopped—no, Vane corrected herself, Tamsin stopped, and Vane was the one who had risen halfway off the stool without noticing, which was a thing that had never once happened in forty-one interrogations.

"One more honest thing," Tamsin said. "Since we're trading."

"I'm not trading with you."

"No. But you'll want this anyway." The gray eyes held hers, steady as a compass needle. "Your cartographer. Wren Alcott. He's dying upstairs—they cut something they shouldn't have. And the child keeps asking for her fox, and the fox is in your kennels wearing a collar that's eating its voice, and every hour you wait, both of them get further from anything either of us would call mercy." A beat. "You wanted arithmetic, Commander. There it is. Two lives, one order, and nobody watching but you."

Then she was back on the cot, hands loose, ankles crossed, as if she hadn't moved at all, and Vane was left standing in the middle of the cell holding a folder she no longer believed in.

She climbed the stairs past the dozing guard without waking him. In the corridor above, the fortress breathed its night sounds—boots, torch-crackle, the far complaint of the kennels where the taken beasts waited out their service. She walked past the kennels. She made herself walk past them.

But at the end of the corridor she stopped, because from inside came a sound she knew, a sound she had signed away three winters ago in the snow outside Harrow Ford: a small, cracked, repetitive crying, an animal calling for something it could no longer remember wanting.

And beneath it, thinner, from the direction of the surgery ward, another sound: a man's voice, feverish, repeating numbers over and over—elevations, bearings, the coordinates of a pass that did not exist on any map the crown owned.

Vane stood between the two sounds for a long time.

Then she went to her office, took out the duty roster, and looked at the line where the great Bell's assembly was scheduled, at the mountain's heart, seven days from tonight.

Her pen hovered over it and did not move.
