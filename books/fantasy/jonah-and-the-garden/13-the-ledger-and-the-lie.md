## 13. The Ledger and the Lie

Wren Alcott had spent eleven years drawing maps for a kingdom that believed maps were truth, and he had learned one thing above all others: the moment of greatest danger was never the lie itself. It was the second after, when you waited to see if anyone had caught it.

He was living in that second now, stretched out behind a fallen lintel in the servants' corridor of Thornhold, watching Tamsin Thornbury walk away from him in chains.

She'd insisted on it. *Bait works only if it looks like bait worth taking,* she'd said, and then she'd let Vane's soldiers bind her wrists and march her through her own front gate like a trophy, and Wren had stood in the shadow of the granary with his heart hammering out a rhythm he didn't have a name for and thought: *I have lied to kings. I have never once told the truth this loudly.*

Now the corridor smelled of tallow and wet stone, and somewhere above them the great Hollow Bell sat in its tower like a tumor made of bronze, and Wren counted seconds.

The plan was elegant, which should have warned him. Elegant plans were what he drew on paper; in the world, plans came back smudged.

*One hundred breaths,* Tamsin had said. *That's how long I can hold Vane's attention. Then the wolf comes over the wall with the ledger, and we go out through the cistern gate, because no one guards a door rats use.*

The blind wolf—Ashen, Pip called her, though Wren suspected the name was older than the girl—had gone over the east wall an hour past, guided by owls Tamsin had spoken to at dusk in a voice Wren still couldn't quite believe he'd heard. A voice like water finding its level. He'd spent his whole career translating the land into lines and symbols, and here was a woman who simply *asked*, and the land answered.

Ninety-one. Ninety-two.

A rat appeared at his elbow. Not scurrying—*arriving*, the way a courier arrives, with purpose. It looked at him with small black eyes that held, unmistakably, a message.

*West stair. Now.*

Tamsin's voice in his head would have been too much to hope for; the gift didn't work that way on people, she'd said. But the rat's urgency translated fine.

Wren went, keeping low along the wall where the torchlight thinned. Below the west stair, in the old guardroom they'd claimed as their staging point, Pip sat cross-legged on the floor with the scarred hawk perched on her shoulder and her fox pressed against her side, both animals rigid as drawn bows.

"She says hurry," Pip whispered. Her face was pale in the dark. "The hawk says it too. Something's wrong."

"What's wrong?"

Pip's chin trembled, just once, and steadied. She'd stopped trembling easily about four days ago, after the burned village, and Wren hadn't decided yet whether that was growth or grief. "She won't say. She says if she says it, it'll be true faster."

Children, Wren thought, were the only honest cartographers left.

They climbed.

* * *

Vane had put Tamsin in an interrogation cell on the ground floor—the same cell, Wren realized with a sour twist, where he'd spent his own brief captivity—and the door stood open, which meant either triumph or catastrophe.

It was catastrophe wearing triumph's coat.

Tamsin stood in the middle of the room, wrists free, holding a ledger wrapped in oilcloth, and her expression was the one Wren had learned to dread: calm as a frozen lake, with something enormous moving underneath.

"You got it," he said.

"The wolf got it." She handed it to him without ceremony, but her hand shook on the release. "And Vane is dead."

The word landed wrong. "Dead? Vane doesn't die. Vane defects. That was the whole—" He'd staked the route home on it, on the crack he'd watched widen in Serah Vane across two conversations and one shared flask of terrible brandy. "What happened?"

"Not Vane." Tamsin's jaw worked. "Her second. Captain Orliss. He found us talking. Found her unlocking my chains." Her voice went flat and careful, the way it did when she was carrying something heavy so it wouldn't spill. "There was a knife. There wasn't time. Vane took it instead of me."

"Is she—"

"Alive. Barely. Orliss is calling it treason, which means every soldier in Thornhold is now hunting two of us instead of one, and the garrison bell will ring within the hour, and when it does—" She looked up at the ceiling, toward the tower, toward the tumor of bronze. "They'll ring the great Bell. Not the portable ones. The one that doesn't ask. The one that *takes*."

Wren felt the color leave his face. He'd read the archive folios; falsifying border maps bought him years among documents most men never saw. "Permanent binding. Every beast within the mountain's voice."

"Every beast," Tamsin said. "Including ours."

Above them, faint but rising, a bell began to swing.

* * *

They ran.

The cistern gate was three hundred yards of service corridors away, and Thornhold was waking around them like a kicked hive—shouting, boots, the groan of portcullis winches. Wren clutched the ledger to his chest and ran the map in his head, because that was the one thing he was for: *left at the wine stores, down through the laundry gallery, the low arch, the steps that shouldn't exist but do because I put them there twenty years ago and no one ever checked.*

That was the joke of his life, really. His crime—the crime they'd flogged him for, stripped his commission for, chained him for—was drawing doors onto maps that weren't there. Escape routes for refugees, hidden in plain sight among surveyor's marks. And now the only way out of the crown's own fortress was one of his lies made stone.

"The hawk says soldiers in the laundry gallery," Pip panted beside him. The fox ran ahead like a grey flame. "The fox says the other way smells of smoke."

"Smoke?"

"Torch smoke," Tamsin said grimly. "They're burning the undercrofts. Orliss isn't chasing us. He's *sealing* us. Burn us out or burn us in, he doesn't care which."

They cut left instead, toward the cistern, and Wren knew before they rounded the corner that it was wrong, because the air changed—that cold mineral breath of standing water replaced by woodsmoke and heat, and orange light crawling up the far wall of the passage.

Two soldiers stood at the cistern gate with torches, feeding the fire that was eating the wooden frame of the old door. Behind them, black water. Beyond it, the tunnel out.

The soldiers turned. Young. Nervous. One of them raised a spear with hands that shook.

Wren did the arithmetic in half a heartbeat, the way he did everything: two spears, one burned doorway, a child, a wounded woman somewhere above them, a ledger against his ribs, and maybe forty feet of open ground between here and the water.

Forty feet was nothing. Forty feet was everything.

"Give me the ledger," Tamsin said.

"No."

"Wren—"

"I'm the fastest over open ground and you're the one who has to swim it carrying a child who can't." He was already unbuckling the oilcloth strap, pressing the ledger into her arms, and he heard himself say, in the crisp professional tone he used for kings' clerks, "Chapter six, the pass under Ravenmere. My best forgery. It's real now. I made it real. Go make it true."

Then he ran at the soldiers, arms wide, shouting like a man possessed—which, in fairness, required very little acting—and the younger one broke first, stumbling back into his fellow, and the spear went wide, and Wren got a shoulder into the torch-bearer and felt the fire catch his sleeve and didn't stop, didn't feel it yet, *chose* not to feel it yet—

"TAMSin!" Pip's scream cracked the corridor.

He reached the water. Cold swallowed him to the waist. He turned, waist-deep in the dark, and saw Tamsin hauling Pip toward the gate, saw the fox streak past them both, saw the older soldier recover his spear and draw it back with the smooth economy of a man who'd done this a hundred times and felt nothing—

Wren didn't think. Thinking was for maps. He threw himself back out of the water, up the two steps, and put his body between the spear and the running woman and child.

The world went white, then red, then very quiet and very simple.

He was on his knees. He was holding his own side, and it was warm, warmer than it should be, and the soldier was raising the spear again, and then the darkness at the edge of the torchlight unfolded into teeth.

Ashen hit the man like a landslide given legs. Blind, ancient, and utterly silent until the moment she struck—and then the sound she made was not a wolf's sound at all but something from the mountain's oldest dreams, and the soldier dropped the spear and ran, and his friend was already gone.

The wolf stood over Wren, sides heaving, milky eyes aimed at nothing, and lowered her great grey head to his shoulder almost gently, as if checking whether he was still worth guarding.

"Wren." Tamsin was there, kneeling in the shallows, her hands pressing hard against his flank where the blood came hottest. "Look at me. Look at me, you lying, brilliant bastard, look at me."

"That," Wren managed, "is slander. I'm mostly honest. Selectively."

"Don't talk."

"It's a puncture," he said, because talking kept the white edge off the pain. "Through and through, by the feel. Missed anything vital, or I'd know. I've catalogued vital things professionally." His teeth wanted to chatter; he refused them. "The ledger. Did you—"

"I have it." Her voice cracked clean down the middle. "You absolute fool, I *have* it."

"Then we're even," Wren said. "You gave me a map out of your mountain. I gave you one out of mine." He tried to smile and discovered smiling required muscles he'd apparently lent to someone else. "We should get out of the water. Hypothermia is undignified."

* * *

They got him out. That was the phrase Wren's mind kept returning to, later, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth: *they got him out.* Through the flooded tunnel, Ashen swimming at his side bearing half his weight in her jaws' grip on his collar, Tamsin carrying Pip on her back and the ledger wrapped in its oilcloth held high above the waterline, the hawk scouting ahead through some sense that wasn't sight and wasn't smell.

They came up through the rocks below Ravenmere as the fortress behind them lit with torches and shouting, and the great Hollow Bell rang once—a single deep stroke that rolled down the valley like the mountain clearing its throat—and every bird in the sky bent toward it, and every bird in the sky *resisted*, because Tamsin stood on the shore with her head thrown back and said something in the old tongue that Wren's ears received as music and his bones received as law, and the birds fought free of the Bell's pull one wingbeat at a time.

Then they walked. Slowly, because of him. He hated that. He hated being the weight.

They made camp in a hollow beneath a leaning pine, and Tamsin built a fire with hands that didn't shake until the work was done, and then shook. She cleaned the wound with snowmelt and spirits from Wren's own flask, sewed it with sailmaker's thread from her go-bag, and worked in silence while Pip sat against Wren's good side, holding his hand with the fierce unsophisticated grip of a child who has decided that holding on is a job and she will do it perfectly.

"You know what the wyrmling said," Tamsin said at last, not looking up from her stitching. "About the Door."

"Surrender what you most hide," Wren said. "Yes. I've been turning it over. As a cartographer, I find it suspiciously vague. No coordinates whatsoever."

"It's not vague." The needle paused. "I've been thinking about what it asks. And I think—I think it doesn't want a secret whispered into a hole. I think it wants a *sacrifice*. Something given up that costs. That's why grandmother's notes call it surrender. That's why speakers who enter come out remade." Her voice dropped. "Something like this. Something like a man bleeding out in a tunnel so the rest of us could carry the map."

Wren went very still, insofar as a man with a sewn wound can be still.

"No," he said.

"You don't know what I'm going to say."

"I know exactly what you're going to say, because I've read enough prophecies-adjacent folklore to recognize a self-sacrificing protagonist when I'm bleeding next to one, and I am telling you *no*. You are not walking into that Door planning to feed it your life. Or mine. Or anyone's."

"It might not be death," Tamsin said. "It might be worse. It might be *less*. It might take the part of me that—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Whatever it takes, I'll pay it. But it doesn't get to take any of you. That's the line. That's the whole line, Wren. I've spent my whole life paying other people's prices by staying silent. I'm done."

Pip's grip tightened on his hand. The fox had crept close and laid its chin on Wren's knee, and its amber eyes were fixed on Tamsin with an expression Wren could only call *witnessing*.

"Then hear my counteroffer," Wren said. He was tired; tiredness made him honest, which was rare enough that he intended to spend it well. "I falsified maps for six years knowing it would end me. Do you know why? Because every line I drew was a door for someone else, and I never once got to see anyone walk through one. Not once. I sent three hundred people out of Thornfell and I have no idea what happened to a single one of them." He looked at her, and at Pip, and at the wolf whose blind face was turned toward the fire's warmth, and at the hawk preening on the pine branch above. "So here is what I actually want, Tamsin Thornbury, and I have never told anyone this, which makes it the largest thing I hide: I don't want escape. I want to *see it work*. Once. I want to stand on the far side of a door I helped open and watch people be free on the other side of it. That's all. That's the entire ambition of my shabby life."

The fire popped. Somewhere upslope, an owl called, and Tamsin's head tilted toward it the way it always did, listening to a language Wren would never own.

"So we don't trade lives at that Door," he said. "We find another price. Or we find another way. Or we kick its roots until it negotiates. I didn't survive a flogging and a firing squad and a spear to be a plot device in your tragedy."

Tamsin stared at him for a long moment. Then she laughed—a short, startled, genuine sound, the first one Wren had ever heard from her, and it transformed her face so completely that he understood, suddenly, exactly what the Door would be taking from her if it took her wildness, and exactly what kind of person it would be taking it from.

"Fine," she said. "No sacrifices. We negotiate with a sentient archway. Naturally. Why would anything about this be simple."

"Nothing worth mapping ever is."

Pip yawned enormously and slumped against Wren's side, already half asleep, trusting him absolutely with the weight of her, and Wren sat very carefully still and thought: *this. This is what I hid. Not the maps. This.*

Tamsin banked the fire and settled the ledger on her knees, opening it at last to the pages her grandmother had sealed, and the owl called again from the dark—closer now, urgent, insistent—and Tamsin went rigid.

"What is it?" Wren asked.

She looked up at him, and the firelight made her eyes look like they belonged to something older than a smuggler's guide.

"The owl says the Bell rang twice more while we slept-bound the wound," she said. "From the south. From the lowlands." Her hand flattened on her grandmother's ledger. "Wren—they're not ringing it to hunt us anymore. They're ringing it in every square in Thornfell. All at once. They're not trying to catch speakers."

"They're conscripting the mountain," Wren breathed. "Before we can reach the Door."

"No." Tamsin rose, and the wolf rose with her, and the hawk dropped from its branch to her shoulder like a falling leaf coming home. "They're trying to steal it. Every beast, every voice, every wild thing in Thornfell—bound to the crown before sunrise." She looked north, toward the high snows, toward the Verdant Door, and her jaw set like bedrock. "Then we don't sleep. We climb tonight. We reach the Door before the Bell reaches the summit—because if they bind the mountain first, there'll be nothing left on the other side of that archway worth opening it for."

Behind them, far to the south, the Bell rang again. And again. Closer each time, like footsteps.

Like a countdown.
