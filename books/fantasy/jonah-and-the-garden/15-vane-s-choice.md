## 15. Vane's Choice

The order came at dusk, written in the king's own hand, and Serah Vane read it three times before she understood what she was being asked to do.

*By command of His Majesty: proceed to the Hollow at the mountain's heart. There you will ring the Great Bell until every beast in Thornfell answers. This is not a request.*

She had known, of course. She had helped design the campaign. The portable Bells were for finding speakers, one valley at a time, one village at a time — slow work, careful work, the kind of work that let a commander tell herself she was being surgical. The Great Bell was different. The Great Bell did not find. The Great Bell *took*. Every creature within its voice — wolf and wren, hawk and hare, the old blind dog sleeping on a hearth in Millbrook — would turn toward the sound and never turn away again. Not enslaved in the way of chains. Enslaved in the way of rivers: bound to a course they could no longer choose.

Every speaker in Thornfell would be rooted out in a single night. And every beast in Thornfell would spend what remained of its life as the crown's instrument.

Vane stood at her tent flap and watched the light die on the western peaks, and she thought about her mother's hands.

Her mother had been a speaker. Had whispered to the geese that came over the marshes every autumn, and the geese had come to her garden instead of the neighbor's fields, and no one had starved those years. Vane had been nine when the first Edict came, eleven when the Bell arrived in their market town, and she remembered with terrible clarity how her mother had stood in the square with her hands folded, jaw tight, while the dog beside her lifted its head and answered the Bell's song with a low, confused whine — because the Bell called to beasts the way a lost child calls, and beasts were made to answer.

Her mother had surrendered her gift that winter. There had been a ceremony. A cold iron band placed against her throat, a prayer, a document signed. Afterward her mother had wept for three days, and then never spoken of it again, and the geese had gone back to stripping the neighbor's fields, and the family had survived. That was the arithmetic Vane had learned before she learned long division: one gift, weighed against five lives. She had made the same trade herself at nineteen, when the recruiting officer found her talking to his horse and offered her a choice between the collar and the commission.

Thirty years she had told herself the trade was clean.

* * *

The wyrmling was kept in a wagon cage at the rear of the column, and Vane went to it alone, after midnight, without a lantern.

It lay coiled at the bottom of the cage like a heap of wet rope, its scales dull, the wound along its flank where the portable Bell had burned it crusted black. It was smaller than she expected. Everything about the stories of dragons was larger than the thing itself; this was a creature the size of a large hound, with eyes like chips of amber, and when it saw her it did not flinch. It had stopped flinching days ago. That was the worst thing she had seen in thirty years of soldiering — a wild thing learning that flinching changed nothing.

"You're the one who bargained," Vane said. "At the pass."

The wyrmling's head came up an inch. Its speech was broken, half-hiss, half-word, but she had interrogated enough prisoners to follow anything.

*"Thorn-blood owes. Thorn-blood pays."*

"I know. I read the report." Tamsin Thornbury. The guide who had walked into Thornhold as bait, who had taken the ledger out from under Vane's own seal, who had stood in a cell across from her and said, in a voice with no anger in it at all: *You think the Bell protects them. It doesn't protect them. It deletes them. You gave up your gift, Serah. Would you have wanted it cut out of every goose your mother ever loved?*

Vane had ordered her transferred to maximum custody after that conversation. She had also, that night, sat alone with the memory of her mother's hands for the first time in twenty years.

"The order says ring the Great Bell at the Hollow," Vane said now. "When it rings, everything answers. Including you."

The wyrmling watched her. Waiting. Wild things did not beg; that was something else she had learned too late, watching her mother stand in the square.

"There's a debt," Vane said. "Between your kind and hers. Grandmother to granddaughter. Safe passage." She reached through the bars — slowly, the way you'd reach toward a wounded hawk — and set her hand flat on the cage floor. "I'm going to open this cage. When I do, you'll owe nothing to me, and I'll owe everything to you. Do you understand what I'm asking?"

*"Askkk,"* the wyrmling said, *"what you fear."*

And there it was. The question she had spent thirty years not asking, asked by a burnt lizard in a wagon cage, and Vane found her answer came easily, which frightened her more than anything else that night.

"I'm afraid of becoming the Bell," she said. "Of ringing it so long I forget there was ever another song."

She drew her knife and cut the lock.

* * *

The camp woke to fire.

Not the wyrmling's doing — Vane had done that herself, a lamp knocked into the supply tent, oil doing the rest, because a commander knew that chaos was worth ten swords. By the time the shouting started she was already moving through the smoke with the wyrmling's rope-scarred body pressed low against her side, past the picket lines where horses screamed and pulled at their tethers, and here was the thing she had not planned for, the thing that broke something open in her chest as she ran: the horses were not screaming at the fire.

They were screaming at the Bell.

The portable Bells hung in their transport frames near the command tent, and even silent, even cased, the animals could feel them — had been feeling them for weeks, the way a tooth feels weather coming. As Vane passed, a bay mare turned her great dark eye toward the cages and made a sound Vane had never heard a horse make. Not fear. *Accusation.* And Vane thought: thirty years. Thirty years of standing next to these animals, riding them, feeding them, asking them to carry her into battles that were hers and not theirs, and they have known the whole time what hangs in the wagon behind us. They have known, and they stayed anyway, because staying is what they were made to do, and I mistook their patience for consent.

"Left, girl," she whispered to the mare nearest her — not speaking, just breathing the word, the way her mother used to breathe words to geese — and the mare quieted, and stepped aside, and let her pass.

It was such a small thing. It cost her nothing. It was the first honest act of her life, and it took four seconds.

Then the sentries saw her.

"Halt! Commander— *Commander Vane?*"

She did not answer. She ran.

The arrows started before she cleared the tree line. One took her shoulder — a hot punch, spinning her, and the wyrmling tore free of her grip and vanished into the dark with a hiss that sounded almost like grief, and Vane rolled behind a fallen trunk as the shouts organized themselves behind her into the thing she had spent her whole career building: a pursuit. Efficient. Disciplined. Hers.

*Hunted by my own soldiers,* she thought, pressing her hand to her shoulder, feeling the blood come through her fingers. *Well. I built them well.*

She laughed — actually laughed, once, a short ugly sound — and crawled north into the trees.

* * *

She found them two days later, or they found her; by then the distinction had stopped mattering. She had gone north on instinct alone, following the high valleys, following the pull of something older than strategy, and on the morning of the third day a hawk dropped out of a white sky and screamed at her twice, imperiously, and Vane understood she was being summoned.

They were in a hollow below the glacier road — the guide, the cartographer propped against a stone with a bandaged chest, the child. The fox saw her first and put itself between Vane and Pip with its hackles up, and the child looked at Vane with the flat, unblinking stare of someone who had already seen what soldiers do.

Tamsin Thornbury stood with her arms crossed, and did not look surprised.

"The wyrmling told you where I'd be," Vane said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. Blood loss, she told herself. Just blood loss.

"It told us a woman opened its cage and got shot for it." Tamsin studied her the way she'd probably study a suspect bridge — testing weight, finding rot. "It also told us you were the one who rang the Bell at Millbrook. And Ashford. And the Shepherd's Road."

"Yes."

"And you're here because—"

"Because tomorrow night they ring the Great Bell at the Hollow." Vane swayed, and Wren caught her elbow, and she hated him a little for it, and let him. "Everything in Thornfell answers it. Permanently. Every beast, every bird, every animal in every barn and every house. Your Door, your passage, your escape — none of it matters after tomorrow, because there will be nothing left in this country that isn't the king's ear." She looked at Tamsin directly. "I know the Bell better than anyone alive. I know its range, its rhythm, the interval between rings. I can get you inside its silence. But I cannot stop it. I've run every way to stop it and there isn't one."

Tamsin was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere above them the wind moved through the pines, and Vane realized she could feel it — actually feel it, the attention of the wood, dozens of small lives turned toward this hollow, listening. Thirty years of deafness, and now that she had betrayed everything, the world was suddenly full of voices.

"You gave up your gift," Tamsin said. "To save your family."

"Yes."

"And now you've given up everything else. To save ours." Something moved in Tamsin's face — not forgiveness; Vane hadn't earned forgiveness, and they both knew it. Recognition, maybe. "That's a bad trade, Serah Vane."

"It's the only trade I haven't made yet," Vane said. "I'm told the Door likes those."

Tamsin almost smiled. Then she unslung her pack, dug out a strip of dried meat, and held it out — not to Vane. To the fox. "Take it to her," she said quietly. "She hasn't eaten in two days and she's too proud to ask."

The fox trotted over, sat primly before Vane, and laid the meat at her boots.

Vane looked down at it for a long time. Then she knelt — shoulder screaming, knees in the snow — and picked it up, and ate, while a child's fox watched her with ancient amber eyes, and somewhere overhead the hawk circled once and flew north, and the mountain, which had every reason to close against her, let her stay.

Redemption, she thought, chewing. So this is what it feels like. Not granted. Earned, in installments, paid in blood and dried meat, and the debt never fully clears.

It was enough. For the first time in thirty years, it was enough.

Above the glacier road, far to the south, faint as a heartbeat, the first test-ring of the Great Bell rolled through the stone of the world — and every bird in the forest went silent at once, and turned south, and waited.
