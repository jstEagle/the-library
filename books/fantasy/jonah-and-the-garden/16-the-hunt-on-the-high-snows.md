## 16. The Hunt on the High Snows

The glacier road was a scar of old violence, and they climbed it in the dark because the dark was the only country left that belonged to them.

Tamsin went first, boots wrapped in fleece, testing each step with her staff before she trusted it. Behind her came Wren, leaning on Pip's shoulder more than he would admit, his wound bound in strips of Vane's own cloak. Vane walked last, facing backward half the time, watching the switchbacks below for torchlight. The wyrmling flew above them in slow circles, a darker shape against the darker sky, its broken wing held stiff.

And the wolf walked beside Tamsin.

He was slower now. She had known it for days without letting herself know it—the way you know a roof beam is cracked but keep walking under it anyway. Old Ash, the smugglers had called him once, before anyone called him anything at all. His muzzle had gone white as the snow around them. His blind eyes caught no light at all, and still he kept his head turned toward her, reading her footsteps through the ground the way he'd read them all his life.

*You should have left me at the tree line,* he said.

*I've never once done what I should,* Tamsin said. *Don't start expecting it now.*

His laugh was a breath through his nose, fog in the cold. *Stubborn woman. Your grandmother was the same.*

They made the shoulder of the mountain an hour past midnight, where the glacier road narrowed between a wall of blue ice and a drop into blackness, and there Tamsin stopped, because the wind changed.

It came down from the passes carrying woodsmoke and iron and the oiled smell of many men. And under it, faint as a thread pulled through cloth, came the Bell.

Not ringing. Not yet. But humming—she could feel it in her teeth, in the hollow behind her sternum, a held note waiting to be released. They were coming up the road behind them with the great portable Bell lashed to a sledge, and Serah Vane's former soldiers were not hurrying anymore, because they knew now that their quarry had nowhere to go. Above this pass there was only the high snows, and beyond the high snows, if Wren's erased map told true, the Verdant Door.

"Rest," Tamsin said aloud, and her voice came out rougher than she meant it to. "We rest here. We can't outrun what's coming, so we stop choosing to run."

Wren lowered himself onto a stone with a sound that wasn't quite a groan. "That's either wisdom or surrender."

"It's arithmetic," Tamsin said. "We're four walkers, one lizard, and a dying wolf. They're two hundred soldiers and a machine that turns every living thing into a traitor." She looked at Vane. "You said the great Bell at the mountain's heart would enslave everything permanently. This one is smaller. What does it do?"

Vane stood with her arms crossed against the cold, and for a long moment she didn't answer. When she did, her voice was flat, the voice of a woman reciting something she'd helped write. "It doesn't enslave. It compels. Every beast within earshot must answer its call—and answer *honestly*. It finds speakers through their own companions. That's how we took Millbrook. That's how we took the shepherd villages." A pause. "That's how they'll find us."

"The fox," Pip whispered. She was sitting with her knees drawn up, the little red fox curled in her lap like a scarf. "It hears the Bell too. Everyone hears it."

"Yes," Tamsin said gently. "Everyone hears it."

She crouched beside the wolf then, and put her hand on his neck, into the thick fur that had gone coarse and gray at the throat. He leaned into her hand with his whole weight, which was how he apologized for things, and how he asked for things, and how he loved.

*How far can you walk tomorrow?*

*As far as you need,* he said. *That has always been the arrangement.*

*Ash—*

*Don't.* But there was no sharpness in it. There never had been. *I'm old, Tamsin. Older than wolves get. I've been running on borrowed legs since the winter your grandmother died, and I knew it, and I stayed anyway, because staying was mine to choose.* He turned his blind face toward the pass below, toward the thread of the Bell's hum. *Let me choose one more thing.*

"I won't," she said out loud, because some things were too large to say only inside.

The others looked at her. The wolf huffed, almost amused.

"She talks to me," he said, in the way wolves speak to people who already understand—that low subvocal rumble that isn't language until someone decides it is. "She's been talking to me since she was nine years old and fed me a frozen rabbit out of pity. Worst hunter I ever met. Best friend I ever had." He lay down heavily, chin on his paws. "Go sleep. Someone should be rested when the killing starts."

Nobody slept much.

* * *

They came at first light, and they came with the Bell singing.

The sound rolled up the glacier road ahead of the soldiers like weather—a note too deep to be heard properly, felt instead in the bones, in the fillings of teeth, in the water freezing in the rock cracks. Tamsin felt it seize the air itself. Beside Pip, the fox went rigid, ears flat, and began to tremble. Above them the wyrmling screamed once, a sound like tearing metal, and fought its own wings to stay aloft.

*Hold,* Tamsin said to it, to the fox, to every creature within reach of her voice. *Hold to my voice. The Bell is loud but it doesn't know your names. I do.*

That was the whole trick, and she'd learned it only three nights ago, bleeding in the snow: the Bell commanded beasts the way a whip commands a horse, but a name was not a command. A name was a door opened from the inside. She poured herself down that open door—*Russet, who sleeps in Pip's coat; Ember-of-the-Cliff-Shadow, whose wing will mend by spring; Grey-Muzzle, who carried me across the Ford of Bones when I was twelve and too proud to ask*—and the trembling eased, and the fox pressed closer to the child, and the wyrmling banked and held its circle.

But the soldiers kept climbing.

They came in column along the narrow road, forty abreast impossible here, so they came in twos and threes, pikes bristling, the Bell on its sledge in the middle like a shrine being carried to war. And at the head of them, on foot like her men because the road allowed nothing else, walked a captain Tamsin didn't know—young, competent, following the orders Vane had refused.

"They'll reach the narrows in an hour," Vane said. She had her sword out and was looking at it as if she'd forgotten why people carried such things. "Then they'll ring the Bell at full voice, and every animal on this mountain becomes theirs. Including yours."

"Including ours," Tamsin said.

"There's a place," the wolf said suddenly, standing. He was facing the narrows, his blind face lifted, nostrils working. "Where the road pinches between the ice wall and the drop. One wide body cannot pass. Two cannot fight abreast." He looked back at her, and though his eyes saw nothing, she had never once in twenty years felt unseen by them. "I can hold it."

"No," Tamsin said.

"You didn't let me finish." He sat, deliberate, an old man arranging himself by a fire. "I can hold it long enough. You take the others up to the Door. I stay. When it's done, it's done, and I—" 

"*No.*"

"Tamsin." Wren's voice, quiet. "There's no other way. I've been looking at the ground for an hour. There's no other way."

"There's always another way. I've spent my whole life finding other ways. That's what guides *do*—"

"Tamsin." The wolf again, gentle. "Come here."

She went. Of course she went. She knelt in the snow in front of him and he put his great head against her chest, and she felt the laboring of his heart, the hitch in it that hadn't been there last winter, and understood that he had been right about the borrowed legs, and that she had known, and that knowing had changed nothing because love is not a strategy.

*Nineteen years,* he said. *You found me starving in a snare and cut me loose, and I bit you, and you came back the next day anyway. Do you remember what you said?*

*You said,* she managed, *that I was the worst-smelling human you'd ever met.*

*And then?*

*And then you said you'd stay. Until I didn't need you anymore.*

*And do you?*

She couldn't answer. The wind took her silence and scattered it.

*Good,* he said. *Then this is the day. Not because I don't need you—I will need you until the last breath leaves me, and probably after, complaining about your cooking. But because needing isn't the same as holding. You taught me that, all those years you let me pretend I was still hunting for myself.* He pulled back and shook himself, snow flying, and stood tall the way he used to stand, the way he'd stood over her between her and every threat since she was nine years old. *Now listen to me carefully, because I'll only say this once. Whatever happens down there—whatever you hear—do not come back for me. Go through the Door. Become whatever it makes you. And when you come back down this mountain—and you will come back, stubborn woman, you'll come back for all of them—tell them about me. Tell them a blind wolf held the pass at the top of the world. Tell them his name.*

*Ash,* she said. *Your name is Ash.*

*My name is Ash,* he agreed. *Now go. The soldiers are close enough to smell.*

She kissed the top of his head, between the ears, where the fur was softest, where she'd kissed him ten thousand times across nineteen years, and then she stood up and did the hardest thing she had ever done, which was turn away.

Pip was crying without noise, the way children cry when they've learned already what crying costs. Tamsin gathered her up one-armed and did not look back. Wren limped beside her. Vane walked with her sword drawn and her jaw set like a woman attending her own execution.

Behind them, single and silent, the wolf turned down the road toward the narrows.

* * *

They heard it begin a quarter hour later, from the shelf of stone below the Door's approach, where Wren said the arch waited and Tamsin felt it like warmth through a wall.

First the challenge—no words, just the wolf's voice rolling off the ice walls, doubled and tripled into something vast, something that sounded less like one animal than like the mountain clearing its throat. Then the shouts of men. Then steel on stone, and steel on teeth, and the terrible sliding sounds of bodies losing footing on the glacier road.

Tamsin stood with her fists clenched and her lip bitten through, and she did not go back. It was the promise. It was the hardest vow of her life, harder than silence, harder than hiding, and she kept it the way she'd kept everything else: barely, bloodily, one heartbeat at a time.

*Hold the narrows,* she sent down to him, over and over, a rope of thought thrown into a storm. *Hold, hold, hold.*

*Holding,* he answered, and then, after a long time, *Holding,* and then, after a longer time, nothing at all, and the sounds below changed, and Vane put her hand on Tamsin's shoulder and Tamsin threw it off, and Pip buried her face in the fox's fur, and the wind came up the mountain carrying the smell of blood and gunpowder smoke and wet fur, and Tamsin Thornbury stopped running.

Not her feet. Her feet were still. Everything else in her stopped—the part of her that had spent thirty years making herself small, making herself quiet, making herself a rumor in her own life. Something rose in its place, something with her face and her grief and a voice she had never once permitted herself to use at full strength.

"He's dead," she said aloud, and the words came out level and enormous. "They killed him. An old blind wolf, and it took forty men and most of their courage, and they're standing in the narrows right now afraid of a corpse." She turned to look at the others, and Pip flinched, and Wren took a step back, because whatever was looking out of her eyes was not entirely the woman they'd crossed the mountain with. "They think that was the price of stopping us. They think the Bell bought them this mountain."

"Tamsin," Wren started, "what are you—"

"They're wrong," she said. "This mountain was never theirs. It was never even *his*. It belongs to everyone who was born free on it, and every one of them has been hiding, the way I hid, the way he hid me, and hiding is *over*."

She walked to the edge of the shelf, to the place where the wind came up raw off the glacier, and she filled her lungs with air cold enough to burn, and she did not ring any bell.

Bells compel. Bells are for slaves and machines and things that obey because they must. What Tamsin did was older than bells, older than crowns, older than the Edict of the Quiet Tongue and every law built on the fear of it. She reached down into the ground beneath her feet, into the roots and the dens and the frozen burrows, into the eyries and the drifts and the dark places under the ice where wild things wait out the winter, and she spoke—not a command, never a command—but a roll call.

"Bracken-foot of the birch valley, who knows every deer trail on the south face—your mountain calls you."

"Frost-ears, last of the white hares of the high snows, who has lost three litters to the hunters' dogs—your mountain calls you."

"Stone-beak of the crag nests, who watched them burn Shepherd's Hollow—your mountain calls you."

"Old tusk of the boar caves. Night-wing of the owl firs. Silver-tail, who ran the smuggler roads before I was born. Broken-horn of the elk herds. Every fox in every hollow. Every hawk on every wind. Every creature of Thornfell who has hidden, and starved quietly, and died unnamed—"

Her voice cracked on that, and she let it crack, and the crack became a howl that was not hers alone, that had the wolf in it somewhere, that had her grandmother in it somewhere, that had every silenced speaker back five generations in it somewhere—

"—*your mountain calls you home.*"

The answer came up out of the earth.

It came as a sound first, a rising tide of voices—howl and scream and bugle and drumming hoofbeat, the whole hidden choir of Thornfell answering at once—and then it came as movement. From the tree line far below, dark shapes spilled onto the white slopes like ink dropped in snow. Wolves in packs, elk in columns, bears ambling up switchbacks with their breath steaming, hawks falling out of the sky in spirals, foxes flowing through the rocks like fire through dry grass. The soldiers in the narrows, still celebrating their hard-won victory, looked up and saw the mountain itself beginning to move toward them.

Tamsin stood at the edge of the shelf with the wind tearing at her hair, and every creature on the slope could hear her now, could hear her always, the way the wolf had heard her, and she gave them the only order she would ever give them, the only one she'd ever needed:

"Not to kill," she said, and her voice carried to the farthest ridge. "To *witness*. Let them climb down through a thousand eyes. Let them go home and tell the crown what followed them. Let every soldier who rang a Bell learn what it sounds like when the mountain answers back."

Below, the column broke. Pikes dropped. Men ran, and the running men were escorted—flanked, herded, watched by hundreds of unblinking eyes—all the way down off the high snows.

Tamsin turned from the edge. Pip was staring at her with her mouth open. Wren had both hands pressed to his wound as if holding himself together. Vane had gone pale.

"What are you?" the ex-commander whispered.

Tamsin looked up the last stretch of the road, to where the light through the ice glowed green as new leaves, to the place her grandmother had kept and her grandmother's grandmother before her.

"I'm the Keeper's granddaughter," she said. "And I think it's finally time I read the rest of the ledger."
