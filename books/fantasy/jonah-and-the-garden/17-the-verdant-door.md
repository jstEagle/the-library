## 17. The Verdant Door

The snow stopped an hour before they found it, as if the mountain itself had decided to hold its breath.

Tamsin felt it first in her feet—a warmth rising through her boots, through the packed ice of the glacier road, like standing on the back of something sleeping. The wyrmling, riding draped across her shoulders like a scarf of green scale and old scars, lifted its head and made a sound she had never heard from it: a low, thrumming hum, almost a purr, almost a prayer.

*Here,* it said. *Here here here.*

They came around the shoulder of the peak at dawn, and there it was.

An arch. That was the only word for it, though the word was absurdly small. Two pillars of living root, thick as watchtowers, rose out of the snowfield and curved together overhead, woven so tightly that no light showed between them—except that light was all there was. It came from inside the wood itself, a slow green-gold pulse, like a heartbeat seen through skin. Snow did not settle on it. Wind bent around it. Where its roots plunged into the ice, the glacier had melted into a ring of black water, perfectly still, perfectly round, and in that water Tamsin could see stars even though the sun was climbing.

Pip stopped dead in the snow, fox pressed to her side, and for once the child said nothing at all.

Wren leaned on his crutch—his leg bound tight, his face gray with pain he'd refused to name since Thornhold—and stared up at the arch with the expression of a man checking his own work against reality. "It's not on any map," he said quietly. "I erased it myself. Twenty years ago, ink still wet on my first commission, and I looked at this valley and thought: *no one should find this.*" He laughed, a small broken sound. "I thought I was hiding a smuggler's route."

"You were hiding a door," Tamsin said.

"I was hiding a door," he agreed.

Vane stood apart from them, as she had stood apart since the high snows, since she had turned her back on her soldiers and everything she'd built to survive them. She carried the broken Hollow Bell wrapped in sacking across her back, and she had not slept, and she looked at the Door the way a woman looks at a country she betrayed and was betrayed by in turn. "It's real," she said. "God help me, it's actually real."

"It's real," Tamsin said. "And it's waiting."

She could feel that most of all. Not menace—patience. The Door had been waiting longer than the crown, longer than the Edict, longer than the first speaker who ever whispered to a frightened ewe in the dark. It waited the way mountains wait, and now that they had come, its attention settled over them like weather.

The blind wolf sat down at the edge of the ring of black water and would not come closer. Tamsin knelt beside him, hand on the great scarred ruff of his neck. He was old, so old; he had held the pass two days ago against thirty soldiers and paid for it in blood she'd thought would take him. He was still breathing, but each breath cost.

"Not you?" she asked him.

He turned his blind face toward the arch. In the language of images he used—the only language he had left—he showed her what he saw there: not light, but roots going down forever, and among the roots, every creature he had ever known, running free in a dark that was warm instead of cold. Then he showed her himself, small and tired, and shook his great head once.

*Not yet,* the image meant. *But soon.*

Her throat closed. She scratched his ears the way he liked, behind the left one where the old fighting scars were, and stood up.

"Right," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Wren. You first."

He blinked at her. "Me?"

"The Door doesn't want anything from you. You're not a speaker." She nodded at the arch. "Go through. Find out what's on the other side. That was always your job—mapmaker. Go make us a map of the rest of our lives."

Wren looked at the Door for a long moment. Then he looked at her, and she saw him doing the thing he did best, the thing that had gotten him imprisoned and nearly killed: reading truth and deciding whether to print it. Whatever he read in her face, he didn't argue.

"Twenty paces," he said. "If I'm not back in a hundred heartbeats, assume the worst and get Pip out of Thornfell by any other route, however impossible."

"There are no other routes," Vane said.

"Then assume the worst creatively," Wren said, and limped forward.

He crossed the ring of black water at its narrowest point, boots crunching, crutch punching holes in the crust—and then he passed under the arch, into the green-gold light, and the light took him the way water takes a stone. No flash. No sound. One moment he was there, silhouetted against the glow; the next the arch stood empty, humming, and the snow where he'd walked was already filling with new flakes.

Tamsin counted heartbeats. At sixty, her hands started shaking. At ninety, Pip grabbed her sleeve.

Then the light pulsed—once, twice—and Wren stepped back out of it.

He looked the same. He looked exactly the same, except that he was crying without seeming to know it, tears freezing in the wind lines of his face, and when he spoke his voice was strange with wonder.

"There's a valley," he said. "There's a whole *country.* Rivers, forests, terraced fields gone wild, ruins with roofs still partly standing. Empty. Empty for generations—I could feel it, the way you feel an empty house. But—" He stopped, wiped his face with the back of his hand, and Tamsin watched him decide, professionally, to tell the whole truth. "But it's dying, Tamsin. Or sleeping. Everything in it is waiting for something. The trees are all leaning the same direction. Toward the Door. Like they've been listening for footsteps for a hundred years."

Nobody spoke. The wyrmling's hum had gone very quiet.

"And it let you come back," Tamsin said.

"It let me come back because I have nothing it wants." Wren met her eyes, and she saw that he understood—he had understood since the wyrmling told them the price on the shepherd's road, and he had been carrying it silently ever since, the way he carried his maps. "It wants what you hide. And Tamsin—" His voice gentled. "You've been hiding something your whole life. We both know it isn't just the gift."

* * *

They argued about it for an hour, huddled in the lee of an ice ridge while the wind hunted them and the Door glowed on, patient as winter.

"We go back down," Vane said. "We have the Bell. We have proof of what the crown intended. We rally the villages, we—"

"With three exhausted people and a wounded dragon-lizard?" Tamsin said. "Serah. They rang the Bell at Millbrook. They'll ring it in every square by month's end. Every speaker in Thornfell will be conscripted or dead before we could walk to the lowlands."

"Then we wait. We come back stronger."

"The Door may not wait," the wyrmling said, from Tamsin's shoulders. Its broken speech had grown clearer the closer they came, as if proximity to the arch reminded it of older words. "Door opens when Keeper comes. Grandmother kept. Now grandmother's blood keeps. If blood turns away, Door sleeps long time. Maybe always." A pause, and then, with terrible gentleness: "Mountain chose you before you born, Tamsin-of-the-ledger."

"That's not a choice, then," Tamsin said bitterly. "That's a trap with good manners."

"No," the wyrmling said. "Trap is when you don't see bars. You see. Choosing anyway—that's different thing. Bigger thing."

Pip had been silent through all of it, sitting in the snow with the fox in her lap, watching the Door with those flat gray eyes that missed nothing. Now she stood up, brushed off her knees with tremendous dignity, and walked to Tamsin.

"You're scared," Pip said.

"Yes."

"Of the Door?"

"No." Tamsin crouched to the child's level. Somewhere on the burned road past Ashford she had learned to stop lying to this child; it was the one luxury her life had never allowed before, and she found she couldn't give it up now. "I'm scared of what's behind me. Of what I have to admit to walk through."

"What do you hide?"

And there it was. The question, plain as a stone in a shoe.

Tamsin looked at the child—at the feral girl who spoke to a fox because she hadn't yet learned that speaking was a death sentence, who had never hidden anything in her life because no one had ever taught her shame—and felt the whole shape of her own life rise up in her throat.

What did she hide?

She hid the gift, yes. But the gift was only the lock. What she hid was the reason the hiding had never cost her anything—had never truly *hurt*—and that was the thing she had never said aloud, never let herself think past midnight, never confessed to firelight or snowfall or the blind wolf's patient ear:

That she loved it more than them.

More than the smugglers she guided. More than Millbrook, more than her cabin, more than any human voice. When she whispered to goats on a cliffside, when the hawk rode her shoulder, when the wolf pressed his warm flank against hers in the dark—some door inside her swung open, and the world of men, with its ledgers and edicts and market squares, seemed like the smaller country. The poorer one. For twenty years she had told herself she hid her gift to survive. The truth was uglier and simpler: hiding was easy, because some traitorous part of her had never wanted to come in from the wild at all.

And if she admitted that—if she surrendered it to the Door—what would be left? Would she still be able to stand in a kitchen and love a human friend? Would she still be Tamsin? Or would she be the thing the crown said speakers were: a beast wearing a woman's shape?

"Tamsin?" Pip said.

"I hide that I'm happier with beasts than with people," Tamsin said.

It came out flat and quiet, and the wind took it, and the Door's light pulsed—once, slow, attentive, like an eye opening.

Pip considered this with the seriousness of a judge. "That's not bad," she said finally. "Fox is my best friend. Fox doesn't lie."

"Pip—"

"People leave," Pip said, with the terrible simplicity of a child who had been left. "Fox stays. Beasts stay. People make rules and bells and burn houses." She shrugged. "Wanting beasts isn't hiding-worthy. Wanting them is just true."

Tamsin stared at her. Out of the mouths of feral children and talking foxes. Twenty years of shame, and a seven-year-old had picked it up, turned it over, and found nothing there worth the fear.

Except—

Except it wasn't that simple, and she knew it wasn't, because the shame had never been about preferring beasts. The shame was about what it meant for the people she loved. Wren, bleeding on the retreat from Thornhold because she wouldn't leave him. Vane, who had given up everything and still stood in the wind guarding their backs. Pip, who needed teaching, who needed someone human enough to teach her how to be both. If Tamsin walked through that Door and let the wild have all of her, who would come back? Who would teach Pip the human words for things? Who would sit with Wren while his leg mended?

The gift wasn't the secret. The secret was that she had always been willing to abandon the human world—and the people in it—to get back to the wild. And the Door was asking her to say so. To surrender it. To stop hiding it.

Or to stop hiding *from* it.

* * *

The soldiers came at midday.

Vane saw them first—a glitter of steel on the glacier road below, a column winding up out of the pass, maybe forty strong, with the great Hollow Bell itself lashed to a sledge behind them. Her replacement had brought the mother bell. Ring it at the mountain's heart, and every beast in Thornfell would belong to the crown until it died.

"How long?" Wren asked.

"They'll reach the ring of water by dusk." Vane's face was grim and calm. She unslung the broken bell from her back and weighed it in her hands. "I can hold the approach alone for a while. I'm good at holding things. It's the rest of my life I'm bad at."

"You're not dying on a glacier for me," Tamsin said.

"Everyone dies on a glacier eventually. Mine might as well buy something." But she smiled, thin and real, and Tamsin realized with a jolt that this was what Vane looked like when she was at peace: a woman with her back to a wall and her face toward the enemy, finally on the right side of the wall.

Tamsin looked at the Door.

The column crawled. The light pulsed. The blind wolf lay at the edge of the black water, breathing in long, labored pulls, and the hawk circled once overhead and screamed down at her—not warning, not fear. *Now,* the scream said. *Now now now.*

All her life she had stood at thresholds like this one and chosen the smaller thing. Chosen silence over speech. Chosen the smuggler's path over the open slope. Chosen to bite through her lip rather than answer the Bell. Every choice had kept her alive, and every choice had made her smaller, and she had called the shrinking survival.

But the Door didn't ask her to shrink. That was the thing she finally understood, standing in the wind with the enemy climbing toward her and the child's hand in hers. The Door asked her to say the true thing out loud. Not to give the secret away—to give it *up*, to stop carrying it, to set down the weight she had hauled over every pass in Thornfell since she was six years old and first heard a mule answer her thoughts.

Surrender what you most hide.

Not lose it. Admit it.

"I want to belong to the wild more than to people," she said aloud, to the Door, to the wind, to the wolf and the hawk and the wyrmling and the child and the two broken adults who had become, somewhere on the burned roads, her family. "I have always wanted that. I chose people anyway, over and over, and I told myself it was duty, but it was love too, and I hid the wanting because I thought it made me a monster." Her voice cracked. "I'm done hiding it. I'm done hiding."

The Door's light flared—green-gold, deep as summer, warm as a hearth in a house she had never had—and beneath her feet the whole mountain seemed to exhale.

The wyrmling slid from her shoulders and reared up before her, scarred head level with her eyes, and its ancient voice was almost tender. "Say goodbye small," it advised. "Come back big."

Tamsin knelt by the blind wolf. He was too weak to stand, and she gathered his great head into her arms and pressed her forehead to his.

"Hold the pass a little longer, old man," she whispered. "Just a little longer. Then you can sleep."

He licked her chin, once, and showed her an image: her, walking out of green light, and every creature of Thornfell turning toward her—not enslaved, not summoned. *Coming home.*

She stood. She kissed Pip's hair. She gripped Wren's forearm, warrior-fashion, and Vane's, harder, and neither of them said anything stupid or brave, which was its own kind of eloquence.

Then Tamsin Thornbury, guide, smuggler, liar, whisperer, last heir of the Door's Keeper, walked under the arch of root and light—

—and the world went green.
