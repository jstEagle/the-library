## 19. The Kingdom That No Longer Exists

Wren Alcott had spent his whole life believing in the other side of maps.

That was the trade, really. A cartographer was a professional optimist. Every blank space on a chart was a promise: *here be something better*. He had drawn coastlines he'd never seen and mountain ranges he'd never climbed, and he had always assumed that somewhere past the ink there were green valleys, open roads, a country where the Edict of the Quiet Tongue had never been read aloud. The Verdant Door had been the last blank space left in Thornfell, and he had staked his career, his freedom, and very nearly his life on the conviction that it opened onto somewhere kind.

He stood through it now, and the blank space looked back at him, and it was empty.

* * *

They came out of the Door into light that felt wrong before Wren could say why.

It took him a dozen steps to work it out. The light was clean. That was the problem. Thornfell's light came filtered—through pine, through cloud, through the dust of a living land—and it arrived smelling of resin and cold stone and animal musk. This light fell from a hard blue sky onto bare ground with nothing between it and them. It hit like an accusation.

Behind them, the Door stood in its arch of root and pale glow, impossibly green against what lay around it, a single held breath of color in a landscape the color of old bone.

Tamsin came through last. Wren heard her rather than saw her—there was a new quality to her footsteps now, a softness, as though the ground agreed to hold her weight instead of merely tolerating it—and when he turned, he understood why Pip had gone still and wide-eyed. Tamsin was Tamsin-shaped, more or less. But her hair moved when there was no wind, and her eyes had gone the grey-green of deep water over stone, and when she looked at the horizon she didn't look at it the way a person looks at a view. She looked at it the way a person listens to a voice.

"Where are we?" Vane asked. Her voice was rough. She'd been hunted by her own soldiers for six days; she carried the broken Hollow Bell's frame slung across her back like a yoke, like penance.

"North," Wren said automatically, because he couldn't help himself. "Past the Door, north-northwest, the old charts put farmland within—" He stopped. He turned all the way around, slowly, the way he did when a survey didn't match the terrain and he needed to find his error.

There was no error to find. There was no terrain to match.

The land rolled away in every direction, treeless, trackless, gray-brown grass cropped short as a monk's hair, broken here and there by the ribs of ruined walls. Not ruins, exactly. Ruins implied catastrophe. These were the bones of ordinary things—a farmhouse foundation furred with lichen, a wellhead with its rope long rotted, a stone fence running straight for half a mile toward nothing at all, dividing one emptiness from another. Fences meant fields. Fields meant people. Someone had built these walls to keep sheep off turnips, and they had built them well, and then everyone who had ever eaten those turnips had vanished so completely that even their ghosts hadn't bothered to stay.

"No," Wren said. It came out of him without permission.

Tamsin looked at him. "Wren."

"It should be farmland." His voice sounded strange to himself, thin and official, the voice he used in the archive. "The second survey under Aldous the Third recorded villages here. Harrow's Crossing. The Melford steads. I've *copied the tax rolls*, Tamsin, I've seen the names—there were four hundred hearths north of the Door, there were mills on the—" He gestured at the dry channel cutting through the grass, a riverbed as wide as a road, its stones bleached white. "There was a river."

"There was," Tamsin said quietly. She crouched and pressed her palm flat to the ground, and Wren watched her listen to it the way he would consult a document. Whatever the land said back made her close her eyes. "A long time ago."

Pip had wandered a few paces out, her fox pressed to her hip. The child picked up a handful of the short gray grass and let it fall through her fingers, and then, being Pip, she asked the only question that mattered: "Where did everybody go?"

Nobody answered her. Nobody could.

* * *

They walked, because standing still among the bones was worse than moving.

Wren catalogued as he went. It was how he prayed, if he'd ever prayed, which he supposed he had, in his way: *the map is true, the map is true, if I can just make the map true again everything will be all right.* He counted fence posts. He measured the depth of doorsteps worn hollow by feet. He found a churchyard—or what had been one—its markers tumbled and nameless, and he knelt at the first stone and scrubbed the lichen away with his thumb and found not letters but a carved sign, the old speaker's mark, the same sigil that had been scratched above the lintel of Tamsin's grandmother's cabin.

Every stone in the row bore it.

"They were speakers," he said. "All of them."

"All of them," Tamsin said. She had stopped walking. Ahead of them the land rose into low hills, and on the crest of the nearest hill stood a single tree—the first living thing taller than grass they'd seen since the Door—and it was dead. Not fallen, not broken. Dead the way a monument is dead. Its branches were still spread in the shape of shade it had once given, and beneath it, arranged in a great ring, were the foundations of perhaps thirty houses, all facing inward, all facing the tree.

"A gathering place," Vane said. She knew this history too; she'd been raised on the crown's version of it. "This is where they rang the Bell the first time. The Great Ringing. My grandmother told it as a victory story." Her hand rose to the broken bell-frame on her back, an unconscious gesture, touching the thing that had ruined her. "They gathered every speaker in the old north into one place and rang the Hollow Bell until every beast for fifty miles belonged to the crown. And then—" She stopped.

"And then the beasts led the soldiers house to house," Wren finished. He'd read the dispatches. He'd filed the dispatches. He had always thought of it as a campaign, columns of figures, so many conscripted, so many executed, so many fled. He had never once imagined the houses. Thirty of them, in a ring, facing a dead tree, waiting.

"The ones who could run, ran south," Tamsin said. Her voice had changed since the Door. It was still her voice, but there was something underneath it now, a second voice speaking in unison, faint, like a choir heard through a wall. "Over the mountains. Into Thornfell. They became the smugglers, the guides, the quiet families. They built the hidden routes. They taught their children to stop listening." She looked back, once, at the Door glowing small and green behind them. "My grandmother's family. Mine."

"And nobody ever came back," Wren said. "In two hundred years, nobody ever came back to look."

"Why would they?" Vane said, and her voice cracked down the middle. "You don't go back to the place you were hunted. You don't tell your children about the country behind you. You build the Door a legend instead. You make it paradise, because your children need somewhere to run *to*." She laughed, and it was the worst sound Wren had ever heard from her. "We did it too. The crown. We need the speakers to believe there's nowhere to go. They need the Door to believe there's somewhere. Everyone agrees on the lie from opposite ends."

Pip tugged Wren's sleeve. "Is it our fault? The empty?"

He crouched down to her level, which hurt his ribs, which had been hurt since Thornhold, and he considered lying to her, because lying was his profession and she was a child and the truth was a heavy thing to hand someone so small.

"No," he said. "Not yours. Not mine, either, though I carried the maps that helped them do it. It belongs to the people who rang the Bell, and to the people who ordered it rung, and to everyone who watched the neighbors' dogs answer and said nothing." He said it looking at Vane, and Vane took it, because she'd come to take things.

"But somebody has to fix it," Pip said. It wasn't a question. Children that age believed the world came with a repair crew attached.

"Somebody does," Tamsin said.

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of the farmhouse foundation, and Wren did not sleep.

He sat up with his maps—not the royal surveys, but his own, the falsified ones, the crime that had cost him his post and nearly his neck. For eleven years he had drawn false borders to hide refugee routes, threading imaginary passes through real mountains, and he had told himself it was the best work of his life: cartography as mercy, ink as shelter. Every line he'd invented had been a promise that somewhere on the other side of it, people were getting free.

Now he laid the sheets out in the starlight and looked at what he'd actually done. Every route he'd drawn ended at the Verdant Door. Every one. He had spent a decade guiding desperate people toward a threshold, and he had never once wondered what waited on the far side of it, because the far side was the part of the map that wasn't his job. The far side was hope, and hope didn't require surveying.

Except it did. That was the whole lesson of his profession. Hope unsurveyed kills people. He had known that about mountain passes and river fords and he had somehow exempted the one map that mattered most.

"You're doing the thing," Tamsin said. She sat down beside him without asking, the way she did now, the way the animals did, appearing at your shoulder when you were least fit to be alone. "Where you go quiet and start punishing yourself in complete sentences."

"I'm auditing," he said. "There's a difference."

"What's the total?"

He looked at the maps. He looked at the dark hills where thirty houses faced a dead tree. "Two hundred years of refugees," he said, "who crossed the Door or died trying, chasing a country that stopped existing before any of them were born. And the Door let them through. It opened for them. It took whatever it took from them, and it let them walk out into—" He swept a hand at the emptiness. "Why? If there's nothing here, why does it keep opening? What kind of door leads nowhere?"

Tamsin was quiet long enough that he thought she wouldn't answer. Then she said, "It doesn't lead nowhere. Look again."

He looked. He was good at looking; it was the one skill he trusted absolutely. And slowly, the way a chart resolves when you finally find the right projection, he began to see what she was seeing—or hearing, maybe, because her eyes had gone distant and her head was tilted, listening to the ground.

The emptiness wasn't dead. It was *waiting*. The grass was cropped short because the roots were holding their breath. The riverbed was dry because the water hadn't been invited back. The land lay fallow the way a field lies fallow—not abandoned but rested, saved, kept ready by no farmer he could see. Two hundred years ago something had emptied this country so thoroughly that even the wind seemed to have signed a contract, and in all that time nothing had come to claim it.

Nothing except a Door, standing open, glowing green, patient as seed.

"It's not an exit," Wren said slowly. The idea arrived in him whole, the way the truest ideas always did, and it terrified him. "It never was. Tamsin—it's a *threshold*. Doors lead between rooms. This is a doorway between a country that forgot itself and a country that's been waiting to remember. But a threshold doesn't work from one side. Someone has to cross it. Someone has to stand in the middle and—" He stopped, because the arithmetic finished itself, and he hated the answer. "Someone has to cross it and come *back*. Carry the memory across. Wake the land on both sides at once. That's what a Keeper is. That's what your grandmother was. Not a smuggler. A hinge."

"And the hinge broke," Tamsin said. "And the Door stayed shut on this side of the story for two hundred years while everyone on my side poured through it looking for a way out." She laughed softly, and there was no bitterness in it, which frightened him more than grief would have. "There was never anywhere to escape to, Wren. There never was. All those years I hid, all those years I dreamed about the other side of the mountains—I was dreaming about *this*. Gray grass and dead fences and a sky with nothing in it." She paused. "Except it isn't nothing. It's the loudest silence I've ever heard. The whole country is holding its breath, waiting for someone to give it permission."

"Permission for what?"

"To speak," she said simply. "What else?"

Across the dead campfire's cold ashes, Pip slept curled against the fox, and Vane sat awake with the broken bell in her lap, turning it over and over like a woman learning a new rosary. Beyond her, the hills ran north into darkness, and somewhere in that darkness, Wren knew, the crown's army was still hunting them—Vane's soldiers, Serah Vane's own former command, following the trail of a defector and three fugitives who had walked through a legend and found a graveyard.

He should have felt despair. He kept checking himself for it, the way you check a wound. But what he felt instead was stranger and harder: clarity. The ugly, bracing clarity of a man who has finally surveyed the ground he's standing on. No rescue lay north of the Door. No free country, no open roads, no kingdom that had never heard the Edict. The escape had never existed. It had been a story told by the hunted to keep themselves moving, and he had drawn it, and thousands had believed it, and the belief had been the only real thing about it.

But stories, he thought, staring at his beautiful lying maps, were also the only tools anyone had ever had for building countries that didn't exist yet.

"Tamsin," he said. "I need paper. And I need you to tell me everything the land is saying, and I am going to draw the first honest map either side of the Door has seen in two hundred years." He was already pulling the charcoal stub from his coat, already clearing a space on the flat stone beside him. "Because if there's no kingdom to escape to—"

"Then we stop escaping," she said.

Down in the southern dark, faint as a struck match, a light appeared on the glacier road they'd descended two days ago. Then another. Then a dozen, a slow procession of lanterns winding down out of Thornfell, unhurried, inevitable, an army that had finally stopped pretending it might lose them.

Vane stood up. She didn't reach for a weapon; there weren't enough weapons in the world anymore. She just looked at the lights and said, in the flat voice of a soldier reading a report: "They've reached the Door."

And Tamsin turned toward the lights, and the wind that wasn't wind moved through her hair, and every crow on every dead tree for ten miles lifted at once into the night, heading south, heading home, carrying her voice ahead of her like a banner.

"Wake Pip," she said. "We're going back down."
