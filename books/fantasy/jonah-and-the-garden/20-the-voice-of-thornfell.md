## 20. The Voice of Thornfell

They came down the mountain in the grey hour before dawn, and they came down like weather.

Tamsin walked at the head of it, and she no longer had to look behind her to know what followed. She felt them the way she felt her own heartbeat now: the elk herds moving through the treeline with their antlers low, the foxes slipping along the stone walls, the ravens wheeling overhead in a slow black spiral that turned and turned but never scattered. Behind the beasts came the people—the last free speakers of Thornfell, forty-one of them when they'd counted at the Door, drawn out of attics and root cellars and the deep folds of the high country by a message carried on four legs and two wings.

*You are not alone,* Pip's fox had said in every village, every shepherd's hut, every hidden hollow where someone flinched at the sound of their own animal's voice. *Hiding is over.*

The fox ran ahead of them all now, a red streak against the frost-burnt grass, carrying the child's words to the next valley and the next. Pip rode on the wyrmling's back—she'd grown bold about it in the weeks since the Door—and shouted directions to villages Tamsin had only ever approached by night, by back paths, by the careful art of being nobody.

"Millbrook first," Pip called down. "Old Marta's there. She has goats that talk."

"I know," Tamsin said. "I've bought cheese from those goats for eleven years."

Pip laughed like that was the funniest thing in the world, and maybe it was. Maybe everything was funny now, or terrible, or both—Tamsin couldn't always tell the difference since the Door had remade her. The land spoke through her constantly, a low chorus underneath her thoughts: the river's patience, the pines' slow gossip, the mountain itself breathing under the snowpack. She had spent thirty years learning to be quiet. Now silence was the one thing she could not find.

She didn't miss it as much as she'd expected to.

* * *

Vane walked beside her.

That was still strange enough that Tamsin caught herself glancing over, checking that the woman was real and not some fever-dream of the glacier. Serah Vane, commander of the crown's beast-hunters, in a borrowed coat with her officer's braid torn off, carrying the broken Hollow Bell in a sling across her back like a dead bird. She'd carried it for six days without once setting it down. When Tamsin had offered to take a turn, Vane had shaken her head.

"It's mine to carry," she'd said. "I rang it. I'll show them what I broke."

Now, descending through the birch stands above Millbrook, Vane said quietly, "There will be soldiers in the square. The garrison never left after the burnings."

"I know." Tamsin could feel them too—a knot of fear and boredom and bad rations, human minds were so loud compared to animals, all of them shouting their small anxieties into the morning. "Let them watch."

"They'll have orders. If you're seen—"

"If I'm seen, they'll see a woman walking down a road with her family." Tamsin nodded toward the column behind them: the elk, the wolves, old Bramble the bear ambling with the patience of something that had never once been hurried, and among them the speakers, walking openly for the first time in their lives. A shepherd boy who hadn't spoken above a whisper in four years. Twin sisters from the coast who'd hidden in a sea cave until the tide nearly took them. Old Marta herself, found three days ago in a charcoal burner's hut, weeping into her goat's neck.

"And if they see more than a woman?" Vane asked.

Tamsin turned her face up to the thinning dark. A hawk—her hawk, the scarred one, the one who'd answered the Bell for her before she knew what the Bell was—circled once above her head and screamed.

"Then they see what Thornfell actually is," she said. "And they can write to the capital about it."

* * *

Millbrook woke to find its market square full of beasts.

Not attacking. Not fleeing. Just *there*—standing in the trampled snow where the Bell had been rung, where the neighbor's dog had answered and been dragged away howling, where Tamsin had once stood with her teeth clenched and her heart hammering while a herald read the Edict of the Quiet Tongue. The dog's owner, a cooper named Aldous Fen, stood at his doorway with his mouth open, watching a wolf sit calmly in front of his step as though waiting to apologize.

Tamsin walked into the middle of it, and the crowd gathered the way crowds do, at first a few faces at windows, then doorways, then the whole square, drawn by the impossible sight and held by something else—the stillness of it. Nothing ran. Nothing snarled. The beasts stood like a congregation.

She didn't make a speech. She'd thought about speeches on the glacier road, and they'd all sounded wrong, too much like heralds, too much like the Edict read aloud. Instead she did what she'd done her whole life, just not where anyone could see.

She listened. And then she gave the square what she heard.

"The river says the ice will break early this year," she said, and her voice carried strangely, doubled somehow, as though the water itself were speaking just beneath her words. "It wants the millers to move their stones back from the bank by the new moon. The pines on the north slope are sick—blister rot—and they're asking to be cut before it spreads to the healthy stand. Your goats—" she looked at Aldous Fen, and his face went white, and she gentled her voice, "—your goats remember your daughter. They ask why she stopped coming to the pen."

Aldous made a sound like a man taking a wound. His daughter had been taken by the hunters in the first week of the Edict. Everyone in the square knew it. Nobody had known the goats knew it too.

"She didn't stop," he said finally, hoarse. "She was taken."

"They know," Tamsin said. "They've been calling for her every evening. That's what the Edict took from you—not your safety. Your family is bigger than you were told."

For a long moment the square was silent except for snow sliding off a roof. Then an old woman pushed through the crowd, dropped to her knees in front of a mule, and put her arms around its neck, and the mule lowered its head and breathed into her hair, and something in the square broke open—not like breaking glass, more like thaw.

By noon, half of Millbrook had come to Tamsin with questions they'd been afraid to ask for a generation. By evening, three of them had spoken aloud to their animals in front of witnesses, hands shaking, tears running, and the sky had filled with crows who'd come to watch because crows love a momentous thing.

Vane stood at the edge of it all with the broken Bell on her back, and when a miller's boy asked her what it was, she unslung it and set it on the well's lip and let him look.

"This was going to be rung at the top of the mountain," she told him. "It would have taken every beast in Thornfell and made them slaves. I was going to ring it." She let the boy turn the cracked bronze in his hands. "I'm the proof that the crown lies to its own soldiers. Remember me however you want. But remember the Bell."

* * *

They moved valley to valley after that, and the movement grew the way fire grows in dry grass—slowly, then all at once.

Pip's fox became famous before Pip did. Children named it in songs before anyone had properly learned Pip's name; the fox would trot into a village square, sit on the well, and say in its bright cracked voice, *You are not alone. Hiding is over. Come to the mountain*, and then trot out again, deeply pleased with itself. Wren complained that it was stealing his lines—he'd started drafting a proclamation, because Wren believed in documents the way other men believed in gods—but even he admitted the fox had better delivery.

Wren himself worked nights now, redrawing his falsified maps into true ones: where the garrisons were, where the roads closed, which passes the crown had sealed and which the mountain had sealed better. He'd recovered most of the use of his wounded leg, though he walked with a stick and refused, absolutely refused, to discuss it. At night he sat by fires drawing borders that no longer existed and muttering, "Every kingdom is a fiction. This one's just been caught at it."

The wyrmling flew reconnaissance, which mostly meant flying in enormous slow circles visible for ten miles and daring anyone to comment. It had decided, somewhere in the weeks since Vane freed it, that it liked being seen. Debt paid, it announced to anyone who'd listen, debt of Tamsin's grandmother paid in full, and now it stayed because it *chose* to, which was different, and don't confuse the two.

And the blind wolf—

Tamsin still caught herself turning to tell it things. It had died on the high snows holding the pass, and she had called every free creature of Thornfell by name in her grief, and that call had become this march, this army, this morning. But grief doesn't check facts. Twice on the road she'd begun a sentence—*the old man thinks—*and stopped, and the space where the wolf should have been filled instead with the whole pack of younger wolves who had taken up its post without being asked, flanking her day and night, as though the pack understood inheritance better than any kingdom ever had.

On the ninth night, camped in the ruins of a shepherd's steading burned in the first purges, Vane sat across the fire from her and said, "They'll send the army now. Not hunters. The Fourth Legion is garrisoned at Cael Ford, eight hundred men, and the writ will come within the fortnight."

"I know. The birds have been counting supply wagons for days."

"You're not afraid."

"I'm terrified," Tamsin said. "But it's a different terror than the one I lived in. Do you understand? I spent my whole life afraid of being *found*. There's nothing left to find. Whatever comes down that road now comes to meet us, not to search for us." She fed a branch to the fire. "Fear of discovery is a cage, Vane. I've been out of it since the Door, and I'm never going back in."

Vane watched the flames a long time. "When the legion comes, they'll bring a new Bell. The foundry at Cael Ford cast three. I broke one. There are two."

"Then we have work to do," Tamsin said, "before they finish marching."

* * *

The message reached them at moonrise, carried by a kestrel that dove into the camp and landed on Tamsin's shoulder, breath ragged, speaking in the compressed shorthand of birds in a hurry: riders, crown colors, fast, on the Cael Ford road, bearing a box that hummed.

Tamsin stood. Around the fire, heads lifted—every speaker feeling it at once, the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm. The beasts beyond the firelight went silent, then turned, hundreds of eyes catching the flames, all facing south.

The second Bell was on the road. And it wasn't heading for the mountain.

"It's going village to village," Wren said, already spreading his maps, finger tracing the road. "They're not waiting for the legion. They're ringing it town by town as they come—rooting everyone out ahead of the army so there's nothing left for us to rally. Ashford tonight. Millbrook tomorrow."

Pip was on her feet, jaw set in a way that was far too old for her face. "Marta's goats. The twins' sea cave won't hide them from a Bell."

"No," Tamsin agreed. "Nothing hides from a Bell. We proved that." She looked around the fire—at Vane with her broken burden, at Wren with his maps, at Pip with her fox already standing, hackles up, reading her girl's anger like weather. "So we stop hiding *other people*. We get between the Bell and every village on that road."

"How?" Vane asked. "Eight riders with a Bell and an escort—we could take them, but the next party rings the next town, and the next. You can't guard every square in Thornfell."

"No," Tamsin said. The land was speaking through her now, loud, insistent, the rivers and the ridgelines and the roots all leaning toward the same word. "But I'm not going to guard squares. I'm going to give Thornfell its voice back—all of it, at once, everywhere. Every speaker ringing true at the same hour. Every beast answering. Let them ring their Bell in a hundred villages and hear a hundred answers come back, and let the crown learn what I learned on the glacier: you cannot enslave a country that has stopped pretending to be tamed."

Wren stared at her. "That's not a rescue plan. That's a proclamation."

"Yes," Tamsin said, and smiled, and the smile had the mountain in it. "Start writing, cartographer. We ride at dawn."

Far to the south, faint as a toothache, she felt it: the second Hollow Bell humming its hungry note into the night, calling, calling—and for the first time since the Edict, something answered back.
