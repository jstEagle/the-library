## 22. Jonah and the Garden

The foxes wake me before dawn, the way they always do on the turning days.

Not with words—Sorrel stopped speaking in full sentences years ago, now that she doesn't have to—but with a pressure against the edge of my sleep, soft as a paw, insistent as hunger. *Up. Up. She's coming.* And I rise from my bed in the Keeper's house, which is built from the stones of Thornhold's east wall, and I put the kettle on, and I go out into the garden to wait.

The garden is where the fortress used to be.

You wouldn't know it, walking through it now. The crown meant Thornhold to stand for a thousand years—black granite quarried from the mountain's own bones, walls thick enough that Wren once measured them at eleven feet and wrote *overbuilt* in the margin of his survey, which got him laughed at and then, later, got him believed. When the mountain took the fortress back, it didn't crumble it the way men would have. It grew through it. Vines split the mortar like fingers opening. Birch trees came up through the parade ground, and then alder, and then things with no name that had been waiting under the scree since before there were maps. Within three summers you couldn't find the armory. Within ten, the only stone still visible was the foundation of the great hall, and Tamsin planted the garden on top of it, because she said the ground there remembered being a gathering place and might as well remember something kinder.

That was twenty-two years ago. I know because I was nine when we came down the mountain, and I am thirty-one now, and this morning my hands are dirt to the wrist and the first frost is coming off the beds in silver threads, and somewhere up-slope, a woman who is not entirely a woman anymore is walking down to see me.

The children call her the Green Lady. I let them. It's easier than explaining.

* * *

There are fourteen children in the valley settlement this year, and every one of them born free. That phrase does work that I don't fully understand even now—it isn't a legal thing, though Wren spent a decade making sure the treaties were legal too, because he never trusted anything that rested on goodwill alone. It's something else. It means a child who has never had to learn the first rule. It means a child who talks to the goats in front of the whole village and nobody flinches, because the goats are talking back, and everyone can hear them, or almost everyone, or enough people that it no longer matters who can't.

I teach them. Not letters—Wren does letters, when his hands allow it, sitting by the big hearth with his maps spread out and his old surveyor's patience, showing them how a line on paper can be a lie or a promise depending on who draws it. I teach the other thing. The thing nobody taught me until I was feral in a cave with a fox doing my talking for me because I couldn't stop and didn't know how.

"Again," says Marta, who is seven and missing a tooth and has Sorrel's old stubbornness without any of Sorrel's grace. "Tell the Jonah part again."

"It's not called the Jonah part," I say, for the hundredth time. "It's just a story."

"It's called the Jonah part," Marta says, "because of the garden."

She has a point. The children gave the story its shape long before I did. They heard it in pieces—Wren's version, dry and precise; Vane's version, when she still came down, all edges and regret; my version, which is mostly feelings and wrong dates—and they stitched it together into something with a beginning and an end, the way children do, the way stories insist on doing whether or not the truth cooperates. In their version there is a woman named Jonah—not Tamsin, never Tamsin, because Tamsin is real and comes to visit and you don't put a real person in a story, but Jonah, who is the story's Tamsin—and Jonah hid so long and so well that she forgot she was hiding, and forgot she was hiding *from*, and finally forgot she was a door.

"A door to what?" little Tam asks. Named for her, of course. Half the children under ten are named for her. She pretends to hate it. She does not hate it.

"That's the question," I tell him, and sit down on the low wall—the last true wall of Thornhold, the one we kept, because Vane said you should keep one stone of every bad thing so you remember what it cost to be rid of it. "Everyone wants it to be a door to somewhere else. That's what we all thought, climbing the glacier. A way out."

"And it wasn't."

"And it wasn't." I pull a weed while I talk, out of habit, out of love. "There was nothing on the other side. Dead valleys, empty for generations. No kingdom to flee to. Nowhere that wasn't here." I look at their faces, fourteen of them, none of whom have ever hidden a single word from a single beast, and I try to make them understand what that morning was like, standing on the far side of the Verdant Door with the wind coming off dead land, watching Tamsin understand it. "We hadn't found an exit. We'd found a threshold. And a threshold only means something if someone crosses it and comes back."

"Jonah came back," Marta says.

"Jonah came back changed. She could still speak to beasts—that was never taken from her, that was the bargain, the Door keeps what you give and gives back what you are—but now they heard her always. And she heard—" I always pause here, because I've never found the right words and I've stopped apologizing for it. "She heard the land itself. The water moving under the stones. The roots deciding where to grow. Everything Thornfell was saying, all at once, all the time. Imagine never being able to unhear your own home again."

Tam considers this with the terrible seriousness of the very young. "Was it loud?"

"So loud," I say. "And so quiet. Both at once. Like being inside a heartbeat."

* * *

She comes down through the birch row at midmorning, and the garden knows before I do.

I watch it happen every time and it never gets ordinary. The frost lifts off the beds nearest the path first, a slow silver surrender, as if the ground is exhaling. The bees come out of the hive even though it's nearly too cold for bees. The old crow with the crooked wing—who is older than any crow should be, and we both know why, and neither of us says—drops from the alder and flies a lazy circle around her head, and she raises one hand without looking, and lands on it, and rides her shoulder like a captain's bird.

Tamsin Thornbury is fifty-three years old and looks like weather. That's the only way I can describe it. Her hair went white early and grows in long and loose, braided with things—feathers, a sprig of rowan, once, memorably, a live adder that stayed two days and left. Her eyes are the same eyes that looked at a terrified child in a cave and decided, against all her own instincts, to stay. But the rest of her has gone green at the edges, moss in the creases of her knuckles, bark-texture along her forearms where the Door's remaking settled deepest. When she stands still too long, small things grow on her. She finds this funny. She is the only one who finds it funny.

"You're letting the comfrey crowd the calendula," she says, by way of greeting.

"The comfrey and I have an arrangement."

"The comfrey is bullying you."

"The comfrey is expressing itself," I say, and she laughs, and the laugh is still entirely human, and I hold onto that every year like a coin I'm afraid of spending.

She sits on the wall beside me. The crow relocates to the hive roof with the air of one who has seen this ritual many times and finds it adequate. For a while we just look at the garden—the kale going blue-green in the cold, the late beans, the rowan saplings she planted the first spring after the reckoning, one for every speaker who died before the Bell was broken. There are forty-one rowans. She can name every tree and the person it stands for, and every year she walks the rows and greets each one by name, and the leaves turn toward her, and I have stopped pretending that doesn't undo me.

"Vane sent word," Tamsin says eventually. "From the coast. The southern lords are petitioning the new king to reinstate border seals. Something about the free settlements being 'a contagion of disorder.'"

"Wren's already drafting the reply. He says the petition misreads three treaties and one river."

"He would know. He drew the treaties and moved the river." She smiles, but it thins at the edges. Twenty-two years, and the world beyond Thornfell still sends its weather toward us—petitions, edicts, the occasional fool magistrate who thinks the Bell can be rebuilt. The Bell can't be rebuilt. I watched the land take it apart. But fools are renewable, and we both know the peace is not a possession, it's a practice.

"They'll come around or they won't," Tamsin says, which is what she always says, and which is either wisdom or exhaustion, and after all these years I've learned it's both.

"Marta wants the Jonah part again," I tell her. "The whole thing. From the wolf."

"From the wolf," she agrees. "It should start with the wolf. Everything started with the wolf." She is quiet a moment. Every turning season, this quiet. Grief doesn't shrink with time; it just learns to sit quietly in the room. "How is she buried? The pass?"

"Snowed in already. I checked with the ravens. She's sleeping warm."

"Good." She rubs her thumb along the wall-stone, over the worn place where her hand has rested every visit for two decades, until the granite remembers her. "Good."

* * *

In the afternoon I take her to see the newest thing, because I always save the newest thing for last, and because I am forty percent teacher and sixty percent show-off.

At the garden's center, where the great hall's foundation lies deepest under the soil, there is a hollow. Not a pit—a hollow, a place where the grass grows in a spiral and the light falls differently, and if you lie down in it, you can feel the mountain's voice the way Tamsin feels it all the time. We found it the year the last wall-stone sank. Tamsin said the Door left an echo of itself there, a root of the Verdant Door that traveled underground the way roots do, patient and unreasonable. She planted the first garden bed in it. Things grow there out of season. In deep winter, the hollow stays green.

The children call it the Listening Place. They lie in it in rows, arms folded, deadly serious, and report afterward on what the ground told them. Mostly it tells them about worms and drainage, which delights them beyond reason. Once, a boy named Aldis lay there and came up crying, saying he'd heard his grandmother, who died the spring before, and the ground confirmed it in the way the ground confirms things—which is to say, undeniably and without detail—and Tamsin sat with him until sundown and told him that grief is also a kind of listening, and that the land keeps what we give it, and that this is not a comfort exactly, but it is not nothing.

Today the hollow is blooming with something none of us planted: small white flowers, star-shaped, that have never grown anywhere in Thornfell in living memory. Wren pressed one and sent a drawing to a botanist in the capital, who wrote back in visible agitation that the flower has been extinct for four hundred years.

"Look at that," Tamsin says softly.

"What is it?"

"Jonah's flower." She crouches, careful of her knees, and doesn't touch the blooms. She never touches them. "The story got one thing right that we didn't plan. You bury a woman deep enough in a place, tend what she planted, tell her story true—sometimes the ground starts telling it back."

I look at her kneeling in the spiral of grass, white-haired, green-veined, half woman and half Thornfell entire, and I think about the girl in the cave who taught me the first rule: *the beasts are not witnesses, they are family.* I think about how she spent thirty years teaching herself to be small, and then one day stood on a glacier and called every free creature by name, and the mountain answered, and it has been answering ever since.

"Do you ever miss it?" I ask. "Being only human. Being able to walk into a room and have it stay quiet."

She considers this honestly, the way she considers everything, which took me years to recognize as her greatest kindness.

"I missed it for about six years," she says. "Then I stopped counting. Pip—you were there. You know what the silence cost. I paid it for thirty years, in blood in my mouth and a lip bitten through and a whole life lived sideways. The noise is better than the silence. Even on the hard days. Especially on the hard days." She stands, brushing off her hands, and the crow drops back onto her shoulder. "Besides. I get to grow flowers that have been dead for four centuries. What did being human ever give me that good?"

"Soup," I say. "You complain constantly that you can't taste soup properly anymore."

"The soup situation is a genuine tragedy," she agrees gravely, and we walk back through the rows toward the smell of Wren's bread, and the children come running, and the turning day goes on.

* * *

Evening. The children are fed and abed, all except Marta, who has stationed herself on the Keeper's doorstep with the transparent strategy of a child waiting to catch the Green Lady alone. Tamsin sees through it instantly and lets herself be caught.

"One more question," Marta says. "About Jonah."

"Ask."

"At the end. After the reckoning. After everything. Was she happy?"

And here is the thing I have learned about stories, in twenty-two years of telling this one: the questions children ask are the ones adults are too polite to ask, and they deserve true answers, and the true answers are usually stranger and better than the tidy ones.

Tamsin crouches to Marta's height. Behind her the garden breathes in the dark, forty-one rowans holding their leaves like cupped hands.

"Happy is the wrong measure," she says. "Here's what she was. She was needed. She was known—every creature on this mountain knew her voice, and she could not hide from them, and she stopped wanting to. She had a fox who grew up brilliant and stubborn"—Marta glows—"and a mapmaker who never stopped arguing with rivers, and an enemy who became a sister, and a garden growing on the grave of the worst thing anyone ever built. Some days her legs hurt and the land is too loud and she misses soup. Most days she wakes up and the frost comes off the ground to greet her, and she thinks: *I chose this. Nobody made me. I walked through the door myself.*" She taps Marta's chest, gently, right over the heart. "That's the treasure, little one. Not happiness. Chosenness. Guard it when you find yours."

Marta nods solemnly, files it away, and goes inside.

Tamsin stays on the step a moment longer. Then she looks at me, and her eyes in the lamplight are amber-shot, ancient, amused, and still, underneath everything, unmistakably the eyes of a smuggler's guide deciding whether to trust you.

"The hollow's blooming," she says. "Four-hundred-year flowers, out of season, in the middle of autumn."

"I noticed."

"Flowers don't come back from extinction for no reason, Pip. The land doesn't waste miracles on decoration." She rises, and the crow stirs, and far up the dark slope something howls—not the blind wolf, who sleeps her last long sleep under the snow, but her descendants, the pack that guards the pass, calling down toward the garden in a rising chorus. "Something's waking up out there. Under the dead valleys, past the Door. It's been stirring for three seasons now, and it's starting to say a word, and the word sounds—"

She stops. Listens. The whole garden seems to lean with her.

"It sounds like a name," she says. "And I think it might be mine."
