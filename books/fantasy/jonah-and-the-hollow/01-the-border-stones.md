## 1. The Border Stones

The last stroke of the pen was a river, and Jonah Marchetti held his breath while he drew it, the way he always did at the end, as though the ink might change its mind.

He had been working on the map for eleven days. It covered the whole of his kitchen table and hung over the edges like a tablecloth for giants: the Silverwash Delta in all its braided glory, forty channels splitting and rejoining like the roots of some inverted tree, the drowned forests marked in pale green where the water went shallow enough for herons to wade, the pearl-fishing towns strung along the coast like beads on a wet string. He had never seen any of it. No one in Corrow Vale had. The delta lay nine hundred miles east, past three mountain ranges and a desert with no name that even the crown's own cartographers refused to chart twice.

But Jonah knew it anyway. He knew it the way other men knew their own backyards—knew that the Silverwash ran shallow and quick-tempered in autumn and slow and sullen in spring, knew that the town of Vell had a crooked bell tower that leaned a hand's width south because its foundations had been laid on old oyster shells, knew that if you stood on the mudflats at dawn you could hear the tide coming in from four miles off, a sound like the valley breathing. He knew these things without knowing how he knew them, the way he knew the shape of places he'd never walked, the color of harbors he'd never smelled, the names of streets in cities whose kings he couldn't have picked out of a crowd.

He dipped his pen. The ink was cheap and brownish, cut with too much water, but it didn't matter. Ink was only ink. What came through the pen came from somewhere else.

* * *

Corrow Vale sat in the cupped palm of the mountains like an offering no god had come to collect. It was a long day's walk from border stone to border stone, north to south, and the stones themselves were older than the valley, older than the kingdom that claimed it, older than anything with a name anyone still used. They were granite pillars taller than two men, each one carved over with spirals so worn they were more suggestion than script, and they stood in a broken ring around the valley floor like the ribs of something enormous that had lain down there to die a very long time ago.

Everyone in the vale knew what the stones were for, though nobody said it plainly. Magic did not cross them. Whatever strange blood ran in a family, whatever gift or curse or oddity—it stayed inside the ring or died trying to leave. Old Marta Two-Rivers, who could find water under stone by the taste of the air, had tried to walk out once when Jonah was a boy, walking beside her husband who carried nothing stranger than a bad temper. She made it three steps past the northern stones before she collapsed, gray-faced, her gift gone from her like a tooth pulled, and she wept for a week—not for the magic, her neighbors said afterward, but for the silence it left behind.

So the gifted of Corrow Vale lived small. They kept their strangeness folded up inside them like letters never sent, and they traded what they could not use for what they needed, and they told their children the stones were a mercy, which was true, and a prison, which was also true, and mostly they did not think about it very hard, because thinking about it too hard was like pressing on a bruise to see how deep it went.

Jonah traded sketches for bread.

It was a good arrangement, quiet and unremarkable, and he had kept it that way for years. The baker's wife wanted pictures of the sea; she had never seen it and her hips were too bad now ever to make the journey over the pass, so every week Jonah brought her a different stretch of coastline drawn from memory—or from whatever lived where memory should have been—and every week she gave him a loaf and half, and stood looking at the paper for a long time before she folded it away into the cedar chest where she kept such things. The farrier wanted mountains. The widow Hollis wanted her husband back, or the next best thing, which was a drawing of the inn at Ferrow Cross where they'd honeymooned thirty years ago, rendered so exactly that when she looked at it she said she could hear the rain on the roof, and she would press the paper flat against her chest with both hands until Jonah had to look away.

Nobody in the vale thought this was magic. That was the beauty of it. A man who could draw a place he'd never seen—that was a talent, wasn't it? Like a head for numbers, or a voice for singing. The stones kept out sorcery, and Jonah's gift slipped under the wire dressed in ordinary clothes, wearing the face of craft. He had built his whole life in the gap between those two words, *talent* and *magic*, and he had kept the gap narrow and dark and well-swept, and for twenty-six years nothing had come through it to hurt him.

He finished the Silverwash map on a Thursday, in the last of the afternoon light, and signed it in the corner the way he always did—a small J.M., plain as a farmer's mark, nothing that would draw the eye—and then he sat back and looked at it for a long time, the way a father looks at a sleeping child, with love and with a fear he couldn't have put words to if you'd asked him to try.

Then he rolled it up, tied it with twine, and set it aside for the carter who would carry it over the pass to a buyer he would never meet, in a world he would never walk.

* * *

The proclamation came on Sunday, nailed to the meetinghouse door in wax-sealed scarlet, and the whole valley turned out to read it because nobody wanted to be the one reading it alone.

It was short. Crown proclamations were always short; length was for petitions and pardons. *By order of His Majesty's Justiciar of Gifts,* it began, in a clerk's careful hand, *all persons within the realm possessing, concealing, or trafficking in the uncanny arts are hereby declared traitors to the crown. Sheltering such persons is treason likewise. Rewards are offered for information leading to seizure. Seizures to commence upon arrival of the King's Hunters.*

There was more—the usual boilerplate about the mercy of the throne, the justice of the realm—but the crowd stopped reading after the third line. Jonah stood at the back of it, hat in his hands, and watched the words travel through the people around him the way frost travels through a windowpane, one neighbor at a time going still and white.

*Talents* were fine. Talents were charming. But the Justiciar of Gifts did not deal in distinctions, and the word *trafficking* sat in Jonah's stomach like a swallowed stone, because what else did you call selling drawings across the border for bread? What else did you call a life built entirely on moving the impossible from one pair of hands to another?

Beside him, old Tam Bracken spat into the dirt and said, too loudly, "Half the vale's got a touch of something. They'll be carting off grandmothers." And everyone nodded, and everyone laughed, a thin bright laughter with nothing underneath it, and no one met anyone else's eyes for longer than a second.

That night Jonah took down every sketch from his walls and burned them in the stove, one by one, feeding the fire carefully so the flames wouldn't flare and show through the window. The sea-coast for the baker's wife. The mountains for the farrier. A dozen unfinished things. The fire ate them without comment. He kept the Silverwash map. He told himself it was because it was already promised, already paid for, and that was true, but it was also because he could not make his hands pick it up and put it in the stove, and he noticed that about himself, and filed it away in the place where he kept the things he didn't want to look at directly.

* * *

They came for the Hedleys on the ninth night.

Not the hunters—the hunters hadn't arrived yet, wouldn't arrive for another week according to the carter's gossip. It was the reeve and six men from the next valley over, men with lanterns and lengths of rope and the particular careful loudness of men doing something they know is wrong and have decided to do anyway. The Hedley farm sat a quarter mile down the lane, close enough that Jonah heard the dogs first, and then the voices, and then, worst of all, the absence of voices, the sudden terrible quiet that meant the door had been opened.

Young Aldous Hedley was nineteen. He could make plants grow—not conjure them, nothing so dramatic, just coax them, lean over a row of winter wheat and breathe on it and have it stand a finger taller by morning. Everyone had known since he was seven. It was the vale's worst-kept secret and its best-loved one, because Aldous had coaxed the Hedley orchard through three killing frosts and shared his trick freely with any farmer who asked, and half the cider in the valley had passed through his hands somewhere along the way.

Jonah stood at his own window in the dark and listened to them take him. There wasn't much noise. A shout from the mother, cut short. The dogs whining and then being made quiet. Boots in the lane, going away toward the border stones, because whatever they intended to do to the boy, they would do it outside the ring, where the stones couldn't interfere—or couldn't protect, depending on which story you believed, and Jonah had begun to suspect, standing there in the cold with his breath fogging the glass, that the two stories had always been the same story.

He did not sleep. At dawn he walked down the lane past the Hedley farm, because not walking past would have been its own kind of answer, and found the mother sitting on her step with her apron wrung into a rope between her fists, staring at nothing. The orchard behind her was in full winter bareness, every tree stripped and silent, and Jonah thought of Aldous's breath on the branches in spring, and had to stop himself from reaching for his sketchbook right there in the lane, because the thought that came was a terrible one, a thought shaped like a door:

*I could draw him out.*

He didn't know yet what that thought would cost. He only knew that it arrived whole, fully formed, the way his knowledge of the Silverwash arrived, from somewhere outside himself, and that it did not feel like a talent.

It felt like the first page of a map to a country he had no wish to enter.

He went home. He locked his door. And in the drawer beneath the loose floorboard where his mother had kept the things she never spoke of, he began—for the first time in the six years since her death—to search.
