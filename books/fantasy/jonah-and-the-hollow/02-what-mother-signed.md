## 2. What Mother Signed

The house smelled of cold ash and dried lavender, the way it had for three days now, and Jonah could not make it smell of anything else. He had opened every window. He had swept the hearth twice, laid a fresh fire, set a kettle to singing on the hook. But grief had soaked into the plaster the way damp soaked into the valley's stone in autumn, and no amount of lavender or lye would draw it out.

His mother had been dead four days. The neighbors said she'd gone gently, in her sleep, with the look of a woman who had finished a long letter and finally set down her pen. Jonah wanted to believe that. He wanted very much to believe that she had not known what was coming, that the cough which had hollowed her out over two winters had simply taken her the way winter takes a late garden—without malice, without meaning.

But then there was the chest under her bed.

He had known about it his whole life, the way children know about things they are forbidden to know about: by the shape of the silence around them. His mother kept it beneath the bed frame, wrapped in oilcloth, and once a year—always at midwinter, always alone, always behind a closed door—she took it out and did something with it that made her come back into the kitchen afterward looking like a woman who had swallowed a stone and was waiting for it to dissolve.

She had never told him to leave it alone. She had never needed to.

Now he knelt on the floorboards beside her bed with the chest open in front of him and the last of the afternoon light going amber through the window, and he understood, finally, why she had looked like that.

* * *

The chest was not large. It might have held a winter's worth of wool socks, a good blanket, a christening gown. Instead it held paper.

Papers, rather—dozens of them, bundled in twine, wrapped in waxed linen, layered like the strata of some buried city. Letters with seals he did not recognize. Receipts written in ink that shifted color when he tilted the page, green to black to a bruised violet. A child's drawing—he recognized his own hand, age six or so, a crooked house with smoke curling from the chimney—and on the back of it, in his mother's careful script, a column of figures that meant nothing to him and everything to someone.

And at the bottom, folded into a square no bigger than a hymnal, sealed with black wax stamped by a signet ring whose crest was a door standing open onto darkness:

The contract.

He knew it was the contract before he broke the seal. Some part of him—the part that drew maps of realms he would never see, the part that felt the border stones humming at the valley's edge like struck bells that never quite finished ringing—knew it the way a compass knows north. His hands were steady as he unfolded it. That surprised him. He had expected them to shake.

It was written in two hands. The first was formal, angular, a clerk's hand trained in some school where they taught you to write so that no word could ever be mistaken for another:

*Let it be entered into the record of the Court Beneath the Mountain, this ninth day of the Dying Year, that one soul, unbought and unpledged, is offered into keeping—*

The second hand was his mother's.

He had seen her handwriting ten thousand times. On shopping lists. On the margins of his childhood drawings, correcting his perspective. On the letter she'd left him, tucked under the teapot, telling him where the money was hidden and that she loved him and that the bread would keep three days if he wrapped it in cloth. He knew her hand the way he knew his own face.

*the soul of my unborn child, Jonah Marchetti, born of my body, in exchange for his safe passage into the world and his mother's life thereafter. Let him live. Let the valley keep him. Let nothing cross the stones to claim him before his time.*

Before his time.

Jonah read it four times. The fire crackled. Somewhere outside, a cart rattled past on the frozen ruts of the road, and a dog barked at it, and the ordinary world went on being ordinary, indifferent, blessedly unaware that anything had changed.

His soul. Sold before he drew breath. Not to a devil out of a fireside tale, not to something with horns and a taste for riddles, but to a *court*—a bureaucracy, a ledger-keeping office beneath the mountain, with clerks and seals and a king who signed things. It was almost funny. It would have been funny if his whole body hadn't gone cold, if his lungs hadn't forgotten their work for three full seconds while the words rearranged themselves into meaning.

*In exchange for his safe passage into the world.*

He had nearly died being born. He'd known that much—his mother told it to him as a story when he was small, how the midwife had given up, how she had prayed to nothing in particular, how he had come wailing into the world an hour after everyone stopped expecting him to. He had always thought it was a story about luck.

It was a story about a debt.

And debts, his mother had taught him, were never forgiven in this valley. Only deferred.

He sat back on his heels. The light had gone from amber to copper to the bruised gray of early dusk, and he had not lit a lamp, and the contract's ink seemed to drink what little remained, the letters growing darker, more certain, more awake.

There was a clause near the end. Of course there was a clause. There is always a clause; that is the first lesson of any bargain, and his mother, who had haggled with millers and tanners her whole hard life, must have read it a hundred times and hated it a hundred times and signed anyway, because the alternative was a grave with both of them in it.

*The debt falls due upon the death of the pledgor, or upon the coming of the Debt-Herald, whichever shall first arrive at the debtor's door.*

Upon the death of the pledgor.

His mother was dead four days.

Jonah folded the contract along its old creases, with hands that were still, impossibly, steady, and put it in his coat, over his heart, where a man carries the things that are trying to kill him.

* * *

He burned the rest in the hearth.

Not because burning would help—he understood, watching the first letters curl, that a court which kept ledgers beneath a mountain did not lose track of its accounts because a farm boy fed papers to a fire—but because he could not bear the thought of the witch-hunters finding them. The proclamation had gone up on the meetinghouse door that morning, nailed there by a rider in crown colors who hadn't even dismounted: *By order of the Regent, all persons gifted beyond natural law are declared traitors to the realm. Shelter them and be sheltered no more.* Old Tam Whitlock, who could coax a scythe to cut straighter than any man alive, had been taken in the night before the ink was dry. They said his wife had screamed until dawn. They said the hunters had smiled.

So Jonah fed the bundles to the flames, one at a time, and watched twenty years of his mother's secret correspondence go up in sparks and violet smoke, and told himself it was grief that made his eyes water.

The shifting-ink receipts burned strangely. They flared green, then white, then a color he had no name for, and the smoke rose straight up the chimney without bending, as if it were being drawn somewhere on purpose, as if somewhere far above the valley—or below it—a clerk was ticking off items received.

He was feeding the last bundle—the child's drawing, the crooked house, saved for last because he could not decide whether it was evidence or treasure—into the fire when the floorboard behind him creaked.

He turned with a poker in his hand before he had decided to pick it up.

The woman in his doorway stood very still, the way soldiers stand, weight balanced, hands loose and empty at her sides. She wore no colors—not the scarlet of the hunt, though the cut of her leather coat was unmistakably a hunter's cut, and the sword at her hip was unmistakably a hunter's sword. Red hair cropped short. Freckles gone pale in the cold. Eyes the gray-green of the valley in March, and currently very wide, fixed not on him but on the hearth, on the burning papers, on the smoke rising straight as a plumb line.

"You're burning correspondence," she said. Her voice was roughened, like a road much traveled. "With the court."

"I'm burning my mother's letters." He did not lower the poker. "Who are you, and why is your door-key in my lock?"

"It isn't. Your latch is broken; I lifted it." She didn't apologize. Hunters didn't, presumably. "Wren. First Hunt, third rank—formerly." Something crossed her face, quick as a fish under ice. "As of four days ago, I'm nothing at all."

"Formerly," Jonah repeated. "You deserted."

"I was ordered to put a knife into a nine-year-old girl in Harrow Fen because she made her brother's fever break by singing to it." Wren's jaw tightened. "I declined. The captain is still deciding how he feels about that." She stepped into the room at last, slowly, showing him her empty hands, and nodded at the hearth. "That smoke. It doesn't drift. Do you know what that means?"

"No."

"It means someone is collecting. And whoever collects from this house already has your name." She looked at him then, really looked, and whatever she saw in his face made her stop pretending this was an interrogation. "They came for Tam Whitlock last night. They'll work outward from the strongest gifts. Maps, boy. Do you know what the crown pays for a mapmaker who can draw things true?"

"I draw maps of places I've never seen," Jonah said. "For bread. For travelers' gossip. They're just—" He stopped. Because even as he said it, he heard the lie in it, the lie he had been telling himself since he was six years old and his mother caught him sketching the pass through the mountains and wept over the drawing without ever explaining why. *Just pictures.* Just a gift that couldn't hurt anyone, in a valley where magic died at the border stones, where he had been promised safety—

Where he had been *purchased* safety.

His hand went, without his willing it, to his coat, to the folded contract over his heart.

Wren saw the gesture. She was too good a hunter not to. And he watched her do the arithmetic—the proclamation, the smoke, the gesture, the dead mother upstairs-turned-downstairs in the churchyard, the boy alone in a house full of secrets—and arrive at the sum.

"They hold paper on you," she said quietly.

"My mother sold my soul before I was born." Saying it aloud did not make it lighter. It made it real, the way naming a wound makes it bleed. "To the court beneath the mountain. For my life. For hers." He laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "It worked. Twenty-two years. She bought me twenty-two years, and the bill comes due now, and the crown is hunting people like me at the same hour, and I'm standing in my kitchen holding a poker at a deserter because I don't know what else to do with my hands."

"Put it down," Wren said. "I'm not here for you. I'm here because your name was in the captain's book, and I memorized the book before I left, and I've been walking all night, and I thought—I hoped—" She exhaled, and for a moment she looked less like a soldier and more like a tired young woman standing in a stranger's kitchen. "I hoped to warn you before they came. That's all. That's the only amends I know how to make."

"When?" His voice sounded far away to himself. "When do they come?"

"Dawn. Maybe sooner. They ride fast when the quarry's small." She glanced at the hearth, at the last violet embers sinking into ash. "You can't stay. You understand that. The valley won't protect you—it was never protecting you, it was *holding* you, like a coin in a purse. Whatever your mother paid, it bought time, not sanctuary. And the time is spent."

Jonah looked around his kitchen. At his mother's kettle. At the maps pinned to every wall, the coastlines and mountain ranges of realms no traveler had ever confirmed, drawn by a boy who had never been farther than the mill. At the crooked-house drawing, its edges browning in the fire, the chimney-smoke curling up and up toward a sky he was about to see the far side of for the first time in his life.

Every map he had ever drawn had been a window painted on a wall. In one day, the wall had become a door.

"I'll need paper," he heard himself say. "Ink. My pens. And I'll need to be over the border stones before dawn."

Wren blinked. Then, slowly, she smiled—not the hunter's smile, but something smaller and more startled, the smile of a woman who had expected begging or paralysis and gotten neither. "You're coming with me, then. Just like that."

"Not just like that." He took the drawing from the fire's edge—one corner scorched, the little house surviving—and folded it away beside the contract. "I'm leaving because staying is dying. I'm taking the pens because drawing is the only thing I own that the crown wants." He looked up at her, and the grief in him had stopped being a soft thing, had cooled and hardened into something with edges, something that could be carried. "And I'm going over those stones because my soul is owed to something under a mountain, and I mean to walk in and take it back."

Outside, the wind turned, and came down off the peaks carrying the first smell of snow.

Wren pulled her coat tight. "Then we'd best move," she said. "Winter's coming early this year—and so are the hunters."
