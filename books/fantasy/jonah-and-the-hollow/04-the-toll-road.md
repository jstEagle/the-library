## 4. The Toll Road

The town announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke and wet wool, roasting chestnuts, and beneath it all something sweeter, like honey left too long in the sun—a smell that made Jonah's mouth water and his stomach clench at once.

They crested the last hill at dusk, and there it was: Bellhollow, strung along a river like beads on a cord, its windows lit amber against the coming cold. Smoke rose from a hundred chimneys in straight, unhurried columns. Wagons crowded the gate road—farm carts, pilgrims' wagons, a slaver's van with its shutters painted over—and no one was turned away. No one was even questioned.

"It's a toll town," Wren said, pulling her hood lower. "Free ground. No crown law past that gate." She said it the way she said most things now, flat and careful, as though each word had to be checked for hunters before it was allowed out.

"Then we can rest."

"Then you can be robbed." She looked at him sidelong. "Toll towns don't take coin here, Jonah. Not this close to the mountain."

The gate itself was not stone but living hawthorn, grown into an arch so old its trunk had gone silver and split into doorways. A woman sat beneath it on a three-legged stool, wrapped in a shawl the color of river mud, and before her stood a young shepherd boy holding out his cupped hands like an offering.

Jonah slowed. The woman took the boy's hands in hers, leaned close, and breathed in—once, slow, like a woman smelling bread she meant to buy. Then she nodded, pressed something small into his palm, and waved him through. The boy walked under the arch lighter than he'd entered it. Jonah could see it in his shoulders, in the loose swing of his arms. He also could see what the boy had left behind on the stool: nothing visible, nothing at all, and yet Jonah felt certain something had changed hands.

"What did she take from him?" he whispered.

"A memory. One or two. Whatever the toll is." Wren's jaw worked. "I told you. This close to the hollow court, they don't want your money. They want your *past*. Coin can be stolen. Memory has to be given."

They passed under the arch because there was nowhere else to go. The memory-woman barely glanced at them—two travelers with nothing worth taking, perhaps—but her eyes lingered on Jonah a half-second longer than comfort allowed, the way a moneylender's eyes linger on a man wearing good boots.

Inside, Bellhollow was all noise and warmth. Market stalls crowded the single street, selling everything a body needed and several things it didn't: charms against bad dreams, jars of preserved summer light, ribbons dyed in colors Jonah had never seen outside his own imagination. His fingers itched for charcoal. He caught himself cataloguing rooftops for a map and stopped, ashamed. Maps had gotten people burned.

"An inn," Wren said. "We need walls tonight. It's going to snow."

She was right; the sky had gone the color of dirty pewter, and the wind carried that particular iron taste that meant weather was coming down off the high passes. They found the inn at the street's bend—the Gilded Tooth, its sign swinging on rusted chains—and inside it was low-beamed and loud, thick with peat smoke and the smell of spilled ale.

The innkeeper was a broad man with a beard braided into two fists, and when Wren set a copper on the counter he picked it up, weighed it, and set it back down with the gentle pity of a man declining a child's button offered in payment for a horse.

"Toll town," he rumbled. "You knew that coming in."

"We have nothing else," Wren said.

"Everyone says that." He leaned forward. "Everyone's wrong. Everyone's got *something* stored away. First kiss. The smell of their mother's kitchen. The name of the street they grew up on. Little things. Nobody misses the little things." He smiled, and his teeth were very white and very even, like piano keys. "Room and supper and breakfast, hot water for washing, stabling if you've a beast. That'll run you... say, one good memory apiece. Nothing you'd cry over."

Wren's hand found Jonah's wrist under the counter. Her grip said *no*.

But Jonah was looking at the fire, and thinking about the pass behind them, and the border stones, and the proclamation nailed to the meetinghouse door with his neighbor's name on it, and the ash still drifting through the valley like gray snow. He thought about how far they still had to walk, and how little of him would be left to walk it if he spent himself worrying every mile of the way.

A memory, then. Something small. Something he wouldn't miss.

"I'll pay," he heard himself say. "For both of us."

* * *

The innkeeper led them to a back room Jonah hadn't noticed—a small chamber behind the kitchen, windowless, lit by a single lamp of green glass. It held only a chair and a table, and on the table a shallow bowl of dark water.

"Sit," the innkeeper said. "Breathe out slow. I do the rest. You'll feel a tug, like a hook in soft cloth, and then it's done. Most folk don't even remember which memory went until later. That's the mercy of it."

"And if I want to choose?" Jonah asked.

The innkeeper shrugged. "You can tell me what to reach for. Doesn't always work. Memory's like eels—grab where you want and come up holding something else." He flexed his fingers. "So. What'll it be?"

Jonah opened his mouth to say *the taste of turnips*, or *the name of my third-grade teacher*, some gravel from the bottom of his life. But the lamp's green light moved on the water in the bowl, and in the water he saw—not reflected, but *shown*—a face. His mother's face. Younger than he remembered her, laughing at something out of frame, flour on her cheekbone.

He hadn't known he still had that. He had thought grief had eaten it years ago.

"Something small," the innkeeper prompted. "Quick as you like, lad. Snow's coming and I've a house full of thirsty people."

Don't, said Wren's voice in his head, though she wasn't in the room. Don't you dare.

But here was the thing Jonah understood in that moment, standing at the edge of his own past like a man at a frozen pond: he could pay with the mother who had sold him. Who had signed him away before he drew breath, trading his soul for his safe birth, and never told him, and died with the secret warm in her keeping. He could pay with *her*, and it would almost be justice, and it would cost him almost nothing, and that was exactly why it was a lie.

Because the face in the bowl was laughing. Because she had been someone before she was a bargainer. Because if he gave up the laughing face, he would keep only the signature, and the signature would become all she ever was.

"The turnips," Jonah said hoarsely. "The taste of—"

His mother's face surfaced again in the dark water, closer now, patient, the way the drowned are patient.

"—my mother's face," he finished. "That's what I'll pay."

The innkeeper didn't blink. Perhaps he'd heard stranger penances. He reached across the table and laid two fingers on Jonah's temple, and Jonah breathed out, slow, as instructed.

The tug came exactly as promised: a hook in soft cloth. And then—

Flour on a cheekbone. Gone. Laughter at something out of frame. Gone. The particular way she'd said his name when she called him in from the fields, two syllables worn smooth as river stone. Gone, gone, gone—he felt them lift out of him like splinters drawn whole from the flesh, and the strangest part was that he felt them *leave*, felt the exact shape of each absence bloom bright and then fade, until he was left kneeling at a well he knew had once held water, staring down into a darkness where something precious had very recently been.

"There's a good lad," the innkeeper said, and his voice came from very far away. "Paid in full."

* * *

Supper was mutton and barley and dark bread, and it tasted like nothing much, and Jonah supposed that was normal after the day they'd had. The common room roared around them. Wren ate in silence, watching him eat.

"You're different," she said finally.

"I'm tired."

"You're *lighter*." She set down her spoon. "I've seen men come out of that back room. There's a look. Like they've set down a pack they forgot they were carrying, except the pack was part of their spine." She leaned closer. "What did you give them?"

"Nothing important." The lie came easily, which frightened him more than the truth would have. "A smell. Something like that."

"Jonah."

"My mother's face." He said it quickly, before courage failed. "I paid with my mother's face. And before you say anything—I *wanted* to. She sold me, Wren. She signed me over to whatever lives under that mountain before I was born. Let them have her face. It's the least she owes me."

Wren was quiet a long moment. Around them the room laughed and drank, and somewhere a fiddle struck up, and the fire cracked and settled, and none of it touched the cold spreading between them.

"That's how it gets you," she said at last. "Not with pain. With relief. You'll tell yourself it doesn't matter, and it won't, because you won't be able to remember why it should." She turned her cup slowly on the table. "My captain used to brief us on these towns. Officially they don't exist. Unofficially, the crown lets them run, because—" she lowered her voice until he had to lean in to catch it, "—because everything poured out here flows downhill. To the court. These aren't tolls, Jonah. They're *harvests*. Every memory traded in Bellhollow ends up in a bottle on a shelf beneath the mountain, and the thing that rules down there grows fat on them. You haven't paid for a room. You've made a deposit."

Jonah's hands had gone cold around his mug. "Then I'll buy it back."

"With what? You saw the prices. Memories trade against memories, and yours just went wholesale." She shook her head. "And it doesn't stop at one. That's the other thing my captain said. The first one's cheap. The second costs more than you think, because you're poorer now and you know it. By the tenth you're paying with things you'd have sworn you'd die before giving up, and you'll hand them over smiling, because you can't remember why they mattered." Her eyes were very steady and very dark. "I've seen huntresses come back from leave in towns like this and not remember their own vows. Not remember *why* they'd taken them."

The fiddle swelled. Someone laughed too loudly near the fire. Jonah looked down into his ale and saw, faintly, the green gleam of the lamp in the back room, and in it a face he could no longer picture, laughing at something he would never know.

"I feel lighter," he admitted. "That's the worst part. I feel *good*."

"I know." Wren's hand crossed the table and closed over his, rough and warm. "That's how the hollow king feeds, Jonah. Not by taking. By making you glad to be less."

Outside, the first snow began to fall—fat, silent flakes drifting past the windows, erasing the road they'd come by, erasing the world one white inch at a time. And at the far end of the common room, unnoticed by everyone but Wren, a man in a gray traveling cloak set down his untouched drink, rose, and slipped out into the storm—walking, though neither of them could have said how they knew it, toward the mountain.
