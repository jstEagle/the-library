## 5. Hunters in the Snow

The snow came down like ash, and Wren thought of burning things.

She had learned to read weather in the hunt, back when she'd been someone the crown trusted with a blade and a warrant. Snow like this—fine, dry, hissing against the pines—meant quiet. Quiet meant sound carried. Sound carrying meant that somewhere behind them, on the road they'd broken two days ago, men in grey cloaks were reading their tracks the way Jonah read ink: patiently, hungrily, line by line.

"Stop fidgeting," she said.

Jonah looked up from the fire he was trying to coax out of wet wood, his hands cupped around a spluttering flame no bigger than a sparrow's heart. "I'm not fidgeting."

"You're doing the thing with your fingers." She nodded at them. He had a habit of drawing in the air when he was thinking, invisible lines only he could see, sketching the world into shape even when there was nothing to draw with. She'd caught him doing it in his sleep once, his finger tracing doorways on the bedroll between them.

"I'm thinking about the pass," he admitted. "If we cut east through the scree instead of following the river—"

"The river is the way."

"The river is *a* way. It's also the obvious way, which means it's the way they'll expect."

She crouched beside him, close enough to feel the heat off his palms, and hated how much she'd come to rely on that small warmth. Three weeks since the border stones. Three weeks since his sketch of a mountain pass had become an actual pass, real stone under her boots where the maps said there was nothing but cliff. She still dreamed about it. Walking into a drawing. The moment her foot came down on ink and the ink held.

"They'll expect the river," Jonah went on, "because anyone sensible follows water. But if we go high—"

"If we go high we freeze."

"If we go high, we live."

He said it simply, without bravado, the way he said most things, and she found herself looking away first. That was new too. In the hunt you learned to hold a stare until the other person broke; it was practically doctrine. But Jonah didn't stare to win anything. He stared because he was mapping you, she suspected, committing her to memory the way he committed everything—the turn of a valley, the bend of a river—and some traitorous part of her wanted to be drawn well.

"We'll go high," she said. "Tomorrow. Tonight we sleep low and cold, small fire, no smoke."

He nodded and fed another twig to the sparrow-flame, and above them the snow kept falling with its soft, patient hiss, like something being erased.

* * *

They heard the horses at dusk the next day.

Wren knew the hoofbeat before she knew the horse—a particular heavy, deliberate rhythm, left foreleg landing a half-beat late. She'd ridden behind that gait for six years. She'd oiled that tack, mended that saddle, sharpened that sword on a whetstone worn into a smile by a hundred years of hunters' hands.

Captain Aldric Vane.

"Off the trail," she breathed, and pulled Jonah into the rocks before the words finished leaving her mouth.

They climbed. The scree shifted under their boots with a sound like whispered names, and Wren pressed them flat into a hollow beneath an overhang while below, at the frozen edge of the river, four riders drew rein. Grey cloaks. Crown grey, the grey of prisons and proclamations. Steam rose off the horses in slow ghosts.

Vane dismounted last. He always did—let his men do the looking so his hands stayed clean for the finding. Even from thirty feet up, through falling snow, she could see him exactly as she'd last seen him: broad, grey-templed, patient as winter itself. A man who had taught her everything she knew about tracking and half of what she knew about killing, and who had never once, in all those years, told her a kind thing without a use for it.

"Wren," he called, not loudly. His voice carried anyway, the way it always did, pitched to travel across valleys and into dreams. "I know you're up there. You were always better in the high ground than any of them."

Beside her, Jonah had gone very still. She could feel his heartbeat through his sleeve where her hand gripped his arm—or maybe that was her own heartbeat, by then she couldn't tell.

"He can't know that," Jonah whispered. "Can he?"

"He taught me." Her mouth was dry. "Jonah. Listen to me. Whatever happens—if I say run, you run. If I say don't look, you don't look. Do you understand?"

"No," Jonah said. "Because whatever you're about to do, I'm not going to let you do it alone."

"There is no *alone* in this. There's just who walks away."

Below, Vane turned a slow circle, scanning the treeline, unhurried. One of his riders said something she couldn't catch, and Vane raised a hand—not to strike, just to still them, the gesture of a man conducting weather.

"You deserted," he called up. "You burned your warrant. I signed the report myself, because I wanted it in my hand, not some clerk's. Do you know why?" He paused, letting the snow fill the silence. "Because I thought you'd come back. Deserter or not, I thought you'd get cold, and hungry, and tired of sleeping next to a witch, and you'd remember whose table fed you. I left the door open, Wren. I've left it open three weeks."

"That door closed when you started hunting children," she said, louder than she meant to. The words fell into the snow.

Something moved in Vane's face—something old and tired and almost gentle, which was the worst thing about him. "Children grow up," he said. "Into witches. Into worse. I've seen what's under the mountain, girl. I've seen what they harvest. Better a quick fire than—" He stopped himself. Shook his head. "Come down. Bring the mapmaker. The court doesn't want him dead. You know that. There's a place for both of you, warm, fed, *remembered*. All you have to do is walk him down the mountain on a leash and pretend it's a road."

And that was the trap, Wren thought. Not the four riders, not the swords. The offer. Because Vane never lied—he'd taught her that too, *never lie to prey, prey can smell it*—and every word he'd said was true, and true things are so much heavier to refuse.

She felt Jonah shift beside her. Felt him reach, slowly, into his coat—for what, she didn't know. A pencil stub. A scrap of paper. Some desperate cartography.

"Don't," she breathed.

But Vane saw the movement, or guessed it, or simply ran out of patience, because he sighed—the sigh of a man closing a ledger—and said, quietly, to his men: "Take the boy alive. Wren... I'm sorry. Bring her body down. She'll want a hunter's burial."

Three riders started up the slope.

What happened next, Wren would spend the rest of her life failing to remember clearly. There was snow, and the shriek of scree, and the first rider's face appearing over the lip of the hollow—young, freckled, a boy who couldn't have been older than she'd been when she took her oath—and her knife was already moving, already in, already turning, because six years of training does not wait for permission from the heart. He made a sound like a question. Then he slid past her, down into the hollow, and did not ask it again.

The second rider she took from the side. The third nearly had Jonah—she saw the sword rise over him, saw Jonah holding up not a weapon but a *sketch*, a torn piece of paper with a drawn ridge on it, as if paper could stop steel—

And then the ground under the third rider's horse simply wasn't there anymore.

Later she understood: Jonah had drawn it. Drawn the ledge breaking, drawn the fall, quick and crude in the freezing dark, and the mountain had obeyed him. Horse and rider went down into the river gorge in a long, screaming arc, and the snow swallowed the sound.

Which left Vane.

He stood at the bottom of the slope, alone now, sword drawn, snow gathering on his shoulders like a mantle. He hadn't rushed up. He watched her stand over the freckled boy with her knife red, and he said nothing at all, and that silence was the worst thing he could have given her, because it was the silence of a man revising a report.

"Captain," she said. Her voice cracked on it. "Walk away."

"You know I can't." He started up the slope. Slow. Deliberate. Giving her every chance, or giving himself one. "You know what happens to deserters, Wren. You know what I have to write."

"I know what you *choose* to write."

He was ten feet away. Five. Close enough that she could see the whetstone-smile on his blade, the one she'd honed herself a hundred mornings, and his eyes—grey, patient, almost kind—were on hers and not her knife, because he still thought of her as his.

She thought of the children's cells in the last village. She thought of the open door he claimed to have left. She thought: *he will never stop. Not this year, not ever. Men like Vane don't burn warrants; they rewrite them.*

He raised the sword.

She put her knife through the gap under his arm, where the mail ended, where she'd once stitched the padding herself, and she knew the spot the way you know your own name.

Vane looked down at it. Looked up at her. And the terrible thing, the thing she would carry up the mountain and into the court beneath it, was that he smiled—small, tired, almost proud—as he sank to his knees in the snow.

"Good," he said. "That's my girl."

Then the snow took him too, and Wren stood in the white silence with her former life bleeding out around her boots, and understood that there was no door anywhere now, open or shut, that led back to anything she had been.

* * *

Jonah didn't speak for a long time.

He buried the freckled rider—she made him, made herself, made them both kneel in the frozen ground and lay the boy down with his sword on his chest—and after that he sat by the fire with his hands wrapped around his knees, watching her clean her knife over and over though it was already clean.

"You saved my life," he said finally.

"I killed three men."

"You saved mine. Twice. Once with the knife and once—" He hesitated. "Once before that. On the border stones. When you decided I was worth walking out of the world for."

She said nothing. The fire cracked. Somewhere far off, a wolf asked its one question of the night.

"They'll send more," she said. "Vane was good, but the court doesn't send its best after a deserter and a mapmaker. They send enough. And enough is enough, eventually."

"Then we need a route they can't follow." He was already pulling out his pencil stub, turning it in his fingers, and she saw the look come over him—the one she'd started to recognize, the cartographer's hunger, the world rearranging itself behind his eyes into lines and elevations and ways through. "Not hidden. Hidden routes get found. We need a route that doesn't exist yet."

"Jonah—"

"It nearly killed me drawing that ledge today. I know." He flexed his fingers; she saw the tremor in them, fine as a plucked string. "A ledge is small. A day's work, maybe less. But a route—all the way to the mountain, over the passes, through country nobody's ever mapped because nobody's ever survived it—that's..." He laughed, softly, and there was something frightening in the laugh. "That's the biggest thing I've ever drawn. Maybe the biggest thing anyone's drawn."

"How long?"

"I don't know. Days. Maybe more. I'll need to sleep after—I might need to sleep for a week after—but while I work, you keep watch. Can you do that? Keep watch over a man drawing a road out of nothing?"

She looked at him—at the ink stains permanent on his fingers now, at the frost in his hair, at the boy from the dying valley who had walked into a picture and kept walking—and felt the strange, terrible tenderness that had been growing in her since the border stones, the one she had no warrant for and no training against.

"I can do that," she said.

* * *

He worked for four days.

She kept the fire small and the watch long, and she watched him hunched over sheets of paper scavenged from Vane's own saddlebags—there was a bitter rightness in that—drawing until his nose bled, drawing until his hands shook so hard she had to hold them steady, drawing passes that had never existed and marking each one with a small, careful symbol she didn't recognize. A circle with a line through it. She asked him once what it meant and he said, without looking up, "It means I paid for this part," and she didn't ask again.

On the fourth night he finished. He sat back, swaying, grey as the snow, and the map in his lap showed a route up through the Teeth—the jagged range no hunter would enter in winter—threading impossible switchbacks, a bridge across a gorge rendered in strokes so confident they looked remembered rather than imagined.

"Will it hold?" she asked. "The bridge?"

"It holds because I drew it holding." He smiled faintly. "Ask me again when we're on it."

Then his eyes rolled back and she caught him before he hit the fire, and for three days he slept like the dead while she sat vigil with a knife across her knees and the map folded against her heart, listening to the wolves and the wind and the distant, patient world.

On the third morning his eyes opened, clear and hungry again, fixed on the peaks beyond the trees.

"The mountain," he said hoarsely. "We should go. Before winter seals it."

She helped him stand. And neither of them spoke the thing they both now knew: that the road home was buried under three bodies and a captain's grave, that the only direction left was up, toward the court that owned his soul—

—and that somewhere ahead, beneath the snow, something ancient was already waiting to collect.
