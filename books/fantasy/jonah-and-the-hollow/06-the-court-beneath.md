## 6. The Court Beneath

The mountain did not have a door. It had a throat.

Jonah felt it the moment they stepped off the glacier's shoulder and into the cleft Wren had been navigating by dead reckoning for three days—a passage so straight, so deliberate in its downward plunge, that no water or earthquake could have carved it. Something had *made* this opening, and whatever had made it had wanted visitors to feel small.

The dark swallowed them by degrees. First the sky went, then the blue-white glare of the snowfields, then even the grey ghost-light that lived in stone. Jonah lit the stub of tallow candle he'd carried since the valley and watched the flame lean toward the depths as if being drawn down on a thread.

"It's pulling," he said.

"Everything down here pulls." Wren's voice came flat and close. She had not spoken much since the snow, since the captain, since the red stain she'd scrubbed from her hands until her knuckles went raw. "That's how they get you. You think you're walking. You're being poured."

He wanted to ask what she meant, but the passage answered for her. The walls began to change. Rough granite gave way to dressed stone, dressed stone to something smoother and darker, veined with threads of pale light like marrow in bone. And set into those walls, at intervals of a man's height, were niches—and in the niches, jars.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. They descended past shelf after shelf of glass, each vessel glowing faintly from within with a color that had no name Jonah knew: not amber, not rose, but the color of a face you almost remember, a word on the tip of your tongue, your childhood kitchen seen through steam. Some jars pulsed slowly. Some flickered like guttering lamps. A few had gone dark as river stones, and it was those, somehow, that frightened him most.

"Souls," Wren said quietly. She did not touch them. She kept her hands folded into her sleeves as though the shelves might reach out. "I was told we'd find treasure down here. Gold. Grimoires." Her laugh was a dry click. "They never tell the hunters what the crown actually banks."

Jonah stopped before one jar mid-shelf. Inside, suspended in nothing at all, hung a curl of light shaped—barely, suggestion more than form—like a sleeping child. As he watched, it turned over. The jar warmed under his palm.

"Don't," Wren said. "Don't look too long at any of them. That's how they notice you."

But he was already moving faster now, his candle trembling, because the jars were becoming familiar. Not individually—he'd never seen any of them before—but *kind*. The light in them matched something in him, the way a tuning fork answers its twin across a room. His skin prickled. His pulse picked up an answering rhythm, two beats where there should be one.

*You are missing something,* the mountain seemed to murmur against his ribs. *Come and see what.*

* * *

The city opened without warning.

One moment the passage was narrow enough that Wren's shoulders brushed both walls; the next, the stone fell away beneath their feet into a cavern vast enough to hold weather. Stalactites hung like the pipes of some impossible organ, and between them, strung on bridges of black iron, hung lanterns burning a cold white flame. Terraces of buildings spiraled the cavern walls—houses, towers, market halls—all of it built inward, all of it facing down toward the center, the way flowers face the sun.

And at the center, on a plain of polished obsidian that mirrored the whole inverted city perfectly, stood the court: a long hall roofed in glass, and behind its glass, shelves. Rows upon rows upon rows of shelves, rising into the dark, every one of them full.

"The Hall of Accounts," said a voice, and Jonah's heart stuttered, because the voice came from everywhere—from the stalactites, from the obsidian, from inside his own skull, patient and vast and terribly, courteously tired. "Every soul in my keeping, catalogued and shelved. You have excellent timing, Jonah Marchetti. The winter ledgers close soon."

Figures moved along the iron bridges—people, or things shaped like people, pale as grubs, their faces smooth where faces should hold expression. They did not look up. They carried jars with both hands, reverently, the way priests carry relics.

Wren's hand found her knife. "Stay behind me."

"There is nothing here to fight," the voice said, almost gently. "That is the first mercy of my court. Nothing needs fighting. Everything only needs *arranging*."

The obsidian floor rippled beneath their feet—not breaking, but parting, folding itself into a stair that descended smoothly toward the glass hall. Jonah looked at Wren. Wren looked at Jonah. And then, because the alternative was standing forever on a stair that wanted them elsewhere, they walked down.

* * *

Inside, the hall smelled of cold wax and old paper. The shelves stretched away into darkness beyond counting, and between the nearest of them stood a figure that Jonah understood, in one lurching instant, was the king.

He was tall and thin as a winter branch, robed in grey that might once have been royal purple, and his face—

His face was fine. Handsome, even, in a carved way. But it was *still*. Not calm-still; empty-still, the stillness of a house whose owners left years ago and took everything movable with them. When he smiled, the smile arrived a half-second after the muscles moved, like a memory of smiling rather than the act itself.

"Cartographer," the king said. He pronounced the word with something that in another mouth would have been hunger. "Do you know how long I have waited for a cartographer?"

"I don't know who I am to you," Jonah managed.

"You are a debt." The king drifted closer—not walking, Jonah realized, but *arriving*, each position simply replacing the last. "Your mother signed well. Clean terms, fair interest. A safe birth for a soul payable at majority. She haggled me down from the standard rate, actually. Clever woman. She loved you very expensively." He gestured, and somewhere deep in the shelves, a single jar began to glow brighter, warm as a banked fire. "Would you like to see it?"

Jonah's feet moved before his mind agreed to anything. Down an aisle of bottled strangers, past souls that turned in their glass to watch him pass, to a shelf at the exact height of his heart.

The jar sat alone on its own bracket, apart from the others, as if even the archivists had sensed it was different. Inside was not a curl of light but a small, dense, roiling brightness, packed tight, alive. And it was beating. Slowly, steadily, unmistakably—a heartbeat made visible, the glass warm under his palms like the flank of a sleeping animal.

It knew him. The moment he touched it, the beat quickened, stumbling like a dog hearing its name after years.

"Oh," Jonah said. It was all he could say. His eyes were burning. "Oh, that's—that's mine. That's *me*."

"A third of you," the king corrected softly. "The rest walks around holding my ledger. But yes. That is the piece your mother sold, and it has been waiting for you longer than you have been alive." He paused, and the pause had weight, like a hand laid on the shoulder. "Take it, if you wish. Right now. Walk out with it."

Jonah's fingers curled around the glass. Warmth flooded up his arms, into his chest, a feeling so much like *coming home* that he made a sound he didn't recognize as his own voice.

"But," the king continued, "you should know what happens next. The moment you leave my hall, the crown's remaining hunters will be told precisely where you are. Not by me—I keep no bargains badly—but the mountain has many ears, and I am not the only thing listening beneath it. You will run. You will hide. And one day, perhaps this winter, perhaps ten years hence, someone will take that jar from your cold hands and shelve it here anyway, and your mother's clever bargain will have bought you nothing but a longer road to the same shelf." He tilted his beautiful, vacant head. "Or."

"Or?" The word came out cracked.

"Or you stay. As my royal mapmaker." The king spread his hands, and the shadows between the shelves stirred, and Jonah saw—projected in the cold lantern-light, vast and turning—maps. Maps of kingdoms Jonah had only ever dreamed of drawing. Coastlines no valley-born hand had ever charted. Mountain ranges with passes marked in gold. Cities stacked on cities, realms layered on realms, the whole living architecture of the world rendered in ink and light. "You have walked your drawings since you were a child. Imagine what you might walk with my archives. Every border ever drawn. Every realm ever dreamed. I will give you the world to map, Jonah Marchetti, and the world will hold still for you. No hunter will touch you. No winter will stop you. Your friend may stay or go as she pleases—my hospitality does not extend to deserters, but neither does my interest."

"And the price," Jonah said. His hands were still on the jar. He could feel his own heartbeat through the glass, doubled, calling to him. "There's always a price. The toll road taught me that."

"The price," the king said, "is service. Years of it. Map for me as your mother mapped out your safety, and when our term ends, you walk out of here with every piece of yourself, plus everything you've earned." He smiled again, that delayed, hollow smile. "I am not a monster, cartographer. I am a *banker*. Banks do not break what they invest in. Look at these shelves—" he swept a hand at the endless dark, the thousands of sleeping lights, "—every soul here was *sold*. By mothers, by kings, by desperate men at crossroads. I have never taken one that was not freely given. Whatever stories they tell about me above the snow, remember: I only ever hold what people surrender."

And the terrible thing—the thing Jonah would turn over in his mind for nights afterward—was that it sounded like mercy. It sounded like the kindest voice he had ever heard. Standing there with his own heart warm under his palms, exhausted and hunted and so tired of running, he felt the offer settle over him like a blanket after fever, like his mother's arms, like sleep.

*Say yes,* whispered something that was either the jar or his own longing. *Say yes and stop being afraid.*

Beside him, Wren had gone very still. "Jonah," she said quietly. "Look at his shelves. Ask him why none of the lights ever leave."

But the king was already extending his hand, pale and elegant, an open palm with nothing in it—which was, Jonah understood dimly, exactly the point. Nothing given. Nothing forced. Only a door, held open, into warmth.

"All I require," the king said, "is your signature."

Jonah reached for the stylus floating patiently at his elbow—and froze.

Because in reaching, his sleeve had pulled back, and from the top of his boot, a corner of paper showed. Folded small, soft with handling, creased from weeks of being carried against his skin.

A sketch. He couldn't remember drawing it. He couldn't remember anything at all just then except the sudden, irrational certainty that whatever was on that paper, he had promised himself—some earlier self, some self he no longer entirely was—that he would not sign while it was in his boot.

The king's empty eyes followed his gaze down.

"Ah," said the hollow king, and for the first time, something moved behind his face.
