## 7. A Kingdom Drawn

Wren did not sleep. She told herself it was the cold, the stone, the strange amber light that never quite dimmed in the corridors beneath the mountain—but that was a lie, and she had promised herself she would stop lying when she deserted the hunt. The truth was simpler and worse: Jonah had said *yes*.

She heard the quill through the wall of their guest chamber. Not scratching—*singing*, a thin silver note like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass, going on and on into the small hours while the mountain hummed around them like a struck bell that refused to finish its tolling. Once, near midnight by her reckoning, she pressed her ear to the stone and felt the whole mass of rock above them shudder, as if something vast had turned over in its sleep.

The hollow king's servants had given Jonah everything. A desk of black wood polished to the depth of still water. Inks in stoppered vials—red that moved, gold that whispered, a green so dark it seemed to drink the candlelight. A chair carved with antlers. And maps, dozens of them, the kingdom rendered in the old court style with borders drawn in faded thread-stitch, waiting for a new hand.

*Redraw them*, the king had said, his voice like paper turning over on itself. *Make the kingdom true again. Do this, and your soul goes back in your chest, warm and beating, and you will want for nothing under my roof.*

Wren had watched Jonah's face when he said yes. She had looked for hesitation, for the boy who traded sketches for bread, who blushed when she caught him staring at the border stones like a man staring at the sea. What she saw instead was hunger wearing his face like a borrowed coat. He had touched the desk the way a starving man touches bread.

So she sat up through the singing of the quill, and she sharpened her knife, and she did not know which of them the blade was for.

* * *

Near dawn—or what passed for dawn down here, where the light came up gray-gold through veins of crystal in the ceiling like frost forming in reverse—the singing stopped.

Wren went to him.

The map room opened off their chambers through an archway hung with beads of smoked glass, and she pushed through them into air that smelled of hot metal and snowmelt and something sweeter underneath, like crushed mint. Jonah stood over the great table where the kingdom lay spread in all its new-drawn glory, and he was swaying slightly, smiling, ink to his elbows, ink at the corner of his mouth where he'd pushed back his hood with a stained hand.

"Look," he said softly. "Wren. Look what I made."

She looked.

The kingdom beneath the mountain had always been a wound in her understanding of the world—a city grown sideways out of the dark, its borders ragged as torn cloth, its territories disputed in a dozen places by rebel holds and free villages that had never sworn to any crown. She had seen those villages on the old maps during her years in the hunt. Ashford-in-the-Deep. The Two Sisters. Bellhollow. Places that grew mushrooms and kept goats and paid no taxes because no tax collector could find them, tucked into folds of stone where the mountain's veins forked and tangled.

On Jonah's new map, the borders ran clean as cut silk. Every hold sworn. Every road accounted for. And where three villages should have been—

Nothing. Smooth parchment. Unmarked country, blank as an egg.

"Ashford," Wren said. Her voice came out flat. "Where is Ashford?"

Jonah blinked at her, slow and pleasant, like a man waking from good sleep. "Ashford?"

"The village. Bellhollow. The Two Sisters." She crossed to the table and put her finger on the blank space, pressing hard enough to whiten her knuckle. "Here. They were *here*. You drew them out."

"I redrew the borders," Jonah said, and there was something in his voice now, a patience that was worse than anger, the tone one uses with a child or a fool. "That's what he asked. That's what I agreed to. The kingdom is cleaner now. Truer. You can see how much better it looks."

"It doesn't look better, it looks—" She stopped. Breathed. "Jonah. Three hundred people live in those valleys. Goat farmers. Mushroom growers. Children."

He tilted his head, and for one horrible moment Wren saw him actually try to remember—saw the effort move behind his eyes like a fish under ice—and then the effort failed, and he smiled again, easy and untroubled.

"They can't have mattered very much," he said. "Or I'd remember why I erased them."

* * *

She made him walk with her. Down through the crystal-lit corridors, past shelves of bottled souls that glowed faintly in their rows like wasps' nests made of lantern-light, past servants whose faces were smooth as the backs of spoons, until they reached the gallery of windows.

There were no windows in the mountain. Everyone knew that. But the king's gallery held windows anyway—great panes of something like glass, and behind them not sky but *renderings*. The kingdom painted in miniature, alive, moving: smoke rising from chimneys, goats on scree slopes, laundry snapping in wind that existed nowhere but inside the frames.

Three of the panes had gone dark overnight.

Not broken. Not empty. Just... dark, the way a mirror goes dark when you breathe on it, except the fog did not fade. Wren stood before the middle pane with her palm flat against the cold surface and felt nothing behind it. No draft. No life. The pane showed only a faint gray suggestion of valley walls, and even those were fading even as she watched, softening like a memory of a place she'd never been.

"The map doesn't just record," she said quietly. "It decides. You didn't erase them from the drawing, Jonah. You erased them from the world."

"That's not—" He stopped. His smile flickered, guttered, came back wrong. "The king said the rebels were a sickness. He said—"

"The king eats memories. You've seen his shelves. And you took his word for who deserved to exist?"

"He offered me my soul back." For the first time something cracked in Jonah's voice, a hairline fracture running through the pleasantness. "Do you know what it's like, Wren? Walking around with a hole in your chest where you should be? Every day since the border stones I've felt it leaking out of me. He can give it back. He *said* he would. One night of work, and I'm whole—"

"You're not whole." She grabbed his ink-stained wrist and held it up between them, and his hand was trembling, though his face wasn't, and that discrepancy frightened her more than anything else she'd seen beneath the mountain. "Look at yourself. You don't remember Ashford. You barely remember—" She stopped herself. Swallowed it.

"Remember what?" he asked.

And there it was. The question hanging between them like a blade on a wire. Because she had spent weeks watching him, sleeping in shifts beside him, bleeding beside him, and somewhere on the frozen passes she had started to believe that what lived between them was a thing worth naming, and she had thought—he had seemed to think it too—that whatever they were building was being built out of shared remembering. Out of the pass where they'd first crossed. Out of the toll-road market where he'd sold his mother's face and she'd held him while he shook. Out of her captain's blood on the snow and the four days she'd nursed him after, mapping his escape route while he slept.

If he had lost the villages, what else had the ink taken? What else had the king skimmed off the top like cream?

"Why do you stay with me?" she asked.

Jonah frowned—not confused, exactly. More like a man consulting a ledger and finding the columns balanced but the reasons for the arithmetic gone. "Because we came here together," he said slowly. "Because... you're the one who knows the way things are. Because—" He paused, and she watched him reach, watched him come up with handfuls of true facts and none of the feeling that should have been wrapped around them. "I trust you. I think. I believe I do."

*I believe I do.* As if trust were a rumor he'd heard about himself.

Wren let go of his wrist. Stepped back. The dark window breathed its dead gray breath against her shoulder blades, and she understood suddenly, completely, with the cold clarity of a huntress reading sign, what the king had done. He hadn't lied about the trade. He'd simply built the price into the work. Every border redrawn cost the cartographer the reasons he'd ever have wanted anything else. Every line of clean silk erased a little more of the man holding the pen. By the time Jonah finished the kingdom, there would be nothing left of him but skill and obedience—a perfect instrument, a royal mapmaker with no memories left to contradict the crown.

The king wasn't buying Jonah's service.

He was eating him one border at a time.

"Go back to your desk," Wren said, and her voice was steady, and she hated how steady it was, how much it sounded like her captain's voice, the voice she'd deserted rather than obey. "Finish your map. Smile at the servants. Eat what they bring you."

Jonah stared at her. "Wren—"

"And tonight, when the mountain sleeps, you're going to tell me everything you can remember about the day we met. Everything. And if you can't remember enough, then we'll know how far along you are, won't we? We'll know how much of you is left to save."

Something moved behind his eyes then—far down, deep, like a light in the bottom of a well. Not memory, maybe. But the shape where memory used to be. The ache of a missing tooth the tongue keeps returning to.

"All right," he said quietly.

* * *

She left him at the archway of smoked glass beads and walked the long way back, past the soul-shelves, and there she stopped, because habit made her count them the way a soldier counts exits.

Row upon row upon row. Thousands of jars, each glowing its private color, each murmuring its private name. Somewhere among them was Jonah's—warm and beating, the king had promised, kept safe, kept *close*. Kept as collateral. Kept as bait.

But as she stood there, counting, Wren noticed something she had been too afraid to notice before. The shelves nearest the king's throne were older. Their jars were dusty, their glow gone gray, their contents long since drunk dry. And set apart from all the rest, on a shelf of black iron bolted directly into the living rock, stood a single jar larger than the others, sealed with seven bands of lead, and it did not glow at all.

It was empty. It had been empty for a very long time.

And scratched into the dust on its shoulder, in a hand that shook, someone had written a single word:

*Upward.*

Wren read it twice. Then she looked up—past the shelves, past the vaulted crystal dark, toward the deep heart of the mountain, where something older than the hollow king waited with a debt of its own to collect.

Behind her, far away in the map room, the quill began to sing again.
