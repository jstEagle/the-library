## 8. The Empty Shelf

The days in the mountain court had a way of folding into one another, like pages pressed too long beneath a weight.

Jonah woke each morning in a chamber of white stone that remembered no one—not him, not the servants who lit the braziers, not the king whose footsteps passed his door at the same hour every night, soft as snowfall. He woke, and he worked, and the work was glorious, and that was the trap of it. No one had chained him to the drafting table. He had sat down willingly, ink-stained and eager, and the kingdom had unrolled itself before him like a lover opening her hands.

He drew roads where the crown wanted roads. He drew rivers bending politely around garrisons. He drew, on the fourth day or perhaps the ninth, a border that placed three villages outside the map entirely, and he felt nothing at all when he did it—only the clean satisfaction of a line well made.

That should have frightened him. He understood, distantly, that it should have. But fear required a self to be afraid *for*, and Jonah's self had grown thin as vellum, scraped and stretched until it held only what the king had left upon it: the gift, the work, the slow warm pride of being needed.

*You are my finest instrument,* the hollow king had said, standing over him in the map-room with his face like a mask carved by someone who had forgotten faces. *Instruments are cared for, Jonah Marchetti. Instruments are kept.*

Kept. The word had sounded, for one shining moment, like love.

* * *

It was the boot that saved him. Or rather, what the boot had carried all this time without his knowing.

The chamber was cold that night—the deep cold of the mountain working its way through the stone—and Jonah knelt to pull off his boots, meaning to sleep. His fingers found a stiffness in the left one, a fold of paper wedged between sole and leather where no paper had any right to be. He pried it loose. It was soft with sweat and wear, creased into a small hard square, and when he unfolded it by brazier-light, his own hand looked back at him.

A sketch. Quick, unfinished, done in the margin of some other page and torn free—a woman's profile in three-quarter view, hair escaping its braid in stubborn dark wisps, a scar cutting through one eyebrow, and an expression he could only call *unimpressed*, drawn with such affection that the affection itself seemed to bleed through the graphite.

Beneath it, in his own handwriting, smaller: *Wren. She laughs at me. I want to keep that.*

He did not know her.

That was the first thing—the absence where a person should have been, a Wren-shaped hole in him with edges so clean they could only have been cut. He turned the paper over. Blank. He held it closer to the light, searching the lines for something familiar, and found instead a strange wrongness in himself: his eyes slid off the drawing the way water slides off oiled cloth. Some part of him had been taught not to look. Some part of him had hidden this from himself, folded it into leather and sweat and the blind spot of his own attention.

*She laughs at me.*

His chest hurt. That was the second thing. Not grief—he had no memory to grieve—but the ghost of grief, the socket where a tooth had been, aching at weather that never came.

And then, through the ache, a crack ran up through the smooth white walls of the king's hold, thin as a hairline fracture in porcelain, and Jonah heard—faintly, from very far away—his own voice saying words he had no record of saying: *I won't let them have you too.*

He sat on the cold floor a long time, holding the sketch, letting the crack widen.

* * *

The king's hold did not break all at once. It broke the way ice breaks on a spring river—one sound, then silence, then everything groaning.

By morning Jonah remembered fragments. Not Wren herself—she stayed a name and a face and an ache—but *himself*. He remembered the valley. The border stones gray in the rain. Bread traded for sketches, his mother's kitchen smelling of tallow and flour, the proclamation nailed to the meeting-house door with its black wax seal. He remembered burning papers in a hearth while a huntress watched him from the doorway with a sword she hadn't yet decided how to use.

He remembered why he had come beneath the mountain. His soul. A debt signed before his birth, in ink his mother had wept over—or maybe she hadn't wept; maybe that was a kindness he'd invented. The jar. He had seen the jar, warm and beating like a heart in its niche among hundreds of others, and the king had offered him a trade dressed as mercy, and somewhere in the offering there had been a hook, and Jonah had swallowed it whole.

*I accepted,* he thought, standing in the map-room amid his own beautiful lines, and the thought arrived without horror, which was itself horrifying. *I accepted, and I don't even remember choosing.*

The three villages. He went to the great map and found the place where they had been—or rather, found the smooth blank parchment where they were not, erased so thoroughly that even the mountains around them seemed to lean away, embarrassed. Three villages. He did not know their names. He suspected he had known them once, and that knowing had gone into the king's shelves along with everything else, filed and stoppered and labeled in a hand like frost.

*What did I trade?* he asked the empty room. *What did I pay?*

The room answered with silence, and the silence had a shape, and the shape was a shelf.

* * *

He went looking for answers the way he did everything now—with a pencil in his hand.

The soul-vaults lay below the map-rooms, down stairs worn concave by centuries of feet that had climbed them carrying jars and returned carrying nothing. The guards let him pass; the king's instrument went where it pleased. And the vaults opened around him like the inside of some vast pale throat—shelf upon shelf upon shelf, rising into darkness, each niche holding a single vessel of smoked glass, and within each vessel a light that moved.

Not souls, quite. He understood that now, standing among them. Souls were the whole of a person, indivisible. These were pieces. Memories, mostly—the vaults were full of memories, bought and bargained and harvested, each one glowing with the particular color of what it held. Amber for childhoods. Deep blue for griefs. One jar near the aisle's end burned a fierce embarrassed red, and Jonah did not let himself wonder what someone had sold that they'd been glad to lose.

He found his own niche on the third gallery. His jar still beat, slow and patient, like a heart waiting out a long winter. Beside it, a label in the frost-hand:

*MARCHETTI, JONAH — PAID IN FULL — SEE LEDGER*

Paid in full. But paid *with what*? His mother's coin had bought his safe birth; the king's coin had bought his service. Somewhere between those two transactions, Jonah Marchetti had been spent like currency, and he was only now counting the change.

He took the ledger from its stand. It fell open, obliging, to a page near the end, and there in the frost-hand was the accounting of his own dismantling:

*Face of mother — tendered, market town, toll road.*
*Reason for flight (three villages) — tendered, voluntary.*
*Name of huntress — tendered, voluntary.*
*Circumstances of tenders above — tendered, voluntary.*

Voluntary. Every line said *voluntary*. He read it four times before he understood what he was reading: the king had not stolen anything. The king had arranged matters so that Jonah would hand over each piece himself, freely, gladly—and then had purchased Jonah's memory of the handing-over, so that the man walking out of the vaults would believe himself intact.

*That is how you hollow a man,* said a voice behind him, low and dry as ash settling. *You don't take. You let him give, and then you take the giving.*

The king stood at the end of the gallery, robed in gray, his face doing its best impression of a face. Jonah had thought, these past weeks, that the hollowness was appetite—the hunger of a thing that fed on memory and was never full. Now, with the crack running through him, he saw differently. The face wasn't hungry. It was *waiting*. It had the patience of something that had already lost everything and was merely managing the estate.

"You sold your own soul," Jonah said. "Didn't you. That's why the vaults exist. That's why you need us."

Something moved across the king's features—too fast to be called an expression, more like wind over a field of dead grass. "Clever instrument," he said. "I told them you would be."

"Them?"

"The ones below." The king's gaze went past Jonah, past the shelves, down through the floor as though the stone were glass and he could see all the way to whatever waited at the bottom of the world. "You think I built this court? I inherited it. I was a mapmaker too, once—in another kingdom, under another sky. I came here to bargain, as you did. I gave everything I had, and it was not enough, because it is never enough. So I learned the trade from the other side." He gestured, one gray sleeve sweeping the endless shelves. "Every jar is a payment made upward. Every memory harvested keeps the ledger balanced a little longer. I am not a king, Jonah Marchetti. I am a *collector*. And collectors are collected."

"The chain of debts," Jonah said slowly, "has a bottom."

"It has a bottom." For a moment—just a moment—the king's voice cracked, and underneath it was something old and tired and terribly human. "And the bottom is hungry. And winter comes, and the passes seal, and the bottom expects its due. This year the due is larger. This year—" The face reassembled itself, smooth and blank. "This year I have acquired a most remarkable instrument, and remarkable instruments buy much."

"No." Jonah's hand closed around the sketch in his pocket, the creased square of paper, the woman who laughed at him. The crack in the king's hold widened, and through it poured everything the fragment had been holding back: the pass drawn real where no path existed, snow, blood on snow, a captain falling, Wren's face above him in the dark saying *stay with me, mapman, stay with me.* "No. I know what I am now. I know what you took and how you took it. I'm not your instrument."

"Instruments always say that," the king said, almost gently. "Right up until the moment they are played."

But Jonah was already moving—backing toward the stair, the ledger's truth ringing in him like a struck bell. *Paid in full. Voluntary. Voluntary. Voluntary.* If the takings had been voluntary, then perhaps the *returnings* could be too. If a man could give away his memories one by one—

Then perhaps a man could take them back.

Behind him, in the dark of the third gallery, his jar beat on, patient as a heart. And far below the vaults, below the shelves, below the bottom of the chain, something older than the king shifted in its sleep—and Jonah felt it stir against the soles of his feet, faint as a pulse through stone, waking early, waking hungry, waking because a cartographer had finally read the fine print of the world.

Winter was coming. The passes would seal in weeks.

He had a soul to steal back, a huntress to remember, and a kingdom of bottled lights to set free—and now he knew exactly where the bottom of everything was buried.
