## 9. Winter Falls

*Jonah*

The first snow came down through the mountain's throat like ash from a burned book.

I stood at the window of the map-room I had been given—had *earned*, the king said, as though a kingdom were wages—and watched it fill the great chimney of the world. The court beneath had no seasons of its own. It borrowed them. When winter sealed the passes above, the hollow city went dark and quiet as a held breath, and every door in the mountain was locked by ice that even the king's servants could not argue open.

That was when I understood why he wanted me.

Not for my hands. Not for the borders I could redraw or the rebel villages I had already unmade—I still carried the shame of that like a stone in my boot, though I could not remember lifting it. He wanted me because in three days, maybe four, the last pass would close, and whatever bargain he had struck with the thing older than himself would come due, and he needed a cartographer who could draw him a road out of a place with no roads.

He thought I didn't know. Hollow men are careless with their secrets; they have sold the parts of themselves that kept secrets well.

But I knew. The sketch in my boot had told me—a scrap of paper creased soft as cloth, a woman's face drawn in six quick lines, and beneath it, in handwriting I did not recognize as mine but could not deny: *Wren. Do not forget her. She is the only true thing.*

I did not remember drawing it. I remembered nothing about her except that the paper was warm, warmer than skin, as if the ink itself were alive and beating. And that warmth was enough. It was a splinter under the king's fingernail, and all night while he slept his dreamless sleep I worked it deeper.

In the cellar below the throne room stood the shelves. I had seen them on our descent—rows upon rows of glass jars, each one lit from within by a slow pulse of light, each one a soul someone had sold or lost or gambled away. My own jar had been there once. Warm. Beating. I had felt its absence in my chest ever since, a hollowness shaped exactly like home.

Tonight I went down to them with a hammer wrapped in my cloak, and I told myself I was doing it for Wren, whose face I could not recall but whose name tasted like an oath.

The first jar I broke was my own.

* * *

*Wren*

They kept me in the guest wing, which is a polite word for a cage with better tapestries. Hollow courtiers drifted past my door all evening, their faces smooth as river stones, asking questions they already knew the answers to. *Do you sleep well, huntress? Do you dream?* They asked because they envied it. Dreams were the one currency they couldn't mint.

I counted the hours by the snow. Above us the passes were closing; I could feel it in the way the mountain groaned, a long tectonic sigh, stone settling into its winter grave. By morning there would be no way out for anyone. Whatever Jonah meant to do—and I had seen it building in him for days, that terrible quiet, the way he touched the sketch in his boot like a man touching a wound—he meant to do it before the ice finished its work.

I should have stopped him. I told myself I should have stopped him. But I had killed my captain to save this man, and I had burned my own name off the crown's rolls, and there was no version of me left that chose safety over him. So instead I sharpened my knives, both of them, and I waited, and I listened to the snow bury the world.

At midnight the lights in the corridors began to gutter.

Not fail—*gutter*, the way candles do when something breathes near them. The hollow court ran on stolen memory; I had watched the courtiers feed at the king's table, ladling recollections out of jars into themselves like soup. If those jars were breaking, then everything the court was made of was coming loose.

I heard the first scream around the second bell. It was not a scream of pain. It was worse. It was a scream of *returning*—a courtier in the east gallery suddenly remembering a mother, a childhood, a debt unpaid, forty years of a life crashing back into a body that had been built to hold none of it. I heard him weep. Grown men do not weep like that unless something has come home to them.

Then the second scream. The third. A chorus now, spreading through the mountain like fire through dry grass, and underneath it a sound I will hear until I die: glass breaking, jar after jar after jar, and each broken jar releasing a pulse of light that rose through the stone like a soul going home.

I put on my boots and went toward the noise. Toward him. Of course toward him.

* * *

*Jonah*

The cellar was drowning in light.

Every jar I broke released what it held—not just souls but *memories*, whole weather systems of them, pouring up through the cracks in the floor and into the dark. I broke them with the hammer and then, when my arm failed, with a shelf levered loose and swung like a battering ram, and the light rose around me so thick I could barely see my own hands. Hundreds of lives. Farmers' summers. Sailors' storms. Children's names. All of it climbing, climbing, finding its owners somewhere in the world above and below, flooding back into bodies that had forgotten they were missing anything.

It should have been holy. Some of it was. But some of it was horror, because a soul returning after decades does not arrive gently. In the galleries overhead people were being remade against their will, stitched back together with years they had sold gladly and now received like a sentence. I heard a woman beg the empty air to take it back. I heard a man laugh and sob in the same breath.

And I heard the king.

He came down the stair like a landslide wearing robes, and his face—which had always been smooth, featureless, a blank oval of pale stone—was beginning to crack. His own memories were rising to meet him. The thing older than the mountain had held his soul for nine hundred years, and now, freed, it was flying home to him through the broken dark, and he was not ready for it. No one could be ready for nine hundred years arriving at once.

"You," he said, and his voice was still beautiful, still the voice that had offered me a kingdom over supper. "You foolish, magnificent boy. Do you know what you've done? There was an order to things. There was a *chain*—"

I swung the shelf-leg into the last row of jars, the oldest ones, the ones sealed with lead and wax and sigils I couldn't read. They shattered all at once, and the light that came out of them was different—older, denser, the color of deep water.

The king stopped mid-sentence.

I watched it happen. I will carry the watching of it even if I carry nothing else. One moment he was speaking; the word in his mouth was *bottomless*. The next, his memories reached him—all of them, the whole drowned cargo of his centuries—and there was too much. There is always too much. A hollow thing filled all at once doesn't become whole. It becomes full, and then it becomes gone.

His face cracked like lake ice, and through the cracks I saw flashes of what he had been: a young man with a crooked smile, a soldier, a beggar at a gate, a hand signing a contract in a language older than kingdoms. Then the cracks widened, and the light poured *out* of him instead of in, and he came apart mid-word, mid-breath, unraveling like a rope cut at both ends. What fell to the cellar floor was a robe, a crown, and a drift of pale dust that the rising souls carried away with them, grain by grain, up through the mountain, out into the snow.

The chain of debts had a bottom. I had found it. It was made of glass.

* * *

*Wren*

I found him standing in the ruin of it, hammer still in hand, lit from below by the last few pulses of light rising past him like bubbles in dark water. He looked smaller than any man I had ever seen look after winning. He looked like a man who had spent himself entirely and knew the bill was coming.

"Jonah." His name in my mouth felt like the only solid thing in the mountain.

"Wren." He turned, and for a moment—the sketch in his boot had bought him this much—he knew me. His eyes found my face and held it, and I saw him trying to gather whatever fragments remained, the way a man cups water in shaking hands. "I remember—" He stopped. Started again. "There was a bridge. You taught me to skate on black ice, you said the trick was to never stop moving. Was that real?"

"That was real." My throat closed around it. "Two winters ago. You fell through your own confidence and I pulled you out."

"Good." He nodded slowly, filing it away somewhere I could see was nearly empty. "Keep that. Keep all of it. Because I don't know how much longer I'll—"

The mountain shuddered.

Above us, the weight of the freed memories had loosened something in the stone itself, and the long winter, which had been sealing the passes with patient ice, now sealed them with falling rock. Corridors collapsed two floors up. The great chimney of the world, the throat the snow fell through, choked and roared. Every servant, every courtier, every bottled soul's owner scattered through the dark was running now, running upward toward air, and the mountain was coming down behind them like a tide.

"We have to go," I said. "Now. Before the last way closes."

"I can't map it," he said, and the words broke my heart in a new place, because mapping was the one magic he'd had, the gift that had walked us across impossible country, and I could see it flickering in him like a dying lamp. "I can't hold the shapes anymore. Everything keeps—sliding."

"Then hold onto me," I said, "and let me be the map."

* * *

*Jonah*

We ran.

Her hand in mine was the only coordinate I trusted. Everything else—the stairs, the galleries, the turning shafts of the mountain—kept dissolving as the memories drained out of me, taking the world's edges with them. I had spent my life drawing places so I could walk them, and now I was walking a place that was erasing itself faster than any pen could follow, and I understood, finally, what I had paid. Not coins. Not years. The *lines*. The lines between things, the lines inside things, the line between what happened and what I wished had happened. All of it going, going, going up the stairwell and out into the snow.

She knew the way anyway. Of course she did. Huntresses read ground the way I used to read ink, and she moved through the collapsing dark like water finding its level, pulling me after her, shouting directions I obeyed without understanding because understanding had already been spent.

Twice the passage behind us closed. Once the passage ahead almost did, and she dug us through a seam of rubble with her knife and her shoulder and a fury I could feel through her grip. Somewhere below us the last of the light was going out. Somewhere below us the throne sat empty under a drifting haze of dust, and the shelves stood bare as winter branches, and the thing older than the mountain waited alone in the deepest dark, its debtor dissolved, its contract written in the name of a man who no longer remembered signing anything.

We broke into the open air an hour before dawn, on the south face, where the snow came down clean and cold and smelled like the world instead of like glass.

I looked at the sky and could not have said what season it was. I looked at her and knew her the way you know a song from childhood—by heart, not by mind. The sketch was still in my boot. I took it out and it was blank. Every line of her face had faded to white paper, and I understood that the last payment had cleared.

"What happens now?" I asked.

She looked east, where the sky was going grey, where the valley lay, where the border stones stood, where the crown's hunters would find nothing left to hunt. Then she looked at me, and her eyes were the only thing in the world I recognized.

"Now," she said, "we learn the map from the beginning. Both of us."

The snow kept falling. It fell on the mountain, on the ruined court, on the hundreds of souls flying home through the storm like embers rising from a fire—and it fell on us, two small figures walking down out of the wreck of a kingdom, toward a spring neither of us could yet imagine, carrying between us every memory that mattered, because only one of us had lost them, and she had promised to keep them safe until I could earn them back.
