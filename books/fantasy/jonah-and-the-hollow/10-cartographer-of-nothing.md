## 10. Cartographer of Nothing

I woke to the smell of thawing earth and the sound of water running somewhere below the window, quick and silver, the way snowmelt sounds when it has decided, all at once, that winter is over.

The room was small and clean. A plank ceiling, whitewashed walls gone gold with morning light, a clay pot of something green on the sill—chives, I would learn later, though at that moment I knew nothing of the kind. My body ached in the deep way that bodies ache after a long illness or a long climb, as if every bone had been taken out, washed, and set back slightly wrong.

There was a woman asleep beside me.

She lay on her stomach, one arm flung toward my side of the bed as though even in sleep she had been keeping watch over it. Her hair was dark brown shot through with early grey, cropped short and uneven, the way hair gets when someone cuts it themselves with a knife. There was a scar along her jaw, thin and old, and another on her forearm where the skin had healed rough. Her hands were calloused in the pattern of someone who has held weapons for years and then, for some reason, stopped.

I did not know her name.

That was the first thing I understood about myself: not who she was, but that I did not know. It arrived in me like cold water arriving in a boot—slowly, then all at once. I searched for her name the way you search a pocket you are certain held a coin, and found nothing. Worse than nothing. I searched for *my* name and found a shape there, an outline, like a country on a map whose interior has never been surveyed. I knew I was Jonah. The knowledge sat in me the way a border stone sits in a field—solid, planted by someone else's hand, explaining nothing about the land around it.

Jonah. That was all. Everything else—the valley, the mother, the mountain, the ink, the debt, the king, the shelves, the shattering—I reached for and my fingers closed on air.

I lay very still and listened to the meltwater and felt the woman breathing beside me, and I made myself do the thing I would apparently always do, the thing that was left of me when everything else was gone: I observed. I catalogued. I began, without deciding to, to draw the room in my mind. The crack in the plaster shaped like a river delta. The light falling from the east window in a long trapezoid. The woman's sleeping face, which I could not have described from memory five minutes ago and which I now understood I would need to describe from memory for the rest of my life, because there was no other place to keep it.

My hands were scarred too. Burn scars across the right palm, ink stains worked so deep into the whorls of my fingers they looked like tattoos. When I turned my hands over I felt—not remembered, but *felt*—that these were working hands. Hands that had made things. Whatever else had been taken from me, the taking had been careful. They had left the craft and removed the life. It was like being handed a finished map with every label scraped off: I knew how to read the lines. I no longer knew what country they were.

Beside me, the woman opened her eyes.

She didn't startle. She looked at me the way you look at weather you've been expecting—steadily, with a kind of tired tenderness—and I understood that she had done this before, woken beside a stranger wearing a familiar face, and that she had chosen to keep doing it.

"Jonah," she said. Testing it, or offering it. "Do you remember your name today?"

"Yes." My voice came out rusted. "Just that."

"Just that is good," she said. "Yesterday it was just that, too. And the day before." She pushed herself up on one elbow. Up close her eyes were grey-green, the color of the sea over sand, and there was something in them that had been through a great deal and had decided, deliberately, to remain gentle anyway. "I'm Wren."

"Wren," I said, and the name went into me and found no hook to hang on, and fell through, and landed somewhere deep, where it made an ache I couldn't account for. Saying it hurt. Not knowing why it hurt hurt more. "Wren," I said again, softer, the way you'd say a word in a language you used to speak.

"That's the one," she said, and smiled, and the smile cracked something in the ache, and I thought: *whatever you were to me, I paid for losing you with everything else I had, and I would pay it again.* I didn't know how I knew that. I only knew it the way I knew my own name—planted, solid, unexplained.

"There's bread," she said. "And the maps came back from the printer's in Millbrook yesterday. I didn't look at them. I wanted you to see them first."

"The maps," I said.

"Yours." She said it simply, without ceremony, the way you'd tell a man the sky was up. "You're a cartographer, Jonah. You've been one since before either of us can prove. Come and see what you made while you were sleeping."

* * *

The maps were spread across the big table downstairs, weighted at the corners with river stones and a teapot, and when I saw them I stopped in the doorway the way you stop at the edge of a cliff that turns out to be beautiful instead of terrible.

They were mine. I knew that before I knew anything else about them. The hand was my hand—the same strokes that had stained my fingers, the same confident economy of line. But I had no memory of drawing a single one of them, and that absence was its own strange vertigo, like reading letters from a man with your handwriting and someone else's soul.

There were dozens of them. Maps of the valley I must have come from, drawn with such loving precision that I could almost feel the borders stones' cold radiating off the parchment. Maps of the pass through the mountains, of the toll-road towns, of forests I did not recognize and rivers I could not name. And in the corner of each one, small and neat, a signature: *J. Marchetti.*

Marchetti. The surname surfaced like a fish rising, touched the light, and sank again.

And then I saw the last map, the one laid face-down at the table's center, apart from the others, and Wren watched me with careful eyes as I turned it over.

It was a map of a mountain. Drawn in section, cut away like an anatomical plate, showing the courts and corridors and shelf-halls beneath it in meticulous detail—and every hall was marked in red ink with a single word, repeated down through the dark of the drawing like a heartbeat:

*Empty. Empty. Empty.*

At the bottom of the page, in a different hand—hers, I somehow knew, blunt and slanted where mine was fine—a note had been added: *He drew this from memory the night we came down the mountain. He said someone should keep the record even if no one believes it. No one believed it. We kept it anyway.*

"I don't remember any of this," I said. My voice sounded far away. "The mountain. The court. Any of it. I look at this and it's like looking at someone else's dream."

"I know," Wren said.

"And you—" I looked up at her, and the ache behind her name swelled until I could barely speak around it. "You knew me. Before. You knew all of it, and I gave it up, and you stayed anyway. Why would anyone stay?"

She came around the table slowly, the way you'd approach a deer you didn't want to spook, and took my scarred right hand in both of hers, and turned it palm-up, and pressed her thumb into the burn scars there one by one, as if reading them.

"Because I made a promise," she said. "To a man who gave up everything he was so that a hundred stolen souls could go home and a king could stay dead. He asked me to keep his memories safe until he could earn them back. Some of them can't be earned back, Jonah. Some of them are just gone, spent, and no spring will bring them up. But some of them—" her voice caught, steadied— "some of them are still out there. In the people whose memories flooded home when you broke the shelves. Somewhere in this world there are folk walking around carrying pieces of you they don't know they have. Your mother's face. Your tenth birthday. The first time you ever drew a map and it *walked*. So we're going to travel. Village to village, town to town, following the trail of what flew out of that mountain. And you're going to draw every place we come to, because that's what you do, and I'm going to write down every story people tell us, because that's what I do now. And maybe, piece by piece, year by year, you'll survey yourself back into existence."

"And if I don't?" I asked. "If the pieces never fit?"

She looked at me for a long moment. Outside, the meltwater ran, and somewhere a bird was trying out its voice for the first time since autumn, badly, hopefully.

"Then I'll know you," she said. "For both of us. And you'll draw me, and someday you'll have a map of me detailed enough that between the two of us we'll make one whole memory of what we were." She shrugged, fierce and fragile at once. "It isn't nothing, Jonah. It's just not what we planned."

* * *

That afternoon I sat at the table with fresh paper and a new pen, and I did the thing that was left of me.

I started with the window—the delta-shaped crack, the chives, the light. Then the road below, and the river it followed, and the hills beyond, going out and out into country I had never seen and would now see for the first time, twice: once with my eyes, once with my pen. It was terrifying, mapping a world with no past attached to any of it. It was also, I discovered within the hour, the purest joy I had ever known. Every line was a first line. Nothing was already lost. The whole world was unsurveyed, and I was the man with the pen.

Near evening Wren came and stood behind my shoulder, and I felt her warmth at my back like a hearth.

"What will you draw tomorrow?" she asked.

"The road east," I said. "Wherever it goes. And after that—" I hesitated, pen hovering, and then, because it seemed suddenly urgent, because three days of waking beside a stranger was three days too long to leave a thing like this unmapped, I pulled a fresh sheet toward me and turned in my chair to face her.

"First this," I said. "Hold still."

She laughed—startled, uncertain, and underneath the uncertainty, hopeful—and she held still, grey-green eyes open, evening light catching the scar along her jaw, while I began to draw her face.

Line by line, from nothing, from the beginning: the country of her, surveyed for the first time.

And if some part of me, some deep unsounded region below the border stones of my forgetting, whispered that I had drawn this face before—that I had once hidden it in my boot against a king's hunger, that I had once loved it enough to trade eternity for its safety—I did not hear it clearly, and I did not need to.

Some maps you make to find your way home.

And some maps, drawn carefully enough, in the right light, by the right ruined and remade hand—

*are* the way home.
