## 1. Charms for the Fearful

The rain came down on Vessengard the way it always did in autumn: patiently, as if it had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

Petra Hartwell watched it from behind the counter of The Lucky Crow, his charm-shop on Candlemaker's Row, and tried very hard not to feel anything about it.

That was the trick of it, these days. Feeling nothing. Or feeling very little, which was the next best thing. Because the truth—the thing he kept folded up small inside himself like a letter he could never send—was that storms answered him. Not metaphorically. Not in the way a poet might say a woman's temper was a storm. Actually answered him. When his grief crested, thunder rolled over the rooftops of Vessengard and rattled the good china of people who had never done him any harm. When his anger flared—and it flared less often now, because he had practiced, oh, how he had practiced—lightning found the tallest spire in the district and wrote his name across the sky in fire.

So he had learned to be small.

He was quite good at it now. He made a profession of it, in fact. He sold luck-charms to the fearful, and every one of them was harmless, and every one of them was a lie, and together they were the most honest work he'd ever done.

* * *

The bell above the door gave its little tin cough at half past nine, and Mrs. Aldery came in shaking out her umbrella with the vigor of a woman who believed weather was a personal insult.

"Morning, Mr. Hartwell." She set a basket of turnips on his counter without asking, because Mrs. Aldery did not ask; she informed. "You look terrible."

"Thank you, Mrs. Aldery. I've been cultivating that."

"Hmph." She peered at him through spectacles thick enough to start fires, if one were inclined—which no one in Vessengard was inclined to be, not anymore, not legally. "It's your roof again, isn't it. Leaking."

"It's the whole city leaking. It's Vessengard's national character."

"Well, I brought you turnips, so you'll eat something besides bread and self-pity." She began unpacking them with the ceremony of a general deploying troops. "And I want a charm for my grandson. He sits his guild examinations Thursday."

Petra reached beneath the counter and produced a small wooden bird, carved from rowan wood, painted with a dab of blue at the wing. It was, he could have told her, worth perhaps two coppers in materials and nothing at all in magic. It was also, if you held it just right in the morning light, rather beautiful, and beauty did more for an anxious boy than any spell ever had.

"A Cleverwing," he said. "For clear thinking. He should hold it in his left hand when he writes."

"The left? Why the left?"

"Because the right hand is busy writing, Mrs. Aldery."

She considered this with the gravity the question deserved, then nodded once, satisfied by its nonsense. "Three coppers?"

"Four. Inflation. The rain."

"Everything is inflation and rain with you." But she paid, and she tucked the little bird into her shawl, and before she left she paused at the door with her hand on the frame—that pause she always made, the one Petra both dreaded and treasured.

"You'd have been good at something grand, once," she said. "I can always tell. You've got the hands for it."

"My hands are for carving birds and counting coppers."

"Mm." She didn't believe him. She never believed him, and she never pressed, and that was the closest thing to kindness the city allowed these days. "Thursday, then. If the boy passes, I'm telling everyone it was the bird."

"If the boy fails, I trust you'll tell everyone it was the turnips."

She laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her—and went out into the rain, and Petra stood alone in his shop with his four coppers and his turnips and the enormous quiet that lived in the space where his life used to be.

* * *

He spent the middle of the day carving, which was safer than thinking.

The knife moved through rowan wood the way water moves downhill: without effort, without decision. Hands remembered what minds were forbidden to. That was the arrangement he had made with himself five years ago, in another city, under other circumstances he did not permit himself to examine too closely, because examining them made his chest tighten, and a tight chest in Vessengard was a public safety hazard.

He carved a hare for luck in travel. A fish for luck in trade. A small fat cat for luck in love, though he suspected the cat worked only because anyone who received it laughed, and laughter was luckier than anything.

Outside, the Hollow Wardens passed on their rounds.

They came down Candlemaker's Row twice a day, in their grey coats and their blank iron badges, and the whole street performed the same pantomime it always performed: shutters closed a fraction, voices dropped a degree, everyone became suddenly fascinated by their own goods. The Wardens walked slowly. That was the point of walking slowly. A man hurrying has somewhere to be; a Warden strolling wants you to know he has nowhere else to be but here, watching you.

Their leader today was a sergeant—tall, dark-haired, with a face that gave away nothing and a way of looking at each shopfront as though reading a ledger written in the glass. Her eyes crossed The Lucky Crow and stopped.

Petra kept carving. His knife did not waver. Inside his chest, something old and vast turned over in its sleep, and he laid his free hand flat against the counter and thought, with the discipline of long practice: *I am a man selling wooden birds. I am bored. I am slightly damp. Nothing else.*

The storm overhead, which had been gathering all morning in a sullen grey heap, sighed and let go of its worst intentions.

The sergeant looked at him a moment longer. Then she moved on, and the street exhaled, forty shopkeepers at once, a chorus of relief.

Petra set down the knife. His hands were steady. They were always steady; it was everything underneath them that shook.

* * *

In the afternoon, a boy came in wanting a charm to make a girl notice him, and Petra talked him into buying the fat cat instead, on the theory that anyone carrying a ridiculous wooden cat is already interesting, and interest is nine-tenths of love. The boy left looking like he'd been handed a secret, which, in a way, he had.

An old man came in wanting a charm for his wife, who was dying, and Petra did not sell him anything, because there was no charm for that, and he would not insult grief with rowan wood. He gave the man tea instead, and they sat in the back room among the wood shavings while the rain kept its patient time, and the old man said, "She likes the sound of rain. Always has. Says it sounds like the world still bothering to continue."

"That's a fine thing for rain to say," Petra said.

"It's a fine thing for anyone to say." The old man finished his tea, thanked him in the embarrassed way men of that generation thanked people, and left a copper on the table despite everything, and Petra let him.

Afterward Petra sat alone while the light went thin and grey, and he felt the ache arrive, the one that came every evening around this hour, punctual as the Wardens.

It was the ache of the shrunk life. He had been—once, elsewhere, in a chapter of his story he kept locked—he had been large. There had been a tower, he sometimes almost remembered, and students, and a voice calling him by a name that wasn't Hartwell. There had been weather that obeyed him not out of fear but out of something like affection. And now there was a shop twelve feet wide, and turnips, and a ceiling that leaked, and the daily labor of being nobody, which was harder work than being somebody had ever been, though nobody warned you about that. Nobody tells you that erasing yourself is a full-time occupation. That it takes strength. That some mornings you wake up and have to decide, all over again, to be small, and the deciding costs more than the doing.

He blew out the lamp. He counted the day's coppers. Fourteen, plus the tea. A fortune, by his standards.

* * *

And then, near closing, the strangest thing happened, and it happened by absence.

The rain stopped.

Not eased. Not thinned to drizzle. Stopped—all at once, everywhere, mid-drop, as though the sky had been pinched shut by unseen fingers. Petra felt it in his teeth before he heard it: the silence pressing against the windows, total and wrong, a held breath the size of the horizon.

He went to the door and looked out.

Candlemaker's Row lay gleaming and motionless under lamps that burned without flickering, because there was no wind to flicker them. No wind at all. Smoke rose from chimneys in straight grey columns, perfectly vertical, like drawn lines. Somewhere a dog began to bark and its bark fell dead in the air, flattened, muffled, as if the world had been wrapped in wool.

Every hair on Petra's arms stood up.

He knew dead calm. Every storm-caller knew dead calm. It was what the air did in the instant before lightning, the pause where the sky gathered itself—but this calm was not gathering toward anything. It simply *was*, vast and complete, and it did not belong to him. He checked himself, quickly, thoroughly, the way a man pats his pockets for a lost purse. His chest was quiet. Whatever had swallowed the weather, it hadn't come from him.

Down the row, doors were opening. People stepped out into the impossible stillness and stood in it, faces tilted upward, and Petra saw Mrs. Aldery three shops down with her umbrella half-raised, staring at the smoke standing straight as soldiers, and her lips moving in what was either a prayer or a complaint.

Then the lamps guttered—not from wind, there was no wind, from something else—and relit, and guttered again.

And far off, at the wrong end of the row, at the mouth of the alley where the lamplight gave out entirely, Petra saw a small figure standing barefoot in a puddle that had stopped rippling.

A child. A girl, by the size of her, small even for a child, standing utterly still in the dead calm as though the stillness had been arranged for her benefit. She was looking directly at his shop. At him. From fifty yards away, in the dark, he could feel her looking the way you feel sunlight through a window.

His first instinct—the sensible one, the survivor's instinct, the one that had kept him alive and small for five years—was to close the door. Turn the sign. Blow out the last lamp and be a man who was already asleep.

His hand was actually on the door when the girl took a step forward, and the puddle beneath her feet lit up.

Not reflected light. Not lamplight. It glowed from within, a soft gold, ember-colored, spreading outward from her small bare feet in slow rings, and where the light touched the wet cobblestones the rain-steam rose and hung in the windless air like breath in winter.

Petra Hartwell, who had spent five years teaching his heart to beat quietly, felt it slam once, hard, against his ribs like a fist on a door.

"No," he said aloud, to no one, to himself, to whatever was walking up Candlemaker's Row trailing gold through the dark. "No, no, no. Not here. Not me. I'm nobody. I sell birds."

The girl stopped at his threshold, dripping, barefoot, unbothered, and looked up at him with eyes that caught the lamp-light and held it a moment too long, the way embers hold heat after the fire seems gone.

She raised one small hand and knocked on his doorframe—three soft raps, polite as a neighbor—and the dead calm over all of Vessengard, every chimney-column and every flattened sound and every held breath of the sky, leaned in to listen.
