## 3. What the Wardens Burn

*Fire first. Always fire first.*

Before the shop there was a gate. Before the gate there was a burning, and before the burning there was a voice that said *little ember* in a language that was not a language but a warmth, a warmth that said things instead of words.

She is small here. She is always small in this remembering. She stands on a floor of black glass and something enormous breathes above her, slow as weather, and each breath makes her chest-bright thing flicker like a candle cupped by careful hands.

*Little ember,* says the voice. *Little ember, they will come for you when you are small. They will come wearing law like a coat.*

And she — she is not afraid, which is strange, because later she will be afraid of everything, of doors and boots and the sound of keys, but here she is not afraid, because the enormous breathing thing curls around her like a wall made of patience, and it says:

*Burn slow. Burn long. Find the one who hides his sky.*

---

The shop smells of rain and copper and the man who pretends.

Wren knows pretending. Pretending is what you do when your chest holds a piece of sun and everyone else's chest holds only ordinary dark. The man who pretends — Petra, Petra, she has learned to say it inside her head even though saying names aloud invites them into your dreams — sells little knotted strings and painted stones and tells people they are lucky, and they are not lucky, they are nothing, and he knows they are nothing, and his eyes go sorry when he hands them over.

He does not know she watches him from the stairs.

He talks to his kettles. That is the first thing. He boils water for tea nobody drinks and mutters at the kettle when it sulks, and once Wren saw him tap it twice and whisper something with too many vowels, and the water came to boil all at once, angry-quick, and he looked around the empty shop like a boy caught stealing honey.

He hums when it storms. Not happy-humming. Holding-humming. Like the storm is a big dog straining at a rope and the humming is the rope. When thunder comes close the cups on the shelf rattle and his knuckles go white on the counter and he hums lower, lower, until the thunder gets bored and goes away to frighten some other street.

Wren thinks: *he is hiding his sky.* She does not know yet that she knows this phrase. It arrives in her already known, like the smell of smoke arrives before you see the fire.

She has no shadow. She checked. She stood very still in the afternoon light that comes through the warped window and looked down and there was the shadow of the shelf, the shadow of the teapot, the shadow of the man who pretends, and between them a patch of floor as bright and bare as if the sun had chosen it specially. The floor where she stands forgets to be dark.

Maybe that is why the cats follow her. There are three cats now. They sit outside the door in a row like judges, and Petra steps over them every morning and says "good morning, your honors" under his breath, and Wren loves him so much in those moments that her chest-thing flares and one of the cats' ears glows faintly orange for an hour and the cat is extremely pleased about it.

She should tell him about the cats being pleased. She cannot tell him anything. Words live in her mouth like fish in a dry pond. They flop and die. But in dreams —

In dreams she speaks.

---

*Dream:*

There is a hall of tall doors and every door is shut except one, and behind the open door is rain falling upward, and a woman made of river is laughing, and someone is counting coins that are actually seeds, and Wren walks through the open door because the other doors have locks shaped like teeth.

On the other side: the gate again. The black glass floor. The enormous breathing.

*Little ember,* it says, and its voice is tired now, thinner, like a fire left alone too long. *Little ember, I am guttering. Do you feel it? When I dim, the world forgets its promises.*

She wants to answer. In the dream she can answer. The words come up out of her like bubbles through deep water, and they are not words, they are meanings wearing word-shapes, and the meaning she gives him is: *I am here. I am lit. I am small but I am lit.*

And the great voice makes a sound that might be laughter in a language older than laughing, and says:

*They are coming. Boots, little ember. So many boots. And a man at the head of them whose face is a borrowed coat. Wake the one who hides his sky. Wake him, even if waking hurts him. Especially if waking hurts him.*

She wakes with her hand pressed flat against her own sternum, and the shard under her ribs is beating like a second heart, faster than the first.

Downstairs, the kettle is screaming.

Not boiling-screaming. *Screaming.*

---

*Now:*

Rain, but wrong rain. Rain that falls straight down like it has been ordered to. No wind anywhere in Vessengard tonight; the whole city holding its breath. Wren knows dead-calm. Dead-calm is what the sky does when something terrible is deciding.

Boots. She hears them before anyone. Boots have a taste, almost, a metal-and-mud taste in the air, and her chest-thing tastes them three streets away.

Petra is blowing out lamps. His hands are steady but his humming has started, low, the rope-humming, and Wren comes down the stairs on silent feet because her feet are always silent, another thing about her that is not ordinary, and stands in the doorway of the back room, and looks at him, and tries with everything in her to make words happen.

What comes out is a sound like a struck bell.

It is not a word. It is the First Tongue trying to use her throat and finding the throat too small, and the sound goes through the shop like light through water, and every charm on every shelf — the nothing-charms, the honest nothings — rings once, softly, all together, like a room full of tiny bells agreeing about something.

Petra turns. His face does a complicated thing. Fear first, then wonder, then fear again, layered like paint.

"What," he says. Then, gentler, crouching to her height: "Wren. What was that?"

She points at her chest. She points at the window. She mimes boots with two fingers walking across her palm. She mimes fire. She mimes *run*, which is just grabbing his sleeve and pulling, which is the oldest mime in the world and needs no tongue at all.

He doesn't run. Of course he doesn't run. Men who hide their skies never run until the sky itself insists.

The knock comes anyway. It always comes in the remembering. Three knocks, patient as arithmetic.

"Wardens," says a voice through the door, bored and official. "Open in the name of the Quiet."

*The Quiet.* What a name for them. As if magic were only noise. As if the loudest thing in Wren is not a silence, a held silence, a sun holding its breath.

Petra opens the door because refusing makes it worse, and four of them come in, gray-cloaked, brass badges dull as old spoons, and their leader is a woman with a scar through one eyebrow and eyes that keep doing something strange — flicking toward Wren, away, toward, like a person trying not to look at a thing they've been ordered to find.

"Charm licenses," the leader says.

"You've seen my licenses, Sergeant," Petra says. "Twice."

"Laws change." The sergeant's gaze snags on Wren and sticks this time. "Who's the child?"

"My niece," Petra lies, smoothly, terribly, because Wren can hear the lie wobble in his mouth like a loose tooth. "Visiting from—"

"From where?" says one of the others, young, eager, smelling blood in the wobbling. He crosses the room. He reaches for Wren.

Do not let him touch me, Wren thinks, in the First Tongue, in the old warm language, thinking it so hard it burns: *do not let him touch me do not let him touch me do not—*

And Petra hears her.

That is the thing. That is the hinge of the whole night. He hears her — not with ears, with whatever he buried five years ago, whatever sleeps under his skin beneath the charm-shop and the polite smiles and the kettle-muttering — and what sleeps there wakes up.

The kettle explodes first. Politely, almost. A gout of steam that takes the young Warden full in the face and sends him staggering backward into the shelf, and the shelf goes down, and a hundred painted stones scatter across the floor like a spilled sky.

Then the storm arrives.

It was waiting. Wren understands this immediately, the way you understand a dog has been waiting at a gate. All those nights of humming, all that rope-straining, all the thunder sent away bored — it did not leave. It sat outside the city in the dead calm, ordered nowhere, and waited for him to stop holding the rope.

Lightning comes down the chimney.

Not near the chimney. *Down* it. A white vein of it, and the room goes blinding, and Petra is standing in the middle of it with his arms half-raised like a man surrendering to himself, and his eyes have gone the color of the sky before hail, silver-green, and he is shouting something in the First Tongue — actual words, real ones, Wren knows them, they mean *not her, take the sky instead, take all of it* — and the storm agrees.

The front wall leaves.

It does not break. It *leaves*. Lifts clean off its foundations and deposits itself in the street in pieces, gently, almost courteously, as if the storm has manners its owner lacks. Rain pours in sideways. The Wardens are on the floor. The sergeant is on her feet with her cloak whipped around her like a flag, and her face — Wren will remember her face — her face is not frightened. Her face is *grieving*, which is worse, which means she has seen this before, or been this before, or lost this before.

"Run," the sergeant says. To Petra. Quietly, under the thunder. "For both our sakes, charm-seller. *Run.*"

The street outside is chaos. The lightning has split the cobblestones. A lamp-post is lying across a fruit cart and oranges are everywhere, rolling, glowing briefly in the flashes like small embarrassed moons. Neighbors hang out of windows. Somewhere a bell starts ringing the alarm-pattern, three-and-one, three-and-one, and more boots are coming, many more, the taste of them thick in the rain now, iron and mud and law.

Petra grabs Wren's hand.

His hand is shaking. Hers is not. Between them, where the palms press, her chest-thing and his woken storm touch, and for one heartbeat the rain around them stops falling and hangs in the air like a held chord, thousands of drops suspended, each one lit gold from inside.

Then he pulls her into the dark and lets it fall.

---

*Fragment:*

—the oranges rolling—

—a cat, the fat judge-cat, sitting untouched on a dry doorstep watching the wall-less shop with an expression of profound vindication—

—the sergeant standing in the ruined doorway not following, and Wren understands without knowing how she understands: *she let us go, she chose, there is a crack in her and the light gets through*—

—Petra's voice, ragged, in the dark: "I didn't want you to see that. I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I kept it down so long, I kept it down for years—"—

—and Wren answering him the only way she can, pressing her small bright hand flat against his shaking one, and the First Tongue rising in her like water finding its level, and what she says, though he cannot fully hear it yet, is:

*I saw. It was beautiful. Hide it no more.*

---

They go down.

There is a grate behind the tanner's row, rusted, and Petra wrenches it with strength that surprises them both, storm-strength still leaking out of his arms, and below is the undercity, the old city, the city under the city where the rain does not reach and the bells cannot follow. Brick throats of forgotten chimneys. Staircases going down to cellars that were cellars when Vessengard had a different name. Water dripping somewhere with the patience of stone.

Wren's feet know these darks. That is the strangest part. Her feet, which have never been here, walk sure, and turn left at a junction where turning right means drowning, and Petra follows her because he has no choice, because his lantern drowned in the first puddle, because the only light down here is hers.

Because that is the truth of the undercity: the girl with no shadow is the only lamp.

Her chest-thing brightens as the dark deepens, a soft gold glow through her shirt, and Petra stares at it as they walk — stares, and does not ask, and Wren realizes he is afraid to ask, afraid of what asking will wake next. His magic is loose in him now like a tooth gone from its socket, the tongue keeps going to the gap.

"I heard you," he says finally, hoarse, in the dark. "Back there. Before the wall. You said — I don't know how I knew what you said. I haven't spoken that language since—" He stops. His jaw works. "Since before. Since whatever happened before."

*Before,* Wren thinks. Everyone's favorite country. Nobody can get a visa.

She squeezes his hand. In the First Tongue, silently, in the privacy of her own skull where the words work properly, she tells him: *the gate remembers you. The fire remembers you. Something enormous called you the one who hides his sky, and it asked me to wake you, and I am sorry it hurt, and I am not sorry it woke.*

Somewhere above them, muffled through fathoms of brick, the alarm bell changes pattern. Three-and-two now. Hunting-rhythm.

Petra hears it and his step quickens, and he says, "There's someone. Under the archive district. An old woman who owes me — or I owe her, I genuinely can't remember which, which is probably important and I'll worry about it tomorrow." A shaky almost-laugh, the kind people make when the alternative is screaming. "Odelia. Odelia Vance. If anyone knows what you are, little—"

He stops dead.

Stares at her.

"Little ember," he whispers. "Why do I know to call you that? Why do I—"

His hand drifts toward his own temple, toward the place behind the eyes where taken things leave a shape of absence, and Wren watches him touch the hollow where a memory used to be, the way you touch the socket of a missing tooth, and his face goes pale as bone-dust.

Above them, the hunting-bells. Below them, the dark. Ahead of them, an archivist of banned things, and behind them a street that used to be a shop, and inside Wren's chest the last ember of a broken promise beats on, stubborn, lit, small.

*Find the archivist,* the great voice had said, in the dream, in the burning, in the before. *She keeps what they burn.*

Wren tugs his sleeve. Points downward, deeper, the way her feet already know.

And the man who hid his sky takes a breath, and lets her lead him into the dark.
