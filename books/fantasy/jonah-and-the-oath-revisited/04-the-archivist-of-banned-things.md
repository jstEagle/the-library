## 4. The Archivist of Banned Things

The undercity of Vessengard had been a city once, before the river changed its mind.

Petra knew this the way every child raised above ground knew it: as a story told to keep them from the storm-drains. *The Old Vessengard drowned two hundred years ago,* the schoolmasters said, *and everything in it belongs to the water now.* It was a tidy lesson. The water kept what it was given. Don't go looking for gifts from the river.

Wren led him through the storm-drains anyway, barefoot on slick stone, her small hand wrapped around two of his fingers like she was afraid he'd float away if she let go. She did not hesitate at any turning. Twice she stopped, tilted her head as though listening to something under the drip and echo, and chose a passage that made no sense to Petra until he saw where it went — around a collapsed stair, past a nest of pale blind things that scattered from her lightless approach as if they'd been warned.

"You've been down here before," he said quietly.

She shook her head. Then she paused, reconsidered, and shrugged in a way that managed to be both honest and deeply unhelpful.

The dark down here was not empty. That was the thing no one told you about the drowned city — it wasn't empty, it was *full*, full of old weight, old names, the pressure of all that masonry holding back all that river. Petra felt it against his skin like a second rain. And threaded through it, faint as a hairline crack in porcelain, ran something else. A hum. A warmth. Faintly, absurdly, it reminded him of his shop on a good day, before the bells, before the burning — the smell of cedar oil and candle smoke, the little brass charms ticking softly on their hooks.

Magic, soaked into the stones. Old magic, patient, drowned but not dead.

His own answering power stirred in his chest like a dog hearing its name, and he pressed it down with the ease of long practice, the way you'd smooth a wrinkle from a shirt you'd worn ten thousand times. *Not here. Not now.* Though he was beginning to suspect, with a tired sort of clarity, that the days of pressing it down were over. You could not un-level a street. You could not put a girl's shadow back where the Wardens' lanterns said it ought to hang.

Wren stopped so suddenly he nearly walked into her.

Ahead, the tunnel opened into a vaulted chamber that must once have been a cistern or a temple — with the Old Vessengard, Petra suspected, the distinction had been academic. Water lay across the floor in black glassy sheets, ankle-deep, perfectly still, mirroring the arches overhead so completely that the room seemed to extend both up and down into infinity. And rising out of that doubled darkness stood shelves. Hundreds of them. Iron-framed, rust-bled, stacked with books and ledgers and scroll-cases and things Petra had no name for, all of it arranged with a precision that felt almost aggressive in the middle of a ruin.

Lanterns burned along the shelves. Real fire, fed by real oil, in a city where lighting an unsanctioned flame could cost you your name.

And at a desk built from a salvaged pew, a woman sat writing, pen scratching, utterly unbothered, as though two fugitives wading into her archive were simply the evening's third interruption.

She was perhaps sixty, grey-haired, built like a well-fed sparrow, wearing three sweaters of clashing colors and a pair of spectacles pushed up onto her head for no reason Petra could determine, since she clearly wasn't using them. Without looking up she said:

"Boots off. There's a rack by the north pillar. I will not have river-mud tracked across four hundred years of careful scholarship."

Petra looked down at his boots. At the water they were standing in. At the mud that was, at this point, less a substance than a lifestyle.

"We're being hunted," he said.

"Everyone who comes here is being hunted. That is rather the point of coming here." She finished her line, blotted it, and looked up. Her eyes were quick and brown and missed nothing; they went from Petra's face to Wren's and stopped there, and something moved behind them — recognition, yes, but also a kind of grief, the way you grieve a letter you'd stopped expecting. "Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you're *real*."

Then, briskly, as if she hadn't just said something enormous: "Boots. Rack. North pillar."

* * *

They sat on overturned crates while Odelia Vance — for she introduced herself with a small formal bow that belonged to some older, politer century — poured tea from a dented kettle balanced on a brazier of coals. The tea tasted of iron and mint. Wren held her cup in both hands and watched the archivist with the fixed attention of a cat watching a bird that has made an interesting decision.

"Odelia Vance," Petra repeated. He turned the name over. "I know that name."

"Flattering, but unlikely. They struck me from the rolls eleven years ago." She sipped her tea. "Former Keeper of Restricted Holdings, Grand Archive of Vessengard. Convicted of cataloging banned histories instead of burning them. Sentenced to public erasure — my name scraped from every record I ever touched." Her mouth twitched. "Which taught me a valuable professional lesson about the durability of ink versus memory. Memory loses, incidentally. Every time. People forget their mothers' faces. Ink lasts."

"They erased you," Petra said slowly, "and you responded by building the largest collection of forbidden books in the city."

"I responded by building the largest collection of forbidden books *underneath* the city, which shows I learned something about humility too." She set down her cup. "Now. You are the charm-seller whose shop used to be on Candlemaker's Row, whose street is currently a crater, and whom every Hollow Warden in Vessengard is describing to their sketch-artists. Do not ask how I know. The rats tell me things. Rats hear everything and gossip worse than archivists." She leaned forward. "And this is the child with no shadow, who walked out of a Warden raid untouched, and who is currently glowing."

Petra's head snapped toward Wren. "Glowing?"

"Faintly. Under the skin. Here—" Odelia gestured at her own sternum— "like a coal seen through a closed stove-door. You can't see it? You're standing right next to her."

He looked. And once it was named, he couldn't unsee it: beneath Wren's collarbones, deep down, a warmth in the air around her chest, a shimmer like heat off summer cobblestones. How many nights had she slept in his back room while he sat nearby and never noticed? Or had it grown? Had *he* made it grow?

"She came to my door three nights ago," he said. "No shadow. No family. Speaks in her sleep in a language I haven't heard since—" He stopped. Since the tower. Since the life with the scorched edges. "Since a long time ago."

"The First Tongue," Odelia said.

He stared at her. "You know it?"

"I know *of* it. Knowing it and knowing of it are different trades." She rose, wading to a shelf with the ease of someone who had navigated this room ten thousand times, and pulled down a scroll-case sealed with wax gone amber with age. "Every archive keeps a copy of the Concordance of the Two Realms. Officially, the Concordance is a treaty record. Unofficially, it is the most dangerous book in Vessengard, which is why there are exactly three copies left and I own one and the Wardens would burn this whole district to get it." She carried it back, broke the seal with her thumbnail, and unrolled it across the desk, weighting the corners with teacups.

The parchment was covered in two columns of script. The left column Petra could read — high formal Old Vessengardic, the kind carved on courthouse lintels. The right column was the language from Wren's dreams. His skin prickled. He hadn't dreamed it; he'd *heard* it, years ago, spoken by someone whose face lived just beyond the scorch-mark in his memory.

"A thousand years ago," Odelia said, tapping the left column, "there were two realms. This one. And Ashfall." She tapped the right column without touching it, the way you'd gesture near a sleeping animal. "Dragons, if you want the nursery version. Older than dragons, if you want mine. The two realms were bound together by an Oath — a covenant, sworn in fire, that each would sustain the other. Our world gave Ashfall its seasons, its tides, its *story*. Ashfall gave us—" she hesitated— "warmth. Not the weather kind. The other kind. The reason a candle lights for a stranger and a hearth holds a family. The reason magic worked at all, once."

"And then?"

"And then the Oath broke. Nobody agrees how. The Gate between realms was sealed, Ashfall went dark, and within a generation the magic drained out of this one like blood from a wound. What little remains leaks through the cracks — hedge-witches, storm-callers, children born wrong." She glanced at him without apology. "Yes, I know what you are. Your street announced it loudly enough."

Petra's jaw tightened. "The Wardens say magic is a plague."

"The Wardens say what they were built to say." Odelia's voice lost its dryness. "The Hollow Order didn't rise after the Oath broke, charm-seller. It rose *because* of it. Someone broke the Oath on purpose, and then built an army to make sure no one ever asked why." She turned the scroll slightly. "But none of that is why I'm showing you this. Look at the seal clause. The final provision."

Petra bent over the parchment. Beneath the twin columns, in script finer than the rest, ran a short passage. He read it twice, because the first time his mind refused it.

*"Should the Oath fail, the covenant shall not die entire. Its last ember shall pass out of fire and into flesh, and shall abide there, hidden, until such time as the realms have need of it. The ember-child shall know neither what she carries nor why. She shall be sought by those who would extinguish her and by those who would spend her. Guard her, for she is not a victim of the breaking. She is the remedy for it."*

The chamber was very quiet. Somewhere far above, muffled by two hundred feet of stone and history, the hunting-bells were still ringing, and here they sounded like rain on a distant roof.

Petra looked at Wren.

She was looking at the parchment too, at the words she couldn't read, and her small hand had drifted up to rest flat against her own sternum — over the warmth, over the coal behind the stove-door. When she lifted her gaze to his, there was no fear in it. That was the worst part. There was only a grave, patient question, the look of someone who had always known there was something inside her and had finally been introduced to it.

"That's her," Petra said. His voice came out rough. "That's — you're saying this thousand-year-old treaty describes *her*. By name. Ember-child."

"It describes what she carries." Odelia took off her spectacles, polished them on her innermost sweater, and put them back on, which accomplished nothing except visibly steadying her hands. "Dragon-fire, charm-seller. Living dragon-fire, in the chest of a living child. The last ember of the Oath between the realms. If the old records are true — and I have staked my erased life on the proposition that they are — then that girl is not a refugee. She is not a curiosity. She is the single most important object in either world, currently wearing shoes she doesn't own and drinking my terrible tea."

Wren held up her cup as if in evidence, then drank the terrible tea with dignity.

Something in Petra's chest cracked — not painfully. More the way ice cracks on a river in late winter: a sound that means the water underneath has been moving all along. He thought of the dream-voice, the great slow voice that had said *find the archivist, she keeps what they burn.* He thought of the way Wren had led him through the drains without hesitating, as if the drowned city were a map drawn on the inside of her eyelids. Of course she had. She wasn't following a map. She was following herself. The Oath had been walking home through him the whole time.

"Why does she glow brighter?" he asked quietly. "It's growing. I'd swear it's grown since she came to my door."

Odelia was silent a moment. When she answered, all the briskness had gone out of her voice, and what remained was careful, the way you speak near a wound.

"Because embers answer each other. That's their nature. Fire knows fire." She met his eyes. "There is a storm-caller sitting six feet from the last living piece of the covenant that gave his gift its source, and he's asking me why the room feels warmer. Charm-seller — Petra, isn't it? — I spent eleven years cataloging banned histories, and I am telling you that nothing in any of them is an accident. She found your door. Ask yourself someday, when you have leisure, *why yours.*"

Before he could answer — before he could even decide whether the question terrified him more than the Wardens did — Wren set down her cup, slid off her crate, and padded to the desk. She studied the great unrolled Concordance with the seriousness of a scholar three hundred years her senior. Then she reached out and laid one finger, gently, on the right-hand column. On the First Tongue.

And she read it aloud.

Not haltingly. Not like a child sounding out letters. Her voice was small and clear in the vast drowned room, and the syllables came out of her shaped and certain, and every one of them landed in Petra's ears like a key finding a lock he hadn't known was in him. He understood her. Not the way he'd understood her dreams — half-glimpsed, translated by sleep. Fully. Awake. The language was pouring back into him through her voice like water into a dry channel, and with it came fragments: a tower, wind, a friend laughing, a door—

Odelia had gone white. "Eleven years," she whispered. "Eleven years I've stared at that column. It's not dead text. It was never dead text. It's a *language*, and the last person alive who speaks it is nine years old and likes my tea—"

Wren finished the passage. Then she looked up at Petra, and pointed one small finger at the left column — the readable one, the treaty record — and then at the shelf behind Odelia. At a specific shelf. At a specific section, near the bottom, where the ledgers stood oldest and most water-swollen.

She said one word in the First Tongue. Petra heard it and understood it and felt it settle into him like a stone dropped into a well:

*Trial.*

Odelia followed the girl's finger. Her brow furrowed. "That's the legal holdings. Convictions, sentences, erasure orders. Boring as silt, mostly." But she was already moving, wading to the shelf, running her fingers along the spines. "What in the drowned deeps do you want with court records, child—"

Her hand stopped.

She drew out a ledger — thin, scorched at one corner, its cover stamped with the seal of the Hollow Order — and turned it over in her hands, and Petra watched the color leave her face entirely.

"This shouldn't exist," she said. "This is a Warden trial transcript. We don't get Warden transcripts. They burn their own paperwork, they're fanatics about it—" She looked up at him, and her eyes behind the useless spectacles were wide and bright and afraid. "Charm-seller. The date on this. It's five years ago. And the name on the spine—"

She held it out to him.

Petra took it. The leather was cold and damp and heavier than a thin book had any right to be. He turned it over and read the name stamped in faded gilt on the spine, and the world went very quiet and very far away, the way it does in the moment before a storm breaks.

The name was his own.

* * *

Behind them, unnoticed, Wren climbed back onto her crate and picked up her tea, and watched the two adults with the patient expression of someone waiting for a story she had already heard — and already, somehow, forgiven.

Odelia lit a fresh lamp and drew it close. "Well," she said, in the voice of a scholar confronting the one book she'd never dared open. "Shall we find out who you were, before the Wardens tell everyone?"

The ledger fell open in Petra's hands to a page scored with scorch-marks — and the scorched places were not empty.

They were *waiting*.
