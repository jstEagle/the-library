## 5. Memories in the Walls

The flooded archive smelled of old paper and older water.

Petra had expected a vault—stone, iron, the sort of place where banned things went to be forgotten properly. Instead Odelia Vance led them down a crooked stair behind a false wall in her bookbinder's shop, and the stair simply kept going, past the waterline of the old cisterns, into a cavern where hundreds of shelves rose out of black, mirror-still water like the masts of a drowned fleet.

"You're storing forbidden histories *in water*," Petra said.

"Water remembers nothing," Odelia said briskly, lifting the hem of her coat and stepping onto a plank walkway barely wider than her boots. "That's rather the point. Paper burns. Wardens love fire. Nobody loves wading." She glanced back at Wren, who was riding on Petra's hip with the solemn authority of a small admiral. "Mind the third plank. It lies."

Wren patted the third plank as they crossed it, gently, as if apologizing to it.

The walkways branched and braided overhead, a wooden nervous system strung between the shelves. Lanterns hung from hooks on wires, each one burning with a flat, colorless flame that gave light but no warmth. Cold-lights. Petra hadn't seen one since—since—

He stopped walking.

Since his tower. Since the lesson room where Tamsin used to fall asleep over her sigil-work and he'd drape his coat over her shoulders and turn the cold-lights down one by one so she'd think morning came slower than it did.

"Petra?" Odelia said.

"Nothing." He kept moving. "Just remembering that I don't remember."

* * *

Odelia's workroom occupied a shelf-island near the archive's heart: a desk, two chairs, a stove that shouldn't have worked down here and yet did, and walls made entirely of pigeonholes, each crammed with scrolls, folios, and objects too strange for shelves—a bell with no clapper, a key made of salt, a jar of what appeared to be bottled thunderstorm, which made Petra deeply uneasy in a way he chose not to examine.

"Sit," she said. "Both of you. Tea?"

"Do you have anything stronger?"

"I have tea, and I have opinions, and you'll find the second is stronger." She was already pouring. "Now. The child."

Wren sat very straight on her chair, feet not reaching the floor, hands folded in her lap like a duchess awaiting bad news. In the cold-light her shadow did its usual vanishing act, and Petra watched Odelia notice it—the archivist's eyes flicking down, narrowing, then up again with the particular hunger of a woman who had spent thirty years cataloguing things that shouldn't exist and had long ago stopped being frightened and started being *delighted*.

"May I?" Odelia asked, and when Wren nodded, she held her palm an inch from the girl's sternum, above the faint ember-glow that showed through her shirt when the light was low. "Well," Odelia said softly. "Well, well, well."

"That's three wells," Wren said gravely. "Is that good?"

"It's more wells than I've dug in a decade, child." Odelia drew back, and for the first time since they'd met her, something in her face was not delight. It was closer to reverence, and reverence, Petra thought, looked a great deal like fear wearing its Sunday clothes. "Dragon-fire. True dragon-fire, not the guttering imitations the collectors sell. And not merely a relic—it's *alive*. It's burning *her*."

"We know," Petra said. "It's eating her warmth. Slowly."

"Mm. And it called you, didn't it? Out of everyone in this city of two hundred thousand souls, it walked to your door." She sipped her tea, watching him over the rim. "Because you're an oath-keeper, Master Hartwell. Or you were. Which raises the question of why an oath-keeper doesn't know he's an oath-keeper."

"The Wardens took my magic. Five years ago. Trial, sentence, exile. I remember the trial."

"Do you." Odelia set down her cup. "Describe it."

He opened his mouth—and found, to his surprise, that describing it was like describing a dream after noon. There had been a hall. Grey stone. Faces. A voice reading charges. He could feel the shape of it all, the way you can feel the shape of a tooth that's been pulled.

"There was a hall," he said slowly. "High windows. I stood alone—I remember thinking that was wrong, that I should have had someone standing with me. Someone was supposed to—" His brow furrowed. "Someone was *supposed* to be there. I can't—there was a voice reading the charges. I don't remember the charges. I remember the verdict. Exile. Memory-binding, they called it. Standard sentence for unregistered practitioners above the third rank."

"And the crime itself? What did they say you *did*?"

Silence. The stove ticked. Somewhere far off in the drowned stacks, water dripped with the patience of a thing that had all the time in the world.

"I don't know," Petra said at last, and heard how thin his voice came out. "I've never known. I assumed—I assumed I'd done something terrible, and my mind refused to hold it. Guilt does that. I built my whole life around not knowing. You get good at it. You make it a kind of craft."

Odelia was already rising, moving along the pigeonhole wall with the speed of long practice, pulling folios. "Guilt doesn't do that," she said over her shoulder. "Not cleanly. Guilt leaves smears, fragments, nightmares. What you're describing is *scarcity*. Uniform absence. That's not a mind protecting itself, Master Hartwell. That's a mind that's been *edited*."

She returned with an armload of grey-bound volumes and dropped them on the desk with a thump that made Wren jump. "Trial records. The Wardens keep copies of everything—they burn the originals publicly, for the theater of it, but the bureaucracy of tyranny is wonderfully sentimental about paperwork. Hartwell, Hartwell... storm-caller, rank six, Vessengard circuit..." She pulled a slim volume, blew dust from its spine, and opened it.

And stopped.

"What?" Petra said. "What is it?"

Odelia turned the book around without a word.

The page was scorched blank.

Not torn, not redacted, not inked over. Scorched—the paper browned and brittle at the edges of a perfect rectangle, as though a hand of pure heat had pressed down upon it and lifted away everything written there. Petra touched it and snatched his fingers back; the scorch-mark was still warm. After five years underwater, in a cold archive, in a city where magic was punishable by the rope.

It was still warm.

"Every record of your trial will look like this," Odelia said quietly. "I'd stake the whole collection on it. Someone didn't want the crime remembered. Not by the courts, not by the archives, not by *you*." She looked up at him, and her eyes behind their spectacles were very bright. "Master Hartwell. Your memories weren't lost in the binding. They were *taken separately*, before it, by someone with considerable power, and taken so thoroughly that even the record of them burns. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"No," said Petra, whose mouth had gone dry. "Tell me anyway."

"Someone stole your past twice. Once from your head, and once from the world." She closed the volume gently, the way one closes a coffin. "The question that should be keeping you awake tonight is not *what did I forget*. It's *who was afraid enough of what I knew to burn it out of existence*."

* * *

They tried four more records while the stove burned low. Trial transcript: scorched. Sentencing writ: scorched. Even the mundane entries—the tax rolls listing his charm-shop's address, the guild registry of licensed practitioners—were scorched wherever his name appeared beside the word *oath*. His name alone survived. His name next to the Oath did not.

Wren had gone quiet in the way that Petra was learning meant the opposite of quiet. She sat with her hand pressed flat against her own chest, and through her fingers the ember-light pulsed—not the slow warm breathing rhythm it usually kept, but quick, arrhythmic, like a heart recognizing a drumbeat.

Then she gasped.

"Wren—" He was off his chair and kneeling before her before he'd decided to move. "What is it? Are you burning? Is it the shard?"

Her eyes had gone wide and silver-rimmed, fixed on some middle distance that wasn't the workroom at all. And the light under her hand flared, and the flare reached out—not fire, nothing so crude, but a warmth that poured off her like lamplight through a doorway—and touched him.

The workroom vanished.

*A tower. High, high up, above a city he almost recognized—Vessengard, but younger, or himself younger in it. Rain on tall windows. A room full of cold-lights, and a man laughing in the doorway, a big man with a beard going grey at the edges, saying,* "You can't bind a storm, Petra, you can only ask it nicely. That's the whole secret. That's the whole *art*—" *and the voice was warm as bread and Petra knew it, knew it the way you know home, and the name rose in him like a bubble through deep water, almost, almost breaking the surface—*

*The tower again, night now, and running footsteps, and the bearded man's voice sharp with fear:* "They're coming tonight, do you hear me? Whatever they offer you, whatever they threaten—don't let them take the ember's twin, don't let them seal the Gate, swear to me, swear it on the storm—"

*A choice. He couldn't see it, only feel it, the way you feel weather coming—a vast pressure, a door opening onto two futures, and his own voice saying something he couldn't hear, and—*

The vision snapped shut like a book.

Petra came back to himself on his knees on the archive floor, gasping, Wren's small hands gripping his face, her forehead nearly touching his, the ember-light fading back to its usual banked glow.

"Petra," she said—her ordinary voice, the small hoarse human one, though he'd begun hearing the First Tongue underneath it like a second melody. "You went somewhere. Your face went somewhere."

"He had a name," Petra whispered. "There was a man. A friend. He taught me—he had a tower, and he told me not to let them take the twin—" His hands were shaking. He looked at them as if they belonged to a stranger, which, he was beginning to suspect, they might. "Wren, what did you show me?"

"Not me." She shook her head slowly. "The ember. It knows you. When we got close to the burnt pages, it got... hungry. Like it remembered you." She hesitated, then added, with the terrible directness of children: "It's sad for you. I can feel it being sad. Why is it sad for you, Petra?"

"I don't know."

"You always say that."

"I know." He laughed, and it came out cracked down the middle. "I'm starting to hate the phrase."

* * *

Odelia made them stay the night. "The tide-tables," she explained, which meant nothing to Petra until she elaborated that the undercity flooded on a schedule set by the river-witches upstream, and the plank-roads became genuinely impassable at high water, and also that she had a spare cot and no intention of arguing about it.

So Petra lay on the cot in the workroom's antechamber, listening to black water lap against the shelf-legs, and did not sleep.

The bearded man's voice kept returning. *Swear it on the storm.* He turned the words over and over like a charm he was trying to price. Who was he? What was the twin? What choice had Petra made in that tower, on that last night, that someone had considered worth erasing from every record in the world?

Here was the thing he could not stop circling, the thought with teeth: people erased crimes to protect the guilty. But they also erased *secrets* to protect the innocent. And the scorched pages hadn't felt like shame. They'd felt like *care*. Something in the burn-marks' perfect neatness, in the warmth that lingered like a hand withdrawn reluctantly—something about it felt less like punishment than like protection.

Which was worse, somehow. Because if his past had been taken to protect someone, then somewhere there was a person alive today whose safety depended on Petra never remembering.

Or—his stomach turned over—whose safety depended on Petra never *being found*.

From the next room, softly, Wren began to talk in her sleep. Not the First Tongue this time—or not only it. Words drifted through the wall in a half-language, human syllables braided with the older music beneath: *gate... little ember... hold the door... hold the door...*

Petra lay in the dark with his heart hammering and understood, finally, completely, that he was not a man recovering from a misfortune. He was a locked room, and something outside it wanted in, and something inside it had died to keep it shut.

At dawn, Odelia shook him awake, already dressed, already holding a folio wrapped in oilcloth.

"Tide's out," she said. "We have perhaps two hours before the Wardens finish sweeping the district above my shop—you didn't think that raid on the charm-quarter was random, did you? They're looking for the child, and now they'll be looking for us. So." She thrust the folio into his arms. "There's a woman downriver who can read what's left on those burnt pages. Residue. Echo. The witches call it ash-reading, and before you ask—yes, she owes me, and yes, she's exactly as pleasant as that suggests. Sable Yew, of Marrowfen. We leave within the hour."

"Sable Yew," Petra repeated. The name landed somewhere in him with a strange weight, like a key fitting a lock he hadn't known he carried. "I've heard that name before."

"Everyone who's ever been punished by the Wardens has heard that name before," Odelia said dryly. "She makes a living off the ruined. Now pack up the child. And Master Hartwell—" For a moment the archivist's briskness slipped, and he saw the fear underneath, plain as the water below the planks. "Whatever the ash shows you downriver. Whatever you were. Try to remember that the man who sold luck-charms to frightened people in the rain—that man was real too. Don't let the past take him from you the way it took everything else."

Petra looked down at Wren, asleep on the cot, one fist curled against the faint glow in her chest, keeping her ember safe the way children keep treasures.

"I'll try," he said.

But all the way up the stairs, past the false wall, into the grey waking noise of Vessengard, one line rang in him louder than any other, warmer and more terrible than all the rest:

*Swear it on the storm.*

He just wished to heaven he knew whether he had.
