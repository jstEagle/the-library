## 6. The Hollow Warden's Bargain

The report on the charm-shop raid was due by sundown, and Sergeant Ilva Dren had not yet decided what to write in it.

She sat in the garrison house on Candlewick Row with the window shut against the rain and a cup of tea gone cold beside her elbow, turning her brush in her fingers the way she did when a lie needed to be built properly. A bad lie was a wall of stone. A good lie was a wall of brick — it looked like all the other walls, mortar and all, and you could take out one brick at a time if you ever had to.

The truth, for instance, would have read: *Subject Hartwell's suppressed magic detonated during arrest. Three Wardens injured. Street partially collapsed. Subject escaped into the undercity with an unidentified child.*

The truth would have gotten her hanged.

So instead she wrote: *Charm-seller Hartwell resisted arrest. In the ensuing scuffle, a lamp was overturned near a stockpile of volatile tallow-charms, causing structural damage. Subjects fled. Pursuit continues.* She signed it, blotted it, and set it on the pile with the other forty reports she'd filed in six years of service, each one a small brick in a wall she had been building since she was nineteen years old.

"You're still here." Corporal Fenwick leaned in the doorway, drying his hair with a rag. He had a lump rising on his forehead where a piece of Vessengard had hit him, and he wore it like a medal. "Sergeant. That man. Hartwell. The way the street came apart — that wasn't tallow."

"No," Ilva agreed.

"That was *magic*. Real magic. I felt it in my teeth."

"Then your report should say tallow," Ilva said mildly, "because teeth are not evidence, Corporal, and High Warden Corvess does not pay for feelings."

Fenwick opened his mouth, thought better of it, and went off to complain to someone who would listen. Ilva looked at the window, where the rain ran down the glass in long silver threads, and let herself think about her father.

Her father had been an oath-keeper. Not the way the old ballads meant it — no sword, no banner, no dying dramatically at a gate — but the quiet kind, the kind who kept a covenant alive by keeping its memory alive, in a cellar, in a jar of ink, in a daughter's head. He used to say: *Ilva, when they come for the last of it, don't fight them in the street. Fight them from inside the house. It's warmer in there, and nobody checks the pantry.*

He had been wrong about one thing. They hadn't come for the last of it. They had come for the *first* of it — the ember-child, the Oath reborn — and they had come wearing her own uniform.

* * *

Corvess summoned her at dusk.

The High Warden's office occupied the top floor of the Spire, and it was famous for two things: the view over all nine districts of Vessengard, and the fact that no one could remember Corvess ever being young. There were portraits in the lower halls of Wardens dead two hundred years, and Corvess looked like none of them and all of them at once, the way a mountain looks like every mountain.

"Sergeant Dren," he said, without looking up from his desk. "Your report on the Hartwell raid."

"You've read it, sir."

"I have read it." He set down his pen with great care, as though pen-setting-down were a discipline he had mastered over long centuries. "It is a competent report. It is also, I believe, inaccurate in several particulars. Sit."

Ilva sat. Her heart was doing something undignified, so she made it stop.

"Do you know why we burn magic, Sergeant?" Corvess said. He rose and went to the window. Below, the city glittered wetly, ten thousand hearths and lanterns, none of them enchanted. "It is not fear. Fear is for constables and grandmothers. We burn magic because magic is a *door*, and doors admit things from both sides. A hundred years ago this realm stood at the threshold of Ashfall, and everything that came through nearly ended us. The ban is not cruelty. It is a locked door in a house with children sleeping upstairs."

"I understand, sir."

"Do you?" He turned. His eyes were the color of banked coals, which everyone said was a trick of the lamplight, and which Ilva had privately never believed. "Then explain to me why, when your squad cornered a fugitive storm-caller, you allowed him to escape rather than deploy the null-chains issued to you."

The room went very still. Rain ticked against the glass.

"The chains were deployed, sir," Ilva said. "They failed. Whatever that man carries, it burned through them like—"

"They did not fail." Corvess crossed back to his desk and lifted a small object from it: a null-chain, scorched black, its binding-runes eaten away as though by acid. "I examined these myself. Null-chains do not fail, Sergeant. They are the single most reliable instrument this order possesses. Which means either your armorer is a criminal, or you never intended to use them."

Every instinct Ilva owned screamed at her to deny it, to brazen it out, to build another brick. But her father's voice was in her ear again, patient as ever: *when the wall's coming down anyway, dear, be the one who decides where it falls.*

"Suppose I didn't," she said.

Corvess's eyebrows rose a fraction — the most expression anyone in the garrison had ever seen him produce.

"Suppose I looked at that man," Ilva went on, "and saw a storm-caller who has spent five years selling luck-charms to frightened grandmothers without once calling a cloud. Suppose I looked at the child with him and thought, *that child is barefoot in the rain and the Wardens are the ones chasing her.* Suppose I decided the door you keep talking about doesn't need locking so much as it needs *guarding*, and that a sergeant who burns a little girl to protect a lock is not guarding anything worth the name."

Silence. Somewhere below, a cart rattled over cobblestones.

"You are either the bravest officer I have ever employed," Corvess said at last, "or the most foolish. Fortunately for you, I am in need of both." He set the ruined chain down. "You will lead the search for Hartwell and the ember-child. You will find them before any other unit does. And when you find them—" he smiled, and it was like watching frost form, "—you will bring them to me. Alive. All of them. Do we understand each other?"

"Yes, sir," said Ilva, who understood him perfectly, and who understood also that she now had perhaps three days before he decided she was more foolish than brave.

* * *

Finding Petra Hartwell turned out to be embarrassingly easy, because finding him was not the problem; the problem was deciding what to say when she did.

She tracked them the way her father had taught her — not by magic, which she had none of, but by listening. The undercity talked. Barge-men talked. Rat-catchers talked. And somewhere beneath the Fishmarket, in the flooded archive district where even the Wardens' maps gave up, people had begun whispering about a tall charm-seller asking after banned books, accompanied by a small girl who carried no shadow.

Ilva went alone, out of uniform, in a fishwife's oilskin with her hair bound up under a scarf. She told herself it was tradecraft. She knew it was because she wanted, just once, to knock on a door as a person.

The archive entrance was a drowned stair behind a shuttered chandlery, and the man who answered her knock was not what she expected. She had prepared herself for the storm-caller of the raid — the wild-eyed mage whose grief had torn a street open. What she got instead was a tired man in a patched coat holding a candle, squinting at her like she might be a customer.

"If you're here about the luck-charms," he said, "we're closed. Also we never sold any. Also I'm not Petra Hartwell, whoever that is."

"I led the raid on your shop," Ilva said.

The candle flame wavered. Behind him, deeper in the dark, she heard a small intake of breath — the child — and the soft scrape of an archivist's chair being pushed back in a hurry.

"You," Hartwell said. His voice changed entirely, the shopkeeper draining out of it and something older and colder filling the space. Rain-light from the stairwell caught his face, and for a moment Ilva saw what the Wardens' files must have seen five years ago: a storm given a man's shape. "You put three of my neighbors in the infirmary."

"Their houses are standing," Ilva said. "That was me too. I wrote the report that kept the Wardens from tearing this whole district apart looking for you. Brick by brick." She held his stare. "I can stand out here in the rain and we can admire each other's suspicions, or you can let me in, because I have maybe two days before Corvess sends someone who isn't me, and what I have to tell you won't keep."

From the darkness behind him, a small voice — clear, unhurried, utterly certain — spoke a word in a language Ilva had only ever heard in her father's forbidden notebooks. One word. *Come.*

Hartwell flinched as though the word had been spoken into his chest. He looked back over his shoulder, then at her, and something in him made a decision he clearly hated.

"Two minutes," he said. "And if you reach for iron, the rain and I will both be very disappointed in you."

* * *

Down in the flooded archive, by the light of lamps that were definitely illegal, Ilva laid it out.

Odelia Vance listened with her chin propped on her fist, radiating the particular skepticism of a woman who had catalogued four thousand lies professionally. The child — Wren — sat cross-legged on a dry crate, watching Ilva with eyes that made her feel catalogued right back. Hartwell stood with his arms folded, radiating suspicion with such commitment that it was almost architectural.

"Corvess knows about the girl," Ilva said. "Not suspects. *Knows.* He called her the ember-child before my report reached his desk. Before the raid, even. He sent me to find you because he believes I'm loyal, and because I'm the best tracker he has, and because—" she paused, choosing the brick carefully, "—he wanted the failure. A failed raid gives him grounds for martial law. District lockdowns, house-to-house searches, the works. He doesn't want to catch you quietly, Hartwell. He wants the whole city turned over, because whatever he's hunting, he expects it to run."

"And what is he hunting?" Odelia asked. "In your professional opinion."

"The ember," Ilva said. "The last fire of the Oath. My father—" she stopped. Started again, quieter. "My father was an oath-keeper. A real one. He taught me the histories the Wardens burned. The covenant between the realms, the Gate of Ashfall, the oath-keepers who tended it. He taught me that the ban isn't a locked door protecting children." She looked at Wren. "It's a tomb. Corvess means to seal the realms forever, and the ember is the key, and the key is sitting on that crate eating an apple."

Wren paused mid-bite. She considered Ilva for a long moment with those unsettling, unshadowed eyes. Then she took another bite, which Ilva chose to interpret as a verdict of provisional acceptance.

"This is touching," Hartwell said flatly. "It's also exactly what a Warden would say. You want me to believe the woman who raided my shop, who put hands on that child, is secretly the resistance?"

"I want you to believe the evidence," Ilva said. "Check my boots."

Everyone stared at her.

"Warden-issue boots have a null-ward stitched into the sole," she said, pulling one off and tossing it across the crates. "It deadens small magics within a pace. Your archivist can verify the stitching. Now check the left heel."

Odelia turned the boot over, went very still, and made a sound like a scholar finding an uncatalogued manuscript. Stitched into the heel, invisible unless you knew to look, was a second mark — an old one, hand-worked, in a pattern Ilva had known since childhood.

"The keeper's knot," Odelia breathed. "That's an oath-keeper's sigil. That hasn't been worked in this city in twenty years."

"My father's work," Ilva said. "I've worn it under their ward for six years. Every raid I've led, I've led with that in my heel. I burned nothing I didn't have to. I lost more reports than I care to count. And I am telling you that in approximately two days, Corvess stops trusting me, starts hunting me, and comes for that girl with a legion and instruments I genuinely hope you never see." She took her boot back and laced it. "So. Do we understand each other?"

Hartwell studied her for a long time. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed — a short, exhausted sound with no humor in it whatsoever.

"A Warden," he said, "with a rebel stitch in her heel, offering to help the fugitives she was sent to catch. Do you know how many times I've dreamed of this exact conversation? Usually there's more wine and everyone's taller."

"He's like this," Odelia murmured to Ilva, sympathetically. "You get used to it."

"Ignore him," said Wren — aloud, in trade-tongue, her first full sentence in it, delivered with the gravity of a magistrate. Everyone turned to look at her. She pointed at Hartwell, then at Ilva, then brought her small hands together, lacing her fingers. *Together,* the gesture said, plain as print.

"She's not wrong," Hartwell sighed. He rubbed his face. "Fine. Sergeant. Ilva. If Corvess wants the city turned over, we can't stay in it. Where's the hole in his net?"

"There isn't one in the walls," Ilva said. "There's one in the river. Cargo barges leave the Fishmarket locks twice a week for the fen-country downriver — Marrowfen and beyond. Wardens don't inspect river traffic; it's beneath our dignity, which is the most useful thing about us. I can forge transit papers. I've been forging them for six years." She stood, and found, to her mild surprise, that some knot she'd been carrying since the raid had loosened. "But we move tomorrow night, before Corvess's patience runs out. And Hartwell — one more thing."

He looked up.

"When Corvess spoke of the ember-child, he wasn't guessing. He described her shadow. Or the lack of one." Ilva met his eyes steadily. "Whatever that girl is, he's known about her longer than you've been in this city. Longer than your exile, I'd wager. Possibly longer than either of us has been alive." She pulled her fishwife's hood up. "Sleep fast. Tomorrow we go downriver, and I suspect the water is the last place in this realm that will feel safe."

Behind her, in the lamplight of the drowned archive, Wren finished her apple and set the core aside with great ceremony — and in the dark water beyond the lamplight's reach, something vast and patient turned over in its sleep, and began, slowly, to rise.
