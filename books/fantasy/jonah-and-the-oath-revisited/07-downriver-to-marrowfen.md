## 7. Downriver to Marrowfen

The forged papers said we were a wool merchant, his daughter, and two hired hands bound for the tanneries at Marrowfen. The papers were very good. Ilva had a forger's hand and a traitor's nerve, and she'd filled out the manifests with the bored precision of someone who had stamped ten thousand documents she hadn't read.

The daughter was the problem.

"You can't put her down as my daughter," I'd argued, hunched over the kitchen table in Ilva's borrowed safehouse while she inked the last line. "I have a face people remember. A child with no shadow is a thing people *never* forget."

"Then teach her to make one," Ilva said, not looking up.

Which is how, an hour before dawn, in the grey wet dark of the Vessengard docks, I found myself crouched beside a seven-year-old girl on the rotting planks of Pier Nine, holding a stub of charcoal and explaining shadows.

"It's simple," I said. "Everyone has one. You just—hold your hand like this, let the lantern light catch it, and look bored. Boredom is the best disguise there is. Nobody looks twice at a bored child."

Wren looked at me with the enormous patience of the very young and the very old, took the charcoal from my fingers, and drew a shadow on the dock boards. A good one, too—long and faithful, with little wing-edges where her fingers had smudged it, as if the shadow itself might take flight.

"That's cheating," I said.

She shrugged. It was a very eloquent shrug. Five days of traveling with her had taught me that Wren could conduct entire arguments without a single sound, and win most of them.

The barge was called the *Patient Merle*, which struck me as either hopeful or ironic, and its captain was a broad woman named Doss who chewed cloves and asked no questions, because Ilva had paid her enough not to. We boarded in the blue hour before sunrise, four travelers and a cargo of wet wool nobody would ever inspect, and Vessengard slid away behind us in the rain.

I stood at the stern rail and watched the city go.

You would think a man would feel joy, leaving the place that hunted him. And there was some—I felt it loosen in my chest, the constant low hum of fear I'd carried so long I'd mistaken it for a heartbeat. But underneath the relief was something sadder. Ten years I had lived in Vessengard. Ten years of small rooms and smaller talk, of selling luck-charms made of nothing but string and confidence, of swallowing every thunderhead that gathered behind my teeth. I had shrunk myself down to fit inside the city's law, and now, leaving it, I found I didn't know what size I was.

Beside me, Wren gripped the rail with both hands and watched the walls recede, and her grip was white-knuckled, and I understood that she was watching something burn. Not the city. Something else. Something only she could see.

* * *

The river took us south through country I half-remembered and half-invented. There were willows leaning over the water like tired women washing their hair. There were kingfishers, two of them, stitching blue along the banks. There were locks and toll-stations where Doss presented our papers and officials squinted at them and waved us through, each time, and each time my heart performed a small ugly gymnastic routine and then apologized for the fuss.

Odelia spent the first day below decks with her catalogues, wrapped in three blankets despite the mild air, muttering about damp and the mortality of paper. Tamsin sat apart from all of us, arms crossed, radiating the specific fury of a person who has been dragged into a rescue against her will and has not yet decided whether to be grateful. She and I had not spoken more than six words since the undercity. Most of those words had been "don't."

And Wren—

Wren discovered the river.

It happened near midday. She had been sitting very still by the rail, the way she did, watching the water with that unnerving total attention of hers, when a fish jumped—a big silver carp, lazy and glittering—and hung in the air a moment longer than physics strictly allowed. Just a heartbeat. Just long enough to wink, if a carp could wink.

Wren's whole body went rigid with delight.

And then she spoke.

"*Sela-veth,*" she said, aloud, clear as a bell over the water. "*Sela-veth, orru en-dai.*"

Every hair on my arms stood up.

I knew the language. That was the horror of it—I *knew* it, the way you know a face in a dream, the way your mouth remembers a taste from before memory starts. The words moved through me like cold water through a dry channel, finding grooves I didn't know I had. *Sela-veth.* Small-bright-one. Leaper. It was a name for the fish. She was greeting the fish.

"*Orru en-dai*" — come again, return soon.

The carp splashed down and was gone, and Wren turned to me with her face lit up like a window at dusk, waiting for me to share the joke, and I realized my mouth was open and nothing was coming out of it.

"What," said Tamsin, from across the deck, "was that."

"I don't—" I started, and stopped, because that wasn't true. I did know. Somewhere under ten years of careful forgetting, I knew exactly what that was. "That's the First Tongue."

Odelia came up the companionway so fast she tripped on the top step and finished the ascent on her knees, dignity abandoned, eyes enormous above her blanket-wrapped shoulders. "Say it again," she breathed at Wren. "Child. Please. Say anything."

Wren looked at the three of us—at Odelia hungry, at Tamsin bristling, at me apparently having a religious experience against the rail—and, being Wren, she chose mischief. She pointed at the clouds.

"*Vessu-marr,*" she said solemnly. "*Vessu-marr, ketha-lune.*"

"She says the clouds are tired," I heard myself translate, my own voice strange in my ears. "Tired, and going home to sleep."

Silence on the deck. Even the river seemed to quiet.

"How do you know that?" Tamsin demanded. Her voice had gone sharp, the way it went sharp when she was frightened, which I remembered now—remembered with a lurch—she always had done, even as my apprentice, even at eleven years old, even mid-lesson with the candles guttering. Fear first, questions second. I had never managed to teach her out of it. I had never tried hard enough.

"I don't know how I know," I said honestly. "It's like remembering a language I never learned. Like it was scooped out of me and left the shape behind."

Odelia had produced, God help me, a notebook. "The First Tongue," she said, writing as she talked, "predates every kingdom on this coast. The archives hold maybe forty attested words, mostly carved on gate-stones older than the Gate itself. Scholars have killed careers over less than what this child said about *clouds*." She peered at Wren over her spectacles. "Where did you learn it?"

Wren considered the question with great seriousness. Then she touched her own chest, right over the place where the shard lived—the ember-light I could feel humming under her ribs whenever I carried her, faint as a second heartbeat—and shrugged.

From in here, the shrug said. From the thing I'm made of.

Something cold walked down my spine. Because I had been translating without thinking, the way you breathe, the way you flinch—and every word she spoke landed in me with a weight of *rightness*, like a key turning in a lock I'd forgotten I owned. Which raised a question I was not ready to ask: why would a man who'd had his memories stolen still carry the language they were written in?

I filed the question away in the drawer where I kept all the others. The drawer was getting full. The drawer was beginning to bulge.

* * *

That evening, moored in the reeds past the third lock while Doss drank tea with a lock-keeper and lied charmingly about our cargo, Wren wouldn't eat.

This was new. The child ate like a stevedore; Odelia had already begun referring to her, with deep affection, as "the appetite." But tonight she sat with bread untouched in her lap, watching the fire Doss had permitted in the little iron stove, and her shoulders had crept up around her ears, and when I sat down beside her she leaned into my side—not for comfort, I realized. To hide. The way you press yourself into a wall when something is coming down the corridor.

"Wren?" I said quietly. "What's wrong?"

She shook her head. Pressed closer.

"Is it the Wardens?"

Headshake.

"The shard? Is it hurting?"

A pause. Then, slowly, reluctantly, the smallest nod I have ever seen.

Everything in me went still. "Does it hurt all the time?"

Another pause, longer. Then she lifted one hand, palm up, and made a gesture I understood immediately and wished I didn't: fingers closing, closing, closing around a light until there was nothing left to close around. Empty. Gone.

"*Enna-sel,*" she whispered. "*Enna-sel, Petra-marr.*"

My name in the First Tongue. *Petra-marr.* Storm-father, or storm-keeper, or something older and truer than either. And *enna-sel* — burning low. Burning out.

The fire crackled. Up on deck, Tamsin's boots paced their irritated rhythm. The river murmured against the hull, patient as everything old.

"You're afraid of burning out," I said. Not asking. Understanding arriving in me the way her language arrived—through grooves I didn't know I had. "The shard. It's using you to stay lit, isn't it? Your warmth. Your—" I groped for it. "*Yourself.*"

She nodded against my arm. And then, because she was Wren, and because even terrified children are braver than grown men give them credit for, she pulled back far enough to look at me and make herself plain. She held up her hands and cupped them, and between them—faint, so faint, a glow I hadn't seen since the night the Wardens burned the shop—rose a thread of ember-light. And as I watched, the thread flickered. Guttered. Dimmed to almost nothing before she closed her fists and snuffed it, breathing hard, like a swimmer surfacing.

Five days ago that light had been bright enough to read by.

"Oh," I said. Very quietly. "Oh, little one."

She was dying. Slowly, invisibly, in the way a candle dies—not all at once but constantly, every hour, every breath, feeding the flame with everything she was. And she had known. She had known the whole time, this child who greeted fish and named clouds and drew shadows on docks, and she had said nothing, because saying it would make it real, and because—God, because she thought it was her fault. I could see it in her face. Children always think it's their fault.

"No," I said, and took her hands, and folded them shut around the last warmth of that guttering light, keeping it safe between my palms. "Listen to me. Whatever that thing is spending—it doesn't get to spend *you*. Do you hear me? We're going to Marrowfen, and there's a woman there called Sable Yew who knows things about rivers and oaths and old fires, and we are going to ask her, politely or otherwise, how to keep you burning *without* burning up. That's the whole plan. That's the only plan. Everything else is details."

Her eyes searched mine. In their depths, that impossible depth, something eased—one degree, one hair's breadth, the way ice eases at the very start of thaw.

"*Ketha?*" she said. Small voice. *Truly?*

"Truly," I said. And then, because I am a charm-seller and lying is technically my trade, I added, "I've never once broken a promise to a customer."

She made a sound I had never heard her make before. It took me a moment to identify it, because it was so ordinary, so heartbreakingly ordinary: a laugh. Small and rusty and startled out of her, quickly smothered, but a laugh. Odelia, pretending not to listen from her nest of catalogues, wrote something in her notebook with a slightly unsteady hand.

We sat together while the fire settled and the reeds whispered and the river carried us south through the dark. At some point her head tipped against my shoulder and her breathing went long and even, and I stayed very, very still, because I have held many things in my life—storms, secrets, my tongue—but I had never held a sleeping child, and I discovered it was the heaviest and lightest thing I had ever held at once.

Across the stove, Tamsin stopped pacing. She looked at Wren asleep against me, and her expression did something complicated—anger and grief and longing all trying to occupy the same face at the same time and none of them willing to yield the ground. For a moment I thought she would say something. Her mouth opened.

"Your cloak's on fire," she said instead, flatly.

I looked down. A thin curl of smoke was rising off my left sleeve, where the storm under my skin had leaked out sideways, as it did now when my feelings ran strong. I patted it out with as much dignity as I could gather, which was not much.

"Noted," I said.

"Good," said Tamsin, and turned away, and I would swear on any god still listening that her shoulders were shaking, and that it was not entirely rage.

* * *

Near midnight I went up on deck alone, leaving Wren tucked in blankets between Odelia's watchful bulk and the warm side of the stove. The sky had cleared. Stars everywhere, reckless with themselves, and the river running silver under them, and somewhere ahead—two days, maybe three, if the locks were kind—the marsh country called Marrowfen, where a witch waited who could read the residue of scorched memories, though I didn't know yet that she could, though I only knew her name and that Odelia trusted it.

I stood at the rail and let myself want things. It was a dangerous practice; I recommend it sparingly. I wanted Sable Yew to have answers. I wanted the ember fed by something other than a child. I wanted Tamsin to forgive me for whatever it was I'd done to her, which I couldn't remember doing, which somehow made it worse. I wanted to know whose voice it was that lived in the scorched pages of my past, the friend's voice, the tower, the choice.

And beneath all of it, quiet as sediment, another wanting stirred—the question the river had been asking me all day with every word of Wren's that fell into its grooves inside me:

If I still carried the First Tongue... what else had they taken, and left the shape of?

Behind me, below decks, a child slept with a dying star in her chest. Ahead, the fen-country waited with its lights and its lies and its tolls. And far back upriver, so far I could almost believe it was another life, Vessengard's towers stood black against the stars—and on the tallest of them, though I could not see it and would not know it for weeks, a signal fire kindled, and word began to travel down every road and river after us:

*The ember-child lives. Find her.*

But that night the river was kind, and the stars were generous, and a man who had shrunk himself small for ten years stood at a rail and grew, inch by inch, back toward his true size—because a small voice had trusted him with its fear, and he had promised, and he intended, whatever it cost him, to keep it.
