## 8. The River-Witch's Toll

Marrowfen smelled the way old rivers dream: mud and mint and something faintly sweet rotting under everything, like fruit left too long in a grandmother's cellar.

Petra stood at the edge of the stilt-town with Wren's small hand wrapped in his and tried to decide whether that smell was comforting or ominous. He settled on both, which seemed to be his default setting these days.

"Charming," Odelia said behind him, in the tone of a woman who had catalogued four hundred banned books and found exactly none of them charming. She adjusted her satchel of rescued records and peered down the boardwalk. "It smells like a library after a flood."

"That's because it is one," said Ilva, leaning on her crutch at the rail of the barge. Her leg was still angry from the raid — the wound had gone from red to an ugly violet, and she'd stopped pretending it didn't hurt somewhere around the second day on the river. "Half of Marrowfen floods every spring. The other half floods out of spite."

The town rose around them on wooden legs — houses perched on pilings above brown water, connected by walkways that swayed when you breathed on them wrong. Lanterns hung from eaves on long cords so they could be hauled up when the river got ambitious. A cat watched them from a rooftop with the expression of a customs official who already knew they were smuggling something.

Wren tugged Petra's sleeve. She didn't speak aloud much yet — the First Tongue came to her in murmurs and half-words, like someone remembering a song — but she had developed an entire vocabulary of tugs. This one meant *I'm hungry*, or possibly *that cat is judging us*, or possibly both.

"I know," Petra said. "Food first. Witch second. That's the order of operations."

"You can't feed her," Odelia said. "We have eleven coppers and a button I believe used to be yours."

"It was a good button," Petra said with dignity.

They found food anyway, because Marrowfen ran on barter and Petra still had hands that remembered charm-work even if half his past did not. He traded three luck-knots to a fishwife for smoked eel and bread, and if the luck-knots were only string and patience and no magic at all — well. That was rather the point of them. Wren ate her eel sitting cross-legged on the boardwalk with her eyes closed in a way that suggested she was thanking it personally.

*Little ember,* he almost said, and stopped himself. The name kept rising in him unbidden, along with flashes — a tower, a voice, a gate — fragments the shard in her chest stirred up whenever they were close. He was learning to let them drift past like fish under ice. Grabbing at them only made the emptiness ache worse.

Because that was the thing he hadn't told any of them yet. Since the archive, since Odelia had shown him the scorched pages of his own trial, he could feel the holes. Not as absence, exactly. Absence you don't notice. These were more like the way you notice a missing tooth — your tongue keeps going back, back, back, probing the gap, finding nothing, going back again.

Five years of his life had been taken from him. And something in him kept trying to walk into those rooms and finding them swept bare.

"Petra." Odelia touched his elbow. "You've been staring at that eel for a full minute."

"Just admiring it."

"Mm." She gave him the look she probably gave misfiled documents. "Sable Yew lives at the far end, where the water goes dark. Try not to insult her. She's older than the Wardens and twice as unforgiving."

"How do you know her?"

Odelia's mouth did something complicated. "She tried to ban my first book."

"And?"

"And she was right," Odelia admitted. "It was terrible. Come on."

* * *

The far end of Marrowfen was quieter, the houses farther apart, the lanterns fewer. The water here moved slow and black, and things lived in it that the locals left offerings for rather than names. The last house stood alone on its pilings, wider than the others, its walls hung with drying herbs and river-glass and dozens of small bells that made no sound at all until you looked away from them, at which point they chimed, briefly, smugly.

A sign hung over the door, hand-painted:

**SABLE YEW — READINGS, REMEDIES, REVENGES**
*(Inquire within. Inquire politely.)*

"She sounds delightful," Ilva muttered.

Petra knocked. The door opened before his knuckles landed the third time.

The woman in the doorway was small and round and wrapped in shawls the color of pond-bottom, with iron-gray hair coiled up and pinned through with what appeared to be actual fishing hooks. Her eyes were pale green and utterly unblinking, the eyes of something that had spent centuries watching the water watch back.

"You," she said, looking at Petra. Then at Wren. Her nostrils flared, very slightly, the way a horse does. "Oh," she said, softer. "*Oh.* You brought it here. To *my* river."

"We brought her," Petra said carefully. "Her name is Wren."

"Wren. Yes." Sable Yew stepped back from the doorway. "Come in before the river notices you. It gossips."

Inside, the house was warm and dim and crowded in the way of homes that have decided furniture is optional but *things* are not. Bundles of herbs. Jars of river-mud labeled in a spidery hand (*Good Mud, Better Mud, Do Not Open*). A kettle already boiling, though no fire sat beneath it. Three chairs arranged by the window, exactly three, as if she'd known.

Ilva lowered herself into one with a grunt of relief she'd deny later. Odelia took the second, already scanning the shelves with the hunger of her profession. Petra guided Wren toward the third — and Sable Yew put a hand on the girl's shoulder, gently, stopping her.

"May I?" the witch asked Wren directly, bending to her level.

Wren studied her. Then she nodded once, gravely, the way she approved most things — like a tiny magistrate.

Sable Yew laid two fingers against Wren's sternum, over the heart, and closed her eyes. The bells on the walls all rang at once, softly, and went silent. When the witch opened her eyes again they were wet.

"Well," she said briskly, straightening and wiping her face with a shawl-corner as if tears were simply weather. "That explains the dreams. My river's been dreaming of fire for a month. I thought it was indigestion."

"What did you feel?" Odelia leaned forward.

"An ember. The ember." Sable Yew fixed Petra with her unblinking stare. "You know what she carries. The archivist told you, or I'll eat my hat, and I don't wear a hat because hats are for people with something left to hide."

"We know," Petra said. "Dragon-fire. The last light of the Oath between the realms. We need to reach the Gate of Ashfall before it gutters out entirely. We were told—" he hesitated, "—we were told there were three oath-keepers still living. That you were one."

Something moved behind the witch's face, old and slow, like a shape passing under dark water.

"Three," she repeated. "Yes. There were three of us who swore the keeping-oath, back when keeping meant something. Me. Vess, who guards the Gate itself, poor dear, and has been dying of duty for a hundred years. And—" she paused, and her gaze flicked over Petra, head to foot, with an expression he couldn't read, "—and a third. Who is why you have holes in you, storm-caller. Don't look surprised. You carry your missing years like a scent. Any witch worth her salt can smell a theft."

The room went very quiet. The kettle boiled on without a fire. Ilva's hand had drifted, unconsciously, toward the knife at her belt; she caught herself and pretended she'd been scratching her knee.

"I don't remember," Petra said. His voice came out flat. "That's rather the problem."

"No," Sable Yew agreed. "But you remember *wanting* to. And wanting is a kind of key." She turned away, busied herself with the kettle, poured five cups of something that steamed green and smelled of mint and marsh-root. "Now. You want my help. Passage-craft, warding-song, the old words for the Gate — I can give all of it. I swore an oath, and oaths outlive their makers' good sense."

"But," Odelia said.

"But there's a toll." Sable Yew handed out cups. Wren took hers in both hands and sniffed it suspiciously. "There's always a toll. Not coin — what would I do with coin? Fish don't take it and the dead won't. Magic moves on exchange, child. Always has. If you take power from the old places, you pay from yourself. That's the whole grammar of it, and everyone's forgotten the grammar, and that's why the world is cracking at the seams."

"What kind of payment?" Ilva asked warily.

"A memory. One true memory, freely given. Freely is the important word — a toll paid grudgingly curdles, and I'd have to throw it back." She sipped her tea. "And before the archivist starts composing a treatise: yes, I know what was done to this man's memory already. No, I will not be taking from the same wound. I'm a witch, not a vulture."

Petra set down his cup. His hands were steady, which felt like a lie his body was telling on his behalf.

"Which memory?"

"Yours to choose. That's the cruelty and the mercy of it." Sable Yew's voice gentled, just slightly. "Most people assume they'll pick a bad one. A grief, a shame. They think pain is the price. But pain isn't what memories are made *of* — love is. Every memory worth keeping is made of love wearing some costume or other. So when you choose, storm-caller, understand what you're choosing. You're not giving me your worst day. You're giving me one of your true ones."

The silence stretched. Outside, the river lapped patiently at the pilings, patient as a creditor.

"I'll do it," Petra said.

"Petra." Odelia's voice cracked like a whip. "You have *five years missing*. You're walking around with a hole in your head the size of a betrayal, and you want to dig a second hole next to it?"

"I want to get Wren to the Gate while there's still a Gate to reach." He looked at the girl. She was watching him over the rim of her cup, eyes enormous, and she tugged her sleeve down over her hand the way she did when she was frightened and pretending not to be. "Every day we wait, she burns a little warmer and a little shorter. I've seen it. You've all seen it. If the price of the road is one memory, then the road is cheap."

"The road is never cheap," Sable Yew said. "It only sends the bill later." But she was already rising, moving to a low cabinet, drawing out a shallow bowl of dark river-clay. "Sit, storm-caller. On the floor, by the hearthstone. The floor remembers better than chairs do."

Petra sat. The clay bowl went into his lap. It was cool, then warm, then cool again, breathing somehow.

"Now," the witch said, kneeling across from him. "Choose. And say it aloud. Freely given means freely *named*."

He cast through himself the way a man casts through a drawer, feeling for what's real. Five years gone, and beyond the hole, a life assembled from surviving: the shop, the rain, the charms, the long gray discipline of being small. And scattered through it —

The morning he'd taught Wren to fold a luck-knot and she'd gotten it wrong and made something better instead, and laughed — her first laugh, silent, whole-bodied, like a bell rung inside out.

No. Not that one. He flinched away from it. Not that one, not yet, maybe not ever.

The taste of the bread in Vessengard, the first week of his exile, bought with his last coins, eaten on a church step in the rain. Warm. Stupid. Perfect.

Closer. Smaller. He went digging for something he could stand to lose, and found —

The harbor at Callowmere, the summer before everything. He'd been young. There had been a festival, paper lanterns on the water, and a woman whose name he could still reach — Maren, Maren with flour on her sleeves — and they'd stayed on the pier until the lanterns burned down to embers and drifted out on the tide, and she'd said, *look, they go out slow, like they're deciding to,* and he'd thought: *I will remember this exact moment for the rest of my life.*

He'd been right, as it turned out. Just not in the way he'd meant.

"This one," Petra said quietly. "The lanterns. Callowmere harbor. A woman named Maren. Paper lights going out on the tide." His throat tightened. "Freely given."

Sable Yew held out her hands, palms up, over the bowl. "Then let it go. All the way. Holding a corner back is how people come back haunted."

He reached for the memory the way you reach for a bird you intend to open your hands around. The pier. The flour on her sleeves. The lanterns dimming one by one, deciding to.

*Look. They go out slow.*

And then he opened his hands, and it went.

There was no pain. That was the worst part. There was a soft *sliding*, like a knot coming loose, and the bowl in his lap glowed faintly gold for a heartbeat — he saw the light reflected in Sable Yew's wide green eyes — and then it was done.

He sat there, breathing.

He knew he had chosen something. He knew he had said a woman's name — Maren, the syllables still in his mouth like a stone he'd spat out. But when he reached for her there was only the shape of reaching. A pier-shaped emptiness. Lantern-colored nothing. He knew that something about lanterns had mattered to him once, the way you know a room used to hold furniture from the pale rectangles on the walls.

"Oh," he said. His voice sounded far away. "Oh, it's — it doesn't hurt. That's horrible. Why doesn't it hurt?"

"Because the wound isn't in the body, child. It's in the story." Sable Yew lifted the bowl and poured its faint glow into a stoppered jar of river-water, where it swirled like a captured sunset. "Welcome to the honest cost of magic. Everyone thinks it's lightning and blood. It's this. It's always this." She corked the jar and set it among her shelves, between *Good Mud* and *Better Mud*, and for a moment her shoulders sagged, just slightly, as if the jar were heavier than jars should be. "I'll keep it safe. What's given freely can't be stolen, only returned. Whether it ever *is* returned depends on how the story goes."

Petra pressed his tongue against the new gap in his life. Back, back, back. Nothing. Back again.

Wren crawled into his lap without asking, the way she did, and put her small palm flat over his heart. She looked up at him with an expression far too old for her face, and she said one word aloud, in her rusted little voice, in the First Tongue — and this time he understood it without trying, the way you understand warmth:

*Why?*

"Because it was mine to give," he said. "And you're worth more than one evening of lanterns."

She frowned at him, deeply unsatisfied with this answer, and burrowed against his chest. The shard under her ribs pulsed once, faint and gold, and for a moment he felt it press against the hollow where the memory had been — felt it *notice* the gap, the way a current notices a hole in a dam.

Sable Yew saw it too. Her eyes narrowed.

"Hm," she said.

"*Hm* what?" Ilva demanded. "When a witch says 'hm,' I want details."

"The ember knows him," the witch said slowly. "Not just recognizes — *knows*. Like a key knows its lock. Which means whatever was taken from him, it wasn't taken cleanly. Something of him is tangled in that fire, and something of the fire is tangled in him." She began gathering cups, brisk again, businesslike, but her gaze kept sliding back to Wren's glowing chest. "Which is either very good news or the worst news I've heard this century, and I've heard some competition."

"Which?" Odelia asked.

"I don't know yet. Ask me after I've taught you the warding-songs." She paused at the shelf, and her voice dropped. "But I'll tell you this for free, storm-caller, since you've already paid more than enough tonight. The road to Ashfall runs through the Greymarch, and the Greymarch has eyes now. My river's been dreaming of fire for a month — but last night, for the first time, it dreamed of *boots*. A great many boots, marching north."

Ilva went very still. "Corvess."

"Or something wearing Corvess. Same thing, near enough." Sable Yew set the last cup down and looked at each of them in turn, and for a moment the small round woman seemed to fill the whole dim house, the way deep water fills a well. "Rest tonight. Eat. Laugh, if you can manage it — laughter is warding too, the oldest kind. Because tomorrow we make you ready, and the day after, you walk into the wastes carrying the last light of the old world on a child's heartbeat."

She turned to the window, where the black river slid past, and added, almost to herself:

"And the day after *that* — if my river dreams true — you'll find out you've been followed the whole way. By someone who knew your face before she ever saw it."

Petra looked up. "What do you mean, followed? Who—"

But the witch only smiled, sad and knowing, and began hanging fresh herbs by the fire, and would say nothing more that night.

Outside, far off down the dark water, something moved against the current — small, deliberate, alone — heading for Marrowfen like a debt coming home.
