## 9. The Apprentice Who Waited

Tamsin Vale had rehearsed this moment for five years.

She had rehearsed it in the bath, in the bread queue, in the long grey hours of Warden patrol when the rain came down like a judgment and her boots filled with water. She had rehearsed it standing at attention while Sergeant Ilva read out the morning's burn-list of confiscated grimoires. She had rehearsed it so many times that the words had worn smooth as river stones: *You left me. You left me, you left me, you left me.*

And now Petra Hartwell was standing in the doorway of Sable Yew's river-hut, holding a lantern he had no business holding, looking older and thinner and exactly the same, and every rehearsed word had evaporated like breath on glass.

"You," she said. Which was not one of the rehearsed words.

"Hello, Tamsin." He said it carefully, the way a man steps onto ice he suspects is thin. "You look well."

"I look *well*?" Her voice came out higher than she intended. Somewhere behind him, the mute child peered around his coat, and the archivist woman—Odelia Vance, whose banned histories Tamsin had personally catalogued for the Wardens' pyres—winced sympathetically, which made everything worse. Being pitied by Odelia Vance was like being pitied by a tax collector. "I have been *drowning*, you absolute—"

"Tamsin," Sable Yew said mildly, from her chair by the hearth, without looking up from the tea leaves she was reading. "Mind the jars."

Tamsin looked down. She was standing on the edge of a shelf of preserved newts, and one of the jars was wobbling. She stepped back. The jar settled. The newt inside gave her a look of profound amphibian disappointment, and she felt it was deserved.

* * *

Here is what Tamsin Vale had been doing for five years, in case anyone had wondered, because apparently nobody had:

She had been surviving.

When Petra vanished—mid-lesson, mid-sentence, mid-word, actually; he'd been explaining the difference between a called storm and a summoned one, and then he simply wasn't there anymore, his chalk still rolling off the desk, his teacup still steaming—she had waited three days before she panicked properly. Four days before she went to his lodgings and found them emptied, scrubbed, erased, as if the city itself had decided he'd never existed. Five days before the Hollow Wardens kicked in the door of their schoolroom and asked her, very politely, where her master had gone.

She hadn't known. That was the thing that had kept her up nights for years afterward. She genuinely hadn't known, and they'd looked into her eyes with those grey Warden eyes and seen only truth, and somehow that had been worse than if she'd lied. A liar they could sentence. An ignorant girl they could *use*. They'd let her go, but they'd remembered her name, and in Vessengard being remembered by the Wardens was like being marked by frost. It didn't hurt right away. It just meant everything you touched afterward was cold.

So she'd run too, in her way. Smaller distances. She'd apprenticed herself to a charm-maker who turned out to be a Warden informant, and escaped that, and apprenticed to a hedge-healer who turned out to be merely terrible, and endured that. She'd learned to bind small magics into thread and bone because big magic got people burned. She'd learned to keep her head down so thoroughly that sometimes she caught her reflection in shop windows and didn't recognize the hunched, careful creature there.

And all that time, the question sat in her chest like a swallowed coal: *Why did he leave? Why me? Why mid-lesson?*

She had imagined a hundred answers. He'd been arrested. He'd died. He'd never cared about her at all and had simply walked out of her life the way men walk out of rooms. Each answer had its own particular flavor of hurt, and she had tasted them all until she knew them better than her own handwriting.

None of the answers had been: *he's standing in a witch's hut holding a lantern, looking scared of me.*

* * *

"You can't be here," she said, once everyone had wedged themselves into Sable Yew's single room and the tea had been poured and ignored. "Do you understand what I'm saying? There's a bounty notice with your description on the board outside the Marrowfen ferry office. I've *read* it. I've nearly memorized it. 'Storm-caller, male, forty-ish, dangerous, approach with extreme caution.' Forty-ish. They flattered you."

"Did they," said Petra faintly.

"'Dangerous.' Yes. Very accurate, actually." She jabbed a finger at him. "The last time I saw you do real magic, you set fire to the lesson plans. On purpose. Because you said fear teaches faster than patience."

"That doesn't sound like me," said the child suddenly, and then clapped both hands over her mouth, eyes going enormous.

Everyone stared at her. It was the first time any of them had heard her speak aloud—the First Tongue or otherwise—and the word hung in the damp air of the hut like a struck bell.

"It isn't," Petra agreed softly, and something passed between them that Tamsin couldn't follow and didn't like following. Some current. Some shared weather.

She hated how much she wanted to know what it was.

* * *

Sable Yew, who had been letting the silence stretch the way other people let dough rise, finally spoke. "The girl needs an oath-keeper's craft, and Hartwell needs someone who remembers what he taught. You're the only apprentice he has left."

"He has no apprentices," Tamsin said. "He has a former student with a bounty-adjacent name and a talent for making charms that don't work. That's different. Apprentices get taught. Students get abandoned."

"Tamsin—"

"No." She stood up. The low ceiling forced even her anger to hunch. "No, you don't get to say my name like that, like we're still in the schoolroom and I've forgotten my lines. Do you want to know what happened after you disappeared? Because I've composed a list. I've had five years. It's a *long* list."

Petra sat down slowly on Sable's second chair, the rickety one, the one that groaned under anyone with grievances. "Tell me."

That was the worst part. He didn't argue. He didn't defend himself. He just sat there with his hands around the untouched tea like a man warming himself on someone else's fire, and waited, and the waiting was so patient and so familiar that her list nearly dissolved on the spot.

She held onto it anyway. She'd earned her list.

"One: the Wardens came. Two: they burned everything you ever wrote, including my notes, which were mine, thank you, I wrote them. Three: my mother lost her stall license because her daughter was known to study with a storm-caller, and we ate charity bread for a winter, and my mother never once complained, which was its own kind of unbearable. Four—" her voice cracked and she pushed through it, furious at the crack, "—four, I spent two years checking every stranger's face on the street because some part of me was sure you'd come back for me, and you know what? You never did. Not a note. Not a whisper. Nothing. I found out more about your disappearance from a *bounty notice* than from you."

The child had gone very still by the door. Odelia was studying her own hands. Even Sable had stopped pretending to read the leaves.

"And now," Tamsin finished, quieter, because the fury had burned down to the coals underneath, "now you show up needing things. Needing an oath-keeper. Needing help with the girl. And I'm supposed to what? Fall over myself? Pick up chalk again? Pretend there wasn't a winter of charity bread?"

"Yes," said Sable Yew, with the serene cruelty of the very old. "Ideally."

"No."

* * *

She meant it. That was the terrible thing. She stood in that hut meaning it with her whole scorched heart, and she watched Petra nod slowly, accept it, begin to rise and thank Sable for the tea and apologize for the intrusion—he was already folding himself away, already becoming smaller, and she recognized the motion with a sick lurch because she'd seen it in mirrors for five years—

—and the child moved.

Not toward Petra. Toward Tamsin. Bare feet silent on the floorboards, and she stopped just short of touching distance and looked up, and her eyes were ordinary brown eyes except when the hearthlight caught them, and then they weren't ordinary at all. There was an ember in there. Tamsin had seen dragon-fire exactly once, from a mile away, the night the sky over Vessengard turned the wrong color, and she had never forgotten it, and here it was, banked small, in a girl who couldn't weigh more than a bundle of kindling.

The child reached into her collar and drew out a cord, and on the cord hung a shard of something that glowed like a coal deciding whether to become a fire. And she held it out—not offering it, Tamsin realized, just showing it, the way you show a wound to a healer—and she said, in that language Tamsin didn't know but understood anyway, the way you understand thunder:

*"It hurts him too. He just forgets which parts are missing."*

Then she looked embarrassed, as if the words had used her mouth without permission, and retreated to Petra's side.

Nobody spoke. The newt jar bubbled gently, which Tamsin chose to interpret as commentary.

"What did she say?" Petra asked quietly. He clearly hadn't understood either. That was strange—that was wrong—but Tamsin filed it away, because her chest was doing something inconvenient and she needed both hands free to manage it.

"She said," Tamsin heard herself reply, "that you hurt too. And that you forget which parts are missing."

"Oh," said Petra. And then, after a moment, with the ghost of the old dry humor she'd missed like a limb: "She's not wrong. I've recently learned I'm missing rather a lot."

* * *

She didn't agree to anything that night. She wanted that on the record, hers if no one else's. She slept in the boat shed, alone, on a net full of knots that pressed their opinions into her back, and she lay awake composing refusals, elegant ones, final ones, and somewhere past midnight she fell asleep mid-refusal and dreamed of the schoolroom.

In the dream, the chalk was still rolling off the desk. It rolled and rolled and never fell, and Petra's voice kept going, calm and warm, explaining the difference between a called storm and a summoned one: *a called storm answers to grief, little wren—a summoned one answers to will, and only one of them forgives you afterward.* And young Tamsin in the dream raised her hand and asked the question she'd never gotten to ask: *called by whom?*

She woke with wet eyes and a stiff neck and the answer sitting in her like a stone dropped into deep water.

Called by grief. Called by whatever had taken him. And she had spent five years cursing a man for a crime she'd never investigated, because investigating meant hoping, and hoping meant the winter of charity bread might crack open and spill its actual shape, and she hadn't been able to afford that. Anger was cheaper. Anger kept.

But the ember-child had looked at her like she was worth crossing a room for. And Petra had sat down on the rickety chair and *waited*, and she knew that patience—it was his teaching patience, the patience he'd had for her when she melted her first focus crystal and cried about it, the patience she'd assumed had limits and discovered, mid-lesson, mid-word, that it did not—

She dressed. She crossed the dark yard. The river muttered against the pilings, and Sable's hut showed one line of lamplight, because of course the witch was still awake; witches were always awake, it was practically the job description.

Petra opened the door before she knocked. He was holding the lantern again. Behind him, the child slept curled in a chair like a cat who had chosen its household provisionally, pending review.

"I'm not helping you," Tamsin said, keeping her voice low and iron-hard. "Understand? I'm not your apprentice. I'm not forgiving you. I want it noted that this is a terrible idea and I think you're a coward and a fool."

"Noted," Petra said gravely.

"But the girl talked to me. Out loud. In a language I don't know, and I understood it anyway, and that has never happened to me, and I need to know why." She lifted her chin. "So I'm coming along. For the girl. Not for you. And if you vanish mid-sentence again, Petra Hartwell, I swear by every newt in that horrible hut, I will find you, and I will finish the lesson myself, and you will not enjoy my teaching methods."

Something moved across his face—relief, maybe, or grief, or the particular expression of a man who has just been handed a debt he knows he deserves. "That," he said, "sounds entirely fair."

Behind him, without opening her eyes, the child smiled.

And far off across the black water, downstream toward the drowned archive and the city and everything burning in it, Tamsin saw—or thought she saw—a light that was not a lamp and not a star, moving with purpose against the current, and felt the fine hairs on her arms rise, because some part of her, the part that had survived five years of Wardens, whispered: *they're coming.*

"They're coming, aren't they," she said.

Petra looked out at the water, and his hand found the child's shoulder, and the ember under her collar pulsed once, like a heartbeat answering a knock.

"Yes," he said. "We should pack."
