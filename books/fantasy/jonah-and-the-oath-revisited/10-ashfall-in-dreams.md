## 10. Ashfall in Dreams

Wren had learned that dreams came in two kinds.

There were the ordinary ones—the ones where she was falling, or late for something, or being chased by a goose with Petra's face—and then there were the *other* ones. The ones that didn't feel like dreams at all. The ones that felt like remembering.

Tonight was an other-one.

She knew it before her eyes were even open, because she could smell it: warm stone and cinders and something sweet underneath, like bread baked in a wood oven, like the air after lightning. She was standing on a road made of black glass, and the road glowed faintly under her bare feet, warm as a hearthstone.

Above her, the sky was not blue and not black but a deep smoldering red-gold, the color of the inside of a coal. Rivers of light drifted through it slowly, like fish through dark water. Far away—so far it should have been invisible—something enormous turned over in its sleep, and the whole sky shivered.

"Ashfall," Wren said aloud, because saying things aloud helped her hold on to them. Her voice sounded strange here, rounder, older. "This is Ashfall."

She had never been here. She had been here always. Both of those things were true, and in dreams both of those things could sit side by side like old friends sharing a bench.

She walked.

The road wound between hills of black glass too, studded with crystals that caught the ember-light and threw it back in colors Wren didn't have names for. Once, something small and bright darted across the road ahead of her—a lizard, maybe, except it left a trail of sparks, and when it paused to look at her, its eyes were little lanterns and it bowed, actually *bowed*, before skittering off into the glass-grass.

"Polite," Wren told it, approving.

The warmth in her chest—the shard, the ember, the thing that hummed under her ribs like a second heart—grew warmer as she walked. Not painful. More like coming home to a house you'd forgotten you owned. With every step, she understood more words she'd never been taught, felt more of the shape of this place settling into her like sand into a footprint.

That was when she heard the breathing.

It wasn't loud. That was the strange part. Something that size—you could hear it from miles away, you'd think, a breath like a bellows, like a storm. But it was quiet. Tired. Each exhale went on so long that Wren thought it might never end, and each inhale was thin, careful, rationed out like the last water in a flask.

She followed it around the shoulder of a glass hill, and there it was.

The dragon.

Wren had seen dragons in pictures—in Odelia's banned books, ink drawings of monsters rearing over burning cities, all teeth and terror. This was nothing like the pictures. This dragon lay curled around the base of a broken archway so vast that the hills looked like pebbles beside it, and even curled up, even *dying*, it was less like a monster than like a mountain that had decided, once, long ago, to be alive instead.

Its scales were the color of cooling embers—deep red going gray at the edges, ash creeping along its spine like frost claiming a window. One eye opened as Wren approached. It was gold, and ancient, and kind, and so tired that looking into it made Wren's own eyes sting.

"*Little ember*," the dragon said.

Its voice didn't come through the air. It came through the ground, through the glass, through the soles of Wren's feet and up into her bones, and it was the voice from her first memory—the fire, the gate, the voice calling her little ember—and hearing it again, Wren started to cry without quite deciding to.

"I know you," she said. "I don't know how I know you, but I know you."

"You have always known me." The dragon's head lowered, slow as a drawbridge, until one vast golden eye was level with her. Up close, Wren could see that the scales around its face were cracked, and through the cracks glowed a light that flickered unsteadily, like a candle in a draft. "I am Vess. Last warden of the Gate of Ashfall. And you—" a plume of smoke curled from its nostrils, soft as a sigh, "—you are the most impossible thing I have seen in a hundred years, little ember. You are walking. In my realm. Asleep in a barge on a mortal river."

"Petra says I talk in my sleep," Wren said. "In the First Tongue. He's learning it again. He doesn't remember why he knows it."

Something moved behind Vess's eye at the name—not surprise exactly. Grief wearing surprise's clothes.

"Does he," Vess said quietly. "And what else does he not remember?"

"That's the problem," Wren said, and sat down cross-legged on the warm glass, because her legs were getting tired and also because it seemed rude to keep towering over a dying dragon. "Nobody remembers anything. Petra doesn't remember. I don't remember. There was an Oath, Odelia says, between here and there, and somebody broke it, and now everything is going out like a lamp." She put her hand flat against her chest, over the shard. "I'm going out too. I haven't told him that part yet."

Vess was silent for a long moment. The great ribs rose and fell, rose and fell, each breath a little thinner than the last.

"Then let me show you," Vess said. "What was. What broke. You carry the last of it in your chest; you deserve to know what it *was*. Come here, little ember. Into the memory."

* * *

Wren had expected the past to look like a story. It didn't. It looked like standing inside a bell while it rang.

Vess touched one claw-tip, gentle as a mother's finger, to the shard-light glowing through Wren's skin, and the world folded—

—and unfolded—

—and Wren stood on a green hill under a green-gold sky, and the Gate of Ashfall was not broken. It was *whole*, twin arches leaning toward each other like two people about to kiss, and between them burned a pillar of light so beautiful that Wren's knees simply gave out and she sat down hard on grass that smelled like rain and honey.

Two fires burned in the heart of that light. Twin embers, turning slowly around each other, one gold and one white, and every living thing for a hundred miles in every direction leaned toward them the way flowers lean toward the sun.

"This is the Making," Vess said, and its voice was different here—younger, though still old, the way a river is old but moves young. "Come. Watch. It is worth watching twice, and I have watched it ten thousand times."

Figures gathered on the hill. Dragons, first—dozens of them, landing in a ring, folding their wings, bowing their great heads. And then, walking up the hill from the mortal side, *people*. Wren counted them as they came. Seven. Seven people in robes of seven colors, and at their head a woman with silver hair and a staff crowned with a piece of the sky.

"The seven oath-keepers," Vess said. "One from each of the seven peoples of the mortal shore. And we, the nine who remained of the dragonkind, to meet them. Between us, the Oath: that magic would flow between the realms like blood between a heart and its body. That neither would starve the other. That no door between us would ever be sealed by fear."

"What happened to them?" Wren whispered. "The seven?"

"Watch," said Vess.

The woman with the sky-crowned staff stepped forward, and a dragon stepped forward to meet her—it was Vess, Wren realized, Vess with uncracked scales and eyes like noon—and together they spoke words in the First Tongue, and the words *did things*. They braided themselves into the twin embers. The gold ember sank down into the mortal world, into the deep places, into stones and rivers and the hearts of those born with the gift. The white ember stayed, anchored in the Gate, the keystone of everything.

"And the Oath held," Vess said softly. "For six hundred years, little ember. Six hundred years of doors that opened both ways. Your healers learned our fire-songs. Our hatchlings learned your lullabies. There were—friendships. Partnerships. Some bonds deeper than either realm had words for." A pause, and the memory-landscape dimmed slightly, clouds crossing the green-gold sun. "And then came the fear."

The world folded again.

Now the hill was the same hill, but the grass was brown, and the sky was the wrong color, and the seven robes were only four, and the four were arguing, and the arguing had gone past words into something colder. Wren saw faces she recognized from Odelia's books—the founders of the Wardens, though they hadn't been called Wardens yet. They wore gray. They spoke of *containment*. Of *the danger of the other shore*. Of a sickness in a border town that they blamed, rightly or wrongly, on dragon-breath.

"They wanted the Gate closed," Vess said. Its voice had gone very quiet, which was somehow worse than loud. "Not sealed forever—that came later, that was Corvess's work—but closed. Guarded. Controlled. And the Oath could not be controlled. So they decided the Oath must be broken."

"But it couldn't be," Wren said. "Could it? Because of the embers."

"No," Vess agreed. "It could not be broken while both embers burned. The gold one they could never find—it hides itself, it passes from keeper to keeper, it is cleverer than they are. But the white ember sat in the Gate, plain as the moon. And so they needed a thief."

The memory shifted one last time, and this time it did not shift gently. It *tore*.

Wren was suddenly inside the night itself—inside the betrayal—and everything was fire and shouting and the Gate's light guttering like a strangled candle. She saw the arches crack. She saw the white ember torn from its cradle of light by human hands, hands in gray sleeves, and she saw the thief turn as the Wardens' horns sounded, and she saw his face.

She knew his face.

Not his name—his face. Younger than Petra, or maybe the same age and just carrying less weight. A jaw she'd watched chew thoughtfully on the end of a quill. Eyes that crinkled sideways when he lied badly, which was often, which was charming, which was—

"No," Wren said.

The memory kept playing. The thief ran. The Gate screamed—a sound Wren felt in her teeth, in the shard in her chest, everywhere—and the white ember, torn from its anchor, began to die in the thief's hands, and the thief looked back once at the collapsing Gate, at the dying dragons, at Vess rising up vast and wounded and *betrayed*, and Wren saw the expression on the known face, and it was not triumph.

It was horror. It was the face of someone who has just realized, one heartbeat too late, exactly what they have done.

Then the memory tore again, and Wren was back on the black glass road, on her knees, and the shard in her chest was blazing so hot and so bright that she had to press both hands over it, and she was crying, really crying, the ugly kind, and Vess lowered its great head and breathed one long, careful, warm breath over her like a blanket.

"Little ember," Vess rumbled. "Breathe."

"He—" Wren's voice came out in pieces. "He looked like— he had a *laugh*—"

"I know whose face you saw." Vess's golden eye was very close, and very sad. "You have been sleeping beside him for weeks. You have seen it aged, and worn, and hollowed by exile. But it is the same face."

"It can't be." Wren shook her head hard. "Petra wouldn't—he *cares*—he makes charms for scared people and he pretends they're ordinary and he gives the real ones away free when nobody's looking, I've *seen* him—"

"Yes," Vess said. "I imagine you have."

"But he *broke* it! He broke the Oath! Everything's dying because of him! *You're* dying because of him!"

The dragon was quiet for a long time. Around them, the ash crept a little further along its spine. When it finally spoke, each word came slowly, placed down like stones across a river.

"When the Gate broke," Vess said, "I cursed him. Understand that. I cursed him with my last full strength, and the curse was this: that he would forget what he had done, and spend the rest of his life searching for the missing piece of himself, and never know whether he was running from punishment or toward atonement. It seemed a fitting torment." The great eye blinked, slow as a chapel door. "I have had a hundred years to reconsider. A hundred years is a long time, little ember. Long enough to learn that the story the victors tell is rarely the whole of it."

"What do you mean?" Wren sniffled. "Whose story? The Wardens won."

"Did they?" Vess's smoke curled upward, thoughtful. "They hunted the white ember for a century and never found it. They built their ban on the ruins of a crime they believe they committed cleanly. And the thief they cursed—" the dragon's voice dropped lower, "—was not working alone, and was not stealing for himself, and did not run *from* the Gate, little ember. Look at the memory again. Truly look. Which way was he running?"

Wren frowned. She pulled the memory back up, the tearing, screaming night of it, and this time she forced herself to watch the thief's feet instead of his face.

He ran toward the mortal side. Yes. But not *away* from the Gate—from the Wardens' horns, which were closing in from three directions, and the fourth direction, the open one, the clear one, led straight through the breaking arches and out. He could have escaped clean into the mortal world. Instead he cut *sideways*, through the worst of the fire, toward the inner chamber where the Gate's foundations kept a second thing, a smaller thing, a little cradle of light that Wren's shard recognized before her mind did, because the shard *sang*—

"Oh," Wren said. Her hands flew to her chest. "Oh. He took the *twin*. He took the baby one. He took—"

"—the seed of the white ember," Vess finished softly. "The last spark of the Oath, saved from the collapse. Carried out through fire. Hidden in the mortal world for a hundred years, passed hand to hand, keeper to keeper, always one step ahead of the gray robes. Until, five years ago, it came to rest somewhere safe." The golden eye crinkled, and it was almost a smile, and it was almost unbearable. "Somewhere with a storm hidden under its skin. Somewhere that would one day answer a knock at its door."

Wren looked down at herself. At the glow under her skin. At her small bare dream-feet on the warm black glass.

"Me," she said. "I'm the ember. I'm the *whole* ember. The white one and the seed. I'm the Oath."

"You are the Oath reborn," Vess said. "Which means, little ember—and mark this, and carry it carefully, because it will be asked of you sooner than any of us wish—" the dragon's head lifted, and beyond it the broken Gate loomed, and through its cracks Wren could see the sky of Ashfall flickering like a failing lamp, "—that what was stolen can be restored. What was broken can be reforged. But reforging asks a price, and the price is paid by keepers, and you are the greatest keeper of all. When the time comes, someone must feed the ember. Someone must give what they value most."

"Like what?" Wren whispered.

"Memories," said Vess. "A life's worth of them. The Oath is made of trust, and trust is made of memory, and memory freely given burns brighter than anything else in either realm." The dragon settled back onto its haunches, and the ash on its spine seemed, just for a moment, to recede. "But that is a weight for another night. Tonight, you are a child asleep on a river, and I am an old fool telling stories, and you should take one thing home with you and no more."

Wren wiped her nose on her sleeve. "What thing?"

"That the man you sleep beside is not the villain of this story," Vess said. "Nor the hero. He is the keeper who failed, and the keeper who saved, and both of those are true, and he does not remember either one. The scorched pages of him, little ember—the blank places—they were not taken by the Wardens." The great eye half-closed. "I took them. My curse. And if you are wise, you will help him win them back before the end, because a man should know what he is sacrificing, if he is asked to sacrifice it."

The world was starting to go soft at the edges. The black glass road was dissolving into warmth, into the smell of a barge and river-water and Petra humming tunelessly in his sleep two bunks away. Vess was fading, but its voice reached her one last time, through the ground, through her bones:

"One more thing, little ember. Tell the storm-caller this, when he wakes: *the Gate remembers him kindly.* Let him make of that whatever he can."

* * *

Wren woke at dawn with the taste of woodsmoke in her mouth and her cheeks stiff with dried tears.

On the deck above, she could hear Odelia arguing cheerfully with the barge-master about the correct classification of a duck. From the bunk across the narrow cabin came the sound of Petra sleeping: a snore, a mumble, a snore.

Wren lay still and pressed her hand flat against the shard, which pulsed under her palm, steady and warm and waiting.

*The Gate remembers him kindly.*

She rolled over and looked at Petra's sleeping face—the crinkly-eyed, quill-chewing, charm-selling, storm-hiding face that belonged to a thief and a savior and a man who gave real luck-charms away for free.

"Who *are* you?" she whispered in the First Tongue.

And because the First Tongue was honest even when she wasn't, the words came out differently than she meant them: *Whoever you are—I'm going to help you remember. Even the bad parts. Even if it breaks my heart.*

Up on deck, the duck argument escalated sharply.

And far behind them, downriver, back the way they'd come, Wren felt—just for a heartbeat, just at the edge of the shard's awareness—a coldness moving. Gray and patient and hungry, following the river down.

Coming for the ember.

Coming for her.
