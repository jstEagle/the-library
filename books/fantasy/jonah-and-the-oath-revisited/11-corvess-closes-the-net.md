## 11. Corvess Closes the Net

Ilva had been a Warden for eleven years, and in all that time she had learned one rule above all others: never be the last one standing near the High Warden when he was pleased. Pleased Corvess gave assignments. Pleased Corvess smiled. And no one who had ever seen that smile and lived to gossip about it had anything good to say.

So when the summons came at dawn—*the High Warden requests Sergeant Ilva in the Hall of Quiet, alone*—she dressed slowly, checked her boot-knife twice, and told herself the fear in her stomach was just bad porridge.

The Hall of Quiet sat at the heart of the Warden citadel, a long room with no windows and walls of black stone that drank sound. Wardens held their tribunals there. Wardens erased names there. Ilva's mother had once described it to her, back when her mother still spoke in full sentences, before the quiet took her: *It is a room built for one voice, little wren. Make sure it is never yours.*

She pushed open the doors.

Corvess stood at the far end, his back to her, studying a map of Vessengard that covered an entire wall. Not the city as it was—the city as it might burn. Red wax marks clustered around the river district, the undercity gates, the barge landings. Someone had drawn a small circle around a charm-shop on Lantern Row and written beside it, in a hand so neat it looked printed: *found*.

"Sergeant." He did not turn. "You led the raid on Lantern Row."

"I did, High Warden."

"And you lost them."

"The charm-seller had more power than his file suggested. The street itself—" She stopped herself from saying *the street itself fought for him*, because it sounded like madness spoken aloud. "We were repelled."

"Repelled." He said the word like he was tasting it. "Eleven years of service, Sergeant, and I have read every report you've filed. You are precise. Careful. You have never once used the word *repelled*." Now he turned, and the lamplight caught his face—that smooth, unlined face that never seemed to change, year after year, while every other Warden aged around him like fruit. "Which makes me wonder what you saw on Lantern Row that you have not written down."

Ilva kept her breathing even. This was the moment. She had known it would come eventually; she had simply hoped for more warning, a better plan, perhaps a miracle.

"I saw a man frightened enough to burn his own life down rather than be found," she said. "Desperation looks like power, High Warden. It often is."

Something flickered behind Corvess's eyes. Not anger. Interest. It was worse.

"Desperation," he repeated softly. "Yes. I know something about desperation. Come here. Look at this."

She crossed the hall. Up close, the map smelled of hot wax and old smoke. His finger traced the circle around the charm-shop.

"A girl," he said. "Barefoot. No shadow. Do you know how long I have waited for a barefoot girl without a shadow?"

The question was absurd, and he asked it the way other men discussed grain tariffs. Ilva felt the floor tilt beneath her certainty. *How long.* As if he had been waiting not years but ages—as if the girl were a tide he'd expected.

"No, High Warden."

"She is called the ember-child in certain documents older than this city. Documents I burned myself, personally, four hundred years ago." He smiled—the smile—and Ilva discovered that her body had gone cold all the way through, as if the black stone walls were drinking *her* now. "But paper remembers, Sergeant. Paper always remembers. That is why we burn it."

*Four hundred years.* The words rang in her skull like a dropped bell. Her mother's stories rose up unbidden—the ones the other Warden children had laughed at, the ones about the first Wardens, the ones about a betrayal at a gate between worlds. Stories her mother had whispered like confessions, before the quiet took even those.

"You look pale, Sergeant."

"It is early, High Warden."

"It is." He turned back to the map. "Martial law begins at noon. Curfew at dusk. Every gate sealed, every barge boarded, every roof watched. I am offering a bounty for the child—one thousand crowns, which is more than most Wardens earn in a decade, because I want greed doing my searching for me. Greed is tireless. Greed does not sleep or doubt." A pause. "Greed has never once disappointed me."

"And the charm-seller?"

Corvess's finger moved to the red circle. He pressed down until the wax flattened.

"The charm-seller is bait," he said. "Or a complication. Or nothing at all. Men who hide are easy. It is things that hide inside men that interest me." He lifted his finger, leaving a small crater in the map. "You will take the eastern districts. Door to door. And Sergeant—if you find them, you will send word before you act. No raids. No heroics. I want to be present when the child is taken. Personally present."

"Understood."

"One more thing." He finally looked at her fully, and Ilva understood, with the calm clarity of a woman watching the axe rise, that he already knew everything. Had probably known since Lantern Row. Was letting her stand here because her fear amused him, or because her hope would be sweeter to crush later, or because four hundred years made games out of small creatures. "Your mother served the Wardens too. Before her silence. A devoted woman. She believed in the Oath, they say. The old Oath." His head tilted, birdlike. "Tell me, Sergeant. When you were a girl, did she ever sing to you? There are songs from before the ban. Songs about gates, and embers, and a promise that was broken."

Ilva's heart stopped, started again, and ran.

"No, High Warden. She only hummed."

"Mm." He returned to the map. "Humming. Yes. The old songs survive that way sometimes. Half-remembered. Like oaths." He waved a hand, dismissal. "Noon, Sergeant. Martial law. Do make your district proud."

* * *

She walked out of the Hall of Quiet with her spine straight and her mind screaming.

*He knows.* Not suspected—knew. Knew about the ember-child, knew about documents he had personally burned four centuries ago, knew about her mother, possibly knew about the forged papers sitting in her desk drawer with three false seals on them, papers that had already carried a storm-caller, a dragon-shard, and a mute child downriver to Marrowfen.

She went first to the records office, because panic was a luxury and work was armor. The duty clerk, a nervous young man named Pell who alphabetized his fears, looked up as she entered.

"Sergeant! We've had orders from above—all bounty notices go out by noon, and there's one for—well, it's odd, actually, it doesn't give a name, just a description—"

"Give it to me."

He handed over the notice, freshly inked, still smelling of the press. Ilva read it twice.

*By order of the High Warden: One thousand crowns for the capture of a child, female, seven to nine years, barefoot, bearing no shadow. Approach with caution. Do not harm. Report directly to the Hall of Quiet.*

Below the description, where a name should have been, the press had stamped a single word:

*EMBER.*

"They're calling her that?" Pell asked. "Like a thing? Not even a name?"

"Greed works faster on things than people," Ilva said, and heard Corvess's voice in her own mouth, and hated it.

She scanned the rest of the day's orders. Martial law provisions. Gate closures. And then, tucked at the bottom of the stack, a directive marked with the High Warden's personal seal—a seal she had seen a thousand times and never examined: a closed gate, barred, with a small flame stamped out beneath it.

The directive ordered all Warden archives purged of materials relating to "the Ashfall Question." All records. All histories. All songs.

*Paper always remembers,* he had said. *That is why we burn it.*

He wasn't hunting a runaway child. He was finishing a war. Four hundred years old, maybe older—a war against memory itself, against every song and page and humming mother, and the girl without a shadow wasn't his quarry. She was his enemy. The last living piece of a promise he had broken.

And Ilva had just spent eleven years wearing the uniform of his army.

* * *

She should have gone home. Instead she went to the chapel of the Wardens—not a chapel to any god, the ban had seen to that, but a cold stone room where Wardens went to swear their yearly oaths of vigilance. She sat in the back pew and stared at the motto carved above the empty altar: *NOTHING BURNS UNLESS WE ALLOW IT.*

Her mother had carved that. Ilva had grown up believing it meant the Wardens protected the world from fire.

Sitting there, with the bounty notice folded in her pocket like a coal, she understood it meant the opposite. Nothing burns unless *they* allow it. The Wardens weren't keeping fire from the world. They were keeping the world from catching.

A junior Warden slid into the pew beside her—young Brannic, earnest, freckled, the kind of recruit who still believed the uniform meant something clean.

"Sergeant? Word is you've got the eastern districts. Rough assignment, door to door with martial law coming. Some of the lads are saying—" He lowered his voice. "They're saying the bounty child might be cursed. No shadow, Sarge. My gran always said a person without a shadow is a person the sun won't claim."

"Your gran sounds wise."

"She also said the Wardens used to be something else, before the ban. Keepers, she called them. Not wardens. Keepers of something." He shrugged, embarrassed. "Old woman talk."

"Maybe," Ilva said quietly. "Maybe not."

Brannic studied her. "You alright, Sergeant? You look like you've seen a gate."

*A gate.* She almost laughed. "I'm fine. Go on. Get your district assignments."

He went. Ilva remained in the pew a while longer, turning a coin over in her fingers—an old coin, worn nearly smooth, the only thing her mother had left her besides the silence. On one side, a gate. On the other, a flame. She had carried it for luck since she was nine years old and had never once wondered why her mother owned such a thing, in a city where both images were forbidden.

*Little wren,* her mother had called her. *Wren.*

She had thought it was affection. Sitting in the cold chapel, she felt the truth of it settle into her bones like snow: her mother had named her for the child in the old songs. The keeper's daughter, raised inside the enemy's house, waiting—without knowing she was waiting—for something to keep.

The barge had left two nights ago. Petra Hartwell, the ember-child, the archivist's trail, all of it already downriver toward Marrowfen, carried on her forged papers, hidden by her forged seals. If Corvess discovered it, she would hang. If he discovered it after martial law began, she would hang slowly, in front of everyone, as an example.

And if she stayed silent—if she did her job, swept her districts, kept her cover—then sooner or later the search would reach Marrowfen anyway, because Corvess was not a manhunt. He was a war, and wars do not stop at city gates.

There was one thing she could do. One message, sent carefully, deniably, through channels she had built for exactly this night without ever admitting she was building them.

She left the chapel and walked to the river district, to a smoke-shop that sold pipes to off-duty Wardens and letters to anyone with coin. The proprietor owed her his son's freedom and asked no questions. She wrote a single line, coded in the merchant cipher her mother had taught her alongside her letters, sealed it plain, and paid for fast courier.

*The net is closing. He is not hunting a child. He is ending a war. Marrowfen will not be far enough. Tell the charm-seller: the oath-breaker wears a Warden's face, and he has worn it for four hundred years.*

She hesitated, quill hovering, then added one more line in smaller script:

*And tell the girl: the keepers' daughters remember. She is not alone.*

She paid the courier triple for speed and walked out into the gray morning, where the bells of Vessengard had begun to ring wrong—out of rhythm, urgent, the martial law peal she had trained her whole career never to flinch at.

Around her, the city was changing. Wardens on every corner. Shutters closing like eyes. Somewhere a mother was pulling her child indoors, humming half a note of some forbidden song before thinking better of it.

Ilva straightened her uniform, set her face into the mask the citadel demanded, and joined the patrol.

Inside, beneath eleven years of discipline, a small stubborn flame had caught—the same flame, she suspected, that slept in a barefoot girl's chest—and it was whispering the oldest lesson her mother had left her, the one hidden under all the silence:

*Keepers do not put out fires, little wren. Keepers decide what they are allowed to burn.*

By nightfall, every gate of Vessengard would be sealed. But Ilva had been sealing gates for eleven years, and she knew a secret the High Warden had forgotten in his four hundred:

Some doors only lock from the outside. And someone, somewhere downriver, was about to learn that the net had a hole in it—cut by the daughter of the woman who sang.
