## 12. The Scorch-Blank Page

The house on stilts at the edge of Marrowfen smelled of river-mud and dried yarrow, which was, Sable Yew explained, exactly the smell a body wanted when someone was about to go poking around in his skull.

"Yarrow for the bleeding," she said, hanging another bundle from the rafters with the ease of long habit. "Mud for the grounding. And the smell—" she paused to look back at him over her shoulder, one eye crinkled, "—well. The smell is because I'm old and I like it."

Petra sat on a stool in the middle of her kitchen while she circled him, and tried very hard not to feel like a specimen pinned under glass. Wren sat cross-legged by the hearth, watching everything with the ferocious attention of a child who had learned that houses could be taken away. Tamsin stood by the door with her arms crossed, radiating the particular displeasure of a woman who had been dragged somewhere against her will and was pretending she'd come voluntarily.

Odelia Vance, meanwhile, was having the time of her life.

"You have *first-edition* Corvessine edicts," she breathed, running a finger along a shelf of water-stained books without quite touching them. "Banned twice over. Once for content, once for the binding glue, which—"

"—was made from drowned men's hair, yes, yes." Sable waved her off. "You can gawk after we've done the work. Sit down, archivist, and don't drip on my floor."

It had taken three days to get here. Three days of Sable being cagey about whether she could even do what they asked, three days of Wren's shard flickering in its strange rhythm beneath her collarbone—bright when Petra came near, dimming when he left—and three days of Petra lying awake in the guest loft, staring at the reed ceiling, trying to imagine what could possibly be worse than not knowing.

He had thought he wanted to know. He had told himself so every morning, firmly, the way you tell yourself the river is safe to cross right up until your boots fill with water.

"Right." Sable planted herself in front of him. Up close she was smaller than she seemed, a wiry scrap of a woman with hands like knotted rope and eyes the flat grey-green of the fen in winter. "How this works, storm-caller, is that I'm going to read the ash. Not you—the ash. What was done to you left residue, same as anything burned leaves smoke behind. I'll read it, and I'll tell you what I find, and then—" she held up one rope-knuckled finger, "—and *then*, you'll decide what to do with it. Not before. Agreed?"

"Agreed," Petra said.

"And if I tell you to stop looking at me like a man awaiting sentencing, you'll stop that too. It makes my tea go bitter."

Tamsin snorted from the door. Petra shot her a look that he hoped conveyed *I am enduring this with dignity*. Judging by her expression, it conveyed something closer to *you look seasick*.

Sable fetched a shallow clay bowl, filled it with river water, and set it on the stool beside him. Then she took his hand—her grip surprisingly strong—and turned it palm-up.

"This will feel like remembering something that isn't there," she said. "Like reaching into your pocket for a thing you're certain you carried, and finding the hole where it fell through. Don't fight it. Fighting it just smears the ash."

"I've fought worse things than an empty pocket."

"Mm. That's what they all say, right before they cry into my yarrow."

She closed her eyes. Her thumb pressed into the center of his palm, hard enough to leave a mark, and began to move in slow circles, and—

Oh. She hadn't lied. It was exactly that: the sensation of reaching for something, the certainty of shape and weight, and then nothing. A pocket with a hole in it. He felt himself lean toward the absence the way a tongue finds a missing tooth, again and again, unable to stop probing the gap.

Wren made a small sound. He opened his eyes—he didn't remember closing them—and saw her watching him with her hand pressed flat over her chest, over the shard, which was glowing now, faintly, pulsing in time with Sable's circling thumb.

"It answers him," Sable murmured, eyes still shut. "Interesting. Very interesting. Hush now, little ember. Let an old woman work."

The water in the bowl began to move. Not ripple—*move*, turning slowly counterclockwise, and in its surface images gathered the way frost gathers on a window: first shapes, then shadows, then something almost like memory.

Petra looked. He couldn't help it.

He saw a tower. His tower—or a tower he knew the way you know your own hands, stone by stone, the crooked stair, the window facing east. He saw firelight. He saw a table strewn with papers, and hands—his hands, younger, unscarred—pressing down on one page in particular.

And then Sable made a sound.

It was not a large sound. It was a small one—a soft intake of breath, the kind of sound a person makes when they step on something soft in the dark. But it went through the room like a crack through ice. Odelia looked up from her shelf. Tamsin pushed off from the door. Wren leaned forward, shard flaring.

Sable Yew, river-witch, terror of three counties' worth of smugglers and tax collectors, took her thumb off Petra's palm as though it had been bitten, and stepped back, and looked at him.

And recoiled.

It was only a half-step. Only a shift of weight, heel to toe, a fraction of distance between them. But Petra had spent five years learning to read people the way a hunted animal reads the wind, and he knew recoil when he saw it. He had seen it from charm-buyers who suspected his luck-knots were more than knots. He had seen it from innkeepers the day after the Wardens posted new edicts.

He had never seen it from an ally.

"What," he said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "What did you see?"

Sable's jaw worked. She glanced at Wren—at the child, at the ember burning quietly under her skin—and something in the witch's face did a complicated thing, grief and anger and pity all trying to occupy the same space at once.

"Girl," she said to Wren, gently. "Take the archivist out to the walkway. There's herons on the mudflats at this hour. Go count them for me."

"I want to stay," Wren said. In the First Tongue, the words came out like water over stones, and Petra understood them without effort, which frightened him more than anything else that morning.

"I know you do." Sable crouched to her level, and her voice softened further. "But some truths are heavy, little ember, and grown folk should carry them first and decide how much of the weight to pass down. Count the herons. Count them *properly*. If there are fewer than nine, come tell me straight away, because that means the fen's gone sour and we've bigger problems than this one."

Wren looked at Petra. He managed a smile. It felt like lifting a roof beam.

"Go on," he said. "Count well."

She went—slowly, pausing at the door to look back once, shard dimming as the distance grew—and then it was just the four of them: Petra on his stool, Sable standing too far away, Odelia frozen mid-reach toward a banned book, and Tamsin by the door with her arms crossed tighter than before.

"Someone say it," Tamsin said. "Whatever it is. Say it or I'll start guessing, and my guesses are meaner than the truth."

Sable exhaled. She picked up the bowl of water, considered it, and poured it out the window into the fen with a kind of ceremony, as though disposing of evidence.

"The pages weren't scorched blank to hide *another's* crime," she said. "That's what I expected, mind. Some betrayer's name burned away to protect them, some grand villain whose identity was worth five years of your life. That's the story you've all been telling yourselves around your campfires. I heard you doing it on the walk up. Very cozy. Very wrong."

"Sable," Odelia said quietly.

"The scorch-mark is a sentence, archivist. A punishment, laid on by the Oath itself or those who wielded it. And a punishment requires a guilty party." She turned and looked at Petra full-on, and her flat fen-grey eyes held no cruelty in them at all, which somehow made it worse. "The name that was burned away, storm-caller. The name under the ash. It was yours."

The room did not spin. Petra would later think that was the strangest part—that the world simply went on. The herons cried out on the mudflats. The yarrow swayed. Tamsin's arms uncrossed, slowly, as though her body had forgotten what to do with them.

"No," Odelia said. "No, that doesn't—I catalogued his trial records, I've read them a dozen times, the *shape* of the redaction—"

"The shape of a redaction tells you how big the secret is," Sable said. "Not whose it is. Read it again with fresh eyes, girl. Ask yourself why a man's own memories were taken instead of merely his freedom. They didn't jail him. They didn't hang him. They *emptied* him. You do that when the knowledge itself is the danger. You do that when the guilty man is the only one who must never know what he did."

"But I don't understand," Petra said.

He said it mildly. Almost politely. He heard his own voice as if from across the fen, a thin thread of sound, and part of him—the charm-seller part, the survivor part, the part that had spent five years shrinking—noticed with detached professional interest that this was what shock sounded like when it came from a man who had practiced calm until calm was all that was left of him.

"I don't understand," he said again, "because I don't remember doing anything. That's rather the point, isn't it? You're telling me I'm guilty of a crime I cannot—"

"He stole the twin," Sable said.

Silence.

"The ember's twin," she went on, and now her voice was quieter, almost gentle, which was somehow the worst register yet. "There were two, once. Every keeper knows the song. Two embers, one for each side of the Gate, bound together so neither realm could close against the other. One burns in the child's chest. The other—" she spread her empty hands, "—is gone. Stolen. And the ash says the thief stood in this room not ten minutes ago, holding out his hand for an old woman to read."

*Little ember,* a voice had called her, in Wren's dreams. *Little ember.*

And somewhere, once, there had been another voice calling something else. Another little flame. A twin.

"Why?" The word tore out of him before he could dress it up. "Why would I—if I stole it, if I broke the Oath, why would I spend five years hiding, selling *luck-charms*, why would I keep my head down and my magic buried like a—like a criminal who doesn't even know what he's paying for—"

"Because you paid to forget," Tamsin said.

Her voice was strange. Flat. Petra turned and found her staring at him with an expression he had never seen on her face in all their fractured days of travel—not anger, not the cold bitterness she wore like armor. Something rawer. Something young.

"That's the trade, isn't it?" she said. "That's how the old oaths worked—you told us, master, back when you still taught me things. Memory for mercy. Confess and be emptied, or lie and be hanged." Her breath shuddered. "You chose to forget. Whoever caught you—they gave you the choice, and you chose the hole in your pocket. You chose to not-know yourself rather than—rather than whatever the other thing was."

"Or," Odelia said slowly, and Petra watched her archivist's mind turn over the problem like a page, "or he chose to forget rather than *reveal* something. Think about it. Why take the memory of a theft? The theft's already done. Unless the memory contains something that hasn't happened yet. Unless he knew something, in that tower, five years ago—something worth more than his own past." Her eyes went to the door, to the mudflats beyond, where a small barefoot figure stood counting herons with fierce concentration. "Something worth protecting even from himself."

"Careful," Sable warned. "Speculation smears ash same as fighting does."

But the thought had already lodged in Petra, cold and bright as a fishhook. *He had already met her, once.* Where had that thought come from? It floated up out of the scorched country inside him like a bubble from a drowned thing, and he could not tell if it was memory or hope wearing memory's clothes.

He looked down at his hands. Charm-seller's hands. Storm-caller's hands. Thief's hands?

Five years he had built a life on the foundation of *I am a man who was wronged*. It had been a small life, a shrunk life, but it had been *his*—the one clean possession left to a man stripped of everything else. The injustice had been the last warm thing he owned. He had warmed his hands at it every night.

And now an old woman with rope-knotted fingers had poured it out the window with the bathwater.

"Say it plainly," he said to Sable. "All of it. Am I the villain of this story?"

Sable crossed the room—slowly, deliberately, undoing that half-step of recoil—and took his face in both her rope-hard hands, the way you'd hold a bird you meant to release.

"You're a man," she said, "who did a terrible thing for a reason the ash won't give me, because the reason was in the part they burned. That's not a villain, storm-caller. Villains keep their reasons. They polish them and show them off at dinner. You—" her thumbs pressed his cheekbones, gentle and implacable, "—you paid five years of yourself to bury yours. That's either monstrous or it's holy, and I've lived long enough to know the two wear the same coat."

She let him go and stepped back.

"So here's your choice, and no one can make it for you. You can decide the ash is the whole truth—that you're the oath-breaker, the thief, the man who broke the world—and spend the rest of this quest waiting for the others to realize it and leave. Or you can decide that a man is not the worst thing he's ever done, and that whoever you were in that tower, stealing fire for some reason worth forgetting—you can let the people in this room decide who you are *now*." She jerked her chin at Odelia, at Tamsin. "That's the harder road. Harder roads usually are the right ones. It's a conspiracy."

Tamsin hadn't moved. She was still staring at him, and when she spoke, her voice cracked straight down the middle.

"Five years ago," she said. "Mid-lesson. You stopped talking in the middle of a word. You looked at me like you were trying to remember my name, and then the Wardens came, and you were *gone*, and I spent five years thinking—" She stopped. Started again, angrier, wetter. "I thought you'd abandoned me. I built my whole self out of that. And now you're telling me you were already hollowed out, that the man who walked away wasn't even—"

"Tamsin—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. Her eyes were shining and furious. "Don't you dare comfort me right now. I need—" She turned, wrenched open the door, and stood in it, breathing the fen air, her back a rigid line. "I need a minute. Counting herons. Or whatever."

The door banged. Through its small window, Petra watched her stomp down the walkway toward Wren, who looked up from the mudflats, took one look at Tamsin's face, and solemnly offered her a hand. Tamsin stared at it. Then, to Petra's astonishment, she took it, and the two of them stood at the rail together, the tall angry woman and the small strange child, counting herons.

"They'll be all right," Odelia said softly. She had come to stand beside him, and she was looking at him differently now—not with fear, he realized. With the expression she wore for rare books. For fragile, valuable, possibly cursed things. "We'll be all right. All of this—" she gestured at the room, at him, at the whole scorched mess of it, "—it changes what we know, not what we owe. You found me in a flooded archive and pulled me out. Whatever you did five years ago, that man also exists."

"Do you believe that?" Petra asked. "Genuinely?"

Odelia pushed her spectacles up her nose—a nervous habit he'd catalogued long ago—and was quiet for a moment.

"I believe," she said, "that the Wardens wanted your memories destroyed badly enough to burn every record of them. And institutions don't destroy information out of shame, Petra. They destroy it out of *fear*. Someone powerful is terrified of what's inside your head." Her eyes drifted to the window, to the small figure on the walkway, the ember-light that glimmered faintly at her collarbone even in daylight. "Or of what it points to."

Sable was already moving, pulling jars down from her shelves, muttering, gathering things with sudden purpose.

"What are you doing?" Petra asked.

"Reading deeper, if you're fool enough to let me." She set a jar of something black and glittering on the table with a thump. "The surface ash told us *whose* crime it was. The deep ash might tell us *why*—and more importantly, it might tell us what Corvess knows, because mark me, that man has been hunting this secret far longer than he's been hunting you." She looked up, and her fen-grey eyes were grim. "But fair warning, storm-caller. The last witch who read your depth drowned in a puddle six inches wide. So. Do we scrape the bottom of the barrel, or do we live comfortably ignorant a while longer?"

Outside, Wren laughed at something Tamsin said—bright and startling, a sound none of them had heard enough of. The shard at her throat pulsed once, twice, like a second heartbeat answering the question before Petra could speak it.

He thought of the tower. The window facing east. The voice calling *little ember* to something small and burning, and the terrible tenderness in it.

"Yes," Petra Hartwell said, and did not recognize his own voice at all. "Scrape the bottom."
