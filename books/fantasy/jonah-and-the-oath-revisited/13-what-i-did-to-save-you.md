## 13. What I Did to Save You

Odelia Vance did not sleep. Petra had suspected this for days—the woman simply appeared at whatever hour he woke, already seated among her papers with a cup of something dark and bitter at her elbow, as though she had been waiting for him personally and had been waiting for some time.

This morning—though "morning" was a generous word in the flooded archive, where the light came green through the water-glass ceiling and the hour was anyone's guess—she was standing over a table that had been cleared of everything except three objects: a scorched page, a shallow dish of river-water, and Wren's shard, which sat in the dish like a coal in a birdbath, glowing faintly gold beneath the surface.

"You look terrible," Odelia said by way of greeting.

"Thank you. I've been practicing."

"Sit down, Hartwell." She pushed a stool toward him with her foot. "I've finished the reconstruction. And before you ask—no, I won't soften it. Softening is how archives rot. You'll get it whole or you'll get nothing."

Wren was already there, of course. She had taken up residence on the far side of the table, cross-legged on a stack of banned hymnals, watching the shard breathe its slow light. When Petra entered, she looked up at him with an expression he had come to dread and cherish in equal measure: the look of someone who knows something about you that you don't know yourself.

He sat.

* * *

"Five years ago," Odelia began, "on the night of the Long Dark, three men came to the Gate of Ashfall carrying instruments of sealing. Not Wardens—not yet; the Wardens didn't exist for another two years. The founders. The men who would *build* the ban." She turned the scorched page over with one finger, delicate as a surgeon. "They meant to close the Gate permanently. To end the Oath not by breaking it but by locking both doors and swallowing the keys."

"The founders of the Wardens wanted to seal the realms *before* the ban?" Petra frowned. "Why seal first? Why not just—"

"Because sealing is quiet, and banning is loud. A ban requires an enemy. A crisis. Something for the people to fear." Odelia's mouth twisted. "The dragons were already withdrawing. Ashfall was dimming. The Oath was old and tired and nobody in Vessengard remembered what it actually *did*—only that magic was dangerous and dragons were worse. Three ambitious men saw a dying covenant and thought: here is a door nobody's guarding. Close it, and we are the men who made the world safe."

"And someone stopped them."

"Someone stole the ember."

The words landed in the green silence like a stone into still water. Wren's shard flickered.

Petra's hands had gone cold. "The twin ember. The one Sable read off my scorch-marks. The one I—" He stopped. Started again. "That was me."

"That was you." Odelia said it without triumph, without accusation—just the flat, merciless clarity of a woman who had spent thirty years cataloguing banned things and had learned that truth does not care how you feel about it. "You took the ember's twin from the Gate five years ago, three nights before the founders arrived to use it. Without the twin, their sealing rite was a lantern show. It failed. They were furious. And they were *embarrassed*, which is worse, because embarrassed powerful men go looking for someone to punish."

"They found me."

"They found you." She pulled a second page from under the table—not scorched, this one, but water-stained and soft as cloth. A transcript. He recognized the format; every archive kept trial records the same way, even illegal ones. Especially illegal ones. "Your trial wasn't public. There were four people in the room: the three founders and a recorder who died conveniently soon after. But the recorder was thorough. Thoroughness is a disease, Hartwell. I caught it early and never recovered."

She slid the page across the table.

Petra looked down at it and could not read it. Not because the words were gone—he could see them, ink-brown and precise—but because his own name kept swimming. *Petra Hartwell, storm-caller, of the Order of the Open Sky.* His own name, in a stranger's careful hand, accused of theft against the covenant of realms.

"Read the third question," Odelia said quietly.

He found it. The founder's voice came off the page dry as dust:

*Where is the ember, Hartwell?*

And beneath it, his own answer—or the shape of one, because the recorder had written:

*The accused declined to answer. Asked twice more, he said only: "It isn't yours to find. It was never going to be yours." Asked a fourth time, he laughed. The record notes the laugh.*

"I laughed," Petra said blankly.

"You laughed." Something that was almost fondness crossed Odelia's face and was gone. "Then they offered you the trade. This is the part I want you to hear properly, so listen." She leaned forward, elbows on the table, and for once the archivist's irony fell away entirely, and what was underneath was older and wearier and kinder. "They couldn't take the memory by force. Memory-work doesn't work that way—it has to be given, or it has to be torn out, and tearing leaves wounds that fester. So they offered you a choice. Give up the memories of the theft—the whole year surrounding it—and live. Or keep your mind whole and tell them where the ember was, and they would find whoever you'd hidden it with."

Petra's breath went out of him slowly.

"Whoever I'd hidden it *with*," he repeated. "But I hadn't hidden it with anyone. I hid it in—" He stopped. Because he didn't remember hiding it anywhere. Because the whole year was a smooth grey wall in his mind, and behind the wall—

Behind the wall, Wren's shard flared gold, and the water in the dish boiled gently, and the child on the stack of hymnals said, in her small clear voice, the First Tongue word for *beginning*.

Odelia closed her eyes briefly. "There it is," she murmured. "Yes. All right. The last piece."

"The last piece of what?"

"Of why you did it." She opened her eyes and looked past him—at Wren, then back at him, and her voice dropped into the register she used for the most fragile documents, the ones that crumbled if you breathed wrong. "Hartwell. Do you know what the ember's twin *is*? Not what it powers. What it *is*?"

"A key. Half a key."

"A seed," Odelia said. "The Oath was made from a single fire, split in two—one half to burn in Ashfall, one half to walk the mortal world, so that each realm would always hold a piece of the other. That's what a covenant *is*, at root. Each side keeping a piece of the other safe." She tapped the dish, and the shard glowed. "You didn't steal a key five years ago, Petra. You stole a *seed*. And a seed wants to be planted."

The green light seemed to thicken. Somewhere above them, water moved against glass.

"It couldn't stay loose in the world," Odelia went on. "A loose ember burns through whatever holds it—wood, iron, flesh—looking for soil. It needed a vessel. A living one. One that could carry it for years without being consumed, until it was ready to become what it was always going to become." Her eyes went again to the child. "The Oath, reborn. Human form. A girl who speaks the First Tongue in her sleep and casts no shadow because shadows are a thing of single realms, and she is *both*."

"No," Petra said. It came out before he could stop it, reflexive, absurd. "No, that's—I met her *this year*. In the rain. At my shop door. She was barefoot and half-starved and she—"

"She was eight years old five years ago," Odelia said gently. "Old enough to walk to a gate. Old enough to be carried."

The wall in his mind did not break. Walls don't break. But something behind it *leaned*, the way a door leans when someone on the other side puts their weight against it, and through the seam came—not images, not yet, but temperatures. Warmth. Rain-warmth. The smell of wet stone and dragon-smoke. A small hand in his. A voice saying, in the First Tongue, the word for *afraid*, and the word for *don't let go*, and—

"—the Gate," Petra whispered. "I was at the Gate. With her. Before."

"You took the ember to the Gate," Odelia said. "To give it back, maybe. To hide it, maybe. The record doesn't say—the recorder's pages for that night are the ones that burned. But the shard remembers, and the shard has been showing you, hasn't it? The tower. The friend's voice. The choice." She paused. "You stood at the Gate of Ashfall with a dying covenant and a stolen fire and a frightened child, and somewhere in that night you understood what the founders would do if they ever learned she existed. What any of them would do. Corvess would do."

"He'd put her in a box," Petra said. His voice sounded strange to him, distant, like weather heard through walls. "Or a cell. Or a blade. An ember-child is a weapon, or a threat, or a—"

"Or a *sealing*. Think, man. If the founders had known the Oath could be reborn—that it *wanted* to be reborn—they'd never have needed the twin ember at all. Catch the vessel, kill the seed, and the covenant dies forever, cleanly, with no embarrassing thefts required." Odelia spread her hands. "So you made your choice. They offered you the trade—your year of memories for your silence—and you understood that silence wasn't enough. Silence can be searched out. Tortured out. *Tricked* out. The only safe place to hide a secret is in a man who doesn't know he's keeping it."

"So I gave it up." Petra was speaking slowly now, feeling each sentence like a step onto ice. "Not to protect myself. To protect—"

"To make yourself *safe to be near her*," Odelia corrected. "Don't you see? You couldn't run with her—you were marked, hunted, and a storm-caller travels like a bonfire in a dark field. You couldn't fight them—you'd have burned the city down around her. So you chose the only strategy left. You became a man with a hole in him. You walked away from the Gate, from her, from your apprentice, from your own name's history, and you buried yourself in the most harmless life you could build, selling luck-charms to frightened people, suppressing the storm in your blood so hard it made your teeth ache—" she gestured at him, at all of him, "—and you did it so thoroughly that when the ember finally came looking for its keeper, it had to knock on your door like a stranger."

The archive was very quiet. Water ticked somewhere. Wren slid off the hymnals and came around the table, and stood beside his stool, and put her small hand flat on his forearm, exactly where a small hand had rested once behind a wall of lost years.

"You knew," Petra said to her. His voice cracked in the middle. "At the door. In the rain. You knew me."

Wren nodded. Then, carefully, in the First Tongue, she said the word for *yes*, and after a moment, the word for *waited*.

Odelia looked away, suddenly busy with her papers, giving them the privacy archivists grant to fragile documents.

* * *

Later—hours later, though time in the archive moved like syrup—Petra sat alone at the edge of the flooded gallery with his boots off and his feet in the cold water, doing the arithmetic of himself.

Five years. Five years of shrinking. Of teaching his hands to be small, his voice to be mild, his temper to sit on its own lightning like a hen on eggs. He had thought it was punishment. He had thought, in the worst hours, in the rain-slick dawns when the Hollow Wardens' boots rang past his shutters, that he had been *sentenced*—that some tribunal of gods or men had weighed him and found him guilty of being too much, too bright, too dangerous, and had exiled him into littleness as mercy.

And it had been a choice. His own. Made at a gate, in the dark, with a child's hand in his.

"Not a sentence," he said aloud, testing the words. "A sacrifice."

It should have felt better than it did. It felt terrible, actually—a terrible, clarifying grief, like a bone setting. Because the man who had made that choice had been braver than the man sitting here now, and also more afraid, and also more in love with the world than he had ever let himself remember. And that man had looked at little Wren—eight years old, ember-hearted, already dreaming in a language older than kingdoms—and had decided she was worth becoming a stranger to himself.

"You could have told Tamsin," he said to the water. "You could have left her a letter. Anything."

But no. A letter could be found. Tamsin could be followed. Every thread leading back to the truth was a thread leading back to the ember, and the ember led to the girl. So he had cut them all. Left his apprentice mid-lesson with no goodbye, and let her believe she'd been abandoned, because abandoned was safer than *hunted-by-association*.

Guilt arrived on schedule and sat down beside him like an old dog. He let it. Some debts you don't get to pay; you just carry them properly.

Footsteps behind him—Odelia's uneven tread, and the lighter patter that was Wren pretending not to follow him, badly.

"There's one more thing," Odelia said. She held out a paper. Not a reconstruction this time—an original, brittle and brown, its edges charred. "It survived the burning. Barely. It's the recorder's note of your final words at the trial, after they took the memories. Most people come out of memory-work weeping or empty or both. You didn't."

Petra took the page. The ink had faded almost to ghost, but he could just make it out, in the recorder's cramped hand:

*The accused, asked if he had any statement, smiled and said: "Keep the year. I'll grow a better one."*

Petra read it three times. Then he laughed—the same laugh, he suspected, the one the record had noted—and it came out wet at the edges.

"Arrogant fool," Odelia said, but her voice was thick.

"Yes," he agreed. "Apparently incurable."

Wren climbed onto the ledge beside him and put her feet in the water next to his, and for a while none of them said anything, and the green light moved overhead like the bottom of a sea.

It was Wren who broke the quiet. She tugged his sleeve, and when he looked down, she pointed upstream—toward the archive's flooded stair, where the water ran dark and fast with the evening tide—and said, in the First Tongue, a word he now understood perfectly, because understanding it was the easiest thing in the world:

*Fire.*

Then, pointing the other way, toward the city above, toward the towers where the Wardens kept their lamps burning all night:

*Fire,* she said again. *Coming.*
