## 14. Tamsin's Choice

Tamsin Vale had been following the ember-child's trail for eleven days, and in that time she had learned three things about her former master.

First: he still overpaid for bread. She'd found the baker in a river town who remembered "the tall storm-eyed fellow" and his "little shadow," and when she'd asked how much he'd charged him, the baker had laughed and said, "Charged? He paid double and thanked me for the crust."

Second: he still hummed when he worked. A bargeman swore the charm-seller sat on deck every evening doing something with wire and river-glass, humming a tuneless little song under his breath like a man who didn't know he was doing it.

Third — and this was the one that made her want to put her fist through a wall — he still walked like a man carrying something heavy just behind his shoulder blades. Tamsin knew that walk. She'd spent five years trying to forget it.

She crouched now at the edge of the pine stand above the old coaching road, rain dripping off her hood, and watched the fire below. Four figures around it. The child, small and bright-edged even at this distance, the way a coal is bright-edged. The archivist Vance, hunched over a book. The Warden-sergeant Ilva, arm in a sling, sitting wrong for someone whose cover was supposedly intact. And Petra.

Petra, feeding sticks to a fire in the open air, in Warden-patrolled country, because the child was cold.

*You absolute fool,* Tamsin thought, with a tenderness that disgusted her.

The bounty notice was folded in her breast pocket, soft as cloth from handling. *THE EMBER-CHILD. ALIVE. 500 CROWNS.* Below it, in Corvess's own hand, an addendum: *And the storm-caller Hartwell, dead or broken.* Dead or broken. Not captured. Tamsin had turned that phrase over for days, looking for the mercy in it, and found none. Broken meant what it meant.

She had told herself a story on the road here, the way you tell yourself stories to make your feet move: *I'll take the child to safety first, then deal with him. I'll get the girl out from under his thumb. He abandoned me once; he doesn't get to collect another person.*

It was a good story. It had kept her walking through sleet and one genuinely humiliating incident with a swollen river. But watching him now — watching him tuck a blanket around a mute child's shoulders with those big clumsy hands of his, hands she'd once watched thread lightning through a keyhole without scorching the wood — the story was coming apart at the seams.

Because here was the thing Tamsin had never said out loud, not to the Wardens, not to herself, not to the empty room she'd lived in for five years:

He hadn't looked like a man abandoning anyone, that last day in the tower workroom. He'd looked like a man being *pulled*. Like a hook behind his navel, reeling him toward some door only he could see. Mid-lesson. Chalk still in his hand. He'd said — she could still hear it, she hated that she could still hear it — *"Tamsin, write this down: the Oath remembers what we forget."* And then he'd walked out of his own life, and three weeks later the Wardens came asking questions about where Master Hartwell had gone, and Tamsin Vale, seventeen years old and terrified, had said *I don't know*, which was true, and then she'd said nothing else for five years, which was also true, and both truths had rotted her from the inside out.

She'd joined the Wardens because they were the ones asking questions. At least they were looking. At least somebody admitted magic existed, even if only to burn it.

And now here she was, a licensed hunter of the forbidden, crouched in wet pine needles, watching her teacher warm a child's hands over a fire, and feeling the whole rotten architecture of her five years groan like ice on a river.

The child looked up.

Tamsin froze. There was no reason the girl should see her — eighty yards, dark, rain-haze — but the child's head came around with the slow certainty of a compass needle, and her eyes caught the firelight and held it, ember-bright, and she stared directly at the place where Tamsin was hiding.

Then she tugged Petra's sleeve and pointed.

*Well,* Tamsin thought. *So much for the subtle approach.*

* * *

"There's a Warden in the trees," Petra said quietly, not turning around. "Wren says there's been one since before full dark."

Ilva's hand went to her sword — left-handed, awkward, the sling swinging. "How many?"

"One." The child made a small gesture, fingers curling like a bird settling. Then another, sharper, two fingers crossed and twisted.

"She says—" Petra stopped. His brow furrowed, and Tamsin, watching from thirty feet away with her hood back and her hands open at her sides, saw him struggle with the words the way a man struggles with a dream on waking. "She says she knows her. Or knows *of* her. She says the woman smells like—" he glanced up, helpless, at Odelia. "What's the word. The word for a thing that's been near fire but didn't burn?"

"Singed?" Odelia offered.

"No. Older than that. *Scorched* isn't—"

"Tried," Ilva said suddenly, very quietly. Her face had gone strange. "The First Tongue has a word for someone who stood in front of a burning and took the smoke instead of running. It translates as *tried*. As in, tried to stop it." She was staring at the treeline. "Who's out there?"

Tamsin stepped into the firelight.

The silence that followed had texture. Ilva's hand froze on her hilt. Odelia's book snapped shut. The child — Wren — tilted her head and studied Tamsin with the frank, unnerving attention of a cat considering a moth.

And Petra — Petra went white, then grey, then did the worst possible thing, which was to say, in a voice gone gentle and careful, the voice he used for frightened animals and first-year students:

"Tamsin."

"Don't." Her own voice came out cracked. She cleared her throat. "Don't do the voice. Don't do the *Tamsin, sit down, let's talk about it* voice. You don't get the voice."

"You're right." He stayed seated, hands visible, which was its own kind of insult — as if she were the dangerous one. Maybe she was. Her hand had drifted to her belt where her Warden credentials hung on their chain: the iron badge, the oath-scroll, the seal of the High Warden's office. Everything she was. Everything she'd built out of the wreckage of everything she'd lost.

"I'm here for the bounty," she said. Five hundred crowns. Alive, the child; him, dead or broken. "Corvess posted it himself. Martial law as of four days ago. Every road from here to the coast has a checkpoint. Every innkeeper, every ferryman, every village gossip is a paid informant now. I tracked you in six hours that should have taken six days, because half the country is *looking* for you." She heard her own voice rising and couldn't stop it. "Do you understand what you've done? Do you understand what you're dragging that child through?"

"Yes," Petra said simply.

"*Yes?*"

"Yes. I understand exactly what I'm dragging her through, because the alternative is letting the Hollow Wardens take her, and I have seen what they do with things they take." His eyes — storm-grey, always grey, grey as the day he'd walked out — didn't waver. "You've seen it too. You wear the badge that does it."

"I wear the badge so I can be *inside*—"

"So did I once tell myself things," Ilva murmured, "to sleep at night."

"Stay out of this," Tamsin snapped.

"It's rather everyone's business," Odelia said mildly, "given the martial law."

"*Stay out of—*" Tamsin rounded on them all, and the words came up out of her like floodwater through a broken door, five years of them, hot and ugly and true. "He left! You all know that, don't you? You've all had your little campfire confessions and your shared tragic pasts, so let me fill in the part nobody mentions. He left *mid-lesson*. He left with chalk dust on his fingers and his tea still steaming, and he looked right through me like I was furniture, and I waited. Three days I waited in that tower, telling myself he'd gone for supplies, he'd gone ahead, he'd send word. And then the Wardens came, and they tore the workroom apart, and they asked me where my master had gone, and I said I didn't know, and do you know what they did to apprentices who *didn't know*?" She laughed, and it came out wrong. "Nothing. That was the worst part. Nothing. Because I wasn't worth anything. I wasn't a storm-caller. I was just the girl he forgot to take."

The fire popped. Rain ticked in the pine boughs.

"He didn't forget you," Wren said.

Everyone turned. The child's voice was small and rough from disuse, but clear, and she spoke in Vessengard common, carefully, like someone handling borrowed tools. Then she frowned at her own words, shook her head, and tried again in the other language — the old one, the one that made the fire lean toward her — and Petra translated, slowly, his voice unsteady:

"She says... he didn't forget you. She says forgetting was the price, and the price was paid *for* you. For all of—" He stopped. His hand had risen halfway to his chest, unconsciously, the way a man touches an old scar. "For all of us. She says she's sorry. She says she's been carrying the memory of it and it's too heavy and she wants to set it down."

"What memory?" Tamsin whispered. "What are you talking about? What happened to you?"

"That," Petra said, "is a very long story, and most of it I only learned last month, and none of it excuses—" he exhaled. "None of it excuses how it felt from where you stood. From where you've stood for five years. I know that. I know what I owe you, Tamsin. I've known since Marrowfen. I just didn't think you'd ever let me say it."

"You didn't *think*," Tamsin said. "That's the whole problem. You never think about—"

"Tamsin." Ilva had gotten to her feet, and there was something in her face Tamsin had never seen on a Warden officer: naked, unguarded respect. "Sergeant to sergeant. I read the raid reports from five years ago. The Vessengard tower purges. Your name is in them — listed as a witness, released without charge, flagged for observation." A pause. "You know what else is in those reports? A requisition order, signed by Corvess personally, for the contents of Hartwell's workroom. Sealed. High clearance. I pulled it two weeks ago and it took me threatening a clerk with latrine duty to get a look." Her jaw tightened. "Corvess didn't want Hartwell punished. He wanted his *notes*. He's been hunting that man's research for five years, and every apprentice, every colleague, every friend of Hartwell's has been a thread he's been pulling since the day your master vanished. You weren't forgotten by the Wardens, Vale. You were *kept*. Like bait in a trap that hasn't sprung yet."

The rain got louder, or maybe Tamsin just stopped hearing anything else for a moment.

*Bait.* The word fit somewhere deep and clicked into place against other words she'd spent years not examining. Why a disgraced apprentice with no recorded talent had been fast-tracked into Warden training. Why her assignments had always, always drifted toward the borderlands, toward river towns, toward anywhere a rumor of a storm-caller might surface. Why Corvess himself had signed her final posting orders, in person, and looked at her with those flat grey eyes and said, *You have a nose for Hartwell, Vale. Use it.*

She had thought it was skill. She had been *proud* of it.

"Oh," she said. Very quietly. The fire crackled. Somewhere in the dark, an owl asked its idiot question and got no answer.

Petra rose, slowly, and crossed the space between them, and stopped an arm's length away — close enough to touch, far enough that touching was her choice. Up close he was thinner than she remembered, and there was silver in his beard that hadn't been there, and his hands had new scars, and none of it mattered, because he was looking at her exactly the way he'd looked at her when she was sixteen and had just, through sheer stubbornness, done the impossible thing he'd said couldn't be done.

"Tamsin Vale," he said. "On the day I left, I told you to write down that the Oath remembers what we forget. I need you to know that wasn't a lesson. It was a message. To you. Because some part of me — the part they hadn't cut out yet — knew I was going to lose everything, including you, and it grabbed the last ten seconds it had and spent them on you." His voice frayed. "I am so sorry. Not for what I chose. For what it cost *you*. Those aren't the same thing, and it took me losing my own past to learn the difference."

Tamsin stood very still.

She thought about the badge on her belt. She thought about five hundred crowns, and a warm billet, and a career, and the particular clean comfort of believing you were on the side of order. She thought about a requisition order signed in flat grey ink. She thought about a girl of seventeen saying *I don't know* and meaning it with her whole heart.

She thought about the child, watching her with ember eyes, holding out — Tamsin realized with a jolt — a small cup of tea, already prepared, offered up with both hands like a peace treaty between nations.

"Oh, hells," Tamsin said, and took the tea, and drank it, because refusing would have been unforgivable, and it was terrible, scorched, over-steeped, absolutely perfect.

Then she unpinned the badge.

It came off easier than it had gone on, five years ago, in a cold ceremony hall with her mother's name spoken over her. She held it a moment — iron, cold, heavier than its weight — and then she dropped it into the fire, where it sat glowing among the coals like a small malevolent eye, and Odelia said, "Those don't melt, you know, they're warded—" and Ilva said, "Give it here," drew her knife, and pried the ward-seal off with three precise strokes, and *then* it melted, and Tamsin felt something in her chest unknot that she hadn't known was tied.

"The scroll too," Ilva said. "Your oath-scroll. Burn it properly or Corvess can scry its location."

Tamsin fed the scroll to the flames and watched her whole legal existence curl and blacken, and discovered that she felt — not light, exactly. Lighter. Like setting down a load you'd mistaken for your own skeleton.

"Right," she said, when she could speak. "Now the bad news, because apparently I work for you people now, gods help me." She looked at Petra, and let him see everything in her face, because he'd earned it. "Corvess has Odelia Vance's archive. Took it nine days ago. The Black Archive under Vessengard — he moved her whole operation to the Citadel, books, ledgers, banned histories, all of it. And he took the archivist who was cataloguing for her. An old man named Fenwick. He's alive, as far as I know. As leverage." She swallowed. "But that's not why I'm here. That's not why Corvess *let* me track you — and he did, by the way, he's had a tracker-mark on your trail since the river town, I found it and broke it, but there will be others, better ones. He let me come because he wanted me to bring him back the location of your camp." She met Petra's eyes. "He doesn't want the child, Petra. Not primarily. He wants *you* to lead him to the Gate. He said — he said, and I'll remember these words until I die — *'The storm-caller is the key that fits the lock. The child is merely the hand that turns it.'*"

Silence. The fire hissed under the rain.

Petra looked at Wren. Wren looked at Petra. Something passed between them that Tamsin couldn't read and didn't try to.

"Well," Petra said at last, and there was something in his voice Tamsin recognized from the old days, from the tower, from the lessons that ended with chalk snapping in his fingers because the idea was too big to hold — terror and delight in equal measure. "Then I suppose we'd better rescue an archivist and steal a library before he realizes his bait came back biting."

Odelia made a sound that might have been a laugh or a sob. Ilva was already pulling a map from her pack. Wren tugged Tamsin's sleeve and pointed at the teacup, then at the kettle, then at her, with the unmistakable gravity of a child assigning permanent kitchen duty.

"And *that's* how it's going to be," Tamsin said, to no one, to everyone, to the rain.

Nobody answered. Nobody had to. She picked up the kettle.
