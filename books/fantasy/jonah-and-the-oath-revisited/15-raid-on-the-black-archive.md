## 15. Raid on the Black Archive

The plan had exactly one good idea in it, and Tamsin had been quick to point that out.

"One good idea," she said, crouched beside me on the roof of the tannery across from the Black Archive's entrance. "Ilva's credentials get us through the door. Everything after that is improvisation wearing a coat."

"Improvisation has served us well so far," I said.

"We're hiding in a tannery, Petra. The smell alone should tell you how well it's served us."

Below us, Vessengard's night shift moved through the streets in the gray drizzle that had settled over the city like a damp blanket. Wardens at every corner—more than usual, their lanterns burning that flat, colorless white that Corvess favored. Since his declaration of martial law, the city had gone quiet in a way I remembered from my childhood, from the years after the Oath broke, when everyone learned to speak softly and trust no one.

Somewhere under our feet, in the flooded levels beneath the city, Odelia Vance was chained in a cell that used to be her life's work.

"She'll be alive," Wren said.

I looked down at her. She was wedged between me and the chimney stack, wrapped in Sable Yew's oilcloth cloak, and she said it with the absolute certainty of a nine-year-old who had dreamed things into being before anyone believed her.

"You can't know that," I said gently.

"I can." She held up her hand. In her palm, the shard glowed—a coal-bright sliver just under the skin of her chest, but its light leaked out through her fingers now, stronger every day, hungrier every day. "It pulls toward fire. Toward *her* fire. She's still burning."

I chose not to examine too closely what it meant that the child could feel people burning. There was a list of things I wasn't examining lately, and it was getting long enough to be its own archive.

* * *

We went down through the drainage grate at half past midnight, because Ilva said the Wardens changed shift then and because she had spent eleven years learning the exact weight of a Warden's boredom. She came with us, against everyone's advice, including her own doctor's—Sable had packed her ribs twice since Marrowfen and pronounced her "held together by spite and string."

"Spite holds," Sable had said, which from her was practically a blessing.

The flooded archive was worse than I remembered. When we'd first come here, weeks ago, it had been a cathedral of drowned books, shelves rising out of black water, lamps floating on cork rings like patient jellyfish. Now it was dark. The water was lower—the Wardens had drained it—and the shelves stood bare as rib cages. Every banned book had been hauled up to the surface, stacked in the great reading hall for the bonfires Corvess had promised.

"They burned them," Odelia's apprentice—no, wait, Odelia had no apprentice; the thought arrived fully formed and wrong, one of those memories-that-weren't that had been plaguing me since the scorch-blank pages—"they burned everything," Tamsin whispered, running her fingers along an empty shelf. "Five hundred years of forbidden history. Gone in an afternoon."

"Not gone," Ilva said quietly. "Moved. Corvess doesn't destroy knowledge. He hoards it. That's how he's lived this long." She didn't explain what she meant by *this long*. None of us asked. Some questions you save for daylight, if daylight ever comes.

Wren's shard brightened as we descended, throwing our shadows huge and wavering across the wet stone. She led us without hesitation—left at the collapsed stair, right along the aqueduct channel, down a corridor where the water reached our knees and something small and blind swam away from the light.

And there, behind a door that had once been magnificent and was now only heavy, we found her.

Odelia Vance sat on the floor of a cell that had been a map room, chained at the ankle to a ring bolted into the stone, surrounded by paper. They'd left her the paper, I realized later. That was the cruelty of it—an archivist imprisoned with blank pages and no ink. She had been writing in the dust with her finger. Whole histories, composed and erased, composed and erased.

She looked up when the light hit her, and her face did three things in quick succession: relief, fury, and arithmetic.

"You brought the *child*," she said. "Into a Warden garrison. You absolute—" She stopped, recalculated, and settled on: "Untie me. We will discuss your judgment later, at length, possibly with visual aids."

Tamsin was already working the lock. "You're welcome," she muttered.

"I didn't say thank you either. Priorities, girl."

The chain gave. Odelia rose stiffly, took stock of us all—me, Tamsin, Wren glowing faintly at my side, Ilva standing watch at the door in her Warden sergeant's uniform—and something in her expression softened by perhaps two degrees, which from Odelia was an embrace.

"You look terrible, Quintana," she said. "Exile suits you about as well as it suited me."

"It's the tannery," I said. "We've been staying near a tannery."

"That explains nothing. It never does."

* * *

The trap sprang on the way out, because of course it did. Because Corvess had not put Odelia in a cell to hold her. He had put her there to hold *us*, the way you leave cheese in a trap—not because you love the cheese, but because you know what wants it.

We were halfway up the aqueduct channel when the lamps came on.

Not gradually. All at once, every lamp in the flooded archive, the flat white glare of Warden-lanterns multiplied fiftyfold, and the water around our knees lit up like a sheet of ice, and I understood with a lurching stomach that we had been visible from the moment we entered. That he had watched us wade to her door. That he had let us untie her because a rescued archivist runs faster than a captive one, and he wanted to see where we ran.

A voice rolled down the corridor, amplified by some art I didn't want to name, smooth as poured cream:

"Petra Quintana. Or whatever name you're using this decade. Do come up. I've kept your chair warm."

I pushed Wren behind me. The shard flared hot against my back through my coat, and the water around my ankles began, very slightly, to steam.

"Ilva," I said.

"I know." Her voice was flat and steady, the voice she'd used to talk me through the raid on the charm-shop, a lifetime ago. "Service tunnel, east branch, exits behind the chandlery. Take them. I'll close the door behind you."

"Ilva—"

"*Take them.*" And then, softer, almost gentle, the way she'd spoken in Marrowfen when she told us about her mother, the oath-keeper whose grave the Wardens had salted: "This is the job, Petra. This is the whole job. Go."

What happened next happened fast, and I have replayed it so many times since that it has worn grooves in me.

She turned and walked back down the corridor toward the light, unhurried, a sergeant going to meet her commanding officer, and the last thing she did before the bend in the passage took her from view was reach back and squeeze Wren's shoulder once, briefly, the way you'd touch something you were leaving in safe hands.

Then the corridor filled with thunder.

Not mine. I hadn't called it—I swear on whatever's left of my honor, I hadn't—but the archive answered her anyway, or answered the shard, or simply decided, the way old buildings sometimes do, that it was done being polite. The aqueduct channel burst its ancient seams. Water came down the corridor in a brown wall, and with it came the sound of stone giving way, and Ilva's voice—clear, furious, *laughing*, actually laughing—shouting words in the First Tongue that I didn't know I knew until I heard them:

*The door is shut. The door is shut. Run, little ember.*

I ran. I carried Wren. Tamsin carried Odelia, who protested the entire way in a tone of scholarly outrage, and the water chased us up the service tunnel and out into the alley behind the chandlery, where we stood gasping in the rain while the ground shook beneath us.

Behind us, the Black Archive began to burn.

* * *

Here is the thing about watching five hundred years of forbidden history burn: you can hear it.

Books don't go quietly. From three streets away, through the rain, we could hear the archive dying—a deep, groaning roar, punctuated by smaller sounds, sharp cracks, like the building snapping its own bones. Sparks climbed into the wet sky and refused, impossibly, to be put out. Warden-lantern fire, someone said later. It burns what it's told to burn and nothing else, and tonight it had been told to burn everything.

Corvess had gotten what he wanted after all. He'd flushed us out, taken the archive off the board, and sent a message we could read from across the city: *this is what resistance costs.*

"He let us go," Tamsin said. We were crammed into the back of Sable's barge, moored in the tannery district, all of us soaked and soot-streaked and silent. "He had us. The whole corridor. He let us go."

"Because I'm meant to lead him to the Gate," I said. My voice sounded far away to me. "He doesn't want to hunt us across the wastes. He wants us to run there. He wants me to carry him straight to the door." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "He's not chasing us, Tamsin. He's *herding* us."

Odelia, wrapped in three blankets and radiating indignation even through exhaustion, fixed me with her archivist's stare. "Then we don't run where he expects. We take the long road. The northern passes."

"The northern passes add nine days."

"Nine days of Corvess guessing," she said. "I've read his correspondence, Quintana—or I did, before he burned my library and my footnotes with it, may his name be struck from every index ever compiled. He's old. Older than the Oath. Old things hate uncertainty. We will give him uncertainty until it chokes him."

Wren had not spoken since the archive. She sat at the barge's rail with her knees pulled up, watching the fire-glow stain the clouds orange, and the shard under her collarbone pulsed in time with the distant burning, and I saw her pressing her hand flat against it as though holding a door closed.

I sat down beside her.

"Hey," I said.

"He's still in there," Wren said. "In the fire. The lady with the laughing voice. She's not dead. The ember would know." A pause. "But it hurts her. It hurts her a lot."

I looked at the burning skyline and tried to make my face do something other than what it wanted to do.

"Can you tell?" I asked, and hated myself for asking. "Whether she—"

"She's holding the door," Wren said simply. "Like she said. But doors are heavy, Petra. And she's only one person, and the person on the other side is very old."

The rain came down harder. Somewhere across the city, a section of the archive roof fell in with a sound like the world clearing its throat, and the sparks leapt higher, and Wren flinched as though each one landed on her skin.

I put my arm around her shoulders. She leaned into me, small and furnace-warm, and after a while she said, in the First Tongue, the words coming easily now between us like a private country we both lived in:

*When we open the Gate—when the fire comes back—will it find her? Will it know her?*

*I don't know,* I answered honestly.

*Then we have to hurry,* she said. *Because she held the door for us. That's what keepers do. That's what the Oath is. Somebody holds the door.*

I looked at this child—the Oath reborn, the last ember, nine years old and already carrying more weight than the entire Warden legion—and felt something settle in me that had been loose and rattling since the night she appeared at my shop door. Not calm. Nothing so kind as calm. Something with edges. Something that intended to be useful.

Fury, I suppose. Fury with a direction.

"Tamsin," I said. "Get Sable. Tell her we sail on the morning tide. Northern passes."

Tamsin looked at me—at the storm-light I could feel gathering in my fingertips despite the rain, at whatever my face was doing—and nodded slowly.

"And Petra? What do we do about Corvess?"

I watched the archive burn and made a promise to the smoke.

"We stop running from him," I said. "We run *to* the Gate. And when he follows us there—because he will follow—we introduce him to the dragons."

Behind us, Wren's shard flared once, bright as a struck match, and high above the burning city—for just a moment, in the gap between clouds—something vast passed overhead, silent as a held breath, and was gone.

None of us spoke.

But none of us, I think, doubted what we'd seen.
