## 16. The Ember's Hunger

Cold is a strange thing to remember.

I did not know cold, before. Before the gate, before the falling, before I was small and made of meat and breath instead of light and law. In Ashfall there was no cold because everything burned — the mountains, the rivers, the sky itself, all of it lit from underneath like coals in a banked fire. Vess used to curl around me when I was only an ember-shaped thought, not yet a girl, and her scales were warm as noon, and she would say, *little ember, nothing that burns can ever be cold.*

She was wrong.

Or she was right, and that is the problem.

The shard in my chest is still burning. That is what nobody understands — not Odelia with her wet papers, not Sable with her river-quiet eyes, not even Petra, who looks at me sometimes like he is reading a book he half remembers writing. They think the ember is dying because it is dimmer than it was. But embers do not die the way candles die. Embers are patient. An ember will eat whatever is near it to keep itself alive, and if there is nothing near it, it will eat itself, and if there is nothing left of itself —

It will eat you.

I figured this out three nights ago, on the barge, when Sable laughed at something Tamsin said and I reached for my own laugh and found the place where it should have been was cold. Not empty. Cold. Like reaching into a drawer for bread and finding frost.

Since then I have been counting.

That is what I do now, at night, when everyone else sleeps and the river or the road makes its long dark sound outside. I count the warm things leaving me. Two nights ago I could not remember the smell of the bakery on Cordwainer Street where a woman once gave me a heel of bread for no reason at all. Last night I could not remember the woman's face. Tonight I am lying in the loft of a waystation barn with hay poking through my shirt, listening to Petra breathe below me, and I try to remember the taste of that bread, and I can't, and the shard in my chest flickers — just a little, just enough — like a mouth closing over something sweet.

*Thank you,* says the ember, in the wordless way it says things. *That was good.*

I hate it. I hate it the way you hate a thing you also love, which is the worst way to hate anything, because you cannot put it down. It is the last piece of Ashfall. It is the Oath. It is me — I am fairly sure I am it, or it is me, or we got mixed up somewhere before either of us had words. And it is eating me one warm memory at a time so that it can keep its little light lit, because the Gate is guttering and the ember is the last coal of a fire the size of the world, and a coal alone in the dark does what coals do.

It survives.

*Nothing that burns can ever be cold,* Vess said.

Vess, I want to tell her, across all the distance between here and the dying Gate: I am eleven years old and I am so cold inside that I have started wearing Ilva's spare cloak indoors, and everyone thinks it is because I like the cloak.

* * *

In the morning there is porridge, because Tamsin has decided that porridge is the answer to most problems, and honestly she might be right. She stands over the waystation's dented pot with a wooden spoon held like a sword and informs us that if anyone complains about the salt content they may take it up with her outside.

"I would win," says Odelia mildly, not looking up from the book she has propped against the water jug. Odelia reads while she eats, while she walks, possibly while she sleeps; Petra once claimed to have seen her reading during a lightning storm, holding the book up as a shield.

"You would not."

"I catalogued dueling manuals for six years, Tamsin. I know the theory."

"Theory." Tamsin snorts and ladles out bowls. "Here. Salt's at the bottom, stir it yourself, I'm not your mother."

Ilva takes her bowl with her bandaged arm cradled against her chest and eats standing at the window, watching the road the way she always watches roads now, which is to say like the road owes her money. The wound from the Black Archive is healing slow. She pretends it isn't. We all pretend along with her, because that is what a family is, probably: people who agree to pretend the same untrue things at the same time.

Petra sits across from me and eats his porridge and watches me eat mine, and I feel the look land on me like a hand on my shoulder, gentle, checking.

"Eat all of it," he says. "You've gone thin."

"I'm always thin."

"You were less thin in Marrowfen." He frowns, and there is a crease between his eyebrows that I have privately named the Worry Crease, and it has been getting deeper for weeks. "Wren. Are you sleeping?"

"Like a rock," I say, which is a lie, and then, because lying to Petra feels like lying to a door: "A cold rock."

He laughs, but the Worry Crease doesn't go away. He reaches across the table and presses the back of his hand to my forehead, the way mothers do in stories, and I almost flinch — because his hand is warm, so warm, and my skin under it must feel like river stone, like cellar air, like the underside of a bridge in January — and I watch him notice. I watch the noticing happen behind his eyes.

"You're chilled," he says slowly. "You're chilled all the time lately, aren't you? The cloaks. You've been wearing Ilva's cloaks."

"It's autumn," I say.

"It's been autumn for two months and you wore those cloaks through summer." His hand stays on my forehead. The ember stirs. I feel it lean toward his warmth like a plant toward sun, and I slam every door inside myself shut, and I say, very casually:

"I run cold. Sable said. Some people just run cold."

"Sable said?" Now he looks at Sable, who is sitting by the hearth doing something quiet and green-smelling with a bundle of herbs, and Sable looks back at him with her flat river eyes and says nothing at all, which from Sable is a whole speech, and none of the words in it are helpful ones.

"I'll make her a tonic," Sable says finally. "For the cold. Warmth from the outside, since—" a pause, delicate as a spider's step, "—since some warmth is being spent elsewhere."

And Petra looks at me again, and I look at my porridge very hard, and the moment passes the way moments do when everyone at the table agrees without saying so to let them pass.

But his hand lingers on my hair when he gets up to fetch more wood, and I sit there feeling the shape of it, and I think: *he knows something is wrong. He doesn't know what. If I tell him what, he will do something reckless and loving and terrible, because that is what Petra does.* I have watched him. He is a man made entirely of doors he opens for other people even when there are wolves outside.

So I don't tell him.

Not yet.

* * *

The confession happens at night, because of course it does. All true things in my life have happened at night. I was born at night, or made, or fell, depending on which part of the story you start from.

We are camped in the ruins of an old shrine — Odelia says it predates the Wardens, predates the ban, maybe predates the kingdoms, a ring of standing stones with their carvings worn to suggestions. Tamsin has the first watch. Ilva is asleep sitting up, which she insists is a Warden skill. Sable has folded herself into the grass like she grew there. Odelia reads by ember-light — actual ember-light, a candle stub she guards like treasure.

And Petra sits beside me on a fallen stone, sharpening a knife he barely uses, because the sharpening calms him, the way counting calms me, and the scrape-scrape-scrape is the only sound in the world.

I have been saving something to say all day. Turning it over. Testing its weight.

Here is the problem with the First Tongue: it is not a language you decide to speak. It is a language that decides to be true. When I speak it, the words come up from underneath me, from the place where the ember lives, and they come out already shaped, already honest. There is no lying in the First Tongue. That is why the old oaths were sworn in it. That is why breaking one broke the world.

I take a breath. The cold goes down my throat and pools in my chest, right next to the hungry little light.

*I need to tell you something,* I say, in the First Tongue, and the words come out glowing faintly, little threads of gold hanging in the dark between us, and Petra's knife stops mid-stroke.

He turns to me. And here is the thing I was not ready for, the thing I will remember for as long as I have a self to remember with:

He understands.

I see it land in his face — not confusion, not the polite frown of a man hearing a foreign tongue. Recognition. His lips move, shaping the syllables back at me, silent, testing, like a man tasting a dish he ate as a child. His eyes go wide and then wet and then wide again.

"How—" he starts, in ordinary speech, and stops, and tries again, and this time it comes out in the First Tongue, rough and rusty and beautiful, a door opening in a house he didn't know he owned: *How do I know that?*

*Your memories are burning,* I say, and the truth comes out of me all at once now, the whole cold weight of it, because there is no stopping a thing once the First Tongue has hold of it. *The language lives in the same place your stolen memories lived. As the ember eats, it digs. Everything it uncovers on the way down, you get back. But Petra — the digging goes both ways.*

*What do you mean,* he says, and his voice has gone careful, the way voices go when they already know.

*The ember is starving. The Oath is dying and the ember is the last of it and it needs fuel. It's been taking mine. My warmth. My memories — the warm ones, it likes the warm ones best. The baker on Cordwainer Street. The taste of bread. I counted, Petra. I've been counting for weeks. It eats one every few nights and it's getting faster and I'm getting colder and I didn't tell anyone because—*

My voice cracks. Gold light spills off the broken word and hangs in the air between us like a small sad constellation.

*Because you'd stop the quest,* I finish. *You'd try to save me instead of the Gate. And then everyone's world ends, not just my memories. And I thought — I thought I could stand it. Being eaten slowly. If it bought the realms another hundred years. I thought that was a fair trade.*

The silence after that is enormous.

Then Petra puts down the knife, very carefully, the way you put down a weapon when you have decided not to use it, and he takes my face in both his hands, and his palms are so warm I nearly cry right there, and he speaks in the First Tongue, and every word comes out steady and furious and gentle all at once, a storm that has decided to be kind:

*Listen to me, little ember. Nothing that burns is ever cold — that is wrong, whoever told you that was wrong, and I will spend the rest of my life proving it. You do not get to be a fair trade. You are not currency. You are a child who likes porridge with too much honey and hides Sable's herbs in Tamsin's boots for fun, and if that ember wants warmth, it can take mine, and if it wants memories, it can have mine, because I have too many anyway and half of them are stolen goods.*

*They're yours,* I whisper.

*Some of them,* he agrees, and something moves behind his eyes — grief, old and huge, the shape of a tower and a friend's voice and a choice, the things the shard showed him months ago. *And I am choosing, right now, freely, what to do with them. Do you understand the difference? That is the whole difference. That is the entire Oath, Wren. Not sacrifice. Choice.*

I am crying now, properly, ugly, eleven-year-old crying, and the tears steam faintly where they hit the gold light still hanging in the air, and Petra pulls me against his chest and holds on, and his heart under my ear is loud and warm and stubborn.

*We go to the Gate faster,* he says into my hair. *Tomorrow. First light. No more easy roads, no more waiting out weather. And when we get there, we find another way to feed it — Vess, the Gate itself, the old covenant-words, something. Nobody eats you. Nobody eats anybody. We renegotiate the terms.*

*Embers don't renegotiate,* I say into his shirt.

*This one does,* he says. *It lives with me.*

Above us, past the ring of stones, the night sky is clear and black and full of stars, and for one strange moment I could swear the stars are arranged wrong — pulled slightly toward the east, toward the wastes, toward the dying Gate, like iron filings leaning toward a magnet. Like the whole sky is hungry too.

Odelia appears at the edge of the firelight, book forgotten in her hand, and her face is pale in the candle-glow.

"Petra," she says quietly. "The eastern horizon. You should come see this."

He lifts his head. Beyond the stones, far away, low on the edge of the world, the darkness over the Ashfall wastes is not dark at all anymore. It glows a deep, angry red — the color of a fire seen through a cracked door, the color of a gate being forced open from the other side.

Or from this one.

"He's not waiting for us to arrive," Petra says softly, and I feel his heartbeat speed up beneath my ear. "Corvess is going to the Gate himself."
