## 17. March to the Gate

The wastes of Ashfall began where the maps gave up.

Petra had always assumed cartographers stopped drawing out of laziness. Now, three days past the last river and two days past the last anything—no tree, no well, no fence post, not even a crow willing to be lost here—he understood it was honesty. The land ahead was a gray-brown sea of cracked earth and slag hills, stitched together with rivers of frozen glass where dragon-fire had once run molten. The horizon did not curve so much as *end*, like a page torn from a book.

"Cheerful place," Tamsin said, spitting into wind that immediately threw her spit back at her. "I can see why they built a city somewhere else."

"You volunteered," Odelia reminded her, adjusting the strap of her pack, which contained more books than food—a ratio Petra had long since given up arguing about.

"I was drafted by guilt."

"Guilt is a legitimate recruiting officer," Odelia said serenely. "It has excellent retention rates."

Petra let them bicker behind him and kept his eyes on Wren, who walked at his left hand as she always did now, small feet finding purchase on ground that seemed actively hostile to small feet. She hadn't spoken aloud since Marrowfen. She didn't need to. When she wanted him to know something, she simply looked up, and the shard behind her ribs answered through his own chest like a second heartbeat slightly out of time with the first.

Right now she looked up, and what she told him was: *the ember is colder today.*

He knew. He could feel it too—the faint wrongness in her warmth when he took her hand each morning, as if holding a cup of tea that had gone lukewarm without ever being hot. Sable Yew's toll-memory still lived in him, one of the few he had left: the taste of river water, the witch's crooked smile, the words *it feeds or it dies, storm-caller, and so does she.* He carried that memory deliberately, turning it over every night like a stone in his pocket, because it was the only thing keeping him walking in the right direction.

Behind them, twenty miles back and gaining, marched the Fourth Legion of the Hollow Wardens under the banner of High Warden Corvess.

*We have until the Gate,* Petra thought. *Maybe less.*

The company walked on.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a slag hill shaped, Ilva observed, "like a dog sitting down and regretting it." It was the most conversation she'd offered all day. Her wound from the Black Archive raid had closed into an angry red seam along her ribs, and she moved like a woman negotiating with her own body and losing several clauses per hour.

Tamsin built the fire—real fire, mundane fire, struck from flint because using magic to light a cookfire in a city that banned magic had been habit for so long that even here, where no law reached, none of them quite trusted themselves to do otherwise. Odelia spread her charts on a blanket and weighted the corners with stones. Wren sat close to the flames with her knees drawn up, and Petra noticed—not for the first time—that she sat closer than comfort required, feeding sticks to the fire like a girl bribing a friend not to leave.

He crouched beside her. "Talk to me."

She looked up. In the firelight her eyes were ordinary brown, but underneath them something flickered, amber and patient, the way coals look when you think they've gone out.

*I dreamed of Vess again,* she said, in the First Tongue that lived now behind his eyes like sunlight remembered rather than seen. *She says the Gate has almost no voice left. She says if we are coming, we must come before the moon turns.*

"The moon turns in six days."

Wren nodded once. Then, after a moment, her small hand found his sleeve and tugged, and what came through was not words at all but a feeling, vast and cold and following: the legion, moving through the dark like a slow tide, and at its center a shape that was not entirely a man anymore.

"She's felt him," Ilva said quietly. She had come up on her bad side, which meant she trusted them enough to be vulnerable, which meant she was frightened. "Corvess. I served under him for nine years and I never once saw him sleep. I thought it was discipline." Her mouth twisted. "It's not discipline. Things like him don't sleep. They *wait*."

"What does he want with her?" Tamsin asked, though Petra suspected she knew; they all knew. "Besides the obvious."

"He doesn't want to kill the ember," Ilva said. "He wants to *keep* it. Sealed. Starved down to nothing, locked in a vault beneath Vessengard, so the realms stay shut forever and nothing from Ashfall ever crosses again." She looked at Wren, and her voice went rougher. "He'd keep her alive, too. However long that takes. Wardens are very good at keeping things alive that should be allowed to rest."

Wren's hand tightened on Petra's sleeve.

"Then we don't get caught," Petra said. It came out flatter and harder than he intended, and he felt the sky notice. That was the trouble with the wastes: there was nothing between him and the weather. No roofs, no walls, no polite distance. Out here his magic heard everything he felt, the way a dog hears its name.

The wind picked up. Somewhere east, thunder cleared its throat.

"Petra," Odelia said mildly, not looking up from her charts, "the barometric pressure."

"I'm fine."

"You are visibly not fine, and the clouds agree with me."

He breathed out, slow, the way he'd taught himself over five years of being small and quiet and harmless. The thunder grumbled off toward the horizon like a scolded animal. But it didn't go far. It never went far anymore. Since the archive burned—since Ilva's blood soaked into the cobblestones and Odelia's life's work went up in smoke and fury had poured through him like a burst dam—his storm-calling had come back whole, and whole was another word for *unmanageable*. In Vessengard he had spent a decade shrinking his gift down to a whisper. Now the whisper was shouting, and the sky leaned in to listen.

"It's getting worse, isn't it," Tamsin said. Not unkindly. Which was somehow worse.

"Yes."

"How much worse?"

Petra considered lying. He looked around the fire—at Odelia's ink-stained fingers, at Ilva's gray face, at Tamsin's jaw set in the stubborn line he remembered from a lesson room five years ago, at Wren watching him with the patience of someone who had been a gate and a child and an oath and would outlast them all—and decided against it.

"When I called lightning at the raid," he said, "I couldn't stop for eleven seconds. Eleven seconds is a long time. It took the street and part of the next one." He turned his hands over, palms up, ordinary hands, charm-seller's hands. "Out here, with nothing to dampen it? If I call the storm fully, I won't be able to choose what it takes. And it will take from me. That's how calling works. You pay in yourself."

"Pay what?" Tamsin asked sharply.

"I don't know yet. Warmth. Years. Words." He smiled, and it wasn't much of a smile. "Sable said the sky keeps accounts. I'd rather not find out the exchange rate."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Then Wren stood up, crossed the space between them, and put both her small hands flat against his palms.

What she gave him was not words. It was a picture: the Gate of Ashfall, vast and broken, and above it a storm—not wild, not hungry, but *held*, gathered in the hands of a man standing before the broken arches like a man holding open a door. The storm was enormous. The man was calm. And the cost of it was written across the sky in letters of light, and the letters spelled a word in the First Tongue that Petra could not read yet, but understood anyway, the way you understand a lullaby your mother sang before you were born.

*Purpose,* the word meant. Or maybe *offering*. The old language was like that—two meanings folded into one sound, the way a wing folds into a body.

"That's the plan, then," Tamsin said, watching his face. "You're going to hold the storm until the Gate, and then spend it."

"If there's anything left of me to spend," Petra said, "yes."

"Well." Tamsin poked the fire hard enough to send up a fountain of sparks. "Then we'd better make sure you arrive with something left. Eat your stew. All of it. Don't give me that look, I learned portioning from *you*, and you were insufferable about it."

Despite everything, Petra laughed. It startled him, the sound, rough from disuse. Around the fire, one by one, the others joined him—even Ilva, who winced halfway through because laughing pulled the seam in her ribs, and kept laughing anyway, which Petra thought might be the bravest thing anyone had done all day.

* * *

On the fourth day, the wastes changed.

The ground began to rise in long black ridges, and the ridges were warm. Not sun-warm—there was little enough sun this far east, only a bruised smear behind cloud—but warm from below, like the flank of a sleeping animal. Frozen-glass rivers ran between the ridges, and when the light caught them they glowed faintly gold, deep down, as if the fires beneath Ashfall had never entirely died but only gone very quiet, waiting.

Odelia walked with her nose practically dragging along the ground. "Do you know what this is?" she said, for perhaps the ninth time that morning. "This is the Meridian Flow. This is the road the dragons burned when they left. Every history calls it myth. I catalogued seventeen accounts of it and dismissed fourteen as forgery, and I have never in my life been so *happy* to be wrong—"

"Odelia," Ilva said.

"—and the thermal residue alone will rewrite the entire chronology of the Sundering—"

"*Odelia.*"

"What?"

Ilva pointed north.

Against the bruised sky, on the crest of the farthest ridge, stood a line of figures. Small at this distance, black against black, still as grave-markers. Even as the company froze, they did not move. They didn't need to. They were scouts; the legion was the tide behind them.

"How far?" Tamsin asked, all humor gone.

"A day," Ilva said. Her voice was level, but her hand had gone to her sword hilt, and Petra saw her knuckles whiten around it and then, deliberately, relax. "A day and a half, if their wounded slow them. They'll have wounded. I burned the pontoon bridge at Sorrel Ford before we left." A ghost of satisfaction crossed her face. "That was mine."

"They'll have engineers," Petra said.

"They'll have engineers," Ilva agreed grimly.

Wren tugged his sleeve. What she showed him was simple and terrible: the ridge line running north-to-south for miles, and beyond it, half-hidden in haze, a smudge of gold that made his heart stutter. The Gate. Close enough now that the ember in her chest could smell it, and was straining toward it like a plant toward light—which meant it was also burning faster, spending itself quicker, eating her warmth in great eager gulps because home was near and it wanted to arrive while there was still something left of it to arrive *as*.

"We can't outrun a legion," Odelia said. "Not with Ilva's ribs. Not with my knees. No offense to anyone's knees."

"None taken," said Ilva, whose knees had been making formal complaints since dawn.

"So we don't outrun them." Petra heard himself say it, and was surprised at how steady his voice was, and how completely unsteady everything underneath it. "We make them regret the pace."

Tamsin stared at him. "You said yourself—you can't control it. Eleven seconds, you said. You'd be calling a storm into a waste full of dry glass and slag, with us standing in the middle of it."

"Yes."

"And you'd be paying for it out of—" she gestured at all of him, "—*that*. Whatever's left."

"Yes."

For a long moment nobody moved except the wind, which had begun, very softly, to circle them. Petra felt it circling. It felt like being recognized.

Then Tamsin shrugged her pack off her shoulders, dug to the bottom of it, and produced a coil of copper wire, three iron stakes, and a strip of silvered cloth that had once, Petra realized with a jolt, been the banner of a Hollow Warden sergeant. She'd kept it. Of course she'd kept it. Tamsin kept things the way other people kept grudges—carefully, and for use.

"If you're going to be a lightning rod," she said, "you're going to be a *grounded* lightning rod. I've read Odelia's notes. Storm-callers who die in the field die because the charge has nowhere to go but through them. So we give it somewhere else to go." She tossed him the wire. "Five years ago you taught me grounding theory and I fell asleep. I'm sorry about that. Consider this my remedial exam."

Petra looked down at the wire in his hands, and then at his apprentice—no. Not apprentice. Not anymore. At the woman who had chosen him twice, once despite him and once because of him, and who had just solved in thirty seconds a problem he'd been carrying like a stone for four days.

"Tamsin Vance Hartwell," he began—he'd learned her full name in Marrowfen, and the middle name had explained a great deal about her and Odelia both—

"Don't you dare get sentimental, we're in a tactical situation."

"—you may pass the remedial exam."

"High praise," Odelia murmured. "He once told me my footnotes were 'adequate.' I framed the letter."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, and it was strange, Petra thought, how good the work felt. Ilva, who could barely lift her sword arm, proved she could still drive an iron stake with a mallet held two-handed, and directed the placement of the ground-lines with a soldier's eye for terrain. Odelia calculated charge dispersal on the back of a chart of the Meridian Flow, muttering numbers like prayers. Tamsin rigged the silvered banner-cloth into a crude sail-mast atop the highest slag ridge—a lure, a bright thing to draw the eye and the lightning both. And Wren walked the perimeter of the camp laying her palm flat against the warm black ground every few paces, and wherever she touched, the deep gold glow brightened, just slightly, just for a moment.

"She's waking the ground," Odelia breathed. "Or—it remembers her. The flow remembers the Oath. She's the Oath. Oh, that's going in the book, that is absolutely going in the book—"

"Later," Ilva said. "Book later. Legion now."

Because the legion had crested the ridge at dusk.

Petra stood on the high ground and looked south, and what he saw made his mouth go dry: not a skirmish line but a *river* of black armor, torchlight rippling along it like current, rank on rank on rank, and at its head a single figure on foot who cast no shadow even by torchlight. Corvess did not hurry. Things that wait do not hurry. But he came on, inevitable as arithmetic.

Petra felt the fear move through him, and then, remarkably, move *past* him, the way a wave passes a pier. Behind it was something quieter and much older: the feeling from Wren's vision, the word he couldn't read but understood. Purpose. Offering. Two meanings, one sound.

He raised his hands, and the sky, which had been circling him for days, finally came home.

It did not feel like calling. Calling was what he'd done in Vessengard—small, guilty, apologetic weather. This was different. This was the storm recognizing him the way Wren had recognized him at his door on the night of the dead-calm, barefoot and shadowless and certain. The clouds came down off the heights like a released breath. The pressure dropped so fast his ears popped. Lightning moved inside the cloud-belly, slow and luminous, like something turning over in its sleep, and every hair on Petra's arms rose to meet it, and he laughed—actually laughed—because after ten years of being small, he had forgotten how large he was allowed to be.

"Ground lines!" Tamsin shouted, and the copper wire sang.

The first bolt came down exactly where he asked it to, which surprised them both.

After that it was less asking and more arguing. The storm was wild, gloriously wild, and it took from him as it came—warmth first, then something subtler, a shelf of memories going dark like windows at dusk, names of streets in a city he suddenly couldn't swear he'd lived in. He paid. He kept paying. He planted his boots on the slag ridge with Tamsin's wire humming around him and he paid, and the lightning walked down the southern slopes in white columns, and the legion's vanguard broke and scattered like ants under a boot, and the thunder rolled out across the wastes announcing, in the oldest language there is, that this ground was spoken for.

When it was over—when the storm finally, reluctantly, curled itself back into the eastern clouds like a dog returning to its bed—Petra was on his knees, and Tamsin was holding his shoulders, and Wren was pressed against his side pouring her thin remaining warmth into him like someone pouring from a jug that was nearly empty itself.

"Never," Tamsin was saying, her voice shaking, "do that again without telling me the price *first*—"

"I told you the price."

"You told me the *category* of price! There's a difference!"

"Did it work?" His voice came out sanded down to gravel. "Is the legion—"

Ilva limped up onto the ridge beside him, and she was smiling, and it transformed her whole scarred face. Through the settling dust, far to the south, the torch-river of the legion had stopped. Stalled. Re-forming, yes, but re-forming *slowly*, and behind it the supply train was a chaos of overturned wagons and screaming mules.

"Bought us a day," Ilva said. "Maybe two. They'll march through it eventually—they always do—but they'll march angry, and angry soldiers make mistakes, and I intend to collect every mistake." She clapped him on the shoulder, gently, the way you touch something you suspect is fragile. "Well done, storm-caller."

Petra tried to answer and found that some words were missing. Not many. A handful. He searched for the name of the ford where Ilva had burned the bridge—*Sorrel*, it had been *Sorrel*—and found a smooth blank place where the name used to live.

The cost, made literal. The sky keeping its accounts.

Wren looked up at him, and he felt her see it—the small absence in him, the new smoothness—and what came through her grip on his hand was fierce and grieving and absolutely resolved: *we will reach the Gate before it takes enough of you to matter. We will. Walk.*

So they walked.

North, along the glowing veins of the Meridian Flow, five ragged figures in the gathering dark—an exiled storm-caller growing lighter by the mile, a dying ember-child, a disgraced archivist clutching her charts, a burned-out Warden, and an apprentice who had fallen asleep in grounding class and grown into the person holding the wire. Behind them the legion licked its wounds and came on. Ahead of them, past the last black ridge, the haze had parted, and for the first time all of them could see it:

The Gate of Ashfall, vast and broken and burning low on the horizon like a coal refusing to die.

And standing before it, motionless across the miles, a shape with wings folded like a mountain at rest, keeping a vigil that had lasted a hundred years.

Vess had seen them coming.

The dragon raised her head.
