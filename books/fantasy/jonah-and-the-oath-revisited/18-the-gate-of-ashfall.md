## 18. The Gate of Ashfall

They smelled it before they saw it.

Not ash, exactly—Petra had spent five days in the Ashfall wastes and had learned every variety of its stink: the sulfur bite near the old vents, the mineral tang of the glass plains where something vast had burned down to mirror. This was different. This was *old*. It was the smell of a fire that had gone out a century ago and never quite stopped grieving about it.

"Like a hearth the morning after," Odelia said beside him, her voice hushed in that way she reserved for primary sources. "When the coals are dead but the room still remembers being warm."

"Poetic," Tamsin muttered from the rear. She'd said little for two days—not coldly, Petra reminded himself, just carefully, the way she now handled everything between them. Carefully, like a charm with hairline cracks. "Smells like a burnt dinner to me."

"You have no romance in your soul."

"I have plenty of romance. I simply ration it."

Wren laughed—a small, startled sound, as if she'd surprised herself by owning one—and Petra felt the whole company straighten a little, the way a rope does when someone takes up the slack. Five days of walking. Ilva riding Sable's conjured litter because her wound wouldn't tolerate a saddle, gray-faced and furious about it in equal measure. And Wren growing quieter by the mile, her shard dimming like a lamp running out of oil, until last night she had pressed her small hot hand against Petra's wrist in the dark and spoken three words in the First Tongue.

*Almost home. Hurry.*

He understood them without trying to. That was the terrible arithmetic of this journey: every day his stolen memories should have been returning, and instead language poured into him like water into a dry channel while everything else drained out the other end. He could no longer remember his mother's face. He could remember, precisely and uselessly, the market price of copper wire in Vessengard across eleven years. The ember wasn't feeding on him yet—but it was leaning.

Now they crested the last ridge of black slag, and there it was.

The Gate of Ashfall did not look like a gate. That was the first thing. It looked like the skeleton of one—the fossil of an idea so large that stone had only been able to hold its outline. Two pillars of fused glass and dragon-bone rose perhaps four hundred feet, leaning toward each other but never touching, and between them hung the air itself, thickened, faintly golden, like honey held mid-pour. Through that golden haze Petra could see *through*—not to a landscape but to a suggestion of one: mountains that breathed. A sky with weather too large to be called weather. Somewhere beyond it, deep and slow, something exhaled.

And at the foot of the Gate, curled around the left pillar like a dog too tired to lift its head, lay the dragon.

"Saints above," Ilva whispered from the litter. Even wounded, even half-conscious, she found the breath for awe. "I've read the accounts. I never believed the accounts."

Vess made the accounts look like flattery done in miniature. The dragon's hide was the color of cooling embers—gray shot through with veins of dull orange that pulsed, faintly, in rhythm with the Gate itself. She was smaller than Petra had somehow expected, though *smaller* was relative; she could have draped herself over the archive district of Vessengard and had scale left over. Age had hollowed her. Her wings lay folded like a tent whose poles had given up, and her eyes—when they opened, which they did slowly, with the reluctance of someone surfacing from very deep water—were the color of banked coals looking for a reason.

"*Little ember,*" the dragon said.

Her voice did not arrive through the air. It arrived through the ground, through their boots, through the marrow of them, and Wren made a sound Petra had never heard from her—not a word, not a cry, something between recognition and homesickness—and broke into a run before anyone could stop her.

"Wren—" Tamsin lunged; Petra caught her arm and shook his head.

"She knows her," he said. He didn't know how he knew. He knew anyway. "Let her go."

The dragon lowered her head until her muzzle rested on the slag, an act that must have cost her enormously, and Wren walked into the curtain of her breath and stood there, tiny and barefoot and glowing faintly through the skin of her chest, and Vess regarded her with one coal-colored eye the size of a cartwheel.

"So you took shape after all," the dragon rumbled. "I wondered. I hoped." A plume of smoke escaped her nostrils, smelling of the same ancient grief as the air. "You are late, little ember. But then, covenants are always late. That is what makes them covenants rather than appointments."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the right pillar, close enough to feel the warmth coming off Vess's body, which was considerable and, Petra suspected, deliberate—the dragon was keeping them alive the way a stove keeps bread rising. Sable fussed over Ilva's dressing. Odelia sat cross-legged with her journals open, writing by shard-light, because Wren had drifted close to her and apparently proximity to the ember was now the best lamp in two realms. Tamsin stood watch on the ridge, spear across her knees, watching the horizon behind them with an expression Petra recognized from mirrors: the expression of someone counting miles and not liking the total.

Petra went to the dragon alone.

There was no protocol for this. He'd checked, in the way of a man grasping for any task that wasn't the actual task. No protocol. So he simply walked to within thirty feet of Vess's head and stopped, and waited, feeling profoundly like a man who had come to negotiate with a mountain.

"You are the storm-caller," Vess said, when she chose to notice him. One eye rotated. "The one who ran. The one who hid among trinkets and taught his hands to forget."

"That's me." His voice came out steadier than he deserved. "Though my hands never quite got the message."

"Hm." Smoke curled from her jaws. For a long moment the only sounds were wind through the pillars and the slow bellows-draw of the dragon's breathing. Then: "Come closer, storm-caller. I am dying. I do not have the strength to pretend you frighten me, so we may skip the part where you tremble."

He came closer. Up near, her scales were not gray at all but the color of a fire seen through heavy smoke, and each one carried a mark—a sigil, worn nearly smooth, and Petra's fingers itched with the sudden certainty that he had once known every one of them by name.

"The reforging," he said. "Odelia thinks she understands it. Sable thinks she does. Nobody agrees."

"They would not." Vess closed her eye. "The Oath was not written by mortals or by dragons. It was written by the space between us—the agreement itself became a thing, and the thing became an ember, and the ember needed somewhere to live. It chose a child. It is choosing again, even now, more thoroughly." Her flank rippled with a breath that might have been a sigh. "To reforge it, the ember must be fed. Not blood. Not magic. Those are kindling. It must be fed with *value*—with the thing an oath-keeper holds most dear, freely given, wholly spent. That is the toll the first keepers paid, and the second, and every generation since. An oath is only worth what it costs."

"And if no one pays?"

"Then the Gate closes forever. Your realm becomes a sealed jar. Mine becomes ash." The eye opened again, and fixed on him, and there was nothing monstrous in it—only a weariness so old it had gone smooth, like sea glass. "I will tell you what no one has told you, storm-caller, because I am dying and the dying may be blunt. You have already paid once. Five years ago. The ember remembers your coin, even if you do not. It has been waiting for you to decide whether to pay again—or whether the first payment was enough."

Petra stood very still.

"What did I pay?" he asked. "The first time."

"You know what you paid." Gentle, for a voice that came up through the bedrock. "You paid it in a tower, in the dark, with Wardens at the door. You simply do not know that you know. That was the shape of the bargain: memory taken, secret kept. Ask yourself, storm-caller—when you look at that child, why does your chest hurt? Not fear. Fear lives higher up. That ache sits deeper. That is the ache of a man standing next to the thing he gave up everything for, holding it, and not being allowed to recognize it."

His eyes were burning. He turned away from the dragon and looked instead across the camp—to where Wren sat wrapped in Odelia's cloak, showing the archivist how the light in her chest brightened when she hummed. Odelia was humming along, terribly. Tamsin, on the ridge, was pretending not to watch and failing completely. Ilva had one hand resting, lightly, on Sable's arm, and neither of them appeared to have noticed.

"There's another thing," Petra said hoarsely. "Corvess. What is he, truly?"

"Older than the ban. Older than the Wardens' founding. He was a keeper once—the keeper who stole the twin ember and tried to own the covenant rather than serve it. We burned him. Burning, it turns out, is not the same as ending." The great head lifted slightly, and all along the dragon's spine the ember-veins flickered brighter, agitated. "He wants the shard not to seal the realms but to replace the Oath with a leash. He has waited a hundred years to be offered a child who carries fire in her chest. Do not mistake him for a man hunting a fugitive. He is a debt come to collect."

Behind him, distantly, Tamsin's voice rang off the slag: "*Riders!*"

Petra counted them before he'd finished turning. A long dark line on the southern ridge, moving fast, unbothered by terrain that had slowed five travelers to a crawl for five days. At its head, a figure on a pale horse who did not seem to touch the ground so much as *disagree* with it less than everyone else.

"He moves quickly for a man in armor," Sable observed, appearing at Petra's shoulder with a witch's gift for arriving at the worst moment.

"He isn't in armor," Petra heard himself say. "Look again. That's not steel. That's absence wearing a shape."

* * *

High Warden Corvess rode down alone.

That was somehow worse than the legion—the legion halted at the top of the ridge, two hundred spears silhouetted against the bruised sky, while their master descended the slope at an unhurried walk, reins slack, helm beneath his arm. Up close, unarmored, Corvess looked almost ordinary: a lean, gray man with neat features and the patient eyes of a librarian. Only the eyes were wrong. They were the eyes of something that had learned to blink recently and still considered it a novelty.

"Quintana," he said pleasantly.

"You have me at a disadvantage. I don't actually know if that's my name."

"It is. I attended your trial." Corvess dismounted, folding his hands, a man settling in for a reasonable discussion. Behind Petra, he heard Tamsin level her spear and Sable murmur something wet and riverish, and Ilva—Ilva dragged herself upright in the litter despite every argument her wound was making, because some habits of command don't consult the body. "You've come far, all of you. Through the wastes, on forged papers, with a dying woman and a dying god-child. It's genuinely admirable. I want you to know that I take no pleasure in what happens next, and I would very much like for there to be no next."

"Then leave," Tamsin said flatly.

"Tamsin Vale. The apprentice." Corvess smiled at her, and Petra's skin crawled at how warmly he managed it. "You were flagged for recruitment twice, did you know? Unusual aptitude. Loyalty under pressure—you proved that beautifully, burning your credentials. Though I confess I hoped you'd deliver them to me rather than the fire."

"You know nothing about my loyalty."

"On the contrary. Loyalty is my oldest subject of study." He turned back to Petra, and the pleasantness dropped away all at once, like a mask that had only ever been resting on the face. What remained was neither cruel nor angry. It was *hungry*, in the way winter is hungry. "Here is my offer, Quintana, and I will make it only once, so attend. You are an oath-keeper. Perhaps the last qualified one. The ember requires a payment you cannot afford, and I can spare you the expense entirely."

Petra said nothing.

"Your memories." Corvess's voice softened into something almost gentle, and that was the cruelest stroke yet, because it was clearly sincere. "All of them. The tower. The choice. The friend's voice. Five years ago and everything before it—your childhood, your mother's face, whatever small warm things you've already lost on this road and grieved without knowing why. I hold them. I have held them for five years in a vault beneath the Black Archive, waiting for you to be worth the storage." He spread his hands. "Walk away. Take the girl north, take your ragged family, vanish again, sell your charms. I will seal the Gate with the ember myself—I have the twin; I have always had the twin—and the realms will close, and the Hollow Wardens will keep a peace that has no more fires in it ever again. No more burnings. No more exile. No more fear. And you will have back every single thing you paid."

The silence rang.

Petra became aware, distantly, that he had stopped breathing. Because here was the trap inside the offer, the beautiful, unbearable architecture of it: Corvess was not lying. Petra could see it in the wrongness of his eyes, could taste it in the First Tongue rising unbidden at the back of his mind—*true, true, true.* The memories existed. They would be returned, whole. The man meant it.

His mother's face. He had lost it somewhere on the wastes road and only discovered the hole this morning, probing for it like a tongue seeking a missing tooth. The friend's voice from the flashes—the tower, the laughter, the hand on his shoulder saying *you'll do great things, Petra, terrible ones possibly, but great.* His trial. His betrayal. Whatever he had done, whoever he had been—he could have all of it back. He could be a whole man again instead of a house with rooms boarded shut.

All it would cost was Wren.

Because he understood, with the clarity that had been following him for days like a stray dog, what a sealed Gate meant for her. She was the Oath reborn. If Corvess leashed the covenant—if the ember's twin closed the way instead of opening it—then the thing living in the child's chest would either gutter out or be *used*, drawn down year by year to feed a lock. He had seen what the shard was already doing to her warmth. A sealed Gate didn't save Wren. It spent her. Slowly. Politely. With paperwork.

And the other truth, the one sitting deeper than fear: he did not need to remember loving her. He loved her now. The love had survived the theft, grown wild in the empty lots where the memories used to be, and it was the single largest fact of his life, larger than any face or voice Corvess kept in a vault.

"Petra," Odelia said quietly, at his elbow. Not pleading. Just saying his name, the way you set a lantern on a table.

Tamsin hadn't moved. Neither had Wren—she had come to stand beside him sometime during the offer, her small hand finding his, her shard pulsing slow and dim, and she spoke in the First Tongue, softly, so only he could hear it, and he understood every syllable:

"*Whatever you choose, I choose too. But I would rather burn open than be locked.*"

Corvess watched them, patient as frost.

"Well, Quintana?" he said. "Your self, returned entire. All you must surrender is the future."

Petra looked at the vault of his own lost life, and wanted it—gods, how he wanted it, a wanting with teeth—and then looked at the Gate, and the dying dragon, and the child whose hand fit his like the last piece of a lock.

He took a breath, and the storm that lived in him rose to meet it, glad at last to be of use.

"No," Petra said. "But thank you for the inventory. It helps to know what I'm fighting for."

Something moved behind Corvess's borrowed eyes—the first honest expression the creature had shown, and it was disappointment, and it was hunger sharpening into appetite.

"Pity," said the High Warden, and reached into his coat, and drew out a shard of black fire that drank the daylight around it. "I had so hoped to avoid the mess."

Behind them, with a groan like a continent turning over, the dragon Vess began—slowly, painfully, joint by ancient joint—to rise.
