## 19. The Reforging

The Gate of Ashfall did not look like a gate anymore. It looked like a wound that had been pretending to be a mountain for a hundred years.

Two cliffs of black glass leaned toward each other across a chasm of cooling ash, and between them hung the last of the arch—a rib of fused stone no wider than a doorway, glowing faintly at its heart like a coal someone had forgotten to bury. The air around it shimmered with heat-haze even though the wind off the wastes was cold enough to bite through three coats. Somewhere below the ash, Petra could feel it: the slow, arrhythmic pulse of the dragon-realm, beating like a heart in a chest that had almost decided to stop.

Vess lay coiled before the arch, and Vess was dying.

Petra had imagined dragons his whole life—had called storms in their honor once, back when he'd still known why—and nothing in his imagination had prepared him for the reality of an old one at the end. She was vast; that part was true. Her body made the ruined gate look like a garden trellis. But she was also *thin*, her scales gone the grey of old snow, her wings folded like a beggar's blanket, and when she lifted her head to look at them her eyes were the color of banked fires—warm, and patient, and nearly out.

"*Little ember,*" she said, and her voice came not through the ears but through the bones, through the ground, through the shard in Wren's chest, which flared so brightly it lit the girl from inside like a lantern held behind skin. "*You brought him.*"

Wren stood at Petra's side, small and barefoot as the night she'd first appeared at his door, her hand buried in his coat pocket because she liked to hold on without admitting she liked to hold on. Behind them, Tamsin had her staff planted in the ash and her jaw set in the particular way that meant she was terrified and would die before showing it. Odelia clutched her satchel of rescued records against the wind. Ilva stood apart, one arm bound and useless since the Black Archive, watching the horizon they'd come from—the horizon where, even now, dust plumes marked the approach of a Warden legion.

"We have until dark," Ilva said. "Maybe less. Corvess isn't marching. He's *coming*."

"Then we don't waste time being poetic," Tamsin said. "How does this work?"

Petra looked at Vess, because he'd asked the same question in the night, alone, when he couldn't sleep, and the dragon had told him. He'd hoped she'd been riddling. Dragons rarely riddled at the end of things.

"The ember is the Oath," he said. "But an oath is only words unless someone pays for them. It has to be fed." His mouth was dry. "Not blood. Not life. Something worth more."

"*What you value most,*" Vess said, in that voice like a landslide remembering gentleness. "*Given freely. Held back nothing.*"

Tamsin went very still. "And what do you value most, exactly? Because if you're about to say your charm-shop—"

"My memories," Petra said.

The wind filled the silence that followed, moaning through the broken arch like a flute with a crack in it.

"All of them," he went on, before courage could fail him. "Not just the ones that were stolen. Everything. My mother's kitchen. The first storm I ever called—I was nine, I wet myself, it was very dignified. Tamsin—" he turned to her, and found that looking at her was already like looking at something through water, "—I'll forget you too. I want you to know that I know that. And I'm doing it anyway."

"No," Tamsin said.

"Tamsin—"

"*No.*" Her voice cracked down the middle. "You don't get to be noble at me. You vanished once already. Five years I thought I'd failed you, that I'd done something, that I'd— and now you're going to stand there and volunteer to do it again, permanently, and expect me to just—" She stopped. Pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. When she lowered it, she was glaring at him with red-rimmed fury, which from Tamsin was practically an embrace. "Fine. Fine. But I'm writing everything down. Every stupid thing you've ever said to me. And when this is over, I'm reading all of it to you, out loud, whether you like it or not."

"That sounds unbearable," Petra said. "Do it."

Odelia was already digging in her satchel. "I have ink," she said thickly. "And paper. And a memory like a steel trap, young man, and I am *not* above embellishment if it makes you sound better than you were."

"He was insufferable," Tamsin said.

"Noted," Odelia said, writing it down.

Ilva said nothing. She only saluted him—awkwardly, with her wounded arm tucked against her side—and Petra understood that for her, that was a whole speech.

It was Wren who hadn't moved.

She was looking up at him with those ember-colored eyes, and her hand had gone tight around his in his coat pocket, and her face was doing the thing it did when she was trying very hard to speak in the waking world, where the First Tongue sat heavy and strange on her tongue.

"You could keep some," she managed. Ordinary words, cracked and small. "You could keep me."

The words went through him like a spear finding the gap in armor. For one long, shameful moment, he wanted it. He wanted to stand here at the edge of everything and bargain, keep the child, keep the apprentice, keep the five years of rain-slick mornings selling luck-charms to frightened people, and feed the fire only the stolen parts, the scorched pages, the tower and the friend's voice and the choice—

But he had seen what half-an-oath did. He had spent five years living inside one. A covenant fed with leftovers would hold for a season and then gutter, and Wren would burn out feeding it, and everything they had walked through the wastes for would curdle into a slower version of the same ending.

He crouched down to her level. Up close, he could see the light under her skin, flickering faster now, hungry.

"Hey," he said. "You remember what you told me on the river? In the First Tongue, when you thought I couldn't understand?"

Her chin trembled. "*I am afraid of burning out.*"

"And I told you—" he tapped his own chest, where his heart was hammering, "—that burning out and burning bright are the same fire, little ember. It just depends what you're lighting." He smiled, and found, to his surprise, that it wasn't a brave smile. It was a real one. "You're what I'm lighting. All of this. So I need you to do the hardest thing anyone's ever asked of you, harder than anything I'm about to do."

"What?" she whispered.

"Remember me *for* me. Both of us can't forget. Somebody has to carry it."

Wren stared at him. Then she threw her arms around his neck and held on with the desperate strength of a child who had learned, somewhere before she could talk, exactly how much things cost. He held her back and breathed in the woodsmoke smell of her hair and let himself memorize it—not because memorizing would save it, but because some things deserve to be felt fully on their way out.

Above the chasm, the sky had begun to bruise.

"They're here," Ilva said.

* * *

Corvess did not arrive with the legion. He arrived ahead of it, walking alone across the ash, and the ash did not dare cling to him.

He looked, as always, like a tall man in a Warden's grey coat, weathered and reasonable, the sort of officer who remembered your name and your daughter's illness. That had always been the horror of him. Even now, standing at the edge of the dying dragon's shadow with a legion fanning out behind him like a hand of black cards, he wore the face of a man arriving to negotiate.

"Quintana." His voice carried easily over the wind, warm as a hearth. "You look terrible. Exile agrees with no one—that was always the flaw in our founders' method. Punishment should rehabilitate, not merely remove." His gaze slid past Petra and fixed on Wren, and for one unguarded instant the warmth fell away and something ancient looked out, something with ember-bright eyes and a hunger older than kingdoms. "There she is. A century of patience, and there she is."

"Stay back," Tamsin said, staff raised. Lightning crawled along its length, answering Petra's mood more than hers.

"I'm not here to fight." Corvess spread his hands. "I'm here to make the offer I should have made in Vessengard, before you burned my archive and cost me two good sergeants' patience." He looked at Petra directly. "Your memories, Quintana. All of them. Restored—every stolen year, every scorched page, every name. You'd know who betrayed you. You'd know what you lost. You'd know *yourself*, whole, for the first time in five years. In exchange, you give me the child and walk away. Rich. Pardoned. Remembered."

The temptation was not subtle. It was a fist closing around Petra's throat.

Because that was the thing—he didn't even know what he'd be getting back. He knew the shape of the hole. He knew there had been a tower, a friend's voice, a choice. He knew the betrayal was his own, that he had stolen the twin ember to save someone, that his exile had been a sacrifice dressed up as a sentence. But knowing the shape of a room is not the same as standing in it. Somewhere inside him was a man who had been whole, and that man was waiting behind a locked door, and Corvess was holding out the key.

Beside him, Wren's grip tightened.

"You're hesitating," Corvess observed gently. "Good. Hesitation is honesty. Take the deal, Quintana. Be yourself again. Let the realms stay shut—let the dragons finish dying in peace, let magic stay banned, let the world be *safe*. What has the Oath ever done for people like us but burn us? I gave a hundred years to it. I gave *everything* to it, and it left me in this—" the mask slipped, just for a heartbeat, and beneath it was something cracked and charred and endlessly tired, "—in this *body*, in this *century*, alone. Seal it. Bury it. Go home and sell your charms."

Petra looked at him for a long moment. And then, because he was who he was—even now, especially now—he answered honestly.

"You're right," he said. "That it left you alone. That's what oaths do when you treat them like property instead of promise." He stepped forward, putting himself between Corvess and Wren, and felt the storm rise in him—not suppressed now, not for the first time in five years, but welcomed, roaring up through his bones like a river through a broken dam. "You want to know what I value most? Not my memories. *Them.*" He gestured—at Tamsin, at Odelia, at Ilva, at the small blazing girl at his side. "And you can't buy them, because they were never mine to sell. They chose me. That's the whole trick of it, Corvess. That's the part you never learned in a hundred years."

Corvess sighed, and the reasonable-man mask came off entirely, and what stood in the Warden's body then made even the legion behind him take a step back.

"So be it," said the oath-breaker.

* * *

It happened fast after that, the way endings do.

Corvess moved—not walking, *unfolding*, the borrowed body splitting along seams no human anatomy admitted to—and the legion, wisely or by command, did not advance. Fire the color of absence poured off him, and where it touched the ash, the ash forgot it had ever been anything. Tamsin's lightning met it and screamed. Odelia dragged Ilva behind the bulk of Vess's flank. And Petra turned his back on all of it, because that was the price and the point, and knelt before the arch with Wren's hands in his.

"*Now,*" Vess said, and her great head bowed until her brow touched the stone beside them, and the last of her strength flowed into the Gate like a tide into a channel. "*Feed it, oath-keeper. Feed it everything.*"

Petra reached into himself and opened the door.

They came willingly—all of them, because he offered freely, and that was the alchemy of it. His mother's kitchen, bread and rain on the window. The first storm, age nine, undignified and glorious. His apprenticeship, his mastery, the tower, the friend whose voice he could no longer hold onto even as he gave it—*goodbye, goodbye, I never learned if you forgave me*—the trial, the theft, the choice, the five gray years of charms and fear and a shop that smelled of cedar and rain. Tamsin at nine years old, furious, brilliant, setting her first warding wrong on purpose to see if he'd notice. He noticed. He always noticed. The river, and Wren's voice saying *I am afraid of burning out*, and the moment on the barge when she fell asleep against his arm and he sat perfectly still for six hours so as not to wake her.

He gave it all. He held back nothing. And it did not feel like losing.

That was the miracle he would not be present to remember: it felt like *spending*, the way a miser feels spending, at last, on something worthy—gold transformed at last into light. The memories streamed out of him and into the shard in Wren's chest, and the shard drank them and blazed, and the blaze ran up through the girl and into the arch, and the dead coal at the heart of the Gate woke like an eye opening.

He spoke the First Tongue. He didn't know how he knew it—perhaps the ember was teaching him even as it ate him, perhaps it had always lived in him under the exile—but the words came up from somewhere older than his forgetting, and they were simple, the way true things are simple:

*I give what I was, so that what comes after may live.*

The Gate took fire.

Not flame—*dawn*. Light poured through the broken arch and up into the bruised sky, and the chasm below roared open like a throat, and out of it rose heat and wingbeats and voices, hundreds of voices, a chorus of the long-asleep realm shaking off its century of dusk. The Oath closed around the light like two hands clasping. Petra felt it seal—felt it in his teeth, his hollowed chest, the ground itself—and understood, in the last sliver of self remaining to him, that it was done, and that he had paid, and that it was enough.

Corvess screamed.

Denied, unmade in his purpose, the oath-breaker hurled himself at the Gate—not to use it, but to break it, to drag the new Oath down with his own burning—and Vess, dying Vess, faithful Vess, opened eyes that were suddenly, finally, fully alight.

"*No,*" she said, in a voice that split the sky, "*you may not.*"

Dragon-fire took him. Not the absence-colored fire he wielded, but the old fire, the first fire, the kind that burns away only what is false. It caught the borrowed body and burned it like a coat off a thief's back, and beneath it there was nothing—no man, no monster, only a century of grievance and hunger, and the fire held it up to the light of the reopened Gate until even the grievance could not bear to exist, and then there was simply ash, drifting, ordinary, done.

The legion fled. Petra watched them go from a great distance, through deepening water, through softening light.

The last thing he saw clearly was Wren.

She stood in the heart of the Gate's radiance, blazing like a small sun, the Oath whole inside her and around her and everywhere—and she was laughing, or crying, or both, and she was turning toward him, reaching for him, shouting his name—

His name. She was shouting his name.

He reached back. He was smiling. He was not afraid.

The light took the rest.

* * *

Then—quiet. Ash sifting down like grey snow. Wind through a Gate that was no longer broken but *burnished*, humming softly, a door held open rather than a wound held shut. Around it, the company of the scattered keepers stood in a circle around a man lying in the ash, breathing, alive, empty.

Wren knelt and put her ear against his chest, listening to a heart that beat steady and blank as fresh paper.

"He's still in there," she said, in the First Tongue, and this time everyone heard it, and this time everyone understood, because the world had changed around them while they weren't looking. She lifted her head, ember-eyed, tear-streaked, luminous. "He's just waiting."

Tamsin knelt on his other side, clutching a notebook already fat with ink, and Odelia's hand rested on her shoulder, and Ilva stood watch at the Gate with her one good arm, and far overhead—farther than the sky had ever let anything fly—the shape of wings crossed the stars.

"Then we'd better start talking," Tamsin said, and opened the notebook. "Chapter one. You were born somewhere rainy, and you were insufferable from the start..."

The man with no yesterday slept on, and dreamed of nothing, and was loved anyway—which, though he would not learn it for some time, had always been the whole of the Oath.
