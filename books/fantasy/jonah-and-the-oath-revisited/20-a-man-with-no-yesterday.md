## 20. A Man With No Yesterday

I had rehearsed this moment for five years. In the rehearsal, I said something cutting and perfect, and he wept, and I forgave him, or I didn't, and either way I won.

In the rehearsal, he knew who I was.

He was lying on a bedroll in the shadow of the Gate of Ashfall, and the whole world smelled of scorched stone and dragon-fire, and when his eyes opened they were clear and calm and completely empty.

"Hello," he said. His voice was hoarse, as if he'd been shouting for days — which, I supposed, in a way he had. He looked at me the way you look at a stranger on a ferry: politely, with mild interest, already looking past you at the view. "Have we met?"

The words went through me like a Warden's pike. I'd carried my anger across half a kingdom to deliver it, sharpened it every night by the fire, polished it with every bitter memory of standing alone in that schoolroom while the Wardens came up the stairs — and now there was no one to hand it to. He had spent himself. Every memory, every grudge, every kindness, every lesson he ever taught me about threading lightning through copper wire without singing your eyebrows off. All of it, poured into an ember to keep a child alive and a gate open, and what was left sitting up on the bedroll was a man with no yesterday.

"You could say that," I managed.

He smiled. It was his smile. That was the cruelest part. The face was worn thin as old parchment, gray at the temples where it hadn't been gray before, lines carved deep around his mouth — but the smile was exactly the same, crooked on the left side, and it made my chest hurt in a way I didn't have time to examine.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I feel as though I ought to know you. You have the look of someone I've disappointed."

Odelia made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been a sob. Ilva, propped against a fallen pillar with her bandaged side and her sergeant's scowl, said nothing, but her jaw did something complicated.

"Don't worry about it," I said, because my voice would betray me if I tried anything longer. "Everyone's disappointed in you. You'll get used to it."

* * *

Wren found us before the sun did.

She came out of the Gate's archway walking slowly, the way you walk when your body is new to you — which, in fairness, hers had been remade overnight into something that glowed faintly at the center, like a lantern wrapped in skin. The Oath was whole. We had all felt it go through the world like a bell through water. And she was alive, which was the only thing any of us had cared about for one long terrible hour while Petra lay still and the ember-light drained out of him and out of her both, until Tamsin — I — had pressed my ear to his chest and heard the stubborn, ordinary drum of a human heart deciding to keep going.

She knelt beside him. She didn't say anything. She just looked at him, and he looked back, and something passed between them that needed no language at all — though I suppose between the two of them, *no language* was never really on the table.

"Hello," he said again, and then, slower, wonderingly: "I know you."

"You know me," Wren agreed. Her voice was steady. Hers always was, even now, even after everything; there was something in her that had been old before it was young. "You're Petra."

"Petra." He tried the name like a coin bitten to test its metal. "Is that mine?"

"It's yours," she said. "I kept it safe."

And then this man with no yesterday began to cry — quietly, without drama, tears simply falling down a face that seemed surprised to be making them — and Wren put her arms around his neck, and I turned away and pretended very hard to check on Ilva's bandages, which did not need checking, and Ilva let me pretend, which was the kindest thing anyone had done for me all week.

* * *

We camped three days at the foot of the Gate, because none of us could walk yet and none of us wanted to leave.

Ilva's wound was the practical reason. Corvess's legion had scattered when their master came apart in a bloom of dragon-fire — I will carry the sound of that to my grave, a sound like a name being unspoken — but scattered soldiers regroup, and Ilva, professional to the marrow, insisted we move before they remembered how. Her body overruled her on the second morning, and she swore at it in three languages, two of which I suspect she invented.

The real reason was that leaving felt like abandoning him. Abandoning *it*. Whatever was left.

On the second day, I watched him discover his own hands.

He was sitting by the fire turning a length of copper wire over and over, frowning at it, and then — not deciding to, exactly, the way you don't decide to breathe — his fingers began to work. Loop, twist, thread, knot. A charm-shape. The simplest one, the luck-knot every hedge-witch teaches first, the one he taught me when I was fourteen and all elbows and ambition. His hands moved through it smooth as water finding a channel, and when he finished he held it up and stared at it like it had spoken to him.

"I don't know how I did that," he said.

"Muscle remembers," Odelia said from behind her stack of salvaged archive-notes. She hadn't stopped cataloguing since the Black Archive burned; grief, for Odelia, looked like ink-stained fingers and a fierce refusal to stop writing things down. "It's the most loyal part of us. Minds forget. Hands hold on."

"What does it do?"

"It's a luck-charm," I said. "For people who are frightened. It doesn't do much. It just says: someone thought of you today."

He looked at the little twisted loop of copper for a long moment. "That seems like enough," he said, "to be."

I had to walk away and inspect the horizon for a while.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about getting your wish. I had wanted, for five years, to stand in front of Petra Hartwell and make him account for himself. Where he went. Why he left me. What could have mattered so much that he'd abandon a fifteen-year-old girl mid-lesson with Wardens on the stairs and not so much as a note.

Now he sat across the fire from me, and he could not account for a single day of his life, and I discovered that what I had actually wanted — what I had wanted under the wanting, the way you want rain under wanting the storm to end — was just to be near him again. To be his apprentice. To be *his*, in the loose, argumentative, fiercely loyal way I had been before everything broke.

The universe has a cruel sense of humor. It gave me back the man and took away the history, and now I was angry at a person who didn't exist, grieving losses he couldn't remember inflicting.

"He'd have hated this," I told Odelia on the third night, when Wren was asleep against Petra's shoulder and Ilva was finally sleeping properly, medicated with Sable Yew's river-tea and her own stubbornness. "Being pitied. Being *handled*. He'd have made some joke and changed the subject and gone off to brood at the weather."

"He is currently asleep at a nine-year-old's bedtime," Odelia observed, "which suggests some things survive the erasure."

"That's not funny."

"No," she agreed mildly. "But it's true, and truth gets to be funny eventually. Give it ten years." She dipped her pen. "Tamsin. Someone has to hold what he gave away. He can't carry it anymore. If we don't, it's gone — and then Corvess wins twice, because he'll have erased a good man and gotten away clean."

"What are you suggesting?"

"A ledger," she said, as if this were obvious, because to Odelia it was. "Of him. Everything we remember. Every story, every lesson, every infuriating habit. We write him back into existence, one page at a time, and we read it to him until the pages become his."

I thought about it. I thought about the schoolroom, and the stairs, and the five years of anger, and the wire charm sitting in his pocket that his hands had made without asking his permission.

"All right," I said. "But I get to record the parts where he was wrong."

"Naturally. Those are the best-documented sections."

* * *

We started the ledger the next morning, and I will admit here, since no one in that company will ever read this page, that I cried twice writing my first entry, and lied about it once.

*Entry the First,* Odelia wrote, in her beautiful banned-archive script. *Subject: Petra Hartwell, storm-caller, oath-keeper, charm-seller, coward, hero, teacher. Age: unknown, older than he pretends. Distinguishing features: crooked smile (left), gray coming in early, hands that cannot leave wire alone.*

"Read it to him," Wren said.

So I did. I sat across the fire and read our own memories of him back to him, and he listened the way he must have once listened to students — head tilted, eyes narrowed, interrupting constantly.

"I don't sound like that."

"You absolutely sound like that," I said.

"I wouldn't have said 'the weather is a colleague, not a weapon.' That's precious."

"You said it the day you flooded the practice yard," I said. "You were soaked to the ribs and Tamsin — I — was laughing too hard to cast the drying spell, and you said it with tremendous dignity while water poured out of your boot."

He laughed. It startled him, I think, laughter arriving from somewhere deeper than memory, and Wren beamed like she'd personally forged it.

*Entry the Fourth: On grudges.* Odelia wrote that one, and I read it, and it was mostly about me, and reading my own grievance aloud to a man who couldn't remember earning it was the strangest penance I've ever performed. *He left his apprentice without explanation. This was unforgivable. It has been forgiven. Both facts are recorded here so that neither is lost.*

He was quiet for a while after that one.

"Whoever I was," he said finally, "he must have been sorry. Every single day. Even if he couldn't say why."

"He was," Wren said, with the flat certainty of a child stating the sunrise. "I dreamed it. He was sorry the whole time."

And because she was Wren, and because she had walked out of a dragon-realm with the dawn in her chest, nobody argued.

* * *

We broke camp on the fourth day, heading south toward Vessengard, because home is home even when it tried to kill you, and because Ilva said the Wardens' legion had dissolved into squabbling garrisons and the martial law had collapsed the moment its architect stopped existing. There were rumors already — travelers on the road spoke of them the way you speak of weather, half-disbelief, half-hope — that charms were being sold openly in the river towns. That a greenwitch in Marrowfen had hung her sigils on her door and dared anyone to object. That magic was seeping back into the world like water into drought-cracked ground, slow and wary and unstoppable.

Petra walked beside Wren. He had appointed himself her guardian within a day of waking, on no evidence whatsoever except that his entire body pointed at her like a compass needle, and nobody had the heart or the reason to tell him otherwise.

"Do I have family?" he asked me on the second day of walking. "Anyone waiting?"

"You have us," I said. "Congratulations. We're terrible."

"What are we, exactly? To each other."

I considered the question. Odelia, walking ahead with her ledger, pretended not to listen, which fooled no one. Ilva, limping along on Sable's tea and spite, also pretended not to listen, less successfully.

"You're my teacher," I said. "Or you were. Now I suppose you're... mine to teach. Fair warning: I'm a worse instructor than you were. I grade harshly."

"And her?" He nodded at Wren, who was several paces ahead teaching herself to whistle, badly, with tremendous joy.

"She's yours," I said. "You're raising her. That part doesn't need remembering. It's already true."

He nodded slowly, absorbing it, and then: "Did I do it well? Before?"

I thought of a barefoot girl at a shop door in the rain, taken in by a frightened man who had every reason to run and didn't. Of a barge downriver, and a child's fear translated word by word. Of an ember fed with everything he had.

"Yeah," I said. "You did it well."

* * *

We reached Vessengard on an evening of soft rain — real rain, unsummoned, the ordinary kind, though I caught Petra watching it with an expression I couldn't read and didn't try to. The city stood. The Hollow Wardens' towers stood empty, doors open, records spilling into the streets where citizens picked through them with the tentative delight of looters at a bakery. Somewhere a bell was ringing that hadn't rung in years.

At the edge of the Charmmarket, his shop — our destination, chosen by Wren with the authority of someone who had lived there — waited with its shutters closed and its sign hanging crooked. *Charms for the Fearful.* The paint had faded. The lock was broken where the Wardens had come through it, that night that started everything.

He stood in front of it a long time.

"I lived here," he said.

"For years," I said. "Alone. Hiding. Selling luck to frightened people and pretending you weren't one of them."

"That sounds lonely."

"It was," said Wren, taking his hand. "But it isn't now."

He looked down at her, and then around at the four of us — the disgraced archivist with her ledger, the ex-Warden leaning on a spear she should have retired, the angry apprentice who wasn't angry anymore, or was, but had somewhere better to put it — and something settled in his face. Not recognition. Recognition was gone, spent at the Gate like everything else. But something adjacent to it. Something that knows a shape without knowing its name.

"I don't remember any of you," he said. "I want to be honest about that. I may never remember. But I know—" he touched his chest, where the ember had burned, where now there was only an ordinary human heart doing its stubborn work— "I know I'd choose this. All of you. Whatever I was, I trust him to have chosen right."

"Well," I said, and my voice cracked on the second syllable, and I pushed through it, "someone has to keep you honest about the flooding-the-practice-yard incident. Consider it a life sentence."

Odelia opened the ledger to a fresh page. Ilva shouldered the broken door open with the ease of a woman who had kicked in considerably worse. Wren tugged him over the threshold into the dim familiar dark of the shop, where dust hung in the lamplight like held breath.

And I swear — I will write this in the ledger myself, entry whatever-number-we're-on, and Odelia can witness it — as he stepped inside, he reached out without looking and straightened the crooked sign on its hook, in the dark, in one motion, the way a man does who has done it ten thousand times.

His hands remembered.

Everything else, we would give him, one page at a time.

Behind us, far off in the evening sky above the rooftops of Vessengard, something vast passed between the clouds and the stars — a wingbeat, slow and deliberate, like the world clearing its throat — and Wren turned toward the window with her ember-bright eyes wide, and smiled.
