## 21. First Flight

The bell above the shop door had a crack in it now, so it rang in two notes instead of one — a bright note and a broken one, like a bird that had learned to laugh. Wren had decided she liked the cracked version better. Perfect bells were for cities that pretended nothing had ever gone wrong. Vessengard was done pretending.

She sat on the counter with her heels drumming against the drawer where Petra kept his twine, watching him work. He was making a charm — a real one, a small true magic, the kind that would have gotten him burned at a stake three years ago and now only got him a modest tax stamp from the Office of Permitted Wonders. It was a rain-charm, shaped like a teardrop of blue glass, and when he breathed on it, clouds bloomed inside the glass like flowers opening underwater.

"You're staring," he said without looking up.

"I'm supervising."

"Supervising."

"It's a very important job." She swung her feet. "Somebody has to make sure you don't breathe on it too long. Last time you did that, Mrs. Halloway's rain-charm made it drizzle *inside* her hat for a week."

"That was an experimental model."

"That was a *hat flood*."

Petra smiled — the small crooked smile he still wore most days, the one that looked like a man trying on a coat he'd found in a closet and wasn't sure belonged to him. He didn't remember her name being the first word he'd ever taught anyone. He didn't remember teaching her anything. His hands remembered charm-work the way rivers remembered downhill, but everything before the Gate was ash, and Wren carried all of it for him: the tower, the trial, the choice, the name.

*Petra*, she thought, tasting it silently, the way you might hold a sweet under your tongue without biting. *His name is Petra.* She had kept it safe for three years, folded up in the place behind her ribs where the ember used to live — because the ember lived there no longer. It lived everywhere now. In the sky, in the river, in the slow green push of weeds through the cracks where the Wardens' black banners had been pried off the walls. The Oath was whole, and its light was in everything, and Wren was just a girl again. Mostly. A girl who dreamed in the First Tongue and could hear what the wind was gossiping about, which was usually other winds.

The bell rang — both notes — and Mrs. Halloway bustled in out of the autumn morning, shaking rain off a shawl that was, thanks to Petra's improved craftsmanship, dry on the inside.

"There's my favorite disaster-monger," she said. "Is it ready?"

"Ready," Petra said, and held up the teardrop.

Mrs. Halloway paid in coppers counted out twice, because she trusted no one's arithmetic but her own, and on her way out she pinched Wren's cheek and said, "You've got your father's look this morning. That stubborn look. Sky-look."

Wren went warm all over. *Father* was still a new word in the city's mouth, and every time someone said it, it landed in her chest like a coal that didn't burn.

Because that was the other thing. Three months ago, a clerk at the Hall of Records had looked at the two of them standing in line — the tall storm-caller with no yesterday and the small girl who spoke to dragons — and asked, wearily, "Related?" And Petra had opened his mouth to say *ward*, and Wren had said "Yes," so fast and so fiercely that the clerk wrote it down and that was that. Paper made it true. Paper was good at that.

He hadn't corrected her. He'd just taken her hand on the walk home, and squeezed it twice, which in their private language meant *I don't remember choosing you, but I choose you now, every day, on purpose.*

Well. It meant several things. But mostly that.

* * *

The sky had been strange all week. Wren felt it first, the way she felt everything first now — a tightening behind her sternum, a hum in her teeth. The winds were talking about something big coming down from the north, something with wings, and they were giddy about it, the way winds get, tumbling over each other: *it's coming, it's coming, the first one, the first one in a hundred years.*

She told Tamsin, who was reorganizing the shop's back room into something between a storeroom and a training hall, stacking crates of bottled weather with the grim efficiency of a woman who had once enforced the law and now broke it professionally, on behalf of it, which she insisted was different.

"A dragon," Tamsin said flatly.

"The first dragon."

"In a century."

"That's what the wind said."

Tamsin set down a crate. "The wind also said last Tuesday that Odelia's cat is 'a spy for the river,' so forgive me if I don't reorganize my whole week around meteorological rumor."

"Sable says the cat *is* a spy for the river."

"Sable Yew talks to fish and holds grudges against a cat. These are not reliable sources." But Tamsin's mouth twitched, and Wren saw her glance up at the window, quick and involuntary, the way everyone in Vessengard had been glancing at the sky lately. Hoping. Everyone was hoping. You couldn't stop them. The city had spent a hundred years looking down — at ledgers, at boots, at the ground in front of it, afraid of what the sky might do if it caught you looking up. Now it looked up constantly, hungrily, like a dog that had finally been allowed on the furniture.

Odelia arrived at noon with bread and rumors, which were, in her case, the same category of goods. She was thinner than she'd been before the Black Archive, and her hands shook sometimes when the ovens' heat reminded her of fire, but her mind was as vast and untidy as ever, and she came in talking mid-sentence, as always, as though conversation were a book she'd left open on the table.

"—so the Guild of Lanternwrights has petitioned to have their craft reclassified as 'permitted wonder' rather than 'trade,' purely for the tax benefits, which Corvess's old council finds scandalous, and honestly it *is* scandalous, lanterns have never once been magic, but—"

She stopped in the doorway. She was looking past Wren, through the window, up.

"Wren," she said quietly. "Come here."

Wren came. And there it was.

It came out of the north like a drawn breath — a shape against the low grey clouds, long and sinuous, wings wide as cathedral doors, scales catching the thin sunlight and throwing it back in colors that didn't have names yet, colors the world had forgotten how to see. It didn't fly so much as *swim*, banking over the rooftops of Vessengard with a grace that made the whole city below go silent.

And then the city didn't go silent. The city *erupted*. Doors banged open. People poured into the streets, into the square, onto balconies, shouting, laughing, some of them crying, some of them dropping to their knees, and somewhere a fiddler started playing, badly, immediately, because apparently even joy needed a soundtrack.

Wren stood in the doorway of the charm-shop with Odelia's hand on her shoulder and felt the dragon pass overhead like a bell struck inside her bones. It turned its head — she was almost sure it turned its head — and one eye, molten gold, ancient and amused, swept across the little shop with the cracked bell.

And in the First Tongue, in the voice that was not sound but meaning, it said:

*Little ember. Still keeping watch?*

*I'm not the ember anymore,* Wren answered, grinning so hard her face hurt. *I'm just me.*

*That,* said the dragon, wheeling away toward the river, *was always the point.*

Then it was gone south along the water, trailing awe behind it like a wake, and Vessengard stood in the streets and cheered at the empty sky for a long time afterward, because cheering at an empty sky felt right, somehow. Like thanking a guest who'd already left.

* * *

Petra hadn't seen it.

He'd been in the back room when the shadow crossed, arguing with Tamsin about whether bottled fog should be shelved alphabetically or by mood, and by the time he came out, blinking, asking why half the neighborhood was in the street, the sky was only sky.

Wren watched his face as the neighbors told him — breathless, overlapping, gesturing upward — and saw something flicker there that wasn't quite grief and wasn't quite longing. He'd fed the Gate everything, including whatever memory he might have had of dragons, of Ashfall, of the hundred years of silence between realms. He knew dragons the way you know a story you've been told. He didn't know them the way you know a thing you've witnessed.

"You missed it," Wren said that evening, when the shop was closed and the lamp was lit and they were eating bread and honey at the counter because the table was still buried under Tamsin's crates.

"So I hear." He tore his bread, gave her the bigger half without thinking about it, the way he always did. "A hundred years, and I was arguing about fog."

"You'll see another."

"Will I?"

"Yes." She said it with the total certainty of a nine-year-old, which is the most powerful kind. "They're coming back. All of them. Slowly. The Gate is open; they're just careful. Dragons are careful. They wait and watch for a long time before—" She stopped.

Before what? Before they trust. Before they land. Before they let themselves be known.

Petra looked at her across the lamplight, and his eyes were the color they always were — storm-grey, ordinary, kind — and she thought, not for the first time, how strange it was that the whole world had changed and this face hadn't. This face was hers anyway. Memory was a funny thing. Everyone thought it lived in your head, but it didn't, really. It lived in the people around you, in the way they handed it back to you piece by piece, day by day. *You take sugar in your tea. You hum when you work. You squeeze twice. Your name is Petra.*

"Tamsin says you used to be insufferable," Wren said.

"Tamsin says many things."

"She says you used to assign reading as punishment and then quiz people while eating apples, loudly, on purpose."

"That sounds like a man I'd like to meet."

"She says he'd like you too. Eventually. After complaining."

Petra laughed — really laughed, the unguarded kind, rare as dragons — and Wren decided it was time. She'd been waiting for the right evening, and this was it: lamp lit, bread shared, laughter still hanging in the air like the smell of honey. Some things you had to give back gently or not at all.

"Close your eyes," she said.

"Why?"

"Because it works better if you close your eyes. Trust me. I'm your daughter. Legally."

He closed his eyes. He was good at trusting her; it was one of the things he'd kept, somehow, past the edge of everything he'd lost — trust arrived in his body before memory did, like spring getting to the roots before the branches know.

Wren reached across the counter and took both his hands. Behind her ribs, in the place where the ember used to be, there was a warmth now instead of fire — the Oath's afterglow, the part of it that had chosen to stay with her, the way a hearth stays warm after the logs are ash. She cupped that warmth around his name the way you'd cup a moth, careful not to crush it.

"I kept something for you," she said. "From the Gate. Before you gave everything. You put your name in my keeping — you probably don't remember doing it, because you don't remember anything, but you did. You said, *hold this where I can't reach it.* So I did. For three years. And it's time."

She spoke it. Not aloud — aloud would have been too small. She spoke it in the First Tongue, the way names are truly spoken, the way the world had named him at the beginning: *storm-caller, oath-keeper, the man who chose the future over himself.* She poured it through their joined hands, into his skin, into whatever deep place in him still received things, and she watched his face.

His brow furrowed. His breath caught. For a moment he was utterly still, the way the air goes still before weather.

Then he opened his eyes, and they were wet, and he was smiling, and he said — hoarsely, wonderingly, like a man reading his own handwriting on a letter he didn't remember writing:

"*Petra.* My name is Petra."

"Yes."

"Petra Hartwell." He tried it again, rolling it around. "Storm-caller. Charm-seller. Father." He stopped on that last word and looked at her, and the look was the whole speech he didn't need to give.

"Father," Wren agreed. "Legally."

He laughed and wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand, pretending it was the lamp smoke, and she let him pretend, because that was kindness too.

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, when the shop was dark and the city outside was still murmuring about wings — Wren woke to a sound she knew in her bones.

Not the bell. Not the wind.

A knock.

Soft. Deliberate. Twice, then a pause, then once more — a pattern, almost polite, almost formal, like a question being asked in a language made entirely of patience.

She lay still in her cot above the shop and listened. Below, floorboards creaked: Petra, awake too, moving to the door. The latch lifted.

Through the floor she heard a voice she didn't know — low, resonant, with a strange doubled quality, as though two throats spoke in perfect unison. It said words in the First Tongue, old and formal, words Wren recognized from her dreams of Ashfall, words that meant roughly:

*I bring greetings from the far shore of the fire, and a request that cannot be refused politely.*

And then Petra's voice, steady, curious, unafraid:

"Come in. It's cold out. We'll want tea."

Wren grinned in the dark, pulled her blanket up to her chin, and began, silently, to plan breakfast for a dragon.
