## 22. The Oath Revisited

The sign above the shop read HARTWELL'S CHARMS in the same flaking gold letters as before, because Tamsin had refused to repaint it. "You spent five years building a reputation under that name," she'd said, arms crossed on the ladder she wouldn't let him climb. "You don't get to throw away the one thing you kept."

He had no memory of building the reputation. He had no memory of the name, either, except that Wren said it like it was something warm held in both hands, and that was enough.

The rain came down soft on Vessengard that morning—not the hard, angry rain of his first years in the city, when the sky had seemed to know what he was and knock at his windows demanding to be let in. This rain fell the way rain was supposed to fall: without opinion. He stood in the doorway with his tea and watched it silver the cobbles, and somewhere behind his ribs, where a storm used to live, there was only a quiet hum, like a kettle deciding whether to sing.

"You're blocking the light," Wren said, ducking under his arm with a crate of new stock.

"I'm admiring the weather."

"You're hiding from inventory." She set the crate on the counter with a thump that rattled the luck-bells. Twelve years old now, or nearly—she'd stopped counting her birthdays somewhere around the end of the world, so they'd picked one together, and Odelia baked a cake every year whether they needed it or not. She had her mother's braid, someone else's freckles, and eyes that still caught firelight strangely, though the ember in her chest had settled into something steadier these days. A hearth instead of a bonfire. Most of the time.

"What's in the crate?" he asked.

"Small true magic." She said it the way the city had started saying it, half joke, half catechism. Since the Gate reopened and the first dragon had crossed the sky over Vessengard like a rumor made flesh, the Wardens' ban hadn't been repealed so much as gently retired, the way you retire an old dog that has bitten too many people. Magic was legal again—but only small magic, only honest magic, only magic sold with its price written plainly on the label. Corvess's legion had seen to it that everyone remembered what large magic cost.

Petra opened the crate. Inside, nested in straw, were the charms he'd spent the winter learning to make all over again—because his hands knew charm-work even when his head didn't, but his hands had only ever known the harmless kind, and true magic required a different grammar entirely.

A knot of river-clay from Sable Yew that would keep a well sweet for a season. A feather that had never belonged to any bird, which hummed faintly when a lie was told near it. A glass bead with a storm shut inside it, no bigger than a currant—one thunderclap per customer, guaranteed, for weddings and grudges alike.

"That one's yours," Wren said, tapping the bead. "Tamsin says if you can bottle a storm that small, you've finally learned restraint."

"Tamsin," Petra said, "has never once in my known life let me forget my shortcomings."

"She loves you."

"I know." And he did know—that was the strange grace of it. Memory lived in other people. He had lost his own yesterday, all of them, every year of himself up to the moment the ember took them at the Gate, and what remained was this: four people who carried him between them like a flame passed hand to hand, each holding a piece so none of it would gutter out.

Odelia kept his history—the trial records, the archive pages she'd un-scorched by patient scholarship and stubbornness, the story of a man who stole an ember to save a child not yet born. Ilva kept his habits—how he took his tea, which stair creaked, the way he whistled off-key when he thought no one listened. Tamsin kept his craft, correcting his knots and his sigils with the fierce tenderness of a woman who had waited five years to be allowed to scold him again.

And Wren kept his name.

* * *

The bell over the door rang at midmorning, and the day began.

It was, by the standards of the old days, an ordinary day, which was to say it was extraordinary in ways Petra had learned to notice. A fishwife bought a clay knot for her well and paid with a smile and two smoked eels. A young couple, nervous as cats, asked about the truth-feather for their wedding vows—"not because we'll lie," the bride insisted, "but because we want everyone to *know* we won't"—and Petra wrapped it in blue paper and charged them nothing, because some things shouldn't be sold, only given.

A boy of maybe nine came in alone, counting coppers, and asked very seriously whether Petra could make something that would help his grandmother sleep without dreaming of the burning years. Petra sat him down and made it while he watched—a simple thing, lavender and ash and a thread pulled from the hem of a Warden's cloak, which Ilva had donated for exactly such purposes—and the boy left holding it like a bird's egg.

"You could charge more," Wren said afterward, sorting coins.

"I could."

"You won't, though."

"No."

She sighed the sigh of a twelve-year-old business partner, and then grinned, because she didn't actually mind. The shop wasn't really a shop. It was what Odelia called a hinge—a place where the two worlds leaned against each other and got used to the weight. People came in afraid of magic and left carrying a little of it, warm in their pockets, and found it hadn't bitten them. That was the whole covenant, really, remade in miniature, one charm at a time. The great Oath bound realms. The small ones bound neighbors.

At noon, Tamsin arrived with bread and grievances.

"The Guild of Charmwrights wants to certify you," she announced, dropping a loaf on the counter like a gauntlet. "Officially. There's paperwork."

"There's always paperwork."

"They want to inspect the premises. There's a man coming Thursday with a clipboard and opinions."

"Tell him the opinions are welcome and the clipboard stays outside. Some things shouldn't be catalogued." He paused. "Odelia's rubric?"

"Odelia's rubric," Tamsin confirmed, and for a moment they shared the particular look of two people who loved the same impossible archivist. Odelia Vance had spent the winter drafting the certification standards herself—thirty-one pages on ethical enchantment, with footnotes—and the Guild, which had no idea how to say no to her, had adopted them wholesale. Somewhere in those footnotes was a line about the moral obligations of oath-keepers, and somewhere in the margins of that line, in pencil, was a note only four people in the world would understand: *he chose right. Make sure the next ones do too.*

The afternoon brought a dragon.

Not overhead—overhead dragons were still rare enough to stop traffic, and one had passed that spring, sending half the city into the streets and the other half into hysterics. No; this one came through the door, in the manner of dragons lately: small, polite, and wearing a person.

She was perhaps sixty, gray-haired, with a traveling cloak still dusty from the mountain passes, and she stood in the middle of the shop turning slowly, taking everything in, the way people did when they'd crossed a long way for something and wanted to be sure.

"You're Hartwell," she said.

"I'm told I am."

"My grandmother was a keeper. One of the hidden ones—there were families, after the sealing, who passed the old words down like recipes, generation to generation, waiting." Her eyes were bright and wet. "She died before the Gate opened. She died believing it never would. I came to tell someone it did open. It seemed wrong that she should have been right and no one should ever know."

Petra came around the counter. He didn't remember his own mother's face, but he knew what to do with grief that had traveled far; some knowledge lived below memory, in the hands, in the chest.

"Would you like tea?" he said. "And would you tell me her words? We're collecting them. Every version of the Oath anyone kept alive—we're writing them down, all of them, before they scatter."

The woman blinked. "Writing them down where?"

"In a book." Wren appeared at his elbow, chin lifted, suddenly every inch the daughter of a covenant. "A real one. Odelia's making it. It's going to be very long and have footnotes."

The woman laughed, and cried a little, and drank her tea, and gave them her grandmother's words—which turned out to differ from Sable Yew's version by exactly one phrase, and Odelia, when she heard of it that evening, made a sound of scholarly joy that sent the cat under the sofa.

That was the shape of the days now. Not dramatic. Not safe, exactly—nothing with dragons in it was ever quite safe—but *earned*, which was better than safe. The city was learning magic the way you learn a language you'd been punished for speaking: slowly, warily, with long pauses and sudden fluencies. And the shop sat at the center of it like a heart, beating out its small commerce of charms and tea and second chances.

Petra still woke some mornings reaching for a past that wasn't there. He'd surface from dreams of a tower, a friend's voice, a choice he couldn't see the edges of, and lie in the dark feeling the shape of an absence, the way an amputee feels weather in a missing limb. On those mornings he'd go down early and stand in the shop among the stock, and one of them would find him eventually—Ilva with tea, Tamsin with a task, Wren with nothing at all, just her presence, leaning against him like a small warm weight—and the absence would become bearable. Not filled. Bearable. You didn't replace a self. You built around it, the way a tree builds around a wound, until the wound was part of the shape.

"I don't regret it," he told Wren once, in the second winter, surprising himself. "I can't remember choosing, but I know—I know the way you know your own hands—that I'd choose it again."

"I know," she said. "I was there."

* * *

Closing time came the way it always did: Wren counting the till with her tongue between her teeth, Petra sweeping, the lamps being lit one by one against the early dark. Outside, the rain had stopped and the streets shone, and Vessengard smelled of woodsmoke and wet stone and, faintly, impossibly, of sulfur and high cold air—the smell of Ashfall riding in on the wind from a realm that was, once more, next door.

Wren blew out the last lamp but one. Petra hung the CLOSED sign, then turned it back over, then hung it again, because he could never quite commit.

"You do that every night," Wren observed.

"And every night it works out."

The knock came just as she reached for the bolt.

Three knocks. Unhurried. The kind of knock that knows it will be answered.

They looked at each other. In the years since the Gate, strangers had come to the door at odd hours—keepers with inherited words, travelers with questions, once an actual Warden delegation with a form in triplicate that Tamsin had made them fill out twice. But this knock had a quality to it, a resonance, like a finger run around the rim of a glass.

Petra opened the door.

The stranger on the step was tall and lean, wrapped in a traveler's coat the color of cooled lava, and for a moment Petra thought: *another keeper come down from the mountains.* Then the stranger looked up, and Petra saw the eyes.

Ember-bright. Not catching the lamplight—*emitting* it, softly, the way coals breathe light in the dark hours before dawn. Eyes like the last thing you'd see at the bottom of a forge. Eyes like Wren's, on the night the shard had blazed, but older, wearier, lit from a fire that had burned a very long time indeed.

"Master Hartwell," the stranger said. The voice was low and resonant, with a crackle underneath it, like words spoken over a hearth. "Forgive the hour. Forgive the distance. I've come a very long way—from farther than the mountains, farther than the maps—and they told me at the river, and at the archive, and at the fen, that if I needed a door in this city, it would be this one."

"Who told you?" Wren asked, though Petra suspected she already knew. She had gone very still beside him, the way she went still when the First Tongue moved in the air.

"Sable Yew. Odelia Vance. A sergeant of Wardens with a scarred shoulder and a poor opinion of my kind." The stranger smiled, and the smile was tired and hopeful and ancient. "And a child at a gate, a hundred years ago, who fed a dying dragon her warmth and told me—if I ever woke, if the fire ever reached me across the dark—to follow the sound of a bell over a shop door."

The wind moved in the street. Somewhere far off, high above the clouds, something vast circled once and wheeled away, and the lamplight in the shop flickered in a rhythm that was almost, almost, a heartbeat.

"We're closed," Petra said. His voice was steady. His hands, hanging at his sides, had already begun the old motions, the ones that lived below memory—the small preparations, the warming of the air, the readying. "But for family of the Gate, we reopen."

The stranger's ember eyes brightened. "Then I have news," they said, stepping across the threshold into the light, "and a request, and a warning—and I think, Master Hartwell, that you will want to sit down for at least two of the three."
