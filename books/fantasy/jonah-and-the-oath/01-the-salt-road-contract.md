## 1. The Salt Road Contract

The mule knew the way before Ines did.

That was the first wrong thing about the night, though it took them three switchbacks to admit it. The mule — a gray, sour-tempered creature named Debt, because Ines had a sense of humor that ran to the bitter — picked her path up the Thornfell trail with a confidence that bordered on insolence, hooves finding stone in the dark as if the mountain had whispered its shape to her. Ines rode with the reins slack and let her, and told themselves the tightness under their ribs was only the cold.

Salt made the cargo. Forty pounds of it, wrapped in oilcloth and packed in false-bottomed panniers above a layer of dried figs, because salt was cheap at the coast and dear past the wards, and because nobody searched a salt-runner twice. Salt didn't feed anyone. It just made food keep. It was the most honest contraband in the world: it asked nothing of you except that you carry it, and it kept everything else from rotting.

Ines liked that about it. They thought about it too much, maybe. There was a lot of road behind them and not much else to think with.

The Thornfells rose ahead like a jaw of black teeth against the last bruise-colored light, and somewhere inside that jaw was the pass Ines had been running for six years, and somewhere deeper than the pass was the shrine where they would pay what they owed.

The tithe. Always the tithe.

They touched the brand without meaning to — two fingers to the inside of the left wrist, where Veth's mark sat under the skin like a coin pressed into wax. It didn't hurt. It never hurt. That was the thing people who'd never sworn an oath couldn't understand: the silence of the silent god wasn't absence. It was attention. You could feel Veth listening through the brand the way you could feel a hand resting on the back of your neck — no weight, exactly, but you knew it was there, and you straightened your spine because of it.

*One more run,* Ines told the god silently, the way they did every night, a habit worn smooth as a river stone. *One more run and the tithe's paid full. Then we're square.*

Veth said nothing. Veth always said nothing. But the brand warmed, faintly, the way it did when Ines spoke to it, and Ines chose — as they chose every time — to read that warmth as forgiveness rather than appetite.

* * *

The toll-house at Graywater Stair was a squat stone box crouched at the mouth of the pass, one window lit amber against the gathering dark, smoke rising thin and crooked from its chimney. Ines had stopped there perhaps forty times over six years and could not have said why the sight of it always loosened something in their chest. Familiarity, they supposed. A smuggler's life was mostly strangers; a lit window was a small mercy.

The toll-keeper was old Marta Kess, wrapped in a shawl the color of wet slate, and she was standing outside the door when Ines reined up, which was itself unusual. Marta did not stand outside after sundown. Marta had opinions about the dark that she expressed by refusing to be in it.

"You're late," Marta said.

"Roads were soft." Ines swung down and offered the toll — three coppers and a lie, per the custom of the trade, the lie being whatever story explained the panniers. "Figs for my aunt in Highvale. She has a sweet tooth and no teeth."

Marta didn't take the coppers right away. She stood looking up at the mountain instead, her face turned toward the peaks where the ward-stones were set into the rock every half-mile like vertebrae, each one carved with Veth's sign — the closed eye, the open hand. In the dying light they were just lumps of granite. By rights they should have been glowing faintly blue, the way they had glowed every night of Ines's six years on this road, a thread of cold light stitching the whole range together.

Ines looked, and the stones were dark.

"Marta," they said carefully. "Your lights are out."

"I know what's out." Marta took the coppers at last, closing her fist around them hard enough that her knuckles went white. "Been flickering since moonrise. On, off, on, off. Like a lamp with a bad wick. Forty years I've kept this stair, Almeida, and those wards have burned steady as scripture the whole time. Steady through the quake year. Steady through the fever winter when half my village died and the other half prayed themselves hoarse." Her voice dropped. "Tonight they're stuttering."

"Wards fail. Everything fails."

"Not these. These were cut into the living rock by the Wardens before your grandmother drew breath, and they don't flicker, they *endure*, that's the whole point of them, that's the word cut into the foundation stone — endure — and tonight they're—" She stopped herself. Ines watched her decide to be practical instead of afraid, the way people do when fear is a luxury their work can't afford. "You're going up regardless."

"I'm going up regardless."

"Then hear me." Marta stepped close enough that Ines could smell woodsmoke and the medicinal bitterness of willow bark tea. "There's a fog coming down the high passes. Not weather-fog. It doesn't move with the wind. My husband was a Warden's man before the order thinned out, and he used to say the mountain breathed, and when it breathed wrong you got off the mountain. It's breathing wrong tonight, Almeida. I can feel it in my knees."

Ines glanced at the sky. Stars in the east, sharp and many. No fog that they could see, but the air did have a stillness to it, a held-breath quality, like the pause between lightning and thunder stretched out over hours.

"I'll keep to the low trail," they said. "I'll be through the pass by midnight."

"You'll do what the road tells you," Marta said, which was oddly put, and then she did something odder: she caught Ines's wrist, the branded one, and turned it over in her rough grip, looking at the mark beneath the skin. Her thumb hovered over it without touching, the way people hover a hand over a stove they've been burned by.

"When did you last pay?" she asked.

"Spring tithe. Why?"

"And it took? It — settled?"

"It settled." Though now that Ines reached for the memory of it, the memory was strangely smooth, featureless, like a stone worn blank. They remembered kneeling at the shrine. They remembered the warmth of the brand. They did not remember anything said or seen, which was normal, which had always been normal, which they had never once thought strange until this exact moment, holding a stranger's gaze while a stranger held their wrist.

Marta let go. "Pay your tithe and get off the mountain," she said. "Don't linger at the shrines. Don't answer if anything calls your name. And Almeida—" she hesitated, and Ines saw that under the practicality the fear was still there, banked like coals, "—if the stars go out, don't stop walking. Whatever you see, however dark it gets. Don't stop walking."

"That's grim even for you, Marta."

"I'm old. Grim is free." She shuffled back toward her door, then paused on the threshold, silhouetted against the lamplight. "Your mule knows the trail better than you do tonight. I watched you come up. She wasn't following you. She was leading."

Ines had no answer for that, so they gave none, and led Debt on through the mouth of the pass while the toll-keeper's lamp shrank behind them to a spark, then a memory.

* * *

The low trail wound along the shoulder of the mountain where the pines grew sparse and wind-bent, all of them leaning east like listeners. Ines walked with the reins looped over one arm and let their body do the work their mind refused to examine.

Because here was the second wrong thing, the one they'd been circling since Graywater Stair: Ines knew this road. Not the way a smuggler knows a route — memorized landmarks, counted paces, the professional cartography of repetition. Six years of runs should have given them that kind of knowledge, and some of it was that. But underneath the earned knowledge was something older and stranger, knowledge that lived in the body instead of the head. Their feet found the flat stones in the streambed without looking. Their hands rose to push aside branches before the branches arrived. Twice they took forks in the trail without deliberation, choosing left where the map said right, and both times the left fork proved passable where the right had washed out — washouts they hadn't known about, couldn't have known about, and yet some part of them had.

It was like reading a letter written in their own handwriting that they had no memory of writing.

Everyone who swore to Veth got a little of this, the priests said. The god of silence kept its own counsel and shared it sparingly, and oath-bound couriers sometimes developed a knack for hidden ways — a blessing, the priests called it, the god clearing the road before its servants. Ines had accepted that explanation for six years the way you accept a floor: without thinking, because thinking about it meant looking down.

Tonight, with the ward-stones dark and Marta's warning lodged under their skin like a splinter, Ines looked down.

And the brand answered.

Warmth spread from the wrist up the forearm, across the chest, a heat like standing near a hearth, and with it came — not words. Veth did not send words. But something adjacent to words, something that pressed against the inside of Ines's skull with the unmistakable texture of *meaning*, and the meaning was simple and enormous and terrible:

*Come.*

Not *go*. Every other night of Ines's life, the brand's warmth had felt like permission, like a hand on the shoulder saying *well done, walk on*. Tonight it pulled. Tonight it was a hook set behind the sternum, reeling line in, gentle as anything, patient as anything, and Ines stood in the middle of the trail with the mule's breath steaming beside them and understood that they were being summoned, and understood, worse, that they had been moving toward the summons all evening without knowing it — the forks taken without deliberation, the flat stones found blind. The road hadn't been guiding them to the far side of the mountain.

It had been guiding them home.

"No," Ines said aloud, and their voice sounded very small under the pines. "We're square, you and I. One more run and we're square. That's the bargain."

The warmth didn't argue. That was somehow the worst part. It simply continued, steady as a tide, indifferent to refusal, and Ines found their feet moving again — forward, upward, toward the high trail they'd promised Marta they'd avoid — and the horror of it was how natural it felt. How much like choice. Their legs carried them willingly, their breath came easy, and only the watcher behind their own eyes, the small cold self that had survived twenty years of smuggling by noticing things, stood apart and observed that Ines Almeida was walking toward something they had not decided to walk toward, and could not make themselves stop.

Debt followed, ears pinned, making a low continuous sound in her throat that Ines had never heard from her before and recognized anyway, from somewhere deep, as grief.

* * *

They gained the high trail as true dark fell, and that was when Ines saw the fog.

Marta had been right. It hung in the upper passes like spilled milk, motionless despite the wind that bent the pines, and it was wrong in a way Ines's eyes kept trying to correct and failing. Fog should drift. This stood. And within it — Ines squinted, dismounted, stood with the reins gone taut in their fist — within it there were lights. Faint, blue-white, arranged in a line that climbed and climbed along the ridge. The ward-stones, flaring and guttering, flaring and guttering, each pulse a little weaker than the last, a heartbeat counting down.

Ines had grown up on stories about what the Thornfell wards kept out. Everyone had. Border raiders, bandit lords, the hungry dead of the old wars — nursery versions of danger, vague and external. Standing beneath the guttering line of stones, watching their light stutter like a dying man's pulse, Ines felt the first true vertigo of the night, because the stories had never once asked the obvious question, and the obvious question arrived now with the force of a blow:

What if all that power, all those years, all that enduring — what if it had never been pointed outward at all?

The brand pulled. Ines resisted, and discovered that resisting the brand was like resisting sleep, possible in increments, doomed in aggregate. They bought themselves a moment by checking the panniers — the salt intact, the figs uncrushed, the small stupid dignities of the job — and while their hands were busy the sky happened.

It began at the edges of things. A star over the eastern ridge dimmed, steadied, dimmed again — and went out. Not occluded. Out. Then its neighbor. Then a whole constellation, unraveling star by star like a seam picked loose, and Ines stood with their head tipped back and watched the night sky being unpicked, darkness spreading across heaven in slow rags, and the silence that came down with it was not the silence of the quiet god. It was the silence of a held breath. Of a room where everyone has stopped talking because someone has just said the unsayable thing.

Debt screamed. An actual scream, equine terror, and she tore the reins from Ines's hand and bolted down-trail into the dark, panniers and salt and six years of careful work crashing away into the pines, and Ines let her go, because the sky was going out and a mule was a problem for a world that still existed.

Below them, downslope, in the black throat of a ravine where the trail had switchbacked, there came a sound that cut through everything — small, human, and utterly lost.

A child crying.

Later, Ines would try to reconstruct the next minute and fail. What remained was fragments: the slope rushing under their boots, gravel giving way, their body moving with that same uncanny certainty it had shown all night, finding holds in the dark as if the mountain were handing them up its own anatomy. The ravine floor. Rockfall — fresh, the scree still settling, a slide come down from the heights for no reason any quake could explain. And beneath an overhang of shattered stone, curled into a ball no bigger than a bundle of firewood, a child. Maybe seven years old. Barefoot. In a nightgown the color of undyed wool, filthy at the hem, with a smudge of blood at the temple that might have been theirs and might not have.

The child looked up. Their eyes caught the last guttering light of the ward-stones, and Ines, kneeling in the scree with the sky going dark above them and the brand burning like a coal at their wrist, felt those eyes look *through* them — inventory them, weigh them, know them — with an attention far too old for the face wearing it.

"Ines Almeida," the child said.

Their name. Not smuggler's alias, not the road-name everyone used. Their name, the buried one, the one they'd been born with and buried under six years of aliases, the one they themselves barely used anymore, the one that surfaced in their own thoughts less and less often, like a coin sinking in deep water.

Ines had never told anyone that name. Had possibly never told anyone, ever, in the whole of their remembered life.

The brand flared white-hot, and the pull behind their sternum became a command, and the child watched them with those old, old eyes, and somewhere far above, the last ward-stone on the ridge guttered once, twice —

— and went out.

Every star in the sky went out with it.

In the total dark, the child reached up and took Ines's branded wrist in both small hands, gently, the way you'd hold a bird, and said, in a voice like a bell struck once in an empty cathedral:

"You're late. We have to hurry. He's been waiting such a long time."

And Ines Almeida, who had spent twenty years trusting no one and nothing but their own two hands, heard themselves answer:

"Then we'd better not keep him waiting."
