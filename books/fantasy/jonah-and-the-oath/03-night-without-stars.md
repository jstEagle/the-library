## 3. Night Without Stars

The Thornfell crossing had a rhythm to it, and Ines Almeida trusted rhythm the way other people trusted prayer.

Three days up from the salt flats, two days along the spine of the pass, one day down into the valley markets where a crate of unmarked vials would fetch enough to cover half a year's tithe to Veth. The road was closed in winter, haunted in autumn, and watched in every season by ward-keepers who were paid too little to care about one more shadow on the stone. Ines knew all of this without knowing how they knew it. That was the arrangement they'd made with their own mind years ago: don't ask where the knowledge comes from, just use it.

So when their boots found the narrow shelf above the Gallowmere drop — the one that wasn't on any map Odile Ferran's people kept, the one that existed only as an ache in Ines's ankles before they ever saw it — they didn't ask. They walked it. The mountain air tasted of iron and old snow, and somewhere below, water moved through rock with a sound like slow swallowing.

*One last job,* they told themselves, the way they'd told themselves for three years running. *Then the tithe is paid, then the oath rests easy, then I stop.*

They never stopped. They suspected, in the honest hour after midnight, that they were not meant to.

The brand sat under their collarbone like a second heart. Everyone sworn to Veth carried one — a small knot of scar tissue, cool to the touch, that warmed faintly at dusk and dawn as if the god turned over in its sleep. Veth was the silent god, the god of roads and thresholds and things said plainly or not at all. Its temples had no bells. Its priests spoke only in writing. And for as long as Ines had been alive, Veth had answered no prayers at all, which the faithful called *the Silence*, and which the tithe-houses called *a reason to keep paying.*

Ines paid because the alternative was the itch. Every mercenary learned it young: swear the oath, take the brand, and the god's protection settled over you like good leather armor. Miss your tithe, and the brand began to whisper. Not words. Just a pulling, a homesickness for a place you'd never been, until you paid or went mad or both. Ines had seen a man claw his own brand out with a belt knife on the steps of the Salt Road chapel. He'd survived. He'd never stopped crying.

So: one last job. Half a year's tithe in a crate of vials wrapped in oilcloth, riding against Ines's spine, humming faintly the way contraband always hummed when you thought about it too hard.

They made camp that night in a hollow beneath an overhang of black granite, a spot their body chose before their eyes did — flat ground, defensible approach, a chimney of open sky exactly wide enough to see stars through. Ines built a small fire, ate salt pork and hard bread, and looked up through the chimney at the wheeling dark.

That was when the first wrongness arrived, quiet as frost.

The stars were gone.

Not clouded. Not dimmed. *Gone.* The chimney of sky above the camp showed a black so complete it looked less like night than like a hole cut out of the world. Ines sat very still, firelight crawling up their hands, and counted their own breathing the way Odile had once taught them to count during a panic — four in, seven hold, eight out — except Odile had never taught them anything; they'd simply known her methods on sight, the first time they met, and she'd gone pale and asked them who their teacher had been, and Ines had said *no one*, and neither of them had spoken of it again.

Four in. Seven hold. Eight out.

The stars did not come back.

Ines had crossed these mountains eleven times. They had seen storm-fronts swallow the sky whole, seen ash-fall from the far volcanoes turn noon to dusk, seen the aurora come down so low the ward-towers hummed. Never once had they seen a sky with nothing in it. The eye kept reaching upward and finding no purchase, no depth, no distance — just a ceiling pressed close against the world, close enough that Ines felt they could stand on their horse's back and touch it.

And the silence underneath the silence — the Silence of Veth, the great ambient quiet the faithful lived inside like fish live inside water — that silence *shifted*. Ines felt it move the way you feel a house settle at night. Something vast had changed position. Something vast had noticed.

The brand woke.

It didn't warm. It *ignited* — a white-hot coin of pain under the collarbone, and Ines doubled over the fire with a sound they'd be ashamed of later, both hands pressed to their chest, feeling the scar tissue pulse like a fist knocking from the inside. Heat spread outward across their ribs, down their arms, and with the heat came something worse than pain: *direction.* A compass needle behind the sternum swinging hard and holding, pointing north-northeast, up-slope, toward the high shoulder of Thornfell where the old ward-keeps stood and where no smuggler in their right mind had business going.

"No," Ines said aloud, to the fire, to the empty sky. "No, we're not doing this."

They got up anyway.

That was the horror of it, the part they would try and fail to explain afterward: there was no possession in it, no blacking-out, no hand on the back of the neck. Their legs simply decided, and the rest of them came along, arguing the whole way. They kicked dirt over the fire, shouldered the pack with the vials, took up their staff, and started climbing into the starless dark because their body belonged to something that wanted them higher on the mountain, and the body, apparently, held voting rights.

*This is what the tithe buys,* some cold rational corner of Ines observed, walking. *You thought you were renting protection. You were building a leash.*

The trail their feet found was not a trail. It was a goat-path, then not even that — a seam in the rock that opened as they approached and closed behind them, as though the mountain were a mouth remembering an old route. Pine gave way to bare stone. Stone gave way to scree. The air thinned and sharpened, and every breath carried that taste of iron again, stronger now, blood-and-forge, the smell of the deep places.

Somewhere above, glass broke.

There was no other way to describe it. A sound rolled down the mountainside — high, crystalline, enormous, the sound a cathedral window would make falling from heaven — and with it came a pressure change, a popping in the ears, and the hair on Ines's arms lifted all at once. Far below and behind, faint as memory, they saw it happen: the ward-lines. Every smuggler knew them, the pale threads of light stitched along the passes, the ward-keepers' lattice that had guarded Thornfell since before the border wars. For one heartbeat the whole lattice flared — every thread blazing white, a map of light drawn over the whole dark mountain —

— and then shattered, thread by thread by thread, top to bottom, like ice giving way under too much weight, the light raining down in silent motes and going out.

The wards were gone. All of them. At once.

Ines stood on the scree slope with their breath smoking and understood, in the wordless way of the body rather than the mind, that a door had just opened somewhere. Something had been shut in. Now it wasn't. Or something had been shut *out*, and the way in lay open, and the needle behind Ines's sternum pulled harder, dragging them up toward the broken threads like a fish on a line.

"Veth," they said, and their voice cracked on the name. "If this is you — if you're finally answering after all these years — *not like this.*"

The Silence, of course, did not answer. It never had.

* * *

The child screamed twenty minutes later, and the scream saved Ines's life, though neither of them would ever know it.

It came from the left, downslope, thin and human and utterly impossible — nobody traveled this face of Thornfell, not ward-keepers, not madmen, not smugglers with death warrants. But a scream is a scream. Ines turned off the compulsion's line as much as they could (which was not much; their path bent back toward it within ten strides, like a river finding its bed) and followed the sound down through a field of fallen boulders, staff out, free hand on the knife at their hip.

The rockfall had come down recently. That was plain even in the dark — a fresh scar on the mountainside above, dust still hanging in the air, and a fan of broken stone spilling across the path below. And pinned in the middle of it, wedged between two slabs the size of oxen, was a child.

A girl, maybe eight years old, maybe younger — it was hard to tell in the dark, hard to tell anything, because the girl was *glowing*. Faintly, fitfully, like a coal breathing. Her skin threw off a pale silver light in slow pulses, and each pulse lit the hollow around her: the crushed stone, the dust, her own face — calm. That was the thing Ines would remember longest. The girl had been screaming, yes, but now that Ines was close she had stopped, and her face in her own strange light was perfectly, terribly calm, the face of someone watching weather.

"Hey." Ines dropped to a crouch at the edge of the rubble, keeping their voice low and level, the voice they used for spooked horses and green recruits. "Hey. I see you. Don't move, all right? There's a man here to get you out."

"You shouldn't have come," the girl said. Her voice was odd. Not childish — *layered*, like two speakers sharing one throat, a child's pitch with something older underneath it, something that resonated in the stone. "The mountain is calling you and you came anyway. You always come."

"I got lost," Ines lied.

"No." The glow pulsed brighter, and the girl tilted her head, and her eyes caught the light strangely, silver on silver. "You didn't. You never do. That's the whole trouble with you, Ines Almeida."

Every hair on Ines's body stood up. Nobody out here knew their name. They hadn't given it at the toll-house; they'd given the false one, the trade one, *Merrin of Lowmark*. The girl was alone on a dead mountainside under a starless sky, pinned under a ton of rock, glowing like a lantern, and she knew Ines's true name — the name from before, the name they barely used, the name that felt in their own mouth like a coat that almost fit.

"How do you know me?"

"I knew you before you knew yourself." The layered voice said it gently, the way you'd tell someone sad news. "I'm sorry. I forget, sometimes, what forgetting feels like. Hold on. I'll show you where to dig."

"Show me—" Ines looked at the rockfall, a chaos of slabs and shards, no pattern any eye could read. "Show me how?"

"Put your hand on the big stone. The one shaped like a sleeping dog. Your hands remember things your head doesn't. Mine too." She smiled then, and the smile was a child's smile entirely, gap-toothed and crooked, and it undid something in Ines's chest. "We're the same, you and I. We're both carrying things we can't carry."

Ines should have run. Every instinct that had kept them alive through eleven crossings and two wars said *run*: the sky was wrong, the wards were broken, the brand was burning, and a glowing child pinned under rocks was speaking in a voice that echoed. Run down, run south, run to the lowlands and the loud gods and let the mountain keep its secrets.

Their hands found the sleeping-dog stone instead.

And the hands *did* remember. That was the vertigo of it — Ines's palms settled against the granite and knowledge poured up through the contact like cold water through cloth: grain of the stone, fault-line running diagonal, weight distributed there and there, the single point of failure no engineer would find but a body that had moved mountains before would know in its marrow. *Before.* The word arrived uninvited. Ines shoved it away and pulled.

The slab came free with a groan of grinding grit, impossibly, easily, the whole mass pivoting on a fulcrum Ines's shoulders seemed to already know. Dust billowed. The girl rolled clear, unhurt — of course unhurt, Ines realized, the rockfall had never touched her, the stones around her lay in a perfect nest, a cradle — and stood up in her own silver light, brushing dust from a dress that was too fine for the mountains, dyed a deep blue that Ines's eyes slid off of, refusing to hold the color.

"There," the girl said. "Now you've done it."

"Done what?" Ines's arms were shaking. The brand under their collarbone had gone from burning to *singing*, a high thin note only the bone could hear, and the compass-needle pull was no longer a pull but a current, the whole mountain leaning on them, urging them up-slope. "Done what, exactly?"

The girl looked past Ines, up the broken face of Thornfell, toward the place where the wards had died. When she spoke again, the child's voice had almost entirely drowned beneath the older one, and the older one was grief-stricken and gentle and vast, and it said:

"You've started walking home."

She took Ines's hand. Her fingers were hot — fever-hot, furnace-hot, though her skin showed no sweat — and the moment they touched, the compulsion in Ines's chest eased by a fraction, as though the mountain deferred to her.

"My name is Wren," the girl said, in her own voice again, small and tired. "I fell. I think I fell a long way. Will you carry me? My legs are tired, and we have to hurry, because the new moon is coming, and when it comes, everything that's hidden gets sealed up for a hundred years."

Above them, in the chimney of black sky, where the stars should have been wheeling — nothing. Only the ceiling-dark, pressing close.

But as Ines lifted the child onto their hip and turned, as their boots found the up-slope path their body had been begging for all night, a single point of light kindled far to the north, above the high shoulder of Thornfell. One star, hanging low, red as an ember, red as a brand.

It was not rising. It was *waiting.*

Wren saw it too, and hid her face against Ines's neck, and whispered, in the old layered voice, with something that might have been hope or might have been fear:

"She knows we're coming."
