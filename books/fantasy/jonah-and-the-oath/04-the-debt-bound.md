## 4. The Debt-Bound

The summons came the way debts always came to Cassian Vole: quietly, and at the worst possible hour.

He was three fingers into a bottle of Thornfell rye in the back room of the Drowned Bell, counting coin that wasn't his yet, when the candle guttered. Not from wind — the room was sealed tighter than a coffin — but from cold. A cold that had no business being in a low-ceilinged smuggler's den above a tavern that smelled permanently of spilled beer and old fish. The flame bent sideways, stretched thin as wire, and went out.

In the dark, something knocked on the inside of the door.

Cassian's hand found his knife before his eyes found anything else. He'd survived eleven years running the salt roads by trusting two things: his blade and his instinct for when a room had gone wrong. Both were screaming now, and both were useless, because when he finally pried the door open with the flat of the knife — not touching it, never touching it — there was nothing on the other side but a corridor, a lit lamp that hadn't been burning a moment ago, and an envelope of black vellum lying square in the center of the floorboards, addressed in silver ink:

*C. VOLE.*

No courier. No footprints in the dust. The lamp itself was wrong, too — it burned without flickering, steady as a painted flame, and its light fell in a color Cassian had no name for. Not gold. Not white. Something older than either, like the light you saw behind your eyelids when you pressed them hard enough.

He should have burned it unread. Every instinct honed on the salt roads told him so. Letters that arrived without couriers were letters from people who didn't want to be followed home, and people who didn't want to be followed home were people you did not want to owe.

But the letter bore the sigil of the Undercourt — a black key crossed with a white thorn — and Cassian Vole had spent six years learning exactly what that sigil meant, because his family owed the court more money than three generations of smuggling could pay off. His mother's debt. His dead brother's debt, inherited like the family nose. Debt that gathered interest in ways no ledger could show, because the Undercourt didn't charge in coin alone. It charged in years. In names. In the small, irreplaceable pieces of a person that you only missed after they'd been taken.

So he read it. Of course he read it. A drowning man reads anything thrown into the water.

*You are invited to attend upon Her Ladyship's agents at the Hollow Door, beneath the old tithe house on Candlemaker's Row, at the turning of the tide. Come alone. Come unarmed. Come willing.*

*What is offered cannot be offered twice.*

Cassian read it four times. Then he poured the rest of the rye down his throat, strapped his knife to his forearm anyway — because *come unarmed* was the kind of instruction you obeyed in the breach or not at all — and went out into the night to see what shape his ruin had decided to take.

* * *

The old tithe house on Candlemaker's Row had been abandoned for as long as Cassian could remember, which was itself strange, because everything in Saltreach got bought, sold, or squatted eventually. Buildings here didn't stay empty. Empty was waste, and waste was theft, and theft was the one sin the harbor districts punished swifter than murder.

But this building stayed empty. People walked past it faster than they walked past other buildings. Dogs crossed the street to avoid its shadow. And once, years ago, Cassian had watched a drunk stumble through its doorway on a bet and come out again thirty seconds later, sober as a magistrate, weeping without knowing why.

Now a door stood in its cellar where no door had been. The Hollow Door was aptly named: a slab of black wood set into the stone with no frame around it, no hinges visible, no handle. It simply stood there in the dark, upright, like a thing that had grown rather than been made. The air around it tasted of iron and myrrh and something sweeter underneath, something like the smell of a temple after the congregation has gone home.

Two figures waited beside it. They wore grey — not cloaks, not livery, just grey, the way fog wears grey, the way ash wears grey — and their faces were smooth in a way that took Cassian a long moment to diagnose. It wasn't that they lacked features. It was that their features were *uncommitted*, like a sketch an artist had abandoned halfway through deciding who they wanted the person to be.

"Cassian Vole," said the taller one. Its voice was pleasant. That was the worst part. "Thank you for coming armed."

"It seemed polite."

"Her Ladyship anticipated as much." The creature extended a hand, palm up, and Cassian felt the knife tear itself free of his forearm sheath and cross the room to lie in that waiting palm like a dog coming to heel. The agent closed its fingers and the knife was simply gone. "She asks that you consider this a courtesy. Everything you carry tonight will be returned to you, if tonight goes well."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then what you carry will no longer matter to you." The agent smiled. The smile arrived a half-second after the mouth moved, like thunder after lightning. "Come. She dislikes waiting, and time below runs strangely enough already."

The Hollow Door opened inward onto darkness, and Cassian Vole, who had spent his whole life calculating odds and finding the angle of escape in every room he entered, walked through it because the alternative was six more years of watching interest eat his family alive, and because some traitorous part of him — the part that had once believed in things — whispered that whatever waited below might finally explain why the stars had started going out.

* * *

He had expected a throne room. What he got was a parlor.

Lady Sable received him in a chamber that managed to be simultaneously magnificent and shabby, the way a great house looks after the family has died but the servants keep dusting. Mirrors lined every wall, tall as doors, and each mirror showed a different version of the room — one hung with banners, one flooded ankle-deep in dark water, one empty except for a single chair facing away from the viewer. In the center of the actual room sat a woman at an actual table, pouring tea from an actual pot, and she looked up at Cassian with the mild, tired courtesy of a landlady collecting rent.

"You're smaller than your reputation," she said.

"Lady Sable." He didn't bow. He'd decided that on the stairs, and now that he was standing in front of her he regretted it immediately, the way you regret any small defiance performed for an audience of mirrors. "Your letter said—"

"My letter said what gets people through doors." She gestured at the chair across from her. It was a good chair, walnut and velvet, and sitting in it felt like stepping into a trap that had been upholstered for comfort. "Tea?"

"I'd rather hear the offer."

"Of course you would. You're a businessman." She poured two cups anyway, and the steam rose straight up in perfectly parallel columns, unbothered by any draft, as if the air itself had been instructed to behave. "Here is the situation, Master Vole, stated plainly, because I find plainness disarms people far better than mystery ever did. Your family owes my court a considerable sum. Six years of payments have covered the interest and touched none of the principal — you know this; you've seen the ledgers; you've watched the numbers grow in your sleep. At the current rate, your grandchildren will die still owing me. I am prepared to forgive the entire debt. Tonight. Fully. Principal, interest, and all the... subsidiary obligations."

"Subsidiary obligations." His mouth had gone dry. "Meaning what, exactly?"

"Meaning the parts of the arrangement you've never been shown." She turned her cup a quarter-rotation on its saucer, precisely, like a hand moving a piece on a board. "Every contract signed under the old wards carried clauses written in the god's name. Veth's name. You swore oaths over your mother's debt, Cassian. Do you remember? Three years ago, in the little shrine office above the Harbor Registry. You put your hand on the stone and you promised payment, and the stone drank the promise, and you walked out feeling lighter because that's what oaths do — they take the weight off the one who speaks them."

He remembered. God, he remembered. The cool of the oath-stone under his palm. The clerk's bored voice reciting terms. The strange, floating ease afterward, like setting down a pack he'd forgotten he was carrying.

"What about it?"

"The stone has been drinking from the wrong well for some time now." For the first time, something moved behind Lady Sable's face — not quite an expression, more the memory of one, worn like a borrowed coat. "The god whose name sanctified your oath is... indisposed. Its obligations have passed to my court. Which means, Master Vole, that every promise you made in Veth's name is now a promise made to *me*. Including the one that says your family's debt is sacred and must be honored unto the third generation."

The room was very quiet. In one of the mirrors, Cassian saw himself standing, though he was sitting; in another, he saw himself sitting, but older, grey at the temples, with a scar he didn't have.

"So erase it," he said. "If you hold the paper, burn the paper. Why do you need me?"

"Because I don't hold all the paper." She rose, and the mirrors rose with her, a dozen Lady Sables rising in imperfect unison. "There is a person walking toward my mountain right now, carrying something that belongs to me, protected by an oath I cannot easily break. This person is skilled, lucky, and knows the hollow ways better than anyone living — better, perhaps, than anyone *should*. Their name is Ines Almeida."

The name landed in Cassian's chest like a dropped anchor.

He kept his face still. Eleven years of card tables and customs houses had taught him that much. But inside, something old and complicated turned over, the way ice turns over on a lake in spring — a slow, groaning shift that couldn't be undone once begun.

"Ines Almeida is dead," he said. "Left Saltreach four years ago. Nobody's seen—"

"Nobody you've spoken to." Lady Sable drifted closer, and up close she smelled of ink and cold candlewax and, faintly, unbearably, of a specific brand of orange oil that Ines used to rub into their boot leather. "They crossed the lower pass nine days ago. They are heading for the mountain, and what they carry — or what they *are*, the distinction grows less important by the day — represents a threat to arrangements older than your family's debt, older than your family, older than the concept of families. I want them stopped. Brought to me, ideally. Dead, acceptably. Delayed, minimally acceptable."

"And in exchange—"

"Your ledger burns. All of it. Your mother stops flinching when collectors knock. Your brother's grave stops being collateral." She tilted her head. "You'll walk out of this mountain owing nothing to anyone. Can you imagine it, Master Vole? Freedom. Actual freedom. Most people can't. Most people prefer their chains once they've stopped noticing the weight."

Cassian looked at his hands. They were steady, which surprised him. Somewhere beneath the fear and the arithmetic, a colder process was running, sorting facts into piles.

Fact: Ines Almeida had taught him the salt roads. Had pulled him out of a rockslide on the Widow's Stair with a rope and a stream of curses. Had split every take fifty-fifty even the year Cassian's mother sickened and he needed the coin more than pride allowed. Had left without goodbye, without word, vanishing between one season and the next like smoke through a shutter.

Fact: people who vanished like that had reasons, and the reasons were rarely innocent, and four years was long enough for anyone to become someone else.

Fact: his mother was sixty-one years old and coughing blood, and the collectors had started mentioning her name in ways that made his skin crawl, and there was no version of the future where he outran this debt honestly.

Fact: he hated himself already, and this would make it worse, and worse was survivable. He'd survived worse. Everyone from the harbor districts had.

"One condition," Cassian heard himself say.

Lady Sable's eyebrows rose a fraction. "Negotiation. How refreshingly mortal."

"When it's done — however it's done — I never see this place again. No new contracts. No invitations. The door stays shut for me and mine until the mountains wear down to gravel."

"Granted." She said it instantly, which meant it cost her nothing, which meant he should have asked for something harder, and he knew it, and it was too late. "Sign, and we're concluded."

She slid a sheet of black vellum across the table. There was no text on it. There was only a space at the bottom, and above the space, faintly, like heat-shimmer, the outline of words that refused to be read directly — words that slid away from the eye whenever he tried to focus on them.

"What does it say?"

"It says what I told you it says." Her voice softened, almost kindly. "That is the nature of our agreements, Master Vole. You take the meaning on faith and the consequence on signature. Everyone who deals with my court does exactly that. Your god did it. Your Wardens did it. Your partner—" she let the word sit there, deliberate, a needle finding the seam "—did it, long before you ever learned their name."

His hand hovered over the signature line. In the mirrors, a dozen versions of him hovered too, and in one of them — just one, at the edge of his vision, in the glass that showed the empty room and the chair facing away — the figure that wore his face had already signed, and was already weeping.

Cassian Vole thought about his mother's hands, which had mended nets for forty years and now shook too badly to hold a needle. He thought about the weight he'd set down on the oath-stone three years ago, and how light he'd felt, and how he'd never once wondered where the weight had gone.

He signed.

The ink sank into the vellum like water into sand, and somewhere far below them — so far below it might have been beneath the world — something vast exhaled.

* * *

They gave him back his knife at the top of the stairs, and a packet of papers besides: routes, timetables, a description of Ines's last known position at the flooded pass below Greywater. The grey agents escorted him to the Hollow Door and stood aside, and as he stepped past them the shorter one spoke for the first time, in a voice like dry leaves.

"She will know if you warn them."

"I know how contracts work," Cassian said.

"Do you?" The agent's unfinished face did something that was almost sympathy. "Then you know the saddest clause of all. Go and look at your ledger tomorrow, Master Vole. See what's already been struck from it. And then ask yourself what a man owes to the one who freed him — and whether gratitude isn't just a leash you tie yourself."

The door closed behind him. The cellar was dark and smelled of dust, and the stairs up to Candlemaker's Row were ordinary stairs, and the night above was ordinary night, except that the sky held fewer stars than it had yesterday, and Cassian stood in the street counting them like a man counting coins he already knew wouldn't cover the bill.

He walked home the long way, past the Harbor Registry, past the shrine office with its little window and its oath-stone he could see gleaming dully through the glass. Tomorrow he would check the ledgers. Tomorrow he would watch his mother's face when the collectors' letters stopped coming. Tomorrow he would buy passage east and start asking questions in the border towns about a smuggler named Almeida.

Tonight he sat on the cold steps outside his own door with his head in his hands, and he hated himself thoroughly, professionally, with the full attention he usually gave to lockpicking — because hatred, at least, was work he knew how to do.

And then, near dawn, a boy came running down Candlemaker's Row shouting that the stars had all gone out at once, every one, the whole sky dark as a pocket, and the wards on the mountain had fallen like glass, and something was dragging the oath-sworn up the roads toward Thornfell whether they willed it or not.

Cassian lifted his head. High above the rooftops, where the mountain's silhouette cut the sky, a faint red glow was pulsing against the starless black — steady, rhythmic, patient.

Like a heartbeat. Like a summons. Like a brand.

He looked down at his own forearm, at the faded oath-scar he'd stopped seeing years ago, and watched it begin, very softly, to shine.
