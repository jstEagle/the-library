## 5. Wren Remembers

The fire had burned down to a bed of orange coals by the time the child spoke again.

Ines had carried Wren out of the rockfall with a broken arm and a silence that felt less like shock and more like waiting — the way a held breath waits, not for air, but for permission. They'd made camp in the lee of a shattered waystone, one of the old boundary markers that studded the Thornfell passes like rotten teeth, and wrapped the child in the oilcloth from Ines's own pack because the blanket they'd found them under was soaked through with mountain meltwater and something darker that smelled of iron and old prayer.

Wren was maybe seven years old. Small enough that Ines could have carried the child on one hip without noticing the weight, if their left arm hadn't been hanging wrong in its sling. A fine-boned face, gray-eyed, hair the color of wet slate cut short and uneven — someone had sheared it recently, roughly, with a knife rather than scissors. There were marks on the back of both hands: small, precise brands, still pink at the edges. Ines had seen brands like that before. Every oath-sworn mercenary on the salt road wore one. But no one branded a seven-year-old. No one *could* — the tithe-houses required a name, a contract, a witness. Children were exempt. It was the single mercy the trade allowed itself.

These weren't tithe-brands anyway. Ines had looked closer, in the firelight, while the child slept against their good shoulder. The marks were older than the skin around them somehow, the way a scar can seem older than the wound that made it. And they weren't the sigil of Veth — the closed eye, the vertical line, the mark every sworn blade in the mountains carried like a leash. These were different. A circle, open at the top. An eye that was not closed but *absent*, the socket empty, looking inward at nothing.

Ines knew the shape. Had known it without knowing where from, which was becoming an uncomfortably common condition.

"You're staring," the child said, without opening their eyes.

"I'm checking your hands," Ines said. "The brands. Making sure they're not festering."

"They don't fester." Wren's voice was small and even, a child's pitch with something underneath it that didn't belong to a child — a steadiness, a patience, the tone of someone who has waited a very long time for a very specific moment and is in no hurry now that it's arriving. "They're not wounds. They're doors."

Ines went cold all down their right side, from shoulder to boot-heel, in a way that had nothing to do with the mountain night.

"Doors to what?"

"To me." The eyes opened then, and Ines understood, in the space of a single breath, why the rockfall had happened, why the wards had fallen, why the stars had gone out like snuffed candles — all of it folding backward into this moment, into these gray eyes catching the coal-light and holding it the way deep water holds a dropped stone. "Or to what's left of me. Which is the same thing, if you say it right."

* * *

They sat across the fire from each other after that, because Ines could not hold the child and also think, and thinking had become the only work available. The coals ticked and settled. Above them, through a seam in the cloud cover, the sky showed nothing at all — not clouds, not haze, but nothing, a black so complete it had texture, like velvet turned inside out. Ines had crossed the Thornfells forty times, sixty, had navigated by stars since before they could read a contract. The sky's emptiness was not darkness. Darkness had edges. This was absence wearing darkness as a disguise.

"Say your name," Wren said.

"Ines Almeida."

"No." The child tilted their head, and there was something almost gentle in the correction, the way a teacher corrects rather than rebukes. "Your other one."

Ines stared at the fire. "I don't have another one."

"You do." Wren said it with total certainty, without cruelty, without the particular relish a child takes in knowing something an adult doesn't. It was worse than that. It was matter-of-fact. "You lost it. Or you sold it. Those look the same from the outside." A pause, long enough for Ines to feel the silence pressing in like water pressure. "But I remember it. I remember everything that was given away. That's what I am. I'm the part that remembers."

"And what did it call me?"

The child looked at the fire for a moment first — really looked, the way you look at a thing when you are deciding whether telling the truth will break it — and then said, quietly:

"Ashkavael."

The name landed in Ines's chest like a stone dropped into a well whose bottom they'd never found. Not recognition. Nothing so kind. But resonance — a hum, a vibration, the feeling of a tuning fork struck near a bell that shares its metal. Ines had been called many things in twenty years of smuggling: partner, traitor, the Ghost of the Salt Road, that Almeida bitch, that Almeida bastard, depending on the year and the buyer. None of those names had ever produced a sound in the body. Ashkavael did. It produced a sound like a door opening in a house Ines had never lived in, revealing a room full of furniture covered in sheets.

"That's not my name," Ines said.

"It's your oldest one." Wren pulled the oilcloth tighter around their shoulders. Their hands, with those strange open-circle brands, emerged just far enough to cradle a coal-warmed stone Ines had rolled into the fire earlier. "Names wear out. Like boots. You've worn through several. But the first one is stitched into you somewhere deeper than leather, and when I say it, you hear the stitching."

Ines did. That was the horror of it. Not that the child was lying — liars were legible, and Wren was the least legible creature Ines had ever met — but that the name fit a body Ines had spent two decades believing was fully mapped. Ines knew their own scars. Knew the burn on the forearm from the Kesselgret job, the missing nail on the left ring finger, the tooth replaced with river-ivory after a customs officer's cudgel. Knew the roads of Thornfell so well they could walk them blindfolded, which had always been a private joke, a professional boast, a curiosity filed under *talent*. Tonight, for the first time, the joke had stopped being funny. Muscle memory wasn't memory. Muscle memory was a scar in the shape of a road, and scars have causes.

"When did I lose it?" Ines asked. "The name."

Wren considered. The child's face in the coal-light was uncanny not because it was old — it wasn't, it was a child's face, soft-jawed, a scratch healing on one cheekbone from the rockfall — but because of how it used stillness. Adults performed thought. Children leaked it. Wren simply *was* thoughtful, the way a stone is heavy.

"I don't know when," the child said finally. "Time is slippery down where I was kept. But I know why." The gray eyes came up and fixed on Ines, and there was no accusation in them, which was somehow more terrible than accusation would have been. Only fact. "You gave something away once. Something large. And the part of you that held your first name went with it, because names are payment too. Everything is payment, down there. Under the mountain, everything is currency. Grief. Loyalty. Memory. Names. Especially names."

"The Undercourt." Ines said it flatly, testing the word against the dark. Odile Ferran had screamed it at them three days ago across a burning ward-station — *they sold it, they sold the god, the court bought it* — before vanishing into the smoke with her satchel of records and her face like a woman who had read her own death warrant and found her own signature at the bottom.

"Before that," Wren said. "And during. And after. Time doesn't move properly around a stolen soul. That's why the stars went out all at once instead of one by one. A theft that size folds the sky."

* * *

Later — much later, past the hour when the cold stops being weather and becomes a presence that sits down beside you and waits — Ines lay awake on the oilcloth with their broken arm propped on the pack and listened to the child breathe.

This was the arrangement they had fallen into without discussing it: Wren slept curled against Ines's side, under Ines's cloak, because the child was cold and light-starved and trembled in sleep like a bird. And Ines lay rigid, half afraid to move, half afraid not to, listening to that small breathing and trying to reconcile it with the voice that came out of it when the child woke in the dark hours and spoke without transition, mid-thought, as though conversation were a place they lived rather than an activity they performed.

"You were praying," Wren said at some point in that black interval. "Just now. While you thought I was asleep. You pray wrong."

Ines didn't deny it. Prayer was the one habit they'd never managed to break or hide, twenty years of silent god notwithstanding — a mutter under the breath at the tops of passes, a touch to the brand before a dangerous crossing, the reflexive *Veth keep me* that escaped at moments of genuine fear like blood from a cut. Everyone swore by Veth. Nobody expected answers anymore. The god had been silent for longer than most of the salt road's living memory, and the faithful had metabolized that silence the way mountain people metabolize everything: as weather. You didn't ask why it snowed. You dressed for it.

"How do I pray wrong?"

"You apologize." Wren's voice, half-asleep, was softer than their waking voice, and stranger — the cadence loosened, slowed, took on a rhythm that Ines recognized the way you recognize a song heard once through a wall years ago. A falling inflection at the end of each phrase. A pause before the final word of a sentence, as though gathering it. As though words were heavy things being set down carefully rather than thrown. "You say *sorry* before you say anything else. Sorry for the cargo. Sorry for the lie to the toll-keeper. Sorry for wanting coin. You've been apologizing to Veth for so long that you've forgotten the prayers themselves. Do you know what you used to ask for?"

"No."

"Neither do I." A breath. "That's the joke. I'm the memory, and I don't have that one either. It was taken separately. Someone wanted it very badly."

Ines lay in the dark and felt the vertigo open beneath them again, wider this time. Because here was the thing they had not said aloud, the thing sitting in their chest like a swallowed hook: the child talked about Veth the way you talk about yourself. Not *the god did this*. Not *my god wants that*. But *I was kept*. *I remember what was given away*. *Someone wanted my memory*. The pronouns were wrong. Or they were right, and everything else was wrong.

"You said you're a fragment," Ines said into the dark. "Of the god's memory. Hidden in mortal flesh."

"Yes."

"Hidden by who?"

A long silence. Long enough that Ines counted their own heartbeats against it — eleven, twelve, thirteen — before the answer came, and when it came it came in that cadence, that falling, weighted rhythm, and Ines felt the hairs rise along their unbroken arm:

"By me. Before the taking. I saw the sale coming the way you see weather coming over a pass — too late to stop it, early enough to save one thing. So I saved the remembering. I folded it small and hid it in flesh, because flesh is the one vault the Undercourt cannot pick. They own oaths. They own souls that are bound. But a child is free until someone binds her, and no one knew to look." A pause, and then, quieter, with something that was almost — almost — humor: "Until tonight. When you pulled me out of a rockfall on a road you shouldn't know, toward a mountain you were dragged to, carrying a brand that flared like a signal fire. I did say hidden. I didn't say well."

"Why me?" Ines asked. "Why my arms? Why did the brand drag *me*?"

"Because you're leashed," Wren said, simply, terribly. "Every oath sworn in Veth's name is a chain now, and chains run both ways. When the soul moved, the leashes pulled. You didn't come to me, Ines-of-the-salt-road. Your oath did. It smelled its owner."

Ines stared up into the starless black and understood, with a clarity that felt like a knife laid flat against the skin, that they had not rescued the child. That the compulsion in the brand, the dragging, the wrongness of walking toward the mountain instead of away from it while the wards fell like glass around them — that had been the leash doing exactly what leashes do. They had been reeled in. They had dug a god out of a rockfall because their god's captor's machinery had told their muscles to.

And yet.

And yet the arms that pulled Wren free had been Ines's arms. The choice to go back into the settling dust a second time, after the first boulder, when any sane runner would have kept running — that had been made by whatever was left inside Ines when the leash slackened for a moment. Sable's machinery had aimed them. It had not dug for them. It had not wrapped them in oilcloth. It had not sat up all night warming stones.

"Ask me," Wren said suddenly, awake now, sitting up in the nest of cloak and oilcloth, and the coal-light caught the empty-eye brands on the small hands. "Ask me what you were going to ask. You've been chewing it for hours."

Ines was quiet. Then, because lying to this child had begun to seem not merely difficult but structurally impossible, like lying to a mirror:

"Are you Veth?"

The question hung between them. Below it, unspoken, the real question, the one with teeth: *because if you are, then the god I've served, apologized to, tithed to, bled for — is seven years old and shivering in my cloak, and everything I believed about the silence, the distance, the vast indifferent heaven above the pass, is a lie I've been warm under.*

"No," Wren said. "Not anymore than a single verse is the whole song. I am what Veth hid. The rest is hanging in a lantern under the mountain, chained by every promise ever made in its name." The child looked at Ines then, and the gray eyes were not a child's eyes, had never been a child's eyes, and Ines finally let themselves see it without flinching. "You want to know the worst part. Ask."

"What's the worst part?"

"You already love me." Wren said it without vanity, without softness — a diagnosis. "It started when you pulled me out. It isn't yours and it isn't mine. It's residue. Love is what leaks out of a soul when you carry a piece of it next to your heart. You've been leaking since the rockfall. By the time we reach the court you'll mistake it for your own feeling entirely, and Lady Sable is counting on that. She'll offer you a bargain shaped exactly like this tenderness. She always does. She knows what people leak."

Ines sat with that. Around them the mountain night pressed close, mineral and absolute, and somewhere far below — below the trail, below the scree, below the roots of the range itself — something waited that wore courtesy like a coat and hunger like skin.

"Wren." Ines used the child's name deliberately, anchoring them both to it. "If you're the remembering — then tell me the thing I actually need. Not my old name. Not my sins. Tell me how to get into the Undercourt."

The child smiled then, and it was the first wholly childish thing Ines had seen them do, quick and crooked and bright, and it broke Ines's heart in a place they hadn't known was load-bearing.

"I was going to wait until morning," Wren said. "But since you asked — you won't get in. You'll be *let* in. That's the trap and the door; they're the same architecture down there. Someone is already coming for us. Someone who owes the court a debt and hates himself for it, and he'll find us on the flooded pass in two days, and whether he's our death or our way in depends on—" The child paused, head tilting, listening to something Ines could not hear, some frequency under the mountain's silence. "—on whether he remembers you the way I do. And he does, Ines. Oh, he does. He's been dreaming your other name for years and calling it a nightmare."

Ines went very still.

"Cassian," they said.

"Cassian," Wren agreed, and lay back down, and pulled the cloak to their chin, and closed the gray eyes that were not a child's eyes. "Sleep now, Ashkavael. We have two days, and a pass to flood behind us, and an old partner to survive. And after him—" the falling cadence, the pause before the last word, the careful setting-down of something heavy, "—a door that only opens for the one who sold it."
