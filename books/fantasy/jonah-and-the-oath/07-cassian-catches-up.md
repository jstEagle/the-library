## 7. Cassian Catches Up

The flooded pass announced itself long before we reached it — a sound like the mountain clearing its throat, water churning somewhere below the trail in a darkness that had no bottom. I had crossed this way a dozen times in years I could not account for. My feet knew it. That was the trouble with my body these days: it remembered everything, and I remembered almost nothing.

Odile came first along the ledge, her ward-keeper's coat stiff with dried mud, one hand trailing the rock wall as though she could read it. Behind her, Wren rode my shoulders, small hands knotted in my collar, and every so often the child would go still in a way that made the hair on my arms rise — listening to something under the world.

"Left," Wren would say. "The left stone is lying."

And the left stone was always lying. A slab that looked solid enough to bear weight but rang hollow when Odile tapped it. A shelf of gravel that would have poured us into the flood like grain from a sack. The child could not have weighed more than forty pounds, but they carried more than I did.

"How much farther to the crossing?" Odile asked. Her voice was hoarse from days of smoke and confession. She had burned everything she owned back at the ward-house; she traveled light now, the way people do when what they carried was too heavy to survive.

"The rope bridge is gone," I said. "Flood took it two seasons back. There's a ford above the falls — narrow, ugly, but the water's shallow if you know where the old paving stones sit."

"You know where the paving stones sit."

"I know where they sit." I did not say *I don't know how*. Neither of us said it anymore. We had passed through that particular silence on the second day and come out the other side of it into something like companionship — two people carrying different halves of the same ruin.

Wren shifted against my neck. "Someone's coming."

I stopped. "What?"

"Behind us." The child's voice was flat, matter-of-fact, the way children report terrible things. "Heavy boots. He walks like he's angry at the ground."

Odile turned, hand going to the short blade at her hip. "How far?"

"Half a day yesterday. Quarter of a day now." Wren frowned, concentrating. "He stopped where we slept. He touched the ashes of our fire. He's not tracking us by sign — he knows where we're going."

I knew who walked like that. I knew it in the same place I knew the roads: somewhere below memory, in the marrow. Cassian Vole moved like a man perpetually arguing with the earth beneath him, as if it owed him money. We had run cargo together for four years, back before — before whatever it was I'd lost. Before the tithe-houses and the oath-brand and the god-shaped hole in my head. He'd been the only person whose silences I never had to interpret.

"He'll be armed for me specifically," I said. "Not for a ward-keeper. Not for a child."

"Then we go around," Odile said. "There are other ways down."

"There aren't. Not before new moon. Not with the wards down and the stars out and half the mountain trying to remember what it's for." I looked up, out of habit, at a sky that gave me nothing back. Five nights now without stars. You stop flinching at it eventually, and then you hate yourself for stopping. "We take the ford. If he wants me, he can have me at the water, where I choose the ground."

* * *

The ford was exactly as ugly as I remembered, which was its own strange comfort. The river came down off the glacier in three braids, white and loud, and the old paving stones sat just under the surface like the backs of sleeping animals. Someone had built them before men built roads — the stones were each cut as long as I am tall, fitted without mortar, worn smooth in a way water alone doesn't manage. God-work, Odile called it. She said it quietly, like an apology.

We were halfway across, Wren on my shoulders with their heels locked under my collarbone, when the ambush opened.

It was good work, I'll give him that. Cassian had always been better at waiting than fighting; his patience was a weapon most people never saw coming because they expected smugglers to be all nerve and no discipline. He'd found the one place on the far bank where the scree funneled travelers into a kill-lane between two fallen slabs, and he let us commit to the crossing before he showed himself.

A crossbow bolt hit the water a hand's width from my knee, close enough that the spray stung my cheek. Then his voice, carrying over the river with the ease of long practice:

"Ines Almeida. You're a hard woman to find and an easy one to aim at."

*I'm not a woman*, some old reflex thought, and then I couldn't remember why the reflex existed, or whether it was true, or whether it mattered. The mountain had taken my certainties one by one. I kept the ones that helped me walk.

"Cassian." I didn't raise my voice. The river would do the work. "You're standing in Sable's boots."

"I'm standing in dry boots. It's the first time in six years." He stepped out of the shadow of the slab, crossbow leveled, and even at fifty paces I could see what the last years had done to him — thinner, grayer, a scar through one eyebrow I didn't remember giving or taking. His oath-brand showed at his wrist where his sleeve had ridden up: the same knotwork sigil mine bore, the same leash. "They cleared my family's debt, Ines. All of it. My mother's hospital bills, my brother's contract, the whole rotten ledger. Do you know what that's worth to someone like me?"

"Yes," I said. "That's why they offered it to you."

Something flickered across his face. He hated being predictable. He always had.

"Put the child down," he said. "Walk toward me slow. The ward-keeper can stay in the water — I've got nothing against her, and nothing for her either."

"And if I don't?"

"Then I put a bolt through your thigh and drag you the rest of the way. Lady Sable wants you breathing. She didn't say anything about comfortable." He sighted along the crossbow, and his hands were steady, and that steadiness frightened me more than any tremor would have. Cassian's hands shook when his heart wasn't in something. Steady meant he'd made himself mean it.

I set Wren down on the widest paving stone, behind me, in the lee of my body. The water pulled at my knees. Cold came up through my boots like a rumor.

"Cassian," I said again, and this time I used the other voice, the low one, the one we'd used across campfires when the cargo was dangerous and the night was long. "Four years. Salt road, wine road, the winter we nearly died in the White Saddle and you sang to keep me awake. You carried me a mile on your back with a broken ankle. Did they wipe that too, or do you still have it?"

His jaw tightened. The crossbow dipped a degree. One degree.

"I have all of it," he said, quieter now. "That's the problem. They gave me the debt-clearance and kept the memories, and I've spent nine days walking through everything we ever did together wondering which version of you is real. The partner who'd have taken a bolt for me, or the traitor who sold a god." He laughed, and there was no humor in it, only weariness with nowhere to go. "Sable says you brokered the sale. Says you're the reason Veth is in a cage under this mountain. Says everyone who ever swore to Veth is chained because of you."

"Do you believe her?"

"I want not to." The crossbow dipped another degree. "But wanting isn't evidence, Ines. You taught me that. *Trust the mark, not the man*, you used to say. Well, right now you're the mark, and everything I can verify says you're guilty."

"Everything you can verify," I said, "was written by the person who bought you."

The river roared between us, filling the silence neither of us wanted to own. And then Wren stood up.

* * *

I felt it happen more than saw it — a change in the pressure of the air, the way a storm changes the taste of wind before it arrives. The child rose from the paving stone and stepped out past my shoulder, into the open, into the line of Cassian's crossbow, and held up both small hands as if to show they were empty.

"Don't," I started, reaching —

But Cassian had already stopped moving. Completely. The crossbow stayed level, but the man behind it had gone rigid, the way animals go rigid when something older than fear looks at them. I knew the feeling. I'd been living inside it for five days.

Wren spoke, and it wasn't Wren's voice. It was Wren's voice the way a bell is a hammer-strike — the same material, transformed. It came out layered, doubled, a chord instead of a note, and it crossed the water without needing to be loud.

"*Cassian Vole.*"

The name landed on him like a hand on the shoulder. I watched his knees try to buckle and his pride hold them up.

"You swore to me," the child said. "In the spring of the year the salt road washed out. Kneeling, in a waystation chapel with a leaky roof, because your brother was dying and no one else would answer. You promised me your loyalty until the debt was paid, and I accepted, and I marked you — here." The small hand gestured at his wrist, at the brand. "Do you remember what you asked me? At the end? When the words were done and the wax was cooling?"

Cassian's throat worked. "No," he whispered. Then, honestly: "Yes. Gods help me, yes."

"You asked me to remember you kindly. Whatever you became. Whatever you did." The chord-voice softened, and somehow that was worse, that was unbearable, mercy delivered in a voice that vast. "I am the part of me that remembers. I am the fragment they could not sell, hidden in mortal flesh, and I have carried your promise across everything they did to me. So I ask you now, Cassian Vole, oath-sworn, debt-bound, standing in a hired killer's boots with a loaded crossbow aimed at a child of the god you swore to—"

He fired.

Or — he didn't. His finger squeezed and the mechanism caught, jammed, the string slack, and I will swear until I die that I saw the bolt simply refuse. It sat in the groove like a thing asleep. Cassian stared at it, then at his own hands, then at Wren, and the crossbow dropped from his grip into the scree with a clatter that the river swallowed whole.

He sat down. Just sat down, right there in the wet stone, like a puppet with cut strings, and put his face in his hands.

"I can't," he said, muffled. "I can't do it. Six years I've been telling myself the brand was just ink, just a formality, just the price of doing business in Thornfell. And it talks. It *remembers*. It remembers me better than I remember myself." He dragged his hands down his face and looked at Wren — really looked, the way you look at something you're afraid will vanish. "What are you?"

"A piece of what they stole," Wren said, in their own voice now, small and ordinary and tired. "Mostly I'm just a kid. Mostly."

"Mostly," Cassian repeated. He laughed again, and this time there was a crack of real laughter in it, hysterical and human. "Gods. I reported your position to Sable's agents two days ago. I told them the flooded pass. I told them you'd take the ford, because I know you, because you always take the ford—" He stopped. Looked at me. "They're coming, Ines. Not just agents. She's sending the Hollow Guard. Twelve of them, maybe more, and they don't hesitate and they don't grieve and they don't jam."

"How long?" Odile asked from the water behind me. She had waded closer during the exchange, blade drawn and useless, watching everything with the expression of a woman cataloguing sins.

"Half a day. Less, if they pushed through the night." Cassian got to his feet slowly, swaying, a man relearning his own weight. He looked at the dead crossbow in the scree and left it there. "I can buy you time. I know their rotation, their signs, the countersign for this week. I can send them to the White Saddle and swear blind you went east."

"Why?" I asked. Not cruelly. Genuinely. "Why turn?"

He was quiet a moment. Water thundered. Somewhere above us, in a sky with no stars, clouds moved like slow thoughts.

"Because I knelt in a leaky chapel once," he said finally, "and asked a god to remember me kindly, and it did, and it's hanging in a lantern under this mountain in pieces, and the people who hung it there paid me in *debt relief*." His voice roughened. "My mother's free. My brother's free. Fine. Good. But nobody's free while that brand is on my arm, Ines — I understand that now, I *understand* it, and I can't un-know it, and I can't shoot the person who might unmake it."

He picked his way down to the water's edge, keeping his distance, keeping his hands visible — an old smuggler's courtesy, a flag of truce in body language. Up close I could see the toll the last nine days had taken: hollow cheeks, bloodshot eyes, the look of a man who hadn't slept since the stars went out. Same as me. Same as all of us, now. The mountain was doing that to everyone it touched.

"Wren," he said, and crouched, careful, at the edge of the child's reach. "Can I — is it allowed — I'd like to shake your hand. If gods do that. If fragments do that."

Wren considered him with those too-old eyes, then reached out and put one small hand in his scarred one. "Allowed," they said. "Remembered."

Cassian held that hand for perhaps three seconds. I watched something leave him — some load-bearing certainty, some wall he'd been leaning his whole weight against. When he let go and stood, he looked lighter and more frightened, both at once, the way people look after confessions.

"The Hollow Guard travels fast and doesn't double back," he said, briskly now, all business, because business was the only language he had left for feelings that size. "White Saddle buys you two days, maybe three. After that, every road down closes for the sealing — new moon, the court seals for a century, and anyone inside stays inside. You understand what that means. Whatever you're going to do about the god, about the vault, about *her* — you do it in three days or you do it never."

"We know," Odile said. "We have a map."

"Then move. And Ines—" He hesitated. The old argument-with-the-ground posture, weight shifting, jaw working. "If it's true. What she says you did. The sale. If you really were the one who—"

"It might be true," I said. Because it might be. Because Sable had shown me a glimpse of a face wearing my history like a stolen coat, and I could not prove a single word of it false. "I don't know. I genuinely don't know what I was, before."

Cassian nodded slowly, as if that answer — the honest, horrible one — settled something for him better than innocence would have.

"Well," he said. "Then we're all building on rubble. Welcome to Thornfell." He turned away, then turned back, one last time. "Take care of the kid. The kid's the best of what's left of — of all of us, maybe. Of everything."

And he was gone up the eastern scree, moving fast, angry at the ground, buying us time with lies he'd hang for if Sable ever learned the truth of them.

* * *

We crossed the rest of the ford in silence. On the far bank, Odile wrung out her coat and studied Cassian's fading track marks with narrowed eyes.

"You trust him?"

"No," I said. "But I trusted him once, before I can remember trusting anyone, and the brand on his arm trusts *me* — did you feel it? The way the bolt refused? These chains cut both directions, Odile. Every oath sworn to Veth is a leash, but it's also a thread, and threads can be followed home."

Wren had climbed onto a boulder and stood there looking east, into the empty sky, small hands shading eyes that saw too much.

"They're coming faster than he thinks," the child said quietly. "Two days. Maybe less. And I'm getting tired, Ines. Holding the remembering is heavy, and we're getting close to the place where it's heaviest." The child looked down at me, and for a moment the too-old eyes were just a child's eyes, frightened, asking. "Don't let them seal me in the dark with the rest of it."

I took the child's hand. It was cold. It had always been cold.

"Three days," I said to Odile, to Wren, to the mountain, to whatever was left of the god listening through a fragment of itself in a mortal child. "Then we go down the hollow ways, and we knock on the Undercourt's door, and we ask for our soul back."

Above us, where stars should have been, the clouds parted for one breath — and in that black seam, faint as breath on glass, a single point of light flickered. Once. Like something very far away, very deep down, turning over in its sleep.

Then it was gone, and the dark closed like a mouth, and we started walking.
