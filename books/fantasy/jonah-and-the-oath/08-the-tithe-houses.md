## 8. The Tithe Houses

The agents of Lady Sable kept their offices in a tithe house at the foot of the mountain, and Cassian Vole had walked past that building four hundred times in his life without once looking at it properly.

That was the trick of it, he realized, standing now on the threshold with snow melting off his boots and his heart going like a trapped bird against his ribs. The building was designed to be overlooked. Grey stone, grey slate, a door the color of old dishwater, a sign above it — *Veth's Tithe, Faithfully Kept* — worn so smooth by weather that the letters had become suggestions rather than words. It looked like every other administrative building in every other border town: the kind of place where men filed ledgers and argued about tariffs and died of boredom before they died of anything else.

He knew better now.

He had been summoned by letter three days after the stars went out — a letter delivered not by post but by a woman who simply appeared at his campfire, sat down across from him uninvited, and waited until he stopped reaching for his knife. She wore grey. Everything about her was grey, down to the irises of her eyes, which were the flat silver of coins left too long in water. She said her name was Mistress Corvane. She said Lady Sable would see him. She said the word *see* the way other people said *summon*, and Cassian, who had spent thirty years learning to read the silences between words, understood that this was not an invitation.

So here he stood. And behind him, half a mile back up the switchback trail, hidden in a hollow beneath a leaning slab of granite, were Ines Almeida, a disgraced ward-keeper, and a child who claimed to be a piece of a god.

And in his breast pocket, folded twice, was the lie he intended to tell.

*You can do this,* he told himself. *You've lied to magistrates, to guildmasters, to your own mother on her deathbed.* But those lies had been small currency, spent to buy small mercies. This one would be spoken in the house where oaths went to die, to servants of the thing that held his family's debt like a fishhook through the cheek, and if he flinched — if his voice cracked, if his eyes darted wrong — then everything Ines was planning would end here, in this grey doorway, before it ever reached the dark below.

Mistress Corvane opened the door before he could knock.

"Master Vole." Her voice was pleasant. That was somehow worse than if it hadn't been. "Lady Sable's household thanks you for your promptness. Come in. Wipe your feet."

* * *

The entry hall smelled of tallow and cold iron and, beneath both, something faintly sweet — the smell of a room where flowers have been burned, or where blood has been cleaned very thoroughly and recently. Cassian catalogued it the way he catalogued everything: exits first. Two behind him, one ahead, narrow windows set too high for climbing. A corridor running left toward what sounded like a kitchen. A stair rising right into shadow.

Corvane led him right.

"You'll report to the Steward of Accounts," she said over her shoulder. "Not to the Lady herself, I'm afraid. She is... occupied. Preparations for the sealing consume her attention."

"The sealing," Cassian repeated. He kept his voice bored. Boredom was armor; he'd learned that in a hundred interrogation rooms, though never one he'd been brought to as a guest. "The new moon. Everyone in the lowlands is talking about it. They say the court closes for a century."

"They say correctly." Corvane paused at a landing and turned to look at him, and her coin-grey eyes did not blink often enough. "Which is why your report matters, Master Vole. Whatever you know of Ines Almeida's movements must be given now, completely, or not at all. After the moon turns, there will be no more hunting. Only keeping."

"Keeping," he said.

"Of what we have." She smiled. It was a well-made smile, fitted to her face like a good boot, and it moved nothing behind her eyes. "This way."

The Steward of Accounts received him in a room lined floor to ceiling with ledgers — thousands of them, tens of thousands, their spines cracked and their corners soft with handling, arranged in ranks so precise they made Cassian's teeth ache. A single lamp burned on a desk of black wood. Behind the desk sat a man so old that his skin had gone translucent at the temples, showing the pale architecture of skull beneath, and he was writing when they entered, pen scratching, scratching, scratching, and he did not stop until the sentence was finished, though it took him the better part of a minute.

"Sit," the Steward said.

Cassian sat.

"Now," the Steward said, folding his hands. His nails were yellowed and long, and each one bore a tiny stamped mark — a seal, a sigil, the same mark over and over. "Your sister's debt stands at two thousand crowns, accrued across eleven years, held under contract number—" he recited a string of figures without looking at any book, "—and payable, per the original terms, in full, upon demand, with forfeiture of assets and persons. You understand forfeiture of persons, Master Vole?"

"I understand it."

"Your niece is nine."

The room got very small. Cassian kept his hands loose on his knees and his breathing even and thought about how many ways there were to kill an old man in a chair, and how few of them would matter.

"She's nine," the Steward repeated, gently, almost kindly. "And Lady Sable has offered to extinguish the entire contract. Two thousand crowns. Eleven years. Gone. For one service." He slid a sheet of paper across the desk. On it was a charcoal sketch of a face Cassian had known since they were both seventeen and stupid and sharing a bedroll in the passes: sharp-boned, scarred through the left eyebrow, eyes that always looked slightly past you toward some road only they could see. "Ines Almeida. Alive, preferably. Dead, acceptably. Delivered to any tithe house, any hour. Your report begins now. Where is Ines Almeida?"

Here it was, then. The lie, or the rope.

Cassian let the silence stretch just long enough to look like reluctance — reluctance was expected, even creditable, in a man selling out his old partner — and then he spoke, and heard his own voice come out rough and steady, pitched in the register of a man doing something he hated for reasons he couldn't refuse.

"Last I saw them, they were heading for the flooded pass. Three days ago, before the stars went. They had company — a woman, older, moved like a soldier. Ward-keeper's bearing, though the wardens are done, far as I know. And a kid." He shrugged, and made the shrug ugly. "I didn't ask questions. Ines never answered them anyway. My guess is they're making for the high trails, trying to get over the spine before winter closes it. If they're smart. Ines was always smart about roads and stupid about everything else."

"And you did not follow."

"I followed until the wards came down." He met the Steward's eyes. "Then I turned around and went home and waited for your letter, because I'm not suicidal, and whatever knocked those stars out isn't something I want to meet on a mountainside with a knife and good intentions."

Something flickered in the old man's face — not belief, exactly, but the absence of disbelief, which was all Cassian needed. The Steward made a note. The pen scratched. Outside, wind worried at the high windows with thin fingers.

"Your account will be verified," the Steward said mildly. "We have... means. If it proves accurate, the contract burns. If it proves otherwise—" He did not finish the sentence. He didn't need to. Nine years old. He'd said it once already and the number would sit in Cassian's chest like swallowed glass for the rest of his life, however long that turned out to be.

"Then I'll want proof it's done," Cassian said. "When the contract's burned. Paper. With seals."

"Naturally." The Steward rang a small bell. "Mistress Corvane will show you out. Oh — and Master Vole?" Cassian paused at the door. "You wear Veth's brand. May I see it?"

Every instinct screamed. Cassian turned back, worked his glove off, and extended his left forearm, where the oath-brand sat in its familiar grey: a circle crossed by a single vertical line, the mark of the silent god, seared into him at nineteen when he'd sworn his first tithe-oath for coin to bury his father.

The Steward touched it with one long fingernail. The touch was ice.

"Yes," the old man murmured, almost tenderly. "Yes, I thought so. A strong one. So much loyalty in it still. You'd be surprised, Master Vole, how thin most of them run, over the years. How the thread wears. Yours hasn't worn at all." He released the arm. "It will make such excellent thread."

"What does that mean?" Cassian asked, and heard, too late, how the question cracked down the middle.

The Steward smiled with all the warmth of a ledger. "It means walk with Mistress Corvane. She'll show you the houses. Every contractor should understand what he's contracted to."

* * *

There were six tithe houses in Thornfell town. Cassian had known that the way you know the number of churches — as furniture, as fact, as nothing. Now he walked through them, all six, in the space of an afternoon, and understood that he had lived his whole life inside a body whose blood was being siphoned through walls he'd leaned against, drunk beside, slept behind.

They started with the smallest. From the street it was a chandler's shop, shutters closed, no smoke from the chimney. Inside, past a storeroom of tallow and wicks, a door Corvane unlocked with a key she wore on a chain around her neck, and then—

Then a hall.

Long and low and lit by lamps that gave light without heat, and filled with cots. Hundreds of cots, rank upon rank, and on each cot a person lying perfectly still with their eyes open, staring at the ceiling, and from each person's chest — from the brand, from the circle-and-line seared over the heart — ran a thread.

Not silk. Not wire. Something between: a filament of grey light, fine as spider-spit, rising from each sleeping chest, gathering with hundreds of its fellows into bundles, the bundles into cables, the cables flowing up and along the ceiling and out through a vent in the far wall, all streaming in the same direction. Toward the mountain. Downward.

Cassian stood in the doorway and could not make his legs work.

"They aren't asleep," Corvane said beside him, conversational, a guide in a cathedral. "Or rather, sleep is incidental. What matters is stillness. Loyalty moves, you see. Loyalty wants things, walks toward things, fights for things — and movement frays the thread. So we keep them quiet. The oaths do most of the work. An oath sworn in Veth's name is a channel, and channels flow whether the swearer wills it or not. Every promise ever made to the silent god runs downhill to the silent god." She tilted her head. "Or to what holds the silent god. Same plumbing, these days."

One of the figures on the nearest cot turned its head. Slowly, like something underwater. It was a woman, maybe fifty, sunburnt, a caravan guard's calluses on her hands, and her eyes found Cassian and focused on him with terrible effort, and her lips moved.

No sound came out. But he read the shape of it, because he'd seen dying men mouth the same thing in a dozen border skirmishes:

*Help.*

The thread at her chest pulsed, faintly, in time with her heartbeat, and Cassian saw — he would see it for the rest of his life, in every dark room, behind every closed eye — the way her eyelids dragged back down, the way her head settled, the way the wanting drained out of her face like water out of a cracked cup, leaving only the smooth blank patience of the others, row upon row upon row.

"How many," he said. His voice came out sanded flat.

"In this house? Four hundred. In the town? Eleven thousand. Across the range?" Corvane considered. "We stopped counting at sixty thousand. There wasn't a reason to keep counting. They don't go anywhere."

Sixty thousand. Sixty thousand oaths, sworn over decades by mercenaries and pilgrims and desperate fools like his nineteen-year-old self, kneeling at roadside shrines because the tithe was cheap and the protection was real — *had been* real, once, when the god still had a soul to answer with — and every single vow a straw stuck into their chests, and every straw running downhill, and at the bottom of the hill a lantern hanging in the dark, drinking.

His own brand sat under his glove like a coal.

"That one's nearly empty," Corvane said, nodding at the woman on the cot, following his gaze with professional detachment. "Forty years of service. She'll last another decade, perhaps. We rotate the deep draws through the rest-houses when they start to fray." She began walking again. "Come. The third house has the children's wing. You should see it. People always say the same thing."

"I don't want to see it."

"Nobody does," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "That's rather the point of the design."

* * *

He refused the children's wing. He planted his feet in the corridor and said no, and Corvane looked at him for a long moment with those coin-grey eyes, and then — and this was the detail he turned over for hours afterward, walking the streets in the starless dusk, buying time, buying distance, buying the hour he needed to get back up the trail — she had nodded. As if the refusal itself had been informative. As if she'd written something down behind her eyes.

The sixth house was the largest, and it was not a house at all. It was built into the mountainside itself, a gate in the rock that Corvane opened with three keys and a word in a language that made Cassian's brand burn cold, and beyond it stairs descending, and warmth rising up the stairs like breath, and a sound.

A sound he felt in his teeth before he heard it with his ears. A vast, slow, rhythmic pull, like the tide of some ocean miles underground, like the intake of lungs the size of caverns. Every thread in the world seemed to bend toward it. His own thread — the thing in his chest he had called faith, called habit, called the price of doing business — strained toward it so hard it hurt, and he understood suddenly, wholly, with the clarity of a man watching the gallows built for him, what the Steward had meant.

*It will make such excellent thread.*

His loyalty was a leash. His love — the stubborn, unkillable thing in him that had tracked Ines Almeida across twenty years of betrayal and silence and one unforgivable night in the flooded pass — was raw material. The court didn't want his obedience. Obedience was cheap. It wanted the *attachment itself*, the living fiber of it, drawn out of him strand by strand while he lay on a cot staring at a ceiling, until there was nothing left in him that reached toward anyone, until he was as smooth and patient and empty as the woman who had mouthed *help* at him and then forgotten how to want help.

He stepped back from the stair. The warmth breathed upward. Somewhere below, something drank.

"Questions?" Corvane asked.

"One." His voice was steady. He was almost proud of it. "The contract. My sister's. When it burns — the debt, the forfeiture clause, all of it — it burns clean? No transfer. No successor claim."

"Lady Sable's word is iron."

"Her word," Cassian said, "is a leash too. Isn't it. Everything here is."

For the first time, something moved behind Corvane's eyes — not warmth, nothing so human, but a kind of appraiser's interest, the look of a woman finding an unexpected hallmark on a piece she'd assumed was base metal. "Go home, Master Vole," she said. "Verify your information. Return within two days, or the offer expires with the moon." She closed the mountain-gate. The three locks fell like verdicts. "And Master Vole — a small observation, free of charge. You asked whether the contract burns clean. Contractors who intend to deliver don't usually ask that."

The street outside was dark. All the stars were gone, and the town's lamps seemed thinner for it, and the mountain rose over everything, blacker than the sky, wearing its stolen light somewhere in its guts.

Cassian walked. Past the chandler's shop. Past the shrine where he'd knelt at nineteen, its little alcove cold and swept, the god's symbol worn to a suggestion. Past the tavern where he'd shaken Ines's hand for the last time before the night that ended everything, and where — he realized now, stopping dead in the middle of the empty street — he had sworn a second oath, a drunk and laughing oath, *partners till the roads run out, Veth witness it*, and Ines had laughed and sworn it back, and Veth had witnessed.

Two threads. His and theirs, twisted together, running downhill side by side into the dark.

If Ines went down the hollow ways — if they actually reached the vault, the lantern, the soul — then every chain on that soul was an oath, and his oath was one of them, and his loyalty was a link in the cage. He wasn't just failing to stop Ines. He was *part of the lock*. Everything in him that loved his old partner was being used, right now, tonight, as bar and bolt and binding.

Unless it was renounced. Unless the thread was cut —

— and cut it how, cut it *how*, when the cutting might mean the debt survived, the forfeiture clause woke, a nine-year-old girl in a lowland town became property?

He stood in the starless street with his brand burning cold and did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since the moment he'd lied to the Steward of Accounts. There was no version of this where he stayed clean. There was no version where he saved his family and his soul and his partner. There were only versions where he chose what to lose, and the losing started now.

Two days. Corvane wanted verification in two days, and the moon sealed in three, and Ines was already walking down.

Cassian Vole turned his back on the tithe houses, pulled his collar up against a wind that smelled of tallow and cold iron, and started up the mountain trail in the dark — moving fast, moving quietly, a man going to commit the only treason left to him: telling the truth.

Behind him, deep in the rock, the vast slow breathing went on. And he could have sworn — though he would never swear anything again, not in any name, not for any price — that on the last turn of the trail, just before the town dropped away below him, the pull in his chest changed direction. Faint as breath on glass. Like something far below had noticed him climbing away, and turned over, and begun, very slowly, to hope.
