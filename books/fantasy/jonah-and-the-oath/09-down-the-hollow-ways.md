## 9. Down the Hollow Ways

The door was where Odile said it would be, and that alone should have frightened me.

A slab of grey stone set into the cliffside above the drowned pass, no wider than a coffin stood on end, unmarked except for a ring of iron gone green with centuries of rain. I had crossed this stretch of Thornfell more times than I could count — had slept in its shadow, eaten in its shadow, bled in its shadow once when a scree slope took my footing and left me a rib short. And in all those years I had never seen the door. Not because it was hidden. Because I had never *looked*. Smugglers learn not to look at things that aren't theirs to see; curiosity is a tax you pay in blood out here. But Odile had looked, and Odile had found it, and now we stood before it in the blue hour before dawn, four of us and a lantern, waiting for something none of us could name.

Cassian pressed his palm flat against the stone. "Cold," he said. "Not mountain-cold. Colder."

"Underground-cold," Odile corrected him. She had her records-burned hands wrapped around her own lantern like a woman holding onto the last proof she'd ever existed. "Stone keeps the temperature of what's beneath it. This is keeping the temperature of somewhere very deep."

Wren put an ear against the rock the way children do, all ear and no caution, and then pulled back so fast they stumbled into me. "It's talking," they said. Their voice came out strange — flat, syllables clipped at the ends, the cadence I had learned to dread and love in equal measure since the rockfall. "It's saying the same thing over and over. It says *down*. It says *down*."

I put my own ear there. I am not a sensitive man. I have spent my life trusting rope and ledger and the honest weight of coin, and I have never once heard stone speak. But I heard this. A vibration, low and patient, below the threshold of sound but not of feeling — a pulse, almost, like a heart beating inside the mountain. And threaded through it, faint as a whisper under a door, something rhythmic. Words, maybe. Or the memory of words.

The iron ring turned without resistance. The door swung inward on hinges that should have been rusted solid and moved instead like they'd been oiled that morning, and cold air rolled out over us, carrying a smell I knew from somewhere I couldn't place: wet limestone and old smoke and something sweeter underneath, something like incense burned down to nothing, like a prayer that had finished a long time ago and left only the shape of itself behind.

Steps went down. Cut stone, each tread deep enough for a stride twice mine, worn smooth in the centers into shallow glassy basins by feet far larger than any human foot. The lantern light reached perhaps ten paces and gave up. Beyond that, dark.

"Well," Cassian said, and his voice had the brittle cheer of a man whistling past something. "After you."

I went first. Not bravado — habit. Someone walks point on every road, and on every road I've walked since I was fifteen years old, that someone has been me. My feet found the steps. My knees took the drop of each tread without complaint. And about thirty paces down, when the ceiling dropped low enough that Cassian had to fold nearly double and Odile turned sideways to protect her lantern flame, I did something I didn't decide to do.

I ducked.

Not bent — *ducked*, the way you duck under a beam you know is coming, the way your body flinches before your eyes find the obstacle. Except there was no beam. There was nothing. The passage ahead was clear for twenty feet at least. But my body had already committed, already lowered my head and shortened my stride, already braced for a blow that never came — and then, ten paces later, exactly where my body expected it, there *was* a beam. A lintel of black stone, carved with spirals, hanging at precisely the height my duck had cleared.

Odile saw it happen. She stopped walking. "Ines," she said carefully, the way you speak to someone standing on ice. "How did you know that was there?"

"I didn't," I said. Which was true, and also the most frightening lie I have ever told, because some part of me *had* known, and that part wasn't speaking to the rest of me.

* * *

The hollow ways do not behave like tunnels.

I have run contraband through caves and mines and abandoned quarries for most of my adult life. Underground is underground: it's dark, it's damp, it's indifferent, and if you're lucky it's empty. You navigate by counting steps and marking turns and leaving chalk where chalk will survive. The mountain doesn't care whether you live or die. That indifference is the whole compact between smuggler and stone, and I have honored it faithfully.

The hollow ways broke the compact within the hour.

The passages were too regular to be natural and too strange to be built — corridors that widened into vaulted chambers tall enough that our lanterns couldn't find their ceilings, then narrowed again into slots barely wider than my shoulders, and always, always following a logic I could feel but not articulate. The road never doubled back the way real tunnels do. It descended in long patient spirals, and at every third turning there was a niche cut into the wall, waist-high, its floor polished to a mirror finish, and in every mirror-niche our light reflected wrong — dimmer than it should be, delayed by a heartbeat, as though the reflection had to travel farther than the surface in front of us.

"Gods' roads," Odile said, finally, somewhere around the fourth hour. She said it quietly, reluctantly, like a doctor naming a disease. "This is what the old texts meant. The Wardens' archive had fragments — survey notes from the founding generation, before the order went quiet. They called these the god-paths. Roads made for beings who don't walk the way we walk." She touched one of the mirror-niches with two fingers. "Beings who travel by reflection. By resonance. Who need places to *stand* in the stone, the way we need places to rest."

"And the doors?" Cassian asked. We had passed three of them by then — full-height portals set into walls with nothing behind them, hinged stone slabs that opened onto solid rock. He'd checked. Of course he'd checked; he checked everything, compulsively, the way debt-bound men check their ledgers. "Doors that open on nothing. What kind of road has doors to nowhere?"

"A road for someone who can be *in* the rock," Wren said.

The child had been quiet for hours, which was new, and worrying. Wren talked constantly in the first days after the rockfall — small sharp observations, fragments of cadence, that name I didn't recognize spoken like a mother speaks a child's name, softly and often. But down here the talk had thinned. The pauses between words had stretched. When Wren did speak, the voice came out lower, slower, weighted at the bottom like a bell that had picked up silt. And the child walked differently too — smaller steps, frequent stops, a hand trailing along the wall not for balance but for contact, the way a drowning swimmer trails a hand along a hull.

At the fifth hour Wren sat down in the middle of the corridor and would not stand up.

"It's heavy here," Wren said, when I knelt beside them. Their face had gone pale in a way that wasn't illness, exactly, or wasn't only illness. "It's heavy like being underwater. Everything pushes down. The memories push down. I'm holding them and the mountain is holding them and we're getting close to where they live and they want to go home and I—" The sentence collapsed. Wren pressed both hands against their own sternum, hard, as though holding a door shut from the inside. "They're loud. They're so loud down here. All of them at once. Every prayer anyone ever said in Veth's name, they're all down here, and they know they're going home, and they keep pulling."

I lifted the child. Ninety pounds of mortal boy, or whatever Wren was, and they weighed nothing in my arms and everything in the air around us. I carried them for a while. Then Cassian carried them. Then Odile, who complained the entire time in the flat bitter voice of a woman who has recently burned her life's work and has decided complaining is the only ritual left to her, and whom Wren nonetheless seemed to find comforting, possibly because bitterness is at least an honest register, and everything else down here was registers we couldn't read.

We rested in one of the vaulted chambers, the ones whose ceilings we couldn't find. I laid Wren against my pack and fed them the last of the honeyed bread, and the child ate three bites and stopped, and stared up at the darkness above us, and said:

"There are stars down here."

There were not stars down there. I checked. I am a smuggler; checking is my profession. The chamber was sealed stone, black and absolute, and our two lanterns made islands of amber that died within arm's reach. But Wren pointed, insistently, at a patch of ceiling high above us, and described what they saw: a scatter of lights, dim, arranged in a pattern, and one of them brighter than the rest and slightly red, and they named it — named it the way sailors name stars, with a proper name, an old name, a name I had never heard and that Odile, who had memorized half a dead archive, had never heard either.

"The court keeps starlight," Odile said. "That's what the texts said. The Undercourt lit itself with stolen sky. If Wren can see it from here—" She stopped herself, the way she kept stopping herself, mid-assertion, mid-damnation. "If the child can see it, then the child isn't seeing with eyes."

"No," Wren agreed placidly, chewing. "I'm seeing with the part that used to be up there."

And then, because the child was tired and honesty comes easily to the exhausted, Wren added, in a voice suddenly, terribly young: "It's getting harder to be a boy. The god-part wants to go home so much. It's like being a dog on a chain that smells dinner. I'm trying to stay. I promised Ines I'd stay. But it's pulling harder every hour, and when we get there, I don't know if I'll be able to choose anymore." The child looked at me across the bread and the lantern light. "You should know that. Before we go further. You should know that I might not come back out of me."

I did know it. Some part of me had known it since the rockfall, since the moment I dug the child out of the rubble and they opened their eyes and spoke in a dead god's cadence. You do not carry a fragment of divinity in mortal flesh without paying freight. I had simply been refusing to do the arithmetic.

Down in the dark, with the mountain's pulse ticking through the floor and the stolen stars overhead that only Wren could see, I did the arithmetic.

* * *

Somewhere past the sixth hour, my body stopped asking permission.

It began small. My feet chose turns before my mind did — left here, right there, always correctly, always a half-step ahead of any reasoning I could produce. I told myself it was smuggler's instinct, the accumulated grammar of a thousand crossings, the way a sailor reads swell. But smuggler's instinct works on smuggler's roads, and these were not smuggler's roads. These roads predated the smugglers. These roads predated, Odile said grimly, the Wardens, the border, possibly the kingdom. And still my body read them fluently, the way it had read the salt-road shortcuts I could never explain knowing, the passes I'd found blind in fog, the cache-sites I'd dug at coordinates I'd never been given.

Then it got less small.

There was a junction where the corridor split three ways, and standing at the split I felt — not thought, *felt* — a pull toward the left-hand branch, physical, directional, like a current in water. And in the left-hand branch, forty paces along, there was a carved relief on the wall: a figure with too many joints, arms folded inward, kneeling. An offering, maybe. Or a prisoner. Below it, worn nearly to invisibility, a line of script in no alphabet I knew — and my hand rose to trace it before I decided anything, and my fingers followed the grooves in the right order, stroke after stroke, as though I had traced this exact line before, many times, until the sequence lived in my hand the way a knife-grip lives in a fighter's hand.

"What does it say?" Cassian asked, watching my fingers move with an expression caught between awe and alarm.

"I don't know," I said. "But I know where the strokes go."

Odile watched longer than the others. She had the trained eye of a woman who had spent decades reading wards, and wards are just writing that guards things, and she knew what fluent tracing meant. "Your body remembers this place," she said. "Or something in your body does. Ines — the oath-brand. May I?"

She turned my wrist to the lantern light. The brand — Veth's mark, the silent spiral, seared into me at seventeen when I swore my tithe-oath — had changed. I had felt it changing for days, a warmth, an itch, a sense of the scar tissue tightening like a knot drawing closed, and I had told myself it was the altitude, the cold, nothing. But in the lantern light the spiral was no longer a scar. It had fine lines running through it now, hair-thin, branching, a lattice — and the lattice was brightest at the center, and the center pulsed. Slowly. In time with the pulse in the stone. In time, Wren said later, unprompted, with the pulse of everything.

"You're not just reading the road," Odile said. "The road is reading you. Whatever they wove into that brand when you swore — it's waking up. It's *attuning*. The closer we get to the court, the more of you it will use."

"Use for what?"

"For finding the way. For opening what needs opening. For—" she hesitated, and I watched a woman who had already lost everything weigh how much truth I could carry, and decide I needed all of it. "For paying. Ines, listen to me. Oaths are currency down here. That's the whole architecture of the place. Lady Sable holds the god's soul with chains made of promises — every vow ever sworn in Veth's name is a link. Yours included. Mine, probably, though the Wardens swore through intermediaries, laundered it, made it deniable. And the deeper we descend, the more the mountain can feel the weight of what you're carrying. Your body knows these roads because your oath knows these roads. The god's soul is down there, and the leash runs from the vault to every sworn throat in the world above, and yours is one of the throats, and the leash is *taut*, and it is pulling you exactly the way it's pulling Wren."

I stood very still for a while after that, in the dark, in the cold, listening to the pulse.

Because she was right, and I had known it, and knowing it changed nothing about the fact that my feet still wanted to walk forward. That was the horror of it and also, I realized slowly, the strange comfort: the compulsion and the mission pointed the same direction. Sable held the leash. The leash ran to the vault. To free the god, we had to reach the vault, and the leash was hauling me toward the vault like a fish on a line, and all I had to do was stop fighting the hook and let it reel me in.

"That's her design," Odile said sharply, when I said as much aloud. "Don't mistake the trap for a map. The moment you stop resisting, you belong to the road. And the road belongs to her."

"Then we resist," I said, "and we arrive anyway. Both things. At the same time. Like walking against a river current and letting the river tell you where the ford is."

Cassian laughed — one breath, surprised out of him. "That's the most smuggler sentence I've ever heard."

"It's the only kind I know."

* * *

We slept in shifts in a side chamber that my body selected — again, without consulting me — a low rounded room off the main spiral, warm by underground standards, dry, with a floor of packed earth instead of stone. Later I understood why the room existed: it was shaped like the inside of a cupped hand, and gods, presumably, also need places to sleep. Mortals were never meant to find it. I found it the way water finds a crack.

I took second watch, after Cassian. I sat with the lantern trimmed low and listened to the mountain breathe, and I thought about the arithmetic again, the one I'd done over Wren's sleeping weight. The child might not come back out of themselves. The brand was waking, and every hour of descent wove it deeper into me. Odile carried guilt like ballast and called it experience. Cassian carried a contract with Sable's seal folded against his heart and hadn't yet decided whether it was a leash or a weapon. Four people, four burdens, descending a road built for something that was none of us.

And still — this was the part I kept returning to, the part that felt like wonder even as it cost — the road was beautiful. I had spent my life underground treating stone as obstacle, as cover, as commodity. I had never once considered that a tunnel could be *made with love*. But these ways were made with love, or with devotion, which is love that has learned discipline. Every surface bore the marks of care: edges softened where hands had worried them smooth over centuries, niches positioned so that a traveler resting in one would face the direction of travel, drainage channels cut with a patience that made human masonry look like children's scribble. Whoever built this wanted whoever traveled it to feel *held*. Even now, even corrupted, even serving a thief-queen's prison, the bones of the road remembered kindness.

Maybe that was Veth. Maybe the god had helped build its own cage, the way a bird helps build the aviary, because the alternative was no road home at all.

Near dawn — if dawn means anything eight levels down — Wren woke, and came to sit beside me without a word, and leaned their small hot head against my arm, and watched the lantern flame with me.

"Ines," the child said, after a long time. "Can I tell you something true?"

"Always."

"When we get close — tomorrow, maybe the day after — the pulling is going to get very bad. Louder than I can hold against. And I'll say things. God-things. Big things. Things that sound like commands, because that's how the memory talks, it doesn't know how to ask." Wren's fingers found my sleeve and gripped. "When I say them, don't obey me. Don't obey the voice. Only obey the boy. The boy is the promise I made. The boy wants to help you. The voice just wants to go home. Try to hear which one is talking."

"How will I know the difference?"

Wren smiled, and it was a child's smile, quick and crooked and entirely mortal. "You'll know. You've known me since the rockfall. You know my laugh. The voice doesn't laugh."

No, I thought. It wouldn't. Silence was the whole of Veth, in the end — a god that answered prayers without a word, that kept its counsel for centuries, that let a whole border country build its faith on the absence of speech. Of course the voice wouldn't laugh. Laughter was the boy's. Laughter was the mortal fragment, the piece small enough and warm enough to hide in a child's chest and survive.

I blew out the lantern and let the dark hold us, and the mountain pulsed beneath us like a heart deciding something, and far below — Wren said so, and only Wren could see it — the stolen stars burned on, one of them slightly red, waiting.

In the morning we went down. My feet knew the way. I let them lead, and I watched every step they took, and I trusted nothing, and I followed anyway.

An hour into the day's march, the corridor ended at a wall — and the wall was a door, the largest we had seen, twice a man's height, banded in metal that was neither iron nor bronze nor anything Odile could name, and across its face, in the script my fingers had traced in the dark, ran a single line of carving, fresh-cut, the grooves still sharp, the stone dust still pale in their bottoms.

Someone had carved it recently. Days ago. Perhaps hours.

My hand rose to read it, the way it had risen before, and this time, before my fingers touched the stone, Wren grabbed my wrist and stopped me, and the child's eyes went wide and old and afraid all at once, and Wren said, in the voice that didn't laugh:

"Don't read that one. That one isn't for you. That one is *about* you."
