## 10. The Court Beneath

The hollow ways ended at a door that was not a door.

It was a seam in the rock, thin as a knife's edge, and it breathed. I felt the exhalation against my face before I saw anything — warm air that smelled of candlewax and cut flowers and something older underneath, something like a cellar where wine had gone to vinegar a hundred years ago and no one had ever bothered to open it again. Ines stood before the seam with one hand flat against the stone, eyes closed, reading it the way they read every road: with their body, not their mind.

"It opens inward," they said. "It always opened inward."

"Always," Wren repeated softly, from my hip where the child rode wrapped in my spare cloak. "That's how you know it's a mouth."

I did not like that sentence. I liked even less that none of us laughed at it.

Behind us, Cassian Vole leaned against the passage wall with his arms crossed and his face arranged into the particular boredom he wore like armor. He had been arranging himself all day — since the flooded pass, since Ines had spared him and then, worse, forgiven him, since Wren had looked at him with those too-old eyes and said *your brand is a leash and you've been walking in circles at the end of it your whole life*. He hadn't spoken much since. But he had kept walking with us, and in the country I came from — the country of ledgers and ward-lattices and oaths written in triplicate — keeping walking was its own kind of confession.

"The map says the threshold ward is keyed to tithe-marks," I said, unrolling the vellum one last time by lantern-light. My hands were steady. I had spent thirty years making them steady; it was the only discipline the Wardens had ever taught me that still felt like mine. "Ines, yours will open it. Mine might. Cassian's will. Wren—" I stopped.

Wren looked up at me. "Wren isn't marked," the child said. "Wren *is* the mark."

The seam opened without anyone touching it.

* * *

Light came out first.

Not firelight, not lamplight — starlight. That was my first thought, absurdly professional, the ward-keeper in me cataloguing before the rest of me could scream: *that is starlight, that is the exact spectral signature of starlight, and there are no stars left in the sky above Thornfell.* It spilled through the widening seam in long pale shafts, silver-white with a faint blue bruise at the edges, and it moved. Real starlight doesn't move. This light drifted across the stone like water finding its level, pooling in the low places, sliding toward our feet as if it had been waiting for company.

Then the door finished opening, and I saw where the stars had gone.

The Undercourt lay below us in a bowl of living rock, and it was beautiful, and I want that recorded plainly, because everything that followed — everything I did and failed to do in the hours after — began with the fact that it was beautiful. A city built inside the mountain's hollow heart, tiered and terraced, every surface faced with mirror. Not glass. Mirror-silver grown straight out of the stone, walls and spires and stair-rails all polished to a depth that made the eye slide off and keep sliding, so that looking at the city was like looking down a well full of itself. And everywhere, caught in those ten thousand faces of silver, burned the stolen stars. They hung in the air between buildings like lanterns on invisible chains. They drifted down the canals in slow processions. They clustered thick as fruit over what looked like a market square, and beneath them the people of the court went about their evening, buying and selling and bowing to one another in light that had been torn out of heaven.

"There," Cassian said quietly behind me. His voice had lost its armor. "There's the sky."

"No," I said. "There's what someone did to the sky."

But even as I said it I felt the pull of it, the awful seductive gravity of the place, and I understood for the first time — truly understood, in my body — why the wards had pointed inward. You do not need walls to keep a prisoner. You need only make the prison lovelier than anywhere else, and let the prisoner's own eyes do the guarding.

We descended the stair cut into the bowl's rim, and the mirror-light rose around us like water.

* * *

The first noble we met wore the face of a young man I had buried.

His name had been Aldric Vane. He had been a tithe-sworn mercenary under contract to the Wardens' outer garrison, twenty-two years old, a farm boy from the valley settlements who swore Veth's oath because it paid better than farming and because everyone told him the silent god asked nothing but silence in return. He died of a fever in the winter of my fourth year of service. I signed the death record myself. I remembered the ink smudging because my hands had been cold.

He was standing at a balustrade overlooking a canal of drifting starlight, holding a cup of something dark, and he turned when we passed, and it was Aldric's face — not an impression, not a likeness, but the face itself, down to the small crooked tooth visible when he smiled at some joke I couldn't hear. His skin was perfect. Too perfect. Fever leaves marks; time leaves marks; this face had been taken from him somewhere between the fever and the grave, or perhaps *at* the grave, and whatever wore it now wore it the way a hand wears a glove.

He looked directly at me. There was nothing behind his eyes except a courteous, bottomless attention, like a well you could drop a coin into and never hear it land.

"Do you know him?" Ines murmured. They had seen me stop.

"I buried him." My voice came out flat, which was the only way I could get it out at all. "Fourteen years ago. I signed the paper."

"And now?"

"Now something is wearing him to a party."

Ines's jaw tightened. Wren's small hand found mine and gripped it, and the child said nothing, which from Wren was louder than screaming.

We walked on. I counted the faces after that — I couldn't help it, it was the ledger-keeper's reflex, the ward-keeper's inventory — and by the time we reached the first terrace I had recognized eleven of them. Eleven dead oath-sworn men and women, going about the court's business in bodies that had been harvested like fruit from the tithe houses. A woman with grey braids who had held a ferry license on the Salt Road for forty years. A boy who couldn't have been more than seventeen, still wearing the calluses of a spear-hand. An old sergeant whose funeral I had attended, whose widow I had personally delivered the condolence letter to, written in the standard form, stamped with the standard seal, promising that his oath had been honored and his soul had gone home to the silent god.

*His soul has gone home,* I thought, watching him laugh across a table of mirrored food. *Home is here. Home is a lantern in a canal.*

I had written that letter. I had believed it. I had been the institution's pen for thirty years, and the institution had been the Undercourt's glove, and every condolence I had ever delivered was a receipt for goods already sold.

"You're doing the thing," Cassian said quietly, falling into step beside me. "Where you go somewhere behind your own eyes and start writing reports."

"That's exactly what I'm doing."

"Don't. Not here. Here, the report writes you." He nodded, very slightly, at a mirrored wall beside the stair, where my reflection walked with us — except that my reflection was smiling, and I was not.

I stopped looking at mirrors after that. It didn't help. In a city made of them, not looking is just another way of being looked at.

* * *

We found the inn — if it was an inn; the building had no sign, only a doorway of black wood that stood open onto warmth and noise — because Ines knew it was there. Of course Ines knew it was there. That was the part of all this that frightened me more than the faces did, though I never said so: the way the roads of this place fit Ines like a glove fits the hand that broke it in. Ines moved through the Undercourt's streets with the unhurried certainty of a native, turning left where the alleys doubled back, pausing at corners a half-second before the patrol of masked servants glided past, and every time, a small crease appeared between their brows, the crease of a person catching themselves at something they don't remember learning.

"You've been here before," I said, once we were inside, at a corner table where the lantern-star overhead burned low and forgiving.

"No," Ines said. Then, honestly, the way they had learned to answer since Wren: "I don't know. Ask my feet."

Wren sat between us, chin propped on both hands, watching the room with the old, unchildlike attention. "Your feet remember," the child said, "because your feet were here when the bargain was struck. Everyone's feet were. That's what a mountain is. It's where the ground remembers what happened on it." The child's voice dropped, and for a moment the cadence shifted — that other voice, the deep slow one that came out of Wren like water out of a crack in a dam — and the nearest tables seemed to lean in without knowing why. "*They brought the god down these stairs singing. The god sang back. That was the last song.*"

The moment passed. Wren blinked, small again, and stole a piece of bread from Ines's plate.

It was while Wren chewed, while Ines watched the door with a smuggler's patience, that I saw her.

* * *

She was crossing the far end of the common room, moving with two other nobles toward the private stairs, and I knew her the way you know your own name — instantly, totally, with no gap for doubt. Maren Solde. First Warden of Thornfell. Keeper of the Lattice. The woman whose signature was on the founding charter of the order I had given my life to, whose portrait hung in the great hall at Cindermark with her hand resting on a closed book, whose maxims I had copied out as an apprentice until I could recite them in my sleep:

*The ward is a promise. The promise is the wall.*
*What we keep, keeps us.*
*No light goes out on our watch.*

She had vanished forty-one years ago. Official history said she had died sealing a breach in the deep wards — heroically, alone, her body unrecoverable, her name carved into the Roll of the Kept. Every apprentice learned her death date before they learned their own letters. I had lit a candle for her every year of my service. I had *prayed* to Veth for her soul's rest, in the god's silent name, and the prayer had fallen upward through the lattice I tended, through the wards she built, down into—

Down into here.

She was serving drinks.

That was the detail that broke something in me, more than the face, more than the impossibility. She carried a tray through the crowd of mirrored nobility with the smooth efficiency of long practice, and she poured dark wine into dark cups, and she bowed when thanked, and her movements had the particular economy of a servant who has done a task so many thousands of times that the self has worn away along the grooves of it. She was dressed beautifully. She was, I realized with a lurch, dressed in the formal grey-and-silver of a First Warden's ceremonial garb, altered — shortened, hemmed, fitted for service — the way one alters a uniform for a new posting. Someone had taken the highest honor the Wardens could bestow and turned it into a livery.

Her face was her own. That was the worst of it. All the others wore harvested faces, gloves and masks, but Maren Solde's face was hers, lined and sharp-eyed and unmistakable, which meant she had not been killed and copied. She had been *kept*. She had been kept for forty-one years, alive, aware, pouring wine in the court her wards had sealed shut, wearing her own honor as a servant's dress.

And then she looked up, across the crowded room, directly at me.

For a moment — one heartbeat, two — her eyes widened. Recognition, yes, but more than recognition. Something urgent, something straining, like a woman pressed against the far side of a window. Her gaze flicked to Ines. To Wren. Back to me. Her lips parted.

A noble at the next table raised one finger, lazily, without looking.

Maren Solde closed her mouth. She bowed — a small, precise, servile bow, executed flawlessly — and turned, and carried her tray up the private stairs, and was gone.

I was standing. I didn't remember standing. Ines's hand was on my arm, firm, holding me down or holding me together, possibly both.

"Odile." Low. "Say something."

"She tried to speak." My voice sounded like someone else's, someone reporting from a great distance. "Forty-one years in this place and she hasn't forgotten how to try. Do you understand? Everything else here has forgotten. The faces, the nobles, the things wearing the dead — they're content. They're *fed*. She's the only person in this entire city who is still fighting, and she's fighting with a tray in her hands, and she lasted one second before—"

"Before the leash pulled," Cassian said. He was staring at the private stairs. His hand had gone, unconsciously, to the oath-brand on his forearm, and his thumb was pressing hard against it, hard enough to leave a mark. "One finger. One lazy finger. That's all it takes. That's what mine would look like." He laughed, short and terrible. "Gods. I thought I came down here hunting Ines. I think I came down here to see what the end of my road looks like. Now I've seen it. It pours wine."

"We should follow her," I said.

"No," Ines and Wren said together, and then Ines glanced at the child, and something passed between them — that strange current, the fragment and the body that hid it — and Ines added, more gently: "Not tonight. Tonight we learn the rhythms. When the patrols turn, when the court sleeps, which doors pretend to be locked. Smuggler's work. Slow work." Their eyes went to the stairs Maren had climbed. "But we'll come back for her. That's a promise."

Wren looked up sharply. "*Don't* promise. Not here. Not in this city." The child's voice had gone very quiet and very serious. "Every promise made under this mountain is a thread, and Lady Sable holds every spindle. If you mean it — and you mean it, I can hear that you mean it — then say it plain, later, outside, where she can't tie a knot in it."

The room seemed, very slightly, warmer after that. Or colder. I couldn't tell anymore. The lantern-stars overhead burned on, serene and stolen, and somewhere above us — impossibly far above, beyond leagues of stone — the empty sky where the stars used to be went on being empty, and no one down here missed them, because down here the sky had been bottled and served.

I sat back down. I ate a little. I drank nothing. Across the room, mirrored walls returned the scene to itself a hundred times over: four travelers at a corner table, one dead child, one broken smuggler, one liar in a burnt cloak, and between them a silence full of everything we each owed and everything we each intended.

And on the private stairs, faint through the noise, I heard — or imagined I heard, though I have never since decided which, and I no longer believe the difference matters — a woman's voice, pitched low, singing three notes of a warding-hymn. The hymn every apprentice learns first. The one Maren Solde wrote.

*No light goes out on our watch.*

One light had gone out on hers. Forty-one years ago, and it was burning down here still, in a bottle, in a servant's dress, waiting for someone to come and break the glass.

Tomorrow night, I promised silently, to no one, in no god's name — because I would not give Sable another thread, not one — tomorrow night we would find the private stairs.

I did not yet know that the private stairs led to her audience chamber. I did not yet know that Lady Sable had been watching us since the door opened, courteously, patiently, the way a host watches guests she has been expecting for a very long time.

I did not yet know that she had sent Maren Solde across that room deliberately — bait on a hook, shaped exactly like my oldest grief.

But the summons came at midnight anyway, borne by a servant whose face I recognized from a funeral, bearing a card of black silver that read, in elegant script:

*Lady Sable requests the pleasure of the Oathbreaker's company.*
