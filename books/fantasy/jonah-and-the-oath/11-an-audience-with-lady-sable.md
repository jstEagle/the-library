## 11. An Audience with Lady Sable

They did not drag them in chains.

Ines had expected chains. Had braced for them through the last mile of the hollow ways, when the walls stopped being rough-hewn stone and became something older and more deliberate — basalt cut so fine it drank the lantern light, corridors wide enough for processions, for funerals, for gods being led to market. They had expected to be taken the way contraband was taken: bound, blindfolded, valued.

Instead, a door of black glass had simply opened, and four figures in grey silk had bowed — *bowed* — and gestured them through with the courtesy of innkeepers welcoming a long-expected guest.

Now Ines stood in an antechamber that made no sense and made perfect sense at once. The ceiling was high as a cathedral's, ribbed with veins of pale light that pulsed slow as sleeping breath. The floor was polished obsidian, and in it Ines could see their reflection walking beside them, except the reflection was always half a step behind, and sometimes it looked at things Ines hadn't turned toward yet. Wren's small hand was clamped in theirs, the child's palm fever-hot. Odile walked on their other side, jaw set like a woman attending her own trial. Cassian had been left above, under guard that was polite the way a knife is polite.

"They're going to offer you something," Odile murmured. "That's how the court works. It doesn't take. It *trades*. And every trade it makes, it wins."

"Then I'll refuse."

"You won't." Odile's voice was flat, tired, certain. "Nobody refuses down here. That's the first thing they teach ward-keepers, before they stop teaching us anything true. The Undercourt never asks for what you'd fight to keep. It asks for what you'd crawl to get back."

The doors at the end of the antechamber were silver, and they were already open.

* * *

The hall beyond was lit by stars.

Not real ones — Ines knew real stars, had navigated by them for fifteen years, had watched every one of them go dark three nights ago. These were caged lights, hundreds of them, drifting in glass spheres near the vaulted ceiling, and each one burned with a slightly different color, a slightly different temperature, the way no two fires are ever quite alike. Stolen starlight, Odile had called it, and standing beneath it Ines understood that she had meant it literally. Each little sphere held a piece of sky that some oath-sworn soul had once loved enough to remember perfectly. The court had harvested the remembering.

At the far end of the hall, on a dais of unpolished stone — deliberately humble, Ines thought, the humility of a thing too powerful to need ornament — sat Lady Sable.

She was smaller than Ines had imagined. That was the first wrongness. Everything about the Undercourt had been vast, oppressive, scaled for something inhuman, and here at the heart of it was a woman who might have run a counting-house in Thornfell's lower town: middle-aged, silver-streaked hair pinned simply, hands folded in her lap like a grandmother waiting for tea. She wore grey. Not the grey of mourning or the grey of servants. The grey of a mountain in fog, of a road at dusk, of all the colors between yes and no.

Her face was kind. That was the second wrongness, and the worse one.

"Ines Almeida," she said, and her voice carried without effort across the whole impossible length of the hall, warm as a hearth heard through a wall. "And Wren. And Odile Ferran, whom I have missed. Come up. My knees are old, even if nothing else about me is, and I dislike making guests stand at a distance like petitioners. You are not petitioners. You are *arrivals*."

Ines did not move. "We didn't come to be received."

"No," Sable agreed pleasantly. "You came to steal. Do sit down anyway. Theft goes better seated; there's less running involved."

There were chairs. Of course there were chairs — three of them, placed precisely where guests would want them, upholstered in something soft as moss. Ines looked at them the way they'd look at a suspiciously well-maintained bridge over a gorge. But Wren's weight was increasing against their hip, the child burning through whatever strength the god-memory gave them, and pride was a luxury priced beyond what Ines carried.

They sat. Odile remained standing, arms crossed, until Sable sighed and gestured, and a chair appeared beside Ines so smoothly that Odile flinched, then sat in it out of sheer spite.

"There," said Sable. "Now we can talk like civilized people, which is more than can be said for anyone on the surface these days. Do you know what I admire about you, Ines Almeida?"

"I couldn't guess."

"Punctuality." Sable smiled, and the smile reached her eyes, which somehow made it worse. "Every smuggler I've ever bought has been late. Late to meetings, late to payments, late to understanding what they'd become. But you — you arrived exactly when the court calculated you would arrive, by exactly the road we built for you to find, carrying exactly the burden we needed you to carry." She tilted her head. "Though I'll grant you brought one item off the manifest. The child. That was improvisation. I do enjoy improvisation. It's the only spice left."

Ines's hand found Wren's shoulder. Beneath their palm, the child trembled like a struck bell.

"You keep saying *we*," Ines said. "*Calculated.* *Built.* Speak plainly, my lady. What am I to you?"

"A courier," said Sable, "delivering yourself."

The words landed in the silence like a stone into deep water, and the ripples went out and out, touching everything.

* * *

Sable rose, and the caged stars dimmed respectfully, and she came down from the dais unhurried, her footsteps making no sound at all on the stone. Up close she smelled faintly of cold iron and old paper — the smell of contracts, Ines thought, the smell of every tithe-house ledger they'd ever signed against their name.

"You want Veth's soul," Sable said. "Of course you do. Every oath-sworn mercenary in those mountains wants it, in the way a dog wants the moon — vaguely, hopelessly, without any notion of what it would do if it caught it. But you actually came. Which means you believe it's possible. Which means the child told you things, and the Ferran woman showed you her map, and somewhere on the road down here you decided that you, a smuggler with a brand on your arm and coin-debts on your conscience, could walk into the oldest court under the world and walk out again carrying its crown jewel." She stopped an arm's length away. "I find that belief beautiful. Genuinely. It's the same belief your god had, once."

"Veth didn't believe anything," Ines said. "Veth was *sold*."

"Yes." Sable's eyes were very dark and very still. "By you."

The hall did not spin. Ines wished it would have; spinning would have been honest. Instead everything stayed perfectly fixed — the stars, the walls, the woman's kind terrible face — while the ground opened inside them, quiet as a trapdoor.

"That's a lie," Odile said sharply, rising. "That's the oldest trick in the—"

"It isn't a trick, Keeper. Tricks require effort." Sable raised one hand, palm up, and in it appeared a small object: a lens of smoked glass, ringed in silver, no larger than a plum. "It's a memory. Yours, specifically. Removed at your own request — though *request* is a generous word for what you were in no state to understand. The court keeps such things. We are, if nothing else, librarians." She turned the lens slowly, and light moved inside it like smoke. "I am going to show you thirty seconds of it. Not more. The whole of it would break you before the new moon, and I have no interest in broken goods. Thirty seconds. Then you may call me a liar to my face, and I will thank you for the exercise."

"Don't look," Odile said. "Ines. Whatever you think you owe yourself, don't—"

But Ines was already leaning forward. Because that was the truth underneath everything, the truth that had been waiting for them since the wards fell: not dread, not defiance, but *hunger*. Fifteen years of roads they knew without knowing why. A name Wren had spoken that fit their mouth like a key fits a lock they'd forgotten they owned. A god whose silence they had mourned like a parent's. Some part of them had been walking toward this room since before the mountain, and it was done pretending otherwise.

"Show me," Ines said.

* * *

The lens touched their forehead, and the world folded.

Cold. Snow on the pass above Thornfell, the bad winter, the one the songs still complained about. Ines saw the pass from outside themselves, from a height, as memory sees — and saw themselves kneeling in the snow, younger, thinner, blood freezing black along their side where a crossbow bolt had gone through. Around them, their crew: Maro with his arm bent wrong. Sefa gray-lipped, breathing in bubbles. Little Tam crying without sound because his throat was frozen shut. The storm coming down the mountain like a closing fist, and no shelter within a day's walk, and Ines dying, and knowing it, and doing the arithmetic every captain does at the end and hating every number.

Then the snow brightened. Not with dawn. With presence.

Veth stood in the storm the way a cliff stands in weather — unmoved by it, part of it, silent as the god had always been silent, wearing a shape like a tall figure wrapped in a traveler's cloak with no face under the hood, only a stillness that hurt to look at. And dying Ines, who had sworn their tithe-oath to that silence years before, who had paid and paid and paid, crawled forward through the snow and did not beg for their own life.

Ines watched themselves beg for the crew's.

Watched themselves offer everything — their memories, their name, their fifteen future years of roads and runs and loyalty — watched themselves say the words that no oath-tongue should know, the broker's words, the *sale* words. Watched the silent god bend down, and place one hand — if it was a hand — over the wound in their side, and Ines felt, watching, what dying-Ines had felt in that touch: not forgiveness. *Relief.* As if the god had been waiting years for someone to ask. As if a door long locked had finally been knocked upon from the inside.

And then the memory's thirty seconds ended on the image Ines knew would follow them past the grave: Veth turning from the saved crew, from the healed bodies, from the child breathing again, and walking willingly down into the dark of the hollow ways, cloak trailing in the snow, while above it young Ines slept and forgot and woke owning a life they had traded away without ever knowing they'd held the deed.

* * *

The hall came back. The stars. The smell of cold iron. Sable watching them with her hands folded, patient as erosion.

"Well?" she said softly.

Ines discovered they were on their feet. They had no memory of standing. Their legs held; mercenary legs hold on principle. Their voice, when it came, came from very far away.

"The crew lived."

"All of them. Maro's arm healed crooked but he swings a hammer with it now. Sefa runs a ferry on the Vell. Tam—" Sable paused, delicately. "Tam is a ward-keeper's apprentice. He shows promise. You might meet him someday, if things go differently than either of us plans." She let that sit. "You bought four lives with your past, Ines Almeida. The court merely held the collateral. Can you truly say the trade was evil? I've seen trades far worse made for far less, and made by people who kept their memories and their sleep."

"It wasn't yours to hold."

"No," Sable agreed. "It was Veth's. And Veth *gave* it — that's the part your keeper's histories omit. Your god could have refused you. Gods refuse constantly; it's most of what they do. Instead it took your bargain and walked into our vault like a bridegroom, and it did it to spare you the guilt of knowing what you'd done. So you see, the leash runs both directions, and always has. You sold a god, and the god let itself be sold, and now you've come to rob the buyer." Her smile returned, gentle as frost forming. "I could almost let you. Sentiment is a weakness I'm told I should cultivate."

Wren made a sound. Small, cracked, furious. The child had come off the chair and stood between Ines and Sable, swaying, burning, and when they spoke it was in that cadence that was not a child's cadence, the cadence Ines had heard once in a dream of snow:

"*She lies by telling truths.*"

"Oh, certainly," Sable said warmly, bending to the child's level, and Ines's hand went to a knife that wasn't there. "That's the family trade, little fragment. But tell me which part is false. Go on. You carry his memory — you must know. Did he not choose the vault? Does the memory lie about the snow?"

Wren's mouth worked. No answer came. And Ines understood, with a lurch, that the child didn't know either — that the fragment of god-memory burning inside that small body held Veth's love but perhaps not Veth's reasons, and that Sable had chosen her truths the way a surgeon chooses incisions.

"So," Sable straightened, brushing invisible dust from her grey skirts. "Here is my offer, and I will make it only once, and then we will speak of other things, unpleasant things, things involving your friend Mr. Vole and the uses a court has for a man who hates himself. I will return your memories entire. All of them — the sale, the years after, the name you buried, everything you have been crawling toward since the wards fell. You will be *whole*, Ines Almeida, for the first time since that pass. Whole people make such excellent decisions." She extended her open hand, and above her palm a counter-oath began to form out of the air itself, silver script coiling like smoke, waiting for a voice. "All it costs is a word. Sworn to the court, in the court's style — the style you used once before, on a mountain, in the snow. You already know the grammar. Your mouth remembers."

Ines stared at the coiling script. It did look familiar. That was the worst of it — the shapes of it sat in their mind like a language half-remembered from childhood, and some muscle behind their tongue ached to pronounce it.

Behind them, Odile said quietly, "If you swear it, the brand stops being a leash and becomes a root. You'll never leave. None of us will."

Ahead, Wren looked up at them with eyes gone silver-bright and exhausted, and said nothing at all, in a silence that sounded exactly like a god's.

And Ines Almeida, who had spent fifteen years being loyal to a silence, stood in a hall of stolen stars holding a choice shaped like a mirror — and felt the ground of everything they called themselves begin, gently, terribly, to dissolve.

"My lady," they heard themselves say, and their voice was steady, and they had no idea yet what was going to come out of it next, "before I swear anything — show me the rest."

Sable's smile widened by a single degree.

"There it is," she said. "The hunger. I *did* enjoy improvisation." She closed her empty hand, and the counter-oath dissolved, and the caged stars overhead all dimmed at once, as if the whole court leaned in to listen. "Very well. But the rest, courier, is not given free. The rest has a price, and the price is time — and the new moon is in two days."

Somewhere far above them, muffled through a hundred fathoms of stone, a bell began to toll.
