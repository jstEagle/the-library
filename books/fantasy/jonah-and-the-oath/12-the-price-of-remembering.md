## 12. The Price of Remembering

The corridor Sable's servants left them in was long and white and wrong. Not the white of snow or salt or bone — those whites Ines knew, had slept against, had bled into — but the white of a page before anything is written on it. The walls gave back no echo. Their footsteps fell as if into wool. Even Wren's small hand in theirs made almost no sound.

Ines walked because walking was the only thing their body still trusted itself to do.

*You were the one who brokered Veth's sale.*

The words kept rearranging themselves inside their skull, trying to find a shape that could be lived with. They could not. Every arrangement collapsed under its own weight, like a bridge built over nothing.

They had seen it. That was the worst of it — not told, not accused, *shown*. A glimpse, a single heartbeat of memory poured into them like molten lead: a younger pair of hands, hands with Ines's scars already half-formed on them, pressing a seal into wax. A map unrolled on a table lit by tallow. A voice — their own voice, hoarse with desperation — saying words Ines could not now recall but whose meaning sat in their chest like swallowed stone: *here, take it here, only let them live.*

And behind the memory, at its edge, something vast and silent watching. Not struggling. Watching. As if the god had already agreed.

"No," Ines said aloud, to the white corridor, to no one. "No. That isn't— I wouldn't."

"Wouldn't you?" Wren asked.

Ines looked down. The child walked beside them with their hands folded behind their back like an old man taking air outside a chapel, which was a thing Wren did sometimes, wore other people's postures like borrowed coats. Their eyes were gray as river fog and far too old for the face they sat in.

"I don't know," Ines admitted, and the admission cost more than any blade ever had. "That's the whole of it, Wren. I don't know what I would have done. I don't even know who I was before the oath. Everything before Thornfell is—" They groped for the word. "Fog. Warm fog. I remember being cold once, and someone laughing, and the smell of pitch burning on water. That's all. Three scraps. And now she offers me the rest of it, wrapped in her counter-oath, and I can't even tell if what she showed me was true."

"It was true," Wren said quietly.

Ines stopped walking. The corridor stretched away in both directions, featureless, patient. "How would you know?"

Wren tilted their head, and for a moment the child-ness fell away entirely, the way cloud falls away from a mountain peak, and what looked out through those gray eyes was older than the mountain, older than the salt road, older than the first oath ever sworn in a name that had since gone quiet. Then it folded back down again, and Wren was a child once more, scuffing one bare foot against a floor that refused to make a sound.

"Because I was there," they said. "Not seeing it. *Being* there. I'm the part of him that remembers being taken." They said it without drama, the way a child reports that it rained on their birthday. "I remember the table. The wax. Your hands were shaking so hard the seal came out crooked. He noticed that. He thought it was — " Wren searched for the word, frowning. "Kind. That you were ashamed. He decided that mattered more than the betrayal."

"That's insane," Ines said. "That's — no god thinks like that."

"You've met so many?"

Ines laughed, and the laugh came out broken, a hinge rusting. They pressed the heel of their hand against their eyes until sparks bloomed in the dark there. "I need it to be a lie, Wren. Do you understand? If it's a lie, then I'm just a smuggler who got in over her head. If it's a lie, I can hate her cleanly and walk out of this mountain and never think about it again. But if it's true—" Their voice cracked down the middle. "If it's true, then everything I am is built on top of the worst thing anyone has ever done. Every mile I ran for him. Every tithe I paid. All of it bought with thirty pieces of somebody else's silver. *My* silver. I sold him, and then I spent years loving him for the privilege of having done it."

They hadn't meant to say all that. It came out anyway, the way blood comes out of a wound you keep insisting is closed.

Wren took their hand. Small fingers, warm, chapped at the knuckles from the rockfall. The child guided them onward down the white corridor, because standing still in Sable's house was a kind of dying, and Wren seemed to understand that instinctively.

"Sit," Wren said, when they reached an alcove where a bench had been set — set deliberately, Ines realized, the way furniture is set in a trap. They sat anyway. Wren climbed up beside them, legs swinging, heels drumming silently against the wood.

"Odile says the Wardens' records were burned," Ines said after a while. "She says Sable's people fed the Wardens lies for years. Cassian says his contract was written in ink he wasn't allowed to read. Everyone in this mountain keeps their truth locked where I can't reach it. So how do I verify a single thing she showed me? I can't. She owns every witness. She owns the wax and the seal and the table. For all I know she grew that memory in a jar somewhere and poured it into my head like wine." They stared at the blank wall. "There's no floor anywhere. There's nothing to stand on."

"There's me," Wren said.

Ines turned to look at them.

"I'm evidence," Wren said simply. "I'm the piece she couldn't buy, because I was hidden before she started buying. He hid me himself, right at the end, when the chains were going on. He took the part of himself that remembered being free and he put it somewhere she'd never think to look." A small, fierce smile. "Inside a child nobody wanted. On the salt road. With the smugglers." The smile widened a fraction. "With *you*. He knew you'd come back for the roads. You always come back for the roads."

"Wren—"

"He hid me with the person who betrayed him," Wren went on, relentless, gentle, terrible. "Do you want to know why? Because he wasn't hiding me from Lady Sable. He was hiding me from *you* — from the version of you that would find out too late and eat herself alive. He wanted you to find me slowly. He wanted you to have time." Wren swung their legs once, twice. "Gods are strange about time. They have so much of it that they spend it like poor people spend coins. Carefully. On purpose."

Ines found they were crying. It happened distantly, the way weather happens to a country you're only passing through. Tears ran down their face and dripped off their jaw and made no sound on the white floor, because nothing in this place made sound, because Sable had built a house out of silence and Ines was beginning to suspect the silence had been purchased from the same seller as everything else.

"I can't do it," they whispered. "Whatever it is she wants me to choose — I can't. If I take her counter-oath, I get myself back, and the person I get back might be someone I can't bear. And if I refuse, I stay this — this hollow thing, this smuggler with three memories and a brand, and I go down into whatever's under this court carrying nothing but muscle and spite." They wiped their face with their sleeve, roughly, angrily. "Either way I lose. She built it so either way I lose. That's what she does, isn't it? That's what the whole Undercourt is. A house where every door is a debt."

"Yes," Wren said.

Ines blinked. "You're supposed to argue with me."

"I'm supposed to tell the truth. You keep mixing them up." Wren scooted closer, until their small shoulder pressed against Ines's arm. "Yes, she built it so you lose either way. Yes, you can't verify anything. Yes, maybe you sold him. Maybe worse. Maybe when you get your memories back you'll find out you did ten things worse than selling him, and maybe you'll find out you did none of them, and maybe you'll never find out at all." The child shrugged, a small economical motion. "None of that is the choice."

"What is, then?"

"The choice is what you do next." Wren said it the way Odile recited ward-formulas — flat, precise, each word load-bearing. "He taught me that. Or — I remember him teaching it, which is nearly the same thing. He used to say: mortals think faith means believing a story is true. It doesn't. Faith is believing a story is *worth continuing*. Anyone can believe a thing that's proven. The oath was never about whether you were good, Ines. He never cared if you were good. He cared what you did with your hands in the next hour."

Ines sat with that. The white walls offered no comfort, no distraction, nowhere to look except at the child beside them and the fog where their own past should have been.

"If I brokered his sale," they said slowly, working it through like a knot in rigging, "then I'm the reason he's hanging in a lantern somewhere under this mountain. And if I didn't, then I'm just a tool Sable is using to feel guilty enough to swear her oath. Either way she wins by making me stand still." They flexed their branded hand. Beneath the skin, the oath-mark pulsed faintly, warm as a banked coal, pointing — it had always pointed — toward the deep places. Toward him. "So the only move that costs her anything is the one where I stop caring which version is true and start moving toward the vault."

Wren beamed at them, and for one unguarded instant the grin was pure child, gap-toothed and sunlit, and Ines's chest ached with a tenderness so sharp it felt like another kind of grief. Somewhere in the fog of their forgotten life, they suspected, they had loved someone like this. Someone small. Someone who grinned at them across a fire while pitch burned on water.

"There it is," Wren said. "See? You didn't need the memories. You needed the next hour."

"Don't get smug. You're eight."

"I'm a fragment of a god wearing an eight-year-old. Smugness is structural."

Despite everything — despite the white corridor and the trap-bench and the lead weight of the memory sitting in their chest — Ines laughed. It came out ragged and wet and real, the first true sound the corridor had heard, and the walls seemed to flinch from it, as though Sable's perfect silence had never accounted for the possibility of laughter.

Then the flinching stopped being metaphor.

The corridor shuddered. Far off — below them, beneath them, down through miles of stone — something groaned like a ship changing tack in a storm. The white walls flickered, and for half a heartbeat Ines saw through them: black rock veined with pale light, stairs descending into a dark that breathed. Then the illusion snapped back, seamless, but the sound remained, rolling up through the floor and into the bench and into Ines's bones.

Wren had gone rigid. Their small hand clamped around Ines's fingers hard enough to hurt.

"He felt that," the child whispered. All the smugness was gone; what remained was fear, and beneath the fear, a terrible hope. "Down there. In the lantern. Something touched him — or something near him moved — and he *felt* it, and I felt him feeling it, and—" Wren's breath hitched. "Ines. The new moon. We counted wrong. It's not days away anymore. It's *hours*. She's pulling the sealing forward. She knows we're coming, and she's closing the doors early."

The corridor's white light dimmed, guttered, steadied. Somewhere ahead, a door that had not been there before now stood open at the corridor's end — tall, black, patient, breathing cold air that smelled of wet stone and old starlight.

Beyond it, faintly, came the sound of many feet. The court's nobles, converging. Coming to see what the traitor-smuggler would choose.

Ines stood. Their legs held. Their brand burned steady, pointing down, pointing home.

"All right," they said to Wren, to the door, to the fog where their past lived. "Next hour, then."

* * *

They found Odile and Cassian waiting in a junction chamber three turns on — a round room of raw black stone, the only honest architecture Sable's palace had shown them yet, as though the mountain itself were surfacing through the court's paint. Odile stood over a spread of papers, her burned replacement maps, muttering corrections from memory. Cassian leaned against the wall with his arms crossed and his face doing a poor job of looking unconcerned.

Both of them read Ines's expression in a heartbeat. Smugglers and ward-keepers, Ines reflected, were trained in the same language.

"She told you," Cassian said. Not a question.

"She showed me." Ines crossed to the center of the room. The brand on their hand pulsed with each step, faster now, eager. "She says I sold him. She showed me the moment. Wax, seal, my own voice." They held up a hand before either could speak. "I can't prove it. Neither can she. Every record in this mountain belongs to her. So I've decided to stop trying."

Odile's brow furrowed. "That's either wisdom or surrender."

"It's the next hour," Ines said. "It's all any of us have. Ask the god — oh, wait." A ghost of their old crooked smile. "Ask Wren, then."

Wren, perched on a stone shelf, raised one small hand solemnly. "Confirmed."

Cassian pushed off the wall. His jaw worked for a moment, and when he spoke, his voice was rougher than usual. "For what it's worth — I've known you fifteen years, Almeida. Before the oath, even. And I remember a woman who wouldn't sell a *horse* she'd promised to someone, let alone a god." He hesitated. "But I also remember you came to Thornfell running from something, and you never said what, and you paid tithe like a man paying blackmail. So maybe both things are true. Maybe that's just what people are."

Ines looked at him. Old partner. Old wound. "When did you get wise?"

"Bought it same place I bought everything else. On credit." He jerked his chin toward the passage leading down. "Floor plans are current as of yesterday. Vault's two levels below the tithe houses, behind a door that answers to oath-brands. Which means—" he tapped his own marked wrist, grim, "—we don't pick it. We knock."

"And she'll be expecting us," Odile said quietly. She rolled her maps, tied them, tucked them into her coat like a mother bundling a child against cold. "A sealing pulled forward. Doors closing early. This isn't a heist anymore, Ines. It's a race, and she's chosen the track."

Below them, through miles of breathing stone, the mountain groaned again — longer this time, nearer, like something vast turning over in its sleep. Or waking.

Or being woken.

Wren slid off the shelf and took Ines's hand without asking, the way they always did now, and Ines found they no longer needed to know who they had been before the mountain in order to know what to do with the hand they had now.

"Then we run," Ines said, and stepped toward the dark.
