## 13. Cassian's Bargain

The tithe houses had a smell Cassian Vole would carry to his grave: hot copper and old incense, the sweetness of it cloying over something underneath that was not sweetness at all. He had walked out of that district three nights ago with his brand humming against his wrist like a wasp in a jar, and he had not slept since. Sleep, he suspected, was a luxury the debt-bound were never meant to reclaim.

He found them camped in a hollow beneath a shelf of black stone, where a dead stream had cut a channel through the mountain's flank and left behind a floor of smooth grey pebbles. A small fire, banked low. Odile Ferran sat with her back to the rock and her crossbow across her knees, which was how she did everything — as if apology were a weapon she'd forgotten how to load. The child Wren slept against Ines's side, breathing in a rhythm too slow for any living child, and Ines sat awake beside her, sharpening a knife they didn't need to sharpen.

Cassian stood at the mouth of the hollow for a long moment before they noticed him, or before they let him believe they hadn't noticed. The mountain air tasted of iron and coming snow. Somewhere far below them — deeper than any mine he'd ever run contraband through — something vast turned over in its sleep, and the pebbles under his boots shivered.

"You're supposed to be hunting us," Ines said, without looking up from the blade.

"I am." He came forward anyway, hands loose at his sides, palms out. An old smuggler's courtesy: *nothing in my sleeves.* Though with Cassian there had always been something in his sleeves. "That's rather the problem."

Odile's crossbow came up an inch. Not aimed. *Considering.*

"Give me one reason," she said, "why I shouldn't put a bolt through your throat and save Lady Sable the postage."

"I can give you several," Cassian said. "But you won't like any of them, because none of them are about you." He lowered himself onto a pebble across the fire, deliberately slow, deliberately visible, and let the silence stretch until even Ines looked up. "They're about me."

* * *

He told it plainly, because he had learned — late, and at cost — that plainness was the only currency worth anything in a mountain full of counterfeiters.

He told them about the summons: the grey-cloaked agents who had come to his safehouse in Lowmark with his family's ledger in one hand and a writ of erasure in the other. Three generations of Vole debt, contracted down through his grandmother's gambling, his father's failed freight concern, his own disastrous first run — every mark of it, gone, wiped clean as a slate held to rain, if only he brought them Ines Almeida alive.

He told them about the tithe houses. About the rows of oath-sworn mercenaries lying on stone biers with their brands glowing like banked coals, their strength siphoned off in thin silver threads that ran down into the dark, feeding something. Feeding *someone.* He described the sound they made — not screaming, worse than screaming: a low, contented murmur, like sleepers dreaming of home — and he watched Ines's face go carefully, terribly still.

"And you reported to these agents," Odile said. "Three days ago. What did you tell them?"

Cassian met her eyes. She had the kind of stare that could take a wall apart brick by brick and bill you for the mortar.

"I told them I'd cornered you at the drowned chapel," he said. "That you had wards I couldn't crack and a hostage child. That I needed four days to starve you out." He shrugged. "It bought time. It also means when I don't deliver, they'll know exactly what I am. There's no walking that back, Ferran. I've burned the bridge behind me, and I did it standing on it."

"Why?" Ines asked quietly.

And there it was. The question he'd carried up three switchbacks and through two frozen passes, turning over and over like a stone in a riverbed until all its rough edges wore smooth enough to speak aloud.

"Because I saw what my brand actually is," he said. "I've worn it eleven years. Swore my oath to Veth at nineteen because everyone swore to Veth, because that's what you did — you gave the silent god your loyalty and the god kept your roads safe and your contracts honest, and nobody ever asked what the god got out of it." His hand drifted to his wrist, to the scar-pale sigil beneath the wrapping. "Except now I know. The god gets *drained.* Every promise I ever made in Veth's name is a thread in some lantern down there, and Lady Sable holds the spool. My loyalty isn't mine. It's inventory."

Wren stirred against Ines's side but did not wake. In the firelight the child's face had the waxy translucence of someone burning fuel no body should contain, and Cassian felt again the crack that had opened in his certainty the moment he'd seen her — the moment he'd understood that the thing Sable wanted him to drag back to the tithe houses was a *child*, whatever else she might also be.

"My family's debt gets erased either way," he went on. "If I deliver you, Sable keeps her word — she's scrupulous that way, it's how monsters stay in business. If I don't deliver you, and she finds out why..." He smiled without any humor in it. "Well. Debts don't matter much to the dead. But here's the thing, Almeida. Here's the whole thing, laid flat."

He reached inside his coat — Odile's crossbow snapped fully up, and he froze, and moved again with exaggerated care — and drew out a folded square of oilskin, unwrapped it, and spread it flat between them.

Floor plans. Inked in his own cramped hand, drawn from memory over three sleepless nights: the Undercourt's upper galleries as Sable's agents had sketched them for his benefit, the approaches they'd planned for him to use, the guard rotations, the names of the door-wardens and the hours of their tithes. And beneath those, in a different ink, everything he'd pried loose that they hadn't meant him to have — the vault antechamber, the sealed gallery called the Mirrorwalk, the drainage ways that ran beneath the court like veins beneath skin.

"Sable's people gave me the parts I needed to hunt you," he said. "I memorized the parts they didn't know they showed me. Every corridor, every gate, every shift change. I'm giving it to you. Free. No contract, no counter-oath, no conditions." He pushed the oilskin across the pebbles toward the fire. "You want to know my price? My price is that there isn't one. I want my name cleared by *act*, not by ink. I've spent my whole life signing things — contracts, confessions, other men's names on manifests. One more signature and I'm just the same man with cleaner paper. So no. No bargain. Take the maps and leave me here if you want. Take the maps and put a bolt in me after, Ferran, if that's what trust costs these days. But I'm done trading."

The fire popped. Far below, the mountain breathed.

* * *

Odile picked up the oilskin before anyone else could touch it, which surprised no one. She read it the way she read everything — twice, once for content and once for traps — her lips moving slightly, her thumb pressing hard against the edge of the paper as though testing whether it might lie.

"The drainage ways are wrong," she said at last.

"They're not wrong, they're *old.*" Cassian leaned forward. "Sable's surveyors redraw them forty years ago to route around the Mirrorwalk. But water doesn't care about redrawings. Ask any smuggler — the mountain remembers its real shape, and if you follow the damp instead of the map—" He stopped. Something crossed Odile's face, quick and involuntary, and he realized he was preaching to the one person in the hollow who already knew this. "Right. You're a ward-keeper. You probably wrote half the redrawings."

"I audited them," Odile said coldly. "Which is how I know the originals weren't drawn for human feet." Her eyes lifted to his, and there was something in them he hadn't expected — not trust, nothing so generous, but a grudging recalculation, the look of a woman revising a ledger entry she'd already inked. "These are good. That's what troubles me. Good enough that I can't immediately prove they're bait."

"If Sable wanted to feed you false plans," Cassian said, "she'd have fed them to me wrapped in a better story than *hunt your old partner.* She thinks I'm stupid, Ferran. That's my one advantage. Everyone has always thought I was stupid — the charming Vole boy, all grin and no ledger — and I have spent thirty years letting them think it while I memorized everything." He spread his hands. "Check them against whatever you know. Check them against the child's dreams, if she dreams true. I'm not asking you to believe me. Belief is another contract, and I told you — I'm done with contracts."

"You'll forgive me," Odile said, folding the oilskin with precise, hostile neatness, "if I keep this somewhere you can't reach it."

"Ferran." Ines's voice was quiet, but it changed the temperature of the hollow. They had been silent so long that Cassian had almost forgotten they were more than witness; now they set the knife down, finally, and looked at him across the small fire with an expression he had seen perhaps twice in all the years they'd run together — once before a doomed job in the Salt Road caves, once the night Ines had pulled him, drunk and weeping, out of the Lowmark canal. Recognition. The terrible, unsentimental mercy of someone who knows exactly what you are.

"Cassian Vole has double-crossed me exactly once," Ines said. "In '41, the Kestrel job. He took the payment and ran, and I spent a winter eating snow and hating him for it." Cassian flinched; Odile's eyebrows rose. "What I didn't know then — what I found out two years later, from a letter he never knew reached me — was that the Kestrel's client had put a contract on *my* life, and Cassian took the money and vanished so the client would believe the job was done and call off the knife." Ines turned to Odile. "He let me hate him for two years because the truth would have put me in danger. That's the kind of coward he is. The kind who takes the ugly road so someone else doesn't have to."

"That's not vouching," Odile said. "That's an indictment with a bow on it."

"It's both." Ines's gaze came back to Cassian, steady and unblinking. "I'm not telling Odile you're trustworthy. I don't know that you are. Trustworthy is a thing you prove going forward, not backward. I'm telling her that when you break faith, it's because breaking faith was the faithful act — and that I've bet my life on that arithmetic before, and I'm still here." A pause. The firelight moved on their face like weather. "Also, you owe me a winter of snow, Vole. Consider the maps a down payment."

Cassian laughed — a short, cracked sound, startled out of him, the first laugh in longer than he could measure. "You always did keep accounts."

"Somebody in this partnership had to."

* * *

They talked until the fire burned to embers: routes and rotations, the timing of the new moon, the hours when the court's nobles grew sluggish and the hours — worse — when they grew hungry. Odile annotated the oilskin margins in her small fierce handwriting, correcting two of Cassian's distances and conceding, with visible displeasure, that his memory of the guard rotations was better than her records had ever been. Wren woke once, briefly, and looked at Cassian with eyes that were old and kind and utterly wrong in a child's face, and said, "You brought the map of the way down. Good. She's afraid of the way down." Then the child slept again, and none of them discussed it, because none of them could.

Near dawn, Odile rose to take the watch, and paused beside Cassian on her way past. For a moment he thought she meant to say something — an apology, maybe, or at least a truce. Instead she pressed two fingers briefly to her own brand, then to his, ward-keeper's shorthand, an old gesture meaning *same chain, same weight,* and walked away without a word. It was not forgiveness. It was, he understood, the closest thing to forgiveness her hands remembered how to make.

Cassian stayed by the embers and watched the darkness above the hollow, where the sky should have been. No stars. Only a smooth, starless black, like a lid on a pot, like a page on which someone had written nothing at all. Somewhere beneath his boots, a god hung in chains made of promises, and every hour that passed was an hour off the calendar of the world.

Four days, he had bought them. Two were gone.

He was still counting when Ines spoke from across the ashes, softly, so as not to wake the child.

"There's a door in the drainage ways," they said. "Below the Mirrorwalk. Wren's been dreaming about it — a door that only opens for someone who owes nothing." Their eyes caught the ember-light, and held him. "Cassian. When we go down tomorrow — walk last. Walk behind all of us. Because if that door needs a free man to open it..."

They didn't finish. They didn't have to.

Cassian Vole looked at the starless dark and understood, at last, what he had purchased with his name: not safety, not absolution, but a place at the front of a losing fight — and the door at the bottom of the world, waiting to see whether he was the man he'd claimed to be.
