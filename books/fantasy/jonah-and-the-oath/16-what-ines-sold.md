## 16. What Ines Sold

They did not bind Ines when they took her.

That was the worst of it, at first — the courtesy of it. Two of the court's servants, their faces smooth as river stones and just as anonymous, walking her down a corridor of black glass while her hands hung loose at her sides, unchained, unneeded. She could have run. She knew it and they knew it, and the knowing was the chain. Somewhere behind her, Odile was screaming words that the walls drank before they could become sound. Cassian's name had been in her mouth a moment ago — *run, take Wren, go* — and then the corridor had folded like paper, and now there was only this: the soft click of her own boots on glass, and ahead, a door that opened before she reached it.

The room beyond was small. That surprised her. She had imagined the regent's chambers as vast — cathedrals of mirror and stolen light — but this was almost humble. A low table. Two chairs. A single lamp burning with a flame that gave no heat and cast no shadow, which meant it was not fire at all but something older wearing fire's shape. On the table sat a bowl of water so still it looked cut from the mountain itself.

Lady Sable stood at the window, if you could call it a window, looking out at nothing.

"You came all this way," she said, without turning. Her voice was warm the way a hearth is warm after you've walked out of a snowfield — the kind of warmth that makes you forget the snow will kill you anyway. "Through my wards, past my tithe houses, with a god's ghost on your shoulder and a keeper's map in your pocket. I confess I expected you sooner."

"Where are they?" Ines said. "Odile. Cassian. The child."

"Alive. Unharmed. For values of alive and unharmed that apply beneath Thornfell." Sable turned then, and Ines made herself look at her face — the face from the audience hall, the courteous one, the one that had offered her everything she'd ever lost. It hadn't changed. That was the trick of these people, she thought. They never changed. Change was for things that lived.

"You're going to kill me," Ines said.

"I'm going to show you something." Sable gestured to the bowl. "Killing is easy. I have nobles in my court who collect deaths the way surface-folk collect pressed flowers. But understanding—" she smiled, and it was almost gentle, "—understanding is rare down here. Sit."

Ines did not sit. She stood over the bowl with her arms crossed and her oath-brand cold against her ribs, colder than it had ever been, cold as a coin left overnight in winter grass. "Whatever you're about to do," she said, "it won't work. I know what I am. I've known since the pass, since the hollow ways, since every road my feet remembered without asking me first. You want me to doubt myself. I'm done doubting."

Something flickered across Sable's face — not anger. Something more tired than anger.

"Child," she said, "I have ruled this court for longer than your language has had a word for *doubt*. Do you truly believe I would waste an hour of the last night of my century trying to *unsettle* you?" She touched the rim of the bowl, and the water shivered though no wind reached it. "I am going to give you back what was taken from you. All of it. Not the glimpse I showed you at our meeting — not a taste, not a taunt. The whole meal. Every hour. Every choice." Her eyes lifted to Ines's, and for the first time Ines saw something in them that frightened her more than malice would have: patience. Ancient, bottomless patience. "Because you deserve to know. And because, once you know, whatever you do next — you'll do it *knowing*. That is worth more to me than your despair. Despair is cheap. Informed sacrifice is priceless."

"And if I refuse to watch?"

"Then you'll spend the rest of your short life wondering, and the wondering will do my work for me." Sable drew back her hand. "But you won't refuse. You crossed a dying mountain for answers. Sit down, Ines Almeida."

*Ines Almeida.* The name rang strange in her own ears, like a bell struck in an empty house. She sat.

* * *

The water moved.

It did not ripple — it *opened*, the way an eye opens, and the room fell away, and Ines fell into it.

Cold. Snow. A night with stars in it — real stars, hundreds of them, wheeling over the shoulder of Thornfell, and she was seeing them through someone else's eyes. Through her own eyes, older ones. She knew the body she wore the way you know a coat you haven't worn in years: the ache in the left knee, the scar along the thumb where a wire had bitten deep, the weight of a pack full of something that clinked. Medicine. She was carrying medicine up a road she should not have been on, at an altitude where blood frothed in the lungs, because—

Because the crew was dying.

She saw it in pieces, the way memory keeps its worst hours: the cave above the salt line, three of them laid out on bedrolls gone stiff with frost, breath rattling wet and wrong. Fever-cough. Blacklung from the deep mines they'd taken a contract to clear, a contract that had paid too well because the mine owners knew what the air would do. And the medicine she carried was half what they needed, and the pass back down was closed by avalanche, and there was one other way — one road — that every smuggler in Thornfell had heard of and none had ever found twice.

The god-road. The silent way. The path that ran, it was whispered, straight through the body of the sleeping god Veth, whose silence had held the mountain's borders safe for nine hundred years.

Ines — younger, wilder, twenty-three and terrified — knelt in the snow at the mouth of that hidden way and did the thing that desperate people do. She prayed. Out loud. To a god who had never once answered anyone.

And the god answered.

Not in words. Veth had no words; silence was its nature and its sacrament. It answered the way snowfall answers — a pressure, a presence, a vast attention settling over her like a hand too large to feel as anything but weather. And into that silence poured understanding, wordless and total: *you may pass. The road is open. My people are your people. Ask.*

Young Ines wept into the snow. Asked for her crew's lives. Was told — felt, rather, the way you feel sunlight on closed eyelids — *done, and freely given, and ask again whenever you are afraid.*

She saw herself walk that road. Saw the interior of a god: not flesh, not stone, but a long cathedral dark lit by veins of patient silver, and everywhere the sense of being *held*, of being known down to the marrow and forgiven for it. She saw herself carry the medicine through. Saw her crew breathe. Saw herself come back to that hidden mouth eleven more times over four years — for smuggled children, for fugitives, for a wounded warden once, though she didn't know yet why that mattered — and every time, the door opened, and every time, the silence said *ask again whenever you are afraid.*

And then she saw the night she sold it.

* * *

The memory turned, and the turning was like a knife between ribs.

Different cave. Deeper. Colder in a way that had nothing to do with temperature. She was thirty-one now — she counted the years off her own hands without meaning to — and she was bleeding from a wound in her side that she knew, with the flat certainty of the dying, was mortal. Around her, her crew. All of them. Every soul who had ever run the salt roads under her flag, dragged into this by a trap she still didn't understand — someone had sold their route, someone had wanted them buried in this specific hole in this specific mountain, and the why of it was a question she was no longer going to live long enough to ask.

Twelve people. Nine of them already dead or close to it. The others looking at her, the captain, waiting for the miracle the way crews do, believing in her the way you believe in a rope while you fall.

And into the dark of that killing hole, the silence came again. *Ask,* it said. *You are afraid. Ask.*

But this time the asking had a price, and she felt it before she understood it — felt the presence draw taut around her like a snare, felt the vast attention narrow to a point, and understood, in the last lucid minute of her old life, that they were not alone in the dark. That something else had found the god-road. Something that had been hunting this exact passage for a very long time, and had needed exactly this: a dying woman whom the god loved, standing at the god's threshold, desperate enough to trade.

The voice that spoke to her then was not Veth's. It was silk drawn over teeth. It offered her a bargain with the simplicity of all great evils: *your twelve lives, freely walked out of this mountain by morning — for the location of the door. Not the door itself. Only the telling of where it stands.*

She saw herself hesitate. That was the part that broke her, watching it — she hesitated. Some part of young Ines, bleeding out in the dark, actually weighed it. Twelve lives against a secret. And then she saw herself think — she *felt* herself think it, the thought arriving whole and terrible — *the god is strong. The god is old. What harm can a telling do?*

She told them.

She watched herself speak the location into the dark, watched the silk-voice drink it in with something like delight, watched her crew carried out by hands she never saw, watched herself wake at dawn in a healer's tent with a scar where the wound had been and no memory of the price — no memory, because the price had been taken *out of her*, scooped clean, the better to keep her useful. The better to keep her running roads her body remembered and her mind could not explain.

And then the memory did the cruelest thing memory can do. It stayed.

It stayed with her, that hidden self, that scooped-out ghost, and Ines watched from inside her own forgotten skull as the thing in the dark came back to the god-road with armies of mirrored faces, and the door — the door that had always opened — did not open, because Veth had heard the telling too, and had understood in one endless silver instant what had been bought with its faithful servant's blood.

She watched the god make its choice.

It could have fought. She saw that clearly — saw the mountain tremble with the weight of what Veth might have unleashed, saw the mirrored host falter at the threshold, saw centuries of stored power coil like a spring. It could have fought, and the fighting would have split Thornfell from crown to root, and the collapse would have buried every village on its skirts, every tithe town, every smuggler camp — every one of the small, stubborn, oath-sworn people who had prayed to it for nine hundred years. Including twelve souls it had just spent its last free act saving.

So instead, the god opened the door from the inside.

She felt it do it. Felt the surrender as a kind of *unclenching*, vast and deliberate, and threaded through it — aimed at her, at the dying woman whose telling had made it possible — one final pulse of that wordless voice, and the pulse said:

*This is not your guilt to carry. I choose this. Ask again whenever you are afraid — and when you cannot remember how to ask, I will send the asking to find you.*

And then the lantern closed over the god's soul, and the stars began to go out, and the memory ended.

* * *

Ines came back to herself on the floor of the small room. She did not remember falling. The lamp burned its heatless fire. Lady Sable stood over her, and Ines struck out at her — a blind, animal blow that Sable caught at the wrist without effort and held, gently, the way you hold a bird.

"Now you know," Sable said.

"It's a lie." Ines's voice came out shredded. "You forged it. You forge faces, you forge light, you—"

"I could have," Sable agreed. "I did not. Check it against your brand, mercenary. Your oath-brand has drunk every promise you ever made in Veth's name. Ask it whether the memory rings true."

Ines didn't want to. She did it anyway. She reached inward, down the cold channel where the brand lived, and asked — and the brand, which had been silent for years, which had been a leash and a wound and a question mark, answered with a single pulse of recognition so absolute it felt like grief. *True. True. True.*

She pulled away from Sable and retched into the corner, and there was nothing in her to bring up, and she knelt there shaking, and the shame was a living thing with her own face.

"I brokered it," she whispered. "I sold the door. Everything — the tithe houses, the drained mercenaries, the stars going out, Wren—" Her throat closed on the child's name. "All of it. Me."

"Yes," Sable said. "And here is the question that matters, Ines Almeida, and I will ask it only once, because I honor you enough to be direct." She crouched, unhurried, until her face was level with Ines's. Outside, far above, something vast had begun to hum — the sealing, the new moon gathering itself, the century closing like a fist. "The god forgave you before you even knew there was anything to forgive. It chose captivity to spare you. It has been waiting, chained in my vault, for *years*, for you to come down those roads your body remembers. So tell me —" and her voice dropped to something almost tender, almost true, which was the most dangerous register she owned, "—what exactly do you imagine you owe it now? Guilt? Guilt is a debt with no creditor. Or love? Love is a debt it has already cancelled." She rose. "Think carefully. Because in a few hours, the doors seal, and the chains on that soul become permanent, and the only hand that can reach the vault in time is yours — and hands move fastest when the mind holding them is *quiet*."

She walked to the door. Paused.

"For what it is worth," she said, not turning around, "of all the bargains struck in this mountain in nine hundred years, yours was the only one where both parties tried to lose." The door opened onto noise — shouting, running feet, a red bloom of alarm-light spreading through the black corridors. "Ah. Your friends. They are either rescuing you or ruining everything; with wardens and smugglers it is generally both. Go and join them, if you like. I find I am curious what a woman does with a truth like yours."

She was gone. The corridor beyond was chaos — Odile's voice, hoarse and furious, somewhere to the left; the smell of burning lattice; and beneath it all, rising through the floor like a heartbeat, the hum of the sealing accelerating, hours collapsing into minutes.

Ines got up. Wiped her mouth. Put her hand flat against her brand, over the place where the god had said *this is not your guilt to carry*, and made herself stand under the weight of it.

Then she ran toward the sound of her friends, carrying the whole of her forgotten life like a lantern she had finally been allowed to open — and somewhere below them all, in a vault of chains, something ancient stirred at her approach, and the first chain began, very softly, to sing.
