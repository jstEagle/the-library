## 17. The New Moon Rises

### Ines

The dark came down like a lid.

Ines felt it before they saw it — a pressure behind the eyes, a tightening in the oath-brand at their wrist, as though someone had slipped a noose of cold silk around the whole mountain and begun to draw it closed. They stood at the mouth of the hollow ways with Odile's map spread across both hands, and by the light of Cassian's lantern they watched the ink begin to move.

Not the ink. The *roads*. The lines on the parchment crawled like worms, rerouting themselves, and where a passage had been marked open an hour ago there was now only a smudge, a scar, a place where the map simply refused to say anything at all.

"It's starting," Odile said. She had gone the color of tallow. "The sealing. I thought we'd have until moonset."

"We have less than that." Ines folded the map along its new creases, the ones the paper had chosen for itself. Their fingers were steady. Their heart was not. Somewhere beneath them, down through six miles of stone and older dark, the Undercourt was drawing its doors shut for a hundred years, and everything inside those doors when they closed would belong to Lady Sable until the stars forgot their names.

Wren made a small sound. Not a word. A sound like a struck bell that couldn't finish ringing.

Ines turned. The child sat against the tunnel wall with their knees drawn up, and around them the air shimmered faintly, the way it does over a fire in high summer. But this was no heat. This was light leaving — pouring out of Wren in threads too fine to see, feeding something below.

"Wren." Ines crouched. "Hey. Look at me."

The child looked up, and for half a heartbeat Ines saw the sky in those eyes — not the starless sky above Thornfell but the old one, the crowded one, the one that had burned over the world before the court began eating it. Then the child blinked and was only Wren again: small, soot-streaked, seven years old or the shape of seven years worn like a coat.

"I'm holding it," Wren whispered. "I'm holding all of it. Every prayer he ever heard. Every name." A breath that shuddered. "It's very heavy, Ines."

"How heavy?"

"Like carrying the sea in my mouth."

Cassian swore softly under his breath. Odile pressed her knuckles to her lips. And Ines took the child's hands — small, fever-hot, trembling like plucked strings — and said the only true thing available:

"Not much longer. We go now. We run."

* * *

### Odile

Odile had spent thirty years learning to read wards the way sailors read weather, and what she read now made her want to sit down in the dust and refuse to move.

They were closing from the outside in.

She could feel it through her boots — the lattice she had tended, patched, and lied about for three decades singing its final notes, each ward-stone going dark in sequence like candles snuffed by a passing hand. The outer ring first, then the deep ways, then the hollow roads themselves. Sable wasn't merely sealing the court. Sable was collapsing every road back to the surface, folding the mountain shut like a fist around whatever it held.

And they were inside the fist.

"The Salt Road's gone," she said, tracing the dead line on the map. "The Pilgrim Stair. The Weepers' Cut. All the southern exits — sealed within the hour, if the pattern holds." She looked up at the others, at their faces lit gold and unsteady in the lantern light, and hated herself for what she had to say next. "There's one way out that isn't on any map I ever kept. The Founders' Gate. It predates the Wardens. It predates the bargain. If it still opens—"

"If?" Cassian said.

"Everything down here is *if*, Vole. That's what none of you understood when you signed your brands. The whole mountain is a conditional sentence, and we are living inside the clause."

Wren laughed — a thin, cracked sound, wrong coming from a child, right coming from something older. "She talks like him," the child said, looking at Odile with terrible fondness. "Veth talked like that. Like the world was a contract he'd been tricked into signing."

Odile's throat closed. Thirty years of service, thirty years of lies, and a seven-year-old fragment of a buried god had just told her she reminded him of the divine.

She shouldered her pack. "Founders' Gate is two days' walk. We have—" she did the arithmetic against the closing pattern, and the answer curdled in her stomach, "—perhaps ten hours. Less, if the court sends anyone after us."

"They will send everyone," Wren said quietly. "She knows you have me. She can feel me holding him. Every moment I keep his memory whole, she keeps reaching for it." The child smiled, and it broke Odile's heart in two clean pieces. "So we shouldn't waste time standing still, should we."

They ran.

* * *

### Cassian

Cassian had been in collapses before. He'd smuggled silk through a war zone, pulled his brother's body out of a flooded mine, watched a bridge full of people come apart in an avalanche winter. He knew the sound a world makes when it decides to end: not a roar but a hush, a great inhale, everything holding its breath at once.

That was the sound now.

Behind them, far below, the Undercourt had begun to sing. He could hear it through the stone — a chord of voices, hundreds of voices, the harvested faces and stolen throats of every oath-sworn soul the court had drained, all of them raised together in something between a hymn and a scream. The sealing-song. Sable winding the century shut.

And ahead of them, the corridors were changing.

He saw it happen. They came around a bend into a gallery of black pillars, and between one step and the next the gallery was longer — thirty paces more, smooth-walled, seamless, as if the mountain had grown new bone while they watched. The hollow ways were not being blocked. They were being *rewritten*, stretched and coiled and turned back on themselves, so that running toward the surface might mean running deeper forever.

"This is a trap," Cassian said. "This whole last stretch. She's not trying to catch us. She's trying to *keep* us."

"She doesn't need to try," Ines said grimly, hauling Wren up onto their hip as the child's legs gave out. "We're already caught. Everything from here is about how gracefully we lose."

"That's a hell of a pep talk, Almeida."

"You've met me."

Despite everything — despite the debt on his family, the false report cooling in Sable's agents' ears, the brand on his wrist that meant *property* — Cassian laughed. It came out ragged. It came out real. And then the corridor behind them filled with light.

Not lantern-light. Court-light: white and sourceless and cold as the underside of ice, spilling around the far bend in a slow tide. Shadows of figures moved within it. Tall figures. Graceful figures. Figures whose proportions were almost right and therefore entirely wrong, and as the light brightened Cassian saw their faces.

He knew some of those faces.

Tomas Ellery, who'd sworn tithe to Veth eleven years ago and walked into the tithe house and never walked out. Mara Quist, who used to sell roasted chestnuts at the Thornfell gate. Faces he'd drunk with, traded with, owed small kindnesses to — now mounted on tall bodies and moving with the gliding unanimity of a single will wearing many masks.

"Nobles," Odile breathed. "The convergence. They're gathering to witness the seal."

The nearest noble turned its head — Tomas Ellery's face, smiling with someone else's smile — and spoke in a voice like wind through a keyhole.

"*The regent bids you come home.*"

Cassian drew his knife. It felt absurd, a toothpick against a tide. But beside him, Ines shifted Wren higher on their hip, and the child lifted their head, and the light in the corridor *stuttered* — flinched, guttered, recoiled — because whatever Wren carried was still, even now, a god's memory, and even a memory of divinity burns.

"*No,*" Wren said, in a voice that was not a child's voice, that was thunder remembering itself, "*he is not yours yet.*"

The nobles stopped. All of them. A forest of stolen faces frozen mid-glide, and in the silence Cassian heard the cost: Wren slumping against Ines's shoulder, skin grey as ash, breath coming in shallow pulls.

"Run," the child whispered. "While they remember how to be afraid."

They ran.

* * *

### Ines

Time went strange.

Ines had felt it once before, years ago, crossing the Weepers' Cut in a rockslide — the sense that hours were not a river but a rope, and somewhere behind them something was pulling the rope shorter. Now the rope was burning through their hands. Ten hours became eight without any hour passing that they could name. Eight became five. The corridors lengthened and shortened around them like breathing; Odile navigated by touch and instinct and fragments of pre-Warden lore, calling turns that turned out to be true, and Ines understood with a lurch that the old woman was reading the mountain the way Ines read it — by a memory that belonged to neither of them, a memory of when these roads were built.

For gods. By gods. Before the sale.

"Left," Odile snapped, and left it was, and the passage beyond opened into a vaulted hall where the walls were carved with processions of figures — tall, robed, crowned with small flames — walking upward toward a door of daylight. The Founders' Gate, promised in stone if not yet in fact. Two days' walk compressed into what remained of their hours, if the roads stayed honest, if the nobles stayed flinching, if Wren—

Wren coughed.

It was a small sound. It should have been a small sound. But Ines felt it land in their own chest like a dropped stone, and when they looked down at the child in their arms they saw why: the shimmer around Wren's skin was brighter now, visible even in the lantern light, threads of silver peeling off the child like smoke off wet wood. Every thread was a prayer. Ines could almost hear them, whispering as they unraveled — *keep my daughter safe, let the harvest hold, forgive me, forgive me, forgive me* — ten thousand voices leaking out of a seven-year-old body that was never built to hold them.

"Wren. Stay with me."

"'m here." The child's smile was ghastly and serene. "I'm everywhere, a little. That's the problem."

"How long can you hold?" Odile asked, and her voice was gentle, which from Odile was worse than shouting.

Wren considered the question with the gravity of a scholar. "Until the moon rises," they said. "Or until I forget his face. Whichever comes first." A pause. "Don't let it be the second one."

Ines's arms tightened around the child without their permission. *Don't let it be the second one.* As if it were a thing Ines could prevent. As if anything down here were a thing anyone could prevent — except, whispered a voice at the bottom of their mind, the voice of the oath-brand burning colder and colder at their wrist, *except the one thing you were always going to do, Almeida. You've known since the vault. You've known since the chains.*

Every chain on Veth's soul was an oath. Including theirs.

To free the god, they would have to unmake the self that loved him.

"Not yet," Ines said aloud, to no one, to the brand, to the dark. "Not until we're clear of her reach."

But the dark didn't answer, and the moon was rising somewhere far above the stone, and the rope of hours burned shorter with every stride.

* * *

### Wren

Wren walked inside the god's memory the way other children waded in a river, and the river was rising.

It had been manageable at first — a few prayers, a few names, the taste of starlight and the weight of a thousand answered pleas. But as the sealing tightened, as Sable's will pressed down through the mountain like a thumb on a beetle, the memory fought back, and fighting meant *spending*, and spending meant burning, and Wren was so very small, and the sea in their mouth had become an ocean, had become the whole sky falling into them cup by cup.

They held anyway.

They held because Ines's arms were warm and smelled of road-dust and iron, and because the tall woman with the guilty eyes had called them *little one* exactly once, in a voice that cracked, and because the sharp-smelling man had given them the last of his dried apricots without being asked. These were small things. Mortal things. Wren had learned, in the short fierce lesson of being a child, that mortal things were the only anchors that held.

*Hold him,* they told the memory, cradling it like a bird with a broken wing. *Hold on. Almost home.*

The god-memory stirred against them, vast and dim, and spoke without words — a pressure, a warmth, a grief the size of centuries:

*You will burn out before the gate, little fragment.*

*Then burn me slowly,* Wren answered. *I'm not using most of me anyway.*

Somewhere above, unseen, the new moon cleared the peaks of Thornfell, a blade-thin crescent, and the sealing-song below rose to meet it, and the first of the inner roads slammed shut with a sound like a tomb door, and the hours left to them could be counted on one hand.

Wren counted anyway. It passed the time. It passed the *self*, a little more each hour, silver threads streaming away into the dark.

But they held the face. Through the burning and the fading and the ocean pouring in, they held the face of the silent god, so that when the moment came — and it was coming, soon, in a vault of shattered chains — someone would still remember what Veth looked like when he woke.

* * *

### Odile

The Founders' Gate, when they reached it in the ninth hour, was already closing.

Not shut — closing. A seam of grey daylight at the top of a stair of white stone, narrowing as they watched, the mountain knitting itself together with the patience of geology. Beyond it Odile could see actual sky, actual air, the starless night of the surface, and the sight of that robbed sky hurt worse than any threat the court had made.

"Ten minutes," she said. "Maybe less. The lattice won't reopen for a hundred years."

"Then we don't need ten minutes," Ines said. "We need nine."

But the light was behind them again. Down the long approach hall, white and sourceless and rising like a flood, the converged nobility of the Undercourt came gliding — dozens now, scores, every stolen face in the mountain arranged into a wall of courteous, smiling flesh, and at their center a figure Odile recognized with a sickness that reached all the way down through thirty years of guilt: her predecessor, First Warden Corvane, his face serene, his hands folded, serving still.

"*The regent offers clemency,*" the wall of faces said, in one voice. "*Return the vessel. Return the thief. The rest may leave.*"

Odile looked at the narrowing seam of daylight. She looked at Wren, barely conscious, burning like a lamp with no oil. She looked at Ines, who had not stopped moving, who was climbing the white stair with the child in their arms as if the offer were weather.

And she understood, finally, completely, the shape of the trap: the gate would close before they reached it. It had been meant to close before they reached it. Sable had never intended to seal them *in* the court — she had intended to seal them in this hall, this antechamber, this last room, with the whole of the court arriving to collect them, and the exit shrinking to nothing overhead.

Unless.

Odile's hands found the ward-lattice spool at her belt — the master-thread, the one tool the Wardens were forbidden to carry into the field, the one she had stolen on the night she fled. With it she could unravel the sealing-pattern from the inside. She could tear the mountain's closing fist open wide enough for four people to pass.

She could also bring six miles of stone down on all of them, if her hands shook.

Thirty years of service. One second of hesitation, once, and Cassian captured; she had paid for that second and sworn there would be no other.

Her hands did not shake.

"Hold the stair!" she shouted, driving the spool's needle into the living rock, into the lattice beneath it, feeling the whole vast pattern of the sealing wake and turn its attention upon her like an eye. "I'm opening the gate — *all of it* — and I don't know what comes through!"

The seam of daylight tore wider.

And the moon rose, and the song below reached its final verse, and Ines Almeida, climbing toward a sliver of stolen sky with a dying god in their arms, felt the oath-brand at their wrist go cold as a key turning — and knew, with absolute clarity, that the last hour had begun.
