## 22. Roads Worth Walking

The pass was open again, and so was Ines's life, in a way they were still learning to walk through.

Spring had come late to Thornfell that year, the way it always did, arriving not as a season but as an argument — snow giving ground grudgingly along the switchbacks, meltwater shouting down every ravine, the pines on the lower slopes releasing their resin-sweet breath into air that smelled like beginnings. Ines stood at the head of the caravan and watched the morning light come over the eastern shoulder of the mountain and pour down the old salt road like something spilled from a cup: gold running into all the cracks the winter had made.

Behind them, twelve travelers adjusted their packs and muttered. A wool merchant named Hesk with a bad hip and worse opinions. Two sisters from the valley farms bringing cheese up to the high summer pastures, their mules loaded with round white wheels wrapped in cloth. A young surveyor sent by the new Wardens' Guild — rebuilt, renamed, its ledgers burned and begun again — who kept looking at Ines like a man trying to read a book written in a language he almost knew.

"Keeper Almeida," he said, for the third time that hour. "Are you certain about this route? The maps show the high road as—"

"The maps are wrong." Ines said it without heat, because it was simply true, the way stone was hard. "The maps have been wrong for sixty years. The Wardens drew them to keep people *on* the roads we wanted them on. The real roads don't care what anyone wants."

They touched the scar at their own temple as they said it — an old habit now, though there was no brand there anymore. Just skin. Smooth, unmarked, ordinary skin where the oath had lived, and beneath it, nothing at all. That was the strange arithmetic of their new life: they had walked out of the mountain carrying less than they'd carried in. Every road before Thornfell, every name before *Ines*, every face of everyone they had ever loved or failed — gone, spent, unmade when they spoke the renunciation in the vault while the chains shattered around them like frost off a branch.

What remained was this: hands that knew knots. Feet that knew grades. An eye that read a slope the way other people read letters. And Odile Ferran, who had stayed.

*You'll want someone to tell you who you were,* Odile had said that first night outside the mountain, when the stars were coming back one by one like lamps relit in a dark house. *I can do that. I watched you long enough.*

And Ines had thought about it — genuinely, painfully, sitting by a fire with a blank where their history should be and a child-shaped absence beside them that hurt worse than any wound — and had said, *No. Tell me instead who I might be. I'd rather build forward than excavate.*

Odile had laughed then, rough and surprised, the first time Ines had heard her laugh. It had sounded like rockfall. Good rockfall. The kind that opens a way through.

* * *

They taught the roads the honest way now: with maps they drew themselves, checking each line against their own feet, and with kindness, which turned out to be a navigational instrument too, though no guild had ever charted it.

"Here," Ines said, kneeling at a bend where the trail narrowed above a scree field, and the travelers gathered around the way people gather around a well. "When the wind comes off the western face, you'll feel it push you toward the edge. Everyone leans away. That's the mistake. You lean away from the wind and your weight goes wrong and the scree takes you. Instead — plant here, lean *into* it, let it hold you up. The mountain pushes harder than it pulls. Work with the push."

Hesk snorted. "Easy for you to say. They say you were born on these roads."

"They say a lot of things." Ines smiled and did not explain that they had no memory of being born anywhere, that their whole past was a rumor they'd heard secondhand, that sometimes at night they lay awake trying to remember their mother's face and found only a shape like warmth, like light through cloth. "But the mountain doesn't care who I am. It cares whether I listen. So listen."

It was strange, teaching what you didn't remember learning. Their body knew these passes the way a riverbed knows water — but the knowing had no story attached, no first time, no teacher's voice behind it. When Ines showed the sisters how to read the lichen on the north-facing stones to find water, they couldn't tell them who had shown them. When they took the surveyor aside and traced, with a stick, the hidden contour of a road that ran beneath the official one like a vein under skin, they couldn't say why they knew it. Only that their hand moved without hesitation, drawing a path no map recorded, and that when they finished, the surveyor stared and said, quietly, "That matches the Warden archives. The ones that were burned. How—"

"I don't know," Ines said, and found, these days, that saying those words felt less like confession and more like freedom. There was a time when not-knowing would have been unbearable — when their competence *was* them, when muscle memory was the only self they trusted. Now the gaps were just weather. You dressed for it and went on.

Some things had come back on their own, drifting up like silt settling. The taste of a particular stew, heavy with caraway, that made their eyes sting without reason. A word in a language no one in the valley spoke, surfacing once in a dream: *selaeth*, which felt like a promise and a farewell at once. And once, crossing the flooded pass where Cassian had come at them with a blade and left with his certainty cracked open — Ines remembered none of that, only what Cassian told them after, in his dry way, over cards he insisted on cheating at — they had stopped mid-stride and known, absolutely, that someone had once bled out on those stones, and that it hadn't been them.

"You're doing it again," Odile said, falling into step beside them as the caravan moved on. "Standing still in the middle of a trail, looking at a rock like it owes you money."

"It doesn't owe me anything." Ines started walking. "Nothing owes me anything anymore. That's the whole point."

"Mm." Odile glanced back at the travelers strung out along the switchback, then ahead at the pass climbing toward the saddle, white against a sky scrubbed clean by wind. "The tithe houses are being converted. Did you hear? The one at Grayfall — they're making it a hospice. Beds for the road-worn. The oath-brands won't fade, of course. Scars never do. But they don't pull anymore. Men have stopped dreaming in Veth's cadence. Mostly."

"*Mostly*?"

"There are reports." Odile's voice went careful, the way it did when the ward-keeper in her surfaced despite everything. "From the deep hollow ways. Footsteps. A lantern-light where no one carries a lantern. The Undercourt wasn't destroyed, Ines. Sable's regency ended, her stolen faces went home to their owners, the court sealed itself — but sealed isn't dead. Something is down there still. Something quiet."

Ines considered this. Below them, somewhere under miles of stone, the hollow ways threaded through the dark like the roots of an inverted tree, roads built for gods, walked last by a smuggler whose body had remembered why. "Good," they said.

Odile gave them a look. "*Good*?"

"A god shouldn't be a leash or a lamp or a thing we own the use of." Ines shrugged. "If Veth wants to walk the dark roads alone for a hundred years, thinking whatever gods think, that's its business. We broke the oath so it could be free. Freedom includes silence." They paused. "Besides. If it ever needs us, it knows our roads. We taught them honestly."

* * *

They camped that night below the saddle, in a hollow of granite that Ines had chosen without deliberation and only afterward understood: it faced east, caught the dawn early, and sat above the reach of cold-air drainage, which meant warm sleep. The travelers praised their judgment. Ines accepted the praise and privately noted, not for the first time, that their instincts were a kind of inheritance they could not trace — some earlier self, erased, still leaving gifts at the door.

Odile brewed her bitter pine-needle tea and refused, as always, to admit it was terrible. The sisters sang a low valley song about cheese, which was funnier than it deserved. Hesk complained about his hip, his mule, the altitude, and the quality of mercy in the modern age, and everyone let him, because complaining was how Hesk said he was happy.

Cassian had come through three weeks ago, heading west with a load of legitimate freight — *legitimate*, he'd repeated twice, wounded — and had stayed one evening to lose money at cards and win something else instead. He wore no debt-brand now; the ledger had burned with everything else in the vault's shattering, and he'd walked out owing nothing to anyone, which he claimed was the strangest wealth a man could carry. Before he left he'd stood at the trailhead a moment too long, looking back up at Ines with an expression he would have denied under torture.

"You know what I can't get used to?" he'd said. "That I remember everything and you remember nothing. It should be the other way. You should have the good parts."

"I have the good parts," Ines had said. "I'm standing in them."

Now, banking the fire, Ines looked up at the sky. The stars were all there — every one returned since the night the mountain shook and a soul poured home through a dissolving child, though Ines held no memory of that either, only the aftermath: Odile's face gray with grief and awe, Cassian silent for two full days, and a god rising through a mirrored city offering even its jailer mercy. The constellations were different now, the surveyors said. Not wrong — *different*. Stars that had been dark for generations burning again in patterns the old charts had no names for. The Wardens' Guild was compiling a new atlas. They'd asked Ines to consult. Ines had said yes, on one condition: that every page include the words *we do not know everything yet*, printed large enough to read by starlight.

Sleep came easy in the hollow. Ines dreamed — they dreamed often now, small ordinary dreams, dreams with no weight of lost history behind them — and in this one there was a road going uphill forever, and someone walking beside them whose face they couldn't see, and the walking felt like company, not compulsion. No brand pulling. No leash humming in the blood. Just two figures on a road, going the same direction because they chose to.

* * *

They crossed the highest pass on the fourth day, the saddle called the Shepherd's Shoulder, where the world opened on both sides at once: behind them the green descending valleys, ahead the long gray spine of the range marching north into cloud, and below, far below on the southern face, a thread of silver where the salt road came up out of the low country — the same road, more or less, that a smuggler had once run alone at night with contraband and dread, guided by a knowledge they couldn't explain. Ines looked down at it and felt nothing but the view. That was the strangest grace of all: the road had no ghosts in it anymore. It was just a road.

The travelers rested at the cairn at the top, passing around water and dried apricots. Wind came up from the south, soft with the smell of coming rain and far-off pine. The sisters argued amiably about which peak was which. The surveyor sketched the skyline with fierce concentration, adding his small page to humanity's slow relearning of the mountain.

And Ines stood a little apart, at the very crest, where the cairn stones threw a long shadow, and listened.

Because there — faint, carried up on the wind from somewhere down the southern slope, from a fold of the mountain where no caravan traveled and no village stood — there was a sound. A child laughing.

Not crying. Not calling for help. Laughing — bright and sudden and delighted, the laugh of a child running downhill with the wind at their back, the laugh of someone playing a game they were winning. It rang once against the rocks and echoed and faded, and the echo had a strange quality Ines had no map for, as if the mountain itself had repeated it fondly, the way an old friend repeats your joke.

The travelers heard nothing. Hesk was complaining about apricots. The wind moved on.

But Ines stood very still at the top of the world, one hand resting on the warm cairn stone, and felt something rise in them that had no name they knew — except that it wasn't grief, exactly, and it wasn't longing, exactly. It was closer to recognition. The way you turn at a market because someone's laugh sounds like someone you loved, and you never see them, and somehow that's enough. Somehow that's a gift.

"Wren?" they said softly, to the wind, to the mountain, to whoever might be listening from the deep places or the high ones. There was no answer. Of course there wasn't. But the air felt briefly, unmistakably *attended* — the way a room feels when someone you trust has entered it — and Ines smiled without deciding to.

Then they turned back to their travelers, to their maps and their mules and their work, and raised their voice in the tone that meant *roads worth walking wait for no one*.

"Up, all of you. The light's good and the pass is kind today. We make the far shelter by dusk." They shouldered their pack and took the lead, feet finding the grade, body reading the slope, the unknown road opening ahead of them step by unremembered, freely chosen step.

Behind them, faint and far down the mountain, the child's laugh rang once more — like a bell, like a blessing, like an old friend waving from a window — and was folded into the wind.

Ines walked on, smiling, carrying nothing but the day.
