## 1. The Weight of Contraband

The moon was a coin spent long ago, worn thin over Thornfell, and Oscar Thackeray moved beneath it like a man who had learned to owe nothing to light.

He came down the goat track above Hollowmere with forty pounds of salt on his back and his breath held the way the old-timers taught him — slow through the nose, shallow in the chest, as if the mountain itself were sleeping and might wake at the smell of fear. The pack frame was willow and rawhide, older than he was. It creaked once when he shifted his weight, and he stopped for a count of thirty, one boot hovering over loose scree, until the wind covered the sound.

Below him, maybe half a mile down the switchbacks, a lantern bobbed along the ward-road. Guild patrol. His patrol, technically — every route out of Salt Hall belonged to the guild, and every man walking it drew guild pay, which meant that technically Oscar was robbing himself. He'd made peace with that arithmetic years ago.

He counted the lantern's swing. Four paces between posts meant a tired walker. A tired walker stopped at the third bend to relight a guttering wick; they always did, because nobody on night patrol ever trimmed their lamps properly anymore. Sure enough, the glow stuttered, dimmed, and held still while the dark swallowed its edges.

Oscar went down the mountain like water finding a crack.

He didn't use the ward-road. Nobody who knew what they were doing used the ward-road. The road was for wagons and fools and the guild's official ledger, where salt moved from the flats to the hall and then vanished into the guildmaster's counting room, only to reappear months later priced like it had been carried down from the moons themselves. The road was a story the guild told about itself. What Oscar ran was the truth underneath: forty pounds of coarse grey salt, packed in oiled canvas, bound for a village the guild had written off two winters ago.

Hollowmere wasn't on any delivery schedule. That was the whole trick of it. You couldn't be caught smuggling to a place that officially had no need of anything.

* * *

He smelled the village before he saw it — woodsmoke gone thin, sour milk, the particular staleness of houses closed up against cold because there was nothing left worth burning. Then he saw the lights. Three of them, maybe four. Two winters past there would have been thirty.

Marta Kell opened her door before he knocked. She always did. She said it was because she heard him coming, but Oscar suspected she simply stood watch most nights, the way old women do when they've buried enough people to know how quietly the end can walk up a path.

"You're late," she said.

"The pass iced." He slid the pack off his shoulders inside her doorway, careful not to let the frame scrape stone. "And there's a new patrol rotation on the north spur. Corvane's doubled it."

"Doubled." Marta weighed the word like a fishwife weighing a gutted catch, deciding what it would fetch. "Doubling patrols to guard roads that carry no goods."

"He has his reasons."

"Corvane Thackeray always has his reasons." She said the name without looking at him. Nobody in the guild ever said it to Oscar's face if they could help it. The guildmaster shared a surname with half the founding families, but with Oscar it sat differently, heavier, and both of them pretended it didn't. "Sit. There's broth."

"There's never broth."

"There's broth tonight because you brought salt, and salt means we're not dead yet, and a woman is allowed to mark that." She was already ladling from a pot that had been stretched, he guessed, across a week of meals. Barley, a shred of something that had once been mutton, a ghost of onion. It tasted better than anything the hall served, and Oscar ate it too fast, the way he always did, ashamed of how hungry he let himself get before these deliveries.

Marta watched him eat with her hands folded. Her knuckles were swollen, her wrists thinner than last season. But her eyes were the same eyes that had pulled him out of a snowdrift when he was nine and too stubborn to admit he was lost, and they missed nothing.

"How many left?" he asked. "In the village."

"Forty-one souls." A pause. "Thirty-eight now. The Aldous boy went in the spring. Fever. And the Harker sisters walked out for the lowlands in the summer and I've had no word." She said it flatly, inventorying her losses the way he inventoried his cargo. "You shouldn't come so often. If the guild catches you—"

"The guild doesn't catch anyone," Oscar said. "That's rather the point of me."

It was true, and hearing it aloud in this small dying kitchen made it sound less like pride than confession. He was good. He was the best the guild had produced in a generation, and everyone said so in the way people discuss a fine knife — useful, admired, kept in a drawer. At nineteen he was the youngest full courier in Salt Hall's history. He knew eleven routes over Thornfell that existed on no map, seven springs that froze late and thawed early, the exact weight of load a mule could take up the Widow's Stair without balking. He could read a patrol's mood by the pitch of its lantern glass. He had never been caught. Not once, not close, not even seen.

And he could count on one hand the number of people who knew his face well enough to greet him by name in daylight.

Marta was one. That thought arrived, as it always did, somewhere between the second spoonful and the last, and he did what he always did with it: set it down and stepped over it.

"I'll need the empties back next month," he said. "Same weight in dried peas if you have them, or wool. Wool keeps."

"You'll take payment in wool from starving people and call it trade."

"I'll call it what it is. You feed me soup and tell me gossip, I haul salt the guild says you don't deserve. Nobody's being robbed." He smiled, and she gave him the look she reserved for his smiles, the one that said she could see straight through to whatever was underneath and found it in need of mending.

"The miller's wife says there are strange men on the high passes," Marta said suddenly, quieter. "Not guild. Not bandits either, by her telling. Watching the peaks. And the stars—" She hesitated, embarrassed, an old woman shy of her own words. "She says the stars have been wrong. Dimmer. My own eyes aren't what they were, so I can't say."

"They're fine," Oscar said. "Stars don't change. That's the whole virtue of them."

"Mm." Marta took his bowl. "That's exactly the sort of thing a young man says right before the world proves him a fool."

* * *

He left by the back window, the way he'd come, and worked his way up the eastern slope where the pine cover ran thick and black. The return leg was the dangerous part — he was lighter now but slower, the adrenaline drained, the body wanting sleep, and dawn was a hard wall at the end of every route. You didn't beat dawn with speed. You beat it with discipline. Rest at intervals whether you felt tired or not, because tired arrives all at once or not at all, and you never got to choose which.

He was three miles up and thinking about nothing, which was his finest skill, when he heard the bell.

One strike, far off to the west, deep-toned, rolling down from the heights above Salt Hall. Then a second. Then a third, ragged, overlapping, as though every bell in every tower along the spine of Thornfell had been seized by the same trembling hand.

Oscar went still against a pine trunk. In nineteen years, in his whole life, the ward-bells had rung twice. Once for a rockslide. Once for the death of the old guildmaster, Corvane's predecessor, whose passing had been announced to the mountains with the same solemn iron voice.

Three bells was not a code he knew.

He turned west. Through a seam in the trees he could see the sky over the high peaks — and he stopped breathing altogether.

There were no stars.

Not clouded. Not hidden behind weather. The night above Thornfell was clear as polished slate, and it was empty, black from ridge to ridge, as if someone had reached up and snuffed the heavens like candles after a funeral. Even the Moth and the Shepherd, the two bright wanderers every smuggler steered by, even those were gone.

Oscar Thackeray, who had never once been seen on a mountain road, stood fully exposed on an open slope and stared upward, and understood that he had just watched the world break open — and that whatever came next, invisibility would not save him.

Far below and behind him, faint as a heartbeat, the bells kept ringing.
