## 2. The Night Without Stars

Oscar felt the wards break the way a sailor feels a keel strike rock—through his boots, through his teeth, before his mind caught up to what his body already knew.

He was in the guild hall's lower gallery, counting bolts of greywool that smelled of mildew and someone else's lies, when it happened. The Salt Guild's hall was carved into the spine of Thornfell itself, three levels of stone galleries stacked around a central hearth where the covenant-flame had burned for as long as anyone could remember. Oscar had grown up under that flame. He'd learned to walk beneath it, learned to read ledgers by its light, learned that as long as it burned steady, the world outside—wards, borders, stars, all of it—held.

The flame guttered.

Not flickered. *Guttered*, the way a candle gutters when a door opens somewhere far away, and every smuggler in the gallery went still at once, the way animals go still.

"Did you feel that?" asked Tam Voss, the old ferryman who ran the river routes. His hands had stopped mid-count on a purse of silverweight.

"I felt nothing," said the man next to him, which was a lie, because everyone had felt it—a soundless snap, like a bone breaking inside the mountain, deep below hearing.

Oscar set down the wool. His fingers were cold. That was wrong; the gallery was always warm, hearth-warm, guild-warm. He climbed the stairs two at a time because standing still felt worse than moving.

The main hall was full. It shouldn't have been full—it was past midnight, past even the night-shift ledger men—but half the guild had drifted out of their bunks and into the open space around the hearth, drawn by whatever that snapping feeling was, the way a toothache draws the tongue. They stood in loose clusters, talking low and fast. Someone had brought wine up from the cellars, which meant someone thought this might be a long night.

Above them, through the great smoke-vent in the vaulted ceiling, you could see the sky.

Thornfell sat high enough that the stars came down close. Every child of the guild learned the constellations before they learned their letters—the Warden's Chain, the Salt Crown, the Long Serpent that marked the pass roads—and smugglers navigated by them the way other men navigated by memory. The sky was the one map Corvane couldn't burn, the one constant no patrol or tariff or war could touch. *Look up,* his mother used to say, back when she was alive and they were poor and honest about it. *However lost you are, the stars know where Thornfell is.*

Oscar looked up.

The vent framed a circle of perfect, absolute black.

Not clouded. Not overcast. Clouds had edges, had depth, had the faint grey breath of moonlight behind them. This was a hole cut in the world. He scanned it the way you scan a page when you're sure the words must be there—found nothing, scanned again, found nothing. No Warden's Chain. No Salt Crown. No moon. The moon should have been three days from full and bright enough to cast shadows off the gallery railings, and there was no moon.

"Oscar." Tam Voss had come up beside him, following his gaze. The old man's face had gone the color of tallow. "Boy. Tell me what you see."

"Nothing," Oscar said. His voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice. "I see nothing."

Around the hall, others were seeing it too. The realization moved through the crowd like fire through dry grass—you could track it by the faces, by the way conversations died mid-word, by the slow upward tilt of a hundred heads. A woman near the kitchens began to laugh, high and helpless, until her husband shook her arm and pointed and she stopped laughing very suddenly. Someone dropped a tankard. Nobody bent to pick it up. Ale spread across the stone in a dark tongue and no one looked at it.

And then the doors slammed.

All of them. At once.

The guild hall had seven entrances—smugglers never trusted fewer—and each was an iron-banded oak slab thick as a coffin lid. Oscar heard them go shut-boom, shut-boom, shut-boom, one after another like a heartbeat running backward, and the bar-fittings engaged with a sound he'd only ever heard during the one drill they'd run in his boyhood, the siege drill nobody believed they'd ever need. Chains rattled home in the walls. Somewhere overhead, portcullises dropped with a clang that shivered dust from the rafters.

The hall went quiet enough to hear the hearth-flame hiss.

Corvane stood on the master's balcony above the great hearth.

Guildmaster Aldous Corvane had run the Salt Guild for thirty years, and he wore those years the way he wore everything: deliberately. Silver at the temples, iron in the shoulders, a voice calibrated to carry across any room without ever seeming raised. He'd taken Oscar off the streets at nine years old, fed him, taught him knots and ciphers and the hundred quiet ways a thing can cross a border unseen. Half the guild would tell you Corvane didn't smile. Oscar knew better. He'd seen the smile, twice, and both times it had been aimed at him alone, and both times it had made him feel like the most important person in Thornfell.

Tonight Corvane did not smile. Tonight he gripped the balcony rail with both hands, and Oscar—who had spent his whole life reading people the way he read border patrols—noticed that the guildmaster's knuckles were white, and that Corvane knew everyone could see it, and that he didn't care.

"The wards are down," Corvane said.

No preamble. No reassurance first. That alone told Oscar more than the words did, because Corvane always led with reassurance; reassurance was how you kept a crew steady, and Corvane had never once let a crew see him unsteady.

"All of them," Corvane continued. "The Thornfell line, the Lowmark beacons, the river wards. Everything the founders built. As of tonight, the border between the settled realms and the dragon-realm is not weakened. It does not exist."

The word *dragon* moved through the hall like a current through water. Nobody said it aloud. Nobody needed to. Every member of the Salt Guild carried the same nursery stories: the founders' pact, the old friendship, the fire that had once lit the peaks. And every member knew the official version—that the dragons were gone, that the covenant was ancient history, that the wards were simply *wards*, maintenance work, masonry and rune-work, nothing more.

If the wards were gone, then the stories weren't stories.

"What happened to them?" That was Brannic Hale, the quartermaster, big voice, bigger doubts. "Wards don't just fall. Stone doesn't rot overnight. What *broke* them?"

"That," Corvane said, "is what I intend to find out."

It was a good answer. It was exactly the right answer, measured and strong, promising action without promising miracles. Oscar watched it land on the crowd and steady them, shoulders easing, the weeping woman in the kitchen corner going quiet. And Oscar watched Corvane's eyes while he said it—watched them move, quick and precise, not over the frightened faces but over the *exits*, counting them, confirming his seals held—and something cold slid down Oscar's spine that had nothing to do with the missing moon.

*He already knows what broke them.* The thought arrived whole, unwelcome, certain. *He's not looking for answers. He's checking his locks.*

"Effective immediately," Corvane went on, "the hall is sealed. No one leaves. No one enters. Runners will be dispatched at first light along prearranged routes—we'll have word from the lowland chapters within two days. Until then, the guild holds together behind its own stone, as it always has. We are the Salt Guild. We do not panic. We do not scatter. We endure."

A ragged cheer went up, thin but real. People wanted to believe him. Believing him was easier than standing under a black hole in the sky and admitting the world had come apart.

Oscar didn't cheer.

He was watching Merrin Sallow, the archivist's second, who stood at the edge of the balcony stairs with a look on her face Oscar had never seen there before. Merrin was sixty and unshakeable; she'd once catalogued a flood-damaged archive without raising her voice. Now she stared at Corvane with an expression Oscar could only call *recognition*. Like a woman hearing a stranger speak in a dead friend's voice.

Their eyes met across the hall. Merrin looked away first.

* * *

They released them to their bunks near dawn—or what should have been dawn. That was the thing nobody could stop talking about in the dormitory corridors: there was no dawn. The hours crawled past and the black above the smoke-vent stayed black, absolute, and eventually the guild's timekeepers simply declared morning by the clock, and the guild ate breakfast by lamplight in a silence broken only by cutlery.

Oscar didn't eat. He walked.

The sealed hall had become a hive with every entrance waxed over. Guards at the doors—Corvane's own hand-picked men, not the usual rotation. Couriers turned away from the rookery. The archive stair, Oscar noticed, now had two guards of its own, which was new, which made no sense, unless the most valuable thing left in a world without stars was *paperwork*.

He filed that away. Smugglers file things away; it's the whole trade. You don't react to the anomaly, you log it, you wait for the pattern to finish drawing itself.

The pattern finished drawing itself in the late afternoon, in the form of a rumor with legs. Tam Voss caught him by the cisterns, pulled him into the shadow of the water-wheel, and spoke fast and low.

"There's a man in the infirmary," Tam said. "Came down the high pass last night, ahead of the wards falling. Half-dead, frostbitten, raving. The watch brought him in before the doors sealed."

"Raving about what?"

"About the sky." Tam's eyes darted toward the ceiling, toward the black beyond it. "Says he saw it happen. Says he was on the Warden's Tooth when the stars went out, and it wasn't like a lamp failing. It was like—" the old man's throat worked— "like being *unmade*. One moment the sky was full, next moment nothing, and the nothing came *down*, boy. Came rolling off the peaks like fog off a lake. And he says—"

Tam stopped. Swallowed.

"He says before it took the sky, it took the ward-line first. Says the wards didn't fall. Says they were *opened*. From our side of the border. From inside Thornfell."

The cistern wheel dripped. Somewhere far off, a door guard shifted his weight, chain clinking.

"Who'd open the wards?" Oscar said. "There's no one left who even knows how. The ward-craft died with the old keepers—"

"Did it?" Tam said quietly. "Then why has the guildmaster locked the archive tighter than his own treasury? Why are his men pulling every record older than fifty years? I've been hauling freight through these mountains for forty years, Oscar Thackeray, and I know contraband when I smell it. Something in this guild's history is being smuggled out of sight, and your precious Corvane is holding the lantern."

"Don't," Oscar said. The word came out harder than he intended. "Don't talk about him like that. Whatever's happening—he sealed the doors to protect us. You think anyone else was going to act? You think the chapter masters in the lowlands would've done anything but run?"

Tam looked at him for a long moment, and there was something in the old man's face that Oscar would spend nights afterward trying to name. Pity, maybe. Or the particular sorrow of watching someone defend a house whose foundations they haven't inspected.

"You're a good lad," Tam said finally. "Best route-runner the guild's produced in a generation. But loyalty's like salt, boy. It preserves what it touches—and it hides everything underneath."

He shuffled off toward the infirmary wing. Oscar stayed by the cistern a long time, telling himself the cold in his gut was just the mountain chill, just the shock, just a bad night stretched strange.

He almost believed it.

* * *

He was summoned to the master's study at the hour the clocks called evening.

The study sat at the top of the hall, behind a door of blackthorn wood older than the guild's charters, and Oscar had been inside exactly four times in his life. Each time had been a promotion, a reward, a hand on the shoulder. *You're the future of this guild, Oscar. The invisible man. My invisible man.*

Tonight the door stood open, and Corvane stood at the window with his back turned, and the window was shuttered, and Oscar wondered if the guildmaster had ordered it shut against the dark or simply couldn't bear to look at it.

"You felt it," Corvane said, without turning. "At the hearth. Before anyone else. I watched you climb the gallery stairs."

"I felt something."

"You always did. Finest instincts I've ever trained." He turned then, and up close, in lamplight, Oscar saw what the balcony distance had hidden: the fine tremor in the guildmaster's hands, the bruised skin under his eyes, the look of a man who had been awake for days inside a single terrible night. "I'm giving you a task, and I'm giving it to you because you're the only one I trust with it."

Oscar's chest did the thing it always did at Corvane's praise—the warm lift, the old orphan hunger answering—and he hated himself a little for it, and leaned in anyway.

"The lowland chapters need to know the hall stands. Word by runner is too slow, and the rooks won't fly blind. There's a route—" Corvane drew a folded paper from his desk, but didn't hand it over yet— "an old one. Pre-covenant. Through the Underfell galleries. It predates the wards entirely, which means it may run beneath whatever's happened to them. You know the deep ways better than any living member."

"The Underfell hasn't been mapped since my grandfather's day—"

"Which is why I need *you*." Corvane crossed the room and put the paper in Oscar's hand, folding Oscar's fingers over it, holding them closed a beat longer than necessary. His grip was cold. "Leave before the third bell. Alone. Tell no one. And Oscar—" the guildmaster's voice dropped, gentle, intimate, the voice he saved for promotions— "while you're below, the archive guards may ask questions. Answer none. Touch nothing. What the guild buried stays buried. Do you understand me?"

*What the guild buried stays buried.*

Every instinct Oscar owned—the ones that had kept him alive through eleven years of patrols and passes and men who smiled before they stabbed—went off at once, silent and total, like the wards must have gone.

"Yes, Guildmaster," he heard himself say.

Because that was the rotten truth of loyalty: it answered before conscience could finish asking its question.

Corvane smiled then—the real one, the rare one, the one that had always made Oscar feel chosen—and it should have warmed him. Instead Oscar stood in the lamplight holding an unmapped route to nowhere, and understood, with the clarity of a man watching the last star go out, that the world he had trusted his whole life had not merely broken open.

It had been opened. From the inside.

By hands he knew.

The third bell rang. Oscar tucked the route-paper against his ribs, where he carried everything worth stealing, and went down into the dark of the mountain—alone, as ordered, toward whatever the guild had buried and whatever waited beyond the wards.

Behind him, above the emptying hall, the hearth-flame guttered again.

And did not steady.
