## 3. What the Guild Buried

The rain found Sera Voss three miles below Thornfell, and she let it.

She had earned it, in a way. Ward-keepers were supposed to walk dry—wards bent weather around them the way a stone bends a stream—but her wards had been stripped from her eleven days ago in a hall full of people who used to call her *sister*, and since then the sky had been free to do whatever it liked to her. It chose rain. Fine. Rain was honest. Rain didn't pretend to be anything but what it was.

She climbed the smugglers' switchbacks with her hood down and her satchel strapped tight across her back, and every hundred steps she caught herself reaching for wards that weren't there anymore—the little reflexive checks, the way you'd touch your tongue to a missing tooth. The mountains felt enormous without them. Not beautiful. Enormous. A body of rock and dark that had been politely holding its breath around human settlements for four hundred years, and now nobody was asking it to hold its breath anymore.

Because the stars were gone.

Sera had been on watch when it happened. That was the joke of it, the cruelty of it—that the one night in thirty years when everything broke, she was actually doing her job. She'd stood on the Widow's Rest platform with her instruments out and watched the border-ward lattice flicker like a lamp running out of oil, and she'd shouted for the senior keepers, and by the time anyone climbed up to look, the lattice wasn't flickering. It was *gone*. Not broken—broken things leave edges, fragments, residue. This was smooth absence, as if someone had reached into the sky and lifted the whole structure out whole, the way you'd lift a bone from a stew.

And the stars had gone with it.

Not clouded over. Not hidden. *Gone*, all of them, at once, in the same instant the wards died—which no natural event could explain, because stars did not care about border-wards, except that apparently they did, except that everything the ward-keepers' order had ever recorded said they should have, and none of the records explained why, because the why had been cut out four generations ago like a tumor, cleanly, deliberately, by people who knew exactly what they were removing and why it had to go.

Sera had spent eleven days being formally disgraced for noticing.

They hadn't even bothered with a real trial. The Conclave had convened at dawn, listened to her report, and then listened to Archkeeper Dannel's counter-report, which was that Keeper Voss had suffered some manner of episode, that the lattice readings were instrument failure compounded by hysteria, and that in a time of crisis the order could not afford superstition dressed as scholarship. Then they'd taken her keys. All of them. The heavy iron ring she'd carried since she was nineteen, the one that opened the deep archive, the observation vaults, the sealed annex under the eastern stair where the oldest documents lived behind a door nobody was permitted to open and everybody pretended not to wonder about.

*You'll thank us,* Dannel had said, not unkindly, which was somehow worse. *When this passes. Grief makes people see patterns, Sera. You lost your mother this spring. We understand.*

Her mother had nothing to do with it. Her mother had been dead five months and Sera had grieved her properly, privately, in the approved fashion. What she had seen on the Widow's Rest had nothing to do with grief and everything to do with the fact that she had spent nine years studying the lattice's resonance harmonics and she knew, the way a musician knows a false note, that what she'd heard the night the stars went out was not collapse.

It was *release*. Something had been held, and something had let it go, and the two events had happened in the same breath because they were the same event.

And there was exactly one organization in the world old enough, secretive enough, and compromised enough to know what that something was.

Which was why she was climbing toward the Salt Guild's front door in the rain, disgraced, keyless, and carrying the only copy of a document she'd stolen from the sealed annex on her last night with keys—a document so old its vellum had gone the color of old teeth, written in a hand that predated the ward-keepers' own founding charter by two centuries.

A covenant. Signed by names the histories called myth.

She crested the last ridge an hour past midnight and saw the guild hall below, squatting in its bowl of granite like a toad in a cupped palm, windows lit against the starless dark. Smoke rose from its chimneys. Somewhere inside, several hundred people who moved contraband through these mountains were pretending very hard that the world hadn't changed.

Sera understood the pretense intimately. She'd been performing her own version for eleven days.

She walked down to the gates and knocked, because sneaking in would have defeated the purpose, and the purpose was the only thing she had left.

* * *

The man who answered was young—younger than her, certainly, though the guild aged people strangely—and he looked at her the way gatekeepers look at trouble: cataloguing, weighing, already composing the report. Rain ran off his jaw. Behind him, torchlight and the smell of salt brine and woodsmoke spilled out into the wet.

"Guild business?" he asked.

"Older than guild business." She kept her voice level. "I need to speak with whoever holds your archive."

"We don't have an archive."

"Every guild has an archive. Yours is just ashamed of it." She let that sit. "My name is Sera Voss. I was first keeper of the western lattice until eleven days ago. You'll have noticed the stars going out. Your guild noticed before we did—I'd wager your founders' ledgers have entries about it. Entries from the last time."

Something shifted in his face. Not belief—curiosity wearing belief's coat. He glanced back into the hall, then stepped aside just enough to be technically not blocking the door.

"You'll want Corvane," he said. "Everyone wants Corvane."

"I want the archive. Corvane can come along if he likes."

He took her to a side room with a brazier and left her there long enough to count the exits—two, plus a window too narrow to matter—and to notice that the guild's panic sat in the building like smoke sits in wool. People hurried in the corridors. Voices dropped when doors closed. Someone had nailed boards over a window on the stairs, which told her the guildmaster had ordered darkness sealed out, which told her the guildmaster believed the darkness was *in here with us*, which meant Corvane Thackeray—or whatever the current one's name was—knew more than he wanted his people thinking he knew.

Good. Guilty men were easier to negotiate with than ignorant ones. They had something to protect.

When the door opened again it was the young gatekeeper who came through it, alone, carrying a tray of tea he set down like a peace offering, and Sera revised her assessment of him upward. Gatekeepers didn't bring tea. Hostages brought tea. Or scouts.

"Oscar," he said. "Oscar Thackeray." He hesitated, then added, "The guildmaster's my uncle."

"And yet you're the one bringing tea to a stranger instead of reporting me." She wrapped her hands around the cup. Her fingers were still cold. "That's either loyalty or doubt, and I'm curious which."

"It's efficiency," Oscar said. "Corvane will want to know what you know before he decides what you are. I figured I'd hear it first and save everyone the theater." He sat down across from her, uninvited, and studied her with the frank appraisal of someone who'd grown up valuing information above manners. "So. First keeper of the western lattice. Except they say you're not a keeper anymore. Word came down the trade roads yesterday—stripped of office, expelled from the Conclave. They say you went mad."

"They would." She drank. The tea was terrible and salty, guild-cut, stretched to make stores last, and it told her more about the guild's condition than any report. "Did the word include *why*?"

"It included that you claimed the wards didn't fail. That they were released. On purpose. By someone who knew how." His eyes didn't move from hers. "The roads think that's madness. The roads also thought the mountain pass at Grey Hollow was haunted right up until the day we found the old toll-station buried under it with dragon-bone in the foundation. So." He spread his hands. "Madness or not. Say it to me."

No one had asked her to say it. In eleven days, no one—not one colleague, not one friend—had asked her to simply *say it*, plainly, while looking at her. They had asked if she was well. They had suggested rest. Dannel had put a hand on her shoulder, which she was still trying to forgive.

Sera set down the cup.

"The border-wards weren't built to keep enemies out," she said. "That's what we tell initiates. It's what the charter says. It's wrong, or rather—it's the polite version. The lattice was a *tether*. Four hundred years ago, when the ward-keepers' order was founded, the world still had dragons in it. Real ones. And the dragons were dying—not by sword or sickness, but by starvation, because their fire isn't theirs alone. It's fed. Fed by the land, by the sky, by a living exchange. The old texts call it the covenant: dragon-fire warms the high places, holds the deep cold back, keeps the seasons turning true. And in return—" She stopped. Made herself continue. "In return, the realms bound themselves to the dragons. Oaths. Obligation. The founders of my order were signatories. So were the founders of yours."

Oscar had gone very still. Smuggler-still, she thought—the stillness of a man listening for the second sound under the first.

"The tether held the covenant's anchor-points stable," she went on. "Five of them. Covenant-stones. As long as the stones sang to each other and the lattice hummed between them, the exchange continued, mostly automatically, generation after generation, and everyone forgot what they were paying and why. The dragons faded into story. The wards became 'protection.' And somewhere in the last four generations, someone in the orders' leadership decided the obligation was expensive and the payment was optional." Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "They didn't break the covenant. Breaking it would have been honest. They *starved* it. Redirected the flows. Quietly, ledger by ledger, revision by revision, until the exchange ran backward and the dragons got nothing and the stones went silent one by one. The lattice didn't fail the other night, Oscar. It finished failing. Eleven days ago the last flow reversed, and whatever was left holding the sky's share of the bargain let go. The wards fell because there was finally nothing left to ward. And the stars went out because the stars were never ours. They were lit from the other side of the covenant. Dragon-fire, burning at a distance we couldn't imagine. And we stopped paying."

Silence. The brazier popped. Down the corridor someone laughed at something, quickly hushed, as if laughter itself needed permission now.

Oscar stared at her. When he spoke, his voice was careful. "You're saying the sky is a debt collector."

"I'm saying the sky is a *creditor* that has finally closed the account."

"And the document you stole." He nodded at her satchel. "You didn't climb three miles of switchbacks in the rain to tell a story. You stole proof."

"I stole the original founding covenant." She unslung the satchel and drew out the oilcloth packet, unwrapping it on the table between them with hands that were steadier than they had any right to be. The vellum inside was ancient, cracked at the folds, dense with script and older script beneath it, and around the margins ran diagrams she had spent nine years learning to read: resonance-charts, anchor-maps, the five points marked in ink that had once, she was certain, been mixed with something that was not mineral.

Five marks. One of them lay almost directly beneath where they were sitting.

Oscar leaned over it and did not touch it, which raised her opinion of him further. "This mark," he said, tapping the air above the fourth symbol. "That's under Thornfell. Under *us*. Isn't it."

"Under the guild hall. Or near enough. Which is why I'm here, and why I need your archive—because your founders kept parallel records. Payment schedules. Maintenance oaths. If we're going to reforge what was broken, I need to know exactly what your people promised and exactly when your people stopped paying, and I promise you the answer is in your ledgers, and I promise you further that your guildmaster does not want it found."

"Why not?"

"Because guilty men seal their archives, Oscar. And your guild has been sealing things for a very long time."

He was quiet for a long moment. Rain ticked against the shuttered window. When he looked up, there was something new in his face—not belief yet, not quite, but the particular hunger of a man who has spent his life invisible and has just been handed a reason to matter.

"My uncle sealed the east annex himself," he said slowly. "Three nights ago. After the stars went out. Before he sealed the main doors." He met her eyes. "I have keys to most of this hall. Not those."

"Then we have a problem."

"We have a problem," Oscar agreed. Then, and she would remember this afterward as the moment the alliance became real—not signed, not spoken, just *real*—he added: "But I've spent my whole life finding ways through doors that were locked on purpose. Give me until tomorrow night."

* * *

He came for her an hour past midnight, as promised, moving through the sleeping hall with the unhurried certainty of water finding a crack, and Sera followed him through corridors she was beginning to realize he knew better than the people who owned them. Twice he paused—not at sounds, but at *absences* of sound, tilting his head like a man reading a page with one word missing—and both times he changed their route without explanation.

At the end of the east wing, behind a storeroom of pickled fish whose smell alone deserved its own guard, he stopped before a wall that looked like every other wall.

"Corvane's seal is wax and authority," he murmured. "Authority's the hard part. But authority sleeps." He pressed at a joint in the stonework, and a section the width of a door swung inward on hinges that had been oiled recently—oiled *that day*, Sera noted, which meant either Corvane feared intrusion or expected it. "There's a service gap behind here. Old smuggling route. Runs behind the annex wall. My grandfather cut it; the family's always kept a back door into our own secrets."

The passage beyond was tight, dry, and utterly black until Oscar kindled a small shuttered lamp. They walked it single file, shoulders brushing stone, until the wall on their left changed character—older masonry, and set into it, at waist height, a ventilation grate through which lamplight flickered.

Through the grate, Sera saw the annex.

It was not large. It was not grand. It was a low vaulted room lined with shelving, and the shelving was *empty*—not dusty-empty, not long-abandoned empty, but recently, thoroughly, deliberately emptied, except for one iron-bound chest sitting alone on a table in the center of the floor, and beside the chest, seated in a chair as though he had been waiting there for hours, a man she had never met and recognized instantly from a hundred descriptions traded along the roads.

Corvane Thackeray, guildmaster of the Salt Guild, sat reading by lamplight at midnight in a stripped room, and he was smiling.

He turned his head. Not toward the grate—toward the wall beside it, the exact spot where a hidden listener would stand, as if the dark itself had told him.

"Come in, then," Corvane said pleasantly. "Both of you. The fish room was clever, Oscar. I taught your father the same trick."

The panel swung open before they touched it.
