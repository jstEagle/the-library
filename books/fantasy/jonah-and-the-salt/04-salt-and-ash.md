## 4. Salt and Ash

The summons came at dawn, and dawn itself was wrong.

Oscar stood in the guild hall's great chamber with two hundred smugglers packed shoulder to shoulder, and above them the skylights showed a sky the color of wet slate. No sun. No stars, because there had been no stars for three nights now, and the wards that had guarded Thornfell since before anyone's grandmother were shattered glass in the ley-lines. The lamps burned low. The guildmaster had ordered it—*conserve the oil*—as if oil were the problem.

Corvane stood on the dais beneath the Salt Crown, the old iron circlet that hadn't been worn by a guildmaster in living memory. He wore it like it fit him. That was the first thing Oscar noticed, and the thing he would think about later, when everything else had already gone to ash: how natural it looked on him.

"Brothers and sisters of the Salt Guild." Corvane's voice carried without effort; he'd never needed to raise it, not in forty years of running the passes. "You've heard the rumors. You've seen the sky. I'll tell you what I know."

He paused. He was good at pauses. He'd taught Oscar pauses.

"The border wards are broken. Not frayed—broken. Cut, by hands I do not yet know. What comes through that breach is not weather and not war as we understand it. It is older than both." He let that sit. "The villages below the pass cannot be held. Thornhollow, Marrow's Bend, the ferry settlements—all of it. We cannot save everyone."

A murmur ran through the hall. Old Tam Vessick, who'd taught Oscar to read a ridge line, called out: "Then we try anyway."

"We *cannot*," Corvane said, and something in his tone closed like a door. "Every sack of grain we carry down to the lowlands is a sack we don't have when the stores run thin. Every hand we spend guarding a wagon is a hand not watching our own walls. I did not build this guild so it could die of generosity." He spread his hands, palms open—the gesture he used when he wanted to look reasonable. "So hear my orders. We seal the stores. We inventory everything. Rationing begins today, and it begins with guild members first. The villages get nothing."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses on the ears.

"They'll starve," someone said from the back. A woman's voice. Oscar didn't see who.

"Some will," Corvane said. "That is the arithmetic of survival. I will not apologize for doing sums." He lifted the Salt Crown an inch off his brow and set it back, a small motion, almost tender. "The covenant is dead. Whatever bargain our founders made with whatever powers they made it with, it failed us. From tonight, the Salt Guild owes nothing to anyone but the Salt Guild. Dismissed."

Two hundred people filed out into the grey morning, and nobody spoke, and Oscar walked among them feeling like a man walking through deep water.

* * *

He found Corvane in the map room an hour later, alone, studying the charts of the lowland roads with a candle guttering beside him. The guildmaster didn't look up.

"You want to argue," Corvane said.

"I want to understand."

"Those are often the same question." He finally raised his head. In the candlelight his face looked older than it had three days ago, carved rather than lined. "Sit, Oscar."

Oscar sat. He'd been sitting across this table since he was nine years old, when Corvane had caught him lifting salt-cellars off the mess table for practice and instead of beating him had shown him how to lift them properly. Twenty years of that. Twenty years of *sit, Oscar*, of lessons in shadow and silence, of being told—and believing—that the guild was the only family that doesn't lie to you.

"You're the best route-runner I have," Corvane said. "Maybe the best I've ever had. So you more than anyone should understand what I'm about to say." He turned one of the maps around, the lowlands, the river towns, the string of small villages clinging to the mountain's skirt like burrs. "In a month, maybe six weeks, the dark comes down out of the high passes. I've seen what it does. Things move in it. Things that were never meant to have shapes." His finger tapped Thornhollow. "These people are dead already. They just don't know it yet. The only question is whether we die with them."

"And if it can be stopped?"

"Stopped?" For a moment something flickered behind Corvane's eyes—too fast to name, gone before Oscar could read it. "By whom? The ward-keepers are scattered or disgraced. The dragons are a bedtime story. The stars went out, boy. Whatever order the world used to have, it's gone, and men who cling to dead orders die holding their little pieces of it." He straightened. "I'm asking you to be smarter than sentimental. Can you do that?"

Oscar looked at the map. At Thornhollow, where the miller's daughter had once paid him in honey-cakes to carry letters to her sweetheart over the pass. At Marrow's Bend, where old Widow Crane left a lamp burning in her window every night he came through, though she'd never once asked what was in his packs.

"I can try," he said.

Corvane smiled, and clapped him on the shoulder, and the weight of that hand stayed with Oscar all day like a brand.

* * *

He lasted four days.

Four days of watching the storehouse doors barred and sealed with guild-wax, four days of ration lines inside the walls while outside, past the palisade, the road down to the lowlands stood empty. Four days of hearing the refugees who'd made it up to the gates turned away—turned away, at the Salt Guild, whose whole history was built on the principle that anything could be moved anywhere, for anyone, if the price was right or even if it wasn't. *We carry what others won't.* That had been the founders' creed, cut into the lintel over the main gate. Oscar had walked under it ten thousand times without reading it.

On the fourth night he went over the wall.

Not through it—over, along the drainage channel behind the tannery where the stones were slick with moss and no watchman bothered to look, because why would anyone leave? He carried one pack, and the pack weighed maybe sixty pounds, and it was the heaviest thing he'd ever lifted. Dried fish. Hardtack. A wheel of salted cheese. A jar of rendered fat. Nothing grand. Sixty pounds of food against a famine, a cup bailed from an ocean.

Thornhollow was three hours down the switchbacks, and he made it in two, running the dark trail by feel and memory because there was no moon to run it by—there was no moon, that was the other thing, the moon itself had gone dim as a coin at the bottom of a well, and the dark between the trees had texture now, had *intent*. Twice he felt something keep pace with him just off the trail, matching his stride, and twice he stopped and it stopped, and neither time did he turn to look. Some part of him understood that looking was permission.

The village smelled of woodsmoke and fear. Maybe forty souls left of the hundred and fifty who'd lived there when the stars went out. They'd shuttered every window and hung salt lines across the doors—old folk wisdom, useless, but it gave them something to do with their hands. When Oscar knocked at the miller's house, the door opened a crack and a knife-point came through it.

"It's me," he said. "Thackeray. Guild."

The knife lowered. The door opened wider, and the miller's wife looked at him the way people look at rain after drought—not joy, exactly. Something rawer. "We thought the guild had forgotten us."

"The guild hasn't forgotten you," Oscar said, and heard the lie in his own mouth, and hated it, and said it anyway, because the truth—that the guild had written them off, that a man on a dais under an iron crown had done sums and found them expendable—would break something here that couldn't be mended by sixty pounds of dried fish. "I'm here. Eat. I'll come back when I can."

He made the delivery to four houses that night, moving door to door like a thief in reverse, and then climbed back up the switchbacks with his pack light and his chest heavy, and slipped back over the wall before the fifth bell, and lay in his bunk staring at the underside of the roof beams until the grey came.

He did it again the next night. And the next.

* * *

It took eight nights before he was caught, and he wasn't caught by a watchman. He was caught by Sera.

She was in the tannery yard when he dropped down out of the drainage channel, pack empty, hands black with moss, and she was leaning against the wall with her arms crossed and her ward-keeper's badge conspicuously absent from her coat, and she said, "Sixty pounds a night won't feed a valley."

Oscar froze. Then, because he was too tired for anything else: "How long have you known?"

"Since the second night. You take the same route every time. Left foot always lands heavier." She pushed off the wall. "I'm not going to report you. I'm going to tell you you're doing it wrong."

"There's a right way to steal food from my own guild?"

"There's a right way to not get caught doing it, which matters, because a dead smuggler feeds no one." She stepped closer. In the dark her eyes were sharp and tired and older than her face. "You want to help these villages? One man, one pack, one trail? That's a candle in a hurricane. You need routes. You need runners who aren't you. You need the guild's own roads—the old ones, the ones on no chart—and you need to stop thinking of this as theft."

"What else would you call it?"

"Honesty." She said the word like it cost her. "Your guildmaster has decided the world ends at his wall. You've decided it doesn't. One of you is going to be right, and either way, you crossed a line tonight that doesn't go back. Do you understand that? Corvane forgives a lot of things in his best runner. This isn't one of them. When he finds out—and he will find out—you stop being his boy and start being his problem."

Oscar stood in the tannery yard with the smell of hides and lye and the memory of Corvane's hand on his shoulder, and he thought about the lamp in Widow Crane's window, and the miller's wife saying *we thought the guild had forgotten us*, and the Salt Crown sitting natural on a man who talked about people like arithmetic.

"He already knows," Oscar said quietly. "Doesn't he. About the wards. About what broke them. He knew before he gave the orders."

Sera went very still. "Why would you say that?"

"Because he didn't ask *what* broke them. Everyone else in that hall wanted to know what happened. He wanted to know how much oil we had." Oscar's voice came out flat and strange, like someone else's. "You don't skip the first question unless you already have the answer."

For a long moment Sera said nothing. Somewhere beyond the wall, out in the starless dark, something cried—a long, thin sound, like a bird, like a child, like nothing that had ever been either.

"Get some sleep," she said finally. "Tomorrow night, I'll show you the old roads. And Oscar—" she paused at the edge of the yard, half-turned away, "—the archive under the east stair. Third shelf, false back. There are records in there your guildmaster doesn't know survived. If you're going to betray him, know exactly what you're betraying him *for*."

She was gone before he could answer.

Oscar climbed to his bunk and lay down and did not sleep. Above the roof beams, where the skylight showed the colorless sky, he kept waiting for the guilt to come—the guilt he'd earned, surely, eight nights of theft and a lifetime of loyalty cracking down the middle like river ice. It didn't come. What came instead was quieter and worse: the memory of Corvane's smile, warm as a hearth, and the thought that he could no longer remember whether the warmth had ever been real, or whether he'd spent twenty years warming himself at a fire that had only ever been lit for its own sake.

On the ninth night, he went back over the wall. But this time he took the eastern trail, the one Sera had marked for him in charcoal on a scrap of sailcloth, and this time his pack held food for Marrow's Bend and, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom, a slim ledger he'd pried from behind the false back of the third shelf.

He didn't read it on the trail. Some doors, once opened, don't close again, and he wanted one more night of not knowing.

Below him, in the valley, a single lamp still burned in a window. He ran toward it through the dark, and the dark ran with him, and far behind, on the palisade wall of the guild hall, a watchman watched him go and made no move to stop him—which was its own kind of answer, and one he wouldn't understand until much later.
