## 5. The Passenger

The stranger was waiting at the Salt Door when Oscar came down from the wall, and that alone should have told him everything he needed to know.

Nobody found the Salt Door. That was the point of it—a crack in the cliffside behind the tannery ruins, wide enough for one man with a pack, invisible unless you knew to count the seventh fallen stone from the lightning-split pine. Oscar had been slipping through it since he was nine years old, back when his father still carried the ledgers and the guild still pretended the Thornfell routes were a secret worth keeping. In twenty years, exactly three people had ever waited for him there.

Two had been guild. The third had tried to kill him.

She stood with her hood back, letting the grey morning light find her face, and she did not look like she was trying to hide. That was its own kind of message. A woman who wanted to be unseen would have kept the hood up. This one wanted him to see her—wanted him to see the scar first, probably, because it was impossible not to. It started above her left eyebrow, dragged down through the brow itself, which had never grown back, and forked at the cheekbone into two pale branches, one ending at her jaw and one running almost to her mouth. Old burn tissue, he thought. Very old. The kind of scar you got from standing too close to something enormous and hot.

"You're Thackeray," she said. Not a question.

"I'm nobody."

"You're the youngest smuggler the Salt Guild ever licensed, you run the High Pass alone in winter, and last week you carried two hundredweight of grain to Hollowmere while Corvane's own quartermasters were counting sacks in the cellar." Her voice was low and even, like water over flat stones. "You're not nobody. You're just very good at pretending."

Oscar looked past her, down the trail, up the scree. Nothing moving. The birds were quiet, but they'd been quiet since the night the stars went out, and he'd stopped trusting them as an alarm anyway.

"Who sent you?"

"No one." She pulled a purse from her belt—not guild coin, not border scrip, but old gold, heavy, stamped with sigils no mint had used in living memory—and set it on the flat stone between them. "I need passage over the pass. Tonight. I'll pay double what the guild charges and triple what you'd ask, because I know you'll ask yourself whether you should take me at all, and I want the answer to be yes before you finish wondering."

He didn't touch the gold. "The pass is closed."

"The pass is *empty*," she said. "There's a difference. Your ward-keepers are gone or dead, your patrols stopped three days ago, and whatever used to live in the high places has either fled or is waiting for something. You know it. I can see you know it." She tilted her head, and the firelight of some memory moved behind her eyes. "You've been crossing anyway. Delivering to the villages Corvane's decided aren't worth saving. So don't tell me the pass is closed, Oscar Thackeray. Tell me why you keep opening it."

He should have walked away. He heard himself say: "Where are you going?"

"Up. Over. Down the far side." She smiled without much humor. "The destination isn't the secret. The passenger is."

* * *

They left at dusk, because Oscar's routes lived and died by the light, and the light had become unreliable in ways he refused to think about too closely. Since the Night Without Stars, the dark came down like a lid. No moon worth naming—the moon hung up there, pale and wrong, like a coin at the bottom of a well. No stars at all. Just black, and the mountains' shapes against slightly-less-black, and the sound of the wind doing what wind does when it has nothing left to say.

Wren climbed well. That surprised him. He'd expected the scarred stranger to be a liability, someone he'd have to haul over the bad stretches, and instead she moved with a sure-footedness that bordered on insulting, finding holds in rock he'd have sworn weren't there. She carried almost nothing. One pack, small. One knife, plain. When he offered her a rope at the Chimney—a forty-foot scramble where he'd watched a guild man named Aldous fall to his death eleven years ago—she looked at the rope, then at him, and said, "Thank you," in a tone that suggested she was filing the offer away somewhere for later consideration.

"Most people take the rope," he said.

"Most people fall anyway. It's not the climbing that kills them. It's the doubting." She took the rope. "But thank you."

They made the first shelter—an old smuggler's cache behind a stone lip, big enough for two if neither minded elbows—at midnight, or what passed for midnight now. Oscar built a fire small enough to hide under his coat if patrols came, though he suspected no patrols would come again for a long time. Wren sat across from it and held her hands toward the flame, and he noticed she did it the way other people warmed themselves at a hearth: slowly, reverently, like greeting someone.

"Where'd you get the scar?" he asked, because silence with strangers had never come naturally to him, and because the question had been sitting in his chest since the Salt Door.

She didn't flinch. He'd expected flinching; people with scars like hers usually had a story rehearsed, a bandit attack, a kitchen fire, something quick and forgettable. Instead she studied him across the coals for a long moment, as if deciding how much truth a smuggler could carry.

"Fire," she said finally. "Very large fire. A long time ago."

"That's not an answer."

"It's most of one." Her mouth curved. "The rest of it isn't mine to give yet. Ask me again after the pass."

"After the pass," he repeated. "That's convenient."

"It's honest." She fed a twig to the flames. "You deal in secrets for a living, Thackeray. You know better than anyone that some cargo can't be opened until it reaches its destination. Consider me freight."

He laughed despite himself—a short, rusty sound. He couldn't remember the last time he'd laughed on this route alone. That was the thing about invisibility, the thing he never said out loud: it worked. It kept you alive. And every year it worked a little better, until you could walk through a room full of people who'd known you all your life and feel certain that not one of them could describe your face. Wren looked at him like she could describe his face. It was unsettling. It was also, he realized with a jolt of something close to grief, the first time in years anyone had bothered to look.

"Corvane put out the order," he said. "No traffic over the pass. Anyone caught crossing gets stripped of license and left for the mountain."

"And here you are."

"Here I am."

"Why?"

Oscar turned his hands toward the fire. Below them, somewhere in the black, Hollowmere's few remaining lamps were going out one by one, the way lamps did now, village by village, as the salt ran short and the hope ran shorter. "Because the guild taught me every road worth hiding," he said, "and then it told me to let the people on those roads die. I'm still working out which lesson I believe."

Wren watched him for a moment longer than was comfortable. Then she lay back against her pack, closed her eyes, and said, "Sleep. I'll take first watch."

"I don't sleep on the first night with a passenger."

"Yes, you do," she said mildly. "You just don't know it yet."

He meant to argue. The mountain took the argument out of him somewhere between one breath and the next, and he slept deeper than he had since the stars went out, dreamless and warm, which should have frightened him more than it did.

* * *

The bandits hit them at the Saddle, halfway through the second day, and they were not the bandits Oscar knew.

He knew the Thornfell reavers—guild-adjacent, half-starved, predictable men who took tolls and mostly honored them, because the guild's retaliation was worse than the toll. These were different. Eight of them, maybe nine, coming off the ridge in the starless noon-dark with no torches and no calls, moving like things that had learned to see in the new black. Their eyes caught the grey light wrong. Flat. Hungry. Whatever had bred in the months since the wards fell, it wore human shapes and had forgotten the rest.

Oscar got his knife out and did the arithmetic he always did—terrain, numbers, exits—and the arithmetic came back the way it always came back lately: bad. They were pinned against the Saddle's drop with scree below and rock above, and the shapes were spreading along the ridgeline with the patience of things that had already won.

"Wren," he said quietly. "Behind me."

That was instinct, stupid chivalric instinct, and he was already ashamed of it when she stepped past him instead.

"Stay down," she said.

He stayed down, mostly because his legs decided it for him.

What happened next, he would spend a long time failing to describe. There was light—but not firelight, not sunlight, nothing with a source he could point to. It came out of her, out of the space around her, a gold so deep it was nearly red, and it moved the way a living thing moves. For one heartbeat—one impossible heartbeat—he saw her as she was: vast, winged, crowned in flame, a shape that filled the sky of some older world, wings like storm-fronts, eyes like the sun remembering itself. The vision lasted less time than it takes to blink, and it burned itself into him permanently anyway.

The bandits did not get a heartbeat. The light hit them and they came apart—not burned, *unmade*, unraveling into smoke and ash that the wind took south, and the sound they made as they went was not screaming but something worse: relief. As if whatever had been wearing them was glad to be let go.

Then it was over. The ridge was empty. The scree smoked gently. Oscar was on his knees in the dirt with his knife still in his hand, useless, and his whole body shaking.

Wren stood with her back to him, and he watched the light drain out of her the way heat drains from a stone at evening. Her shoulders curved inward. When she turned, she looked smaller. Older. The scar stood out white against skin gone grey as ash, and she swayed once before catching herself on the rock.

"You're—" His voice cracked. He started over. "You're a dragon."

"A dragon," she agreed. Her voice was rougher now, scraped thin. "Or what's left of one. Exiled. Diminished. Wearing this." She looked down at her own hands—human hands, scarred knuckles, trembling faintly—as if confirming they were still there. "The fire I just spent took thirty years off what remains of my mortal span. I'd appreciate it if we didn't make a habit of that."

Oscar stared at her. At the ridge. At the drifting ash. His mind kept snagging on the same impossibility: the dragons were gone. Everyone knew the dragons were gone. The covenant that had bound them to the realms had failed generations ago—that was the oldest story the guild told, the founding myth, the reason the Salt Guild existed at all, sworn to keep the old passes and honor the old pacts that no dragon remained to honor. Dead gods. Empty skies. That was the world.

And one of them had just saved his life for the price of a purse of old gold and passage over a pass.

"The stars," he said. It came out before he could stop it. "The night they went out. Did you—do you know why?"

Something moved across her face. Grief, he thought, or guilt, or both wearing each other's clothes.

"I know why," Wren said. "And it's the reason I'm crossing this pass, and the reason I needed a smuggler who knows roads no guildmaster watches, and the reason I couldn't tell you any of it until you'd seen me and hadn't run." She straightened, wincing, and met his eyes. "Your guild's founders made a promise, Oscar Thackeray. A binding promise, sealed in stone and fire. Someone broke it. Badly. And the sky is dying of it."

Below them, far down the southern slopes, the lowlands stretched away into a darkness that no noon should hold. Somewhere out in that darkness, he knew, were villages going lampless, wards rotting off their moorings, and a guildmaster sealing doors against a problem he'd never explained.

Oscar looked at the dragon in her borrowed skin—at the scar, at the exhaustion, at the terrible patience in her face—and felt the ground shift under everything he'd believed about who he worked for and why.

"How many stones?" he asked, because some part of him, the part raised on guild stories, already knew the shape of the answer.

Wren's eyes widened slightly—the first genuine surprise he'd seen from her.

"Five," she said. "How did you—"

"I didn't," Oscar said. "But I think someone's about to tell me."

He picked up his pack, turned north toward the high pass, and began to climb.
