## 6. Covenant-Stones

Sera had not slept in three days, and the not-sleeping had a texture now, a grain to it, like sand worked into the joints of her hands. She sat in the smugglers' safehouse below the old toll road with a cup of barley tea gone cold between her palms and watched the dragon pretend to be a woman.

Wren sat by the hearth the way no human sat—perfectly still, weight distributed as if she might need to rise without warning or never rise at all. The firelight did something wrong against her skin. It didn't flicker across it so much as pool, the way light pools on river stone. The scars on her forearms caught the glow and held it a half-second too long before letting go.

"You're staring," Wren said, without turning.

"I'm studying."

"Those are different words for the same crime." But there was no edge in it. There was very little of anything in Wren's voice, which Sera was learning to read as patience rather than emptiness. A creature that had lived through the death of its own kind could afford patience. It was the rest of them who were running out of time.

Oscar Thackeray came down the ladder from the trapdoor with an armful of wax-sealed ledgers and the particular set of his shoulders that meant he'd been arguing with himself and losing. He dropped the ledgers on the table hard enough to rattle Sera's tea.

"The archive," he said. "You wanted everything from the founders' era. This is what survived the fire of—whatever year it was. Half of these are water-damaged. Half of the other half are cookbooks."

"Cookbooks can hide things," Sera said. "Ciphers live best in boring places. No one guards a recipe for pickled ramsons."

Oscar pulled out a stool and sat across from her, elbows on the table, and looked at her with that flat, measuring stare she'd seen him give border patrols. Assessing whether she was worth the risk. She supposed she hadn't passed yet.

"So talk," he said. "You've been circling it since you walked into the guild hall with your ward-keeper's badge snapped in half. The covenant. What my founders actually did. Say it plain, because I've spent two nights reading about salt-pork preservation methods and I'm losing my patience."

Sera set down the cup. Her hands were steadier than they deserved to be.

"Plain, then." She drew a breath. "Your guild was founded on a lie. Not a small one. Not the ordinary rot of men skimming profits and forging ledgers. A founding lie, load-bearing, the kind that holds up a whole civilization—and when it finally gives way, everything built on top comes down with it."

The fire popped. Wren's head turned by degrees, like a weathervane finding wind.

"The Thornfell wards," Sera said, "the ones that kept the mountain passes safe, that kept the shadow-things in the lowlands and the storms off the high roads—they were never ward-craft. Not really. Ward-keeping is my order's discipline, yes, but we maintain. We do not create. The original wards were laid by dragons. Dragon-fire braided into the stone of the mountains themselves, older than any kingdom on the map. Your Salt Guild didn't build those wards. Your Salt Guild *inherited* them. Under contract."

"What contract?"

"The covenant." She said the word and felt it land in the room like a coin dropped down a well—Oscar flinched, actually flinched, and she understood that somewhere in his childhood, some guild-elder had said that word to him once, hushed, the way you say words you don't want children repeating. "Five stones," she went on. "Covenant-stones. Each one cut from the heartfire of a living dragon and bound into the peaks at five points—the five anchors of the ward-net. As long as the stones were tended, the fire renewed, the oaths spoken at each turning of the year, the wards held. In exchange, the dragons asked for almost nothing. Passage. Respect. A share of the harvest from the lowland fields in famine years. And the promise—" here her voice did something she hated, thinned, went rough, "—the promise that mankind would never again hunt them. That the burning years were over, and the mountains would be sanctuary, forever."

"And your founders broke it," Oscar said. Not a question. He'd read enough of the ledgers by now; she could see the shape of the knowledge sitting behind his eyes, ugly and half-formed, waiting for her to finish casting it.

"They broke it slowly, which is how the worst betrayals are done. First they stopped making the pilgrimage to renew the fifth stone—it was far, and the road was hard, and the dragons seemed distant and unthreatening, and what harm could one skipped year do? Then they stopped speaking the oaths aloud because speaking them required admitting, in front of witnesses, that the guild's authority flowed from someone else. Then, four generations ago, a guildmaster named Aldous Vane discovered that if you chipped even a sliver from a covenant-stone, the fire inside it could be drawn out and sold. Bottled. Burned in lamps that never guttered. There were buyers in the southern courts who would pay more for a thimble of dragon-fire than a village earned in a decade."

Oscar's jaw tightened. "And the guild got rich."

"The guild got rich," Sera agreed. "And the stones got thin. And the dragons—" She glanced at Wren, and Wren gave her a single slow nod, permission granted. "The dragons came to reason with us. Three of them, in their true shapes, landing on the guild hall's roof the way they had every right to. And the guildmaster of that day panicked, or schemed—I've never learned which, and I'm no longer sure it matters. He turned the wards' remaining strength against them. Used the covenant itself as a weapon. Two died on the mountainside. One escaped south, wounded, and was never seen again."

Wren spoke then, still facing the fire. "I was the youngest," she said. "I ran. I have been running for ninety years, wearing this." She lifted one scarred forearm into the light. "This is what is left of me. Skin over embers. Every year there is less fire and more woman, and one day there will be only woman, and then I will die the way humans die, and that will be the merciful version of the story. The cruel version is that I outlive the last of my fire and spend my final centuries as a thing that remembers being vast." She lowered her arm. "So. Now you know what your guild did. Now you know why the stars went out."

Oscar stared at her. Sera watched him work through it—the boy who had grown up invisible, moving contraband through mountains whose safety he'd taken as simply the way the world was, learning that the world had been mortgaged generations before his birth and the note had just come due.

"The stars," he said hoarsely. "How does stealing fire put out the *stars*?"

"Because the stars aren't fires," Sera said. "Not the way you think of fire. The old texts call them the high hearths—the same substance as dragon-heartfire, burning at the roof of the world. The covenant bound the low fires to the high ones. Dragon-fire was never just heat. It was a thread in a weave that runs from a hearthstone in Thornfell all the way up past the sky. When the dragons were slaughtered and the stones were drained, the threads began to snap. The wards fell first because they were nearest. The stars will follow, star by star, unless the weave is mended. Unless the fire is rekindled at its source."

Silence. Oscar rubbed both hands over his face, and when he lowered them he looked ten years older and somehow more awake than he had since the night the sky went dark.

"Tell me it can be mended," he said. "Tell me there's a way, because if you climbed all this way and dug through my guild's rotten archive just to tell me the world is dying and there's nothing to be done, I'm going to walk out that door and keep walking."

"There is a way," Sera said. "It is terrible, and it is probably impossible, and I am telling you anyway." She spread her hands flat on the table. "The five covenant-stones must be gathered. All five, reunited, brought to the first hearth—the original forge where the covenant was sworn, high in the Thornfell massif, above the tree line, above the snow line, in a place the maps stopped marking on purpose. There, the stones must be reforged into one, and the oath must be spoken again—not by a guildmaster, not by a ward-keeper, but by someone with the standing to swear it honestly. Someone who owes nothing to the betrayal and can therefore renounce it clean."

"And then?"

"And then the stones need fire to wake them. True fire. Heartfire." Sera let the implication sit. "There is exactly one heart left in the world that I know of, and she is sitting at your hearth drinking your tea."

They argued for most of the night. Sera catalogued the arguments afterward, in the way she catalogued everything, because cataloguing was how she kept guilt at arm's length: Oscar objected that Wren had barely enough fire left to warm herself, let alone reignite a covenant; Wren replied that she had not survived ninety years of exile to die comfortable; Oscar objected that none of them knew where the stones were; and it was there, at the third objection, that the conversation changed key entirely.

"I know where three are," Wren said.

The room went quiet. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"Ninety years is a long time," Wren continued, "when you are trying to decide whether to hate your murderers or mourn them. I did both. Mourning requires visiting graves, and the graves of my kin are the places where they fell. So I visited. I walked the realms in this borrowed body, and I found what became of the stones your people drained and abandoned." She raised one finger. "The first lies in the Drowned Chapel, in the fen country north of the Saltmere road. When the wards weakened, the lowlands flooded, and a congregation that refused to leave their chapel drowned with it. They remain. They are not friendly. But the chapel's crypt was cut from older stone than the chapel, and the oldest stone in that crypt is ours. The first anchor sleeps beneath the altar, under black water, under the dead."

"A drowned chapel full of ghosts," Oscar said flatly. "Wonderful. And the second?"

Wren's second finger rose. "The second was carried away. After the killing, one of the guild's own—a junior factor named Maren Cole, whose name your histories have scratched out—took the eastern stone and fled rather than let it be drained. She crossed the pass into the refugee lands, and her descendants kept it, generation after generation, without knowing what it was. A hearth-stone. A family heirloom. A pretty rock that never cooled and never cracked and made the crops grow strange and good around whatever house held it." Something moved across Wren's face that might have been the ghost of a smile. "I traced the line as far as the city of Bellharbor, before the darkening. If they survive, the stone survives with them. Cities full of frightened people are good places to hide, and bad places to search."

"And the third?" Sera asked, though she already suspected, because Wren's voice had changed—had gone quieter, the way voices go near open graves.

"The third never left the mountain," Wren said. "It is the stone my mother anchored. When she fell, the guild couldn't crack it—her fire was too fierce, and it sealed itself rather than be desecrated. So they buried it. Sealed it in the caldera of the sleeping volcano at the heart of the Ashreach, walled up behind a hundred feet of basalt, and told themselves the mountain would keep their crime warm." She looked into the fire. "The mountain has kept it. The mountain has also grown restless, these ninety years, the way wounds grow restless. To reach the third stone, someone must go down into the Ashreach while it sleeps, and pray it keeps sleeping."

Oscar had gone pale. Sera recognized the look. It was the look of a man doing arithmetic with lives instead of coin, and finding the sum unbearable.

"That's three," he said. "What about the other two? You said five."

"The fourth," Sera said, "is beyond the maps. Literally. The old ward-texts speak of a fifth anchor laid in the far west, past the Thornfell massif, in country no guild survey ever charted because the dragons asked them not to. Some things were meant to stay unwitnessed. If it still exists, we find it the way pilgrims find anything—by walking until the walking teaches us." She paused. "And the fifth..."

"The fifth is here," Wren said softly. "It never left. The first hearth, the forge where the covenant was sworn, sits above your guild's oldest smuggling road, and the fifth stone was always kept within it. Tended. Fed. At least until recently." Her eyes lifted to Oscar's, and there was no accusation in them, which somehow made it worse. "Ask your guildmaster what burns in the great hearth of the guild hall, Oscar Thackeray. Ask him why the flame there never goes out, no matter how long the hall stands empty. Ask him what Corvane has been feeding it—or feeding *on*—while the rest of the stones starved."

Sera watched the boy absorb that. Watched him think of the guild hall's hearth, the one every member was taught to bow to on entering, the eternal flame, the pride of the Salt Guild—and watch the pride curdle into suspicion in real time. It was a hard thing to watch. It was necessary. Hope, she had learned, was worthless until it had been dragged through the mud of exactly how bad things were.

"So that's the task," Oscar said at last. His voice was steady, but Sera heard the effort holding it steady, a rope under load. "Recover a stone from a chapel full of drowned ghosts. Find a lost heirloom in a city of refugees under a starless sky. Dig a third out of a volcano that hates us. Walk off the edge of every map for a fourth. And take the fifth from the man who runs the most powerful secret society in the mountains, who has spent his life learning to kill people quietly." He laughed—one short, airless syllable. "Three people. That's the expedition. A disgraced ward-keeper, a smuggler, and the last dragon in the world, wearing a body that's running out of fire."

"Yes," Sera said.

"It's impossible."

"Almost certainly."

He looked at her. Then at Wren. Then at the cold tea, the water-damaged ledgers, the trapdoor hanging open above them onto a night with no stars in it anywhere.

"When do we leave?" he said.

Sera felt something shift in her chest then—something she hadn't felt since the night her wards failed and her badge snapped and her order cast her out. She'd have called it hope, except hope was supposed to feel lighter than this. This felt like picking up a burden she'd been pretending wasn't hers for twenty years, and finding that her back remembered exactly how to carry it.

"Dawn," she said. "We leave at dawn. And Oscar—before we go, there's something you should know about the first stone. About the chapel." She hesitated, and hated herself for hesitating, and said it anyway. "The drowned don't give things to strangers. The old rites say they demand a confession first. A true one. Whatever each claimant carries." She met his eyes. "Think tonight about what yours will be. Because I've read the accounts, and the ghosts always know when you're lying."

Above them, through the trapdoor, the starless dark pressed down on the mountains like a held breath—and somewhere far to the north, beneath black water, something ancient shifted in its sleep, and waited to hear what the living would dare to say.
