## 7. The Guildmaster's Ledger

The archive sat beneath the guild hall like a secret the building was ashamed of.

Oscar had known it existed since he was nine years old—every Salt Guild child knew—but knowing and being allowed were different things. The stairs down were behind the wine cellar, behind a door that looked like a wall, behind a lock that took Sera three tries and a muttered oath to turn. The air below smelled of dust and tallow and something older underneath, like stone that remembered being wet.

"Touch nothing with your fingers," Sera said, lighting the first of the lamps they'd carried down. "Oils ruin the vellum. There are gloves in that crate."

"You've been down here before."

"I was ward-keeper for eleven years, Thackeray. Where do you think I learned what wards were *for*?" She pulled on a pair of cracked leather gloves and surveyed the room. Shelves ran floor to ceiling in crooked rows, sagging under ledgers, scroll-cases, bundles of correspondence tied with salt-stiff cord. A map table dominated the center, its surface buried under decades of unfiled papers. "Corvane hasn't opened this door since he took the chair. That's not neglect. That's a man who knows exactly what's down here and wants it staying quiet."

Oscar pulled on gloves too big for his hands and tried to feel like an archivist instead of a thief. It didn't take. He'd spent his whole life reading rooms for exits, locks for weaknesses, men for lies. An archive was just another smuggling route—the cargo was truth, and someone had hidden it well.

They worked in silence broken only by paper. Sera took the ward-records along the eastern wall; Oscar started at the map table, sorting by year. The ledgers were the guild's own hand: tolls collected, routes burned, bribes logged with the cheerful precision of people who'd never imagined anyone would read them. He found his father's name twice, once for a run through the Widow's Teeth and once for a fine levied against him for shorting a client. He put that page down carefully, as if it might bruise.

Hours passed. His lamp burned low. He found nothing but the ordinary machinery of a criminal enterprise—until he found the gap.

"The records stop," he said.

Sera looked up. "Stop how?"

"They don't stop." He turned the ledger toward her. "They get thin. Look—every year from the founding forward, there's a full accounting. Routes, payments, ward-tithes to the dragon-realms. Then here—" he tapped the page, dated ninety-one years back, "—the handwriting changes. Same clerk, same ink, but suddenly half the entries are just initials. And the ward-tithes stop entirely. No record of the last payment. No record of why."

Sera came around the table fast enough to knock her lamp. She read the page twice, and Oscar watched her face do something complicated.

"Ninety-one years," she said quietly. "The wards held for six hundred years before that. Six hundred years of tithe and covenant, and then one autumn the payments just... end." She straightened. "There has to be a closing entry. A settlement. Something. Guild law doesn't let a debt die unwitnessed."

"So where is it?"

She didn't answer. She was already moving, pulling scroll-cases off the shelf, checking dates against their contents with the speed of a woman who had spent years doing exactly this kind of hunting and hated every second of it. Oscar joined her. They tore through the archive's last century like smugglers searching a hold for contraband—which, he supposed, was exactly what they were.

It was Oscar who found the false spine.

He'd learned false spines at fourteen, from a fence in Lowmark who sold banned books to scholars too respectable to be seen buying them. You could always tell because the binding was too neat. Everything else in this archive was warped by damp and time; this one ledger sat on its shelf square and proud, its leather uncracked, its gilt still bright.

He drew it out. It was heavier than it should have been. The cover bore no title, only the guild's mark—a salt cellar crossed with a mountain pick—stamped in silver gone grey.

"Sera."

She came over. He set the book on the map table and opened it, and both of them went very still.

It wasn't a trade ledger. It was a private one. The hand was elegant, old-fashioned, and it began with a date Oscar knew by heart, though he'd never been taught it: the night the last dragon left Thornfell. Ninety-one years ago. The founder's hand. Aldric Vane himself, first guildmaster, whose portrait hung above the hall's hearth with eyes that followed you across the room.

*The tithe will not be paid,* the first entry read. *Let them call it breach if they like. I call it arithmetic.*

Oscar read on. Sera read over his shoulder, her breath shallow.

Page after page. Vane's voice, dry and cold and reasonable, laying out the case like a merchant defending a bad debt. The dragons demanded salt, iron, and first passage through the peaks—and gave back only *wards*, invisible promises stitched into the sky. What did the guild get? Nothing it could hold. Nothing it could sell. Meanwhile the lowland lords paid gold for what the dragons gave free.

*A covenant is a contract,* Vane wrote, *and a contract only binds the party too poor to break it.*

Then, further in, the handwriting changed—not the words, just the pressure. Tighter. Angrier. Entries about negotiations with someone called only *the Quiet Partner*. Payments made in land-grants. In toll rights. In *darkness*, though Vane never spelled out what he meant by that, only circled the word again and again until the vellum wore thin.

And then, near the end, a single page that stopped Oscar's breath:

*The Partner asks what we will do when the sky goes out. I tell him: the same thing we have always done. We carry what others need through the dark, and we charge for the carrying. He finds this amusing. He says the stars were always a tax on hope. I am beginning to agree.*

"He knew." Sera's voice came out flat and strange. "Aldric Vane knew what breaking the covenant would cost. He wrote it down. He *planned* for it." She gripped the table's edge. "Six hundred years of ward-keepers feeding the lines, generation after generation, and the man who founded our order sold the sky for toll rights."

"That's not the worst part." Oscar turned pages with hands that weren't quite steady. "Look at the dates. Vane died forty years after this. But the ledger keeps going." And it did—different hands now, each new guildmaster adding his entries in turn, a relay of betrayal passed down like a family heirloom. Toll schedules renegotiated with the Quiet Partner. Ward-stations quietly decommissioned, their keepers reassigned or dismissed. Sera's own dismissal was in here, eleven months ago, logged in Corvane's precise hand: *Keeper Voss removed. Lines allowed to thin. No inquiry requested.*

"They didn't just inherit the betrayal," she said. "They *maintained* it. Every guildmaster since Vane kept the arrangement alive. Kept the wards starving." She laughed, one note, no humor in it. "I spent my career patching holes in a dam these men were drilling on purpose."

Oscar kept turning pages. Because there was more, and some part of him—the part that had grown up on Corvane's stories, on the guildmaster's rough hand on his shoulder, on *you're the best of us, boy*—needed to reach the end. Needed to find the place where the story changed. Where Corvane found the ledger and burned it, or fought it, or at least hesitated.

The final section was in Corvane's hand alone.

It began eight years ago. Neat entries, businesslike, the same script that signed Oscar's wages. Meetings with the Quiet Partner—no longer a partner now but named, at last, in a single line Corvane seemed to have written without letting himself think about it: *our friend beneath the fells.* Agreements about the border wards. Timetables. Oscar read the timetable twice, three times, matching dates in his memory to nights he'd been running salt through the passes, nights the wards had flickered and guttered and he'd blamed weather, age, bad luck.

*The outer line fails in spring,* Corvane had written. *The inner by midsummer. Let the villages learn to need us. Need is the only loyalty that lasts.*

And then the last entry, dated four days before the stars went out. Four days. Oscar had been in the hall that week. He'd eaten dinner two tables away from this man. Corvane had asked after his mother's grave, ruffled his hair like an uncle, told him he was the future of the guild.

*All is arranged,* the entry read. *When the dark comes, the lowlands will burn and the peaks will endure, and everything between the passes will belong to those strong enough to hold it. The Partner keeps his word—he has kept it for ninety years. Dominion is not given. It is taken from those too soft to keep it. Let the sky go out. We will be the only light left, and they will pay whatever we ask to stand near it.*

Oscar closed the ledger.

For a long moment he couldn't move. He stood in the lamplight with his gloved hands flat on the cover and felt something in his chest come apart—not loudly, not dramatically, just quietly, the way a rope parts strand by strand until the last one goes and the whole load drops.

He had known Corvane was hard. Everyone knew. Hard men built hard guilds; that was the catechism he'd been raised on. He had known the guild took its cut, broke its rules, buried its dead in unmarked rows behind the hall. He had made his peace with all of it, worn it like a second skin, because the guild had fed him when no one else would and the mountains didn't forgive softness.

But this. This wasn't hardness. This was a man who had looked up at the stars—the same stars Oscar navigated by, the same stars his mother had pointed out over the Widow's Teeth when he was small enough to sit in her lap—and decided they were a *tax*. Decided that every village below the passes, every shepherd, every child, was a price worth paying for a throne of darkness. And worse: that he hadn't done it alone, hadn't even *started* it. That the rot went back ninety-one years, back to the founder whose portrait smiled down on every guild ceremony, back through every guildmaster Oscar had ever sworn to. The betrayal wasn't a wound. It was the foundation.

"Thackeray." Sera's hand was on his arm. "Breathe."

"I'm breathing."

"You're not. Sit down before you fall down." She pushed him onto a stool and poured water from their flask, and he drank it mechanically while she took the ledger and read the last pages herself. He watched her face as she reached Corvane's entry about her own dismissal, and saw the exact moment it landed—the flinch, quickly mastered, of a woman learning she'd been discarded on a schedule.

"He used me," she said. "Eleven years I kept those lines alive with spit and prayer, and he was logging my failures like harvest figures." She set the ledger down with exaggerated care, the way you set down something you want to throw. "We have to move. If Corvane learns this book survived—if he ever suspects—"

"He won't suspect it exists. He thinks it's a rumor." Oscar heard his own voice come out level and was vaguely grateful for it. Smuggler's training: panic privately, perform calm publicly. "Why do you think it's hidden so well? Someone wanted it found eventually. Just not by him."

"Who?"

"Doesn't matter yet." He stood. His legs held. "What matters is what's in it. Proof. Dates, signatures, the Quiet Partner's name. Enough to turn every guild member who still has a conscience, and enough to show the lowlands who killed their sky." He wrapped the ledger in oilcloth from his pack, quick and practiced, hiding contraband. Some habits were finally useful. "Wren needs to see this. If her fire is tied to the covenant, then this book tells her exactly how it was broken—and maybe how to mend it."

"And Corvane?"

Oscar paused. Above them, muffled by floors of stone, the guild hall was waking—boots on boards, the clatter of the morning mess, voices calling routes and tallies. The sounds of home. They hadn't changed. That was the terrible thing. The world had ended four nights ago and the guild still sounded like itself, because the guild had always known the darkness was coming. Had *scheduled* it.

"When I was twelve," Oscar said, "I got caught by a toll-warden on the Lowmark road. First time. Corvane paid the fine, walked me back through the pass, and told me the guild's first rule. Not *don't get caught*. He said: *the guild is the only family that will never sell you.*" He slung his pack. "I believed him for fifteen years. Turns out he was right about the rule. He just never said which side of the sale we'd be on."

They climbed the stairs and reset the door behind them, and Oscar carried the ledger against his chest like a coal he couldn't put down and couldn't drop.

He was halfway across the cellar when the messenger found him—young Tam, breathless, snow in his hair.

"There you are. Guildmaster wants you. Now." The boy swallowed. "He's mustering everyone in the hall. Says the wards on the western passes didn't just fail." Tam's voice dropped, and his eyes were wide with a fear that had nothing to do with the message and everything to do with delivering it. "Says something's coming *up* the pass road. Says it's got Wren's scent, and it's asking for her by name."

Oscar's hand tightened on the oilcloth bundle until the ledger's corners bit through.

Behind him, faint through the floor, he could almost hear the sound of a hearth-flame burning steady in a hall built on lies—and somewhere far below the fells, something patient beginning to climb.
