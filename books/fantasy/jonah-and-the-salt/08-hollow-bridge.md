## 8. Hollow Bridge

Wren had crossed a thousand bridges in her long life, and she had burned most of them.

Not literally—though there had been that one in the Ashen Vale, and the one over the Serpent's Teeth, and the whole span at Kell's Landing that had taken three days and a storm to properly consume. Dragons held grudges against bridges. A bridge was a human thing: a promise that two separated places could be joined, that a gap could be trusted not to swallow you. Dragons had never believed in promises that easy.

The bridge below her now was called Hollow Bridge, and it had stopped believing in itself a century ago.

It sagged across the gorge like the spine of something long dead. The lowlands beyond it spread out under a sky with no stars in it—a sky like a lid, like a hand pressed flat over a candle. Wren felt the absence of them the way a man feels a missing tooth: constantly, with the tongue of her soul worrying the empty socket. She had flown beneath those stars for six hundred years. She had named some of them, in the old tongue, when naming was still allowed.

Now they were gone, and something was growing in the dark they'd left behind.

"You're staring at it like it's going to bite you," Oscar said, coming up beside her. He moved quietly even on open ground—the smuggler's habit, weight rolled from heel to edge of foot, as if the world were always listening for him. Maybe it was. "It's just a bridge."

"It's just a bridge," Wren agreed. "It's what's on the other side I don't trust."

Sera crouched at the lip of the gorge, wind pulling gray strands loose from her braid, and studied the far bank through a cracked spyglass. "No movement," she said. "No birds either. No insects." She lowered the glass and looked at Wren, and her eyes did the thing they always did when she was about to say something she'd rather not say: they went very still, very careful, like a woman stepping onto ice. "You've been down there before. Since the stars went out."

It wasn't a question. Sera didn't waste questions on things she already knew the answer to; she spent them like a miser, only where they'd earn interest.

"Once," Wren said. "To look for the first stone's trail."

"And?"

"And the dark down there isn't empty." Wren turned back to the bridge. The ropes of it were furred with frost, though the season was wrong for frost, though the sun had been up for two hours. Cold pooled in the gorge like water in a cup. "Empty dark is just dark. This dark is *occupied*."

Oscar shifted his pack higher on his shoulder. He'd said almost nothing since dawn—not since the night in the drowned chapel, not since his confession to the ghosts, not since he'd walked out of that flooded nave with the first covenant-stone wrapped in oilcloth against his chest like a second heart. Wren had watched him carry it for two days without once asking to hold it. That was its own kind of confession, she thought. The boy who had grown up invisible, learning to carry something precious openly.

"Occupied by what?" he asked.

"By what the dark makes," Wren said. "When the stars are watching, shadows stay shadows. They keep their shape because something above them keeps the rules. Take the stars away—" she shrugged, and the gesture cost more than she let it show, "—and shadows start making their own rules. They get hungry. They get *curious*. They find shapes that fit them."

Sera stood, joints cracking, and slung the spyglass away. "Shade-wights," she said. "My order had names for them. Old ward-lore. We thought they were stories to keep apprentices from wandering."

"They were stories," Wren said. "Stories are how humans remember true things after they've forgotten why."

She didn't tell them the rest. She didn't tell them that she'd seen a shade-wight take the shape of a shepherd on that first trip, standing in a field of black grass, waving cheerfully at her until she got close enough to see that its smile went all the way around its head. She didn't tell them that her fire—the fire she'd carried out of exile in a body that starved it, hoarded it, rationed it like a dying woman's coin—had barely been enough to drive one of them off, and there would be more than one down there. There were always more than one. Dark was cheap. Fire was not.

She especially didn't tell them that every time she burned, she burned a little less. That the mortal body she wore was a wick, and the flame inside it was finite, and each expenditure brought her closer to a threshold she could feel but not name. Somewhere ahead of her was a last ember. She intended never to meet it.

"Then we go around," Oscar said. He was already scanning the gorge's edges, reading it the way he read everything—for routes, for options, for the path nobody else had thought of. It was what made him good at what he'd been made into. It might, Wren thought, be what saved him from it. "There has to be a ford upstream."

"There is," Sera said. "Three days north. And Corvane's hunters are two days behind us, which means we'd meet them at the ford with a river at our backs." She said it flatly, without drama. She'd been doing that more lately—laying out terrible arithmetic and letting it stand on its own. "Or we cross here, today, through whatever's waiting on the far side, and we're a day ahead of them instead of a day behind."

Oscar looked at the bridge. Then at Wren. He was learning to read her too, she realized. Not well yet—he was a boy, and she was older than his language—but he was learning. "How bad?" he asked her. Just her. Directly.

"Honestly?" Wren smiled, and put everything she had into making the smile easy, because an easy smile was the oldest lie dragons knew. "Bad enough that we shouldn't stop in the middle."

* * *

The bridge sang when they stepped onto it.

That was the first wrongness. Old rope bridges groaned; they complained; they muttered in the grammar of fraying fiber. This one *sang*—a thin, high note under their boots, like a wet finger on a glass rim, like something practicing a voice it had found in the dark and wasn't sure how to use yet.

"Don't stop," Wren said softly. "Don't run. Walk like you're boring."

"Boring," Oscar repeated. His hand rested on the stone at his chest. "I can do boring. Boring is my native language."

Ahead of him, Sera walked with her staff held level, her eyes moving in the old ward-keeper's pattern—left, right, center, repeat—and Wren saw her lips moving, tracing the shapes of wards that no longer existed, wards whose anchor-stones had gone dead with the stars. Praying, maybe. Ward-keepers prayed in geometry.

The gorge below was full of fog that didn't move. That was the second wrongness. Fog should drift, should breathe with the wind, should live and die in curls and ribbons. This fog lay in the gorge like something poured, flat-topped, patient, and as Wren looked down between the slats of the bridge she saw—or thought she saw, or told herself she only thought she saw—shapes beneath its surface. Slow ones. Vast ones. Turning over in their sleep the way fish turn under ice.

The singing grew louder.

Halfway across, the fog began to rise.

It came up the bridge supports like water finding a level, silent, unhurried, and where it touched the ropes the frost bloomed thick and black. Wren felt it before she understood it—a cold that wasn't temperature, a pressure behind the eyes, a whispering at the edge of hearing that resolved, horribly, into almost-words. It was trying on their voices. Testing them. She heard her own laugh come out of the fog, pitched wrong, stretched wrong, and then Sera's voice saying *this way, this way, this way*, and then—worst of all—a warm young man's voice, easy and amused, calling:

"Wren! Over here! You're safe! Come down!"

A voice she hadn't heard in four hundred years. A voice that had died screaming while she ran, because running was what exiles did, and the ones who stayed to fight were the ones who taught the victors what dragon-fire smelled like.

Her foot stopped moving. She hated herself for it. Her foot stopped moving on a rotten slat over a gorge full of hungry dark, because a dead man's voice knew her name.

"*Wren.*"

"It's not him," she said aloud, to herself, to the bridge, to the boy watching her with wide eyes. "It's not—it's a *thing wearing*—"

The fog broke upward all at once.

They came out of it like hands out of sleeves—long, jointed, half-formed things, bodies of smoke given just enough will to want. Not one shape but dozens, pouring over the bridge cables, flowing along the planks, and where they passed, the wood grayed and softened like old fruit. They had no faces yet. They were shopping for faces. Wren watched one peel toward Sera and begin, mid-lunge, to assemble her features—gray eyes, gray braid, gray disapproval—and she understood suddenly, completely, with a clarity that felt like falling, exactly how bad this was.

They weren't hunting them. They were *collecting* them. Learning them. Getting the shapes right so that later, somewhere worse, they could wear them.

"No," Wren said.

Just that. One word, in the old tongue, in the voice she hadn't used since the exile—the voice that had once made mountains reconsider their outlines.

And she opened the door inside herself that she kept locked, and let the fire out.

It came up through her like dawn coming up through a valley. Light first—light that had no business existing under a starless sky, gold-white and furious, throwing the shade-wights into relief so sharp they shrieked, if smoke could shriek, if hunger could scream when it finally saw the thing it couldn't digest. Then heat, rolling off her skin in visible waves, and then the change beginning at her edges—the shimmer, the suggestion of wings made of light and heat-haze, the outline of something vast flickering over the small scarred woman-shape like a signature over a forgery.

The shade-wights recoiled as one. The fog fell back into the gorge like a curtain dropping. For one moment the whole broken bridge stood in a sphere of daylight, and Wren stood at the center of it, burning, magnificent, and *dying*—because she could feel it leaving her, feel the finite coin of her fire spilling through fingers that could not close fast enough, and she could not stop, because stopping meant the dark came back, and the dark had already learned their voices.

She fed the fire into the bridge itself. Let the ropes blaze. Let the planks ignite behind them as they ran—Oscar sprinting now, stone clutched to his chest, Sera hauling him by the collar when he stumbled—and the burning bridge became a wall of light between them and the far bank, between them and the rising dark, and the last thing Wren saw as they reached solid ground was the shapes in the fog below, dozens of them, pressed up against the underside of the burning span like children pressing faces against a window, watching the light go.

* * *

She came back to herself sitting against a milestone, with Oscar pressing a waterskin into her hands and Sera's cloak already around her shoulders.

The fire was out. The cold came in behind it immediately, filling the space it left, and Wren discovered that her hands were shaking—not with fear, but with *scarcity*. She flexed her fingers and felt the absence the way you feel a spent muscle, except deeper, except permanent. Somewhere inside her, a number had gotten smaller. She would never know how small. She would only find out when she reached for the flame someday and it answered slower than before, and slower, and then—

"Hey." Oscar crouched in front of her. His face was smudged with soot and he was looking at her the way people had stopped looking at her centuries ago: like she was someone who needed taking care of. "You with us?"

"I'm with you," she said. Her voice came out scraped.

"What were they?" he asked. "Really. Not the ward-lore version."

Wren looked past him, at the lowlands spreading south under the lidless sky, at the road that led toward the second stone and whatever waited between here and there. Behind them, the burnt stumps of the bridge cables still smoked faintly, and the gorge was quiet, and quiet was worse than noise.

"They were what happens," she said, "when a promise gets broken badly enough. The covenant was supposed to be a light. Every light leaves shadows, Oscar—that's fine, that's the bargain, that's how the world works. But break the light, and the shadows don't have anything holding them in place anymore." She drank. The water tasted like ash. "Corvane thinks he traded for darkness. He doesn't understand that darkness isn't a thing you own. It's a thing that owns *you*, and it's patient, and it learns your voice."

Sera stood watch at the roadside, staff in hand, and didn't turn around. But her shoulders were set in the way Wren had learned meant she was listening to every word and filing each one somewhere it would do damage later.

"We rest an hour," Sera said. "Then we move. The hunters won't stop at the bridge—they'll know another crossing."

Oscar didn't move. He was still crouched in front of her, and he was looking at her hands, at the faint tremor she couldn't quite kill, and Wren watched him do the arithmetic she'd been hiding. He was a smuggler. Smugglers knew cargo. Smugglers knew weight, and burn rate, and how to tell when a supply was running lower than its owner admitted.

"You gave too much," he said quietly.

"I gave what was needed."

"That's not what I asked."

For a moment Wren considered lying to him. She was good at it; she'd had practice; the easy smile was right there, warmed up, ready to work. Four hundred years of exile had taught her that the truth was a currency best spent slowly, in small denominations, on people who'd earned it.

But he was holding the first stone against his chest because he'd chosen to carry it openly. And the ghosts in the chapel had wrung a confession out of him that had cost him something real. And the stars were gone, and the dark knew her voice now, and somewhere ahead of them a guildmaster was building a kingdom out of night.

So she told him the truth.

"The fire is finite," she said. "In this body. In any body. I have less than I did yesterday, and yesterday I had less than the day before, and there are four stones left, and Corvane, and the lowlands, and the mountain, and the end of this road—which I have seen, Oscar, in the way dragons see ends of roads, and it does not end cheaply." She closed her hands around the waterskin. "So yes. I gave too much. And I'll give too much again. Because the alternative is a world where nothing burns, and I have lived in that world for four hundred years, and I am *done* living in it."

Oscar held her gaze for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, the way smugglers nodded—no speeches, no vows, just acknowledgment of terms.

"When we get the stones back," he said, "we get you back too. All of you. That's part of the deal now."

He stood and went to check the road south, and Wren sat against the milestone with Sera's cloak around her shoulders, and let herself feel, for exactly ten seconds, the terrifying weight of being believed in.

An hour later they walked. The lowlands opened before them, starless and vast, and somewhere on the road behind them, Wren heard—faint, carried on a wind that shouldn't have reached this far—a voice calling her name in a dead man's warmth.

The dark had learned her voice.

Now it was learning where she was going.
