## 9. The Drowned Chapel

The chapel had been a smuggler's landmark for three generations. Every route through the lowlands passed within sight of its bell tower, and every guild child learned the rhyme that went with it: *When the bell rings dry, the road is true; when the bell rings wet, it's coming for you.* Nobody had ever explained what *it* was. We didn't ask questions like that in the Salt Guild. Questions were contraband of a different kind.

Now I stood at the edge of the floodwater with Wren and Sera, and the bell tower rose out of black water like a finger pointing at nothing, and the water was perfectly still, and I understood, finally, what the rhyme had been trying to tell us all along.

"The stone is under there," Sera said. She had her map spread across a fallen beam, weighted at the corners with stones she'd gathered from the shore. "The old survey puts the reliquary beneath the altar. Twelve feet down, maybe fifteen."

"Twelve feet down," I repeated, "in water that's been dead since before my grandmother was born."

"Dead isn't the word I'd use," Wren said quietly.

She was standing closest to the waterline, and she hadn't moved in a long time. Her fire was still banked from Hollow Bridge—three days now, and she'd barely spoken above a whisper, and when she thought we weren't looking she pressed her hand flat against her own chest as if checking whether something inside still answered. The starless dark had gotten into her somehow, or maybe it had only found the crack that exile had already made. Either way, watching her stand at the edge of that water made me feel the way I used to feel on high routes in winter: aware, suddenly and completely, of how thin the line was between a path and a fall.

"It's guarded," Sera said. "The ward-keepers' records were clear about that. A congregation stayed behind when the valley flooded. They stayed behind on purpose."

"To guard a stone?"

"To guard a secret." Sera rolled up the map. Her hands were steady, but her voice wasn't quite. "There's a difference. Secrets need keepers. Stones just sit there."

I looked at the water again. It reflected nothing—not the tower, not the sky, not us. That was the part that made my skin crawl, more than the cold, more than the smell of rot and old incense rising off the surface. Water was supposed to be honest. It showed you yourself, whether you wanted to see it or not. This water showed you nothing at all, which meant either it had nothing to show or it was choosing not to.

"So how do we get down there?" I asked. "I can hold my breath maybe two minutes. Fifteen feet is fine. Finding an altar in the dark underwater is another thing entirely."

"You won't have to find it," Wren said. "It will find you." She turned to look at me, and in the flat grey light her eyes caught something—just for a moment, a flicker of ember-deep orange, there and gone. "They let people in, these congregations. That's the whole point of them. They don't want thieves. They want penitents."

"I'm not religious."

"Neither were they, probably." She almost smiled. "Doesn't matter. They're not going to ask about your faith."

"What are they going to ask about?"

Wren looked at me for a long moment. Then at Sera. Then back at the water.

"You'll see," she said. "We all will."

* * *

We went in at dawn, if you could call it that—the sky had been the color of dirty wool since the stars went out, and dawn was just a slightly less grey shade of grey. Sera waited at the shore with a rope tied around my waist and a lantern in a glass case, the kind divers used on the river routes. Wren stood beside her. She'd offered to come under, and I'd said no without thinking about it, and then thought about why I'd said it.

Because if something down there took me, I didn't want it taking her too. Because her fire was nearly spent and cold water would finish the job. Because—and this was the thought I kept circling back to, the one I didn't like—I think some part of me already knew what the chapel wanted, and I didn't want anyone I cared about hearing it.

The water closed over my head and the world went silent and utterly black.

Not ordinary black. Ordinary black has texture—you learn to read it, as a smuggler, the way a blind man reads a room. This was black that pushed back. My lantern threw a circle of light maybe three feet wide, and beyond that circle the darkness felt solid, like the walls of a throat.

I swam down along the rope. Ten feet. Twelve. The pressure settled on my ears and my chest like a patient hand. And then, at fourteen feet, the light found the roof of the nave.

It was beautiful. That's the thing nobody tells you about drowned places—they don't rot the way you expect. The vaulted ceiling of the chapel was still intact, furred with white silt like frost, and the lantern light moved across it and made it gleam, and for one stupid moment I forgot to be afraid and just looked.

Then the light swung down and found the pews.

They were full.

They sat exactly as congregations sit—backs straight, heads bowed, hands folded—except they were swollen and grey-green and their eyes were open, and every single one of those eyes turned toward my lantern at the same moment, with the smooth synchronized motion of a field of wheat bending in wind.

I did not scream. I'm proud of that, in retrospect. What I did instead was freeze, which any smuggler will tell you is worse, because freezing burns air, and air was the only currency I had left. Maybe forty seconds of breath. Thirty-five.

The aisle stretched ahead of me toward the altar, and the drowned sat on either side of it, and they did not move, and they did not stop looking.

I swam.

The water between the pews was thick somehow, or my fear was, and every stroke felt like pushing through wool. The faces slid past me at arm's length. An old man with a fisherman's beard. A woman holding a child—a child that had been down here so long it was hard to say it was a child anymore, and I looked away from that one fast, and hated myself for looking away, and kept swimming. Their mouths were closed. That was the worst part, I decided later. Not open mouths, not screaming faces. Closed mouths and open eyes, waiting, patient as debt.

My hand touched the altar rail. Beyond it, the altar itself, draped in silt, and on top of it a shape I recognized from Sera's drawings: a reliquary, iron-bound, crusted with a century of the lake's slow work.

And standing behind the altar, where a priest would stand, was the priest.

He was better preserved than the rest, or worse—it depended on how you counted. His vestments still hung in folds. His face had kept its structure. And he raised one hand as I came over the rail, palm out, the universal gesture: *stop.*

I stopped. Twenty seconds of air, maybe less. Panic makes you burn through it fast.

His mouth opened. No bubbles came out. But I heard him anyway, the way you hear things in dreams—directly, behind the eyes, in a voice like water finding cracks in stone.

*You came for the stone.*

I couldn't speak underwater. I nodded.

*Many come for the stone.* He tilted his head, and the gesture was horribly alive. *Thieves come with tools. Soldiers come with numbers. You came with a rope and a lantern and no one at your back who doesn't already know your sins.* Something moved in his ruined face that might have been approval. *The covenant does not give itself to clean hands. There are no clean hands. It gives itself to hands that know what they are.*

The lantern flickered. My lungs were starting to hurt now, that deep hot ache behind the breastbone. I reached for the reliquary.

His hand came down over it first. Cold radiated off him even through the water, cold like the far side of winter.

*First,* he said, *a confession.*

I shook my head. I pointed upward. *No air.* He understood—he must have understood—but he didn't move, and his eyes held mine with the patience of someone who had been underwater for a hundred years and could wait a hundred more.

*Then breathe,* he said, *and speak. We have time. You do not. Choose quickly.*

And that's when I understood the real design of the place. Not cruelty. Economy. They weren't torturing me. They were making sure that whatever I said next would be true, because there was no air left in me for anything else.

Ten seconds. Five.

I grabbed the front of his vestments and pulled myself close, face to face with a dead man's eyes, and I spoke into the water, into him, in a burst of silver bubbles and desperate compressed words:

"My name is Oscar Thackeray. I've run salt and steel and secrets for the Salt Guild since I was nine years old. I knew the villages were dying. I carried the salt that kept them alive and I never once asked why the guild charged them everything they had for it. I saw the wards failing two years ago—flickering on the high passes, wrong-colored, wrong-sounding—and I told myself it was weather. I told myself it was always been that way. When Corvane sealed the doors and left the lowland towns to the dark, I paid my dues and kept my mouth shut, because the guild raised me and fed me and taught me everything I know, and I loved it, and love is the best excuse there is." My vision was going spotty at the edges. "I'm complicit. I was complicit every day and I called it loyalty. I'm not here to steal from you. I'm here because someone has to carry what we did into the light, and it might as well be the invisible man."

The last bubbles left me. My lungs seized. The priest watched me drown by inches, and then—

He stepped aside.

The reliquary sat open before me. Inside, on a bed of rotted velvet, lay a stone the size of a fist, grey as sea-glass, and it was warm. In all that cold water, it was warm, and faintly, faintly, it glowed—the first light I'd seen in weeks that wasn't fire or lantern-oil.

I took it. The world went soft at the edges, went kind, went out.

* * *

I woke on the shore coughing lake water into the mud while Sera pounded my back and shouted things I couldn't parse. Wren crouched nearby, and her hand was on my shoulder, and her hand was *hot*, hotter than skin should be, and I realized dimly that she'd burned something of herself to pull me back—that the last thing I'd seen underwater, the softness, the kindness, hadn't been dying. It had been her, reaching down.

"Don't," I rasped, when I could talk. "Don't spend it on me. You'll need—"

"Shut up," Wren said, but gently. "You're welcome."

Sera turned the stone over in her fingers, careful as a midwife. "It's singing," she whispered. "Barely. Like a struck bell after the sound's mostly gone. But it's singing." She looked up at me, and there was something new in her face—not hope exactly, hope was too big a word, but the seed casing of hope, the thing hope grows from. "One down."

I lay back in the mud and stared up at the blank sky and laughed until it hurt.

* * *

We camped that night in the lee of the chapel wall, above the waterline, because none of us wanted to sleep closer to the flood than we had to. The bell tower loomed against the grey. Around midnight, Sera shook me awake.

"Listen," she said.

I listened. At first: nothing, the usual nothing, the starless silence that had become the whole world's weather. Then, underneath it, faint and rhythmic:

The bell. Ringing. Underwater.

Not wet and muffled the way a drowned bell should sound. Clear. Bright. Each swing carrying up through the water and the stone and the ground and into my teeth, and with each ring the glow of the covenant-stone in Sera's pack pulsed once, like a heartbeat answering a call.

"One down," Sera breathed. "And somewhere out there, four more just heard it."

From the direction of the lowlands—far off, past the drowned fields, past the edge of everything mapped—something answered. Not a bell. A sound like vast wings, or like a mountain clearing its throat, or like a voice too large for any single ear, saying a word we couldn't make out yet.

Wren sat up. In the dark, her eyes were burning.

"They know," she said. "All of them. Everything sleeping just woke up." She looked east, toward the mountains, toward the volcano waiting in chapter-thirteen darkness none of us could see yet. "And so did everything else. We need to move. Tonight."

The bell rang on behind us as we broke camp, steady as a pulse, counting us forward into the dark.
