## 10. Old Friends, New Orders

Sera heard the hunters before she saw them—the way you hear a storm in the creak of a doorframe, the way you taste iron before lightning. Three days out of the Drowned Chapel, camped in a hollow below the Grey Stair where the old smuggler road bent toward the lowlands, and the night itself had gone wrong. The crickets stopped. The wind changed its mind mid-breath.

She sat up in her bedroll with her hand already on her warding knife.

Oscar slept like a man who'd learned to sleep anywhere and trust nothing: on his side, back to the fire, one hand curled near his belt. Wren didn't sleep at all anymore—she sat at the hollow's edge with her hood down, watching the sky that had no stars in it, and Sera had learned not to look too long at how still she sat. A dragon pretending to be a woman should have been the strangest thing Sera had ever seen. It wasn't. The strangest thing was how quickly she'd stopped being afraid of her.

"Wake him," Wren said quietly. She didn't turn around. "They're coming up the eastern switchback. Four of them. Maybe five."

"You can smell them?"

"I can smell the salt-iron on their blades. Guild steel." A pause. "And I know one of them."

Sera's stomach dropped through the mountain. "Who?"

Wren finally turned. In the guttering firelight her face looked older than it had any right to—scarred, weathered, human in every way that mattered and none that did. "The big one walks like he's listening to the ground. Like a man who spent thirty years reading tremors in stone."

Sera closed her eyes. *Dain.*

Her mentor. Her teacher. The man who'd pulled her out of a border garrison at nineteen because she could see the weave of wards the way other people saw color, who'd taught her that a ward-keeper's first duty was not to the guild but to the land itself—and who had stood silent, three years ago, when the guild council stripped her rank and burned her name from the rolls for saying aloud what everyone knew: that the wards were failing, that someone was letting them fail, that the covenant beneath everything was rotting from the inside.

He hadn't defended her. He hadn't condemned her either. He'd just looked at her across the council chamber with an expression she'd spent three years trying to forget—not anger, not betrayal. *Disappointment.* As if she were the one who'd broken something.

"Five," Sera said. "If Dain's leading, there'll be five. He never moves without a reserve." She reached over and shook Oscar's shoulder. His eyes opened instantly, fully awake, no fog of sleep—which told her more about his childhood than any story ever had.

"Hunters," she said. "Guild. Coming up the east switchback."

He was already rolling his bedroll. "How long?"

"Minutes. They'll have the high ground by dawn if we run now."

"Then we run now."

"No." Wren rose. Firelight moved across her face and for a moment—just a moment—Sera saw something else underneath the skin, something vast and patient and old as grief. "You run. Take the stones south through the scree field. I'll hold them here."

"You'll burn yourself out," Sera said flatly. "You told us. Every flame you spend makes the mortal shell harder to hold. You've got maybe two true fires left in you before—" She didn't finish. None of them said what came after.

"Then I'll spend them well."

"You'll do nothing of the kind." Sera heard her own voice harden, heard the old garrison cadence creep into it, the voice she'd used on green ward-keepers facing their first breach. "Dain Voss is not a man you frighten. He's a man who reads ground and patience and wind. If you burn him, he'll have already sent word down-mountain, and Corvane will know exactly where we are and exactly what we carry. No fires. Not yet. We're too close to the second stone to light a beacon."

Oscar had gone very quiet. That was the thing about him, Sera thought—everyone underestimated the quiet ones, but she'd watched him navigate a patrol line by moonlight alone, watched him talk a dying village into hope with nothing but a sack of contraband salt. Quiet wasn't emptiness. Quiet was load-bearing.

"There's another way," he said.

"Name it."

"He knows me." Oscar's mouth twisted. "Every hunter on the Thornfell routes knows me. Oscar Thackeray, youngest smuggler of the Salt Guild, never caught, never seen, never *anything*." He said the last word like he was spitting out a stone. "I walk out there with my hands empty and my guild badge showing, and I buy you both twenty minutes while they decide whether I'm a lost lamb or a liar."

"They'll arrest you."

"They'll *try*."

"And if Dain decides you're a liar?" Wren asked.

Oscar smiled, and it was the smile Sera hated most—the one that meant he'd already priced his own life into the plan and found it cheap. "Then I run fast and you two get creative. Everybody wins."

Nobody won. That was the whole rotten arithmetic of the last month, and Sera was tired of it. But she nodded, because he was right, and because the alternative was fire, and fire would cost them everything they couldn't afford.

"One condition," she said. "You don't go alone. I stand beside you."

Oscar blinked. "Sera—"

"Dain Voss taught me everything I know about wards and half of what I know about stubbornness. If anyone can read his intentions off his shoulders at forty paces, it's me. And if it goes wrong—" She drew her warding knife and checked the edge, though it didn't need checking. "If it goes wrong, I need to be the one standing between you and him. Not because I doubt you. Because I owe him a debt, and I'd rather pay it clean than let it fester."

Wren looked at them both for a long moment. Then she melted back into the dark above the hollow, taking the high ground, and Sera tried not to think about what a dragon planned to do with high ground if things went badly.

* * *

They walked out onto the switchback together under a sky with nothing in it.

That was the part Sera still couldn't get used to—the nakedness of it. She'd grown up under Thornfell stars so thick they seemed like spilled salt, and now the black overhead pressed down like a lid, like a held breath, like the whole world waiting to exhale. The hunters came up the trail in single file, lanterns shuttered to slits, moving with the economical grace of people who had done this a thousand times.

Four visible. The fifth, as predicted, hanging back in the rocks—a shadow with a crossbow, covering the approach. Good. At least the world still made some kinds of sense.

Dain Voss led them himself, which told Sera everything. Guildmasters didn't send their first blade on errands; they sent him when the errand mattered. He was greyer than she remembered, broader through the shoulders, his beard cropped short in the old military style he'd never shaken. He carried no drawn weapon. He never needed to draw first.

He stopped ten paces away, and his lantern-slit gaze went past Oscar entirely and landed on her like a physical weight.

"Sera Vane." Her burned name, spoken in his gravel voice, hit her somewhere below the ribs. "Three years, and this is where I find you. Running holes in the mountains with smugglers and strangers."

"Not running." She kept her voice level. "Walking. There's a difference. Ask any map."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth—the ghost of the dry humor she'd loved once, before it all curdled. "Still sharp. Good. Sharp keeps you alive." Then his eyes moved to Oscar, and the warmth drained out of them like water through sand. "Thackeray. The guildmaster wants a word."

"The guildmaster has my forwarding address," Oscar said pleasantly. "Care of the whole southern range."

"Don't." Dain's voice dropped, and Sera heard the warning in it, the genuine weariness under the iron. "I've read your file, boy. I trained half the men who trained you. You're good—you might be the best route-runner the guild's produced in a generation. Which is why I'm asking once, plainly, man to man: give me the woman's papers, tell me where the stranger is, and walk away. Corvane's offering full amnesty. Full inheritance, even. He thinks highly of you."

Sera watched Oscar take that in. Watched something move behind his eyes—the same fracture she'd seen growing since Salt and Ash, since the night he'd smuggled food to villages his own guild had written off. *Full inheritance.* Corvane was dangling the guild itself.

"That's generous," Oscar said slowly. "Almost fatherly."

"He means it as such."

"I had a father once. He ran salt for the guild too. Died on the Grey Stair with a patrol bolt in his back, and the guild paid my mother a condolence fee of eleven marks and a bag of grain." Oscar's pleasantness hadn't cracked, but something underneath it had gone very cold. "So forgive me if 'fatherly' doesn't land the way he hopes."

Dain's jaw tightened. "Then I'm sorry for what comes next."

"So am I," Sera said, and stepped forward, and everything happened very fast after that.

Because Dain's hand had already moved—not to his blade but to his belt pouch, and Sera knew that motion, knew it from a hundred training yards, knew the pinch-and-throw of a suppression charm, the little iron sigils that ate light and sound and left a target deaf, blind, and drowning in silence. He hadn't come to negotiate. The negotiation had been courtesy. The courtesy was over.

"*Down!*" she screamed, and threw herself sideways as the first sigil burst against the rocks behind Oscar in a bloom of dead grey light.

The crossbow spoke from the ridge. Sera felt the bolt pass close enough to stir her hair—aimed at Oscar, deflected by pure luck or by whatever patient attention Wren was paying from above—and then the hollow erupted. Hunters fanning wide, blades out, guild steel drinking the starless dark. Oscar rolled behind a boulder, drawing his knife, and Dain came through the chaos straight at Sera with his longblade clearing its sheath in a whisper she'd heard ten thousand times in the training yard.

"Stand aside, girl," he said. "Last kindness I've got."

"I stopped being your girl the day you let them burn my name."

"I stood silent because the council was right and you were reckless—"

"I was *right*, Dain!" She met his first cut and turned it, boots skidding on scree. "The wards were failing! Someone was letting them fail! Everything I said three years ago is coming true overhead right now, in letters of ash across the whole sky, and you're still calling me reckless!"

His blade came again—faster, meaner, the form he'd drilled into her until her hands bled. Parry, bind, riposte; she gave ground along the switchback, giving him the slope he wanted, letting him believe the old lessons still governed her body. Behind them Oscar fought two hunters in the tight space between boulders, all elbows and economy, a smuggler's ugly practical fighting, keeping alive.

"You want to know why I really stayed silent?" Dain's voice was ragged now, and Sera realized with a lurch that he wasn't angry—he was *grieving*, and had been for three years. "Because I checked. After your hearing. I pulled the ward logs myself, the deep archives, the ones nobody reads. And the numbers matched yours. Every line." Their blades locked, faces a hand-span apart, his breath hot with exertion and something worse. "And I said nothing anyway. Because the alternative was burning my own life down, and I was a coward, Sera. I chose the guild over the truth, and I've regretted it every hour since, and none of that changes what I'm ordered to do tonight!"

"Then disobey," she whispered. "One time. Be the man who taught me the land matters more than the guild. Disobey."

For one heartbeat—one suspended, terrible heartbeat—she saw him waver. Saw the choice open in front of him like a door.

Then the crossbowman on the ridge fired again, at Oscar, point-blank as the boy broke cover, and the door slammed shut.

There was no time to think. Sera had spent her whole life learning that wards were promises made in iron and intent, and she broke the oldest rule of her craft in the space between one breath and the next: she reversed her warding knife and drove it through Dain Voss's sword shoulder.

Not deep. She'd angled it the way healers angle—to miss bone, to miss the artery, to hurt and cripple and *not kill*. But he went down with a sound she'd remember until she died, the sound of a mountain deciding to fall, and his blade rang away across the stone.

The crossbowman hesitated—one precious second of confusion—and in that second the night caught fire.

Not Wren's fire. Something smaller, stranger: every suppression sigil on the battlefield flared white and died at once, burned out by a pulse of raw ward-craft that rolled down the switchback like a bell being struck. Sera felt it leave her own hands and understood distantly that she'd done it, channeled through the knife still buried in her mentor's shoulder, turning his own suppression net inside out. Light flooded the hollow. The hunters staggered, blinded, and Oscar was already moving, dragging her back, up the trail, into the scree.

Above them, on the ridgeline, a shape unfolded wings that weren't quite there—a suggestion of vastness against the starless sky, a heat-haze in the shape of a promise—and every hunter on that slope made the same decision at the same instant, which was to stop pursuing anything, ever, in this direction.

* * *

They ran until the guild steel was a memory and the scree gave way to pine, and then Sera's legs simply quit, and she sat down hard against a trunk with Oscar collapsing beside her, both of them hauling air.

Behind them, far below, Dain's hunters were regrouping or retreating or carrying their leader down-mountain. She couldn't make herself care which.

"You stabbed him," Oscar said finally. Not accusatory. Just cataloguing.

"I wounded him. Precisely." Her hands were shaking now that it was safe to shake. "There's a difference, and I intend to hide behind it forever."

"He would have killed us."

"He would have obeyed." She stared at her palms, at the blood on them—his blood, mixed with hers, indistinguishable. "That's worse. Don't you see? He *knew*, Oscar. He pulled the ward logs three years ago. He knew I was right and he knelt anyway, and tonight he came up that mountain ready to drag us back to the man who broke the sky, because orders are easier than truth." She laughed, and it came out broken. "I spent three years hating him for standing silent. Turns out I was hating him for being weak. I understand him completely now. That's the worst part. I could have become him."

"But you didn't." Oscar's voice was quiet and certain in the dark. "You put the knife in. You picked. That's the whole difference between you and him, Sera. He knew and knelt. You knew and *chose*."

She looked at him—at this smuggler boy the guild had raised on invisibility and contraband, who kept finding the exact words she needed as if honesty were just another route he'd mapped ahead of time—and felt something settle in her chest. Not peace. Resolve. The found, patched-together family of the road hardening into something that could bear weight.

Wren materialized out of the trees, unharmed, unreadable. "The big one lives," she reported. "They're carrying him down. He'll live to hate you or thank you. Possibly both."

"Good," Sera said, and meant it, and hated that she meant it.

Oscar unslung his pack and drew out the first covenant-stone, wrapped in wool, glowing faintly through the fabric like a coal refusing to die. Since the chapel, it had been warm almost constantly. Now, as Sera watched, the glow pulsed—twice, steady, deliberate. Like a heartbeat. Like a knock.

Wren went very still. "It's never done that before."

"What does it mean?" Oscar asked.

Wren unwrapped it, and the light that spilled out was the first true light Sera had seen since the stars went out—gold-edged, alive, hungry. The stone was pointing. South-southeast, unwavering, toward the drowned lowlands and the refugee city beyond.

"It means," Wren said softly, "the second stone knows we're coming. And something on that road knows we're coming too."
