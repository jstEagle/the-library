## 12. Corvane's Gift

Corvane had always believed that a man's true character revealed itself in how he handled salt.

Not the trading of it—any fool with a mule and a ledger could trade. But the handling. The way a man weighed a sack, sealed a seam, carried it through the dark without spilling so much as a grain. Salt was honest work disguised as dishonest, and the men who respected it were the men who understood that everything worth keeping had to be carried carefully, quietly, and alone.

Oscar Thackeray had been eight years old when Corvane first watched him carry salt. The boy had come up from the lowlands half-starved, all elbows and stubbornness, and Corvane had put a fifty-pound sack on his back just to see what he'd do. Most children wept. Some ran. Oscar had walked the length of the guild hall's lower corridor without stopping, set the sack down precisely where it was meant to go, and then turned around and asked for another one.

Corvane had thought, *there*. There is the one who will inherit everything.

He sat now in his study above the great hall, listening to the mountain breathe. That was what the silence of Thornfell had become since the stars went out—a breathing, slow and labored, as if the peaks themselves were some vast animal trying to sleep off a wound. Below him, the guild went about its business in candlelight. Inventories. Rationing. The careful arithmetic of survival. He had ordered the stores counted three times, and each count came back worse than the last.

The dark was not temporary. He had known that from the first night, when the wards shattered and the sky emptied itself like a purse turned inside out. The others—the ward-keepers, the priests, the frightened old men who still lit lamps at their windows out of habit—they all spoke of the darkness as though it were weather. A storm that would pass. Something to be endured until the world remembered itself.

But Corvane had read the founders' letters. All of them. Every page the archive held, including the ones Sera Voss had come sniffing after before he'd had her disgraced and driven out. He knew what the covenant had actually said, and more importantly, he knew what it had actually cost. The dragons had not been partners. They had been landlords. And the rent had been fire—regular, scheduled, apocalyptic fire, poured across the mountains every generation or so to keep the wards fed and the sky bright, burning whatever stood in its path. Villages. Roads. People. The founders had called it renewal. Corvane had read the casualty lists appended to the oldest ledgers, written in the same neat hand that recorded grain shipments, and he had understood, finally, what kind of men he'd inherited his guild from.

Men who called slaughter mercy because it kept the lights on.

So when the chance came—not by accident, but by arrangement, years of quiet negotiation finally bearing fruit—to let the wards fail, to let the dragons' fire gutter out forever, Corvane had taken it. The price had been steep: dominion over the darkened lands, yes, but also the knowledge that he would be hated for it, hunted for it, perhaps killed for it. He had accepted all of it. Because someone had to make the choice the founders had been too cowardly to make.

The darkness was mercy. That was the whole of his philosophy, distilled to a single sentence. The darkness asked nothing. It burned nothing. It simply was, vast and even-handed, covering rich and poor alike. Under starlight, men fought over scraps and called it destiny. Under no light at all, they finally learned to share.

He heard footsteps on the stairs—light ones, measured, the tread of a man who had spent his life moving unseen—and did not turn around.

"You sent for me," Oscar said from the doorway.

"I sent for you days ago."

"I was busy."

Corvane smiled and gestured to the chair across from his desk. "You're always busy. It's your best quality." He waited until the young man sat—still standing straight even seated, still watchful, those quick eyes cataloguing the room the way they catalogued everything: exits, weapons, the weight of the strongbox in the corner. "You look tired, Oscar."

"You look well," Oscar said, which was neither agreement nor courtesy but something in between, a smuggler's answer, giving nothing away. "For a man sitting on a hoard while villages starve."

And there it was. Corvane had expected the accusation; he'd have been disappointed by anything gentler. "Harrowgate?"

"Half its children are eating boiled pine bark. I've seen it."

"You've been running food out." Not a question. Corvane had known about the missing sacks within two nights of their disappearance—he knew every number in this mountain the way he knew his own pulse—but he had let it continue, because watching Oscar defy him had told him something valuable: the boy's conscience was awake, and awake consciences could be aimed. "Three runs that I know of. Probably five. You took Marta's boy with you on the last one, which was careless, and you used the Fen road, which I've already had closed behind you, which means next time you'll need my permission whether you know it or not."

Oscar's jaw tightened. "Are you going to punish me?"

"For feeding starving people?" Corvane leaned back. "No. I'm going to promote you."

* * *

He let the silence do its work while he rose and crossed to the cabinet against the far wall. This was an old technique, older than the guild: never fill a pause when the other man's imagination will fill it better. He unlocked the cabinet with a key from around his neck and lifted out a small iron box, worn smooth at the corners by generations of handling, and set it on the desk between them.

"Do you know what this is?"

"A strongbox."

"It's the founder's box. Aldric Thackeray's own." He watched the name land. It always landed. "Your name, Oscar. Did you ever wonder why a lowland orphan got taken into the Salt Guild at eight years old, trained, housed, fed, promoted past boys who'd been born to members? Did you think it was charity?"

Oscar said nothing, but his stillness changed quality—the stillness of a man standing on ice and hearing it begin to sing.

"The guild keeps records," Corvane said. "Even the parts we don't advertise. Aldric Thackeray was the youngest of the nine founders. The only one who argued against the covenant at the end. The only one who voted to let the dragons' pact lapse rather than pay their price again. His line was supposed to be the guild's conscience. Instead it died out—or everyone believed it died out—in the plague winter, sixty years back." He opened the box. Inside, on faded cloth, lay a ring of black iron set with a chip of grey stone. "A wet nurse. A forged register. A guild that preferred its history tidy. These things happen, Oscar. They happened to you."

"That's not possible." Oscar's voice was flat, controlled, but Corvane saw his hands—gripping the arms of the chair, knuckles pale. "You're telling me I'm—what? Founders' blood. Conveniently. Right when you need something from me."

"Yes," Corvane said simply. "Conveniently. I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. I need something from you, and I'm offering you everything in exchange. That's the entire shape of our conversation. Everything else is decoration."

"What do you need?"

"The stones you're carrying."

* * *

He had wondered, sometimes, how much Oscar would deny. He was almost disappointed when the boy didn't bother denying anything at all. He simply looked at the iron box, then back up, and waited—which was, Corvane reflected, exactly what Aldric must have done, four hundred years ago, when the other eight founders told him what the dragons' renewal would cost. The same face. The same stubborn, grieving patience. Blood told.

"Sera Voss came to you," Corvane said. "Or you went to her—I've never been certain which of you recruited which. Either way, she gave you the story. Betrayal, broken covenant, dying sky, five stones, one quest. Very clean. Very dramatic. She's a romantic, our Sera. Always was. It's why she failed her wards and why she blames everyone but herself." He turned the ring slowly on the cloth, letting the lamplight move across the stone. "She never showed you the casualty lists, did she? The founding ledgers. What the covenant actually required, every third generation. Fire across the Thornfells. Whole valleys. She never showed you the names."

"No," Oscar admitted.

"Of course not. The truth doesn't recruit." Corvane closed the box. "Here is what I know, and I ask you to weigh it like cargo, because you are the best weigher of cargo I have ever trained. The wards are dead. The dragons are finished—all but one, and she is a guttering candle wearing a borrowed body. The stones can rekindle the fire. And the fire will burn. Not metaphorically. Not spiritually. It will burn flesh and timber and children, because that is what it has always done, and the men who built it dressed it up in words like *renewal* because they couldn't stomach writing *sacrifice* in their own ledgers. I could stomach it. So I made the choice they couldn't. I let the sky go dark, and I will keep it dark, and in exchange the lowlands are mine to feed—or not feed—as wisdom dictates. No more dragon-rents. No more scheduled apocalypse. Just cold, and hunger, and honesty, and time enough to build something that doesn't run on blood."

"And the villages?" Oscar asked quietly. "Harrowgate. Thefen. The ones you ordered abandoned."

"Costs," Corvane said. "I grieve them. I want that understood—not absolution, I don't expect it, but understanding. I have sat at this desk for thirty years making choices where every option had a body count, and the only difference between me and the founders is that I write the bodies down myself instead of hiring someone to do it." He slid the box across the desk. "Bring me the stones. All of them. Give up this errand, this woman, this dragon, this war you cannot win, and I will give you the guild. Not stewardship. Inheritance. Your birthright, restored, with my seal and the council's blessing. You'll hold Thornfell in your hand. And when the hard winters come—and they will come, darker and colder than any in the ledgers—you'll be the man with the granaries. The man who decides who eats. Isn't that better than being the man who lights the pyre?"

He watched the temptation move through the boy like weather. It was real temptation—that was what made it work. Corvane was not lying to him, not about any of it. The inheritance was genuine. The ledgers were genuine. The horror of the covenant was genuine. Every word he'd spoken was true, and truth was the sharpest blade he owned, and he had honed it for this single cut.

Oscar stood. Slowly, like a man rising from a table where he'd been shown his own grave.

"You believe it," Oscar said. "That's the worst part. You actually believe the dark is kinder than the fire."

"I believe the fire eats children, and the dark only lets them be hungry."

"Hunger kills them too, Corvane. Slower. Quieter. With nobody to blame." He picked up the iron box—the founder's box, his ancestor's box—and held it a moment, and Corvane felt, for one suspended heartbeat, certain that he had won. Then Oscar set it back down on the desk, gently, deliberately, the way he set down contraband he'd decided not to sell. "You're right about one thing. The founders were cowards. But they weren't cowards for lighting the fire. They were cowards because they bound living creatures into servitude to do it—because they took a partnership and made it a leash and called the leash a covenant. The answer to that isn't more darkness. It's keeping the promise they broke."

"And if keeping it burns the world?"

"Then the world gets to see it coming this time," Oscar said. "In the open. Where everyone can choose." He moved toward the door, then paused, and for a moment—just a moment—his voice dropped into something younger, something that might once have been a son's. "You taught me to carry salt without spilling. You taught me that everything worth keeping has to be carried honestly. Somewhere under all those ledgers, you taught me better than you know."

He was gone before Corvane could answer, footsteps fading down the stairs, light and measured and already beyond reach.

Corvane sat for a long time in the breathing dark of his study. Then he opened the iron box again, took out the black-iron ring, and turned it in his fingers until the grey stone caught the candlelight.

"He refused," he said to the empty room, softly, without anger. "Of course he refused. He's Aldric's blood."

He rose, crossed to the window, and looked out over the starless peaks, where somewhere in that vast blind dark a boy, a disgraced ward-keeper, and the last dragon were carrying three stones toward a fourth.

"Double the hunters," he said. "And send word to the refugee city. If he won't take his inheritance—" he closed the box, "—then I'll give him a reason to come home."

Below, in the great hall, the candles burned low, and the mountain breathed, and waited.
