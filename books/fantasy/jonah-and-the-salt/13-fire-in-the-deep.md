## 13. Fire in the Deep

The mountain was called Vessel's Rest, and it had not breathed in four hundred years.

Wren felt it before she saw it—a hollow ache in her chest, like pressing a tongue against a missing tooth. That was how dormant volcanoes spoke to her kind. Not with heat or smoke but with absence. The mountain had swallowed its fire long ago, and what remained was a great stone throat gone cold and silent.

She stood at its base as dusk came on, and behind her she could hear Oscar and Sera making camp—Oscar arguing cheerfully with the wind about where to pitch the tarp, Sera telling him he was doing it wrong, both of them wrong together. Their voices carried thin across the scree. Human voices. Small and warm and impossibly far away already.

"You're going to tell them you're going down alone," Wren said to herself, practicing it. "You're going to say it like it's simple."

It wasn't simple. Nothing about this was simple.

* * *

They'd found the third stone's location three days earlier, scratched into the back of a smuggler's map Oscar had won off a dead man's ghost in the Drowned Chapel—or rather, traded for a confession Wren still thought about at night. The map showed the lowlands, the salt roads, the refugee city they'd passed through, and then, past all of it, a mountain drawn with careful, reverent strokes. Beneath it, in faded ink: *Vessel's Rest. Where the fire sleeps. Do not wake what you cannot carry.*

"Do not wake what you cannot carry," Sera had read aloud, tracing the words. "Charming."

"It's a warning," Oscar said. "Smugglers don't write warnings for fun. Someone went down there and came back changed enough to leave a note."

"Or someone went down there and didn't come back," Sera said, "and their partner wrote the note."

Wren had said nothing. She had known, even then, what the note meant. Dragons wrote warnings like that only when they understood exactly what slept below—and exactly what waking it would cost.

Now, standing at the mountain's foot, she understood it too.

The entrance wasn't hidden. That was the first strange thing. In a world of collapsed wards and vanished stars, where every secret had to be dug out of ruin and ash, the volcano simply offered a crack in its flank—a fissure wide enough for a person, breathing out air that smelled of iron and old rain. As if the mountain had been waiting. As if it knew what was coming down its throat.

"Wren." Oscar's voice behind her. She hadn't heard him cross the camp; that was the other thing about him, the thing that kept surprising her—he moved like she did, quiet as falling snow, though he'd learned it from guild masters instead of centuries of sky. "You're going to say I can't come with you."

"I'm going to say you can't come with you," she said. "I mean—you can't come with me."

"That's what I said."

"It's not what you meant."

He came to stand beside her, close enough that his shoulder almost touched hers. He was looking up at the fissure, and in the starless dark his face was just a pale suggestion, a smudge of warmth against the black. She missed stars so much it was a physical pain some nights. She missed them the way humans missed people.

"The stones sing to each other," she said. "You know that. You've felt it since the chapel."

"I've felt something," Oscar admitted. "Like a hum in my teeth."

"That's the covenant pulling itself back together. And when we get close to the deep places—the true deep places, where fire still remembers being fire—that pull gets... loud. For me it would be unbearable. For you it would be fatal." She turned to face him. "That's the honest version. Here's the crueler one: the stone at the bottom of this mountain isn't sleeping. It's starving. And it will take fire from whatever comes near it. From me, it takes years. From you, it would take everything."

Oscar was quiet for a long moment. Below them, Sera was cursing at a tent peg.

"How many years?" he asked finally.

"Wren—"

"Don't." Her voice came out harder than she intended, and she watched him flinch, and hated herself for it. She softened. "Don't do the arithmetic out loud. Please. Some things should stay uncounted."

"That's a smuggler's rule," he said quietly. "Never count the cargo while it's still moving."

"Then be a good smuggler and look away."

But he didn't look away. He looked at her the way he'd looked at her since the pass, since the bandits and the burning and the moment she'd let him see what she really was—like she was something vast and breakable at once, something he couldn't decide whether to shield or worship. It terrified her more than the mountain did. Mountains only wanted her fire. Oscar wanted her *here*, diminished or otherwise, and every day she spent in this mortal shell made the wanting more mutual, more human, more dangerous than any covenant-stone ever carved by dragon claws.

"If you're not back by dawn," he said, "I'm coming down after you."

"You'll die."

"Probably. But I'll die knowing which way is down, which is more than most people manage."

Despite everything, she laughed. It came out cracked and small, a human laugh, and she realized with a jolt that she hadn't laughed like a dragon in months. She didn't know when she'd stopped. She didn't know if she'd ever start again.

"Dawn," she said. "And if I'm late—"

"You won't be late."

"—if I'm late, don't come down. Take Sera and the stones and go east. Finish it without me."

"No," Oscar said simply.

"Oscar—"

"No. I'll finish it with you or I'll finish it badly, but I'm not finishing it without you. That's not negotiable. I learned negotiation from the Salt Guild, and the Salt Guild taught me that you never give up your best asset, and—" his voice caught, just slightly, "—you're not an asset. You're the whole reason there's anything worth smuggling toward."

Somewhere behind them, Sera called out that the water was boiling, and that if they were going to have a dramatic farewell they could at least do it within earshot of tea.

* * *

The descent took six hours.

The fissure narrowed, widened, narrowed again, winding down through rock that grew warmer and then cooler and then warm again in slow, tidal pulses—as if somewhere far below, a heart was beating. Wren climbed down with her palms flat against the stone, reading it the way she used to read thermals: pressure, temperature, mood. The mountain was dreaming. She could feel the shape of its dream, vast and slow, all fire and pressure and the memory of wings made of magma.

Twice she passed bones.

The first were small—an animal, maybe, though the ribs curved strangely. The second set were unmistakably human, curled in a niche in the wall as if the climber had simply lain down mid-journey and decided sleep was easier than going on. A rusted lantern lay beside them. Wren knelt and closed the skull's empty sockets with two fingers, an old courtesy, older than her exile.

"I'll carry what you couldn't," she told it. "That's the bargain now."

The pull began an hour later.

It started as warmth in her chest—not the pleasant kind, but the kind of warmth that precedes a fever. Then a tingling, like a limb waking from numbness, except the numbness was her immortality and the pins and needles were time itself beginning to move through her again. By the second hour her hands ached. By the third hour her knees ached, joints she hadn't felt in three hundred years announcing themselves like strangers arriving at a door.

*This is what mortals feel,* she thought, climbing. *All the time. Every day. This low constant complaint of the body.* No wonder they were brave. No wonder they were kind. They did everything with this noise in the background.

The chamber opened without warning.

One moment she was squeezing through a crack barely wide enough for her shoulders; the next she was standing in a cathedral of black glass, a bubble blown in the mountain's throat centuries ago and frozen mid-breath. Obsidian walls rose smooth and curved into a dome above her, and the floor was a lake—not of water but of something darker, thicker, that reflected nothing. The air shimmered with heat that had nowhere to go.

And in the center of the lake, on a pillar of basalt, sat the stone.

It was smaller than she expected. Smaller than the drowned chapel's stone, smaller than the market stone they'd traded three favors to locate. A rough oval of grey rock, no bigger than a fist, sitting on its pillar like an egg in a nest. Unremarkable. Almost humble.

Except that the darkness around it bent toward it. The shadows in the chamber leaned inward like courtiers bowing to a dying king. And the heat—all that trapped, breathless heat—flowed toward it in visible rivers, feeding something that never ate enough.

The third covenant-stone. The Fire-Heart. The piece of the original pact that held the dragons' promise to keep the sky lit, carved from the first flame Wren's mother ever breathed.

"Hello, little one," Wren said aloud, and her voice echoed strangely in the dome. "You've been hungry a long time."

The stone pulsed. Once. Like a heartbeat answering.

She waded into the dark lake. It wasn't hot—it was the opposite of hot, a cold that pulled warmth out of her boots, her legs, her hips, drawing it downward toward the pillar in slow greedy threads. She understood then, with terrible clarity, what the lake was. Not lava. Not water. It was the stone's hunger given form, a moat of pure appetite between the world and the thing it fed.

Each step cost her. She could feel it precisely, the way she could once feel the weight of gold hoarded in a cave—every step a coin paid out. Warmth left her. Then color. Then something subtler, something she couldn't name until it was gone: the memory of flight. Not the ability—she'd lost that with her wings—but the *want*, the lifelong ache for open sky that had defined her exile. The lake drank it, and suddenly she felt lighter and emptier, like a house whose furniture has been sold off room by room.

By the time she reached the pillar, she was crying without noticing. Mortal tears, salt and water, nothing magical about them. They steamed faintly where they fell.

"Right," she whispered. "Up we go."

The pillar was slick and tall, and she climbed it with hands that shook. At the top, the stone waited, and up close she could see what it truly was: a fragment of eggshell. Dragon eggshell, ancient beyond reckoning, from the first clutch ever laid beneath the first lit sky. Of course it was hungry. It had been waiting four hundred years for the warmth that was supposed to keep it alive.

Wren cupped it in both hands.

The taking was worse than the walking. The stone drank from her directly now, mouth to wound, and she gave it what it asked because there was no alternative and because, in some deep reptilian place, she *wanted* to. This was what dragons did. They burned so that smaller things could live in the light. Her mother had said it once, in the last conversation they'd ever had: *We are not the fire's owners, little one. We are its hands.*

So she opened her hands, and her fire, and her years.

She felt each one go. Not painfully—worse, gently, the way sleep takes a day from you without asking. Three hundred years of memory dimming at the edges. The taste of cloud at ten thousand feet. The sound of her name spoken in the Old Tongue by throats that no longer existed. Her mother's face, going soft and blurred like a painting left in the rain. She grabbed at them and lost them anyway, and the stone swallowed, and swelled faintly warmer in her palms, and asked for more.

"No," she gasped. "No more. That's—enough. That's *enough*."

The stone pulsed, sullen, and subsided. The lake around her stilled. Somewhere far above, muffled by miles of stone, she imagined she could hear Oscar pacing.

Wren climbed down the pillar on arms that trembled, and waded back across the lake, and the lake took a little more because that was its nature, and she let it because she had nothing left to bargain with. When she reached the far shore and looked back, the chamber was just a chamber—dark glass and cold stone, the shadows no longer leaning, the hunger satisfied for now. Dormant again. Sleeping again.

She had done that. She had fed it enough to sleep for another age.

She wondered, distantly, what she'd bought that sleep with. She tried to remember her mother's face and got an impression: golden, enormous, kind. Just an impression. The rest was gone, paid into a fist-sized stone that now sat warm and heavy in her pack like a sleeping child.

*Do not wake what you cannot carry.*

Too late, she thought. Too late for all of it.

* * *

The climb back took nine hours. It should have taken six.

Her body failed her in small, humiliating ways. Her grip slipped twice on ledges she could once have hung from by one finger. Her vision grayed at the edges whenever she pushed too hard, and once she had to sit down on a ledge over a drop and breathe through a wave of dizziness that felt insultingly, ordinarily human. She was thirty feet from certain death and what frightened her wasn't the fall. It was how normal the fear felt. How *at home* she was in it already.

Near the top, she passed the niche with the skeleton again, and stopped, and found she couldn't remember why she'd closed its eyes. She remembered *that* she had. The act remained; the reason had been drunk by the lake with everything else. She stared at the curled bones and felt a grief with no object, mourning someone whose story she'd already paid away.

"Sorry," she told it. "I think you were brave. I hope that's right."

Then she climbed on, because dawn was coming, and she had promised.

* * *

She surfaced from the fissure as the false dawn was graying the east, and the first thing she saw was Oscar, sitting cross-legged at the crater's rim exactly where she'd left him, exactly where he must have sat all night. Sera was asleep against a rock nearby, wrapped in both their cloaks, which meant Oscar had given up his and sat in the cold all night rather than wake her.

When he saw Wren, he stood up too fast and stumbled on the scree, and crossed the distance between them at a half-run, and then stopped short, because something in her face made him afraid to touch her.

"You're bleeding," he said.

She looked down. Her palms were shredded from the climb, ordinary human blood welling in the lines of them, and she stared at it with distant fascination. She hadn't bled since she took this form. She hadn't scarred, hadn't bruised, hadn't aged a single day in three hundred years of exile. The body had been a costume. Now the costume was becoming skin.

"Yes," she said. "I am."

"What did it cost?" His voice was very controlled, the way it went when he was frightened. "Wren. What did it cost?"

"A few things I can't name anymore," she said. "And some things I can." She held up her hand, watching the blood bead. "Look. I'm mortal now. Really mortal. Not playing at it. The stone took the last of the..." she searched for the word, "...the padding. What's left is what's left. One life. Yours-length, maybe less."

Oscar made a sound she'd never heard from him before—small and involuntary, like something breaking quietly in another room.

"Hey." She reached up and touched his face with her ruined hand, leaving a smear of red on his cheek, and he caught her wrist and held it like it was made of glass, and pressed his lips to her palm, which was ridiculous and unbearable and exactly right. "Listen to me. I knew the price going down. I'd pay it again. I'd pay it twice."

"Why?"

"Because the sky is worth more than I am." She smiled, and it hurt her face, muscles complaining at the unfamiliar work. "That used to be a doctrine. Now it's just arithmetic. I have fewer years than you do, Oscar Thackeray. So we shouldn't waste them standing on a freezing mountain having the same argument."

Sera was awake now, saying nothing, watching Wren with the sharp clinical grief of a woman who had seen ward-keepers spend themselves the same way—light poured out against the dark, never refillable. She handed Wren a cup of tea without a word, and Wren's hands shook so badly around it that Oscar had to help her hold it, and none of them remarked on it, because remarking would make it real, and it was already real.

"We have two stones," Sera said finally, practically, because someone had to be. "Two confirmed. The fourth is with the star-watchers, past the edge of every map I've ever seen. And the fifth..."

"The fifth is in Thornfell," Wren said. "In the guild hall. Under Corvane's hearth."

Everyone was quiet.

"The hearth-flame," Sera said slowly. "The one that's never gone out. The one the guild swears oaths over."

"It's not a flame. It's a stone wearing a flame like a coat. Your founders took it when they broke the covenant, and they've been feeding it ever since—slowly, secretly, with whatever fire they could steal. That's why the wards held so long after the betrayal. The guild was running on stolen light and calling it tradition." Wren drank the tea. It was the best thing she had ever tasted, which was either the tea or the mortality talking. "Corvane knows what he's sitting on. Maybe he's always known."

"Then he'll never give it up," Oscar said.

"No," Wren agreed. "He won't."

Oscar looked east, toward the mountains, toward home, and Wren watched him do the arithmetic he'd promised not to do out loud—the distance, the danger, the odds, the woman beside him with shaking hands and a finite number of mornings. She watched him arrive at the total and refuse to say it.

"So we go back," he said. "Through everything, back the way we came, into the guild's own hall. To take the last stone from the man who raised me."

"Yes," said Wren.

"And Corvane will be waiting."

"He's always been waiting," Sera said. "We just didn't know for what."

Far below them, deep in the mountain, the Fire-Heart slept on the warmth Wren had paid for it, and overhead the sky stayed black and starless, and somewhere in the east, faint as a lie, the first gray suggestion of dawn crept along the horizon—thin, uncertain, and stubbornly, stubbornly there.
