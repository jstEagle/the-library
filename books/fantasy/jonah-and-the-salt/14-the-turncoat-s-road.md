## 14. The Turncoat's Road

The smugglers' roads were the only maps worth trusting anymore.

Not paper—paper lied, or rather, it told the truth to whoever paid for it. The real map lived in the body: in the way a slope should feel underfoot at a certain elevation, in the smell of pine giving way to scree, in the particular silence of a hollow where you could stand upright and no one above would ever know. My father had taught it to me as rhythm before I could read. *Left foot long, right foot short, and when the wind turns cold against your left cheek, you're two hundred paces from the Undercut.* The Salt Guild hadn't built its roads so much as memorized them into existence, generation after generation, until Thornfell itself seemed to know us back.

We were three days out from the drowned chapel, four stones wrapped in oilcloth against my chest like a second heartbeat, when we came to the Doorstep—the first of the true secret ways, a crack in the mountain's flank that opened into a gallery of passages running beneath the whole spine of Thornfell. Sera stood before it with her ward-lamp raised, reading the old sigils cut into the stone, and I watched her face do something complicated.

"These are beautiful," she said. "Do you understand what they are? Pre-collapse wards. Intact ones. Whoever cut these knew dragon-script."

"Guild founders," I said. "Everyone knows the smugglers' roads were the first roads."

"Everyone knows nothing," Sera said, but she was already sketching the sigils into her notebook, hungry as always. Wren stood a little apart, hood up, one hand pressed flat against the rock. When I looked at her, she took her hand away quickly, as if caught stealing warmth.

"Cold?" I asked.

"Old," she said, which was her answer for everything now. Since the volcano she'd been quieter, thinner somehow, the way a candle is thinner after a long night. Her fire had bought us passage through the deep places and it was still being paid off, drop by drop. She slept more. She ate less. Twice I'd seen her stare at her own hands as though checking they still belonged to her.

I didn't ask again. Some questions you save until you can bear the answers.

* * *

Tam Voss found us on the second morning under the mountains, and that was the mistake—or mine, anyway. The betrayal wasn't his. The betrayal was mine, for believing that friendship survived the kind of dark we were walking through.

He came out of a side gallery with his lamp held high and his crossbow slung loose, both hands visible, grinning the way he'd grinned since we were boys running salt past the toll-wardens of Lower Marsh. Tam was the only person in the Guild who'd never once called me *the youngest*. He'd called me *partner*, even when I was twelve and carrying half my weight in contraband. He'd hidden me from Corvane's enforcers twice. He'd sat with me the night my father's body came down from the high passes and drunk most of the mourning brandy himself, because he said someone had to.

"Oscar Thackeray," he said now, spreading his arms. "Alive. They said you were dead. They said you burned a chapel full of ghosts and stole the crown jewels off a corpse."

"They said wrong."

"They usually do." His grin flickered as he took in the others—Sera the disgraced ward-keeper, whose name alone was worth a bounty; Wren in her hood, still as a well. His eyes came back to me and I watched him decide not to ask. That was Tam. Curiosity was a luxury; he'd learned economy early. "Word is Corvane's put a price on three heads. Word is you're carrying something that glows. Word is—" he hesitated, and for just a moment the grin slipped and I saw how tired he was, how thin the man beneath the grin had gotten since the stars went out, "—word is the villages are burning their own furniture to stay warm, and the Guild won't open the granaries."

"That word is true."

"I figured." He shrugged. "So. Where are you going, partner?"

And here is what I want written down somewhere, in whatever ledger survives this: I did not tell him about the stones. I did not tell him about the covenant or Wren or the road to the lowlands. What I told him was that we needed passage through the Undercut and out by the Weeping Stair, and that I'd pay in guild-marks, honest weight. A route. Only a route. Because Tam had carried my secrets since before I had any worth carrying, and some debts you don't audit.

He walked with us a mile, talking about the Guild hall, about Corvane's new men patrolling the surface passes with black lanterns that ate lamplight, about a girl in Millbrook who'd gone snow-mad and sworn the darkness spoke to her. Normal talk. Old talk. At the fork where the galleries split, he gripped my forearm the old way, smuggler to smuggler, and said, "Go safe, Oscar."

I believed him. That's the whole of it. I believed him the way you believe the mountain will hold.

* * *

They hit us six hours later, in the Long Gallery, where the ceiling rose into darkness and the floor ran smooth and pale with ancient salt crust, and there was nowhere—nowhere—to run except forward or back.

I heard them before I saw them, which saved our lives: the particular wrongness of boots trying to be quiet. Twenty years of smuggling teaches you that silence has a texture, and armed silence crunches. I threw up a fist and we went flat against the wall behind a rib of rock, lamps shuttered, and then the black lanterns came around the bend ahead of us, five of them, and behind the lanterns came crossbows, and behind the crossbows came a voice I knew.

"Oscar." Corvane's captain, Maren Hale, a woman I'd shared watch-fires with, whose brother I'd pulled out of an avalanche when he was sixteen. She sounded tired. She sounded sorry. She sounded absolutely certain. "You're surrounded. There are thirty of us and this gallery goes nowhere but where we let it. Give up the stones and walk out alive. That's the offer. It doesn't get repeated."

Thirty. In the Long Gallery, six hours after I'd given Tam Voss exactly one piece of information: where we'd be.

Sera's hand found my sleeve. Wren had gone very still beside me, and I could feel heat coming off her like a banked oven, like the air above a summer road, and I thought, absurdly, *don't, please don't, not here, not for this.*

"Maren," I called out, because there was no point whispering anymore. "Who gave you the route?"

She didn't answer. That was answer enough.

Something went through me that had nothing to do with the crossbows. It was quiet and enormous, like a door closing in a very large house. Tam. Tam's arm-grip, Tam's grin, Tam drinking the mourning brandy and saying *someone has to.* Six hours. He hadn't even haggled over the price, maybe. Maybe Corvane had offered him something better than marks—a granary opened, a family moved inside the walls, a guarantee. Maybe he'd told himself it was mercy. Maybe he'd told himself I was dead already, walking around with stolen fire in a world that was ending, and turning me in was the same as burying me.

It didn't matter. All the reasons in the world weighed nothing next to the fact of thirty loaded crossbows in the dark.

"We can't fight thirty," Sera breathed.

"No," I said. "But we don't have to."

Because I'd grown up in these galleries, and Maren hadn't, and Tam—Tam had known the routes but he'd sold a route, not the mountain. And there was one thing about the Long Gallery every smuggler learned before they were trusted with it, one thing kept deliberately out of every chart and every telling: the gallery breathed. Somewhere below its floor ran a seam of natural flue-channels, old volcanic throats left over from when Thornfell was young and angry, and they connected—through chambers no living person had walked in three hundred years—to the Founders' Vault, the sealed heart of the road-system, where the first guildsmen had kept their oaths.

Where, Sera had told me, the founders had kept their dragons.

"The flues," I whispered. "Under the floor. If we can crack the salt crust at the third marker—"

"It'll bring half the gallery down on us," Sera said.

"It'll bring the gallery down on *them.*"

Wren turned her head toward me. In the shuttered dark I couldn't see her face, only the ember-glow of her eyes, faint as coals under ash. "How far down does that shaft go, Oscar?"

"Deep enough."

"And at the bottom?"

I swallowed. "The Vault."

She was quiet for a moment. Then she said, softly, almost gently, "Then we're going to burn the road either way. You understand that. Once we open the deep ways, once fire gets into these galleries, every secret road your family ever memorized ends tonight. Every route. Every hiding place. Three hundred years of invisible paths, gone in an hour." A pause. "Are you ready for that?"

I thought about my father's rhythm lesson. Left foot long, right foot short. About teaching it someday to a child of my own, on roads that would no longer exist. About the villages, the granaries, the girl in Millbrook, the thirty crossbowmen whose faces I mostly knew. About Tam's forearm grip, warm and easy, six hours before he fed us to the dark.

The Guild had kept its secrets the way a miser keeps coins—clenched, counted, useless to anyone but itself. And look what the secrets had bought. A starless sky. A rotting covenant. A guildmaster selling the dark to its own people, room by room.

"Yeah," I said. "Burn it."

* * *

Getting to the third marker meant crossing forty yards of open gallery floor, and Maren's lanterns made sure there was no darkness left to cross it in. So we made our own.

Sera went first—not with fire, with light, a ward-flare ripped straight out of her lamp-core and hurled down the gallery, a white starburst that seared every black lantern to blindness and left thirty trained killers staring at purple ghosts. Wren went second, hood back, and I heard the shout go up along the line—*the exile, it's the exile, loose the bolts*—and then the bolts never landed, because the air in front of her simply refused them, bending aside like wind around a chimney. She wasn't fighting. She was walking, fast, furious, spending fire she could not afford, and I scrambled along behind her in the churned shadows feeling like a man clinging to the tail of a comet.

The third marker was a squat pillar of salt-crusted stone with the old sign of the Guild cut into it: a closed hand. Fitting. I hit it with the iron pry-bar I'd carried since I was fourteen and it cracked on the second blow, crust peeling away to show a round hatch beneath, banded in bronze, seamed with dragon-script that lit at Wren's approach like letters remembering themselves.

"Bolts!" Maren's voice, raw now, the purple-blindness fading. "Loose at the—"

Wren put her palm flat on the bronze and the hatch fell inward and the mountain inhaled.

The sound was the worst part. Not an explosion—a *breath*, vast and slow, the flue-channels taking their first air in centuries, and the pressure change hit the gallery like a tide. Dust lifted off the floor in sheets. Torches bent horizontal. Men staggered. And down the shaft below us, rising, came heat—the deep heat of Thornfell's buried blood, the heat the founders had once tamed and piped and called dragon-road, and it smelled of sulfur and of something older, something like a sleeping animal's den.

"Down," Wren said, and we went, all three of us, dropping into the throat of the mountain while behind and above us Maren Hale shouted orders that dissolved into coughing as the dust wall swallowed the gallery.

We fell perhaps twenty feet onto a ledge of fused glass—old lava, polished smooth—and then the fire came.

Not Wren's fire. The mountain's own. The open flue drew flame up from some deep chamber the way a chimney draws a hearth, and suddenly the Long Gallery above us was a furnace roar, and the draft pulled sideways through a hundred branching channels, and everywhere, everywhere, the secret roads began to burn.

I felt each one go. That's what I hadn't prepared for. The Undercut, where I'd hidden from enforcers at fifteen. The Weeping Stair, where my father had proposed to my mother, of all impossible places, halfway between a job and home. The Hollow Chapel branch, the Miller's Vein, the Cold Larder, the Widow's Walk—each gallery had a name and a history and a thousand small memories stacked in it like contraband, and the fire went through them all with the impartial appetite of truth. I sat on the glass ledge with my arms around the stones and my forehead against my knees and I grieved. Not elegantly. Not quietly. The way you grieve a parent who died while you were angry at them: all the love and all the rage coming out in the same ugly noise, and Sera holding my shoulder and saying nothing, because she understood, because she'd lost her wards the same way, and Wren standing watch at the shaft's edge with firelight playing over her face like a mask of gold.

"I'm sorry," I said finally, to nobody, to everybody, to the burning roads. "I'm sorry. It was the only way."

Above us, faintly, came the sounds of Maren's company retreating—boots, shouting, the clatter of a dropped crossbow—and then, gradually, only fire.

When I could speak again, I said, "Tam."

"What about him?" Sera asked carefully.

"He sold a road that doesn't exist anymore." I wiped my face with the back of my hand and found I could almost smile. "Corvane paid for a map and got smoke."

But it rang hollow, and we all knew it. Because Tam hadn't sold a map. He'd sold *me*, and I was still here, and sooner or later Corvane would stop buying routes and start asking why his captain kept coming back empty-handed, and then the price on my head would stop being a number and start being an obsession. And Tam—I couldn't think about Tam yet. Later. When grief stopped being a place I was standing in and became a thing I carried.

* * *

The ledge led down, as ledges in old volcanoes do, spiraling through glass and cinder into air that grew warmer and stranger with every turn. Sera's ward-lamp showed script on the walls now—not smuggler marks, not ward-sigils, but flowing lines of dragon-script, dense as scripture, and she traced them with trembling fingers without touching.

"This is it," she whispered. "The Founders' Vault. The records said it was sealed from the inside. Sealed *by* the—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Oscar. The stones. Take them out."

My hands were shaking. I unwrapped the oilcloth, and four covenant-stones answered the dark.

They sang. Quietly, subterraneously, a vibration felt in the teeth rather than heard—but they sang, and the dragon-script on the walls answered, lighting vein by vein, and ahead of us the spiral ended at a door the size of a cathedral gate, bronze and basalt fused into one seamless mass, with a single inscription across its face in letters tall as I was. Wren read it aloud, and her voice broke on the last word, and I understood that she had read it before, long ago, from the other side.

Sera translated anyway, for me, for the record, for whatever future was left:

*"Here lie the promises. Enter only to keep them."*

Below the door, faint through the bronze, something shifted. Something vast exhaled. And in the space between one breath and the next, all four stones flared white-hot in my arms, and from beyond the vault-gate came a sound I had no name for—half groan, half hymn—as whatever the founders had locked away woke, and knew we had come, and began, at long last, to ask us why.
