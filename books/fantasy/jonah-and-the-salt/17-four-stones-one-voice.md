## 17. Four Stones, One Voice

I have not slept in three days, and I do not intend to start now.

Sleep, for me, has become a small death. Each time I close my eyes I feel the fire guttering in my chest like a candle in a drafty chapel, and each time I wake there is less of it. Mortality is not a cliff you fall off. It is a stairway, and I am descending it one step at a time, and every step down makes the human world louder—the cold sharper, the hunger more insistent, the smell of Oscar's woodsmoke cloak suddenly a thing I *notice* instead of merely tolerate.

But tonight none of that matters. Tonight the stones are singing.

We made camp in the hollow beneath the hermit order's mountain, in a ring of standing stones older than any covenant, older than Thornfell itself. Sera laid them out on a flat slab of granite: four stones, four shapes, four weights of history. The drowned chapel's stone, still weeping salt water though it hasn't touched the sea in weeks. The market stone, traded to us by a woman who asked only that we remember her son's name. The volcano stone, which I carried up out of the dark myself and which has never again been warm. And the star-watchers' stone, pale as a fingernail moon, given freely by an order that had guarded it for nine hundred years without once understanding what it was for.

They should not fit together. That was Sera's theory—that they were cut from a single heart-stone and shattered deliberately, so that no one hand could ever hold them all again. She was right. They don't fit. They *interlock*, the way broken things interlock when you press them hard enough, edges biting into edges, seams jagged as lightning.

Oscar set them down with the care of a man handling his own funeral arrangements. "Now what?"

"Now," Sera said, "we find out if we were worth it."

I put my hand on the largest stone. Oscar, after a moment's hesitation, put his beside mine. Sera took the third place without being asked, because she is the kind of woman who takes the third place without being asked, and has been since the day I met her.

The stones began to hum.

Not a sound, exactly. A vibration, felt in the teeth, in the breastbone, in the old hollow where my fire used to live. It climbed through my arm and into my chest and found what remained of my flame—and it *called* to it, the way a mother's voice calls across a crowded square, and my fire answered before I could stop it, flaring up bright and desperate and homesick.

Then the world folded shut around us like a book closing, and I fell into memory that was not mine.

* * *

I stood on a mountainside under a sky so full of stars it looked wounded. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. A river of light pouring from peak to peak, so dense in places that shadows could not fall, and I understood, with a grief that arrived whole and pre-formed, that this was Thornfell before the dark. This was the sky I had been born under and would never see again.

Dragons filled it.

Not the handful of exiles and ghosts my kind has dwindled to—hundreds of them, wheeling in slow constellations, their scales catching starlight until the mountains themselves seemed to burn cold. Below them, on a shelf of rock overlooking the valley, stood eleven figures in grey cloaks, holding candles.

Humans. The founders of the Salt Guild.

And I knew them. Not their faces—I had never seen these faces—but their *posture*. The way they stood in a half-circle, nervous and resolute, the way people stand when they are about to promise something enormous. I have watched humans make promises for two thousand years. I know the shape of it.

The eldest founder stepped forward. A woman, broad-shouldered, salt-white streak in her black hair. When she spoke, her voice carried the particular tremor of someone who knows she is speaking for history.

"We do not ask to be your equals," she said. "We ask to be your neighbors."

A dragon descended. Enormous—larger than any dragon living today, larger than I ever was at my height. Its scales were the grey of storm-clouds shot through with silver, and when it landed the mountain did not even shudder; it settled, gently, the way a large animal lies down near a fire it trusts.

"You ask for passage through our peaks," the dragon said. Its voice was thunder heard through water. "You ask for our fire to warm your villages and light your wards. You ask us to share the sky above your children."

"We ask for a covenant," the woman said. "Freely given, freely kept. Our guild will carry what your realms need and guard what your realms treasure. In return—"

"In return, we will be one people's memory and another people's muscle," the dragon said, and something moved through its voice that I recognized, because I have heard it in my own throat: weariness. Loneliness. The specific exhaustion of a creature that has lived too long among too few of its own kind. "Yes. We were dying of solitude before you came, little candle-bearers. Did you know that? Dragons do not die of wounds. We die of *quiet*."

It extended one foreleg, and upon it rested a stone—a single stone, whole, luminous, beating with light like a slow heart.

"The covenant-stone," the eldest founder whispered.

"Cut from my own heart-fire," the dragon said. "One stone, one promise. As long as it burns, the sky stays open between us. As long as it burns, no ward shall bar a dragon, no border shall divide a friend. Swear it."

Eleven hands came forward. Eleven voices, one oath. And the stone drank the vow the way dry earth drinks rain, and the stars overhead flared so brightly that I—watching, weeping, a ghost inside a ghost—had to look away.

That was the covenant. That was what we had been climbing toward through chapels and volcanoes and markets full of refugees. Not a weapon. Not a key. A *promise*, pressed into mineral form, and I understood then why the stones sang rather than shouted, why they needed four bearers instead of one:

Because a promise this size was never meant to be held alone.

* * *

The vision did not release us. It deepened.

The scene shifted—I felt it shift, the way a dream shifts when it turns against you—and I was standing in the same place, on the same shelf of rock, but the sky had changed. Fewer stars now. Gaps in the river of light, like missing teeth. And the dragons above flew in tighter formations, wary, watching the humans below with eyes that had learned caution.

Generations had passed. I knew it the way you know things in visions, without being told: the founders' faces had grown into other faces, heirs carrying an inheritance they hadn't chosen. And at their center stood a man I knew instantly, bone-deep, the way you know a poison you've been drinking for years.

He wore the guildmaster's chain. Corvane's chain—or Corvane's chain's grandfather, or great-grandfather, some link in the long descent of men who held power they had not earned. He was young. That was the worst part. Young and clever and frightened, standing before the same ancient dragon—older now, grey going to white, its silver scales dulled to pewter—and the stone sat on its foreleg between them, dimmer than it had been in the first vision. Not dead. Dimmed. A fire banked by neglect.

"The wards are failing," the young guildmaster said. "The border wards. Your fire feeds them, and your fire is failing, and the villages in the lowlands are already going dark."

"Then let us renew the covenant," the dragon said. "Let the guild come to the high peaks, as your founders did. Let new hands swear new oaths. The stone asks only to be remembered."

"No." The word came out fast, too fast, the word of a man who had rehearsed his refusal. "No. I've... considered it. With the council. We've decided the covenant is—outdated. The guild's interests have changed. The routes have changed. What we need from the mountains is passage, not partnership. We'll maintain the wards ourselves. Human wards. Human hands."

The dragon was silent for a long time. Long enough that the wind had time to change direction twice.

"You are afraid," it said finally. "Not of us. Of *needing* us. Needing is a debt, and debts can be called. Your founders understood that a covenant freely entered cannot be used as a leash. You want the benefits of the bond without the bond itself. You want the fire without the friendship."

"I want the guild to survive."

"The guild survives *because* of the covenant," the dragon said, and its voice cracked—not with anger, but with something worse. Recognition. "Little candle-bearer. Do you know how many times I have heard this speech? In how many tongues? From how many kingdoms? It always begins with 'the times have changed' and ends with a locked door. Always. Every age, some clever young creature decides that loyalty is a currency to be spent rather than a fire to be fed."

It lowered its head, and its eye—vast, amber, ancient—came level with the young man's face. And the dragon said, quietly, in a voice that broke my heart because I have heard its echo in every century of my long life:

"When you break this, you will tell yourselves it was necessary. You will write it down as wisdom. Your grandchildren will inherit the lie and call it heritage. And the sky will close, and the quiet will come back, and my kind will die of it one by one in the high places, and yours will forget we were ever anything but stories." A pause. "But you will keep the salt roads. Won't you? Those at least will prosper."

The young guildmaster did not answer. That was the answer.

What happened next happened quickly, and I will not describe it fully because some things should not be described fully, they should only be witnessed and then carried. There was a ritual—human-made, human-worded, a counterfeit covenant written in a counterfeit tongue. There were nine hands raised instead of eleven; two of the founders' heirs refused, and were expelled from the guild that night, their names struck from the rolls, their descendants scattered into the very smuggling fringes the guild would later pretend never to exist. There was a severing, done with cold iron and colder intent, and the covenant-stone—whole, beating, alive—was shattered on an anvil of mountain granite while the dragon screamed a sound I will hear until I die.

Four pieces. Scattered to four corners. Hidden, buried, drowned, forgotten—because a broken promise is easier to lose than a whole one.

And the sky, that night, began to die.

* * *

I came back to myself on my knees in the frost, both hands still pressed to the stone, tears freezing on my face. Oscar was beside me, shaking. Sera had both fists pressed to her mouth, and behind her eyes I could see the vision still playing—the particular horror of a ward-keeper who has just learned that every ward she ever tended was built on a severed foundation, a house of locks built over a broken door.

"The ninth guildmaster," Sera said hoarsely. "That's who that was. Aldric Thackeray's era. The schism. We have records—fragments, disputed fragments—they called it the Reformation. They *taught* it as reformation. Modernization. The guild outgrowing its dependencies." Her laugh came out wrong, jagged. "Dependencies. He called the dragons *dependencies*."

"They weren't wrong about the fear," Oscar said quietly. He was staring at his hands. "You saw it too. He wasn't evil. He was scared. Scared men build locked doors and call them safety." He looked up at me, and his face in the starless dark was very young and very old at once. "Wren. The last thing the dragon said. About the salt roads prospering. My family—" His voice caught. "My family *was* the fringe. The expelled ones. The smugglers. The guild struck my ancestors from the rolls and then spent six generations pretending we didn't exist while we carried their contraband through their mountains."

"I know," I said.

Because I did know. I had known since the drowned chapel, since the ghosts demanded confession and Oscar gave them one. But knowing a thing and seeing it are different animals, and now he had seen it, and the seeing was doing its work in him, visible as weather.

"So the guild didn't betray the dragons," he said slowly. "The guild betrayed *us* first. And we became the thing they pretended we weren't. And the dragons—" He stopped. Started again. "The dragons died of quiet. In the high places. Alone. While my family ran their roads and never once looked up."

"Do not," I said, and my voice came out harder than I intended, harder than kindness allows, "do not do that."

"Do what?"

"Carry it all. It's the human disease—you find a wound and you move into it. You saw a choice made by a frightened man eighty years before your birth, and you're trying to serve its sentence." I took his wrist, roughly, the way you grab someone stepping off a ledge. "Listen to me. I was alive when the covenant broke. Did you know that? I was young, by our measure. I remember the sky thinning. I remember the elders saying *the quiet is coming back* and flying higher and higher to escape it, and I remember deciding they were being dramatic." My throat closed. I made myself go on. "I did nothing either. I was not betrayed, Oscar. I was *comfortable*. I had my territory and my solitude and I told myself the silence in the east was weather. So if you're serving a sentence for complicity, get in line behind me, and behind Sera, and behind every creature that watched the sky die and called it someone else's crime."

Sera made a sound that might have been a laugh. "Understanding without absolution," she murmured. "That's what the stones are showing us. Not forgiveness. Just—the truth, plain, so we can't unsee it. The founders chose power over promise. The dragons chose pride over pursuit. The ward-keepers chose procedure over alarm. Everyone chose, and everyone chose smaller than the moment required." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "And now we choose."

The stones pulsed under our hands. Warmer now. The singing had changed—it had been a lament, and now it was something else. A summons. A question, hanging in the frozen air like breath:

*One stone remains. One promise remains unbroken. What will you do with it?*

"The fifth stone," Oscar said. "Corvane has it. Has had it all along—in the guild hall, in the hearth-flame. That's why the wards failed everywhere else first. He wasn't hiding the stone. He was *feeding* it selectively, keeping the guild hall lit while the rest of the sky went dark." His jaw tightened. "He's been sitting on a fifth of the covenant for years, warming his hands on it, telling himself it was stewardship."

"He believes it's stewardship," Sera said grimly. "That's what makes him dangerous. Men who know they're villains can be fought. Men who've written themselves as guardians—" She shook her head. "We go home, Oscar. Through Thornfell, back to the hall, with everything we've learned and everything we are. And we take the last stone back."

I looked up, out of habit, out of two thousand years of habit, at a sky with no stars in it. And for the first time since the night they vanished, I felt something other than grief.

I felt the fourth stone humming under my palm like a heart waiting to be made whole, and I thought: *soon*. Soon the sky would have to account for itself. Soon the quiet would end—one way or another.

Oscar stood, and pulled us both up with him, and there was something in his stance I had never seen before—not the smuggler's low readiness, not the boy's defensive hunch. Something upright. Something that people, I suspected, would follow.

"Pack everything," he said. "We walk tonight. Corvane's had eighty years of head start, and I'm done giving him more."

Behind us, unattended on the granite slab, the four stones sang on—softly, steadily, like a promise rehearsing itself for the day it would finally, at long last, be kept.
