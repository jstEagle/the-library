## 18. Thornfell Rising

The mountain road was never meant to carry this many people.

Oscar had walked the smugglers' paths since he was nine years old—narrow ledges cut into Thornfell's flanks, switchbacks hidden behind waterfalls, tunnels that smelled of old salt and older secrets. He knew every handhold and every blind corner. He knew which stones sang underfoot and which kept silent. What he did not know was how to move four hundred people along them without losing half to the dark.

He stood at the head of the column in the grey hour before dawn, watching the line snake back down into the valley like a thread pulled loose from a hem. Smugglers with their packs of contraband repurposed into grain and medicine. Refugees from the Market of Small Mercies, carrying children and cooking pots and whatever grief they hadn't been able to leave behind. Ward-keepers in their faded blue, Sera at their center like a keel keeping a listing ship upright. And above them all, that terrible ceiling: a sky with no stars, no moon worth naming, just a flat black nothing pressing down on the peaks like a lid on a pot.

"Four hundred," Sera said, coming up beside him. She'd counted twice. "Maybe forty more straggling. The hermits stayed—the star-watchers wouldn't leave their observatory, stubborn as ever—but they gave us everything else. Food, maps, and that." She nodded at the bundle on his back, wrapped in oilcloth. Four covenant-stones, singing so quietly now that only he could feel it, a hum against his spine like a second heartbeat.

"Corvane will know we're coming," Oscar said.

"He's known since the refugee city fell. That's not the question." Sera pulled her cloak tighter. "The question is whether he knows *where*. Your roads, Oscar. Are they still yours?"

He thought about Tam, who'd sold their route for a promise of safety. He thought about the secret roads burning behind them, the tunnels collapsed by their own hands, the map of his whole life going up in smoke so Corvane couldn't follow. Grief and relief had arrived together that night, twins he hadn't asked for.

"The guild roads are dead," he said. "But I grew up on the ones the guild doesn't know about. My mother's routes. The ones she ran before the Salt Guild took me in." He heard his own voice go quiet. "Corvane thinks he knows every path through Thornfell. He's about to learn he only knew half."

* * *

They climbed for two days through country that shouldn't have held them.

Oscar led them up the Widow's Stair, a route so steep the smugglers used it only for cargo light enough to strap to a man's back—and here it carried grandmothers and infants both. He took them through the Hollow Under the Falls, where the water came down cold enough to kill and everyone crossed soaked and screaming and alive. He threaded them through the Needle's Eye, a crevice barely wider than a loaded pack, and watched a ward-keeper named Ilse weep with claustrophobia on one side and laugh on the other.

And everywhere he went, people looked at him.

That was the part he couldn't get used to. All his life, invisibility had been his craft and his pride. A good smuggler was a ghost; a great one was a rumor. He'd spent twenty years perfecting the art of not being seen, and now four hundred people turned their heads when he passed, the way flowers turn toward a light source they can't explain. Mothers pointed him out to their children. Old smugglers—men and women who'd known him since he was a pickpocket running errands for the tally-keepers—nodded to him like an equal, or more than an equal.

"You don't have to look at me," he muttered to a group of refugees huddled around a cookfire on the second night. "I'm just the one who knows the way."

An old woman with a burn-scarred cheek looked up at him. "Boy, do you know what you are?"

"A smuggler."

"You're the first person in three months who's taken us somewhere instead of away from something." She said it simply, the way you'd state the weather. "You walk like the road belongs to you. So we walk like it belongs to us too."

He didn't sleep well after that. He lay under the starless sky listening to the stones hum against his back and tried to understand how a man could spend his whole life learning to disappear and then, in the space of a season, become the thing everyone watched.

Wren found him at the edge of the camp. She sat down beside him without asking, the way she always did, and for a while neither of them spoke. In the dark she was just a shape, but he could feel the heat coming off her—less than there used to be. Less every week. The fire in her was a candle burning down, and they were asking it to light a mountain range.

"You're doing the thing again," she said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you decide you're a fraud and everyone will figure it out any moment." She picked up a pebble and tossed it into the dark. It never made a sound landing. "I've been alive a long time, Oscar. Longer than your guild, longer than your mountains. I know what leaders look like. Most of them are frauds who got loud enough. You're the opposite. You keep trying to be quiet enough to vanish, and the world keeps refusing to let you."

"That's not a compliment. That's a tragedy with better posture."

She laughed—a real laugh, low and warm, and for a moment he forgot to be afraid of how little fire she had left. Then her hand found his in the dark, and her fingers were cool. Too cool. Always too cool now.

"Tomorrow we take the hall," she said. "Whatever happens, I need you to know something first." Her voice changed, dropped its armor. "When I came down off my mountain in mortal skin, I told myself I'd find the stones or die trying, and either way it would be simple. Clean. A dragon's arithmetic. But then there was you, walking me over a pass in the rain, complaining about the weight of my pack, and it stopped being simple. Whatever tomorrow costs—I don't regret the price. I want you to hear that before the fighting starts, because after it starts nobody says anything true anymore."

Oscar's throat closed. He was a smuggler. He was good at moving things past guards, past wards, past danger. He had never once figured out how to move words past his own teeth when they mattered.

So he did what he knew. He squeezed her hand, and he said, "Then we make sure the price is cheap," and hoped she understood everything underneath it.

She did. Dragons, he was learning, always did.

* * *

The last approach to the guild hall began at the Salt Gate, the ancient archway where every smuggler swore their oath. Oscar had passed beneath it a thousand times. Tonight he stopped beneath it and turned to face the column stretching back down the mountain trail, torches doused, four hundred people breathing steam in the freezing dark.

Sera stood beside him. Wren, hooded, at his other shoulder. Behind them, the vanguard of smugglers he'd spent the evening choosing—not for loyalty to him, but for loyalty to each other. Marta Voss, who'd run grain to dying villages when Corvane ordered the stores hoarded. Old Piet, who'd taught Oscar lockwork and never once asked why a boy needed it. The Kell brothers, whose cousin Corvane had left outside the walls when the city fell.

"They'll have crossbows on the wall walks," Sera said. "Guild hunters at the doors. Maybe two hundred loyalists inside, and most of those are scared kids following orders, not believers."

"Most," Oscar said. "Not all."

"No. Not all." She touched his arm. "When we go in, some of them will die. Some of ours. Some of theirs. You need to say it out loud so they've chosen it with open eyes. That's the difference between you and him, Oscar. He spends people. You ask them."

So Oscar turned to face them, and the words came harder than any confession at the drowned chapel, harder than the renunciation he knew was still waiting for him somewhere ahead.

"I'm not going to lie to you," he said, and his voice carried further than he expected, out over the silent column, into the dark. "Behind that gate is the only home most of us ever had. The people holding it think they're defending it. Some of them are wrong, and some of them are just afraid, and we won't always be able to tell the difference in time. People are going to die tonight. Maybe people standing next to you. Maybe you." He let that sit. Four hundred people breathing. "I can't promise you victory. I can't even promise you a clean fight. What I can promise is this: when the sun comes up—if it comes up—no one in Thornfell will ever again be told to stay invisible while the world burns around them. No more villages left to starve. No more silence bought and paid for. That's what we're climbing toward. If that's worth the climb to you, follow me. If it isn't, there's no shame in turning back. There never has been."

Nobody turned back.

For a long moment the column just stood there, and then old Piet spat on his palms, shouldered his crowbar—the same one he'd used on guild locks for thirty years—and said, "Well? Open the door, lad. Some of us aren't getting younger."

And the column moved, and Oscar Thackeray, youngest smuggler of the Salt Guild, invisible man, ghost of the high passes, walked at the front of it and felt the strangest thing of his entire strange year: he wasn't leading them, exactly. They were carrying him. All of them, together, up the last mile of mountain, toward the hall where his whole life had been decided for him—and where, tonight, he intended to decide it back.

* * *

The Salt Gate was barred, but gates had never been Oscar's problem.

While Sera's ward-keepers unraveled the alarm-wards with the quiet efficiency of people undoing their own former lives, Oscar took six smugglers up the chimney route—the old flue behind the eastern kitchens that he'd discovered at eleven years old, escaping a beating from a tally-master. It was a smuggler's axiom that every building has a mouth and an anus, and the guild hall was no exception. They came up through the hearth of the kitchen in a cascade of soot, and the two cooks on night duty took one look at Oscar's face and raised their hands without a word. One of them, a woman named Bree who'd slipped him extra bread for fifteen years, whispered, "The hunters are massed at the main doors. They're expecting you there."

"Then we won't be there."

It went fast after that, the way these things always did—fast and ugly and nothing like the stories. The smugglers took the barracks corridor before the garrison finished buckling their gear. Piet's crew dropped the portcullis behind the hunters at the main doors, cutting them off from reinforcement, and the sound of Sera's voice ringing through the hall—"Ward-keepers! Your oaths were to the covenant, not the man! Stand down and be counted!"—did more damage than any blade. Hunters lowered their crossbows in twos and threes. Some fought. A few fought hard. The Kell brothers carried their wounded into the archive and didn't come back out for an hour.

By the time Oscar fought his way to the great hall itself, the battle was already becoming a memory. And there, at the far end, beneath the cold hearth where the guild's eternal flame had burned for three hundred years and now guttered around the fifth covenant-stone like a dying man's last breath—there stood Corvane.

The guildmaster looked smaller than Oscar remembered. Older. His fine coat was gone, replaced by plain wool, and his hands—those careful, ledger-keeping hands—were folded in front of him like a man at a funeral. Around him stood the last of his loyalists, maybe thirty, weapons ready but wavering. And beside the hearth, one hand extended toward the flame, stood someone Oscar had trusted with his life.

Tam.

Tam, who'd sold their route and gotten four hundred refugees hunted across the lowlands. Tam, whose face was wet with tears even as his fingers hovered near the stone. "Don't," Tam said, seeing Oscar. "Please, Oscar, don't come closer. He told me—he said if the stones reunite, everyone dies. He said the darkness is the only mercy left. I believed him. God help me, I still believe him."

"Step away from the hearth, Tam," Oscar said quietly.

"I can't. He gave me a place when no one else—" Tam's voice cracked. "You don't know what it's like, being nothing to everyone."

"Yeah," Oscar said. "I really don't know anything about that at all."

Something flickered across Tam's face—shame, or its ghost—and Corvane moved. Not toward Oscar. Toward the hearth. Toward the fifth stone, sitting in its nest of guttering flame, and Oscar understood in one white-hot instant that the guildmaster had lost everything else and had exactly one move left.

"No!" Oscar was already running.

Corvane swept his cloak across the hearth, smothering the flame—and the fifth stone went dark. The four stones on Oscar's back went silent as graves. The whole hall seemed to hold its breath, and in the sudden absolute stillness, Corvane turned to face him with tears running freely down his cheeks and something horribly like love in his eyes.

"There," he said softly. "Now no one has to burn."
