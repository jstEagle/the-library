## 19. The Last Dragon-Fire

The guild hall had never looked small to Oscar before.

He'd grown up beneath its rafters, learned to read ledgers by its hearth-light, learned to lie in its shadows. It had always been the largest thing in his world—a mountain of timber and stone with the Salt Guild's whole history packed into its bones. Now, standing at the head of two hundred smugglers, refugees, and ward-keepers who had climbed the Thornfell pass behind him, he saw it for what it was: a building. A big one. With doors that could be broken and a man inside who could be stopped.

"East postern's unguarded," Sera said beside him. She'd stripped her ward-keeper's coat down to the gray undertunic, and her hands kept moving—checking straps, flexing fingers—as if her body already knew what was coming. "Corvane pulled everyone to the great hall. He wants an audience."

"He wants hostages," Oscar said.

"He has them." Sera didn't soften it. Through the high windows they could see figures moving on the gallery—guild hunters, crossbows racked along the balustrade, and below them, huddled in the hall's far corner under guard, maybe forty people from Lowmark and the river villages. Corvane had emptied three settlements to fill his hall with leverage. "Whatever you're planning, plan around them."

Oscar looked at the people behind him. Tam with his crossbow held wrong. Maren the salt-wife who'd fed him soup in a dying village on the worst night of his life. Ward-keepers who hadn't worn their colors in years, standing shoulder to shoulder with smugglers who'd spent those same years robbing the roads they now defended. None of them were soldiers. All of them had come anyway.

"We go in loud," Oscar said. "All of us. He can't shoot two hundred people without becoming exactly what we say he is."

"And if he doesn't care what he becomes?"

"Then we learn something about him." Oscar checked the covenant-stones at his belt—one warm as a hearthbrick, one cold as river stone, one that hummed like a struck bell, one that gave him dreams of falling. Four stones. Four voices, singing low and constant since the volcano, since the bridge, since the chapel where he'd confessed to ghosts. The fifth was somewhere in this hall. It had always been here. *In front of us the whole time,* Wren had said, and laughed until she coughed.

Wren stood at his other side, and he made himself look at her the way you look at someone when you might not get another chance. She was thinner than the day she'd hired passage over the pass. Her fire had been bleeding out of her for weeks—the bridge, the volcano, the ambush on the turncoat's road—and it showed now in the gray at her temples and the way she stood very still, conserving everything, a lamp turned down to its last inch of oil.

"Don't," she said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You did it with your face." But she smiled, and put her hand over his where it rested on the stones. Her skin was cool. That frightened him more than the crossbows. "We finish this tonight, little smuggler. One way or another."

* * *

The east postern opened onto the kitchen corridor, and the kitchen corridor opened onto the great hall's side door, and Oscar had slipped through that door a hundred times in his life carrying contraband past men paid not to see him. Invisibility had been his whole trade. Tonight he walked through the same doorway visible to every eye in the room, and it felt like stepping off a cliff.

They came in loud, just as he'd promised. Two hundred people filling the hall's western end, torches and iron and the smell of cold mountain air. The guild hunters on the gallery leveled their crossbows. The hostages cried out. And at the far end of the hall, on the dais beneath the guild's old salt-white banner, Guildmaster Corvane Thackeray rose from his chair like a man greeting guests he'd expected all along.

"Oscar." His voice carried the way it always had—not loud, but certain, the voice that had read him stories, taught him knots, told him he was worth more than his birth. "You brought me my guild back."

"They're not yours."

"They were never anyone else's." Corvane descended the dais steps slowly, hands open, an old man in a fine coat with no weapon drawn. Somehow that was worse. "Look at them, boy. Smugglers and refugees and failed ward-keepers. I offered you inheritance and you chose this rabble. So be it. You've seen the ledger. You know what I traded for the dark. Ask them—" he gestured at the crowd, at the hunters above, "—ask any of them what the light ever did for the Salt Guild. We starved in its glare. We died in its wars. I gave us a kingdom of night and no witnesses."

"You gave away the sky," Oscar said. "You broke the wards. People are dying in villages you've never bothered to visit."

"People were dying before." For one moment the certainty cracked and something older showed through—tiredness, real and vast. "I have watched this guild rot for forty years, Oscar. The covenant dead, the dragons gone, the roads shrinking every season. I did not break the world. I only stopped pretending it wasn't already broken. There is a mercy in darkness. No one can fail you in the dark. No one can leave you."

"That's not mercy," Wren said.

And Corvane looked at her—really looked, for the first time—and Oscar watched the color leave the old man's face. Whatever he'd believed about the night he'd helped make, he had never expected *her*. Never expected a dragon in a mortal body standing in his hall with four covenant-stones singing at her belt.

"You," Corvane whispered. "The founders' accounts spoke of—"

"Yes," Wren said. "Me."

What happened next happened fast. Later, Oscar could never assemble it into order; it came back to him as fragments, the way disasters do. Corvane's hand closing—not in surrender but in signal. The hunters on the gallery loosing into the ceiling because Sera's ward-keepers had thrown up shields of light and the hunters' nerve broke. Tam shouting. Maren pulling hostages toward the side doors. And Corvane himself turning, not to fight, but to walk—with terrible unhurried purpose—straight to the great hearth.

To the flame that had burned in that hearth for as long as the guild had existed. The flame every smuggler swore his first oath before. The flame Oscar had assumed, his whole life, was just fire.

The fifth stone sat in the heart of it. Had always sat there. The guild's oldest secret, hidden in its most ordinary thing.

"No!" Sera lunged forward, wards flaring, but hunters dropped from the gallery between her and the hearth, buying their master seconds with their bodies. Corvane reached into the fire—he didn't flinch, didn't scream, though the flesh of his forearm blackened—and closed his fist around the stone.

"I buried the dragons," he said, turning to face the hall, holding the stone up so every soul could see it. "Now I bury the last of their fire. Let the dark be honest. Let it be final."

He drove the stone down against the hearthstone's edge.

The flame went out.

Not dwindled. Not guttered. *Out*—all at once, the way stars had gone out, the way the wards had shattered, a single clean erasure. Cold flooded the hall like water. Every torch bent sideways as if a door had opened onto winter. And the four stones at Oscar's belt went silent—all four, mid-song, cut off like throats slit.

In the ringing dark, Oscar heard Wren cry out.

She was on her knees. Not wounded—nothing had touched her—but dimming. He could see it happening, the way you can see a candle drowning in its own wax: the faint gold that had lived under her skin since the day bandits learned what she was, draining out of her face, her hands, her eyes. Without the fifth stone the covenant had no vessel. Without the vessel, her fire had nowhere to live except the body it was burning through.

"Wren—" He was beside her, hands on her shoulders, and she was cold, cold as the hearth, cold as the night without stars. "Stay with me. Stay—"

"It's all right." She smiled up at him, and her lips were gray. "This is how it was always going to end, little smuggler. Fire spends itself. That's what fire *is*."

"Not like this. Not for him—"

"For no one." Her hand found his chest, pressed flat, and through her palm he felt the last of her heat like a coin held too long in a dying hand. "For everyone. There's a difference. Help me stand."

* * *

He got her to the hearth. Half the hall was fighting and half had simply stopped, smugglers and hunters alike frozen by the sight of a woman walking to a dead fire on legs that barely held her. Corvane stood where he'd stood, the fifth stone black in his scorched fist, watching with an expression Oscar couldn't read—horror, maybe, or hope, or the awful recognition of a man seeing the true cost of the mercy he'd preached.

"The stone," Wren said to him. Her voice was smoke now, thin and rising. "Give it back to the fire."

"Why?" Corvane's voice cracked on it. "So the sky returns? So the wars return? So we go back to starving honestly?" He was crying, Oscar realized. The guildmaster of the Salt Guild, the man who had sold the heavens, was weeping like a child. "I did it so it would *end*, dragon. All of it. The hunger, the hiding, the failing. Darkness is just rest. Darkness is just—"

"Sleep," Wren agreed gently. "And death is just sleep that lasts. I know, Corvane. I have flown above your mountains for longer than your guild has existed, and I have wanted to rest since before your grandfather was born. Rest is not evil. But you don't get to choose it for the whole world." She held out her hand. "You chose power once, in the dark, where no one could see. Choose differently where we can all watch."

A long moment. The cold deepened. Somewhere behind them, the fighting had stopped entirely.

Corvane set the stone in her palm.

Maybe it was her words. Maybe it was the sight of his own burned arm. Maybe it was just that a man can hold a stolen heaven in his fist for only so long before it becomes too heavy to carry. Oscar would wonder for the rest of his life. The old man's fingers opened, and the fifth stone—black as the space between stars—lay gleaming in a dragon's hand.

Wren turned to the dead hearth.

"What are you doing?" Oscar grabbed her arm. "Wren. What are you doing?"

"What I came down the mountain to do." She looked at him then, and her eyes were almost all gold, the mortal mask burning away from the inside, and she was beautiful and terrible and going out. "The stones hold the covenant. But covenants need kindling. They need a living flame to wake them. Mine is the last there is." She touched his cheek with fingers that trembled. "Put me in the fire, Oscar. And whatever happens—don't let them grieve too long. Grief is just love with nowhere to go. Find it somewhere to go."

"No." His voice broke on it. "No, there's another way, there has to be—"

"There isn't. There never was. That's what the stones have been trying to tell us since the chapel." She kissed his forehead, and her lips were winter. "You gave a dying village salt in the dark, the first night I met you. You've been carrying fire since before you knew what it was. Carry me the last few steps."

And because he loved her, and because she asked, and because some part of him had known since the bridge, since the volcano, since the moment she first laughed at his fear on the high pass—because of all of it, Oscar Thackeray lifted the last dragon of Thornfell into his arms, lighter than she should have been, light as ash, and carried her to the hearth.

He laid her down among the coals like laying a child in a cradle.

The stones sang—one note, five voices, the sound the world had been missing. Wren breathed in. And then she let herself burn.

It was not like any fire Oscar had ever seen. It rose from her silver and gold, from her hair and her open hands, and it took the shape of wings for one impossible instant—wings spanning the whole hall, casting every watcher in shadow—before it collapsed inward, down, into the fifth stone, into the hearth, into a single flame no bigger than a candle.

The flame caught.

The hearth ignited—not red, not orange, but white-gold, the color of the vanished stars—and heat rolled across the hall like a tide, and the four stones at Oscar's belt blazed alive against his hip, singing full-voiced at last. On the hearthstone, where a dragon had lain, there was nothing left but a drift of pale ash shaped, for a moment, like folded wings.

Oscar knelt in it. He didn't remember falling.

The flame stood in the hearth, small and steady and impossibly bright, and it was guttering—it was *dying*, even now, even after everything, a candle-flame in a cathedral of dark, and every eye in the hall fixed on it the way drowning men fix on light. Everything wagered. Everything spent. One guttering light between the world and the long night.

"Oscar." Sera's hand on his shoulder, her voice raw. "The stones. The covenant needs a voice. It always needed a voice—that's what the founders forgot, that's why it broke. Someone has to speak it. Someone has to *mean* it."

He looked at the flame. It leaned toward him like a listener.

Behind him, two hundred people waited in silence—smugglers, ward-keepers, refugees, even the hunters who had served the man who broke the sky. Even Corvane, kneeling in his own ashes of certainty, watching.

Oscar rose. He took the four stones from his belt and held them out over the hearth, over the fifth, and the five lights reached for each other like fingers interlacing, and the song swelled until the windows hummed.

His hands shook. His voice didn't.

"In salt and in starlight," Oscar Thackeray began, words rising in him from somewhere older than the guild, older than the betrayal, "in fire and in faithfulness—we keep the sky, and the sky keeps us—"

The first stone cracked.

Light poured out of it—not outward but *upward*, through the roof, through the stone, through the mountain itself, and high above Thornfell, in the black vault where the stars had died, something answered.
