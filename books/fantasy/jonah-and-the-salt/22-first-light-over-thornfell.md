## 22. First Light Over Thornfell

The map took me a full season to draw, and I drew it wrong on purpose.

Not the mountains. The mountains were the one thing in this world that had never lied to me. Every ridge and ravine of Thornfell went down true, inked from memory and verified by my own two boots. But the old smugglers' roads—the ones we'd burned at the Turncoat's Road, the tunnels collapsed and the hidden fords drowned—I left them off entirely. Where a secret road had run, I drew nothing. Blank paper. A small honest absence.

Sera caught it the first time she looked over my shoulder, because of course she did. She'd spent thirty years reading wards the way other people read weather, and she read maps the same way.

"You've left gaps," she said.

"I know."

"Deliberate gaps."

"Some roads should stay dead," I said. "Even on paper. Especially on paper."

She considered that for a long moment, her scarred hands flat on the table, morning light coming through the window of what used to be the guild's counting room and was now—by unanimous and slightly bewildered agreement—a cartographer's office. Then she nodded once, the way she nodded when a ward held under pressure, and said, "Good. Leave them. Let the mountain keep its own counsel."

That was Sera all over. She'd spent months since the Reforging doing penance in the most practical way imaginable: teaching. Ward-keeping had nearly died with the old covenant, and now there were eleven students in Thornfell learning to feel the borders of things, and every one of them was terrified of her, and every one of them adored her. She pretended not to notice either.

I rolled the map closed and tied it with plain cord. No guild seal. No salt-mark. Just a name written across the top in my own hand: *Thornfell Trade Roads, First Edition.*

It felt strange to write *First* like I believed there'd be a second. Stranger still that I did.

* * *

The village of Hollowmere got its salt before midwinter, and this time nobody paid for it in secrets.

I made the delivery myself, because some habits deserve to survive. The road down from the pass was the same road I'd run as a boy with contraband sewn into my coat lining, except now my pack carried ledger-books and seed contracts and a letter from the lowland millers' cooperative, and the only thing I was smuggling was hope, which turned out to weigh considerably more.

Old Marta met me at the well the way she always had, though these days she walked without looking over her shoulder.

"They told us you were dead," she said. "After the hall fell. They told us the whole Guild died that night."

"The Guild did," I said. "I didn't."

She pressed a loaf into my hands, warm through the cloth, and wouldn't hear a word about payment. "You'll take bread or you'll take nothing, Oscar Thackeray, and if you argue I'll tell everyone in this village you cried at the well."

"I did cry at the well," I said. "Everyone already knows."

Because they did. That was the thing about truth—it got everywhere, like water finding cracks. The night Corvane walked into the dark rather than face judgment, and the night the five stones fused above the guild hall and fire came down the peaks like a river deciding its course, and the stars came back one by one like lamps being lit in windows up a long dark street—that whole story had traveled faster than any smuggler ever had. People knew what the Salt Guild had been. People knew what it had done: the villages abandoned, the wards sold, the covenant broken by men who thought darkness was a kindness. And people knew that a handful of liars and exiles had stood in the ashes and said the covenant out loud, plainly, where anyone could hear it.

You can't smuggle a story like that. It travels on its own.

Hollowmere had rebuilt its eastern wall. There were children in the lane playing a game where one of them wore a red scarf and roared, and the others scattered screaming with delight, and I understood after a moment that the child in the scarf was being Wren. I stood there longer than I meant to, watching a girl of maybe six breathe imaginary fire at her friends and get tackled into the snow for it.

Marta followed my gaze. "They play dragon now," she said. "Used to be bandits and wardens. Now it's dragons." She shrugged, an old woman's shrug that contained multitudes. "Better monsters, I suppose."

"The best monster I know is currently eating her weight in honey cakes somewhere south of here," I said.

"Give her our regards," Marta said. "And tell her—" She stopped, and something moved behind her eyes, some memory of that first starless winter when the cold came down off the peaks and nothing burned right. "Tell her thank you. From all of us. Even the ones who never learned her name."

"She knows," I said. "She hears everything. It's insufferable."

* * *

Wren was, in fact, eating her weight in honey cakes, though not south of anywhere. She was sitting on the roof of the old counting house when I got back, legs dangling over the eaves, a paper sack from the market in Greyhollow open beside her, watching the sun come up over the eastern ridge with an expression I had learned to read as contentment but which strangers sometimes mistook for hunger.

With Wren those were often the same expression.

"You're back late," she said without turning around. Dragons don't need to turn around. "The pass road was iced. You took the long way down."

"The long way down has better views."

"The long way down has a bridge with a missing rail and you know I hate bridges."

"Hollow Bridge is gone, Wren. We burned it ourselves, remember? What's left is a ford and a plank."

"A plank," she repeated gravely, "is a bridge that has given up."

I climbed up and sat beside her, which took longer than it used to; she offered a hand anyway, and I took it anyway, and neither of us commented on either courtesy because that was how we'd built it—courtesy upon courtesy, no commentary required.

From the roof we could see half of Thornfell. The peaks wore their snow like fresh linen. Smoke rose from chimneys in the valley in straight, unhurried columns, and beyond them, faint as breath on glass, the high passes showed the new beacons—ward-fire, not warning-fire, tended by Sera's students in shifts, burning steady gold instead of desperate red. And above everything, scattered across the paling sky like coins someone had dropped and couldn't be bothered to pick up, the stars were going out one by one in the growing light, each one fading honestly, on schedule, the way stars are supposed to.

For eight months now they had done that. Come up at dusk, gone down at dawn. No drama. No vanishing acts. Just the oldest promise in the world kept, nightly, without comment.

"You're staring at them again," Wren said.

"One of us should appreciate them properly. You had four hundred years and got bored."

"I wasn't bored. I was *exiled*. It's different. Boredom has snacks." She shook the sack at me. Honey cakes. Of course.

"How's the fire?" I asked. I tried to ask it lightly. I never quite managed.

She held up her hand and let a flame stand on her palm—not the pillar of white-gold that had come out of her in the deep places, not the fire that had flooded the peaks and relit the sky, but a small domestic flame, candle-sized, steady as a hearth. She let it burn for ten heartbeats, twenty, then closed her fist and it was gone.

"Longer than last month," she said. "Shorter than I'd like. Getting there." She flexed her fingers. "Your Sera thinks it's the altitude. Says fire remembers where it was happiest."

"Is that true?"

"Sera is right about almost everything and insufferable about all of it, so probably." She leaned her shoulder against mine. Her shoulder weighed exactly as much as a human shoulder ought to, which still occasionally startled me, all these months later. "Ask me again at midsummer. If the answer's good, I'll show you something."

"What kind of something?"

"The kind that requires altitude," she said, "and a certain amount of trust, and absolutely no bridges."

* * *

Sera found us at the mapping table an hour later, which meant she'd been looking for us, which meant something was happening, because Sera did not look for people recreationally.

"Pilgrims on the north road," she said. "Forty-odd. Led by one of the star-watchers—you remember, the hermit order from beyond the map. The one who tested me." Her mouth did something complicated. "He wants to talk about routes."

"Routes?"

"They want to walk the covenant-road," she said. "All of it. Hollowmere to the drowned chapel to the volcano's foot to the place where the stones sang. Every year, they say. A walking of the promises." She set a folded letter on the table between us, sealed with a star worked in grey wax. "They need safe passage marked. Water sources. Shelter. Which passes hold snow in which months."

I looked at the letter and understood, slowly, what I was holding. Not a request for smuggling. Not a contract, not a favor traded in the dark. Someone wanted a map of Thornfell drawn for the express purpose of people walking it openly, in daylight, carrying nothing but themselves.

"They could just follow the trade roads," I said.

"They could," Sera agreed. "But they asked you. Specifically. By name." She tapped the letter. "The invisible man has become a landmark, Thackeray. Congratulations. My condolences."

After she left, Wren read the letter upside down, which she could do, which remained unfair.

"Say yes," she said.

"I'm going to say yes."

"But you're doing the thing where you stare at blank paper like it owes you money first."

"It doesn't owe me money," I said. "It owes me honesty. Those aren't the same debt." I pulled a fresh sheet toward me and weighted its corners. "Every map I ever drew before, I drew so that some things would stay hidden. This one's the opposite. This one exists so nothing has to hide."

Wren watched me rule the first line. "You know what the funny part is?" she said. "Four hundred years, and I never once saw a smuggler become a mapmaker. Bandit kings, sure. Saints, twice. But a man whose whole art was secrecy choosing to draw the world legible—" She shook her head. "The founders would have hated you."

"That," I said, inking the line true, "is the nicest thing anyone's said to me all year."

* * *

We gathered at dawn because that was when we gathered now. Nobody had decreed it. It had simply happened, the way traditions do—one morning someone brought bread to the counting-house roof, and then someone else brought tea, and then it was simply what mornings were.

Sera arrived first, as always, with her students' ward-reports and her tea gone cold because she'd forgotten it, as always. Behind her came Tam Voss, formerly the guild's best lock-man, currently the best door-maker in three valleys, complaining cheerfully about a hinge. Then the twins from the Market of Small Mercies, who had taken over the old toll-houses and turned them into honest way-stations, and who bickered the entire climb up the stairs. Then a dozen others, drifting in on the smell of bread, refugees and ward-keepers and reformed hunters and one extremely retired guildmaster's clerk who still flinched at loud noises but who made, everyone agreed, the best porridge in Thornfell.

And last, as always, because roofs and ladders remained a negotiation, Wren.

She settled onto the parapet with her honey cakes and her human hands and her fire banked low and patient inside her, and she looked out at the sunrise with the rest of us, and for a while nobody said anything, because that was also part of the tradition.

The sun cleared the eastern ridge and laid itself along the snowfields, and the whole range went briefly the color of the old covenant-fire, gold running peak to peak, and below us the valley woke—smoke rising, bells ringing in Hollowmere, the pilgrims' banners visible far off on the north road, small and bright and moving toward us.

"Route report," Sera said, because ritual needed words too. "North pass: clear. Beacon-line: holding. Ward-students: terrified, improving."

"Trade report," Tam said. "Salt's cheap, bread's plenty, and somebody in Greyhollow is counterfeiting my hinges, which I'm choosing to take as a compliment."

"Mercies report," said the twins, in unison, glaring at each other about it.

I unrolled the new map across our knees, all of us, the found family of the Salt Guild-that-wasn't, and read out the roads I'd drawn overnight: the covenant-road in gold, the trade roads in brown, the pilgrimage shelters marked with small careful stars. Nothing hidden. Nothing smuggled. Every line true.

"Wren," I said, "you're listed as a waypoint."

She looked up, delighted and suspicious. "I'm a *what*?"

"Shelter seven. 'A roof, honey cakes, and a dragon.' Pilgrims will love you."

"I am not a *landmark*, Oscar Thackeray—"

"You're the most famous landmark in the mountains. There are children in Hollowmere wearing your scarf."

"That was ONE winter—"

Sera cleared her throat, and we shut up, because some things survived even the end of the world.

She raised her cold tea toward the sunrise, and we raised whatever we had—bread, tea, a honey cake, my ink-stained hands—and she said the words we said every morning now, simple ones, the kind the old guild would have sneered at:

"The roads are open. The fire is lit. The stars came back."

"The stars came back," we answered.

And far above the waking peaks, the last few stars of the night gave way, gently and on schedule, to a sky the color of beginning.

Wren leaned close, her voice warm against my ear, and pointed east, past the edge of my map, past the edge of every map, where the mountains ran on into country no one had ever bothered to make honest.

"Midsummer," she said. "Altitude. Trust. No bridges. Bring the blank paper, mapmaker—we're going to need a bigger book."
