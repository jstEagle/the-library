## 1. The Weight of Unwritten Names

The mule was called Biscuit, and Juniper had named him that for two reasons: first, because he was the color of a badly baked one, and second, because no one suspects a woman who talks to her mule of being anything more than a woman who talks to her mule.

"Biscuit," she said, "if you eat that rope again, I will let you."

Biscuit ate the rope.

Juniper sighed and knelt in the slush to untangle him from the lead-line, which gave her an excellent view of the caravan strung out behind her along the switchback: eleven mules, four salt-sledges, and seven drovers wrapped in enough wool to survive a small ice age. The Thornfell passes in late autumn were not kind to caravans. They were, however, kind to *this* caravan, because this caravan had her, and she knew the passes the way other people knew their own hands.

There were roads through the Thornfells that appeared on no map. There were roads that appeared on maps and killed people who trusted them. And there were roads—sixteen of them, to be exact—that existed only in Juniper Eriksen's memory, because she had spent fifteen years making sure they existed nowhere else.

She stood, brushed the snow off her knees, and became, once again, entirely unremarkable.

This was a skill. People assumed invisibility was a matter of hooded cloaks and shadowed corners, but true forgettability was craftsmanship. It was the way she walked—not a soldier's stride, not a sneak's tiptoe, but the tired, slightly bow-legged amble of a woman who had been walking since before dawn and would walk until after dark, like every other guide on every other mountain. It was the scarf she wore pulled up over her chin, which made her jaw look wider than it was. It was the name she used: *Mira*, plain as porridge, belonging to no one in particular because it belonged to half the women in three valleys. It was the way she laughed at drovers' jokes—a beat too late, a note too flat—and the way she never once, in fifteen years, told anyone what she thought of anything that mattered.

"You're quiet today, Mira," said Old Hesk, the caravan master, falling into step beside her. He was seventy if he was a day, with a beard like frost-killed moss and the conversational appetite of a man who had been married forty years to a deaf wife and missed talking terribly.

"I'm always quiet, Hesk."

"Aye, but you're *quietly* quiet today." He squinted at the sky, then at her. "Storm coming?"

"Tomorrow, midday, from the west. We'll want to be below the tree line by then."

Hesk nodded, satisfied, and asked nothing else, because that was the arrangement between them. She told him weather and road; he paid her double what the passes deserved and never asked where she'd learned them. In fifteen years he had never once asked. Sometimes Juniper suspected Hesk knew exactly what she was and simply didn't care, and sometimes she suspected he genuinely couldn't pick her face out of a crowd of grandmothers, and she honestly didn't know which possibility comforted her more.

They came down out of the high col as the light went long and copper, and below them the valley opened up—the river running silver through it, the ford glinting, and beyond, the smoke of the waystation where they'd sleep tonight. Juniper felt the familiar loosening in her chest that meant the hard part of the crossing was done, and the familiar tightening underneath it that never went away.

Because the pass behind them was called the Shepherd's Throat, and she had not walked it in fifteen years, and she had sworn she never would.

* * *

Fifteen years ago, on a morning much colder than this one, a girl of nineteen had stood in the great hall under Thornfell Keep with snow melting in her hair, and an old woman had put a sword in her hands and said, *Say it.*

The girl had said it.

That was how oaths worked among the swordspeakers. Not signed, not witnessed—*spoken*. A spoken oath lived in the tongue that made it and the stone that heard it, and the mountains of Thornfell had heard ten thousand of them over four hundred years, and remembered every one. That was the order's whole purpose, though the girl hadn't understood it yet. She'd thought she was joining a company of heroes. Sword-maidens of the passes, keepers of the high roads, the stuff of the ballads the drovers sang when the wine went round.

Master Ilsa Vane—who had held the girl's shoulder while she spoke, whose grip had been gentle and whose eyes had been old and sorry—had told her the truth the very next day, standing on the wall above the training yard.

"The ballads are wrong," Ilsa said. "We are not the sword that guards the mountain. We are the lock."

"What does that mean?"

"It means that beneath these peaks there sleeps something the kingdoms agreed, long ago, must never wake. And the agreement is written in oaths, and oaths need speakers, and speakers need keeping." She had looked out at the white peaks with an expression the girl would spend years trying to name and finally, years after that, would recognize as grief. "We do not guard the mountain, child. We guard the *door*. And we are hated for it, eventually, by everyone on both sides of it. Remember that."

The girl had not remembered it. Or rather, she had remembered it the way you remember a warning about weather next year—it slid off her, because she was nineteen and strong and newly sworn, and the sword fit her hand like it had grown there.

Then came the purge.

Juniper did not let herself remember the purge in any detail. This was not cowardice—or it was, but it was disciplined cowardice, the kind you practiced daily until it looked like peace. What she permitted herself was the shape of it only: the year the Crown's inquisitors came into the passes wearing no colors, carrying warrants sealed with wax the color of dried blood, and began asking questions in villages, and the questions were always the same question, and the question was *names*.

An oath-bound swordspeaker could not lie. That was the cruelty built into the gift: the same tongue that spoke binding words could not speak false ones. So when the inquisitors came asking, the swordspeakers had three choices. Fight—which they did, briefly, gloriously, and in vain. Flee—which some did, and were hunted across three kingdoms by men who could read a spoken name out of a room like reading smoke.

Or the third choice, the one Ilsa Vane had seen coming twenty years early, God rest her paranoid soul: *unwrite yourself*.

There was a rite for it. There was a rite for everything, back then. It wasn't forbidden, exactly—it was considered a tragedy, a last resort, the spiritual equivalent of cutting off your own sword hand. But it could be done. A swordspeaker could stand before a wardstone and formally release her oaths, surrender her name to the stone, and walk away as no one. Not hidden. *Unwritten.* The order's records would show a blank where she had been. The mountains themselves would forget her voice.

Ilsa had performed the rite for thirty-one of her swordspeakers in the last winter of the order, sending them out into the world as nobody, and Juniper Eriksen had been the last of them—the youngest, the last one through the door before the inquisitors reached the keep.

She had never learned whether Ilsa survived. She had made herself never learn. Learning things was how you got followed home.

* * *

"Mira! Mira, the ford's iced at the edges!"

Juniper blinked the past away and found the present waiting for her, cold and ordinary: the waystation, the drovers unloading sledges, Hesk arguing cheerfully with the stableman about the price of oats as though the price of oats were a matter of philosophy. She did what she always did at a waystation—found the corner seat, ordered stew, ate it without tasting it, and listened.

Listening was the other half of the craft. You could not be forgotten if you never stopped hearing.

Mostly she heard nothing worth hearing. Oat prices. A wedding in the lowlands. Someone's opinion of someone else's pig. The comfortable static of small lives, which she had come to think of as the sound of safety, the way other people thought of rain on a roof.

Then, near the fire, a tinker she half-recognized said to his neighbor, low, the way you say things you don't quite want said:

"—riders on the north road yesterday. Four of them. Grey cloaks, no colors, no badges. Asked at Millbrook after a courier." He turned his cup slowly. "Asked after a courier *and* after anyone odd passing through. Woman alone, they said. Walks like a soldier."

The neighbor grunted. "Half the widows up here walk like soldiers."

"Aye." The tinker did not laugh. "But they asked it *twice*. Same question, two different men, like they were checking the answers against each other."

Juniper finished her stew. She thanked the serving girl by name—*Lotta*, because knowing servants' names made you memorable, and being memorable to one servant was safe, was human, was camouflage—and she went out to check on Biscuit, and she stood in the cold dark of the stable with her hand flat on his warm neck, and she did the arithmetic she had done ten thousand times before.

Grey cloaks, no colors. That was inquisitor work. Fifteen years, and the Crown still sent men into her mountains asking after names.

Except—they weren't asking after *her*. Not yet. They were asking after a courier. Some poor soul carrying something through the passes fast enough that riders in grey had come out in the first snows to catch him. Whatever he carried, it mattered. It mattered enough that the hunt had come within a day's walk of her ford, her valley, her carefully unwritten life.

And she was Mira, oat-priced, weather-worn, forgettable Mira, and couriers and their troubles were nothing to her, and tomorrow she would take Hesk's caravan down to the lowlands and collect her pay and vanish into another season, and none of it would ever touch her.

That was the plan. It was a good plan. It had worked for fifteen years.

So why, she asked Biscuit silently, why did the back of her neck feel the way it did before a rockslide? Why had the word *courier* landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well, still falling, waiting for the splash?

Biscuit lipped her pocket hopefully. She had nothing for him. She stayed anyway, a long time, listening to the stable breathe.

* * *

They broke camp before dawn, because the storm she'd promised was coming and Hesk trusted her forecast the way other men trusted scripture. The caravan moved down the last stretch of the high road in blue half-light, breath steaming, sledge-runners hissing on frost, and Juniper led them along a track that did not exist on any map in any archive in the capital—a track she had helped cut herself, at seventeen, with a spade and an oath and thirty other laughing trainees, back when she'd believed the work would last forever.

The track came down through pines to the river, and the river ran shallow and quick over grey stones toward the ford, and the ford was exactly where she had left it, because rivers were honest that way.

She saw the crows first.

A dozen of them, hunched in the alders on the far bank, in that particular silent, patient way crows have that is never about nothing. Her hand found Biscuit's halter and stopped him without her quite deciding to. Behind her, the caravan jingled to a halt, and Hesk's voice floated forward, "Trouble, Mira?"—and she raised a hand: wait.

She left the caravan and walked ahead alone, boots crunching, one hand resting easy on the knife at her belt—not drawn, just acquainted with. The crows watched her come with round black eyes.

The man lay among the stones at the edge of the water, on his side, one arm flung forward as if he'd been reaching for the far bank even at the end. Dark hair crusted with blood. A courier's light leathers, slashed. No horse—she could see the story of it in the churned mud: he'd come down the north road hard, been run down at the water, made it partway across on foot, and not made it the rest of the way.

Riders in grey, no colors. Asking twice.

Juniper knelt beside him, already knowing, already grieving, and reached to turn him gently—

His hand shot up and closed around her wrist.

She did not scream, though it cost her. His grip was astonishing, a drowning man's grip, a grip with a whole mission inside it. His eyes found hers, fever-bright, and his lips moved, and she leaned close because the wind took most of what he had.

"—map," he rasped. Blood at his teeth. "Wax. Under—under my coat. Take it. Take it *away* from here—" A shudder went through him like a rope snapping taut. "It's the Sigil. Don't let them—"

His eyes went past her shoulder, up the north road, and widened.

And Juniper—still kneeling in the frost, holding the wrist of a dying stranger, with a word she had not heard spoken aloud in fifteen years ringing in her skull like a struck bell—turned and looked too.

Far up the valley, where the north road bent around the shoulder of the mountain, four riders had come into view. Grey cloaks. No colors.

They had not seen her yet.

They would.
