## 4. Riders in Grey

The trouble with being nobody, Juniper had learned over fifteen years of practice, was that everybody wanted to talk to you.

Nobodies were safe. Nobodies were interesting in the way that a fence post was interesting—something to lean against while the real business of the world went on elsewhere. So she had made herself a fence post: Juniper Eriksen filed down to "Jun," a salt-caravan guard with a bad knee and no opinions, who laughed at the right moments and asked about your mother's cough and never, ever told you anything true about herself.

It was working beautifully, right up until the riders came.

She saw them first from the porch of the Waystone Inn, where she sat shelling peas for Marta because sitting idle invited questions and shelling peas answered none of them. Six horses coming up the valley road at an unhurried walk, which was worse than a gallop. Men in a hurry were weather; men taking their time were a decision.

They wore grey. Not the grey of any house or company she knew—the grey of undyed wool, deliberate as a blank page. No colors meant no allegiance, which in the Crown's service meant only one kind of man.

Inquisitors.

The pea slipped through her fingers and rolled off the porch into the dust. She watched it go with the peculiar calm of a woman watching her own funeral procession arrive early.

"Jun?" Marta called from inside. "You've gone still as a heron."

"Just counting the birds," Juniper said, and picked up another pod.

* * *

There were three ways this could go, and she catalogued them while the riders dismounted at the trough, in the old way her teachers had drilled into her until threat-assessment lived in her spine like a second heartbeat.

One: they were passing through. Unlikely. Inquisitors did not pass through valley towns; valley towns had nothing in them but sheep, salt, and secrets too small to bother with.

Two: they were hunting someone else. Possible. The world produced a steady supply of fugitives, like a river producing silt.

Three.

She didn't finish the thought. Finishing thoughts gave them weight, and weight gave them truth, and truth was a thing she had spent a decade and a half refusing to carry.

Instead she carried the peas inside.

Marta Halloway was sixty-two years old, built like a rain barrel, and ran the Waystone with the iron gentleness of a woman who had buried a husband, raised two sons, and out-negotiated every wool merchant between here and the coast. She had fed Juniper for four winters now without once asking where she went each spring or why her pack was always packed. That was the arrangement, unspoken and sacred: Marta provided soup and a room, and Juniper provided silence, and both of them pretended this was ordinary friendship rather than the closest thing either of them had to family.

"Six grey riders," Marta said, not looking up from her ledger. Her pen kept moving, steady as a metronome. "That's what you were counting instead of birds."

"They're just men on horses."

"Mm." The pen scratched. "And I'm just a woman with a ledger. We're both very good at our work, aren't we."

Juniper set the bowl down. "Marta—"

"I don't want to know." Marta looked up then, and her eyes were the flat brown of river stones, and behind them something moved—worry, maybe, or the memory of worry from some earlier year Juniper knew nothing about. "I have never wanted to know, and I am not going to start wanting to know today. But I'll tell you what I do know, since it costs me nothing. Grey riders ask their questions in the common room, and the common room is full tonight, and Doss Hedley has already had four ales, and Doss Hedley would sell his own grandmother's teeth if he thought there was a copper in it."

"So we say nothing."

"We say nothing," Marta agreed. "You especially say nothing. You'll serve tables."

"I don't serve tables."

"You do tonight. Nobody looks twice at a serving girl, and everybody looks twice at a guard who won't meet their eye." Marta snapped the ledger shut. "Apron's on the hook. And Jun—" she caught her sleeve, briefly, her grip surprisingly strong, "—whatever it is, whatever it's been all these years, I hope it was worth carrying."

Juniper didn't answer. There wasn't an answer that wasn't a lie, and lying to Marta felt like kicking a dog that had brought you a stick.

* * *

The inquisitors came into the common room at dusk, shaking rain off their cloaks like men who owned the weather that fell on them.

Their leader was tall and narrow, with the kind of face that had been handsome once and had then been sanded down by work into something more useful. He had grey eyes to match his cloak and a voice pitched precisely for quiet rooms—he never raised it, and so everyone leaned in to hear him, which was, Juniper recognized with professional appreciation, the entire trick.

He introduced himself as Inquisitor Hale. He thanked the innkeeper for her hospitality. He ordered stew for his men and paid in fresh-minted crown silver, which glittered on the table like bait.

Then, over the spoon-scrape of six hungry men, he said, pleasantly: "We're looking for a courier. Came through the ford two days past. Young fellow, dark hair, rode a bay mare with a white sock. And we're also looking for a woman."

The room did its best to be uninterested, which is to say it became intensely interested while pretending to study its stew.

"A woman," Hale continued, "who walks like a soldier."

Juniper carried a tray of ale across the room. Her knee ached. She let it ache visibly, added a slight hitch to her stride, hunched one shoulder, and became, for the length of the crossing, a woman with a bad hip and a tired back, the sort of woman who walked like weather coming on.

"Soldiers," Hale said to the room at large, "walk a particular way. Weight forward. Eyes doing arithmetic on the exits. You'd know it if you saw it." He smiled at Doss Hedley, who was on his fifth ale and vibrating with the need to be important. "Has anyone seen such a woman?"

Doss opened his mouth.

Juniper set a tankard down in front of him hard enough to slosh.

"Careful, love," she said loudly, in the flat valley accent she wore like a borrowed coat. "That's your last one before food, else you'll be sick on the good lady's floor again like at the harvest dance."

Doss flushed a satisfying beet-red. "I never—"

"You did, and Marta scrubbed it, and she's still sore about it." She turned to Hale with the bright vacant smile of a woman whose entire inner life consisted of floors needing scrubbing. "Stew for the gentlemen? It's mutton. It's always mutton. Marta buys the tough bits cheap and boils them till they surrender."

Hale's grey eyes rested on her for exactly as long as it takes to read a page and decide it isn't worth reading. "Stew for six," he said. "Thank you."

She bobbed a curtsy and walked away, hip hitching, heart hammering somewhere around her kneecaps.

At the bar, Marta was polishing a tankard that had been clean for ten minutes. "Well?" she murmured.

"He looked at me for half a breath."

"How long does it take him to look at someone properly?"

"I don't know. That's the problem."

* * *

The interrogation, if you could call it that—it was more like fishing with a very fine net—went on for two hours. Hale never asked anything directly. He asked about roads, and traffic, and whether the ford had been high or low lately, and whether anyone remembered travelers, and each question seemed harmless alone, and together they wove a net with holes exactly the size of a courier and a woman who walked like a soldier.

And the town lied for her.

Badly.

Old Tammas swore he hadn't seen a soul at the ford, though everyone knew Tammas fished the ford every morning and greeted every traveler like a long-lost cousin. The miller's wife said no woman had taken the east road, though she herself had waved to Juniper on it Tuesday. Doss Hedley, miraculously, said nothing at all—whether from ale-addled confusion or some dim animal loyalty, Juniper couldn't tell, and decided not to examine the gift too closely lest it vanish.

They lied badly, clumsily, with darting eyes and ears going pink, and here was the thing that lodged under Juniper's ribs like a fishbone: they lied badly *for her*. These people she had spent four years being forgettable at—buying their eggs, fixing their fences, laughing at their jokes—they had risked the Crown's displeasure without being asked, without knowing why, on the strength of nothing but four years of soup and small kindnesses.

She had built her invisibility out of other people's trust, and now the trust was visible, and therefore she was.

Hale saw it too. She watched him see it. He didn't press; he thanked Marta for the excellent mutton, complimented the ale, and announced that his men would take rooms for the night. Two of them, he mentioned, would prefer to sleep in the stable loft, where the air was better for a certain complaint of the lungs. Which meant two of them would be watching the yard, the road, and the back gate.

Of course they would.

* * *

Upstairs, in her narrow room with its window overlooking the woodshed, Juniper took out the map.

She had hidden it in the one place no searcher would think to look, because no searcher would believe anyone could be fool enough: sewn into the stuffing of the pillow she slept on. She unpicked three stitches, drew it out, and spread it by candlelight, and the oath-script along its margins seemed to move in the flame-gutter, letters breathing like sleeping things.

*Sigil.*

The courier had died saying it. Riders with no colors had chased him to death for it. Now inquisitors sat downstairs eating Marta's mutton and asking after a woman who walked like a soldier, and the map lay under her hands warm as a living thing, and every hour she stayed, the net drew tighter—not around her, but around Marta, around Tammas and the miller's wife and poor sodden Doss, all of them tangled in a crime they hadn't committed except the crime of liking her.

She had erased herself from every ledger. But she had never learned how to erase herself from people, and it appeared that was the one place she couldn't be unwritten.

A floorboard creaked below—Hale's men making their rounds, unhurried, thorough. She blew out the candle and stood in the dark doing arithmetic.

East road: watched. Ford: watched, certainly, by now. South toward the lowlands: open ground, no cover, and she'd be a lone rider on a horizon by dawn. North—

North was Thornfell. North was the pass she hadn't walked since she was nine years old, since the year the fires came and the order burned and she had sworn, kneeling in ash, never to speak another word of the old tongue, never to touch a wardstone, never to be what she was born to be.

The map's margin-script crawled through her memory unbidden. She knew those marks. She had known them before she could write her own name. Somewhere north, past the pass, the lines converged on a point the cartographer had left deliberately blank—and her hands, resting on the map, trembled with something that was not fear.

Or not only fear.

Below, the floorboard creaked again, closer to the stairs.

Juniper folded the map along its old creases, sewed the pillow shut with fingers that did not shake—she made sure they did not shake, the way you make sure of anything—and began, silently, to pack.

**[Recovered fragment — field report, Inquisitor Hale to the Office of Seals, written by candlelight in the Waystone Inn's common room after the household retired]**

*The town lies, and lies poorly, which tells me two things. First, that the woman is here or was. Second, that she is loved, which is rarer and more useful than guilt. Guilt can be bought off. Love must be broken, and breaking it leaves shards, and shards cut the hand that grips them.*

*I questioned the innkeeper privately. She swore the woman was a caravan guard named Jun, plain as porridge, bad knee, no history. She swore it while polishing a tankard that needed no polishing, and her eyes went to the stairs on the word "no history"—not in fear. In warning. She was telling the woman, not me. They have signals. Four years of signals, at least.*

*A woman cannot live four years among these people and remain invisible. She has simply taught them not to see what they love. This is a discipline I have encountered before, in the last of the sword-orders' remnants, and it is a discipline that fails in only one direction: outward. Such women can hide from kings but not from kindness.*

*Tomorrow I will be patient. Patience is the whole art. The courier's map is in this valley—I feel it the way one feels weather in a healed bone—and maps do not leave valleys by night when every road is watched.*

*Unless, of course, they leave by the one road no one watches, because no one believes anyone would take it.*

*She has not asked about the northern pass. I note this. People ask about the roads they mean to use. The dangerous ones never ask about the road they were born to.*

*We ride at first light. If the loft-watch reports movement in the night, sooner.*

*—H*

* * *

In the woodshed's shadow, well past midnight, Juniper Eriksen stood with her pack on her shoulders and looked north, where Thornfell's peaks cut black teeth out of the starlight.

Behind her, the inn slept, and Marta slept, and the grey riders slept badly in their beds the way hunters do, one ear open. Ahead of her lay a pass full of wardstones that would know her name the moment she touched them, and beyond the pass, a blank space on a map that her whole body already ached toward like a compass needle.

"Fifteen years," she whispered to the mountains, in the old tongue, her first words of it since the ashes. "Don't make me sorry."

The mountain, being a mountain, said nothing.

But far above, at the head of the pass, a wardstone flickered—once, softly, like a lamp relit in a window of a house everyone believed empty—and waited for her to come home.
