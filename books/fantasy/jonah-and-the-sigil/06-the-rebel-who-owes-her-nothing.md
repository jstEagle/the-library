## 6. The Rebel Who Owes Her Nothing

Tam Vey had been crouched behind the same granite boulder for six hours, and his left knee had filed a formal complaint.

He shifted his weight, easing the ache, and watched the road below through a gap in the gorse. The old drover's path wound down from the high passes toward the lowland farms, narrow and brown with early autumn, the kind of road nobody used anymore because the Crown toll-roads had strangled it a generation back. Which was exactly why Brannoc had said she'd use it.

*She'll come down the forgotten way,* the den-keeper had told him, in that gravel voice of his, while mending a net that didn't need mending. *A woman who walks like a soldier but tries not to. She'll look at every face twice and trust none of them. And she'll be carrying something worth more than your life, so don't be stupid about it.*

"Define 'stupid,'" Tam had said.

Brannoc had looked at him over the net with the weary patience of a man who had been answering that question for thirty years. "Everything you're about to do."

That had been two days ago. Since then Tam had climbed half a mountain, eaten nothing but dried plums and one unlucky trout, and rehearsed his opening line approximately four hundred times.

*"Mistress Eriksen—"*

No. She wouldn't answer to that name. Nobody answered to names they'd buried.

*"You're in danger—"*

Everyone was in danger. That line would get him laughed at or stabbed, possibly both.

*"I come from Brannoc—"*

Better. Honest, at least. Though "honest" and "effective" were cousins who didn't speak at family gatherings.

Movement on the road.

Tam went still, breath shallow, the way he'd learned from his mother before she'd taught him anything else about surviving. A figure came around the bend below—alone, leading no pack animal, wearing a salt-carrier's drab cloak gone grey with road dust. She moved wrong for a carrier. Carriers trudged. This woman *flowed*, each step placed like she'd already asked the ground's permission, and every few paces her head turned—not nervously, but the way a hawk checks a hedgerow. Counting exits. Weighing shadows.

*There you are,* Tam thought, and his heart did something complicated that he chose to interpret as professional interest.

He let her pass below him. Ten yards. Twenty. Then he stood up out of the gorse, hands open and empty, and said the line he'd finally settled on:

"Don't turn around fast."

Which, in hindsight, was a terrible opening, because she turned around *immediately*—faster than anyone he'd ever seen move—and then there was a knife in her hand and her weight already sunk low, and Tam had the sudden vivid understanding of how a rabbit felt about hawks.

"Easy," he said, palms out. "Easy. I'm the friendly kind of ambush."

"There isn't one," she said. Her voice was flat as slate, but her eyes were doing rapid arithmetic: his hands, his hips, the treeline behind him, the slope above. "Who sent you?"

"Brannoc. The den-keeper at Hollowbridge. Grey beard, bad knee, cheats at cards." He paused. "Well. Doesn't cheat, actually. Just makes you think he does so you'll play again."

Something flickered across her face—recognition, quickly smothered. "Never heard of him."

"He said you'd say that." Tam grinned. "He also said you'd have the knife out by now, and I should tell you that if you meant to use it, you'd have used it already. He said you're careful, not cruel. His words. I'm just the messenger."

The knife didn't waver. Up close, now that he dared to really look, she was younger than her bearing suggested—or older than her face, depending on how you counted the years people carry invisibly. Road-worn, sharp-eyed, with a scar along her jaw she probably thought nobody noticed because she kept her chin angled to hide it. She noticed everything. That was the trouble with people who noticed everything; they assumed nobody else did.

"You've been following me," she said. Not a question.

"Since the ford. Well—" honesty seemed suddenly important, the way it does when someone is holding a knife, "—since Hollowbridge, technically. But I only caught up at the ford, where I watched you bury a man under a cairn and say words over him. Old words." He watched her flinch, a small thing, quickly mastered. "Then I followed you up the shepherd's cut instead of the toll-road, which, for the record, saved you a day and three shillings. You're welcome."

"Why?" Flat. Unblinking. The knife hadn't moved an inch, and Tam was beginning to develop a genuine respect for her forearm.

"Because Brannoc sent me, and because—" he gestured vaguely at the mountains ringing them, grey and gold and indifferent, "—because there are men in this valley asking questions, and I'd rather you hear the answers from a friend than learn them from a rope."

"I don't have friends."

"No," Tam agreed cheerfully. "But you have a knife pointed at the only person in ten miles who knows where the inquisitors aren't. So really, we're practically engaged."

That got a reaction—a blink, a twitch at the corner of her mouth that might have been the ancestor of a smile before it was executed for treason. She lowered the knife an inch. An inch. Generous.

"Inquisitors," she said. "How many?"

"Four riders came through Hollowbridge two days behind you. Grey cloaks, no colors, the tall one has a warrant box chained to his saddle and a voice like a tax notice. They're asking after a dead courier and a woman 'who walks like a soldier.'" He spread his hands. "Their description leaves something to be desired. Half the valley walks like a soldier; we all carry too much firewood. But the innkeeper at the Drowned Goose lied for you—badly, bless her—and lies that bad get people visited at night."

The knife lowered another inch. Her eyes, though, never stopped working. "And you? What do you want?"

"Ah." Tam rocked back on his heels. Here it was, then—the part he'd rehearsed four hundred times and was going to fumble anyway. "Straight to business. Fine. I represent a group of people who call themselves the Thornfell Compact. Farmers, miners, smugglers, one extremely opinionated goat. We believe the mountains belong to the people who bleed on them, not to a Crown that's never walked higher than the toll-gates. We've heard what you're carrying. Brannoc saw the map's seal. And we'd like to offer you shelter, food, and a very secure hole in the ground while you decide what to do next."

"And what do you want in return?"

"The map," Tam said. Then, quickly, because her knuckles whitened on the haft: "Eventually. Maybe. Look—I won't lie to you, lying to armed women is against my religion. The Compact wants the Sigil. They want to use it to break the Crown's grip on these valleys for good. That's the whole pitch. Shelter in exchange for consideration. You keep possession, you keep your secrets, and you get to decide later whether we're allies or just neighbors with excellent walls."

For a long moment she said nothing. Wind moved through the gorse, dry and whispering. Somewhere upslope a ptarmigan complained about the general state of the world, and Tam privately agreed with it.

"You want a weapon," she said at last, and there was something in her voice he couldn't name—something under the flatness, like water under ice.

"I want my people free," Tam said. "If the Sigil is the key, then yes. I want it."

"It's not a key." Her jaw tightened. "It's a wound. Whatever you think it opens, it opened once before, and what came out of it buried my—" She stopped. Closed her mouth. Recalibrated, visibly, the way a cart recalibrates after hitting a rock.

"*Your*?" Tam prompted gently.

"Nothing." She turned away, scanning the road ahead as if the conversation had concluded itself. "Your shelter. Where?"

Tam exhaled slowly. He hadn't realized he'd been holding his breath, which was embarrassing for a scout. "Dunmarrow. Two days' walk, if you let me take the shortcuts. It's an old mining hold—deep galleries, more doors than a drunk architect could count. Sera Ilves runs it. She'll want to talk to you herself."

"Sera Ilves." Juniper—*the woman*, he corrected himself; she hadn't given a name and he wasn't fool enough to ask—turned back, and studied him with that hawk arithmetic again. "The levy-breaker."

"You've heard of her."

"I've heard of everyone," she said, and started walking. "Keep six paces behind me. If you fall behind, I won't wait. If you get ahead of me, I'll assume it's a trick."

"That seems fair," Tam said, and fell into step six paces behind the most interesting disaster Brannoc had ever thrown at him.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow beneath a leaning rowan, off the road, cold-camped because she refused a fire. Tam shared his dried plums; she shared nothing, but watched him eat with an expression he eventually identified as reluctant amusement, the way you watch a dog try to open a door.

"So," he said, around a plum stone. "Six paces. All day. You counting my steps?"

"Yes."

"Any patterns?"

"You favor your right leg on descents. Old injury. Knee or ankle." She tore a strip of dried meat with her teeth. "You check the ridgeline every quarter hour without seeming to. Trained, not instinct. Someone taught you to scout."

"My mother," Tam admitted. "Best tracker in three valleys, worst cook in nine. She said a good scout is just a coward with discipline." He shrugged. "She also said never follow strangers into holes in the ground, so let's hope she's not watching."

"Is she alive?"

The question landed harder than he expected. He chewed for a moment. "No. Levy riots, eleven years back. Hollowbridge. The soldiers came for the tithe-grain and the town came for the soldiers, and it went badly in the way those things go." He found he was looking at the fire-pit he wasn't allowed to light. "Half the Compact is people like that. People the Crown stepped on and then billed for the boot."

She was quiet a long time. When she spoke, her voice had lost some of its slate. "I'm sorry."

"You knew them? The riots?"

"I knew the *kind* of thing that causes them." Something crossed her face then—grief, old and worn smooth like a river stone, handled so often its edges had gone. "Levies. Tithes. Men in grey with warrants. The Crown has been collecting from these mountains a long time."

"Longer than most know," Tam said. "Sera says the old charters go back to the Binding—some pact the first kings made with the mountain clans. Nobody remembers the terms anymore. Just that we pay."

"Nobody remembers," she repeated softly, and there was that thing under the ice again, moving. "Yes. That's rather the point."

Tam tilted his head. "You know something about the old charters?"

"I know things about a lot of things." She lay back against her pack, knife hand resting easy on her thigh, eyes on the stars through the rowan branches. "Ask your archivists. Ask your Sera Ilves. Everyone wants the Sigil; almost no one asks what it *is*. That should worry you."

"It worries me plenty," Tam said. "Mostly it worries me that you talk about it like a man talks about a grave he dug himself."

Silence. Long enough that he thought she'd gone to sleep. Then:

"You should sleep, scout. Dawn comes early, and I walk fast."

"You could tell me your name," he offered. "Just professionally. For the roster."

"No."

"Juniper, then." He said it lightly, watching sideways. "Brannoc let it slip. Well—he didn't slip, exactly, he charged me extra for it, but the effect is the same."

Her head turned. In the starlight her eyes were unreadable and very awake. "He had no right."

"He had a ledger," Tam said, "and you weren't on it, which apparently made you the most interesting entry in the book. Look—it's not a leash. A name's just a handle. What people do with the handle is up to them." He settled back against his own pack. "For what it's worth, Juniper, I don't think you're a weapon. I think you're a person carrying one, which is different, and heavier."

Another silence. But this one had a different texture—less wall, more door.

"Six paces," she said finally. "Tomorrow too."

"Wouldn't dream of five."

* * *

They reached the upper mine-tracks by noon the second day, where the mountain opened its old wounds: adits and shaft-mouths stitched shut with timber, spoil heaps gone soft with moss, rusted rails vanishing into dark like veins into skin. Dunmarrow proper was still hours off, hidden somewhere in the hill's cold interior, but Tam felt the change in her before he understood it. She'd stopped walking like a hawk. She walked like a pilgrim.

At the third gallery entrance she stopped dead.

Old wardstones flanked the mouth—waist-high, lichen-eaten, the kind of marker every miner ignored on principle. Except hers weren't ignoring them. As she passed, her bare fingers brushed the nearest stone, and it *lit*: a thin seam of pale gold waking along the carved lines, guttering like a candle in wind, then steadying.

Tam stopped breathing. He'd grown up in these hills. He'd pissed on that exact stone, probably, during some adolescent emergency. It had never done anything but sit there being geology.

"What," he said carefully, "was that."

Juniper stared at her own hand as though it belonged to a stranger. The light in the stone pulsed once, twice—almost like recognition, almost like relief—and faded.

"The mountain remembers me," she said quietly. To herself more than to him. "I begged it not to."

"Juniper." Tam kept his voice level, which took effort, because every hair on his arms was standing at attention and filing reports. "What is the Sigil? Really. Not the Compact's version. Yours."

She looked at him then, and for the first time since the ambush she looked tired—young, even, and frightened in the specific way of people standing at the edge of something they can see the bottom of.

"It's older than the Crown," she said. "Older than the Compact. And if your Sera Ilves gets her hands on it, she won't free the mountains. She'll wake something that was bound for very good reasons, and the reasons will be the first casualty." She turned toward the dark gallery mouth, toward Dunmarrow and whatever waited there. "Take me to her. And scout—"

"Tam."

"—Tam. When we get there, whatever I say, whatever I refuse—watch the doors. All of them. Including yours."

She walked into the dark, and the wardstones lit one by one ahead of her, a path of small golden fires unspooling down into the mountain, as if the hill itself had risen to greet her—and Tam Vey, standing in the cold with his heart hammering out a warning older than any charter, understood at last that he hadn't found a weapon at all.

He'd found the thing weapons were made *about*.
