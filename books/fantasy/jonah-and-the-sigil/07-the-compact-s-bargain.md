## 7. The Compact's Bargain

Dunmarrow smelled of woodsmoke and wet stone, the way all mountain holds did, but underneath it there was something else—bread, actually baking, in quantities that suggested more mouths than Juniper had counted on the way down.

She counted anyway. It was a habit she couldn't break, counting exits, counting guards, counting loaves. Fifteen years of making herself unmemorable had turned her into a ledger with legs.

Tam Vey walked half a step ahead of her through the tunnel entrance, talking the whole time, which she suspected was a tactic. "Mind the third step, it lies about its height. The hold's older than the Crown, older than the Compact, older than anybody's grandmother's grudges. Sera will want to meet you before you've eaten, because Sera believes no negotiation should ever happen on a full stomach."

"Is that strategy or cruelty?"

"Both. She says hunger keeps people honest." He glanced back at her, and his grin softened into something more careful. "You could still walk out, you know. I'd even point you at the honest exit."

"I know," Juniper said, and didn't move.

The truth was that her feet had decided somewhere on the high road, around the time the wardstones had flickered awake under her palms like old dogs hearing a familiar whistle. The mountain remembered her. She had spent fifteen years teaching the world to forget, and the world had mostly obliged—but the stones under Thornfell had not, and if the land itself was going to keep her name, then hiding from men was a fool's errand performed for an audience of one.

So she followed Tam down into Dunmarrow, past the bread smell, into a hall carved from the mountain's own ribs where perhaps forty people looked up from their suppers and looked at her.

She hated every second of it.

* * *

Sera Ilves received her in a side chamber that had been arranged, very deliberately, to look like it hadn't been arranged. A fire lit but modest. Two chairs, mismatched. A table bearing a jug of cider, two cups, and nothing else—no papers, no maps, no evidence of statecraft. Juniper recognized the staging because she'd once used the same trick herself, back when she'd been someone whose job included making rooms say things.

"You'll be Juniper," Sera said. Not a question. She was a broad-shouldered woman with grey coming in early at her temples and hands that looked like they'd done honest work before they'd done dishonest work, which in Juniper's experience was the most dangerous kind of rebel leader. Honest hands learned exactly how much force things could take.

"Will I?" Juniper said.

Sera smiled like she'd been handed a gift. "Oh, I'm going to enjoy you. Sit. Cider?"

"Cider."

Sera poured two cups, sat, drank first—an old courtesy, or an old performance—and said, "Tam tells me you're the woman who walks like a soldier. He also tells me you saved him from a rockslide by shouting at it, and that the rockslide appeared to listen."

"It was a small rockslide."

"The rocks were very moved, I'm sure." Sera set down her cup. "I'll come to the point, because you strike me as a woman who counts the seconds between sentences. Brannoc sent word that a courier died at your feet carrying something marked in oath-script. And that when you looked at it, you knew what you were seeing."

Juniper kept her face still. It was a skill; she'd paid dearly for it, though she wouldn't understand the currency until later. "Brannoc has sharp eyes for a man who sells smuggled brandy to both sides of a war."

"He sells to anyone who pays. It's practically a philosophy." Sera leaned forward. "I'm not going to pretend I don't know what you are. The swordspeakers are fifteen years dead, the passes are Crown roads now, and the levies take one son in three from every village in these mountains. So here is my case, and then you can tell me to go hang, and we'll have bread."

She talked for a long while, and she was good at it—not the polished kind of good, but the kind that came from having buried people. The Crown's claim on Thornfell rested on old law, law so old nobody living had signed it, law enforced by garrisons the valley folk fed and taxed and died for. Every year the tithe-wagons went down out of the mountains heavy with grain and sons. Every year they came back empty.

"But the law," Sera said, and here her voice changed, dropped into something almost reverent, "the law isn't just paper. Ask any levy-master why the mountain clans have never successfully risen, not once in two hundred years, and he'll tell you it's the garrisons. It isn't. It's the oaths. Something under these mountains holds the old bindings, and the Crown's whole legitimacy sits on top of it like a man sitting on a well lid. Your order built that seal. Your order guarded it. And then the Crown killed your order and kept the seal, because a lock is just as useful when you've murdered the locksmith."

"And you want the key," Juniper said.

"I want the Sigil." Sera didn't flinch from the word. "The courier carried a map to it. You have the map. With the Sigil, the Compact can unbind the old oaths, break the Crown's claim at its root, and free every clan in Thornfell from levies forever. No more tithe-wagons. No more sons in three." She spread her honest hands. "That's my bargain. Give us the Sigil, and we give the mountains back their future."

The fire popped. Somewhere deeper in the hold, faintly, someone was singing—a working song, the kind sung to keep rhythm at a quarry face, and Juniper thought: *those are the sons who came back.* Some of them. The ones the wagons returned.

"You make it sound simple," she said.

"It isn't simple. It's necessary. There's a difference, and I've got the graves to prove it." Sera's eyes were steady. "What would you do with it? Keep running? Bury it deeper? You've seen what the Crown does to keep it. Inquisitors in every valley town. A courier bled out at a ford because he carried a piece of wax and vellum. That thing doesn't get to stay hidden anymore—the hiding is what's killing people."

*The hiding is what's killing people.* Juniper turned the sentence over and found it fit her own life far too well. Fifteen years of erasure, and the erasure hadn't saved the courier, hadn't saved the innkeeper who'd lied badly for her in the valley town, hadn't saved anyone. Invisibility had only ever saved one person, and she wasn't sure it had even managed that.

And yet.

"Sera," she said carefully, "what do you know about how the Sigil works?"

"That it seals the old oaths."

"What do you know about *who* the oaths bind?"

Something flickered across Sera's face—fast, quickly mastered, but Juniper had spent a career reading faces, back when her order's work had been less sword and more silence. Doubt. Or maybe just the irritation of a commander asked a question she hadn't prepared for.

"I know enough," Sera said.

"That's usually the answer of someone who knows too little," Juniper said, "and the answer of someone who knows too much. Which are you?"

For a moment the room went cold in a way the fire couldn't fix. Then Sera laughed—a short, real laugh, surprised out of her. "Gods, you're exactly what Brannoc described. 'Talks like a lawyer and looks like she could carry a mule.' " She stood, paced to the window slit, stood with her back to Juniper looking out at nothing but stone. "I'll tell you what I know. I know that whatever's under that seal, the Crown wants it badly enough to burn villages over rumors of it. I know that in two hundred years no one has opened it, and the mountains have groaned under the Crown's boot the entire time. And I know that I have watched mothers in Harrowdale name their sons' levied regiment out loud so the boy might hear it in his dreams and remember home. Whatever your order buried down there, Juniper Eriksen, it has cost this valley two centuries of blood, and I am *tired* of paying."

The name landed like a stone dropped down a well. Juniper felt it fall and fall and never hit bottom.

"I never told you my name."

"No," Sera agreed pleasantly, turning around. "You didn't."

* * *

They gave her a room. It was a nice room, as cells went—there was a bed with a quilt, a stool, a candle, a window slit too narrow for anything but light and regret, and a door that locked from the outside with a sound like a polite cough.

"For your protection," Tam had said, walking her to it, unable to meet her eyes. "Sera's orders. There are Crown informants everywhere, and if word gets out you're here—"

"Tam."

"Yeah?"

"Does the door lock from the inside?"

He was quiet for a moment. "It locks from wherever Sera needs it to lock," he said finally, and there it was—the honesty she'd started, stupidly, to like about him. "Look. I argued against this. I argued *for* you, which around here is basically a career-ending hobby. But Sera's not wrong that you're safer behind a door than in front of one. It's just—" he grimaced, "—she also thinks the map's safer in a room she controls than in the head of a woman she doesn't."

"So I'm protection and prison both."

"Aren't most good things?" He tried the grin again. It didn't quite land. "I'll bring you supper. Real supper, not the bread-and-water-of-honesty Sera starts everyone on."

After he left, Juniper sat on the bed and took stock, because taking stock was what she had instead of prayer.

She had lost the map. Of course she had—they'd taken it off her while she'd been eating the bread-and-water-of-honesty, which she supposed she should have expected, since she'd have done exactly the same thing in their place. She had a room, a quilt, a candle, and a door that coughed politely. She had one ally whose loyalty ran uphill to Sera Ilves and would always run uphill, because Tam Vey had grown up watching tithe-wagons and she hadn't, not really, not the way he had.

And she had a problem, which was this: Sera was right.

Not about everything. Probably not about the Sigil—Juniper's own memory of what the oaths bound was a locked room in her mind, and she distrusted every instinct that told her to leave it locked. But about the shape of the thing. About the fact that hiding had failed. About the fact that justice, real justice, was owed to these valleys, and that Juniper had spent fifteen years mistaking her own safety for a moral position.

*The trap of good intentions,* her old teacher would have called it, if her old teacher had existed, if her old teacher hadn't been one of the names she'd burned out of every record she could reach. Good intentions made the best cages. Nobody guarded them, because everybody inside them believed the door was open.

She lay back on the quilt and stared at the ceiling, where someone had scratched tally marks into the stone, decades ago, dozens of them, stopping abruptly halfway up. Counting days, probably. Counting loaves. Someone in this room had once been her, waiting for a door to decide what it was.

The candle guttered. Down the corridor, boots passed and passed again—regular, unhurried, a guard doing rounds. Juniper closed her eyes and listened until she had the rhythm of it, forty paces, turn, forty paces, and let herself drift toward sleep with the plan already assembling itself behind her eyes, patient as water finding a crack.

Which was when she heard the voices.

Two of them, low, at the far end of the corridor—one was Tam's, and the other was dry as autumn leaves, and old, and it was saying something that stopped her breath:

"—the margin script, girl, I've *seen* it, and unless my eyes have gone, that's not binding-script at all. That's a *countdown*. Thirteen turns of the moon, and then the seal comes down whether anyone's holding the rope or not."

A pause. Then Tam, sharp with alarm: "Thirteen turns? Maren, thirteen turns until *what*, exactly?"

The old voice answered, and Juniper sat bolt upright in the dark, every invisible hair on her arms standing at attention, because she knew that cadence, she knew that particular way of pronouncing the word *exactly*, she had heard it across a training yard twenty-five years ago from a woman who was supposed to be dead—

"Well," said the archivist Maren, in the voice of someone delivering very bad news to a friend, "until the vault opens *itself*."
