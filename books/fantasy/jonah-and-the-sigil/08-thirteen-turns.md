## 8. Thirteen Turns

Maren Oduya had been old for a very long time and had made her peace with it the way one makes peace with a leaky roof: by arranging her life around the drips.

The archive at Dunmarrow occupied the lowest level of the rebel hold, three rooms carved into the mountain's cold ribs, where the damp got into everything and the candle stubs were rationed like wartime sugar. She had spent forty years down here cataloguing the Compact's scattered inheritance—tithing rolls, smugglers' manifests, clan songs written on birch bark in hands that had shaken with cold or fear or both. Most of it was dull. All of it was precious. Dullness, Maren liked to tell the young ones who came down to gawk at her lamps, was what peace looked like when it was written down. You could measure how badly people had suffered by how boring their receipts were.

So it was a particular kind of morning when Sera Ilves herself came down the stairs with a rolled map under her arm and an expression that promised nothing boring at all.

"Maren." Sera set the map on the reading table with the care of a woman handling a snake she'd been assured was tame. "I need your eyes."

"You have eyes. Very good ones, I'm told. You spotted the Crown assessor's forgery in the Kellin toll ledgers from across a room."

"I need *your* eyes," Sera repeated, "on this."

Maren wiped ink from her fingers, took up her magnifying lens—the good one, rock crystal, bought at scandalous expense from a peddler who had sworn it once belonged to a bishop—and unrolled the map.

It was fine work. Older than fine work. The vellum had gone the color of weak tea, and the roads marked on it belonged to no kingdom she knew: they ran through the Thornfells in patterns that ignored every pass the Crown surveyed, threading instead along routes that only made sense if you assumed the mountains themselves had been asked politely first.

And in the margins—there. Faint as breath on glass.

"Well," said Maren.

"Well?" Sera leaned over her shoulder. "Well *what*? Brannoc says the courier died for this thing. The woman carrying it looked at these marks and went white as flour. I've had two people tell me it's a treasure map and one tell me it's a curse, and I don't pay any of them to guess."

"Nobody pays me to guess either," Maren said mildly, "which is why I read instead." She adjusted the lens. "This is oath-script."

"I know that word. I don't know what it means."

"Neither does anyone else, anymore. That's rather the point of it." Maren traced the margin with a fingernail, not touching the ink itself; some old texts took offense at oil and salt, and she had learned to be polite to things older than she was. "It was the writing of the swordspeakers. The order that kept the passes, before the Crown burned them out of history. They didn't write to record. They wrote to *bind*. Every line of this script is a promise with teeth in it."

"And this one?"

Maren bent closer. The margin-script circled the map's border three times, dense and interlocking, and at its heart sat a single glyph she recognized from exactly one other place: a rubbing of a door lintel from a ruin in the high passes, brought to her twenty years ago by a shepherd who wanted to know if his grandfather's stories about singing stones were true. She had told him no. She had been lying, but kindly.

"This is a term," she said slowly. "A term of years. A binding with an expiry."

Sera had gone very still. "Expiry for what?"

"That," said Maren, "is the interesting question. Give me tonight. And send someone up for more candles. The short ones, not the funeral tapers—I know we're low, Sera, I counted, I count everything, it's my whole personality."

Something that was almost a smile crossed Sera's face and was suppressed immediately, as though the hold couldn't afford one. "Tonight, then. Maren—if this is what I think it might be, the Sigil, the thing the whole mountain has whispered about for a century—"

"Then it will still be what it is tomorrow," Maren said. "Old things are patient. It's only people who aren't. Go. Shout at someone. You'll feel better."

When Sera's boots had faded up the stairs, Maren sat alone with the map and let herself feel what she was actually feeling, which was fear—not the sharp kind, but the deep slow kind, the kind that lived in the bones the way the damp lived in the archive walls.

She had spent her whole life among the Compact's paper ghosts, and she knew better than most that the past was not dead. It was merely waiting for someone literate.

* * *

The candles burned down in threes. Maren worked through the night with her reference texts fanned around her like a card game she was losing gracefully: fragments of swordspeaker liturgy copied by a sympathetic priest before the purge; a merchant's diary describing "the white-cloaked women who spoke and the river obeyed"; a child's primer, recovered from a burned schoolhouse, teaching oath-script letters alongside the alphabet, because the order had believed every mountain child should be able to read the promises made on their behalf.

That primer had always made Maren angry. Tonight it made her useful.

By the third hour she had the structure. By the fifth, the grammar. By the seventh—stiff-fingered, eyes watering, calling twice for tea that nobody brought because everyone sensible was asleep—she had the translation, or enough of it, and she sat back in her chair and read it through four times hoping it would say something different each time.

It didn't.

At dawn, when the hold above began its ordinary noises—hammers, arguments, someone's terrible singing from the kitchen—Maren climbed the stairs with the map held against her chest like a sleeping child, and found Sera already awake, already armored in her leader-face, waiting in the map room with Tam Vey slouched by the window looking like he hadn't slept either, which meant he'd probably never gone to bed, which meant Juniper Eriksen was awake somewhere below and refusing to be, and the whole hold was holding its breath around her like a house around a sickroom.

"Well?" Sera said.

Maren laid the map flat. She had rehearsed this on the stairs and abandoned every version of it except the plain one.

"The margin is a countdown," she said. "A term of years, spoken when the Sigil was last sealed. The binding holds for a fixed span, and the span ends—" she did the arithmetic again, for the fourth time, hating it, watching it come out the same, "—at the completion of the moon's thirteenth turn from the sealing. Which, given the dating conventions of the script and the lunar tables I keep for the planting festivals, falls six weeks from tonight."

Silence. Tam straightened off the wall. "Six weeks until *what*, exactly?"

"Until the binding fails on its own terms." Maren tapped the central glyph. "Oath-magic isn't a lock, whatever the songs say. It's a promise, and promises need a promiser. The swordspeakers who sealed this bound it for thirteen turns because they intended—or expected—to renew it. Someone would come back. Someone would speak the renewal. That was the design." Her finger moved to the final line of the margin, small and cold. "But the order was destroyed. No one renewed. And the script is explicit about what happens when a promise outlives its keepers: it doesn't fade. It *breaks*. Uncontrolled. Unshaped. Whatever force the Sigil holds comes loose all at once, with no hand on it, in the heart of the Thornfells."

"Comes loose," Sera said. "Meaning what? An explosion?"

"Meaning I don't know, and neither does anyone alive, and that should frighten you more than any answer I could invent. These are the voices of an entire buried war, Sera. Bound oaths, bound memories, bound *people*, possibly. Released without ceremony into a mountain full of villages." Maren heard her own voice go dry and quiet. "I have read accounts of single bindings failing. Small ones. A wardstone that snapped after its speaker died. It flattened a valley hamlet and left the survivors unable to remember its name. That was *one stone*."

Tam had gone pale in the way of young men who had joined a rebellion expecting the enemy to be other men. "Then we destroy it. Open the vault, break the seal, done. Six weeks is plenty."

"It is not 'done,'" Maren said, gentler than she felt. "Read the same line I did. The script offers two endings and only two. Renewal—which requires a living swordspeaker, and we have exactly one, whether she admits it or not. Or *unbinding*—a deliberate release, performed correctly, by a speaker who understands the terms. Break the seal crudely, with picks and powder and good intentions, and you haven't destroyed the binding. You've just torn the cage open while the thing inside is still asleep and angry."

"So we need her," Sera said quietly. "Eriksen. Not just her map. Her voice."

"You need her consent," Maren corrected. "Which is a different animal entirely, and considerably wilder. The woman has spent fifteen years making herself unfindable. She didn't run from the Crown alone, Sera—she ran from *us* too, from everyone who'd want to use what she is. And now we're going to tell her there's a clock, and the clock can only be stopped by the exact thing she swore never to do again." Maren sighed and lowered herself into the nearest chair, because her knees had opinions and the night had been long. "I am eighty-one years old. I have catalogued eleven thousand documents. And I am telling you, as the person in this hold best qualified to recognize one: this is a tragedy in the classical style. Everyone in it wants something reasonable. That's how you know it ends badly unless someone is very careful."

"Or very brave," said Tam.

"Brave and careful, boy. Brave *and* careful. Brave alone is just carefulness with the safety off." She rubbed her eyes. "There's one more thing. The margin names the payment."

"Payment?"

"For the unbinding. Oaths are contracts; contracts have price. The script specifies the cost of a proper release, and it's—" Maren paused, and found, to her mild surprise, that her hands were shaking, and pressed them flat against the table until they stopped. "It's not gold. It's not blood, exactly. It's memory. The speaker pays in what they know. The fuller the understanding, the cleaner the release. A partial unbinding by a partial speaker would leave... residue. Fragments loose. Half-freed things."

"Then we find another way," Tam said at once.

"There is no other way written here. There may be no other way written anywhere. Most of the order's books burned, and the ones that survived are in Crown vaults behind locks I couldn't pick with a lifetime and a choir of angels." Maren looked at the two of them—young, fierce, certain, the way she had been once, before the archives taught her humility one yellowed page at a time. "Six weeks. We tell her today. Gently. And we pray to whatever gods listen to archivists that she says yes, because if she says no, we spend those weeks doing the only other useful thing available to us."

"Which is?"

"Writing everything down," Maren said. "Names, villages, songs, grievances. Everything the breaking might take. So that whoever's standing in the rubble afterward has somewhere to start putting the world back together." She managed a smile, the wry one she saved for bad news. "Dullness, remember, is what peace looks like written down. Somebody ought to leave instructions."

* * *

They told Juniper Eriksen that afternoon, in the small room they'd given her, with the door left open as a courtesy and a lie. Maren insisted on being present—her translation, her responsibility—and watched the swordswoman's face as Sera laid out the terms: the thirteenth turn, the failure, the choice between renewal and unbinding.

Juniper listened the way stone listens to rain. When it was done she looked not at Sera but at Maren, at the map spread between them, and something moved behind her eyes that Maren had seen only once before, in an old man reading the death notice of a brother lost fifty years.

"You're certain of the date," Juniper said. Her voice was level. Too level, the way water is level right before it goes over the edge.

"As certain as scholarship allows. The lunar tables agree to within a day."

"Then we have six weeks." She stood. She picked up the map, rolled it with hands that did not tremble, and gave it back—a small surrender, or a small claim, Maren couldn't tell which. "Then you'd best decide quickly what you want from me. Because I already know what I want, and none of you are going to like it."

She walked out. The hold seemed to exhale around her.

Tam started after her; Sera caught his sleeve; Maren, gathering her lens and her notes, said nothing at all. But as she climbed back down toward her candles and her patient, damp, faithful papers, she found herself pausing on the stairs, listening to the mountain around her—the deep stone silence of Dunmarrow, the silence that had held for centuries.

And beneath it, faint as breath on glass, so faint she would spend the rest of her life half-convinced she'd imagined it, she thought she heard the mountain listening back.

That evening, a rider came up the valley road wearing no colors at all, and asked at the gate, very politely, after a dead courier and a woman who walked like a soldier.
