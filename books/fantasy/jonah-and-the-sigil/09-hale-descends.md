## 9. Hale Descends

Inquisitor Corvus Hale had been told, more than once, that he had the face of a kindly uncle. He did not dispute it. Kindly uncles got invited into kitchens. Kindly uncles were offered tea, and bread still warm from the oven, and the small careless confidences that people would never dream of giving a man in grey robes with a warrant in his satchel.

He was fifty-one years old, broad through the shoulders in the way of men who had once carried things heavier than books, and his hair had gone the color of old pewter in a way that people found trustworthy. He cultivated this. He oiled his boots so they creaked gently rather than sharply. He smiled with the corners of his eyes first and his mouth second. He remembered children's names.

It was all, he reflected as the mule track switchbacked down toward Dunmarrow, entirely sincere. That was what the recruits never understood when they asked him how he did the work. They imagined you had to become a monster, or pretend to be one, or build some iron room inside yourself where the screaming was stored. Nonsense. You simply had to be right, and to know you were right, and to love the people you were right *for*. The rest followed as naturally as water finding a low place.

The valley opened below him like a cupped hand: terraced barley going gold at the edges, slate roofs, woodsmoke standing straight up in the windless morning, and there, squatting over the river fork like a toad that had decided to become architecture, the town of Dunmarrow. Hale took out his watch, checked it against the sun out of habit, and made a small note in his ledger.

*Day forty-one since the courier's death at Miller's Ford. Subject believed moving north along the old salt roads. Probability of Compact involvement: high.*

He was quite proud of the ledger. Three decades of them sat in the archive beneath the Citadel, bound in grey calfskin, each entry in his own immaculate hand. Someday, he thought, a historian would read them and understand that the Crown's peace had not been kept by armies. It had been kept by patience.

"Master Inquisitor," said Sergeant Vell, reining up beside him. Vell was twenty-six, earnest, and carried his sword the way other men carried a grievance. "Shall I ride ahead? Announce us?"

"Heavens, no." Hale tucked the ledger away. "We're two travelers come down from the high passes to sell wool and see the autumn fair. Announcing an inquisitor in Compact country is like ringing a dinner bell in bear territory. All you get is bears." He nudged his mule forward. "Besides, I want to count the chimneys."

Vell blinked. "...The chimneys?"

"Smoke tells you how many hearths are lit. Hearths tell you how many people are fed. Fed people are calm people, Vell, and calm people gossip freely. A town with cold hearths is a town that's hiding something, or starving, or both." He nodded pleasantly at a boy driving geese, who stared at them with the frank suspicion of the mountain-born and then went back to his birds. "You'll learn. Observation before interrogation. Interrogation before force. Force only ever means you've failed twice."

"Yes, Master Inquisitor." Vell said this the way he said most things, as though committing it to memory for later report to someone who mattered more than Hale. Hale found this restful. Ambitious young men were predictable, and predictability was nearly as good as loyalty.

* * *

Dunmarrow received them the way a body receives a splinter — no dramatics, just a slow tightening everywhere at once. Hale felt it as he walked the market row: conversations dipping a half-tone lower, a fishwife suddenly very interested in her scales, a door closing on a latch so softly it might have been the wind. Nobody ran. Nobody stared too long. These were mountain folk, and mountain folk knew that the watched pot and the ignored pot boiled at the same speed.

He bought apples. He complimented a weaver on her dye-work and meant it — that madder red was worth every hour of the mordanting. He asked after the fair and was told, by three separate people in three separate tones, that the fair had been cancelled this year on account of the weather, though the sky was insultingly blue.

"The weather," Hale repeated thoughtfully, back in the inn's common room, peeling an apple with a small knife he kept sharp for exactly this purpose. "In autumn. In the mountains."

"Bad year," said the innkeeper, a wide woman named Odile Brask with flour to her elbows and a liar's steady eyes. She'd lied to him twice already — once about strangers passing through, once about whether she kept rooms above the stable. Both lies had been competent. Both had been for someone. He filed that away under a heading he'd labeled, privately, *the good ones*.

"Mm." He set the apple peel down in one unbroken spiral, which he had found unnerved people delightfully. "Madam Brask, do you know what a memory-tithe is?"

The room went quiet in the way rooms do when everyone pretends they haven't gone quiet.

"It's Crown law," she said. "Old law. Nobody's used it in years."

"Which is why nobody remembers how gentle it is." He smiled at her, warmly, and watched her decide not to shudder. "A tithe, madam, is a tenth part. When I tithe a memory, I take one small thing — a name, a face, an afternoon — and nothing else. No pain. Not even a scar. Most folk feel a sneeze coming on and then it's past." He turned the apple in his fingers. "I mention it because a courier died at Miller's Ford six weeks ago carrying something that does not belong in the mountains, and because somewhere between that ford and this valley, he met a woman. And because I am a patient man, but my patience has a budget, like everything else in the Kingdom."

"I couldn't say anything about any woman."

"No," Hale agreed pleasantly. "You couldn't. And I wouldn't ask you to. I'm going to finish my apple, sleep in your excellent beds — the third floor, if you please, I like to hear the weather — and tomorrow I'm going to stand in your square and offer the tithe to anyone who wishes to clear their conscience of suspicion. Entirely voluntary." He stood, bowed slightly, and added, with genuine feeling: "Your bread this evening smelled wonderful. I hope whoever baked it knows they're appreciated."

Odile Brask looked at him for a long moment with an expression he had seen perhaps four hundred times and never tired of: the look of a decent person trying to find the monster in his face and being genuinely troubled that she couldn't.

*She won't come to the square,* he thought, climbing the stairs. *But she'll send word to whoever she's protecting tonight, and word travels faster than warrants. Good. Let it travel.*

* * *

He set up in the market square at midmorning under a grey canopy, at a plain table, with a chair on either side. No soldiers visible — Vell and the others lounged conspicuously by the well, which was its own kind of theater. On the table: the warrant, weighted with a river stone; a carafe of water; two cups; and the device.

The device sat in a padded case like a sleeping bird. It was older than the Citadel's current administration and possibly than the Citadel itself: a shallow bowl of black glass cradled in a frame of tarnished silver, ringed with characters in a script that predated the Kingdom and, Hale suspected, predated several kingdoms before it. The archivists called it a *tithe-bowl*. The mountain folk, when they spoke of it at all, called it the *name-eater*, and spat.

Hale understood the spitting. He'd felt it work. The first time, as a young inquisitor, he had insisted on testing it on himself, and had watched his mother's maiden name rise out of him like a bubble through dark water — a small bright thread of light condensing above the bowl, hanging there, readable. He had gotten the name back afterward; the tithes were returned once a matter closed. But he had never forgotten the sight of a piece of himself floating outside his own head, and he had never stopped believing that anyone who used such a tool carelessly deserved whatever nightmares came of it.

That was the whole doctrine, really. Care. Precision. Take only the tenth part. Leave the person whole.

By noon, eleven people had come to be tithed. This was normal and even healthy — the innocent came eagerly, wanting the paper certificate that cleared them, and their clean bright memories made a pleasant hum in the bowl. A farmer gave up the name of a neighbor whose pig he'd poached (Hale noted it, fined him a chicken, and enjoyed himself thoroughly). A girl of maybe fifteen gave up the face of a boy she liked, blushing ferret-red, and Hale returned it to her untouched with a solemnity that made her giggle. An old man wanted to give up a grief instead of a name, and Hale spent ten gentle minutes explaining that the bowl didn't work that way, and that he was sorry, and that grief wasn't evidence.

It was going beautifully, which was why he knew something would go wrong.

The boy came at the end of the day, when the light had gone long and amber. Sixteen, perhaps seventeen. A Compact runner — Hale recognized the type immediately, all restless feet and badly hidden fear, the sort who volunteered for dangerous errands because sitting still felt worse. His name, when he offered it, was Pell Ashkar, and he said he wanted to clear his conscience like everyone else, and his voice cracked on *conscience* like green wood.

"You don't have to," Hale said, and meant it. "Voluntary means voluntary, lad. Go home."

"I want to." Chin up. Sweat at the temples despite the cool air. "I've got nothing to hide."

"Nobody has nothing to hide. That's not an accusation, it's arithmetic." Hale gestured to the chair. "But sit, if you're sure. Water first. Tithe-reading goes easier on a settled stomach."

What Hale expected from Pell Ashkar was the usual sediment of a teenage boy: a stolen kiss, a poached trout, a resentment against a cousin. What rose instead, in the first minute, was a name.

Not Juniper Eriksen — Hale would have known it instantly, had spent forty days with that shape of syllables folded in his breast pocket like a coal. No. This name was smaller, newer, spoken in a smuggler's den by a keeper named Brannoc to a messenger who'd ridden hard through the night: *the salt-walker, the one who trades on the old roads, she's real, she's here.*

And wrapped around the name, the way context wraps around everything, came the image: a woman at a corner table in Brannoc's den, hood back, studying a map with an expression Pell's memory rendered with terrible clarity — recognition. Not curiosity. Recognition, like a woman reading her own handwriting.

Hale kept his breathing level. He kept his hands loose on the rim of the bowl. Inside, he was doing the cold bright arithmetic of forty-one days collapsing into a single point.

"Thank you, Pell," he said quietly. "That will be all. You're cleared. Here's your certificate."

The boy didn't move. He was staring at the bowl, at the faint afterlight still fading in the black glass, and Hale watched — with the particular sorrow he reserved for these moments — as understanding arrived. Watched the boy realize what *cleared* meant, what had been taken and seen, who else would read the report.

"They'll know I talked," Pell whispered. "Sera Ilves will know. Tam will—" His face did something complicated and young. "They'll think I sold her."

"Would you like me to write in the report that you resisted?" Hale asked. "It wouldn't be true, but reports are written by the victor, and today the victor is inclined to be kind."

"You don't understand." The boy was shaking now. "If the Compact thinks I broke — if they think I gave up the salt-walker — my family holds a steading on Compact land. They'll be *shunned*. My sister's betrothal—"

"Pell." Hale leaned forward, and let the kindly uncle all the way into his face, because it was not a mask, it had never been a mask, and the boy needed it. "Listen to me. Nothing you did was betrayal. You came to a lawful officer voluntarily and answered honestly. If your neighbors punish honesty, that is a failure of theirs, not yours." He paused, and then, because precision mattered, because care mattered, he said the true thing: "And I will find her with or without you. What you gave me saves weeks. Weeks mean fewer soldiers in your valley, fewer doors kicked in, less of everything you're afraid of. You haven't sold anyone. You may have *saved* someone — from worse hands than mine."

"Do you believe that?" Pell asked. Just that. Bare. Sixteen years old and asking the King's inquisitor if he believed his own mouth.

Hale considered lying, found he didn't need to, and was grateful.

"I believe the Crown's law is the only wall between these mountains and the old darkness underneath them," he said. "I believe the order that built that wall was destroyed for reasons I have spent thirty years reading about in sealed archives, and I believe whatever that dead courier carried belongs behind a Citadel seal and nowhere near a rebel armory. And I believe that you, Pell Ashkar, are going to go home now, and eat your supper, and let me do the worrying."

The boy went. At the edge of the square he turned around once, and Hale raised his cup of water to him, and the boy flinched as though the water were a weapon, and was gone into the blue evening.

* * *

That night Hale sat in the inn's third-floor room with the shutters open, because he liked the cold, and wrote his report by candlelight.

*Subject located. Designation: the salt-walker. Identity consistent with last surviving member of the Swordspeaker order, per Archive marginalia 114-c. Currently sheltered within Dunmarrow, likely under Compact protection. Recommend immediate quiet approach; recommend against garrison deployment, which will provoke flight into the deep passes where pursuit is costly and the old wards are... unreliable.*

He stopped. Dipped his pen. Looked for a while at the last phrase.

*Unreliable* was a soft word for what the archives described. Wardstones that woke for certain bloodlines. Passes that remembered. An order of women who could speak oaths into the world and make the world obey, paid for in memories, and a Sigil that bound — what? The archives grew vague there, deliberately vague, vague in the way of documents that had been edited by frightened chancellors. Hale did not frighten easily, but he respected vagueness. Vagueness was where institutions buried the bodies.

Below him, Dunmarrow settled into sleep. Somewhere down there, Odile Brask was lying awake. Somewhere down there, a boy named Pell was staring at his ceiling and learning what his honesty had cost. Somewhere down there — in a cellar, a hayloft, a smuggler's back room — a woman who walked like a soldier was deciding whether to run tonight or tomorrow.

*Run tonight,* Hale thought, setting down his pen. *Please. Run tonight, take the high passes, go to ground in the places your order knew. Because the passes remember you, and the wardstones wake for you, and a running quarry leaves a trail that a patient man can read for years.*

He blew out the candle and sat in the dark, listening to the valley breathe, a kindly uncle keeping watch over his flock.

Down in the town, a lantern moved fast along the river road, heading for the flooded mine shafts at the valley's edge — and beside it, a second figure, arguing in a low urgent voice that carried no further than the water.

Hale did not see it. But he had budgeted for it anyway.
