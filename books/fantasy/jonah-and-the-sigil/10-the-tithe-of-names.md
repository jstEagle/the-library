## 10. The Tithe of Names

The first thing Juniper learned about being hidden in Dunmarrow was that the rebels hid her the way a child hides a sweet—badly, and with visible pride.

They'd put her in the old counting-house above the tannery, a room with one window, one door, and a floorboard near the hearth that announced visitors three steps before they arrived. She'd told them it was indefensible. They'd smiled at her as if she were a grandmother fussing about weather. Sera Ilves had patted the doorframe and said, "Nothing gets past us here," with such sincerity that Juniper had briefly considered writing out a full tactical assessment just to watch her face.

She didn't. She was, after all, pretending to be someone who could be managed.

So she sat by the window on the third morning of her captivity-by-hospitality, mending a sock she did not need mended, and listened to the town below her go about its business. The tannery's vats breathed their sour breath up through the floor. Children were racing hoops down Tanners' Row. Somewhere a woman was arguing cheerfully with a fishmonger about the moral character of eels.

It sounded like peace. It was not peace. Peace did not have Inquisitor Hale two days behind her, descending the mountain roads with a warrant and whatever device he carried in that long case strapped to his saddle.

Tam Vey had told her about the case. He'd come back from a scouting run grey-faced and quiet, which for Tam meant something close to terror. "They call it a tithe-iron," he'd said. "Old Crown law. Reads names out of people. Out of what they remember." He'd rubbed his arms like he was cold. "My gran used to say the tithe-men took her sister's wedding day. Not the sister. The *day*. She couldn't tell you she'd ever been married."

Juniper had set down her needle then. Some things deserved your whole attention.

"And if he uses it here?"

"Then everyone who's seen you remembers you all at once, in front of him." Tam had given her a crooked half-smile. "Good news is, most folk in Dunmarrow haven't seen you. Bad news is, I have. Extensively."

*Extensively.* She'd thought about that word later, longer than was sensible, while she pretended to sleep.

* * *

The interrogations began at dawn on the fourth day, though nobody called them interrogations. Sera's word was *interviews*, delivered in the same tone she used for everything—as if the Compact were simply a very large family having a very long conversation.

Juniper heard the first of them without meaning to. Sound carried up from the square below the counting-house, and Hale had chosen the well-side for his work, where the acoustics were generous.

He began politely. That was the worst part. Through the window she watched him set up a small table, unfold a stool, arrange his documents with the care of a man setting out tea things. He was not a large man. He had the unremarkable face of a good clerk, and he wore grey—not black, nothing so theatrical—and when the first townsfolk were brought to him, he thanked each of them for coming.

An old woman first. Juniper heard Hale ask her about the courier, about the night a man had died at the ford. The woman answered in the roundabout way of the mountain folk, all weather and kinship and no information, and Hale listened to every word as though it were scripture.

Then he opened the long case.

Even from above, Juniper saw the light change. A pale, sourceless glow rose from the case, and the old woman's voice changed mid-sentence—flattened, slowed, like a stream icing over. What came out of her mouth after that was true. All of it. Names, dates, the color of a stranger's cloak seen once through a window eleven years ago.

When it was done, Hale closed the case gently, thanked her again, and asked an attendant to see that she got home safely and something warm to drink. And the old woman stood there in the square, blinking, patting her own pockets as if looking for something she'd lost and couldn't name.

Juniper sat down slowly on the bed.

*He takes days,* she thought. *Wedding days. Faces. He takes the small bright things first because they're easiest to lift.*

And he believed—he clearly, sincerely believed—that this was justice. She'd seen it in his posture, in the tidy courtesy of him. Somewhere in his ledgers, every name he pulled out of a skull was balanced against a crime, and the arithmetic always came out in favor of the Crown. He would grieve, afterward, in his neat way, for the collateral. Then he would record it.

By midmorning they had worked through the fishmonger, two hoop-racing children (Hale skipped them after a single look, and Juniper noted that even monsters have lines, or perhaps only accounting rules), and a young Compact runner named Dellin.

Dellin was nineteen. He had freckles and a nervous laugh and he'd carried Juniper's supper tray twice. Juniper had liked him immediately, the way you like a puppy that brings you sticks.

She watched Hale open the case over him and felt her stomach drop before the boy said a word.

Dellin held out for nearly an hour. She could see him doing it—the set of his shoulders, the sweat, the way he gripped the edge of the stool. He lied about the courier. He lied about the map. He lied about a woman passing through, and for a moment Juniper let herself hope.

Then the tithe-iron's glow deepened, and Dellin's face went smooth and terrible, and he said:

"She's at the counting-house. Above the tannery. The tall woman with the soldier's walk. Sera Ilves ordered her kept there. Tam Vey knows the mine ways out."

The square went very quiet.

Hale wrote it down. He thanked Dellin. He asked whether the boy had family nearby, and when Dellin said a mother on Coopers' Lane, Hale made another note, and Juniper understood that note perfectly: *leverage, held in reserve.*

She was already moving.

* * *

The floorboard by the hearth creaked its warning as she slung her pack—three steps, then a fourth, moving fast. She had a knife at the door before it opened, and lowered it when she saw it was Tam, white-faced, breathing hard.

"You heard," he said.

"I heard."

"Sera's calling a council. She wants to argue with them. Negotiate. Buy time." He shook his head. "Hale doesn't negotiate. He *files*. We've got maybe an hour before they decide the counting-house is worth storming rather than surrounding."

"The flooded shafts," Juniper said. "You mentioned them."

"I mentioned them against my better judgment, yes." He was already pulling the door shut behind him, checking the landing. "Sera gave strict orders—you stay put, we handle it. If I take you out through the mines without orders, that's not disobedience, that's treason with extra steps."

"Then why are you standing in my doorway with a lantern?"

Tam looked at her. For a heartbeat something honest crossed his face—fear, mostly, and under the fear a stubbornness she recognized from mirrors. "Because Dellin gave you up and he'll hang for it or worse, and because if I let them take you I'll spend the rest of my life being the kind of person who lets them take you." He shrugged, embarrassed. "Also you're insufferable company and I'd miss you."

"That's the worst argument I've ever heard."

"It's the only one I've got that isn't treason." He handed her the lantern. "Come on."

* * *

The mine entrance was behind the tannery, hidden under a slab of slate that took both of them and a lever to shift, and beyond it the dark smelled of wet stone and old iron. Tam went first, sure-footed, talking the whole way in a low voice, because apparently silence frightened him more than inquisitors did.

"Three levels down it floods—spring melt comes through the east fault, has for sixty years. Waist-deep in the low galleries, chest-deep in the sump if the rain's been bad. It hasn't. Probably." He glanced back. "You swim?"

"I'm from the mountains."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you're getting."

He laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him—and the sound bounced down the shaft ahead of them like a coin dropped into a well. Juniper found herself almost smiling, and then hated herself for it, because Dellin was in a chair upstairs with his wedding day being weighed out of him like grain, and she was laughing in the dark with the boy who'd helped put her there.

"Tam." Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "What happens to Dellin?"

His footsteps slowed. "Sera will get him out. Ransom, exchange, something. She's good at that part."

"And if she isn't?"

He didn't answer, which was an answer.

They waded into the water at the second gallery. It was cold enough to make her gasp, black and still, holding the lantern-light in a trembling skin on its surface. Old ore carts stood submerged to their axles like drowned cattle. Somewhere far off, water dripped in slow metronome time, and Juniper thought: *this is what my whole life has been. Wading away from things through the dark, leaving people standing in the square behind me.*

"Talk to me," Tam said suddenly. "You've gone quiet in a way I don't like."

"I'm costing people their safety. That's usually when I'm quiet."

"You're not costing anything. Hale's the one with the iron."

"I'm the reason he brought it." She lifted the lantern higher; the light caught a vein of quartz in the wall, glittering like frost. "Every person who helps me ends up in his ledger. Brannoc sent word to you—that's a line in the book. You're carrying me through a mine against your leader's orders—another line. Dellin—" She stopped.

"Dellin chose," Tam said. "We all choose. That's the entire point of the Compact, if you want to know. The Crown treats people like entries. We're trying to be the other thing." He turned, wading backward a step, facing her. "You can't save anyone by being forgettable, Juniper. Believe me, I've tried being forgettable. It's very restful and it saves no one."

She stared at him. Water lapped at her ribs. The lantern hissed softly.

"You have no idea what you're saying," she said finally.

"Probably not. I say a lot of things." He grinned, and turned forward again, and pushed on into the dark. "But I notice you didn't argue."

* * *

They came up out of the mines an hour later, through a collapsed adit on the north slope, into thin cold sunlight and the smell of pine. Behind them, faintly, Dunmarrow lay in its valley—and Juniper could see, even from here, the grey riders massing at the town's south gate. Neat. Orderly. Filing in.

Tam stood beside her, dripping, watching them too. His jaw was tight.

"They'll know it was me by tonight," he said. "Sera keeps better books than Hale does." He said it lightly, but his hand had gone to the hatchet at his belt and stayed there, the way a man touches a wound.

"Then don't go back," Juniper said.

"And do what? Wander the high passes with you being cryptic?" He huffed. "I'm a scout, not a hermit."

"Tam—"

"I know." He held up a hand. "I know what it costs. You keep a ledger too—I've seen you doing sums behind your eyes since the ford. But here's the difference between us." He looked at her, and the humor fell away, and what was underneath was steady as bedrock. "Your ledger says *everyone pays because of me*. Mine says *some things are worth paying for*. One of those books is heavier to carry, and it isn't yours."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The wind moved through the pines. Far below, a bell began to ring in Dunmarrow—not alarm, just the noon bell, indifferent and ordinary, ringing over a town where a boy was forgetting his own name.

"We should move," Juniper said at last, roughly. "The pass road. There's a place I need to reach, and now, unfortunately, so does everyone else."

"Where?"

She looked north, toward the high country, toward the wardstones she hadn't touched in fifteen years and the door at the end of the road that remembered her name.

"The Oathvault," she said. "And Tam—we have six weeks, and I have to tell you what's actually inside it. You deserve to know what you're walking toward."

Tam followed her gaze north. Then he sighed, resettled his pack, and fell into step beside her.

"Six weeks," he said. "Fine. But I'm telling you now—if there's treasure, I get a share, and if there's a monster, it's yours. You brought the map."

Behind them, far below, the grey riders entered Dunmarrow in perfect order, and the counting-house above the tannery stood empty, its door swinging slowly in the wind.
