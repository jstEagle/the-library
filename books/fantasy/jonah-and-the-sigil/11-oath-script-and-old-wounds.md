## 11. Oath-Script and Old Wounds

The high road out of the mine country was not a road so much as an argument the mountains had won. It climbed in switchbacks through scree and wind-bent pine, narrow enough that Tam walked ahead of the mule and I walked behind it, which meant that for two days my entire view of my companion was his shoulders, his pack straps, and the occasional turn of his head when he decided I'd been quiet too long.

"You're doing it again," he said on the second morning, without turning around.

"Doing what?"

"The thing where you answer questions I haven't asked yet by not answering them."

"That's not a thing."

"It's absolutely a thing. Brannoc did it. Sera does it worse. I'm starting to think it's a requirement for people who carry maps that get other people killed." He stopped at the bend where the trail narrowed around a shoulder of granite and waited for me to catch up, which took longer than it should have, because my legs had opinions about the altitude that they were sharing freely. "So. Ask me something easy instead. What's your favorite color? What's the worst meal you've ever eaten? Who taught you to hold a sword like you were born angry at it?"

I looked past him, up the trail, at the thin bright line of sky between the peaks. "You don't want the answers to those."

"Try me."

* * *

We made camp that night in the lee of a ruined waystation—one of the old shelter-stones my order had raised along the passes, back when the passes needed keeping. Its roof had fallen in a century ago or more, but the walls still stood, and someone—shepherds, maybe, or smugglers—had stacked a fire ring against the north wall where the wind couldn't find it. The mountain sheep had been through; there was wool caught on the broken lintel like the building had grown a fleece and then thought better of it.

Tam got a fire going with the competence of a man who'd slept outdoors more nights than in. I sat with my back against the wall and the map against my knees, wrapped in oilcloth, and pretended to study it, and he let me pretend for almost an hour before he said anything.

"You knew the script," he said. "On the map. Back in Brannoc's den. You read the margin notes before Maren did."

"I told you—"

"You told me you recognized the style. That's what you said. 'I recognize the style.'" He fed a stick to the fire. "Maren spent three days on those margin notes. You glanced at them the way I glance at a wanted poster. So either you're the fastest scholar in Thornfell, or somebody taught you that script when you were young enough to still have scraped knees."

The fire popped. Somewhere above us, loose stone ticked down a slope, the mountain clearing its throat.

"My mother used to say," I began, and then stopped, because that wasn't true, and starting a confession with a lie about my mother seemed like a bad way to honor either of them. I started over. "There was a woman called Vessa Koll who came to our valley when I was seven. She was looking for children with good memories."

"Good memories?"

"Children who could hear a story once and give it back whole. Every word, every name, every turn. She'd sit us in a circle and tell us a tale about a fox and a ferryman, and then she'd ask us to say it back, and most of the children gave her the shape of it—the fox, the river, the trick at the end. I gave her all forty-one sentences in order, including the one everyone forgets, where the fox apologizes." I turned the map over in my hands, though I didn't need to look at it anymore. I could have drawn it from memory by then. That was rather the point of me. "She came back the next spring. And the spring after that. And the third time, she didn't leave alone."

Tam had gone very still, in the way of a man who has spent years scouting and knows how to listen with his whole body. "They took you."

"They asked. Which is worse, if you want to know. A child who's taken can hate the taking. A child who's asked gets to spend the rest of her life knowing she said yes." I pulled my knees up. "They brought me to a holdfast under the eastern peaks. There were thirty-one of us that year. We learned the passes. We learned the clans—their banners, their grievances, their marriage lines going back six generations. We learned the oath-script, which isn't a writing the way you write a letter. It's a writing the way a lock is a lock. Every stroke is part of a mechanism. Written correctly, spoken correctly, it *binds*. And we learned what we were for."

"And what were you for?"

I had rehearsed this conversation, in the years of erasing myself. I had imagined telling it to strangers, to inquisitors under torture, to no one at all in empty rooms. In none of those rehearsals had my voice come out steady, and it didn't now.

"We were the order of the Sigil. The swordspeakers of Thornfell. The songs call us the Shield of the Clans—did you ever hear those songs? The ones about the brave sisters and brothers who kept the passes safe from the wars below?" I smiled, and felt it come out wrong. "We kept the passes safe, all right. From remembering."

* * *

He didn't interrupt. I'll grant him that. He sat with his elbows on his knees and his eyes on the fire and he let the silence be a place I could stand, and I have been grateful to him for that ever since, though I'm not sure I ever said so.

"There was a war," I said. "Before your grandmother's grandmother. Before anyone's. A war so bad that the mountain clans nearly ended themselves—clan against clan, valley against valley, and the lowland kings feeding it because a divided Thornfell is a cheap Thornfell. And when it finally burned itself down to embers, the survivors faced a problem. Not how to make peace. Peace is easy; peace is just stopping. The problem was how to keep it stopped. Because every clan carried its dead like a knife, and every mother had taught her children the names of the ones to hate, and the moment some boy grew old enough to lift his father's grudge, the whole thing would catch again."

"So they bound it." His voice was careful. "The memories."

"The griefs. Not everything—just the war. The reasons for it. The names on the lists. They bound the *why*, and left the *that*: people knew there had been a great sorrow, knew their ancestors had suffered, but not at whose hands, or why, or who owed whom. A wound with the memory cut out of it. Healed clean, they called it." I looked at my hands. They were ordinary hands. They had done extraordinary things with them, none of them clean. "And to maintain the binding—to renew it each generation, to patch it where it frayed, to enforce it when someone dug too deep—they made us. Swordspeakers. Oath-bound. We could speak the script aloud, which meant we could unbind things too, which meant we were the only ones who could threaten the peace we existed to protect. So we swore oaths on our own names. Our names became locks. Say a swordspeaker's true name in the old rites, and you can open her like a door."

"That's why you erased yourself."

"That's why I erased myself." I laughed, briefly, without much humor in it. "Fifteen years of being nobody, Tam. Fifteen years of salt caravans and false names and paying innkeepers to forget my face. Because when the Crown came for us—and they came for us, eventually, because a king can't abide a power he doesn't hold—we were the last living keys to the Sigil. They hunted us down one by one. And I survived by making myself into a person no rite could find. No name. No record. No one who remembered me well enough to describe me."

The fire had burned down to a red heart. Tam poked it back to life, not because it needed it, but because his hands wanted work.

"And the courier," he said. "Jonah. He was one of yours."

"He was one of ours." Saying it aloud cost more than I expected. "I didn't know him—I think I would remember—but I know the training when I see it. The way he pressed the map into my hands. The way he spent his last breath on the word that mattered instead of the word that would have saved him. *Sigil,* not *help.* That's not a courier. That's a swordspeaker choosing his last oath."

"So the Crown killed him to stop the map reaching you."

"The Crown, or whoever wears the Crown's coat these days. Yes."

Tam nodded slowly. Then he frowned, and I watched the question arrive in his face before he spoke it, because he was honest that way—his thoughts showed like fish under ice.

"Then here's what I don't understand," he said. "If the binding keeps the peace—if it stopped a war that was killing everyone—then wasn't it worth it? Wasn't it *good*?" He spread his hands. "Sera would say the Compact should wield the Sigil to free the mountain folk from the Crown. Hale would say the Crown should hold it to keep the realm stable. But you—you keep talking about destroying it. Breaking the seal forever. And if the seal is the only thing standing between the clans and the next war..." He shook his head. "Why break it? Unless it's not really about justice at all. Unless it's about you."

The words landed exactly where he'd aimed them, which is the trouble with scouts.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat up through the middle watch while Tam dozed sitting upright against the wall, one hand never far from his knife, and I argued with the fire.

Because here is the truth I had not let myself say in fifteen years: I had never once examined whether the binding was wrong. I had only examined whether *I* was done with it. When the purge came, I ran—not from the order's sins, but from the order's enemies. I told myself I'd left the life behind, but what I'd actually left was the danger, and I'd carried the shame out with me like a stone in my boot, feeling it at every step, refusing to take the boot off and look at it.

And now the map was in my hands, and everyone wanted the Sigil—Crown, Compact, whatever else moved in the dark wearing grey—and my first instinct, my deepest instinct, the instinct I'd have sworn was principle, was: *let no one have it. Break it. End it.* And underneath that instinct, if I was honest, was something smaller and colder: *and then it will finally be over, and I will finally be free of it, and no one will ever again ask me what I was.*

Was that justice? Or was it just a woman trying to burn down the evidence of her own life?

The binding had bought generations of peace. Real peace—weddings across clan lines, trade roads open, children who grew up without inherited hatreds. I had seen that peace. I had helped patrol it. And yes, it had costs—there were things the binding swallowed that maybe shouldn't have been swallowed, old grievances that deserved air, truths that festered in the dark—but every peace has costs, don't they? The Compact's peace would cost blood. The Crown's peace cost levies and gallows. Who was I to decide that the price my grandmothers paid was too high, when I'd be deciding it mostly so I could sleep?

But then there was the other voice, the one that sounded uncomfortably like Vessa Koll on the day she recruited me, saying: *a peace that requires forgetting is not peace, child. It is a held breath. And held breaths end.*

I didn't know. That was the confession under the confession. I had built fifteen years of certainty on top of a foundation of not-knowing, and now the moon was turning toward its thirteenth turn, and Hale was somewhere behind us with his tithe-device, and Sera was somewhere behind us with her reasonable voice, and the map was pulling us east toward the Oathvault, and I did not know whether I was walking toward justice or just toward the end of my own discomfort.

Toward dawn, Tam woke, saw me still sitting, and didn't comment. He built the fire up, boiled water, and handed me a cup, and we drank in silence while the sky went grey and then gold.

"I've been thinking," he said, "about what you said. About the fox apologizing."

I blinked at him. "What?"

"The story Vessa told. Forty-one sentences. The one everyone forgets is where the fox apologizes." He shrugged, embarrassed. "It stuck with me. That you remembered the apology. Out of everything."

"It's the sentence that makes the story bearable," I said. "A trickster who never says sorry is just a cruelty with legs."

"Maybe that's why you kept it." He stood, shouldered his pack, and offered me a hand up, which I took, because my dignity had limits and my knees had already found them. "Maybe you weren't keeping the whole story, Juniper. Maybe you were just keeping the sorry."

We walked. The high road bent east, following an ancient line of leaning wardstones toward a notch in the peaks that I recognized with a lurch I couldn't explain—a valley I had never, in fifteen years of maps and caravans, allowed myself to route through, because some part of me had always known what lived there.

By midday I understood why I'd avoided it. Smoke rose from the valley floor. Woodsmoke, hearth-smoke, the smoke of a living village tucked into the high folds of the mountains—a village that appeared on no map I had ever memorized, in a place my order's records marked with a single line of oath-script and one word:

*Bound.*

Tam squinted at the smoke. "People live out here?"

"No," I said, and my mouth had gone dry. "They can't. That valley isn't supposed to be able to hold a memory long enough to build a fire."

But the smoke rose, patient and gray, and the road bent down toward it, and the road, as it had been doing since the ford, seemed to know exactly where it was taking us.
