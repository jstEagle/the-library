## 12. The Village With No Yesterday

Tam Vey had grown up believing that all villages were the same, give or take a roof.

Dunmarrow had taught him better. The flooded mines, the Compact's cellars full of smuggled grain and smuggled hope. But it was this place—Hollowmere, if the crooked sign at its edge could be believed—that taught him a village could be *wrong* in a way he had no words for yet.

They came down into it near dusk, three days out from the high road, Juniper limping on a knee she swore was fine and Tam pretending not to notice her swearing. The valley opened like a cupped hand: barley fields gone gold, a stream running quick and clear, smoke rising from chimneys in fat, unhurried columns. Chickens. A dog. Children chasing each other around a well with sticks for swords.

"See?" Tam said, gesturing grandly at all of it. "Civilization. Roofs. Possibly bread that isn't hardtack. I might weep."

"You wept at the last hot meal," Juniper said.

"I wept *near* the last hot meal. There's a difference, and it's called dignity."

But something was off, and it took him another hundred paces to name it. He'd spent his life reading villages—the way you learn to read weather, growing up landless. This one read like a page with half the ink missing. The houses were well-kept but oddly placed, as if someone had built them without quite agreeing on where the center of town was. There was no shrine by the gate—unusual enough. No memorial stone. No carved lintels bearing names of founders. And the fields—

"The barley," Tam said slowly. "It's planted right up to the stream bank. Nobody plants to the bank. You lose half a crop every spring flood."

"Maybe they like losing crops," Juniper said dryly.

"Nobody likes losing crops." He squinted at the water. "And look—there's a bend down there, past the mill. Ground's flat as a table. Good ground. Nothing on it. Not even weeds worth the name."

Juniper looked. She was quiet a long moment, and when Tam glanced over, her face had gone still in a way he'd learned to dread, because it meant she knew something and was deciding how much of it to spend.

"Villages keep their dead close," she said finally. "Graveyards behind the chapel, ancestors under the floorboards. It's not sentiment. It's *claim*. You bury your people where you can point at them and say *mine*."

"And here?"

"Here there's no graveyard at all."

The children with their stick-swords ran past them, shrieking with laughter, and one of them—a girl missing a front tooth—skidded to a halt and stared up at Juniper with frank, unafraid curiosity.

"Are you a pilgrim?" the girl demanded.

"Travelers," Juniper said.

"Where are you going?"

"Through."

"Where are you *from*?"

"Gwen!" A woman hurried up, flour on her hands, scooping the girl away with an apologetic smile that didn't reach her eyes. "Forgive her. We don't get many strangers." And then, before they could answer anything: "You'll want the inn. Widow Sarelle's. Tell her Olla sent you." She was already walking away, pulling the girl along, moving fast for a woman carrying nothing.

"She lied just now," Tam said quietly.

"What?"

"'We don't get many strangers.' Her voice did the thing voices do when they're reciting instead of remembering." He rubbed his neck. "My mother lied like that. To the levy-men. 'Just us, just us, always been just us.'"

Juniper said nothing. But her hand had drifted, unconsciously, toward the sword wrapped in sacking on her back, and Tam had been around her long enough now to know what that drift meant. Not danger. *Recognition.*

* * *

Widow Sarelle's inn was warm and low-beamed and smelled gloriously of onion and dripping, and for an hour Tam managed to forget that anything was wrong, because there was stew, and bread with actual crust, and a fire, and his boots were drying by it while his toes experienced something close to religious joy.

"Careful," Juniper murmured, watching him attack the stew. "You'll make yourself sick."

"I'm making myself *happy*. There's a difference, and it's called—" 

"Dignity. Yes. You've mentioned."

The widow herself was a broad, capable woman of sixty-odd who refilled bowls without being asked and asked nothing in return except whether the road from the pass was washed out. When Juniper answered—precisely, in the clipped cadence of someone giving a field report—the old woman's ladle paused, just for a breath, above the pot.

Then she smiled and brought more bread.

It was later, when the common room had emptied and the fire had burned down to a red contemplation, that the elder came.

He arrived without knocking, which told Tam something, because everyone else in Hollowmere knocked. He was small and straight-backed, with a face like a knot in old wood, and he stood in the doorway looking at Juniper for a long time before he came in.

"Widow says you came down from the Bell Road," he said.

"We did," Juniper said.

"Alone?"

"Two of us."

He nodded slowly. Then he walked across the room—not toward Juniper, but around her, a slow quarter-circle, the way you'd circle a horse you were thinking of buying or a snake you were thinking of killing—and stopped, and watched her hands resting open on the table, and watched the set of her shoulders, and watched, Tam realized with a creeping scalp, the way she sat. Balanced. Weight forward. Ready.

"Stand up," the elder said.

"Elder Macek—" the widow began from the kitchen door.

"Stand up, traveler. Just that. Stand and walk to the fire and back."

"I'm tired," Juniper said pleasantly. "I've walked all day."

"*Stand.*"

There was a silence in which Tam heard the fire tick and his own heart do something unhelpful. Then Juniper sighed, rose, crossed to the hearth and back—four steps each way, unhurried, and yet Tam saw it now too, seeing it through the old man's eyes: the heel-toe roll, the shoulders level, the turn on the ball of the foot, economical as a hinge. A soldier's walk. Worse than a soldier's. Something older, drilled so deep it lived in the bone.

The elder watched her sit back down. His face did not change. But his eyes were wet.

"You're one of them," he said. "A swordspeaker."

Juniper went very still. "I don't know what you mean."

"You know *exactly* what I mean." His voice stayed level, almost gentle, which was somehow worse than shouting. "The walk. The hands. My grandmother described it to me when I was a boy. She'd seen one once, when she was small. She said they moved like the mountain moves—like something that has never once had to hurry." He took a breath. "She also said what they *did*."

"Elder," Tam started, "I think there's been some mistake, my companion is a caravan guide, salt and tin, very dull, I assure you—"

"Boy, be quiet." He didn't raise his voice. He didn't need to. "Ask her where the graves are."

Tam looked at Juniper. Juniper looked at the fire.

"There are no graves in Hollowmere," the elder said, to Tam now, as if Juniper had stopped existing. "Did you notice? No churchyard. No cairns. No stones by the road. Four hundred souls, and not one marked death since the village was founded. Do you know why?"

"No," Tam said honestly.

"Because we don't remember anyone dying." The old man's voice cracked on the word *remembering*, bent it into something else. "Not our grandparents. Not the founders. Ask any soul in this village when their line came here and they'll tell you 'always,' and then they'll stop, mid-word, like a cart hitting a wall. Ask them about the flat ground past the mill and they'll say it's poor soil. It isn't poor soil. I've farmed forty years; I know good earth when I stand on it. Everyone knows and no one knows. It's like a word on the tip of the tongue that never comes." He turned back to Juniper at last, and now his voice did rise, thin and shaking. "Something happened here. Something so terrible that your order came and cut it out of us like a tumor. Out of every mind in this valley, generation after generation, because the binding passes down through blood. We are a village with no yesterday. We don't mourn because we don't know what we lost. We don't even know to *ask*."

The kitchen door creaked. The widow Sarelle stood there with a dishcloth wrung tight in both hands, and behind her, half-hidden, the gap-toothed girl, Gwen.

"Sarelle," the elder said softly. "Your husband. What was his name?"

"Brann," she said promptly. Then her brow furrowed. "Bran... Brann..." She pressed the cloth to her mouth. "Why can't I— I named him every day for thirty years, why can't I—"

"Because he died on the flat ground past the mill," the elder said, "and whatever died there, we cannot hold."

The dishcloth fell. The widow made a sound Tam would never be able to describe afterward—not a sob, not a scream, something between a hiccup and a door slamming—and turned and walked back into the kitchen, and they heard water running, and running, long after any basin could have filled.

Gwen hadn't run after her. The girl stood in the doorway staring at Juniper with those frank, unafraid eyes, and Tam thought, absurdly, *she doesn't understand, thank every god she doesn't understand—*

"Was it you?" the girl asked. "Did you take our yesterdays?"

"Gwen, go inside," Juniper said, very quietly.

"Did you?"

"*Go inside.*"

The girl went.

For a long moment nobody spoke. The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled. Tam became aware that his hands were shaking around his bowl of cold stew, and that he was looking at Juniper the way you look at a familiar room after someone tells you a body is buried under the floorboards.

"You said your order kept the peace," he said. His voice came out strange. "On the high road. You said they bound things—memories—to keep a war buried. I understood that. I thought I did. Wars, armies, great names. Big things." He swallowed. "This is a *village*. These are farmers. What could farmers possibly have done that deserved—"

"They deserved nothing," Juniper said.

Her voice was flat. Tam had heard her flat before—inquisitors, ambushes, the flooded dark of the mine shafts—but never like this. Never aimed inward.

"It wasn't punishment," she said. "That's the thing you have to understand, Tam, and I need you to understand it, because I need—" She stopped. Started again. "The massacre happened during the Binding Wars. A clan dispute, or a tax revolt—I don't know. I was never told. That was the point of me; I was raised in the order and even *we* weren't told. Only that the valley had to be quieted, because grief spreads, and grieving clans make armies, and armies wake what sleeps below. So the swordspeakers came and spoke the Severance, and four hundred people forgot their own dead, and the Crown called it mercy, and the order called it duty, and everyone went home and got medals or whatever it was we got." Her jaw worked. "I was taught the Severance at twelve years old. As a technique. There were diagrams."

"Diagrams," Tam repeated hollowly.

"Of memory. Of how it layers, how it binds to bloodline, where to cut so the wound doesn't show." She laughed, one short ugly note with no humor anywhere in it. "I was *good* at it, Tam. Top of my year. They gave me a ribbon."

The elder had sunk onto the bench across from her, suddenly old, suddenly tired, all the fury drained out and something worse left sitting in its place. "You were children," he said. "They taught *children* to do this."

"They taught us it was kindness." Juniper's voice barely carried. "That forgetting was a gift. That a people who couldn't remember their grief couldn't be weaponized by it. Every oath I ever swore had mercy in the wording somewhere. That's how you make children do monstrous things—you write *mercy* on the blade."

Tam looked at his hands. At the fire. At the woman he had followed out of Dunmarrow's flooded dark, whose survival he had bought against his leader's orders, whom he had—there was no other honest word for it, though he'd been carefully not saying it even inside his own head—begun to care for. He thought about the village with no yesterday, and the widow washing a basin that needed no filling, and a girl asking *did you take our yesterdays?*

And he thought about the Sigil in its wrappings, and Sera Ilves saying *it could free us*, and every argument he himself had made for taking it, wielding it, using the Crown's own weapon to break the Crown.

"How many?" he asked quietly. "Villages like this. How many Hollowmeres?"

Juniper was silent so long he thought she wouldn't answer.

"I don't know," she said at last. "The map shows the Oathvault. The vault holds the oaths. And the oaths—" She closed her eyes. "An oath of Severance is spoken over a whole valley at once. Hundreds of voices, bound into a single working. If the vault holds the record of every working ever sworn..." She opened her eyes, and they were terrible and clear. "Then it doesn't hold one massacre, Tam. It holds all of them. Every valley. Every forgetting. Written down in the dead's own words, filed and sealed and *kept*, because the Crown's law runs on the peace we purchased, and the peace was paid for with—" she gestured helplessly at the inn, the village, the gold fields going dark outside the window, "—with *this*. Forever. In installments."

The elder made a sound. Tam realized the old man was crying, silently, without any change in his posture, tears simply falling off a face like a knot in wood.

"My grandmother," he said, "spent her whole life walking to the flat ground past the mill and standing there, and she never knew why. She thought it was just a habit. She thought she was just an old woman who liked the view." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, roughly, angry at the tears. "She was standing at her own mother's grave. She just couldn't know it. For sixty years."

Outside, the moon was rising—fat and pale, six nights short of its thirteenth turn. Tam watched it through the window and felt the clock Maren had described ticking in his chest, faster now, and felt something else shift underneath it, some load-bearing beam of belief groaning and settling into a new shape.

He had wanted the Sigil freed so the mountains could be free. He had wanted justice. He still did. But justice, he understood now, sitting in a warm room paid for with four hundred stolen yesterdays, was not a weapon you picked up. It was a debt you paid down. And somebody—somebody—was going to have to decide what paying it cost, and who got to survive the bill.

He looked at Juniper. She was staring at the fire, and her lips were moving slightly, shaping words he couldn't hear, and for one irrational moment he wondered if she was practicing an oath—or rehearsing an apology four hundred years too late.

The elder rose stiffly. At the door he paused, and did not look back.

"There's a room upstairs," he said. "Sarelle will show you. We'll not turn travelers out at night—we're not monsters in Hollowmere." A pause. "Whatever we were, we weren't allowed to be monsters either."

His footsteps faded down the lane. The fire burned low. And it was Juniper who broke the silence at last, in a voice stripped down to bare timber:

"Toward morning, we should check the eastern ridge. If Hale tracked us to the Bell Road, he'll push scouts tonight."

Tam blinked. "You want to talk tactics. *Now.*"

"If I stop moving, I'll have to feel this," she said simply. "So. Tactics."

And it was then, as she turned toward the stairs, that Tam saw it—the flicker of lamplight on the road below, two riders in grey moving fast along the riverbank toward the village, and behind them, further back, a whole column of torches kindling in the dark like a lit fuse.

"Juniper," he said. "Company."
