## 13. Sera's Second Offer

Sera Ilves had learned long ago that the mountains told you everything you needed to know about a person, if you watched how they walked them. Juniper Eriksen walked the high road like an apology—head down, shoulders folded in, taking the path of least resistance even when it wasn't the shortest. Tam Vey walked like a man who'd been told he was clever so many times he'd started believing it, which meant he stepped on every loose stone just to prove he could.

Sera herself walked like a woman with a ledger in her head and every column slightly out of balance. Which was accurate.

She'd been following them for two days. Not because she'd lost them—a Compact scout didn't lose anyone on her own mountain—but because she'd spent those two days rehearsing what she was going to say, and every version of it sounded worse out loud than it had in her tent.

"You're doing the thing," said Doss, her second, riding half a length behind her mule.

"What thing."

"The thing where your jaw goes sideways. Like you're chewing on a word that bit you first."

"I'm composing a diplomatic overture."

"You're composing a surrender," Doss said. "There's a difference. One of them has dignity."

"The last time I spoke to Eriksen, I locked her in a room."

"Detained," Doss corrected. "For her protection. That's what we're calling it now. History will be very kind to us."

"History," Sera said, "is currently on fire." She nodded back down the valley, where a thread of black smoke rose from behind the ridge at Coldharren—the direction of the mill safehouse, the one with the grain stores and the false wall and old Wenna Tull inside it. They'd seen the smoke at dawn. Nobody had said much about it since. There wasn't anything to say that smoke hadn't already said better.

Hale was burning his way up the mountain after them, patient as rot, and every hour Sera spent rehearsing was an hour he gained.

So she stopped rehearsing, kicked the mule into a trot, and rounded the bend where the high road narrowed between two leaning slabs of granite—and there they were, exactly where her scouts said they'd be: the swordspeaker and the boy who'd betrayed her orders, sharing a fire no bigger than a cupped hand, boiling something that smelled like pine needles and regret.

* * *

Tam saw her first. She knew because he stood up too fast, knocked the pot off its stones, and then tried to catch it in a way that suggested he'd never actually caught anything in his life.

"Sera," he said, in the voice of a man greeting the aunt who'd caught him stealing from the tithe box.

"Vey." She dismounted slowly, letting him watch her do it, because watching her do things calmly was sometimes all the discipline a nervous man needed. "You look well. Prison would have been colder, I imagine. I'm glad you chose desertion instead."

"It wasn't—" He glanced at Juniper, who hadn't moved from her seat by the fire. She sat with her hands around her knees, watching Sera with the flat gray patience of a hawk deciding whether something was worth the dive. "It was necessary."

"Everything is necessary," Sera said. "That's how we justify it. Sit down, both of you. I've ridden since before dawn and my legs have opinions."

Doss hobbled the mules. Tam righted the pot. Juniper continued not moving, which Sera noted was itself a kind of answer: she wasn't running, and she wasn't reaching for the sword at her hip either. Both of those facts meant something. Sera had spent enough years reading people to know that the most dangerous thing about Juniper Eriksen was how little she seemed to want anything.

Which made what Sera had come to ask her both easier and infinitely harder.

"I'll assume you saw the smoke," Sera said, settling onto a rock across the fire.

"Coldharren," Juniper said. Her voice was low and even. "Anyone in it?"

"Wenna Tull got out with the ledgers. Two of Hale's riders didn't get out at all, if the stories coming down the valley are true." Sera held the swordspeaker's gaze. "He burned a house full of flour and family portraits, Eriksen. That's what he does. He doesn't need reasons. He needs momentum. And right now, you are his momentum."

"I know."

"Do you? Because from where I sit, you're walking toward him at a stately pace, stopping for tea."

"This is tea?" Tam looked into the pot.

"It's barley water with juniper in it," Juniper said, without a flicker of humor, and then, catching herself: "The berry. Not me."

Sera felt something in her chest loosen by a single degree. Good. There was a person under all that granite after all, and people could be negotiated with. Fortresses could only be besieged, and she'd already tried the siege.

* * *

She let the silence work for a while. Fire crackle. Wind in the pass. Somewhere below, very faint, the sound of the valley going on with its day—carts, dogs, someone hammering something, the ordinary music of people who did not yet know what was coming for them.

Then she said, plainly, because she'd learned that plainness disarmed this woman more than any eloquence:

"I'm sorry I caged you."

Juniper's eyes came up.

"You heard me. It was a cage. I dressed it up as care because that's what leaders do when they're frightened, and I was frightened—you walked into Dunmarrow carrying the one object in the world that could unmake everything my people have bled for, and I reacted like a woman guarding a treasure instead of a woman talking to a neighbor." She spread her hands. "I'm not here to apologize twice. Once is a gift; twice is begging. But I am here to make you a different offer, and I'd rather make it honestly than prettily."

"Go on," Juniper said.

"Hale has a warrant, a company, and a device that eats names out of people's heads. You have a map, a sword, and one deserter." Tam opened his mouth; Sera raised a finger without looking at him. "A brave deserter. A deserter I'd trust with my life, which is why his betrayal stings, but the arithmetic doesn't change. Alone, you'll reach the Oathvault the week after Hale does, or not at all. With us, you reach it first. Guides who know the passes. Provisions. Eyes on Hale's column so he can't take you unawares again. Everything I refused you when I thought I could simply keep you."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange," Sera said, and here was the word she'd been chewing on for two days, the one that had bitten her first, "when you stand in front of whatever the Sigil actually is—you let the Compact decide what happens to it. Not me alone. The council. All nine voices. The way free people decide things."

The wind moved through the pass. Juniper looked into the fire for a long moment, and Sera watched her and understood, suddenly and uncomfortably, that she was not watching a woman consider an offer. She was watching a woman grieve something. Whatever the Sigil was to Juniper, it wasn't a weapon or a prize. It was a scar, and Sera had just asked to put her thumb on it.

"No," Juniper said.

Tam made a small wounded noise. Sera kept her face still.

"May I ask why?"

"Because you'd choose wrong."

"That's honest," Sera said. "Rude, but honest. Wrong how?"

"You think the Sigil is a key," Juniper said. "Freedom for the mountain folk, an end to the levies, the Crown's claim broken. I've heard the speech—I slept through it once already in Dunmarrow, so I know it by heart. But I've read the margins of that map, Sera. I know what the script says, or some of it. The Sigil isn't a door with treasure behind it. It's a door with something *bound* behind it. And bound things don't come out grateful."

"Then we'd learn what it is before we decided," Sera said. "That's what a council is for. Nine heads instead of one."

"One head that knows what it's looking at," Juniper said quietly, "is worth nine that don't."

"And yours knows?"

Something crossed Juniper's face then—fast, gone, like a fish under ice. Sera had interrogated smugglers and barons and once, memorably, a bishop, and she knew a withheld truth when she saw one. The swordspeaker knew more about the Sigil than she was saying. Possibly much more. Possibly the whole of it.

"Yes," Juniper said, which proved nothing except that she was willing to lie with a straight face, which Sera respected and resented in equal measure.

* * *

Doss cleared her throat. In the grammar of their partnership, this meant *you're losing her, try another door.*

Sera leaned forward. "Let me tell you what happens if you say no. Not as a threat—as weather. Hale reaches the vault with thirty men and a device that reads names. Whatever's in there, he opens it or he dies trying, and either way the Crown writes the ending. Or you reach it alone, with Vey, and maybe you win, and maybe the ending you write is the right one—but nobody will ever know, because you'll be dead or gone, and the story will belong to whoever's left standing. That's how it always goes. The victor keeps the record." She paused. "Unless the record-keepers are the ones who helped you get there. Let us walk with you, Eriksen. Not ahead of you. With you. And when the moment comes—if you still believe the decision must be yours alone, if you can look nine people in the eye and tell them why—then argue your case. That's all I'm asking. Not surrender. Argument."

"That's a longer leash than a cell," Tam said, before he could stop himself.

"It is," Sera agreed. "Leashes are for dogs. I'm asking for a rope between climbers. If she falls, we catch her. If we fall—" she shrugged, "—she's under no obligation. But I don't think she'll let us fall. I've been watching her for three weeks. She saves people. It seems to be the one habit she can't break, and I intend to exploit it shamelessly."

For the first time, something moved at the corner of Juniper's mouth. Not quite a smile. The memory of one, perhaps, kept in a jar.

"You're honest," Juniper said. "That's the worst part. I almost wish you were a liar. Liars are easy to refuse."

"My mother said the same. She also said I'd die poor, and she was right about that too." Sera stood, brushing off her coat, because standing gave the other person permission to stand, and standing people agreed to things sitting people wouldn't. "You don't have to answer now. We'll walk with you regardless—at a distance, if you like, far enough that you can pretend we're not there. Hale doesn't distinguish between allies and bystanders anyway. Ask Wenna Tull."

Juniper rose. Up close, in the flat light of the pass, she looked tired in a way that went deeper than sleep—tired somewhere structural, like a bridge that had carried too many winters. And yet her eyes, when they fixed on Sera, were the clearest thing on the whole mountain.

"If I let you come," Juniper said, "and when we get there, and I decide the Sigil must be destroyed—not used, not studied, not bargained with—destroyed—will your council hear me out? Truly? Even if destruction costs the Compact its dream?"

Sera held her gaze. Somewhere behind her own ribs, the honest answer and the useful answer wrestled briefly, and she did the thing she'd sworn on Wenna Tull's doorstep she would do for the rest of this campaign, which was pick the honest one even when it hurt.

"They'll hear you," she said. "Whether they'll agree, I won't promise. Promises are cheap and I've spent enough of them. But I give you my voice among the nine. When you argue for destruction, I'll argue it with you. That I can promise, because I've already decided it."

"Why?" Tam blurted. "Why would you give up the dream?"

"Because I burned a village once," Sera said, "as a younger and stupider officer, following orders I believed were just, and I have spent twenty years learning what 'just orders' cost the people underneath them. Whatever sleeps behind that seal, Vey, it isn't a treasure. People like Hale don't build prisons around treasures." She picked up her reins. "We leave in ten minutes. Eat something. Both of you. You can't outwalk grief on an empty stomach; I've tried."

* * *

They walked.

By midday the arrangement had found its shape without anyone naming it: Juniper and Tam a dozen paces ahead, Sera and Doss behind, close enough to talk, far enough to pretend independence. By late afternoon, Tam had drifted back to walk beside Doss and was telling her some story involving a goat, a wedding, and what he insisted was only a minor charge of arson, and Doss was laughing in the way she laughed at nothing else, and Sera pretended not to notice that her second—who trusted no one—was being charmed.

And Juniper walked beside her. Uninvited. Unsought. Just there, matching her stride, eyes on the road.

"The safehouse at Coldharren," Juniper said, after a mile of silence. "Wenna Tull. Where is she now?"

"Moving north with the ledgers, to my sister's holding. Why?"

"Because Hale reads names out of heads," Juniper said. "And Wenna Tull has seen my face."

Sera felt the cold arrive all at once, the way mountain weather does—no warning, just present. "Wenna wouldn't talk."

"Wenna wouldn't *choose* to," Juniper said. "That's not the same thing. Nothing is, anymore." She adjusted the strap of her pack, and her voice stayed level, almost gentle, which somehow made it worse. "Send word. Tonight. Get her across the border passes or put her somewhere the tithe-device can't reach, if such a place exists. Do it before you do anything else for me."

"We're doing nothing else for you. We're walking the same direction."

"Mm," said Juniper, in the tone of a woman declining to argue with a child about whether the stove is hot.

They crested the shoulder of the pass as the light went long and gold, and below them the road dropped into a valley Sera had known all her life—she'd fished its river, buried a brother under its birches—and yet tonight it looked different, lit up along its whole southern edge with a chain of watchfires that had no business being there.

Doss saw it at the same moment and swore with impressive variety.

"Hale's vanguard," Sera said quietly. Counting fires. Counting men. Doing the arithmetic she'd promised herself she'd stop doing, because the sum never changed. "He's not chasing us anymore. He's herding us. Every road south burns, every pass gets a garrison—he's funneling us, Eriksen. Funneling us exactly where he wants."

"Where does he want us?" Tam asked.

But Juniper had gone very still beside her, staring down at the fires, and Sera saw that she already knew—that she had perhaps always known—and that the knowledge sat on her like a stone sits on a grave.

"The Oathvault," Juniper said. "He doesn't need to catch us. He just needs us to open the door."
