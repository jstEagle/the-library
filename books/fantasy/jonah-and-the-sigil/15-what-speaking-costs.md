## 15. What Speaking Costs

Tam Vey had learned, in his twenty-six years of living rough along the Thornfell spine, that the most dangerous hours were the ones just before dawn. Not because of wolves or Crown patrols—those you could hear, could smell, could plan around—but because of the way darkness pooled thickest right before it broke, and the way a person's courage pooled thinnest at exactly the same time.

He was sitting up on watch when Juniper started screaming.

It wasn't loud. That was the worst part. He'd heard men scream in the mines when the water came through, heard women scream at Hale's tithe-readings, and those sounds tore the air like torn metal. This was different—a thin, strangled sound, like someone trying to shout through a wall of wool, and it came from the little hollow where she'd bedded down beneath an overhang of grey stone, wrapped in her cloak with her back to the mountain the way soldiers sleep.

He was beside her before he'd fully decided to move.

"Juniper." He kept his voice low, steady, the voice he used for spooked mules. "Juniper. You're dreaming."

Her eyes opened and for one unguarded moment they were full of pure animal terror—the look of someone who has woken in a place they don't recognize, next to a person they don't know. She stared at him. He watched her gather herself, piece by piece, like a woman collecting spilled coins from a floor: the camp, the pass, the bells still faintly ringing somewhere behind them, him.

"Tam." Her voice was hoarse. "What—how long was I out?"

"Half the night. You spoke the oath against Hale's device and then you went down like a felled tree. I carried you two miles up the switchbacks before my arms filed a formal complaint."

She didn't smile. Juniper almost never smiled at his jokes, but she usually gave him something—a twitch at the corner of her mouth, the ghost of an acknowledgment that he existed as more than ballast. This time there was nothing. She looked past him at the sky, which was going from black to bruise-purple over the eastern ridge, and her brow furrowed like she was working a difficult sum.

"The bells," she said. "The Pass of Hanging Bells. We crossed it."

"Yes. Yesterday. Well—yesterday and the day before, depending on how you count the ambush."

"And Hale?"

"Gone to ground, or gone to fetch reinforcements. Either way we bought ourselves time. You shattered his tithe-device into very expensive confetti, in case you were wondering how your evening went."

That should have earned him something. A dry look. A sigh. Anything.

Instead she said, slowly, carefully, like each word was being tested for load-bearing capacity: "I remember the bells. I remember the oath. I remember..." Her hand drifted up toward her own face, hovering near her cheek without touching. "I remember everything."

* * *

They broke camp in the grey half-light, and Tam watched her the way he'd been taught to watch unfamiliar terrain—not staring, but cataloguing. The Compact trained its scouts to notice what was missing as much as what was present. A trail with no tracks is information. A birdless sky is information.

Juniper Eriksen moved like a woman whose body knew every step and whose mind was walking three paces behind, trying to catch up.

She packed her bedroll wrong. He noticed it because he'd watched her pack it a dozen times now, always the same way, always with the blanket folded around the oilcloth first so the damp couldn't creep in—and this morning she rolled it loose and lopsided and didn't seem to notice. She ate her share of the dried plums without complaint, though twice before she'd said they tasted like sweetened leather and traded hers to him gladly. She paused at the treeline where the high road bent north, and stood there a moment too long, head tilted, like a hound listening for a whistle that never came.

"You're limping," he said.

"I'm not limping."

"You're favoring your left leg. From the fall at the mine shafts. It stiffens when it's cold."

"It's fine," she said, and walked on, and her stride evened out immediately—which meant she'd known about the leg, remembered being told, corrected for it. Remembering instructions instead of remembering the thing itself. Tam had done that himself, after his father died; he'd caught himself answering questions about the man from habit, from stored facts, while the actual shape of him—his laugh, the smell of tar on his hands—slid further away every month until one day Tam realized he was describing a stranger who happened to have his father's name.

He knew what forgetting looked like from the inside. Now he was learning what it looked like from the outside, and it made his chest hurt.

* * *

Midmorning brought them to a stream crossing where the water ran shallow and quick over red stones, and Juniper stopped dead.

"What is it?" Tam's hand went to his knife out of pure reflex.

"Nothing." But she was staring at the water like it had spoken to her. "There should be a cairn here. A marker. My order used to—" She stopped. Started again. "There's supposed to be a cairn. Three stones, waist-high, with a flat capstone. I'm sure of it."

"There's no cairn."

"I can see that, Tam."

"No, I mean—" He crouched by the bank, feeling suddenly awkward, like a man explaining to someone that their house had burned down. "Cairns get knocked down. Floods, frost-heave, shepherds stealing stone. Whatever was here might be long gone. That doesn't mean you're wrong about it."

She looked at him then, and there was something raw in her face that she smoothed away almost instantly, the way you'd pull a blanket over a sleeping child you didn't want to wake.

"That's not what frightens me," she said quietly. "What frightens me is that I can't remember whether I last crossed here fifteen years ago or five. I can't remember whether the cairn had moss on it. I can't remember—" Her jaw tightened. "It doesn't matter. Keep moving."

* * *

They made camp early that night, in a hollow below a shelf of rock that would hide their firelight. Tam got a blaze going small and hot, and Juniper sat across from it with her knees drawn up, turning something over in her fingers.

It took him a while to identify it. A smooth river pebble, pale grey, worn round. He'd seen her pick it up days ago, back before the pass, and turn it over the same way, and he'd assumed it was a habit, a fidget, nothing.

Now he watched her turn it, and turn it, and turn it, and understood.

"You're trying to hold onto something," he said.

She didn't answer.

"Juniper."

"Don't." The word came out sharp enough to cut. Then, softer, with the exhaustion showing through underneath: "Don't do the thing where you're kind at me. I haven't got the reserves for it tonight."

"All right." He fed a stick to the fire. "Then I'll do the thing where I'm practical instead. We've got six leagues to the Oathvault approaches and maybe four days if the weather holds. You need sleep. Your body's spent the last day paying a bill your mouth ran up, and if you don't let it collect properly, you'll be no good to either of us when it matters."

"I slept."

"You screamed in your sleep."

Her head came up sharply. "I did not—"

"You did. Just before dawn. Quiet, but I heard it." He held her gaze across the fire, deliberately gentle now, because sometimes kindness worked better when you didn't announce it. "You want to tell me what you lost? Or do you want to keep pretending the pebble is just a pebble?"

For a long moment the only sound was the fire ticking and, far off, wind moving through the high pines with a sound like the sea.

"My mother," she said finally.

The word landed between them like a dropped stone.

"She was a weaver. In the lowland villages, before—" Juniper's hand closed around the pebble until the knuckles went white. "Before the order took me. I used to sit under her loom and watch the shuttle go back and forth, back and forth, and she'd sing these old weaving songs, counting songs, and I remember—I remembered—the songs. I remember that she sang. I remember that her hands moved. I remember that I loved her more than I have ever loved anything or anyone, with the whole stupid ferocity of a seven-year-old." Her voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "But I cannot see her face, Tam. I close my eyes and there is a shape where a face should be. A warm shape. A shape that sang. And the shape has no features at all."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, a nightbird called once and thought better of it.

"The oath I spoke at the pass," she went on, "the one that broke Hale's device—it took payment. They all do. Every spoken oath takes a memory, and the magic chooses what it takes, and it chose—" She laughed, short and terrible. "It chose the one thing I'd have paid anything to keep. Because of course it did. The magic isn't cruel, exactly. It's just honest. It takes what's most alive in you, because that's where the power is. Dead memories are cheap. Living ones cost."

Tam found that his throat had gone tight. He thought about saying something comforting and discarded it, because he'd seen enough grief to know that comfort offered too fast bounces off. Instead he asked the question that mattered:

"How many more oaths between here and the vault door?"

She was quiet for a while.

"At least two that I know of. Possibly more, depending on what the mountain asks." She turned the pebble over one last time and set it down beside her, deliberate, like laying down a weapon. "And then the door itself. Which wants a full name, spoken willingly."

"Your name."

"My name." She looked into the fire. "Do you understand what that means, Tam? By the time we reach that door, I may not remember why I'm doing any of this. I'll be spending myself to buy passage to spend the rest of myself. There won't be much left over for—" she gestured vaguely at the dark, at the world, at him, "—after."

"After," he repeated.

"After." 

* * *

He took second watch again, partly because he didn't trust her to stay awake and partly because he wanted to watch her sleep without it being strange.

She didn't scream this time. She slept like the dead, which he supposed was one way to describe what was happening to her—except that the dead don't lie there with their brows furrowed, chasing something through their dreams that keeps dissolving when they reach for it.

Near dawn, she woke without screaming, sat up, and looked straight at him.

"Tarn," she said. "Tarn Vey."

"It's Tam."

"Tarn." She frowned. "Vey. Yes. Tam Vey. Rebel scout. Brannoc sent you. You owe me nothing." She recited it like a merchant reading an inventory list, checking items off. "Good. Still there."

"Still there," he agreed, and hated how much it cost him to say it lightly.

She rose, stamped feeling back into her feet, and began packing her bedroll—correctly this time, blanket around the oilcloth first, and he felt a stab of relief so fierce it embarrassed him. Small things. Hold onto the small things.

They walked. The high road climbed through pine and shadow, and somewhere past midday they came around a shoulder of rock and the whole Thornfell range opened up ahead of them, peak after blue-white peak marching toward the heart of the mountains, toward the Oathvault, toward whatever waited there wearing the Sigil like a crown.

Juniper stopped and looked at it for a long time.

"I used to know the name of every peak," she said. "My teachers made me learn them. There were songs for them. Counting songs." A pause. "Like the ones my mother sang."

"Do you remember the songs?" Tam asked carefully.

"Some of them." Her voice was very quiet. "I remember that they had words. I can't find the words anymore. But I remember the shapes of them, the way they went up and down." She hummed something—four notes, rising, falling, rising again—and it was the loneliest sound he had ever heard, a song with all its meaning stripped out, a shell of music with nothing inside.

Then she squared her shoulders and started walking again, and Tam fell into step behind her, and neither of them said anything else for a long while.

* * *

They camped that night in the ruins of a waystation—old swordspeaker work, the walls still standing to shoulder height, the hearthstone carved with ward-marks that flickered faintly when Juniper passed, like embers breathing. She touched one absently as she set down her pack, and it glowed a little brighter, and she snatched her hand back as if burned.

"They still know me," she said, half to herself. "The stones. The wards. Everything I bound myself away from, and it all still knows me."

"Lucky you," Tam said. "Most people have to earn that kind of devotion."

That earned him the ghost of a smile—there it was, finally, the twitch at the corner of her mouth—and he counted it a victory and banked the fire.

Later, when she slept, he sat by the coals and did something he hadn't done since he was a boy: he prayed. Not to any god in particular—the Compact didn't hold with gods much, and neither, he suspected, did Juniper—but to the mountain itself, to the old sleeping thing beneath the stone, to whatever kept the ledger of names.

*Keep her,* he asked it. *Whatever she's about to give away—keep some of it somewhere. Write it down. Somebody ought to write it down.*

The mountain, naturally, said nothing. Mountains are terrible correspondents.

But the ward-marks on the hearthstone pulsed, slow and steady, like a heartbeat, like a breath, like something listening. And Tam Vey, who had spent his whole life learning to read silence, decided to take that for an answer.

He was still awake when Juniper woke near dawn, sat up, and looked at him with the careful, inventory-reading expression he was coming to dread.

"Tam," she said. Testing it. Checking it off the list.

"Still here," he said.

"Good." She reached for her boots. "Because today we reach the vault approaches, and I need to ask you something, and I need you to answer honestly."

She looked up at him across the dead fire, and her eyes were clear and steady and frightened in a way he'd never seen before—not afraid of Hale, not afraid of the mountain, but afraid of herself, of the shrinking country inside her own skull.

"When we get to the door," Juniper Eriksen said, "if I've forgotten too much—if I can't remember why any of this matters—promise me you'll stop me. Whatever it costs. Promise me you'll stop me before I speak a name I can't afford to lose."

And Tam, who owed her nothing, who had followed her anyway, heard himself say: "I promise."

He only hoped he'd know, when the moment came, what stopping her would even look like—or whether some promises, once made, were just another kind of oath, waiting patiently to take their payment too.
