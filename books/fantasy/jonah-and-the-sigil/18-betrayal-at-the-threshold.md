## 18. Betrayal at the Threshold

Tam Vey had been in exactly two fights in his life, and both of them had been over card games, and both of them he had lost. So it was with a certain professional humility that he found himself standing between Sera Ilves and Juniper Eriksen in the heart of the Oathvault, holding a mining pick he'd grabbed from a wall bracket without really thinking about it, as though a pick were going to settle anything.

The vault did not help. It was a vast hollow beneath the mountain, ribbed with pillars of pale stone that glowed faintly, like the sky before dawn, and every sound they made came back to them changed—softer, older, as if the room were considering each word before returning it. In the center, on a plinth of black basalt, sat the Sigil.

Tam had expected something grander. A crown, maybe. A sword. Instead it was a disc of grey wax the size of a cart wheel, pressed with an impression so deep and intricate it hurt to look at—the seal of a hand far larger than any living person's. And beneath it, beneath all of it, he could *feel* something. Not hear. Feel. Like standing on a bridge over deep water and knowing, without looking, how many fish were under you.

"Step away from it, Juniper," Sera said.

Her voice was calm. That was the worst part. Tam had known Sera for six years, had watched her talk her way past toll-keepers and tax-men and one memorably hostile bear, and she was never calm unless she'd already decided how things were going to end.

"Sera." Juniper didn't turn around. She stood with her back to them, her hand hovering near the Sigil but not touching it, the way you'd hover a hand near a sleeping animal. "We haven't even read the margin notes Maren gave us. We don't know what unbinding does yet."

"We know enough." Sera took a step forward, boots silent on stone that should have echoed. "The dead down there—" she gestured at the floor, at the whole weight of the mountain above them, "—they're conscripts, Juniper. Mountain folk taken by the Crown's grandfathers' grandfathers, bound into oaths they never chose, their voices used to prop up every levy, every gallows, every 'lawful' theft since. You told us that yourself."

"I told you what the vault *is*. I didn't tell you what to do about it."

"You didn't have to." Sera spread her hands, and Tam hated how reasonable she looked, how much he wanted her to be right. "Give the Compact the Sigil. We wield it. Every bound voice answers to us instead of the Crown, and we use that power to break the Crown's claim forever. Free trade. No levies. The passes open to everyone. And when we've won—" her voice gentled, which was somehow worse, "—we release them. All of them. I swear it on Dunmarrow's stones."

"And if winning takes twenty years?" Juniper said quietly. "If it takes forty? Dead men don't get to renegotiate their enlistment, Sera. You know that better than anyone. Your father—"

"Don't." The word cracked like a whip. For just a moment the calm slipped and Tam saw underneath: not ambition. Grief wearing ambition's coat. "My father died in a Crown mine because the Compact was ten farmers with sharpened hoes. Do not tell me what patience costs."

"I'm telling you what it costs *them*," Juniper said. She turned then, finally, and Tam's chest tightened at what her face was doing—trying to be hard and failing, because she'd spent fifteen years learning to be nobody and here was somebody asking her to become a weapon again. "Every year they wait is another year of voices in the dark being spent like coin. You'd be doing exactly what the Crown did. You'd just spend it more kindly."

"Kindly," Sera repeated. "Yes. Kindly. That's what the mountains need. Kindness." She laughed, short and bitter. "Juniper. I like you. That's inconvenient for both of us. Step aside."

"Tam," Juniper said.

And there it was. His name, said plainly, in that low voice that made everything sound like a decision already made. He looked at Sera. He looked at the pick in his hands, which suddenly felt very heavy and very stupid.

He thought about Dunmarrow. About the young rebel, Pell, who'd been tithe-read by Hale until his mind came apart like wet paper, who now flinched at his own name. He thought about Sera feeding orphans out of her own ration share during the famine winter, no fanfare, no witnesses. He thought about the village with no yesterday, and the elder spitting at Juniper's feet, and the look on Tam's own face in that moment—horror, yes, but also, shamefully, a small hot spark of *finally, someone will pay*.

Everybody in this room believed they were acting for the wronged. That was the trap. There was no villain to point at. Just three people and a pick.

"Tam," Sera said too, softer. "You don't have to do this. Nobody's asking you to fight anybody. Just walk to my side of the room. That's all. Walk over here and let two people who understand this thing sort it out."

"That's the problem," Tam said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "Nobody understands it. That's why we're all standing here with our hands out."

He moved. Not toward Sera. He planted himself between her and Juniper, pick held across his body—not raised, not threatening, just *there*, the way a closed door is there.

Sera looked at him for a long moment. Something moved behind her eyes—hurt, real hurt—and Tam realized with a lurch that he'd mattered to her, that this wasn't just strategy, that she'd genuinely believed he'd see reason because she'd chosen him, trained him, trusted him with things she didn't trust to others.

"Oh, Tam," she said. "You always did fall in love with lost causes."

"It's not—" He stopped. Because arguing would take too long, and because she wasn't entirely wrong, and because somewhere in the walls of the vault, faint as breath, something had begun to hum.

* * *

It started as a vibration in his boot soles. Then in his teeth. The pillars brightened by degrees, dawn becoming morning becoming noon-that-wasn't, and the humming resolved into something almost like singing, hundreds of voices far away, singing the same note.

"The moon," Juniper breathed. Through some shaft or fissure high above, a sliver of light fell across the vault floor—a thin white line, sharp as a blade cut. "Maren's count. It's nearly full. Thirteen turns."

"Then we're out of time for speeches," Sera said, and moved.

Later, Tam would try to reconstruct those seconds and fail. Sera hadn't come unarmed—of course she hadn't; she was Sera—and there was a flash of steel and Juniper met her, and the two of them went together into the space before the plinth like dancers who'd rehearsed, except neither of them was dancing. Tam swung the pick at nothing, hit nothing, nearly brained himself on a pillar, and settled for shouting, which felt inadequate but was at least within his skill set.

"STOP! Both of you! There are *dead people watching!*"

Absurdly, it worked—or seemed to. The two women broke apart, breathing hard, circling. And in the pause, in the ringing quiet, Tam heard it clearly for the first time: the voices below weren't singing anymore. They were listening.

"They can hear us," he whispered. "Juniper. They can hear everything."

"Yes," Juniper said. Her eyes never left Sera. "They always could. That's rather the point of them."

"Then hear me," Sera said, loud now, addressing the whole vault, the whole buried dark, arms open like a preacher. "You who were taken. You whose names the Crown spends. I am Sera Ilves, of Dunmarrow, of the Thornfell Compact, and I am offering you a way *out*. Give your strength to us, and we will break the throne that bound you. Freedom first. Release after. Choose me, and—"

"Stop asking them to choose," Juniper said, and her voice broke on it, and Tam saw that she was crying without any change in her expression at all, tears simply present, like weather. "That's all any of you ever did. The Crown asked them to choose obedience. You're asking them to choose rebellion. Nobody ever once asked them if they wanted to *rest*."

"And you'll decide that for them?" Sera shot back. "You, the last of the order that put them down there?"

The words landed. Tam watched them land. He watched Juniper take them the way you take a blow you've been expecting for years—no surprise, just the dull arithmetic of a debt coming due.

"Maybe," Juniper said. "Maybe I'm no better. But I know one thing you don't, Sera. I know what these oaths cost to speak. I paid a memory for one word in the Pass of Hanging Bells, and I can't remember my mother's face anymore. Whatever gets spoken in this room tonight, someone pays. Always. That's the law under this mountain. So ask yourself what your freedom costs, and who you've decided gets to pay it."

And that was when the doors opened.

Not the main doors—they'd sealed those behind them with half of Maren's notes and all of Tam's luck. These were the *other* doors, the ones Juniper had gone pale looking at, the service entrance the old order had used, and through them came torchlight, and boots, many boots, and the particular crisp cadence of men who had marched a long way in the dark and were glad to be done with it.

Inquisitor Hale stepped into the vault's glow and paused, taking inventory: the Sigil, the two women, the fool with the pick. His tithe-device hung at his belt, cracked from the pass, wrapped in cloth, but he carried it anyway, the way a man carries a wound he means to have avenged.

"Lady Eriksen," Hale said. His voice carried beautifully; the vault returned it gentle and vast. "You spoke your name aloud in the Pass of Hanging Bells. Do you know how long I have waited for someone in this country to make a mistake that honest?" He smiled, thin and genuine, and Tam understood with a cold drop in his stomach that Hale was not a cruel man, which made him infinitely more dangerous than a cruel one. "I have a warrant. I have reinforcements. And I have, at long last, jurisdiction. Surrender the Sigil and the woman, and the rest of you may walk out of this mountain."

"She's not ours to surrender," Sera said, and to Tam's astonishment she stepped—not away from Juniper, but *beside* her, blade still drawn, angled now outward. "Neither is the Sigil. This is Compact ground, inquisitor. Deep ground. Older than your warrant."

Hale regarded her with something like sympathy. "Madam Ilves. I read names out of a boy last week. Do you imagine your ground frightens me?"

"No," Sera said. "But the mountain might."

Above them, the sliver of moonlight widened. The humming rose. And on the black plinth, the great grey seal of the Sigil began—very softly, very terribly—to glow from underneath, like a lamp lit behind wax, like a held breath about to end.

Juniper Eriksen closed her eyes.

"Everyone stop talking," she said. "All of you. Please. It's time, and I need to remember how the oath begins."

Tam gripped his useless pick and thought, with the strange clarity of a man watching the thirteenth moon rise through a crack in the world: *whatever she's about to pay, it's going to cost more than any of us has.*
