## 19. The Thirteenth Turn

The moon did not fall from the sky, though Juniper would remember—briefly, in the time she had left to remember anything—that it seemed to lean.

It hung above the Oathvault's broken crown of stone, swollen and low, the color of a bruise going yellow at the edge, and it filled the great shaft of the vault like light filling a cup. Thirteen turns since the courier's blood had dried on the map. Thirteen turns since the word *Sigil* had crossed his lips and ruined her careful, unwritten life. And now the last sliver of the old moon slid into the new one's lap, and the mountain exhaled.

The crack began as a sound she felt in her teeth before she heard it—a deep, patient groan, like an old woman settling into a chair she intended never to leave. Then a hairline of white light split the floor of the vault, running out from the Sigil's pedestal in branching veins, and through those veins came *voices*. Not words yet. Just voices, hundreds of them, layered like sediment, murmuring the way a river murmurs under ice.

"Juniper." Tam's hand closed on her arm. His fingers were shaking. She could feel it even through her sleeve, and some small, stubborn, human part of her noted that his hands were always steady on a rope, steady on a blade, and that this was the first time she'd felt them tremble. "Tell me you have a plan. Tell me you have *any* plan."

"I have a plan," she said.

"Is it a good plan?"

"It's a terrible plan."

He let out a breath that was half laugh, half prayer. "Good. Those are the only kind you've ever had that worked."

Across the vault floor, Sera Ilves stood with the Sigil cradled against her chest—the way you'd hold an infant, or a bomb, and Juniper supposed there wasn't much difference anymore. Sera's face was lit from below by the cracking light, and it made her look older and younger at once: the revolutionary's certainty stripped away, leaving just a tired woman from the mountains who had spent her whole life being levied, taxed, conscripted, and named by other people.

Behind them all, at the vault mouth, torchlight. Hale's voice carrying down the stone in that maddeningly reasonable tone of his: "Swordspeaker Eriksen. The warrant stands. Give me the seal and no one else need die tonight."

"He really does believe that," Tam muttered. "That's the worst part. He *believes* it."

Juniper looked at the three of them—Hale with his law, Sera with her freedom, Tam with his loyalty—and then down at the light seeping up between her boots, and she understood, with the terrible clarity the old archivists used to call *oath-sight*, that every one of them was right.

That was the trap. Not that they were villains. That they weren't.

* * *

The Crown wanted the dead's voices because legitimacy was a story, and stories needed witnesses, and what witness was more unimpeachable than ten thousand conscripted mountain dead whose oaths, bound and recorded, said *yes, we gave freely, yes, the Crown speaks for us*? Every coronation for four hundred years had been underwritten by men who had never been asked.

The Compact wanted the same voices because freedom was also a story, and Sera meant to turn those ten thousand bound oaths into a weapon that would shatter the Crown's claim forever—and Juniper could not even say she was wrong. The mountain folk had bled for generations. Wasn't it justice, to make the dead's testimony finally serve the living who shared their blood?

And herself—

She looked at her own hands. Sword-callused. Map-stained. Hands that had held the Sigil for thirteen turns without once hearing the voices inside it, because the voices only spoke to someone willing to command them, and some last stubborn scrap of her oath had kept her deaf.

But she had felt the temptation. In the Pass of Hanging Bells, when the oath had come out of her like water finding its old channel—when the power had fit her hand *like it never left*—she had felt, for one bright and shameful heartbeat, that she could fix everything. That she could speak the mountains back into shape. That she alone knew how.

No faction could be trusted with the dead. Not the Crown, which would keep them leashed. Not the Compact, which would set them loose as ammunition. Not herself, who had already tasted how sweet the leash felt in her own grip.

"There has to be another way," Sera said. She hadn't moved from the pedestal, but her voice cracked along with the floor. "Juniper. There are archives. There are rites. Maren will find something—"

"Maren found what there is to find." Juniper walked toward the pedestal, and the branching light parted around her feet like water around a stone, and the murmuring beneath the floor rose to meet her, the way a crowd rises when it recognizes someone. "Thirteen turns. The Unbinding is the only rite that doesn't leave the voices *somewhere*. Every other ending just moves them into new hands."

"And what does it cost?" Sera's voice was sharp now, frightened sharp. "Maren said—she said the payment was—"

"My whole remembered life." Juniper said it plainly, because plainness was a kindness, and because if she dressed it up in ceremony she might lose her nerve. "Every memory I have. Everything I am. The speaker pays with the entire self, so that nothing of the speaker remains to pick up what's been put down."

The silence that followed had weight. Even the voices below seemed to hush and listen.

"No," Tam said.

"Tam—"

"*No.*" He stepped in front of her, which was absurd, because she was the swordswoman and he was a scout with a bad knee and a good heart, and he knew it, and he did it anyway. "You don't get to do this. We didn't—all of this, the mines, the bells, the village that spat at you—we didn't walk all this way so you could erase yourself. You were going to *stay*. You said—" His voice caught. "At the high road. You said after this you'd learn to be somebody. Somebody new. Somebody who wasn't hiding."

"I know what I said." Her throat ached. "I lied. Or—I hoped, which is nearly the same thing but costs less."

Below their feet, the crack widened. A slab the size of a barn door tilted and slid, and the light that poured up through the gap was full of faces—not ghosts, not quite, but impressions, the shapes voices make when they've been pressed flat for centuries. Ten thousand conscripted dead, murmuring in the dark, waiting to find out who owned them next.

"Juniper." Sera had come around the pedestal. Her eyes were wet and furious. "If you do this, the Compact loses the Sigil. The Crown keeps its story. Everything we fought for—"

"You'll still have the truth," Juniper said. "When the voices go free, they go free *speaking*. They'll say what was done to them, once, all together, and then they'll be gone. Hale will hear it. His soldiers will hear it. You'll hear it. No one can wield a testimony that's already been given." She almost smiled. "You wanted the mountains freed, Sera. This frees them. It just doesn't free them *for* anyone. That's the part nobody wants."

"And you?" Tam's voice was very quiet. "Who frees you?"

She reached out and touched his cheek, once, briefly, memorizing the stubble and the scar over his eyebrow and the way he leaned into her hand like he'd been waiting his whole life for permission.

"Nobody," she said gently. "That's rather the point."

* * *

There was a form to it. Of course there was; her order had loved forms the way rivers love downhill. She knelt at the pedestal, laid the map open beside the Sigil, and began the old gestures—thumb to sternum, palm to stone, the stance her teachers had called *the listener's posture*, feet bare against the rock because oaths traveled through skin.

The vault went quiet around her. Even Hale's men at the mouth of the shaft had stopped shouting. She was distantly aware of Tam kneeling behind her, refusing to leave, refusing to help, simply *staying*, which was somehow both the most comforting and most unbearable thing he could have done.

She spoke the opening words, and the mountain took her measure.

It started small, as payments do. The name of her first pony—gone, like a coin slipping through a hole in a pocket. The taste of her mother's honey-bread. She gasped; the rite drank deeper. The layout of the house in Thornfell where she'd been born. Her teacher Aldric's voice saying *"again, slower"*—and with it, his face, and with that, the whole gray stone hall of her childhood, dissolving like sugar in warm water.

She had expected it to hurt. It didn't, exactly. It was worse than hurting. It was *lightening*. Each memory taken left her simpler, cleaner, closer to the surface of herself, and she could feel how easy it would be to let the rest go—to slide out of Juniper Eriksen like a coat left on a chair, and be done with the weight of her forever.

*That,* she thought, with the last cold corner of her mind, *is why the rite demands everything. Because halfway is escape. And escape is what I've been doing for fifteen years.*

So she stopped resisting, and stopped fleeing, and did the thing she had never once done in her entire careful, hidden life.

She gave freely.

* * *

The memories went in order, whether she chose the order or the rite did: oldest first, deepest first. The smell of the training yard after rain. The oath she'd spoken at fourteen, kneeling among the wardstones, so proud she thought she'd burst. The purge—the smoke over the passes, the order scattered like ash, the years of learning to be forgettable, to be *no one*, trading salt on roads only she remembered, keeping her name folded away like a blade wrapped in oilcloth.

Each one, as it left her, rose up through the crack in the floor and joined the murmur below. She understood then, dimly, beautifully: the rite was not a payment into a void. It was a *witnessing*. Her life was being read aloud to the dead, so that when they rose, they would rise knowing they had not been alone—that one of their jailers' daughters had come down into the dark to pay her family's debt in the only coin that mattered.

Somewhere behind her, Tam was crying. She knew it without turning, the way you know weather coming. She wanted to tell him: *don't waste grief on me, spend it on them, they earned it and I'm only spending myself.* But speech was getting expensive. Words cost memories now, and she was hoarding what remained for the final clause.

The moon leaned further. The vault's ribs of stone groaned and shed dust in silver sheets. At the shaft's mouth, torches wavered—Hale's men coming down, boots ringing on the stairs, too late or exactly on time, depending on how you counted.

Juniper knelt in the widening light with almost nothing left. She was aware of her body the way you're aware of a borrowed coat. She knew there had been a man at a ford—knew it the way you know a fact from a book, no face attached, only blood on stones and a word: *Sigil*. She knew someone knelt behind her whom she loved. She could not have said his name to save her life, and the not-knowing broke something in her that was not a memory, because it was the last thing she had left, and she gave that too.

Then only the final clause remained, and the rite waited for it with the patience of mountains.

One memory still glowed at the bottom of her, the last coal in a banked fire: the reason. Not the mission, not the Sigil, not the war. Just the simple, stubborn truth that had carried her down every road since the courier died in her arms—that no one should own the dead, and that someone had to make sure, and that she had decided, long ago and alone, that the someone would be her.

She took a breath she would not finish, and opened her mouth to speak herself out of the world—

—and the floor of the vault split wide, and ten thousand voices rose at once, and the first of them, clear as a bell across water, was calling a name.

Not hers.

A name she did not know, and yet her emptying heart lurched toward it like a hand reaching for a door it had once lived behind:

*"Jonah."*
