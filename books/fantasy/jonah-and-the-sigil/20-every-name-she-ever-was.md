## 20. Every Name She Ever Was

The vault was singing.

Juniper had no word for it at first—only a pressure in her teeth, a hum in the marrow of her, as if the mountain had been holding one long note for three hundred years and had finally, finally been given permission to breathe. The walls of the Oathvault were not stone. She understood that now. They were *names*. Ten thousand of them, pressed flat and layered like leaves in autumn, glowing faintly where the cracks let light through: the recorded oaths of the conscripted dead, every mountain man and woman the Crown had ever bound into service, their voices folded and stored to fuel the throne's legitimacy, generation after generation.

And the moon was finishing its thirteenth turn.

Above them, through the shattered seam in the vault's crown, the moonlight came down thin and silver and wrong, and the first crack widened with a sound like ice on a spring river.

"Juniper." Tam's hand found her shoulder. His palm was bleeding; he'd caught it on the door mechanism, or on Sera's knife, or on something else—she couldn't remember which, and that frightened her more than anything else in this room. "Whatever you're going to do. Do it now."

Behind them, down the throat of the entry passage, boots. Many boots. The ring of drawn steel. Hale's voice, calm as a ledger being balanced: "Seal the exits. No one is harmed until I have the Sigil."

Sera stood three paces away with the Sigil itself—the map, the seal, the thing that had started all of it—clutched against her chest like a child she meant to carry out of a burning house. Her face was wet. Juniper noticed that. Even now, even reaching for the weapon, Sera Ilves was crying, because she believed, honestly and completely, that she was stealing freedom for her people.

That was the whole horror of it, Juniper thought. Everyone here was right. And everyone here would make it wrong.

"No," Juniper said.

Sera's jaw tightened. "You don't get to decide—"

"I'm not deciding." Juniper stepped forward, away from Tam, away from both of them, toward the center of the vault where the light fell thickest. "No one is deciding. That's the point. That has always been the point."

She knelt. The floor was warm under her palms, warmer than stone should be, and when she pressed her hands flat against it she felt the names beneath, pressing back, patient as seeds.

She knew the oath. Of course she knew it. Maren had read it to her from the margin of the map in Dunmarrow, in the archivist's dry, careful voice, and Juniper had heard it and thought, with a strange distant relief, *oh good, there is a way*, the way you think about your own funeral. The Unbinding. It required exactly what everything in her life had required: payment. But not a memory. Not a face, not a name, not a season.

All of them. Everything remembered. The whole of her, poured out.

*So that no one—crown, rebel, or god—can ever wield what she was.*

Tam crouched beside her. "What does it cost?" he asked quietly. He'd learned to ask that question. He'd watched her lose her mother's face to a single spoken oath on the high road and say nothing about it for two days, and he'd learned.

"Everything," she said. "It's fine."

"It is not *fine*—"

"Tam." She looked at him, and she made herself smile, because if this was the last thing he'd carry of her, she wanted it to be a smile and not a warning. "I spent fifteen years trying to erase myself badly. Ledger by ledger, town by town, making myself small enough to slip through the world unnoticed. This is just—" she searched for the words, "—doing it properly. As a gift instead of an escape."

"That's the worst sentence anyone has ever said to me."

"I know." Her voice cracked, and she let it. "I'm sorry. Hold my hand? For the beginning. I'd like the beginning to be held."

He took her hand. His grip was steady, which surprised her; his eyes were not.

Down the passage, the boots were close now. Light flared—Hale's lanterns, and behind them the grey ranks of his men filling the vault mouth like water filling a cup. Hale himself stopped at the threshold, tithe-device in hand, its brass ribs still spiderwebbed from where her oath had cracked it at the Pass of Hanging Bells. He looked at Juniper kneeling in the silver light, and for the first time since the ford, since the valley inn, since the whole long hunt, he looked uncertain.

"What are you doing?" he asked.

"The right thing," Juniper said. "Finally. Watch."

She closed her eyes. She spoke.

* * *

The first oath was small, the way the first step off a cliff is small.

*"I unbind what was bound. I pay in remembrance. Take first my childhood."*

The vault drank it in. And her childhood went—not violently, not torn, but gently, the way snow leaves a field in spring: one morning it is simply no longer there, and the ground beneath is bare and ready.

She felt it go and she wept without knowing why, which was the shape of the whole thing, she realized—the grief arriving ahead of the memory, so that she mourned each loss fully before she forgot what she was mourning. Her mother's kitchen. The smell of bread and lamp oil. A dog, some kind of dog, brown, that had slept against her feet. Gone. Gone. Gone. Each one a small warm coal lifted out of her and set down somewhere she could not follow.

And the vault answered.

The walls began to speak.

Not the hum anymore—voices. Thousands of them, layered, overlapping, rising out of the name-stone like steam off a winter river. Men's voices, women's voices, voices young and cracked and old. They spoke their own names first, each one, in turn, in the old mountain cadence, the cadence Juniper had been taught and sworn never to use: *Aldric Vane. Merta Coalharrow. Bren of the Upper Fells.* Names that had not been spoken aloud in three hundred years, because speaking them had been forbidden, because the binding required silence, because the Crown's legitimacy rested on ten thousand people who could never say who they were.

They were saying who they were.

"Oh," said Maren's voice in Juniper's fading memory, *oh, that's what it holds.*

The dead kept speaking. They told it all. The conscription levies, the villages emptied, the oaths forced on starving families in exchange for grain. The wars they'd been marched to. The massacre at the village with no yesterday—and here the voices rose together, dozens at once, telling it plainly, horribly, *the soldiers came in harvest season and no one was left to bring in the wheat,* and Juniper heard Tam make a sound beside her like a man taking a wound.

And then the voices turned, all of them, and looked—at her? at the room?—and one voice, older than the others, said:

*Witnessed. We witnessed. We were made to forget, and now we remember, and we choose.*

"We choose," said another.

"We choose," said a hundred.

"We choose to go free."

* * *

Hale had lowered his sword.

Juniper saw it through a haze now, through the thinning gauze of herself—she was losing the order, losing Thornfell, losing the passes road by road, and it should have been terror but it was not, it was like setting down a pack she'd carried so long she'd forgotten it was heavy. She saw Hale standing in the vault mouth with his blade pointed at the floor, and his face was the face of a man reading his own family's confession.

Because the dead were telling his part too. The inquisitions. The purge of the swordspeakers—not because the order was evil, though it had done evil, but because the order knew where the vault was and what it held, and a throne cannot rule over a truth it hasn't buried. Hale's own predecessors. Hale's warrant. Hale's device, built to pull names out of living minds the way the Sigil pulled voices out of dead ones.

He heard it. All of it. He stood very straight, the way he did everything, and then, slowly, he knelt, and laid the sword on the stone, and the sound it made was very small in that enormous room.

"I didn't know," he said. It wasn't an excuse. It was worse than an excuse—it was the plain truth of a just man who had served an unjust thing competently for thirty years. "I did not know."

*Now you do,* said the dead, gently, almost kindly. *Witnessing is the whole of the law. Go and witness.*

* * *

Sera was still holding the Sigil.

Juniper turned to her—or turned toward where Sera had been, her vision going soft at the edges now, the world becoming a painting of itself—and found the rebel leader on her knees too, though not in surrender. Clutching the map-seal, shaking.

"If I destroy it," Sera whispered, "they're free. If I keep it, they're an army. An army of the wronged. Don't you see—that's the only way the mountains ever win—"

"Sera." Juniper's voice came out strange to her, thinner, younger-sounding, some earlier version of herself surfacing as the later ones drained away. "Listen to them. Listen. They already chose."

"But the Crown will—"

"The Crown is kneeling." Juniper nodded toward Hale. "Look what witnessing did in one minute. You've fought fifteen years for less than that."

Sera looked at the dead-light on the walls. At Hale, unarmed. At Tam, holding Juniper's hand like a man holding a rope over a drop. And then, with a sound halfway between a sob and a laugh, she opened her hands, and the Sigil slid from her fingers onto the warm stone, and the moment it touched the vault floor it began to come apart—not breaking, *unbinding*, wax and blood and old gold dissolving into the same silver light, joining the voices, adding its own small history to the record.

*Paid in full,* said the vault.

* * *

*"Take the rest,"* Juniper whispered.

The order. Gone—she felt the training leave her like water leaving a cupped hand, the stances, the forms, the seven ways to fall and rise. The purge. Gone. Brannoc's den, the smugglers' firelight, gone. The courier at the ford—she clung to that one a moment longer, the bloodstained map, the gasped word, *Sigil*, the stranger whose face she suddenly could not picture and whose name she realized she had never known—

*Jonah,* said the vault, softly, filling the gap she'd left. *His name was Jonah. He was one of us. He chose.*

Oh, she thought. Oh, that's who you were. Thank you. Thank you for choosing me.

Then that went too.

Her name. She saved it for last, the way you save the best bite, and she held Tam's hand harder, hard enough to hurt, and she said the final words around it:

*"My name is Juniper Eriksen. I unbind myself. Let no one wield what I was. Let what I was be no one's."*

And the vault took it, kindly, the way you take a bird from a child's hands, and she was—

She was—

There was a woman kneeling in a bright place. She was holding someone's hand. The someone was crying, and she thought she should probably comfort him, though she couldn't have said how she knew him, only that his hand fit hers the way a path fits a foot, familiar without a reason.

The voices were leaving. Rising up through the crack in the vault's crown, up through the mountain, thousands of them, thinner now, lighter, going wherever freed things go. One of them paused on the way out—a young voice, laughing—and brushed past her like a hand ruffling her hair, and she laughed too, though she didn't know at what.

The light faded. The stone cooled. Somewhere above, the moon finished its turn and began, innocently, another one.

The woman stood up. Her knees ached. She looked around at the strangers in the ruined room—a dark-haired man with bloody knuckles, a broad woman with wet cheeks, a grey officer kneeling by a sword—and she smiled at all of them politely, the way you smile at fellow travelers.

"Sorry," she said. "Is there a caravan due through the pass? Only I feel like I'm supposed to be guiding someone."

The dark-haired man made a noise that was not quite a laugh and not quite anything else, and pressed something into her hand—a carved token, wood, smooth from handling—and said, hoarsely:

"There will be. Come spring. I'll walk with you as far as the ford."

She looked at the token. It was a little bell, she thought. Or maybe a flower. It made her chest ache in a way that was not unpleasant, the way certain bends in certain roads ached, and she decided, with the easy practicality of a person with no past at all, that she liked it very much.

"All right," she said. "The ford. I think I know the way."

And she did.
