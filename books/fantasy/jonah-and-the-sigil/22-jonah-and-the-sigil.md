## 22. Jonah and the Sigil

Maren had spent forty years among books and only six among people, and she had come to a firm conclusion: books were easier to grieve.

A book that burned was gone, and you mourned it, and you were done. But a village that remembered nothing—that was a wound that kept reopening in new places. She watched it happen every market day in Hollowmere. A grandmother would pause mid-sentence, hand pressed to her breastbone, because some story she was telling had brushed against the hole where the massacre used to be. A child would ask why the old chapel stood at the edge of town instead of the center, and no one could answer, and everyone would go quiet in that particular way that meant *we do not know and we are afraid to find out.*

So when the elder, Odile Brant, came to her with a blank ledger and said, "You're the archivist. Tell me how to begin," Maren had nearly wept into her tea.

"You begin badly," she'd said. "That's the secret nobody tells you. Every honest record begins with someone admitting they don't know something."

Odile had written that down. Maren suspected she'd carved it somewhere too. The woman took notes like siege engineering.

* * *

The first Reading of Names happened one year after the vault collapsed, on the autumn equinox, in the chapel that everyone now knew had been built over a mass grave—because Odile had told them, standing in the square, voice steady as a plumb line, while half the village wept and the other half argued and old Tam Vey sat on the well's edge sharpening a knife he never seemed to use for anything.

Maren had expected anger. There was some. But what surprised her, what she wrote about later in her own private journal with shaking hands, was the hunger. The villagers of Hollowmere had lived their whole lives with a shape missing from their memory, like a tongue searching a gap where a tooth had been. When Odile read the first name aloud—*Hesta Marrow, shepherd, who died shielding her sister's children*—an old woman in the front row made a sound Maren had never heard before and hoped never to forget: grief and relief braided so tightly together you couldn't tell which was holding up which.

They read ninety-one names that first year. It took three hours. Nobody left.

Afterward, Odile found Maren by the chapel door and pressed a cup of cider into her hands.

"They'll want this every year," Odile said. "Forever, probably."

"Records want tending," Maren agreed. "That's their whole nature. Neglect them and they rot; overfeed them and they bloat. You'll want a successor."

"I have one." Odile nodded toward a girl of perhaps ten who was copying names onto strips of paper with the ferocious concentration of a child who has recently discovered that writing things down makes them real. "She asked me yesterday if the dead mind being read aloud. I told her I didn't know. She said—" Odile's mouth twitched, "—she said, 'Well, somebody ought to ask them.'"

Maren laughed until her ribs hurt. It was, she reflected, exactly the sort of question the swordspeakers would have answered with a wardstone and a binding. The girl had simply decided to ask.

Perhaps that was how the mountains healed. Not with oaths. With questions.

* * *

By the third year, the Reading had grown beyond Hollowmere. Folk came up from the valley towns, from Dunmarrow—even, in small and nervous numbers, from the Crown garrisons, soldiers in plain clothes who stood at the back and did not sing and did not leave early either. The Compact sent delegates. Sera Ilves herself came once, sat through all four hours of names, and departed without a word, though Maren noticed she left an offering at the chapel: a rebel armband, folded neatly, laid down like a surrendered weapon.

The list grew, too. That was the strange mercy of it. Freed voices, dispersing through the mountains as the Sigil's dust settled into stone, apparently carried their names with them—and sometimes those names surfaced. A shepherd near Greywater Pass dreamed of a soldier who'd died in the first war and woke knowing his name. A Dunmarrow miner hummed a burial song no one had taught him and, when asked, recited eleven names to go with it. Each year Odile added them, verified them against Maren's fragments of archive, and read them alongside the rest.

"The dead are filing themselves in," Maren observed dryly, in the fourth year. "Better record-keepers than most living archivists, frankly."

It was in the fifth year that the name appeared that stopped everything.

* * *

Maren was sitting beside Odile on the chapel steps afterward, feet aching, throat raw, watching villagers drift home in the blue evening light, when the girl—the successor, Brenna, now thirteen and insufferably competent—came running out with the year's additions clutched in her fist.

"Elder! Elder, there's one we can't place. It came up twice—once from a dream at Thornfell and once from—" Brenna consulted her papers, "—a caravan woman. A guide. She was passing through and heard the reading and just... gave us a name. Said it came to her at the ford."

Odile held out her hand. Brenna put the paper in it.

Maren, glancing over Odile's shoulder, felt the world tilt very slightly, the way a shelf tilts when you remove one book too many.

The name was *Jonah.*

And beneath it, in Brenna's careful hand, the note the caravan woman had given: *He carried something important. He chose not to be taken alive. He was one of them—a swordspeaker. He was the last but one.*

"Last but one," Odile repeated slowly. "Last but one means there was another after him."

Maren said nothing. Her heart was doing something complicated and arrhythmic.

Because she knew. Of course she knew. She had spent years piecing together the account from every fragment: the courier who'd bled out at a river ford with a map sealed in wax; the inquisitor's records describing a pursuit through the eastern passes; and finally, from a captured Crown dispatch dated two days after the courier's death, a single chilling line—*the second swordspeaker located and neutralized; resisted tithe-reading; self-terminated rather than yield his name.*

Jonah had been hunted. Jonah had known what the tithe-device would pull out of him—not just his name but every oath he'd ever spoken, every bound thing he knew how to wake. And so he had chosen death, and a map, and a stranger at a ford, and had bet the whole war on a woman who'd spent fifteen years trying to become nobody.

He had bet on Juniper Eriksen. And he had won.

"He should be on the list," Brenna said, with the fierce simplicity of youth. "If he was one of the order, but he *helped*—he should be read with the others. Shouldn't he?"

Odile looked at Maren. Maren looked at the mountains, which were doing their usual evening trick of turning gold and then rose and then the color of old bruises, beautiful and unbothered, keeping whatever counsel mountains keep.

"Yes," Maren said at last. "Read him. Next year. Last of the names." She paused. "Actually—no. Read him *first.* Let him open the door for the rest. He was rather good at doors, I suspect."

Brenna grinned and ran off to amend the ledger, and Odile squeezed Maren's hand once, wordlessly, and went inside, and Maren stayed on the steps a long while, thinking about a man she had never met, whose entire life fit into three lines of a Crown dispatch and one name on a strip of paper, and who had nonetheless changed the history of the mountains more thoroughly than any king or rebel or god.

Memory survives as story, she wrote that night. Story survives as strangers. Strangers survive as us. Somewhere in the margins she added, smaller: *I hope wherever he is, he knows the map arrived.*

* * *

The caravan woman.

Maren couldn't stop thinking about her. She asked Brenna for details, and got them in abundance, because Brenna noticed everything and forgot nothing, which Maren privately considered the finest possible quality in a successor and mildly terrifying in a teenager.

"Tall-ish? Brown hair going grey at the temples. Walks like—" Brenna demonstrated, shoulders loose, weight forward, "—like she could kill you but probably won't. Guides salt caravans through the high passes. Everyone says she knows roads that aren't on any map. Oh, and she's funny. Dry, you know? Marta the wool-trader says she once told a bandit—"

"A bandit?"

"—well, tried to rob the caravan, and she talked him out of it so thoroughly he ended up helping them fix an axle and left apologizing. Marta swears it's true."

Maren closed her eyes. *Talked him out of it.* Fifteen years ago, a woman had erased herself from every ledger in the kingdom, and now she was out there being unforgettable in the most Juniper way imaginable: quietly, competently, and with devastating wit.

"She comes through every season," Brenna continued. "Never stays. Never gives a name anyone recognizes. But—" Brenna hesitated, and for a moment she looked less like a competent teenager and more like a girl carrying something heavy. "Every season, at the ford, there's a token. Carved wood. Little shapes—bells, mostly, and once a door. Nobody leaves them that anyone's seen. They're always gone by morning. Marta thinks the caravan woman takes them."

"Does she now," Maren said softly.

"And last equinox," Brenna said, "she stayed for the whole Reading. Stood at the back. And when we read Jonah's name—first of the names, like you said—she..." Brenna frowned, hunting for the words. "She didn't cry. But she put her hand flat on her chest, like this—" Brenna demonstrated again, palm over sternum, "—and held it there until the next name. Like something in there was knocking."

* * *

Maren met her the following spring.

It was pure accident, which Maren considered fitting; the whole saga had been built on accidents—a dying man at a ford, a map in the wrong hands, a moon turning one time too many. She had traveled to Thornfell to consult the wardstones for the expanding list, and she was resting at the ford itself, eating bread and feeling her age, when a salt caravan came down the western trail and its guide dropped back from the head of the line to check the crossing stones.

Up close, the woman was exactly as described and entirely indescribable: ordinary in every feature, memorable in every movement, with eyes that had the specific patience of someone who has watched many things end and decided to keep walking anyway.

"Deep water mid-channel this year," the woman said, by way of greeting. "Mind the third stone. It lies."

"It does look dishonest," Maren agreed.

Something flickered across the woman's face—amusement, quickly banked, like a lamp turned low. She turned to signal her caravan, and Maren saw the moment she registered who Maren was: the Compact archivist, the woman from the readings, the keeper of lists. Her shoulders changed. Not fear. Something older and wearier than fear.

"You read the names," the woman said. Not a question.

"Every year. Someone has to, and I'm cheap."

The huff of breath might have been a laugh. The woman crouched at the water's edge, testing the current with two fingers, and for a while neither spoke. The ford murmured. Somewhere upstream, a bird insisted on its opinions.

"I knew him," the woman said at last, still watching the water. "The first name. I never learned the rest of him—his childhood, his people, why he chose what he chose. He was bleeding and there wasn't time. There's never time." Her fingers curled, just slightly, in the cold water. "But I remember his hands. Steadying mine around the map. And I remember thinking, *this man is giving me the only thing he has left,* and I didn't even have the decency to learn his face properly before—"

She stopped. Pressed her lips together.

"There's a village," Maren said gently, "where they keep his name now. They say it first, every year. He opens the door for all the others. I chose that deliberately, though I confess I've never been certain why it felt right."

The woman was quiet a long moment. Then: "Because he was good at doors," she said, and her voice cracked clean down the middle on the last word, and she turned her face away toward the water so Maren could pretend not to notice, which was the greatest kindness one stranger can do another.

When she looked back, her composure was reassembled, though imperfectly, like a book repaired by someone who loved it more than skill allowed.

"I don't remember much," she said. "People assume that's a tragedy. Mostly it's just... quiet. But some things surface. At certain bends in the road I feel—" she touched her chest, briefly, the same gesture Brenna had described, "—something. Grief without a shape. Joy without a reason. Once, at a pass with old bells, I heard wind through them and I *wept*, right there in front of twelve salt merchants, and I couldn't tell you why to save my life." She smiled, crooked and rueful. "I've decided that's allowed. Feeling things without receipts."

"That may be the wisest sentence spoken in these mountains in a century," Maren said, "and I include my own archives in that assessment."

The woman laughed then, really laughed, and for one unguarded instant her face was young, and Maren thought: *there she is. Whoever she was. Still in there, surfacing like a name on a strip of paper.*

The caravan called from the far bank. The woman rose, brushed off her knees, and paused.

"The reading," she said. "Next equinox. Will you—when you say his name—will you say it kindly? He was—" she faltered, groping for a word, and landed on one that seemed to surprise her as much as Maren, "—he was *my friend.* I think. I think he must have been. You don't die for a stranger."

"He was read kindly last year," Maren said, "and will be every year after. That's rather the point of the whole enterprise."

The woman nodded once, sharply, the way soldiers nod, and waded out into the ford, sure-footed on the lying stones, and Maren watched her go until the trail took her—until the bend in the road, the one that would carry some unaccountable feeling she would never be able to explain.

Then Maren opened her satchel, took out her journal, and wrote:

*Saw her today. She remembers nothing and grieves everything, and she is, I think, the happiest person I have ever met who has lost the most. The dead are free. The land is waking gently, like an old dog deciding to trust again. And the woman walks the passes with no name and all the grace of one.*

*Jonah, whoever you were, wherever the voices go: your map arrived. Your friend finished the road. Come spring, we will say your name first, and kindly, and a girl with fierce handwriting will write it down so that no one ever has to hold it alone again.*

*The record continues.*

Far up the western trail, a caravan moved through the gold light, and at its head walked a woman with no past, humming a tune she didn't know she knew—and at the sound of her own humming, she stopped, one hand rising halfway to her chest, almost remembering—

—and then the wind came down off the mountain, kind and cool, and she smiled at nothing in particular, and walked on.
