## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The valley had a way of making you forget you were forgetting.

Isandro Almeida had lived in Stillwater for fifteen years, and in all that time he had never once dreamed. He noticed this the way a man notices a missing tooth—not with pain, exactly, but with the tongue's constant, restless probing of an absence. Other people talked about their dreams at the bakery counter, over the fish stalls, across the washing lines that sagged between the crooked houses like strings of gray pearls. *I dreamt of my mother last night. I dreamt I could fly.* Isandro only nodded and said nothing, because to speak of dreams would be to admit he had none, and to admit he had none would invite questions, and questions were the one crop that refused to grow in the Valley of Stillness, and he intended to keep it that way.

He was a mender of things. That was his trade, or rather, the trade he had assembled for himself out of spare parts, the way he assembled everything: patiently, quietly, with hands that never shook. He fixed clocks that had no business keeping time in a valley where time moved like honey off a cold spoon. He mended nets. He re-thatched roofs. He sharpened scythes until they sang. The villagers called him Tam, though everyone knew it wasn't his name—it was simply what people called a man who had arrived with no name worth speaking—and he answered to it the way a dog answers to any whistle, without complaint and without meaning.

Fifteen years. He had been twenty-three when he came down the eastern pass with frostbitten feet and nothing but the clothes on his back, and now he was thirty-eight, and the mirror told him so in lines around his eyes and a thread of silver at his temples that he pretended not to see. Fifteen years of bread and river-water, of the same four seasons turning like a millwheel, of faces he knew down to the freckles. Fifteen years of safety.

And fifteen years of fear, which was the part he never spoke about, not even to himself, not even in the privacy of his own skull where all his other secrets lived.

Isandro Almeida was afraid of thunderstorms.

It was, he understood, an absurd thing for a grown man—a mender, a fixer, a man who could take apart a clockwork loom blindfolded—to be afraid of weather. But there it was. When storms gathered over the peaks that ringed the valley like a cupped hand, when the clouds piled up gray and bruised above the rim stones, Isandro's heart did something wrong. It didn't race. It *stalled*, the way his own heart had stalled once, long ago, in a life he could not remember, and ever since then the sound of thunder—the first low grumble rolling down from the high country like a barrel tumbling downstairs—made him go still as a hare under a hawk's shadow. Not trembling. Worse than trembling. A kind of listening, deep and involuntary, as if some buried part of him were waiting for the sky to say something.

The sky never said anything. That was the whole point of the valley.

*Stillness*, they called it, and meant it literally. The Valley of Stillness sat in the elbow of the River Vess like a stone in a stream's palm, and things behaved differently here, though no one liked to talk about how, exactly. Lightning did not strike inside the valley; it bent around the rim like water around a boulder. Bells rung here made almost no sound—or rather, they made the sound, but the sound seemed to fall flat a few feet from the bell tower, exhausted, unwilling to travel. Prayers spoken at the old shrine by the ferry landing went nowhere at all. Everyone knew this. It was common knowledge the way the depth of the ford was common knowledge: useful, unremarkable, old as the hills themselves.

The gods are silent here, the old folk said. Have been silent longer than anyone living can remember. Some said the valley was cursed. Some said blessed. Odile, the shrine keeper, who was older than the church she tended and twice as stubborn, said only: *the valley keeps what the valley keeps,* and refused to elaborate, and if you pressed her she'd look at you with those pale, watery eyes and say, *Some doors are locked for a reason, child,* which was not an answer but had the weight of one.

So Isandro kept his head down and his hands busy and his secrets sewn into the lining of his days, and for fifteen years it worked.

Mostly.

* * *

There was one person in Stillwater who came closer to seeing him than anyone else, and she was the ferryman's daughter, and her name was Wren.

Wren Ashby was twenty-six, quick-tongued, sun-brown, with her father's broad shoulders and her mother's habit of laughing a half-second before telling a joke, as if she couldn't hold the amusement in long enough to land it properly. She ran the ferry since her father's knees had gone bad—poled the flat-bottomed boat across the Vess two dozen times a day, hauling farmers and firewood and once, memorably, a bull calf that had opinions about watercraft. She was the closest thing Stillwater had to a mayor, a judge, and a town crier all rolled into one, because everything that happened in the valley happened on the river, and everything on the river passed through Wren's hands.

She had decided, somewhere in the second year of his residence, that Tam the mender was lonely. This was incorrect—he was not lonely, he was *hidden*, which was a different animal entirely—but Wren Ashby had never met a distinction she couldn't ignore, and so she appointed herself his friend with the cheerful tyranny of someone who has never once doubted her right to do so.

"You eat like a man being punished," she informed him one evening, early on, planting herself at his table with a basket of plums and refusing to leave until he'd eaten three. "Nobody eats bread that slowly unless they're either miserable or counting something. Which is it?"

"Counting," he'd said, and she'd laughed, and somehow that had been that. Fifteen years of it. She brought him plums in autumn and pickled eel in winter and gossip in every season, delivered with the relish of a woman who understood that information was a currency and she was the richest woman in the valley. In return he fixed her father's knee-brace, her boat's cracked tiller, the little wind-chime of river-glass she hung from the ferry awning that she claimed told fortunes, though its fortune-telling consisted entirely of clinking when the wind blew, which it always did, which meant it foretold wind, which Wren insisted was deeper wisdom than it sounded.

They never spoke of anything that mattered. That was the unspoken treaty between them, ratified years ago without either of them signing it. She did not ask where he came from. He did not ask why a woman like her had never married, though the whole valley asked constantly and she answered constantly, with jokes of escalating absurdity. They talked about the weather—which was safe, in a valley where the weather never did anything—and the price of flour, and whether the Hendersons' eldest boy would ever come home from the coast, and the proper way to gut an eel. Small talk, acres and acres of small talk, and underneath it, like bedrock under topsoil, something that both of them knew was there and neither of them named.

Once—only once, years ago, on a night when the river fog came up thick enough to drink—she had looked at him across the embers of her cookfire and said, quietly, without her usual armor of laughter: "Sometimes you look at things like you're saying goodbye to them. The bridge. The shrine. My stupid chimes. Like you already know you'll be leaving."

And Isandro had felt the fear move through him, cold and total, the way floodwater moves through a cellar, and he had said, "Everything ends, Wren. Even valleys."

"Hm," she'd said, unconvinced, and poked the fire, and let it go. She always let it go. That was the miracle of Wren Ashby: she collected other people's secrets the way the river collected leaves, and she never once opened them up to look.

He loved her for it. He would have died for her, probably, though he hoped never to be tested, because dying was a loud thing and loud things drew attention, and attention was the enemy. Attention was the thing he had crossed mountains to escape. Somewhere beyond the rim of the valley there was a world that wanted something from him—wanted it badly enough to have hunted him to the edge of exhaustion, to have driven him through passes where his feet froze black—and the only reason it hadn't found him was that the world, on the far side of those rim stones, could not remember he existed.

That was the valley's gift. Not peace. *Oblivion.* The Valley of Stillness did not merely silence the gods; it silenced him, smoothed him over like a thumb smoothing wet clay, so that every eye that turned toward him slid off, uninterested. Hunters had come to the rim, in the early years. He'd watched them from the tree line—three men with swords and a fourth with a caged bird whose eyes were wrong, a scrying-thing, a finder. The bird had screamed itself hoarse at the boundary stone and then gone quiet, confused, its uncanny gaze sliding past the valley like a hand sliding off wet glass. The men had argued, gestured at the mist-filled bowl of the valley, and eventually turned back. Whatever they sought, the valley had declined to confirm its existence.

Isandro had stood among the trees with his heart hammering and thought: *This is what safety costs. Everything. Everything you were.*

He had paid it gladly. He told himself he paid it gladly. There were nights he believed it.

* * *

On the morning the silence broke, Isandro was fixing a clock.

Not a remarkable clock—the innkeeper's wall-clock, a fat, complacent thing that lost a quarter hour every week and had done so for a decade, and the innkeeper had finally, after years of blaming the weather and the phase of the moon and the moral character of clocks generally, brought it to the mender. Isandro had it open on his workbench, its brass guts exposed, its pendulum swinging with the patient idiocy of all pendulums, when the light in the room changed.

He looked up. The window over his bench faced west, toward the peaks, and the light coming through it had gone strange—thinned, silvery, the color of light through a held breath. Outside, the afternoon had gone quiet in a way that was not the valley's usual quiet. The valley's quiet was full: birdsong, river-sound, the distant ring of Marta Cole's hammer at the smithy, all of it softened but present, like music heard through a wall. This was a different quiet. This was the quiet of a held note, of a drawn bowstring, of air before lightning.

His hands stopped moving. His heart did the wrong thing—the stalling thing, the old fear rising in him like cold water in a well—and he sat very still and listened.

Nothing. Minutes of nothing. Then, faintly, from the direction of the ferry landing, a sound he had never heard before and recognized instantly anyway, the way you recognize your own name spoken in a crowd:

Someone was screaming. No—not screaming. *Praying.* Someone at the old shrine was praying, loudly, desperately, the words carrying across the water with terrible clarity in that stretched, silvered silence—and the voice was Wren's.

Isandro was out the door before he knew he'd moved.

He ran the river path with his lungs burning, past the drying racks and the eel traps and the leaning fence posts, and skidded out onto the shingle beach below the shrine just as the words reached him whole:

"—please, please, *please*, she's my father, don't take him, I'll give anything, *anything*, do you hear me, I'll give *anything*—"

The ferry had overturned.

He saw it all in pieces, the way disaster shows itself: the flat boat upside-down mid-channel, snagged against the middle shoal; the brown water of the Vess running fast and higher than it should, swollen with rain that had fallen in the high country overnight; old Ashby in the water, going under, coming up, going under again, his bad knees useless against the current; Wren on the shrine steps, soaked to the waist, having waded out chest-deep before the river shrugged her back, screaming her prayer at a sky that had never once, in living memory, answered anyone.

And the sky—

The sky was answering.

Isandro felt it before he saw it, felt it in his teeth, in the fillings of his bones, in the scar tissue of his forgotten life: a pressure, a gathering, a *turning-toward*. Above the western peaks the clouds had begun to move—not drifting, not building, but *moving*, wheeling inward like birds circling a kill, and between them, in the gap, something was looking down into the valley. He could not see it. He could absolutely see it. It was the absence of seeing it that told him it was there, the way you know a hand is over a candle by the dark it casts.

The valley's silence—the great, ancient, load-bearing silence that had hidden him for fifteen years—cracked.

He heard it crack. Not with his ears. With everything else. It went through the world like ice going out on a spring river, a long groaning report that no one else in Stillwater seemed to hear, running from the rim stones down through the fields, through the roots of the willows, through the floorboards of every house, and along its length, everywhere it passed, small impossible things began to happen. The shrine candles, which Odile lit every morning and which burned straight and dull as nails, all leaned sideways at once, flames bending toward the river as if bowing. The river-glass chime on the abandoned ferry awning began to clink, though there was no wind. And old Ashby, going under for the third time, stopped going under.

The water set him down. That was the only word for it. The current, which had been dragging him toward the weir like a hand closing on a coin, simply released him, bore him up, and carried him gently—gently, obscenely gently, in that roaring brown flood—toward the shallows by the eel racks, where he fetched up coughing on the pebbles while his daughter sobbed on the shrine steps and the whole village came running down to the bank shouting his name.

Isandro stood apart from them, at the edge of the crowd, doing what he always did: nothing noticeable. His hands were shaking. He put them in his pockets. Around him people were laughing and weeping and slapping Ashby's back, and someone said *the river gave him back*, and someone else said *don't be daft, he caught the shallows*, and Wren pushed through them all and dropped to her knees in the shingle beside her father, and over her shoulder, for half a heartbeat, her eyes found Isandro's.

She looked away quickly. But she had seen his face. She had seen what fifteen years of practice should have prevented: the expression of a man watching the sky answer a prayer, and being afraid of it—afraid in a way that had nothing to do with drowned ferrymen, an old, specific, personal terror, worn openly for one unguarded instant like a door left open on a lit room.

Above the peaks, the clouds finished their slow wheeling and hung there, arranged in a ring, patient as a held breath. The light stayed silvered and strange. The shrine candles stayed bowed. And somewhere out under that ring of cloud, whatever had turned its attention toward the Valley of Stillness went on looking, unhurried, the way a reader looks at a page it has just found again after losing its place.

Odile the shrine keeper came hobbling down the path with her stick, took one look at her leaning candles, and went white as bone meal. She did not look at the sky. She looked, instead, directly at Isandro—across the whole crowded beach, through the whole celebrating crowd, with those pale watery eyes that missed nothing—and her mouth shaped a single silent word that no one else saw, a word that landed in him like a hook in soft flesh.

*You.*

That night, Isandro did not sleep. He sat at his workbench with the innkeeper's clock still open before him, its pendulum hanging dead, and listened to the valley groan and settle around its new crack like a house around a broken beam. Toward dawn, rain began to fall on the roof—ordinary rain, valley rain, gentle—and beneath it, so far off it might have been imagination, so far off it was certainly imagination, he heard thunder.

Rolling down from the high country. Coming closer.

Like a barrel tumbling downstairs. Like footsteps. Like something descending, patiently, toward a man who had spent fifteen years learning how to be no one, and who had just, for one unguarded moment, been seen.
