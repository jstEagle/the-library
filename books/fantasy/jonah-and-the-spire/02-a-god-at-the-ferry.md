## 2. A God at the Ferry

The rain stopped an hour after dawn, and Stillwater went about pretending nothing had happened.

That was the valley's oldest skill, Isandro thought as he crossed the square with a basket of clock-parts under his arm. Not forgetting—Stillwater was too small and too stubborn to forget anything—but the gentler art of agreeing, collectively and without discussion, that certain things had not happened. The winter the miller's wife had walked out onto the ice and come back dry. The summer of the silent birds. Fifteen years of weather that never turned violent, of prayers that rose from the shrine like smoke from a doused fire, going nowhere, answered by no one.

The village had agreed not to have heard the thunder. He could see it in the way people moved: heads down, voices low, eyes sliding off one another like water off oiled cloth. Old Tam the smith was hammering with a rhythm just slightly too fast. The baker had burned her first two loaves and sold them anyway without apology. Everyone in Stillwater was performing ordinariness this morning, and everyone knew everyone else was performing it, and so the performance held, the way a roof holds when every rafter leans on its neighbor.

Isandro kept his own head down and his stride even. He had practiced being unremarkable for fifteen years, and he was good at it now—the way a man who has learned to hold his breath underwater is good at it. Do not hurry. Do not linger. Be the clock-mender, quiet, competent, a little dull. A man whose name people sometimes had to pause to recall.

*Isandro.* Even that was a borrowed thing, softened and worn smooth like a river stone. It fit him the way a coat fits a man who has grown into it or shrunk out of his old shape—he no longer felt the seams.

He was halfway to the ferry landing when he saw the crowd.

It was not large. Nothing in Stillwater was ever large. But a dozen villagers gathered at the riverbank in a loose half-circle, none of them stepping closer, all of them staring at something on the mud below the ferry ramp—and the quality of their stillness stopped Isandro's feet before his mind caught up. It was the stillness of people looking at a snake. Or a drowned body. Or a fire that has started in a place where fires do not start.

Mira was there. She stood nearest the water, her father's rope coiled over one shoulder, forgotten, and when she turned and found him at the edge of the crowd her face did something he had never seen it do in fifteen years of friendship. It was afraid *of* him. Only for a heartbeat—a flicker, quickly mastered—but he saw it, and it went through him like a fishhook.

"Isandro." Her voice came out careful. "They're saying—" She stopped. Started again. "There's something on the bank."

"What kind of something?"

"Come and see," said Tam the smith, without turning around. "Or don't. Probably better if you don't."

Which was, of course, the surest way to make him come.

* * *

The thing lay at the waterline where the reeds grew thin, half in the shadow of the ferry ramp, and for a long moment Isandro could not have said what it was. Small. That was the first fact, and the strangest. He had expected—though he could not have said what he'd expected, only that his chest had gone tight as he approached—something vast. What lay in the mud was scarcely bigger than a child. It sat slumped against a mooring post the way a traveler sits when they can walk no farther, and it was made, so far as he could tell, of light.

Not glowing. *Made* of it. A body like a candle flame given bones, guttering constantly, its edges tearing and reforming, and where its face should have been there was a brightness that hurt to look at directly—not because it blazed but because it flickered, and the eye kept trying to find a stable point and failing. Its outline smoked faintly. Where it touched the mud, the mud was not scorched but *bleached*, pale as bone, in a rough halo perhaps two feet across.

The god—if that is what it was, and Isandro's skin had already told him it was, the way skin knows a storm before the ears do—did not rise when he came near. But its face turned toward him through the crowd, through the dozen bodies between them, as though they were so much morning fog.

And it spoke his name.

Not *Isandro*. Not the soft syllables the valley had given him, worn smooth by fifteen years of use. It spoke the other one. The true one. The one that lived folded up inside him like a letter he had never dared open, written in a language of thunder, a name that was less a word than a *weather*—and when the god said it, quietly, the way you would say it across a sickbed, the whole riverbank heard it. Every villager within earshot flinched as though a whip had cracked overhead, though the sky was gray and empty and gave back nothing.

The name rolled down the reeds and out over the water and came back as an echo from the far hills, and the echo was wrong, doubled, as if the hills themselves were repeating it in two voices.

Isandro's basket hit the mud. Clock-parts scattered. He barely heard them.

"You," the god said. Its voice was small and ragged, a voice like a draft under a door, and it cost him something to understand how much effort each word took. "You're real. Oh—oh, thank the—" It made a sound that might have been laughter, if laughter could bleed. "I wasn't certain. I've been wrong before. I've been *wrong*, walking these last miles, telling myself the pull I feel is hope and not just—decay. Burning things dream strange dreams." The flame of it shivered, steadied. "But you're real. You're standing there with mud on your boots and your heart going like a hammer, and you are *real*, and I found you. Fifteen years I looked. Longer. I don't—time is difficult, now. Time slips."

"Stay back from it," Tam said hoarsely behind him. "Isandro. Don't go nearer."

But the crowd had already parted around Isandro without anyone deciding to part it, the way water parts around a dropped stone, and he was standing in the bleached ring of mud with the god looking up at him, and he could not have said how he got there.

"What are you?" His voice came out low and steady, which surprised him; inside, everything was falling. "What do you want?"

"I'm Vess." The name meant nothing to him and everything to him at once, the way a tune means something before you know where you learned it. "I'm what's left of Vess. There was more. There was a great deal more, once—there were bells, there were processions, there were ten thousand candles lit on the long night of the year, and every candle was a little me, and I was in all of them." The flame guttered low, nearly out, then clawed back. "Forgive me. I'm not well. I've come a very long way on very little, and I spent almost the last of myself getting through the—" it gestured vaguely upward, at the valley, at the sky, at the invisible walls of the world "—the quiet. Your valley eats us, you know. Did you know? It ate me for three days. I had to pour myself thin as smoke to pass. That's why I'm—" the gesture again, at itself, at the ruin of itself "—this."

Behind him, someone began to pray. Old habits, old terror—he recognized the cadence, a plea to the saints of the new church, the ones who had replaced the gods who no longer answered. The words died unfinished. Praying felt foolish with the evidence lying right there in the mud.

"Why me?" Isandro said. "Why did you come here? Why my name?"

Vess's brightness dimmed, and in the dimming it seemed suddenly, terribly tired—an old creature at the end of a long road, holding out a hand to a stranger.

"Because you're the only one who can hear me properly," it said. "Because the valley was built around you like a box around a coal, to keep you from catching. Because fifteen years ago someone paid an enormous price to make the world forget you, and the world has begun, just slightly, to remember—and when it remembers, everything breaks loose at once, and there will be no time, and no one left to explain." The flame leaned toward him. "I need your help. I need you to leave this valley. I need you to come to Corvane, and then to Ashkarat, and I need you to—"

It stopped.

Its face turned, slowly, away from him—toward the hills. Toward the east, where the peaks stood gray against the gray sky, and where, very far off, so far it might have been imagination, thunder muttered.

"No," Vess whispered. "No, no, not yet. I'm too early. I'm too *thin*." It turned back to Isandro and its voice cracked like ice on a pond. "Listen to me. Listen. They'll come asking questions—the wardens, the gray ones, the mute ones. They'll be gentle. They're always gentle. That's how you'll know it's serious. When they ask you questions, you must lie, and you must lie well, and you must not let them take anything of yours—not hair, not blood, not a single thread. Do you understand? They'll want to weigh you. If they weigh you, they'll know what you are, and if they know what you are, then *it* will know, and if it knows—"

"It?" Isandro's mouth was dry. "What is *it*?"

But the god was fading. He could see it happening—see the light drawing inward, contracting, the way a fire draws down to embers when the wind dies. Its edges had gone from torn to tattered to translucent. The bleached circle of mud beneath it darkened at the rim.

"I don't have enough left," Vess said, and there was no fear in it, only a kind of furious regret, a craftsman's grief at a job left unfinished. "I miscalculated. The crossing cost more than—I should have waited until I was stronger, but there was no time, there is never any time anymore, everything is coming apart so *fast*—" The flame sank until it was barely taller than the mooring post. "Find Sera," it said. "In Corvane. Find the priestess Sera. She'll hate that I've sent you. She'll say she's finished with gods. Tell her—" the light stuttered "—tell her the candles remember her. She'll know. Tell her the candles remember."

"Wait." Isandro dropped to his knees in the mud, heedless of the crowd, heedless of everything. "Wait—you can't—you haven't told me *why*. What am I? What was taken from me? Who am I?"

The god looked at him with its unbearable flickering face, and for a moment—just a moment—it was not small at all. Something moved behind the light, vast and sorrowful, the way the sea moves behind a wave.

"You are the answer to a prayer," Vess said, "that hasn't been prayed yet."

Then it came apart.

Not violently—that was the terrible thing. It came apart gently, the way a candle goes out when the wax is done, folding inward and downward, collapsing into itself with a sound like a long sigh, and what fell into the mud was not ash but embers: dozens of them, hundreds, tiny points of dying orange light scattered across the bleached ground like a constellation knocked out of the sky. They glowed for the space of three heartbeats. Five. Then they went out one by one, not all together but in ones and twos, randomly, the way house lights go out along a street at night, until the last one—a single ember, stubborn, lingering by the mooring post where the god had leaned—flickered twice and died.

The smell remained. Not smoke. Something older and sweeter, like incense burned in a closed room for a hundred years, soaked into the walls.

Nobody moved.

The river went on moving, indifferent, carrying its freight of rain-water and spring melt down toward the lowlands, and the reeds bent and straightened, and somewhere a bird sang, having understood nothing, and the twelve villagers of Stillwater stood around a patch of dead embers and ordinary mud and did not move.

It was Mira who broke it. She crossed to Isandro, took his arm—her grip hard, harder than friendship strictly required—and pulled him upright.

"Inside," she said. "All of you, go home. Go *home*." And the crowd, blessedly, obeyed her—obeyed the ferryman's daughter because obedience was easier than thought—shuffling backward, muttering, casting looks at Isandro that he felt on his skin like sunburn. Tam went last, walking backward, watching Isandro the whole way, and his face was not unkind, but it was the face of a man memorizing something.

*They'll begin whispering by noon,* Isandro thought. *By evening it'll be everywhere.*

He had known this day would come. That was the truth he had never let himself think plainly: for fifteen years he had lived in the knowledge, the way a man lives on land above a mine shaft, knowing, always knowing, and building his life carefully over the hollow place. He had chosen a trade that required patience and solitude. He had chosen friends who asked few questions. He had chosen a name with no history in the valley. Every ordinary thing about him had been selected, fitted, maintained like the works of a clock—and now the clock had struck, and all the small gears of his careful life lay scattered in the mud with the clock-parts, and the god's last ember was cold, and the name that was not his name was ringing in twelve pairs of ears and would not stop ringing.

"They said your name," Mira said quietly. Her hand was still on his arm. She was looking at the dead embers, not at him. "Not—not the name we call you. The other one. I felt it go through my teeth." She swallowed. "Isandro. What was that?"

"I don't know."

"You do know."

"Mira—"

"My father ferried you across the river the night you came," she said, still not looking at him. "Fifteen years ago. In the rain. You had burns on your hands and you wouldn't speak, and my father carried you up to the inn himself and never asked you a single question, not once, not in fifteen years, because I asked him why and he said, *some men are running from something, and it's not our business to catch them.*" Now she looked at him, and her eyes were wet and angry and loyal all at once, which was the most Mira thing about her. "So I'm not asking. I'm telling you: whatever it is, whatever that thing wanted—my father is down at the boathouse, and Tam's forge is fifty feet from my window, and I would like very much for the people I love not to burn. So either you tell me what's coming, or you tell me it's nothing. But don't stand there and tell me you don't know. I've watched you hear thunder since I was nine years old. You *flinch*, Isandro. Nobody flinches at weather they don't know."

The words went into him deeper than the god's name had.

He opened his mouth—to lie, or to confess, he honestly did not know which—and was spared the choice, because a shadow fell across the both of them, and it was not a cloud.

Odile the shrine keeper stood at the top of the ferry ramp, and she had brought her staff, and she was not alone. Behind her, arranged along the path in a silence that had weight to it, stood four of the wardens.

Gray-robed. Mute. Their faces calm and empty as unscribed tablets, their hands folded, their eyes—Isandro had never seen a warden's eyes move before, not once in fifteen years, they were famous for their stillness—fixed on him.

Odile looked at the bleached circle of mud. At the dead embers. At Isandro, kneeling in the dirt with the ferryman's daughter's hand on his arm and a god's last words still warm in his ears.

"The sky remembered," she said.

Her voice was not loud. It did not need to be. The wardens behind her did not shift, did not breathe audibly, and yet the whole riverbank seemed to lean toward Isandro like a jury.

"You will come to the shrine," Odile said. "Now. And you will tell me what my god died asking you for."
