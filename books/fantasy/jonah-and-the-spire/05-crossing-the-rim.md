## 5. Crossing the Rim

The storm had been building for three days, and Isandro had been counting every hour of it.

He had learned to read weather the way other men read faces—by necessity, in those first terrible years, when a single distant rumble could send him shaking into the root cellar beneath Odile's shrine. Fifteen years of Stillwater's stillness had softened that terror into something more like vigilance. He knew the weight of clouds before they broke. He knew the greenish tinge the light took on when the peaks to the west were gathering their strength.

And he knew, with a certainty that sat in his chest like a swallowed stone, that this storm was not natural.

It had come up out of nothing on the third night after Vess collapsed into embers at the ferry landing—a wall of cloud rising over the western rim where no cloud should have been able to form. The valley did not permit weather. That was the whole point of it. The Valley of Stillness held its air the way a cupped hand holds water: gently, completely, without spill or surge. In fifteen years he had not seen wind bend a reed along the riverbank. Rain came as fine gray mist, if it came at all. Thunder was a story told by travelers, and Isandro had made himself an expert at not listening to stories.

But now the sky over the rim churned like something boiling in a pot, and the wardens had come down from their gray house above the falls, and Mira was gone.

He tried not to think about Mira. He failed, mostly. Every time he closed his eyes he saw her standing in the square outside her father's ferry house, chin lifted, lying to the mute gray-robed men with a steadiness she could not possibly have learned from anyone in Stillwater—*he's never done anything, he mends nets and he's afraid of storms, ask anyone*—and then the warden's hand closing around her arm, not cruelly, which somehow made it worse, and the two of them walking up the river path toward the gray house while the whole village watched and pretended not to.

They had taken her because she lied for him. Because she loved him, in the uncomplicated way she'd loved him since they were children wading the shallows, and love was evidence, and evidence was enough.

Isandro stood in the loft above the boathouse where he'd slept for fifteen years and packed what there was to pack, which was almost nothing. A wool coat. A knife for gutting fish. The letter from the shrine, wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, its one line worn smooth into his memory: *The crown lies.* A crust of bread. His hands, he noticed, were steady, and that frightened him more than trembling would have. Trembling would have meant some part of him still believed he could stay.

Outside, the first thunder he had heard in fifteen years rolled down off the peaks, long and low, like a door being dragged open somewhere very far away.

* * *

He left before dawn, taking the goat track behind the shrine because it was the only way up the rim that didn't pass the gray house. The mist lay thick in the valley bottom, and for the first hour he moved through it like a man wading through milk, the world reduced to the sound of his own boots on wet stone and the growing pressure in his ears—the same pressure that preceded every storm he'd ever feared, except that this time it wasn't fear humming under his skin.

It was recognition.

That was the thing he could not say aloud to anyone, the thing that had lived coiled in him since the night Vess spoke his name: *Isandro Almeida*, two words no one in Stillwater had ever known, and the moment they left the god's flickering mouth, something in his body had answered like a struck bell. Not surprise. Recognition. As though he had spent fifteen years holding his breath and only just now understood that he was holding it.

The goat track climbed through scrub pine and loose shale. Behind him, once, he heard a whistle—one note, flat and carrying, repeated twice—and knew the wardens had found the boathouse empty. They would not shout. Wardens never shouted; the valley's silence was their law and their weapon both. But they would climb. They moved over broken ground like water finding its level, patient and inevitable, and there were nine of them and one of him, and the boundary stone was still four hours above.

He climbed faster.

By midmorning the mist had fallen away below him and the sun came hard off the bare rock, and he could see the whole valley spread out beneath him like a thing in a bowl: the river a bright thread, Stillwater a scatter of brown roofs, the fields stitched in their patient greens. It was beautiful. He had hated it and needed it and hidden inside it for half his life, and looking down at it now he felt the specific grief of a man leaving a room he knows he can never reenter—not because the door will be locked, but because he himself will be different on the other side.

A second whistle sounded, closer. Then a third, from the east, and he understood they had split, were bracketing the slope, driving him upward exactly as they wanted. There was nowhere else to go. The rim was the rim.

His legs burned. His lungs burned. Somewhere below, gray figures moved through the pines without disturbing a single branch, and the wrongness of that—the sheer *unnaturalness* of it—spurred him up the last stretch of scree faster than fear alone could have.

The boundary stone stood in a saddle between two peaks, a slab of black granite taller than a man, older than any carving on it. The carvings themselves had been worn to shadows: spirals, he thought, or eyes, or waves—symbols so old their meanings had drained out of them. On the valley side, the air was still. On the far side, visible even from here, the world moved: cloud streaming over the high passes, trees bending, weather living its loud and dangerous life.

Isandro stopped ten paces from the stone.

Behind him, out of the treeline, the wardens emerged onto the scree. Nine of them, gray-robed, gray-faced, arranged in a crescent with the ease of long practice. Their leader raised one hand—not threatening, never threatening—and made the small precise gesture that all Stillwater knew: *stay*. Stay, be still, be silent, be safe.

For fifteen years that gesture had governed his entire existence. He had built himself around it, hollowed himself to fit it, told himself the hollowness was peace.

Mira was sitting in a gray room right now because of it.

"I'm sorry," Isandro said, and his voice cracked the saddle's silence like a whipcrack, and every warden flinched—he saw it, actually saw it, nine bodies jerking as one at the mere sound of a human voice raised in the open air—and he turned and walked to the boundary stone and put his hand flat against the cold granite.

* * *

Later, he would try to describe it, and fail, and try again, and fail better, and finally settle on this: it was like being deaf for fifteen years and then hearing not a sound but *all* sounds at once.

Magic did not flood back into him. That was too gentle a word, too orderly, as though it poured. It *detonated*. Every sense he owned opened at the same instant. He felt the storm waiting beyond the peaks the way a man feels his own heartbeat—intimately, constantly, whether he wished to or not. He felt the charge in the air, the tension in the rock, the slow electric breathing of the whole wounded sky. He felt the wardens behind him as nine small absences, holes in the world's fabric, and felt the boundary stone itself as a seam, a scar, a place where someone had once sewn the world shut.

And underneath all of it, rising like water finding a crack, came fifteen years of everything he had refused to feel.

The storm-magic had never left him. That was the horror of it, the joke of it, the thing he would spend years untangling. The valley hadn't suppressed it—it had *stored* it. Every thunderhead he'd feared, every night he'd lain awake counting his heartbeats, every flinch at a traveler's tale of lightning: it had all gone somewhere, pressed down and packed tight into the sealed vessel of his body, and now the seal was broken and all of it wanted out at once.

"No," Isandro said, to himself, to the sky, to whatever half-remembered self was waking up inside him. "No, no—"

He tried to let it out slowly. He genuinely tried. He reached for the discipline he dimly recalled from a life he couldn't remember—there were shapes in his mind, forms, words in a language he'd spoken before his memory began—and the shapes shattered the moment he touched them, and the power came through the broken places all together.

Lightning hit the ridge.

Not near him. Not beside him. *From* him—from his chest, his hands, his screaming skin—upward and outward in a branching white catastrophe that struck the eastern peak above the saddle with a crack like the world splitting. The thunder that followed was not a sound but a physical fact; it picked him up and threw him against the boundary stone. And the peak—

The peak came down.

He watched it happen with the detached clarity of pure shock: the whole shoulder of the mountain shuddering, then sliding, tons of rock and pine letting loose in a long roaring avalanche that poured down the far slope and took the ridgeline with it, a plume of dust rising five hundred feet into the churning sky. The ground bucked. Stones rained past him. The sound went on and on, echoing from peak to peak around the entire valley, and in the village far below, he knew, every window would rattle, every bell would swing on its hook, every person in Stillwater would stand in their doorway staring up at the smoking mountain and know, with absolute certainty, that the silence was over.

Silence, indeed. He had just announced himself to the world with a landslide.

Isandro slid down against the boundary stone, shaking so hard his teeth chattered. His hands were burned—not blistered, but reddened, tingling, as if he'd plunged them into cold water that was somehow also fire. Smoke rose from the hem of his coat. When he tried to stand, his knees folded, and he ended up on all fours on the bare rock, gasping, while the last echoes of the avalanche died away among the peaks.

*What am I?*

The question arrived with total sincerity, as if asked by someone who had only just moved into his skull. Fifteen years a fisherman's lodger, a net-mender, a man afraid of storms. One minute at a boundary stone, and he was something that tore mountains open by accident. Both things were true. Neither thing was bearable.

Behind him, gravel shifted.

He turned. The wardens stood at the edge of the scree, and for the first time in fifteen years, they looked uncertain. Their leader's hand was half-raised in the *stay* gesture, frozen there, as though the gesture itself had stopped meaning anything. One of them took a step backward—which Isandro had never seen a warden do, not once, not in any season, for any reason—and then another, and then, slowly, deliberately, the leader lowered his hand and made a different sign, one Isandro had never seen before: two fingers drawn across the throat, and then pointed, not at Isandro, but away. East. Toward the wider world.

*Go,* the gesture said. Or perhaps: *You are no longer ours to hold.*

Or perhaps, and this thought chilled him worse than the lightning had: *We cannot hold you anymore, and something worse than us has already seen.*

The wardens withdrew down the slope, gray shapes dissolving into gray pines, and Isandro was alone at the top of the world with a dead peak and a burning sky.

* * *

He crossed the boundary at dusk.

It should have been a threshold, a moment, a line stepped over with ceremony. Instead he simply walked, because staying on the still side of the stone another hour felt more dangerous than anything on the far side, and because somewhere below in the dark the wardens might change their minds, and because his legs, having decided to carry him, refused to consult him further.

The change was immediate and total. Wind hit him like a living thing—real wind, gusting, smelling of rain and pine and distance—and he staggered and nearly wept at the sheer *noise* of it, the rustle and rush and roar of a world that had never agreed to hold still. Overhead the storm he'd felt all day was fully arrived now, vast purple-black banks of cloud rolling over the passes, sheet lightning flickering inside them like thoughts in a dark mind. It was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen and the most terrifying, and both truths lived in him side by side without resolving, the way terror and exhilaration had been living in him since the stone.

Because that was the unbearable secret, the one he couldn't stop tasting even as his hands shook: some part of him had *loved* it. The release. The raw white power tearing out of him and reshaping the world in a heartbeat. Fifteen years of quiet had been suffocation, and he hadn't known, and now that he knew, he could never unknow it.

He walked east along the high trail as the light failed, putting distance between himself and the smoking ridge, and the storm followed him—or he followed it; the distinction had begun to blur—with lightning walking its slow circuits inside the cloud like a caged animal pacing.

Somewhere out there, he understood, people had felt what he'd done. Not just the wardens. The magic that had come back to him was not private; it rang through the world like a bell through a cathedral, and there were ears in Vhalen attuned to exactly that ring. Sera's voice surfaced in his memory unbidden—no. Not yet. He didn't know her yet. But he knew *of* her, the way every traveler's rumor had carried fragments into the valley these past weeks: a defrocked priestess, gods answering prayers again, a world cracking open along old seams. If he had heard those rumors even in the Valley of Stillness, then others had heard the mountain break. Others were listening.

Every scrying eye in Vhalen, he thought, is turning toward me right now. And I have no idea which of them I should fear most.

He crested a rise and saw, far below, the first lights of the outer world: a road winding down toward the plains, a waystation's orange windows, carts drawn up for the night, woodsmoke standing straight in the windless lee of a hill. Ordinary life, going on, unaware. For one long moment he stood at the edge of the lamplight's reach, a burned and shaking man with a dead mountain at his back and a god's embers in his pocket and a letter against his heart that said *the crown lies*.

Then the storm directly overhead discharged—a single, deliberate bolt, striking the trail thirty feet ahead of him with a flash and a boom that knocked him to his knees—and in the strobing afterimage, every hair on his arms standing on end, Isandro understood that the lightning had not missed.

It had greeted him.

The rain came then, all at once, warm and enormous, and Isandro Almeida got up out of the mud of the wide world, laughing and sobbing in the same breath, and walked down toward the lights—marked, hunted, alive, and utterly unable to say which of those words frightened him most.
