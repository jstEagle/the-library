## 6. The Road to Corvane

The pilgrim road smelled of dust and fear.

Isandro had been walking it for three days when he understood that the two smells were the same. Dust rose from ten thousand feet—farmers' boots, merchants' cracked shoes, children carried on hips—and fear rose with it, a fine invisible haze that clung to the back of the throat like ash. Everyone on the road was going somewhere else. Nobody was going home.

He had learned the road's grammar quickly. Walk in the middle of a group, never alone at the edges where questions found you. Keep your hood up but not too far down—a face fully hidden invited suspicion, and suspicion on this road moved faster than rumor. Say you were from the hill villages if asked; everyone was from the hill villages now. Give your name as *Ando*, which was half of nothing, a name like a pebble dropped in a well.

The valley was behind him, over the rim he had crossed in terror and fire, and every night when he slept he dreamed of the ridge he had leveled—the way the stone had simply *agreed* with him, folding like wet clay—and woke with his hands tingling and his heart hammering out a rhythm his body remembered from fifteen years ago and had never truly forgotten.

*Marked now,* he thought, watching crows wheel over a burned-out waystation. *Every scrying eye in Vhalen.*

He did not know what a scrying eye looked like. He only knew that since the boundary stone, the world felt full of eyes.

* * *

He fell in with the refugees outside a town called Brassen's Ford, or maybe it was called something else—he never learned, because by the time he reached its outskirts there was no town left to name, only a market square of scorched stalls and a well with a child's shoe beside it. The refugees were moving west along the river road, toward Corvane, one of the free cities whose walls people still believed in. They moved the way water moves downhill: not fast, not stopping, finding every path of least resistance.

There was an old woman driving a cart pulled by a mule so thin its ribs showed through its hide like barrel staves. There was a family of tanners with their dye-stained hands wrapped in rags. There were soldiers—not usurper soldiers, just men who had been soldiers somewhere once, walking with their weapons slung and their eyes doing arithmetic about everyone they passed.

And there was her.

He noticed her first because she was praying. Not the furtive, apologetic muttering he'd seen from other travelers—*old gods forgive us, old gods watch over us*, said quickly like a superstition one is embarrassed by—but openly, aloud, kneeling at a roadside shrine so weathered the god's name carved above it had worn to a smooth blank. She knelt the way other people drew swords.

She was perhaps thirty, dark-haired, sun-browned, wearing the gray travel robes of someone who had once worn better ones. At her belt hung a wooden pendant shaped like a flame, and Isandro's stomach tightened when he saw it, because he knew that shape. He had seen it last on a dying god made of candlelight, flickering on a riverbank in the Valley of Stillness, begging him by a name no one there should have known.

*Vess.*

The woman finished her prayer, stood, and caught him staring. She didn't look away. Most people looked away.

"You're limping," she said.

"I'm not."

"You favor your left leg. Blisters, or the old wound?" Her eyes went to his boots, then up, unhurried, cataloguing. "You walk like a man carrying more than a pack."

"I walk like a man who's been walking for three days," Isandro said. "Everyone here does."

"Everyone here has been walking for weeks." She said it without accusation, which somehow made it worse. She adjusted the strap of her satchel and studied him a moment longer, and then, to his surprise, simply nodded and turned back toward the road. "There's room in our column if you want it. We eat at dusk. I'm called Sera."

"I didn't ask."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "But you'll want to know before dusk anyway."

* * *

He told himself he would move on alone at the next crossroads. He told himself that again at dusk, sitting at the edge of the refugee fire eating barley soup that tasted mostly of smoke, listening to the low murmur of sixty people trying not to talk about the armies behind them.

They talked about them anyway, in the way people talk about weather. The Regent's columns had taken Vellin. The Regent's columns had burned the temple district in Marrow. The Regent's men were registering god-touched in every town they passed through—names written into ledgers, and then the names walked away between soldiers and never came back, and the ledgers closed with a sound nobody heard but everybody imagined.

"The free cities will hold," said the old woman with the mule, with the desperate conviction of someone saying it for the tenth time that day.

"Corvane's under curfew already," said one of the ex-soldiers. "My cousin's letter came through a smuggler. They're ringing bells at dusk and nobody goes out after. And they're asking questions at the gates. Questions about—" He lowered his voice, and even so, the word seemed to fall into the fire and burn green. "—storm-callers."

Isandro kept his spoon moving. Kept his breathing even. He had gotten very good, over fifteen years, at being furniture.

"So it's true," said a young man near the fire, half to himself. "A storm-caller. On the road. My brother-in-law swears he saw the sky split over the eastern hills three nights past. Said lightning hit the same ridge twice, and the second time there wasn't any ridge."

"That's army work," someone said. "War-machines."

"Since when do war-machines make thunder roll backward?"

Sera was watching Isandro across the fire.

Not staring—she was too careful for staring—but watching, the way she'd watched him on the road, with that patient cataloguing gaze, and Isandro realized with a cold slide in his gut that she had been watching him since the shrine. That the limp comment hadn't been concern. It had been a hook, cast out to see what bit.

He finished his soup. He thanked the cook. He rolled into his blanket at the edge of the firelight and stared at the stars and did not sleep, and around midnight, soft footsteps stopped beside him.

"You breathe wrong," Sera said quietly.

"I beg your pardon?"

"When you sleep. Or pretend to. People actually sleeping breathe from the belly. You breathe from the chest, shallow, like a man waiting for a door to break open." She sat down on the ground beside him without invitation, close enough to speak softly, far enough to be proper. In the dark her face was mostly shadow and the small reflected orange of the coals. "I've watched a lot of people sleep, these last years. On roads. In cellars. You learn things."

"What do you want?"

"To offer you a bargain." She said it plainly, no coyness in it. "I have passage arranged as far as Corvane—a river barge, leaving from a landing two days east of here, run by people who don't ask names because they don't want theirs asked. Room for two. I also know the city—its gates, its curfews, which parishes still hide god-touched and which ones sell them. Fifteen years I've been moving through the cracks of this country, and I know every crack."

"And in exchange?"

"Protection." The word came out flat and hard. "I'm a defrocked priestess of the old gods traveling alone on a road full of deserters and worse. Sooner or later someone decides a woman alone is easy. When that happens, I need someone standing next to me who makes predators recalculate." She paused. "And I think, though you'll deny it, that you are very good at making predators recalculate."

"I'm a hill farmer," Isandro said. "My family lost everything in the war. I'm going to Corvane to find work."

"Yes," Sera said. "That's your story. It's a decent story. You've got the look of loss down well enough." She reached into her satchel slowly—telegraphing the movement, he noticed, deliberately letting him see her hand—and drew out the wooden flame-pendant, turning it in the coal-light. "Do you know what this is?"

"A charm. Half the road wears them."

"Do you know what it *means?*" She held it up between two fingers. "It's Vess. God of small lights, of hearths and candles and the little mercies. The god of *not being seen when you need to hide.* My order served Vess for nine hundred years, and fifteen years ago Vess went silent like all the others, and my temple burned, and my order called me a heretic for saying the gods would come back." Her voice stayed level, but something underneath it trembled, like ice over moving water. "Three nights ago, on the eastern hills, the sky split. And yesterday morning, every candle-flame within a mile of this road bent toward you when you passed. Every single one. Like flowers turning to the sun."

The cold in Isandro's gut became a lake.

"You're mistaken," he said.

"Probably," Sera agreed. "Probably it's nothing. Probably you're exactly what you say you are." She tucked the pendant away and stood, brushing off her robes. "The barge leaves at dawn the day after tomorrow. If you're with us at the landing, you're with us. If you're not, I'll pray for you anyway. It's what I do." She took a step away, then paused. "For what it's worth—I don't care what you are. I cared, once, about doctrine and orders and the proper forms. The fire cured me of caring. All I care about now is that the gods are waking up, and something out there is eating everything that wakes."

She left him alone with the stars and his hammering heart.

* * *

He should have slipped away that night. He knew it. He lay awake constructing the argument for it with the thoroughness of a man building a wall: she was dangerous, she had seen through him in two days when the valley's wardens had needed fifteen years, and wherever she went, attention followed—attention was the one thing he could not survive. The usurper's inquisitors would have names for people like her. *Heretic. God-touched. Unregistered.* He could be over the hills and gone before dawn, another pebble in another well.

Instead, at dawn, he found himself walking at the column's edge, and at the second dawn after that, standing on a muddy river landing while a barge captain with a face like a closed fist counted heads and took coins and asked no questions, exactly as promised.

The barge was called the *Patient Widow*, which struck Isandro as either very honest or very ominous. It hauled grain normally, the captain said—or didn't say, but Sera explained in a murmur—and now hauled anything that paid, including sixty-odd souls crammed between cargo nets, heading downriver toward Corvane and whatever waited inside its curfewed walls.

The river was wide and brown and slow, and for the first time since the boundary stone, Isandro let himself breathe.

He found Sera at the stern on the first evening, watching the banks slide past—willows, drowned fields, the occasional roofline of a village that had gone dark early. He stood beside her. Neither of them spoke for a long time. It was, oddly, the most comfortable silence he had shared with anyone in fifteen years, and that frightened him more than the conversation probably would have.

"My name isn't Ando," he said finally.

"I know."

"It's not anything you'd guess."

"I believe that too." She didn't turn her head. "You don't have to give me the real one. I'm not buying it with passage. But I'll tell you why I asked you aboard, and it wasn't only the protection." Now she turned, and in the fading light her eyes were steady and tired and burning all at once. "Because I knelt at that shrine for twenty minutes before you came up the road, and I prayed the first honest prayer I've prayed in fifteen years. I prayed: *if any of you are still out there, if any of you are coming back, send me a sign. Send me something I can follow.*" She gestured, slightly, at him—at all of him. "And then the horizon went white twice, and a man walked out of the east with storm-light practically leaking out of his seams, pretending to limp."

"I don't leak."

"You absolutely leak. You're a bad liar, is what you are. Good at the small lies, terrible at the large one. Your whole body is apologizing for it constantly." A ghost of a smile crossed her face, there and gone. "So here's what happens now. You keep your secret. I keep pretending I haven't guessed it. And when the moment comes—and it will come, on this river or in that city, sooner than either of us wants—you use whatever it is you are, and you don't hesitate, because hesitation is how the people I loved died." She looked back at the water. "In exchange, I get you to Corvane alive, and maybe—maybe—you help me find out whether the gods coming back is a resurrection or a funeral."

"That's not a bargain," Isandro said. "That's a trap with polite manners."

"Yes," Sera said. "Most bargains are. Welcome to the world outside your hills."

He almost flinched. She noticed—of course she noticed—but mercifully, she misread it, filing it away as some farmer's sore spot rather than the truth: that he had spent fifteen years in a place designed to erase exactly the thing she was talking to, and that every hour outside the valley, the erased thing grew louder under his skin, like a second heartbeat learning to keep time again.

Below them, the river carried them west. Behind them, far off on the southern horizon, clouds were gathering that had nothing to do with weather, and Isandro could feel them the way a man feels a hand hovering near the back of his neck.

* * *

On the third day of the river, the candles told them.

It happened at dusk, in the barge's cramped cabin where a dozen travelers sheltered from the rain. Someone lit a stub of tallow candle, and the flame stood perfectly straight—too straight, rigid as a nail—and then bent, all at once, every flame in the cabin bending westward against the draft, toward the bow, toward the deck, toward Isandro, who sat very still in the corner and felt the heat crawl up his neck.

Nobody else seemed to notice. Rain, they'd think. Drafts. But Sera noticed. Sera always noticed. Across the cabin her eyes found his, and she gave the smallest shake of her head: *not here, not now.*

Later, in the dark of the deck under the awning, with rain drumming the canvas overhead, she crouched beside him and spoke barely above the water-noise.

"They're getting stronger," she said. "The signs. Whatever answered me on that roadside—it's closer than it was. Or you're getting louder."

"I'm not doing anything."

"I know. That's what frightens me." She pulled her knees up, and for a moment the defrocked priestess looked very young and very old at once. "Vess came to me in a dream last night. First dream in fifteen years. It was small—it's always small, that one, it's the god of candle-ends and crumbs—and it said one thing over and over." Her voice caught, steadied. "It said: *the crown lies, and the bearer bleeds, and the river runs to Corvane, and the caller must not stop walking.*"

Isandro's mouth went dry. *The crown lies.* Three words he had read once, in a sealed letter guarded fifteen years by a shrine keeper named Odile, opened the night the sky remembered. Three words that had sent him over the valley rim and onto this road and into this rain.

"How," he said carefully, "would your god know about a letter?"

Sera went very still.

"What letter?" she asked.

And Isandro, who was a bad liar in large things and knew it, said nothing at all—which was, in its way, the loudest answer he could have given. The rain filled the silence between them, drumming out the shape of everything neither of them would say, until Sera exhaled slowly and laughed, a small unsteady sound with no humor in it.

"Fine," she said. "Keep it. Carry your secrets; I'll carry mine. But hear me, whoever you are." She leaned close, and her eyes in the dark were bright as the flame-pendant at her belt. "If Vess is speaking to both of us—if the same three words are pulling us down the same river—then this stopped being coincidence three days ago, and whatever's waiting in Corvane, we'd best arrive knowing which side of it we're standing on."

Ahead, through the rain-curtain, the lights of Corvane were beginning to show—hundreds of them strung along the dark riverbank like embers in a grate, beautiful and orderly and wrong, because Isandro could see now what he was sure no one else on the barge could see:

Every light in the free city was burning the wrong color.
