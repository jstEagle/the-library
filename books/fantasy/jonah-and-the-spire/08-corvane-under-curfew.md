## 8. Corvane Under Curfew

The gates of Corvane smelled of ash and wet stone.

Isandro had imagined the free cities as bright things—Sera had described them often enough on the road, with a wry fondness she reserved for nothing else: Corvane's hanging gardens, its bell-towers, its fish markets loud enough to drown out prayer. What he saw instead, from the crest of the pilgrim road in the last gray light of evening, was a city holding its breath.

No bells rang. That was the first wrongness. A city that size should have been ringing vespers, or at least arguing about it. Instead the towers stood dark against a bruised sky, and along the walls burned lanterns of a color he had never seen before—pale, chemical yellow, like the eyes of something that had learned to imitate light without understanding warmth.

"Curfew lamps," Sera said beside him. She had pulled her hood forward and her voice down to almost nothing. "Tallow and salt-petre. The Regent's engineers designed them. They burn all night, so no one can claim they couldn't see who was out of doors."

"And if someone is?"

"Then someone is registered." She said it flatly, the way you'd say *then someone is dead*, because in Corvane those had recently become the same sentence.

They joined the trickle of people moving toward the western gate—not refugees anymore; the usurper's edicts had closed the roads to the rootless three weeks ago, Sera told him, and anyone caught traveling without papers was taken for questioning. So they became pilgrims instead, which was cleverer than it sounded. Pilgrimage was still legal. Faith was not yet a crime, only an inconvenience, and inconvenience could be taxed.

The queue at the gate moved with the terrible patience of people who had learned what happened to the impatient. Isandro kept his head down and his hands visible, the way Sera had drilled into him over two days of walking. *Hands always visible. Eyes down but not servile. If they ask your name, give them one you can answer questions about.* His false name was Tam Vessel, a tanner's son from upriver, and he had rehearsed the small biography until it sat in his mouth like a pebble.

The gate clerks wore gray tabards with a black crown stitched at the shoulder—the crown inverted, points downward, which Isandro had learned was the Regent's sigil. One of them held a long brass instrument like a tuning fork, and as each traveler passed, the clerk touched it lightly to their sternum.

It hummed when it touched Sera. A low, sour note, quickly damped by the clerk's thumb, noted in a ledger with a small mark. She did not flinch. Isandro watched her do it—watched her stand there while a stranger wrote down the fact of her soul in a government book—and understood something new about courage. It wasn't the absence of fear. It was fear that had been made to stand very still.

When the fork touched Isandro's chest, it said nothing at all. The clerk frowned, tapped it against his own palm, tried again. Nothing. The valley's fifteen years had soaked into him somehow, or the binding that had made the Valley of Stillness had made him too—a hollow where resonance should be.

*God-touched,* the ledger would have said, if it could have read him. *Not found.*

"Move along," the clerk said, irritated, as though Isandro were a faulty hinge.

They walked into Corvane under the yellow lamps, and the doors closed behind them one by one, street by street, like eyes shutting.

* * *

Sera's contact was a woman named Mother Cassia, whom Sera described as "a priestess, technically defrocked, technically alive," and whose chapel was "technically nonexistent." Finding it took them through districts that grew poorer and quieter as they went, past walls papered with edicts—*REGISTER ALL GOD-TOUCHED PERSONS WITHIN THREE DAYS*—and past a square where a crowd had gathered despite the curfew, standing in silence around a posted list of names.

Isandro read four names before Sera pulled him away. He recognized none of them. He thought about them anyway, all night, the way you think about stones dropped into deep water.

Mother Cassia's chapel was beneath a chandler's shop, down stairs that smelled of tallow and old incense, behind a door disguised as a cellar. It was a low vaulted room that had clearly once been beautiful: the bones of frescoes still showed on the walls, saints and rivers and a crowned figure whose face had been deliberately scraped away. Dozens of candle stubs lined the sills and niches, unlit. Benches had been pushed against the walls to make room for pallets—six of them, seven—and three people lay on the pallets, sleeping the thin sleep of the hidden.

Cassia herself was small and iron-colored, with hands stained permanently blue from dye-work she did above stairs as cover. She looked at Sera for a long moment, then at Isandro, then back at Sera.

"You brought a storm-caller into my cellar."

"I brought a friend," Sera said.

"I know what I smell." Cassia's nostrils flared. "Ozone and river water. You brought weather into my house during a registration sweep." But she stepped aside and let them in, and shut the door, and shot three bolts, and only then let her shoulders come down from around her ears. "There's soup. Don't light anything."

They ate in the dark, mostly by touch. The soup was barley and onion and tasted like the best thing Isandro had ever eaten, which he suspected was less about the soup than about eating it indoors, behind bolts, next to someone who knew his real name and used it anyway when the listening world permitted.

"The sweep comes tomorrow," Cassia whispered across the dark. "Inquisitors, with their forks and their ledgers. They've already taken the weaver's boy. Eleven years old. He sang to the rain, that's all—he sang to the rain and the rain came early." Her voice didn't rise. It got quieter instead, which was worse. "His mother registered herself the next morning just so they'd tell her where he'd gone. They registered her too. Now neither of them is anywhere."

One of the sleepers on the pallets turned over. Someone murmured a name in their sleep—*Vess*, it might have been, or something like it—and Isandro's spine went cold.

"Sera," he breathed. "Did you hear—"

"Hush." But she had heard it too. He could tell by her stillness.

* * *

He woke in the black hour before dawn to the sound of a match striking.

For one blind moment he was fifteen again, in the valley, hearing thunder for the first time since childhood, and his whole body braced against a sky about to open. Then memory caught up: the cellar, the bolts, the smell of tallow. He sat up silently.

Sera was already awake, watching the far wall of the chapel.

The candles were lighting themselves.

Not all at once. One stub in a niche near the door caught with a sound like a drawn breath, flame standing up straight and small. Then the one beside it. Then a sill of six together, then a cluster near the scraped-away fresco, each flame arriving separately, deliberately, the way a congregation arrives—one, then a few, then many—until the underground chapel was full of soft light and every shadow had retreated to a corner to watch.

And in the middle of the floor, where there had been nothing but flagstones, stood a figure made mostly of outline.

It was smaller than Isandro remembered. At the ferry, Vess had seemed tall—tall as a story, tall as a god ought to be—but that had been night and terror and the strange magnification of the impossible. This Vess was barely taller than a child, and flickered constantly, guttering at the edges like a flame deciding whether to exist. Its shape suggested a person the way a footprint suggests a foot. Where its face should have been there was a brightness, and within the brightness, faintly, two darker points that might have been eyes, and looking into them Isandro felt the same vertigo he'd felt on the riverbank: the sense of being known all the way through, past the false name, past the valley, past fifteen careful years, down to whatever was written underneath.

"*You came,*" Vess said. Its voice was better here—steadier, fed by forty flames instead of desperation—but it still sounded like someone speaking through a wall. "*Both of you. I dared not hope both.*"

"You're smaller," Sera said. Her voice cracked on it. She knelt, slowly, the way one kneels to a child or a wounded animal, and Isandro realized with a jolt that this was worship—that Sera, who had spent the whole road pretending faith was a country she'd emigrated from, had crossed the border without noticing.

"*I am what remains,*" Vess said. "*I told you. We burned. All of us. When the Binding closed the sky, our voices had nowhere to go, and a voice with nowhere to go eats itself.*" The flames dimmed as it spoke, sympathetically, the way a face pales. "*I survived by scattering. Candle-flames. Hearth-coals. Every fire any of the faithful ever lit with a prayer in their mouths, I hid a spark of myself inside it. For fifteen years I have been a religion of embers.*"

"And now?" Isandro asked. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "Now the prayers are being answered again. I prayed at the shrine in Stillwater and the valley cracked like ice. Why now? What changed?"

The god turned toward him—all of it turned, the way a candleflame turns toward a draft—and the brightness of its face intensified until Isandro had to narrow his eyes.

"*Because the thing that holds the king is waking fully,*" Vess said. "*And its waking loosens the Binding, the way a fist clenching loosens a net stretched over it. It does not know that. It thinks its hunger is strength. It is strength, yes—but every act of it strains the ritual that made it possible. The gods are returning not because the Binding failed, storm-caller. We are returning because the thing wearing your king's crown is pulling the threads loose trying to feed.*"

The word *your* landed in Isandro like a stone in still water. *Your king.* Not *the* king. He looked at Sera, but she was staring at Vess, and her face had gone the color of the candle-wax.

"Then tell us plainly," she said. "All of it. No more riddles, no more dying mid-sentence. What is wearing the crown? And what do you want from us?"

Vess was quiet for a moment. Around the chapel, the candles leaned, all together, all in the same direction, though there was no draft.

"*Sit,*" the god said. "*This is long, and I will only have the strength to say it once.*"

* * *

What Vess told them, in a voice like wind through a keyhole, while the candles burned lower and lower, was this:

Fifteen years ago, King Aldric of Vhalen died, and the kingdom mourned, and his heir—a young man named Aurelian, beloved, gentle, trained in statecraft and mercy—ascended toward the throne. But between the death and the crowning, something entered the palace. Vess did not know its name; the old gods had names for each other the way stars have names for each other, and this thing had never been a star. It was older than naming. It had slept beneath the world since before the first temples, feeding on divinity itself, and the death of a king—anointed, god-blessed, his soul wide open in grief—had been a door.

It took Aurelian. Not his body—his *place*. It poured itself into the shape of him the way water pours into a mold, and it wore him to the coronation, and it wore him through the coronation, and no one knew. No one could know. It was perfect. It smiled Aurelian's smile and grieved Aurelian's griefs and signed Aurelian's decrees, and the decrees began, very slowly, to change.

The old gods felt it happen. Felt their sibling—no, not a sibling, nothing so kin—felt the devourer settle onto the throne of Vhalen like a spider onto a web's center. And they could not fight it openly, because it fed on exactly what they were. Every god that raised its hand against it became a meal.

"So we did the only thing left," Vess said. "We bound."

The Binding had three strands, the god explained, and it spoke them slowly, as if each word cost fuel:

The first strand silenced the gods—sealed the sky, cut the prayers off at the root, starved the devourer of the divine essence it needed to grow beyond its host. The price had been everything: their voices, their presence, their selves, scattered and smothered. Fifteen years of silence. Fifteen years of dying.

The second strand hid the bloodline. Because the devourer's one fear—the one instinct even hunger could not overwrite—was the true royal line, anointed and unbowed, whose blood could unmake it. There had been two princes born to Queen Liora on the same night. Twins. And the Binding scattered them both: one to a throne behind a parasite's smile, his memory folded away so he would not struggle; one to a valley where magic could not follow, his name erased from every record, his very existence filed down to a rumor.

Isandro stopped hearing the rest for a moment.

The candles were burning very low now, and the chapel was going blue and dim, and Vess's outline flickered like a thing seen through heat-haze, and somewhere above them the curfew lamps of Corvane were burning their flat yellow light over a city full of registered souls, and Isandro sat on a bench built by faithful hands and heard a god say *twins* and felt absolutely nothing where the memory should have been.

That was the worst part. Not grief. Grief he could have borne. It was the *nothing*—the smooth blank place where a brother, a mother, a childhood, a whole unlived life should have been, polished flat by fifteen years of a quiet valley that forgot things on purpose.

"Who was the other prince?" he asked. His voice sounded like someone else's.

"*You know who,*" Vess said gently.

"No. I know what you're implying. That's different." He stood up, because sitting felt suddenly like drowning. "You said yourself at the ferry—you collapsed before you finished. You don't actually know. You're guessing. You found a storm-caller in a hidden valley and you built a story around him."

"*I found the valley,*" Vess said, "*because the Binding put you there. I spoke your true name because I have known it since the hour you were born, when every god in Vhalen bent to look at two infants in one cradle. I do not guess, Isandro Almeida. I remember. It is the only thing I have left.*"

"Then say it. Say what I am."

"*You are the son of Queen Liora. You are the twin of the king. And you are the only living soul whose blood can unbind what the Binding bound—including the strand that binds your brother to the thing that wears him.*"

The silence afterward was total. Even the candles seemed to hold their flames still.

Sera rose and came to stand beside him. She didn't touch him. He was distantly grateful for that; he suspected that any touch right now would shatter him like ice on a pond.

"The third strand," she said quietly. "You said the Binding had three strands. Silence, hiding. What was the third?"

Vess dimmed. When it answered, its voice had dropped to almost nothing, and Isandro understood that this was the part the god had climbed out of its own ashes to say.

"*The third strand binds the devourer to its host. It cannot leave Aurelian. It cannot be cast out while the Binding stands. To free the king, the Binding must be unwound—and the devourer will fight the unwinding with everything it has, which is the strength of fifteen years of stolen divinity and a kingdom's worth of fear.*" The two dark points fixed on Isandro. "*It must be done in Ashkarat, in the palace, at the anchor-point beneath the throne room. And it must be done soon. Because the devourer is growing bolder. It has begun to eat what remains of the lesser spirits—the river-mothers, the field-wardens, the small gods of hearth and threshold. Corvane's empty shrines are not emptiness, priestess. They are tableware.*"

"And if we fail?" Sera asked. "If we never reach Ashkarat, or we reach it and die in the attempt?"

"*Then the Binding frays on its own within a year, and the devourer steps out of the king entirely, and there will be no more kings, or gods, or Vhalen. Only appetite, wearing everything.*"

The last candles chose that moment to gutter. Whether it was the god's doing or simple physics, Isandro never learned. The chapel sank into darkness, and in the darkness Vess's outline burned alone for a heartbeat—small, stubborn, a single coal refusing the ash—before it too went out.

In the black, very softly, Sera's hand found his arm.

"Ashkarat is nine hundred miles," she whispered. "Through checkpoints, through the army's supply lines, into the capital of a paranoid god-eating regime. With no papers worth the ink, no allies, no army, and—" her grip tightened fractionally— "one exhausted god and a tanner's son who's never called lightning on purpose in his life."

Above them, muffled by floors and stone, Corvane began to wake: cart wheels, shutters, the boots of the curfew patrols changing shift. Somewhere a mother was calling a child in from a doorway. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a city that didn't know what sat on the throne of its kingdom, eating.

"We'll need papers," Isandro said.

He heard Sera exhale—half laugh, half prayer.

"Then tomorrow," she said, "we commit some forgery."

Outside, far to the east, thunder rolled over mountains he couldn't see, and for the first time in fifteen years, Isandro did not flinch.
