## 9. The Registry

The papers arrived three days later, smelling of ink and candle smoke and something faintly metallic that Isandro chose not to think about.

Sera laid them out on the floor of the safehouse—a rented room above a dyer's shop where the walls were stained the color of old bruises—and knelt over them like a surgeon over instruments. There were four documents: a travel writ bearing the seal of the Corvane harbormaster, two registration cards stamped with the inquisitors' iron wheel, and a letter of passage for "a lay brother of the Mendicant Order of Saint Vellum, traveling under obedience to his superior."

"That's you," she said, tapping the letter. "Brother Anselm of the Quiet Road. A mute lay brother, if anyone asks too many questions."

"I'm not mute."

"You will be." She looked up at him, and there was no humor in her face at all. "Isandro. The less your voice is heard in this city, the longer you live. You have an accent I can't place and a way of speaking that makes people remember you. Mute brothers are invisible. Nobody interrogates what cannot answer."

He wanted to argue. He found he couldn't, quite. Fifteen years in the Valley of Stillness had taught him the shape of silence—how to wear it, how to move inside it without disturbing it—but it had never taught him to love it. Silence had been his shelter and his cage both, and now Sera was asking him to climb back inside willingly.

"And you?" he asked instead.

"Sister Marguerite, widow of a salt merchant, traveling home to her sister in Delmare." She held up one of the registration cards so he could read the name. Her own false name. She had written it with a slight tremor in the letters, or perhaps that was only the lamplight. "The registry office opens at the second bell tomorrow morning. We go early, we stand in line with the fishwives and the pilgrims, we let some bored clerk look at our faces and stamp our cards, and by midday we're registered god-touched citizens of Corvane with permission to walk the streets."

"Registered," Isandro repeated. The word sat wrong in his mouth.

"It's not a choice anymore." Sera began stacking the documents, squaring their edges with unnecessary precision. "Since the edict, every soul in Corvane who can light a candle with a thought or hear a voice in the well has to present themselves. Those who register get a card and a curfew bell. Those who don't register—" She stopped.

"Get disappeared," Isandro said quietly. He had seen the notices pasted on the temple doors, the lists of names in the market square, growing longer each day. He had seen the families standing outside the registry office holding portraits of sons and daughters who had gone in through one door and never come out of any other.

"The ones they take aren't killed," Sera said. "That's the worst part. Killing would be simple. They take them somewhere, and the gods can't find them anymore, and neither can we." She rose, brushing dust from her knees. "So tomorrow we stand in line like everyone else, and we lie with our whole faces, and we live another week. That's the arithmetic of survival now. Small lies, counted daily."

* * *

The registry office had once been a customs house, back when Corvane traded freely with the river cities. Its great hall still bore the faded frescoes of merchant princes shaking hands across painted seas, though someone had whitewashed the faces recently—sloppily, in haste, leaving ghostly outlines like drowned men beneath the waterline. Now the hall was filled with benches, and the benches were filled with people, and the people were very quiet.

Isandro stood in line behind a woman carrying a sleeping child and ahead of two old men who kept touching the small iron wheels stamped on their registration cards as if checking that they hadn't dreamed them. Rain drummed on the high windows. It had been raining since dawn, a gray persistent rain that turned the city's cobbles to dark glass and made the gas lamps halo themselves in mist. Somewhere beyond the roof, thunder muttered—not a storm yet, only the idea of one, circling the coast like a dog that smelled blood.

He felt it the way he felt everything now: in his teeth, in the roots of his hair, in the scar-tissue quietness of his chest where magic used to live before the valley gave it back. Since crossing the rim, since leveling that ridge with a scream of unspent power, the weather had become a language he could almost read. This rain was honest rain. The thunder was far away and meant nothing. He tried to take comfort in that.

Sera stood beside him in her widow's gray, hands folded, eyes down, projecting the particular invisibility of a woman who has been poor and tired for years. Only her thumb moved, tracing the edge of her forged card, over and over.

The line moved forward in small shuffling increments. At the head of the hall, behind a table scarred with candle burns, sat three clerks of the inquisition—pale men in dark coats who examined faces with the flat patience of butchers. Beside each clerk stood a soldier, and beside each soldier, something worse: a thin man in a gray hood whose eyes never moved at all, who simply watched each registrant pass with the fixed attention of a man reading a page.

Wardens, Isandro realized, and his stomach went cold. Or men trained in the warden ways. The Valley of Stillness had sent its children out into the world sometimes—he'd known that even then, dimly, from the ferryman's drunken stories. Gray-robed, oath-sworn, mute until their oaths released them. Enforcers of magiclessness. And now the usurper had bought them, or borrowed them, and set them in customs houses to smell out the god-touched like rot in grain.

Don't look at them, he told himself. Look at the floor. Be Brother Anselm. Be nothing.

The woman ahead of him reached the table. The clerk took her card, asked her three questions in a bored voice—name, parish, nature of the gift—and she answered each one in a whisper, her hand tight on her child's shoulder. The hooded warden leaned in, breathed once, slowly, like a man tasting soup, and nodded. Stamp. Next.

Then it was Sera's turn, and Isandro watched her transform. Her shoulders rounded; her chin dipped; her voice came out roughened and apologetic, the voice of a woman apologizing for taking up space in the world. *Marguerite Voss, parish of Saltrow, gift of small knowing—she could tell when bread was going bad, your honor, nothing useful, nothing anyone would want.* The clerk barely looked up. The warden breathed and nodded. Stamp.

"Next. The brother."

Isandro stepped forward.

He had prepared everything. His eyes down, his hands folded in the sleeves of the mendicant's robe Sera had stolen from a laundry line, his mouth arranged around the comfortable muteness of a man who has not spoken in years. The clerk gestured impatiently for his card. He passed it over. The clerk read it, grunted, and reached for the stamp—

And the warden in the gray hood went very still.

It was a small thing, the stillness. No one else in the hall noticed. But Isandro had spent fifteen years among wardens; he knew their bodies the way a sailor knows weather. He saw the breath stop halfway. He saw the head tilt, two degrees, the way a hound tilts its head at a scent carried wrong on the wind. He saw the hooded face turn toward him with the slow inevitability of a door swinging open.

The warden's lips moved. No sound came out—the man was still under vow, perhaps, or simply careful—but Isandro read the shapes of the words as clearly as if they had been shouted.

*I know you.*

Fifteen years ago, a boy had come down into Stillwater village in the dead of night, half-dead of cold, delivered by a warden escort that vanished before dawn. One of those escorts had been young. One of them had lingered a moment at the valley's edge, looking back at the boy stumbling toward the firelight, and had made a gesture—small, strange, almost tender—that the boy had never forgotten and never understood.

This was that face, older. Lined now around the eyes. The same gesture beginning again, the hand rising—

The clerk stamped the card. "Move along."

Isandro moved along. He did not run. Running was a confession written on the body. He walked, one step and another, toward the great doors at the end of the hall, and he felt the warden's attention on his back like a hand pressed between his shoulder blades, and he heard—faintly, beneath the rain, beneath the muttering thunder—the sound of a chair being pushed back.

Outside, the rain had thickened. Sera was waiting under the eaves, already reaching for his arm, already saying something about the harbor road, the afternoon boat, and then she saw his face and stopped talking.

"We have to go," Isandro said. "Now. Not the boat. Not the harbor. Anywhere else."

"What happened—"

"He knew me. A warden. From the valley." His voice was low and fast. "Sera, he *knew me*, and he works for them now, and in one minute he's going to walk out that door and point at me, and everything we've built in this city comes down."

She didn't waste breath on questions. She caught his sleeve and pulled him left, away from the main street, into the tangle of lanes behind the old customs house where Corvane kept its poverty: dyers' yards and tanneries, rope walks and bone-boilers, streets so narrow the upper stories leaned together overhead like conspirators. The rain ran in channels down the center of the cobbles. Their footsteps splashed loud in the gray hush.

Behind them, distantly, a whistle blew. Then another, answering it, from a different quarter. Then a third.

"They're calling the watch," Sera said grimly. "Curfew sweep. Or they've put your description on the bells already." She stopped at a corner, pressed herself against the wet stone, and peered around it. Isandro crowded close enough to feel her breath. "There's a rooftop route over the tannery roofs to the river gate. Smugglers use it. If we can reach the gate before they seal the quarter—"

The whistle sounded again, much closer, and this time it was followed by boots. Many boots. Splashing through the flooded lane behind them, coming fast.

They ran.

* * *

Corvane's rooftops were a second city, older and wilder than the first—a country of slate ridges and moss-slick tiles, of chimney forests and clotheslines strung between towers like rigging, all of it silvered and treacherous under the rain. Sera climbed like she had been born to it, hauling herself up a drainpipe with a economy of motion that told Isandro this was not her first flight, and he followed, his mendicant's robes soaked and heavy, his lungs burning with cold air.

Below and behind, the watch poured into the streets they'd abandoned. He could see them through gaps between chimneys: dark uniforms moving in disciplined lines, sealing lane mouths, driving the few pedestrians indoors with flat shouts. And above them, on the neighboring roofline, moving with none of the soldiers' noise—

Gray hoods. Three of them. Running the ridgelines like ghosts, closing the distance with terrible patience.

"They're not shouting," Isandro panted, scrambling over a ridgepole. "Why aren't they shouting?"

"Because they don't need to." Sera's voice was flat. "Wardens don't herd. They corner. They'll drive us toward the river and wait for us at the edges. It's how they cleared the valley of everything wild, isn't it? Patience and geometry."

He hated that she knew that. He hated more that she was right. He could feel it happening—the routes below filling, the whistles falling silent as the net closed, the gray figures on the roofs no longer hurrying because hurrying was no longer necessary. Ahead, the tannery district ended at a sheer drop to the canal, thirty feet of wet brick down to black water. Behind, the wardens walked. Between, a hundred feet of open rooftops, and nowhere to go but across.

They crossed anyway. Slate slid under Isandro's boots; he caught a chimney and swung around it, heart slamming. Halfway across the last roof, his foot found a patch of ice-slick moss and the world tilted, and suddenly he was sliding, arms windmilling, toward the edge and the long fall to the canal—and a hand closed on his wrist. Sera, braced against a vent stack, her face white with effort, holding him over the void.

"Don't," she gritted, "you dare," and hauled him back onto the ridge.

For a moment they clung together, breathing hard, rain streaming down their faces. Below them the canal churned. Above them the sky had gone the color of a bruise, and the thunder that had been muttering all day was no longer muttering. It had come inland while they ran. It stood now over the harbor in a vast slow column of cloud, and lightning flickered inside it like thoughts in a dark mind, and Isandro felt it notice him.

That was the only word for it. *Notice.* The storm had been weather all day, indifferent, mechanical. Now it was aware. It hung over the city like a great ear, listening, and what it listened for was him.

"Not here," he whispered—to Sera, to himself, to the sky. "Not now."

But the wardens had reached the near edge of the roof. Four of them now, not three, spread along the ridgeline in a loose crescent, cutting off retreat. In the lead was the one who had known him, and the man had thrown back his hood at last, and his face in the storm-light was calm and sorrowful and absolutely certain.

His lips moved again. This time, sound came with them—thin, rusty, the voice of a man breaking a fifteen-year vow of silence, and it carried across the rain with unnatural clarity, the way warden voices always had:

"*Storm-caller.* By the edict of the Regent-who-wears-the-crown, I am commanded to take you alive. Come down, and no one else needs to be hurt."

Alive. The word landed strangely. Not *dead*, not *destroyed*—alive, taken, delivered. Someone wanted him breathing. Someone in Ashkarat had given orders with his name in them, and the specificity of that frightened Isandro more than any blade could have.

"There's nowhere to go," the warden said gently. "I watched them bring you into the valley, boy. I carried the order myself. I know what you are better than you do. Come down."

And Isandro—who had spent fifteen years being no one, who had learned to fold himself small as a seed, who had crossed the rim and leveled a mountain ridge and fled across half of Vhalen telling himself that if he was only quiet enough, only careful enough, the storm in him would sleep—felt something in his chest answer the thunder overhead.

"No," he said.

The warden sighed, and raised his hand, and the four gray figures began to advance across the wet slate.

What happened next, Isandro would spend many nights afterward trying to reconstruct, and never entirely succeed. He remembered deciding to run for the drainpipe at the roof's far corner. He remembered the warden leader moving to cut him off, quick and silent on the slick tiles. He remembered the man's hand rising—not a weapon, just a hand, just fingers spreading wide to catch or seize or push—and he remembered the sky answering.

Not summoned. Not called. *Answered.* As if the storm had been waiting all day for permission, and his terror was permission, and the whole accumulated weight of the century's weather came down through him in a single white instant.

Lightning struck the rooftop.

It struck *through* him—that was the horror of it, he was not the target but the conductor, the rod, the door—and it leapt from his outflung hand into the warden's chest and the man lit up from within like a lantern, every seam of him shining, and then he was thrown backward off the ridge with a sound Isandro would never forget: not a scream, but a short sharp exhalation, almost a sigh, the sound of a vow finally ended.

The thunderclap arrived a heartbeat later and shattered every window in the tannery district.

Isandro stood on the smoking rooftop with his arm still extended and the rain hissing to steam around him, and looked at where the warden had been. The man lay crumpled on the canal-side cobbles far below, small with distance, his gray robe darkening with water that was not rain. The other wardens had frozen, staring—at him, at their fallen brother, at the sky—with an expression Isandro recognized from mirrors, from his own worst nights in the valley: the expression of people watching the impossible happen and understanding that the world would never be safe again.

He had killed a man. With the sky. Without meaning to, without choosing, in half a heartbeat, the way a sleeper strikes out in a nightmare.

"Isandro." Sera's hand on his shoulder, hard, shaking. "Isandro, look at me. We have to move *now*. Every eye in this city just saw that. Every scryer, every priest, every informer—they saw. Do you understand? You're not hidden anymore. You're *known*."

He knew. Even as she pulled him toward the drainpipe, even as his legs obeyed her and his mind stayed standing on that smoking ridge, he knew. Somewhere across the rain-dark miles, in a palace in Ashkarat, something wearing a crown had just felt a storm-caller announce himself to the world like a shout in a tomb. Somewhere closer, in the inquisitors' tower, a clerk was already writing his description. And somewhere in the underworld of Corvane, in the smugglers' dens and the underground chapels, the story was already being born—the story of the stranger who had called lightning in the tannery district and killed a servant of the Regent with his bare hand and the sky's consent.

They went down the drainpipe together, fast and graceless, and vanished into the labyrinth of the lower city. Behind them, the storm rolled on over the harbor, spending itself against the sea, and the rain came down harder than ever, washing the rooftops clean, washing away footprints, washing away everything except the thing that could not be washed away.

In a doorway, pressed against him in the dark while soldiers ran past, Sera spoke against his ear, and her voice was not gentle at all.

"Do you understand what you've done? Not the killing—I mean the rest. You've told the whole world you exist." A pause. Rain roared in the street. "And now the whole world gets to decide what it wants to do about you. The Regent wants you alive, that warden said. Alive, and delivered. Do you want to know why, Isandro? Because things that are feared are also *wanted*. And every power in Vhalen is going to want you now."

Above the lower city, far away, the thunder rolled one last time and began, slowly, to move out to sea.

It sounded, Isandro thought, like laughter.
