## 10. Fugitive Weather

The rumor reached Sera the way all true things reached her in Corvane: sideways, in pieces, wrapped in lies.

She was haggling for lamp oil at the Salt Market when a fishwife leaned close over the eels and said, "Did you hear? Lightning on the Tanners' Row, three nights past. Struck one man dead out of a clear sky. Clear sky, mind you—rain falling, but no thunder owed to anyone." She said it the way people said such things now, half-terrified, half-hungry, the way the starving spoke of bread.

"Drunk," Sera said. "Or cursed."

"Cursed by what?" The fishwife's eyes were bright. "The priests are gone. Who curses anymore?" And then, lower, with a glance toward the corner where the usurper's criers posted their edicts: "They say he came off the roof like a man made of storm. They say the god-touched are waking up."

Sera paid for her oil and walked back through the crooked streets with her heart beating strangely, because she knew exactly which rooftop, exactly which rain-slicked night, exactly whose hands had torn lightning down from a sky that had been silent for fifteen years.

*He killed him,* she thought, and was surprised to find that the thought carried no horror in it—only a cold, precise fear of what would come next. Not for the warden. Wardens had burned her temple with everyone inside it. But Corvane's underworld talked faster than its criers could post edicts, and by tomorrow every ear in the city would have heard the same story wearing ten different clothes: a storm-caller loose in Corvane, an omen, a weapon, a prize.

By the time she reached the underground chapel beneath the tannery district, where candle flames bent wrong and gathered into something almost like a small god, the story had already grown a second head.

* * *

Vess flickered on the altar—a guttering congregation of candle-flames that never melted, arranged in a shape too small to be a god and too purposeful to be fire. It had been stronger since the chapel; the old stones still remembered what they were built for. But it spoke sparingly, as though words cost it wax.

"The tale has crossed the river twice," Vess said when Sera came down the stairs. Its voice was the sound of a flame finding its wick. "In one version you are his captive. In another, his priestess. In the third, you do not appear at all."

"Which is closest?"

"The third." A flame leaned. "But versions are weather, priestess. What matters is the wind behind them. The Regent's inquisitors will hear the wind by morning."

Sera set down the oil and looked around the chapel—at the soot-black saints, the drowned font, the low ceiling sweating river-damp. Isandro sat against the far wall with his knees drawn up, staring at his own hands as if they belonged to a stranger who had recently done something unforgivable with them.

He hadn't spoken since the rooftop. Three days of silence, broken only by the smallest necessary words: *yes, no, pass the bread.* He slept badly and woke worse, and once Sera had found him standing at the top of the cellar stairs with his face turned toward the distant sound of thunder, flinching from it like a man listening for footsteps.

"You should eat," she said now, setting bread beside him.

"I'm not hungry."

"You're always not hungry. It's becoming a habit, and habits become graves." She sat down across from him. "You killed a man."

His jaw tightened. "I know what I did."

"Do you? Because you've spent three days punishing yourself instead of asking the only question that matters, which is whether it was necessary. It was. He'd have put irons on you and delivered you to Maren's people, and they don't ask questions—they collect answers out of people like marrow out of bone."

"He was begging." Isandro's voice was flat, scraped raw. "At the end. When the lightning had him. He was begging, and some part of me—" His hands curled. "Some part of me wanted to see it through. That's what frightens me. Not the killing. The wanting."

Sera studied him for a long moment. Fifteen years in a valley without magic, and the first time it flooded back into him, it had come through his grief and terror like water through a cracked dam—and still, underneath, there had been *will*. She had seen trained callers work, before the silence. They were choirboys compared to whatever slept in this man.

"That wanting," she said carefully, "is not evil. It's power noticing itself. Every caller I ever met felt it. The good ones learned to hold it the way you hold a hawk—not by crushing it, but by keeping your arm steady."

"And if my arm isn't steady?"

"Then we make it steady. Together." She stood. "Which brings me to why I woke you from your penance. We're leaving Corvane tonight or we're not leaving at all."

That got his eyes up. "The papers—"

"The papers are ash. You saw to that on the rooftop. Your face is known now, Isandro. Not your name—the underworld doesn't trade in names—but your *shape*. Storm-caller, tall, dark-haired, travels with a defrocked priestess. There are already men drawing that shape on slate for coin." She let that land. "I've made arrangements. Smugglers. The Guild of the Gray Wake runs goods downriver to Delmare, and their master owes me a debt from the burning years. We go tonight, by barge, through the marsh gates."

Isandro rose slowly. "And after?"

"After, we stop running like criminals and start moving like pilgrims. There's a difference, and it matters." She held his gaze. "You want to disappear. I can see it in you—you've been disappearing for fifteen years and your body remembers how. But hiding is how the usurper won the first time, Isandro. The gods went quiet, the faithful scattered, the true heir was erased—and everyone who could have spoken chose silence instead. Silence is not safety. Silence is just a slower death."

"I didn't choose silence," he said quietly. "It was chosen for me."

"Then you above all people should know what it costs."

The words hung between them, sharper than she'd meant them. He looked away first, and Sera felt the argument settle into her chest like a stone dropping into a well—sunk, but not dissolved.

* * *

The Guild of the Gray Wake kept its counting-house behind a fish-pickle warehouse near the Marsh Gate, and its master received them in a room that smelled of brine, tallow, and old money laundered through newer crimes.

Master Quill—or so he was called, though Sera suspected the name was stolen from a dead man—was small and neat and had the eyes of someone who had counted everything worth stealing in Corvane twice. He poured three cups of rough plum wine and pushed two of them across the table.

"Sera Voss," he said. "Fifteen years, and you come to my door smelling of tanneries and trouble."

"Fifteen years ago you were smuggling relics out of my temple while it burned. You owe me."

"I owe you for the reliquary I sold and never reported. Fine. Debts remembered." He sipped. "Now. My people tell me a story about a man made of lightning, and my people tell me you walk with a man shaped exactly like that story. Do you see my difficulty? Right now, the Regent's inquisitors pay twenty crowns for rumors and two hundred for the man himself. You sit in my counting-house drinking my wine. A greedy man would already be rich."

"But you're not greedy," Sera said. "You're careful. And careful men know that the Regent's gold spends badly these days—inquisitors take payment and then take the payer, on principle. Everyone in this room knows someone who's vanished after selling god-touched to the crown." She leaned forward. "Also, you're a smuggler, Quill, and smugglers survive by reading weather. So read it. The gods are answering prayers again. The Regent is registering and disappearing anyone the gods touch. Which side of that do you want your guild standing on when it breaks open?"

Quill was quiet for a moment, turning his cup. "And if I say the safe side is the Regent's?"

"Then I'll know you're lying, because you lit a candle to Vess last winter. Don't look surprised—your dock clerk prays at the drowned shrine under Marsh Bridge, and the candle smoke bends toward him when he does. You've been waiting fifteen years for permission to believe again, Master Quill. I'm here to sell it to you cheap."

Something flickered in the little man's face—fear, hope, and the particular exhaustion of a man who has kept a secret so long it has grown teeth. He set down his cup.

"Two berths on the *Gray Daughter*, leaving on tonight's tide through the marsh gates. You'll ride in the wool hold with the contraband. You touch nothing, you bless nothing, and if inquisitors board us, you're a wool-factor's widow and her mute brother." His eyes flicked to Isandro, silent in the corner. "Can he be mute?"

"No," Isandro said.

Quill smiled thinly. "He can learn. Passage isn't free even against a debt. I want one thing: when the sky opens—and it will open, priestess, the whole city feels it grinding like a bad tooth—I want my guild remembered kindly by whoever ends up holding it. King, council, or god. Someone powerful owes the Gray Wake a favor. That's my price."

"You'll have your favor," Sera said. "Witnessed before Vess itself."

Quill's eyebrows rose a fraction—the first genuine expression she'd seen on him. "Before a god. Hm. Then I suppose I'd best deliver you alive." He stood. "Tide turns at moonrise. Don't be late. The marsh gates only open once, and the mud keeps what it takes."

* * *

They walked to the docks in the blue hour, hooded, unhurried, two more shadows among the curfew-thinned crowds. Isandro had not argued further, but Sera could feel the argument still living in him, unspoken, like weather pressure before a storm.

It broke at the quay.

"We should go north," he said suddenly, stopping in the lee of a rope warehouse. "Not downriver. North, over the hills, alone. If I split off from you—if I'm just one shape instead of two—the scrying loses half its thread. You could go back to Delmare, live—"

"Stop." Sera turned to face him fully. "Do you truly think they're tracking *us*? They're tracking *you*, and not with dogs—with doctrine. You are the first storm-caller seen in Vhalen in fifteen years, Isandro. You are proof the silence is breaking. There is nowhere north of here where that fact stops being true. You could bury yourself in a glacier and the meaning of you would follow." She stepped closer, lowering her voice. "And you'd be doing it alone, half-trained, hunted, carrying a mission a dying god gave you. Tell me honestly—which part of that plan is for the mission, and which part is for your guilt?"

His breath caught. For a moment she thought he'd strike out with words, and she braced for it—she'd been struck by better believers in worse moods.

"It should have been possible," he said finally, and his voice cracked on it. "To just live. Fifteen years I lived. I planted things, Sera. I knew my neighbors' children by name. I was *nobody*, and nobody was allowed to sleep at night." His hands trembled at his sides, and the lanterns along the quay guttered in sympathy, every flame leaning toward him like iron filings toward a lodestone. "And then a god said my name and it all burned down in a week. So forgive me if hiding looks like the only sane thing left."

The lantern flames steadied. Around them, the dockworkers had gone quiet, sensing something they couldn't name.

Sera softened. "I know what it is to lose a life you built," she said. "My temple was my whole world. I had a cell six feet wide and I loved it more than kings love palaces. When it burned, I didn't just lose a building. I lost the person I was inside it." She took his hand—cold, trembling, crackling faintly, like touching a fence before lightning. "But here is what fifteen years of hollow faith taught me: the self you were is not the only self you get. There are others waiting downstream. You have to sail to meet them."

Isandro looked down at their joined hands. The crackling faded. When he raised his eyes, something in them had shifted—not peace, nothing so easy, but a truce. The argument wasn't over; it had simply become something they carried together instead of against each other.

"Together, then," he said. "Downriver. But when this is done—when the king is free or we're dead—remind me to argue with you properly. I feel I've barely started."

"You haven't," Sera said. "I'm told I'm very patient."

* * *

The *Gray Daughter* was a squat, tarred barge with wool stacked high enough to hide a chapel, and by moonrise they were wedged in its hold between bales that smelled of lanolin and long roads. Through a seam in the hull planks, Sera watched the marsh gates open—a pair of black timber doors in a moss-eaten wall, swung wide by chains groaning like church bells—and the river take them.

Corvane slid away behind them: the curfew lanterns, the criers' boards, the rooftops where a man had died in rain and lightning. Somewhere in that sprawl, inquisitors were offering twenty crowns for stories about him, and somewhere above it all, the clouds had begun to gather again, low and thoughtful, following the barge like patient dogs.

Isandro sat across from her in the dark, and for a long time neither of them spoke. The wool ticked and settled. Water whispered against the hull.

"In the valley," he said at last, quietly, "there was a woman. Odile. The shrine keeper." His fingers worked at a loose wool fiber. "She gave me a letter sealed with a royal crest. One line: *the crown lies*. I've been trying not to think about what it means—that someone planned all this. My exile. My ignorance. Somebody decided, fifteen years ago, that I would be safer not knowing my own life." He looked up. "What if they were right? What if every step we take toward Ashkarat is just walking deeper into someone else's design?"

"Then we walk anyway," Sera said. "Designs can be survived. Silence can't." She pulled her cloak tighter. "Besides, whoever designed it left you one thing they couldn't seal in a letter or bind in a valley." She nodded at the seam of gray light, at the clouds massing beyond it. "They left you the sky. Whatever else was taken from you, Isandro—the sky remembers you. Tonight it's been trailing us for thirty miles, waiting to be asked."

He turned toward the light. And Sera watched his expression change—the guilt still there, the grief still there, but threaded now with something older and larger, something that predated both. Recognition. The look of a man hearing music he'd forgotten he knew.

Above the marshes, thunder rolled—distant, questioning, almost courteous.

"Not yet," Isandro murmured to it, and Sera's breath stopped, because he said it the way you speak to a horse you trust, not the way you speak to a weapon. "When I'm ready. When we're ready."

The thunder receded, grumbling, like something enormous settling back to wait.

Sera leaned her head against the wool and closed her eyes, and for the first time since her temple burned, prayer came to her unbidden—not words, just a reaching, a hand extended into the dark. And in the dark, faint as a candle three rooms away, something reached back.

She slept. And dreamed of a river widening toward the sea, and of two figures walking its banks—one crowned, one cloaked—whose faces she could not see, only that they walked in step, and that ahead of them, at the river's end, something vast and hungry was waiting with its mouth already open.

She woke with dawn graying the hold and Isandro shaking her shoulder, his face pale in the half-light.

"Sera," he said. "There's a rider on the towpath. Gray robes. He's been matching us since the gates." A pause, and then, quieter: "He's not riding hard. He's riding *certain*."
