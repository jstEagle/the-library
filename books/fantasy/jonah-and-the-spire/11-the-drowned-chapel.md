## 11. The Drowned Chapel

The river took them down through country Isandro had never seen, and every mile of it was a country that had never seen him.

That was the thought he kept returning to as the smuggler's flat-bottomed barge slid through the morning fog—*you are a stranger here twice over*. Once because he had spent fifteen years behind the valley's rim, and once because, apparently, he had been made a stranger to himself. He still didn't know what Vess had meant. He wasn't sure he wanted to know yet. The god's words had been few and strange: *the records survive, if the water has been kind. Follow the drowned bell.*

Sera sat at the bow with her hood back, letting the mist bead on her close-cropped hair, watching the banks slide past. She had barely spoken since Corvane. When she did speak, it was in that flat, careful voice of hers—the voice of a woman rationing out hope like bread in a siege.

"You keep looking at your hands," she said, without turning around.

"I'm trying to remember if they've always done this." He flexed his fingers. Since the rooftop, since the lightning had come out of him like a scream, there was a faint tremor that never quite left. Or maybe there wasn't. Maybe he was inventing it.

"Storm-callers shake," Sera said. "My order trained them, before. They said the body remembers what the sky wants even when the mind doesn't. It's not damage. It's *listening*."

"And you know this from books."

"I know this from burning them." She turned then, and there was something almost like humor in her face, dry as old paper. "We burned most of our own library when the temple fell. Heretical texts, the new regime called them. I saved what I could carry. Three books and a reliquary that turned out to hold someone's grandmother's teeth." A pause. "One of the books mentions the Binding. Barely. Half a page, half mad. It says the silence of the gods was not a dying but a *deed*."

Isandro looked at the fog. "Vess said the chapel is real. That's more than half a page."

"Vess also said," Sera said quietly, "that the thing we're looking for was written by people who wanted it forgotten. Be careful what you ask the water for, Isandro. Sometimes it answers."

* * *

They found the chapel on the third day, where the river widened into a drowned forest and the hills came down soft and green to meet the flood.

You smelled it before you saw it: old stone under water, the mineral breath of submerged masonry, and beneath that something sweeter—candle wax gone rancid, incense rotted into earth. Then the trees thinned and there it was, rising out of the brown water like the shoulder of a sleeping animal: a chapel sunk to its roofline, its bell tower leaning at a drunkard's angle, the bell itself long since fallen silent—or so they thought.

As their barge drifted closer, Isandro heard it.

Not ringing. Humming. A low, continuous tone beneath the hearing, felt in the teeth and the breastbone rather than the ears, and beside him Sera went rigid and made a small sound, half prayer, half grief.

"It's consecrated ground," she whispered. "Even drowned. Even forgotten. The gods used to answer here, and the stones remember answering."

In the bottom of the boat, in the space between two crates of salted fish, a candle flame stood up out of nothing—a thumb-height flicker of gold that had no wick and needed none. Vess. The god had traveled with them since Corvane in fragments, gathering itself from any open flame, and its voice came now thin and sputtering, like speech through a keyhole.

"*Here.* The vestry stair. Under the east window. The water has been... kinder than the men who came after."

The east window was just visible above the surface, a lancet arch filled not with glass but with a lattice of stone tracery, and through its gaps the flooded interior breathed cold air that smelled of centuries. They tied the barge to a dead cypress, and Sera went first down the vestry stair—which began above the waterline and descended into green-black murk—and found, as Vess had promised, that the stair turned and continued *up*, or seemed to; the chapel had settled unevenly over the years, tilting so that one corner of its interior stood clear of the flood, a triangular attic of dry stone beneath the sagging roof, lit by shafts of gray light falling through gaps in the tiles.

It was the driest room Isandro had ever seen that was also, technically, underwater.

Shelves lined the surviving walls. Most had collapsed into rot, their contents slumped into pulp, but one wall—sheltered by the tilt, facing away from the prevailing wind—still held its books and ledgers and scroll-cases, swollen with damp but whole. And on a lectern bolted to the floor at the center of the dry triangle, weighted down with a stone paperweight shaped like a dove, lay an open register whose pages had been protected by waxed leather and fifteen years of patient darkness.

Sera lit a candle from Vess's flame—with a reverence that told Isandro she was handling fire the way other people handled relics—and carried it to the lectern with hands that were not quite steady.

"This is a parish record," she said. "Baptisms. Marriages. Deaths." She frowned. "But it's not a village parish. Look at the names. These are court names. These are—" She stopped.

She stopped because the register was not organized the way registers are organized. There were no years. Instead each entry began with a phrase, inked in a formal ecclesiastical hand:

*Recorded in the year the gods went silent.*

*Recorded in the first year of silence.*

*Recorded in the second year of silence.*

And then, near the end, in a different hand—shakier, hurried, the hand of someone writing against time:

*Recorded in the fifteenth year of silence, which is to say, recorded too late, recorded in defiance, recorded because someone must.*

Sera read aloud, slowly, translating the formal script into plain speech as she went, and the chapel's dripping dark listened with them.

"'Let it be known to whatever future finds this page,'" she read, "'that the silence of the gods was not their death. It was a binding, performed in the night of the twins' birth, by the last nine voices of the old faith, at the queen's command and the queen's cost. We bound the sky shut to hide a bloodline from a hunger that walks. We silenced heaven to buy fifteen years.'"

Isandro's mouth had gone dry. "Fifteen years," he said. His voice sounded very far away to him. "The valley. My valley. The Stillness. Fifteen years."

"Keep reading," Sera said, though her own voice had thinned to wire.

"'Two princes were born on the same night, of Queen Liora and King Aldric, heirs of Vhalen both. The hunger came for the crown, as it always comes for crowns. We could not kill it. We could only hide what it sought. So we hid the bloodline—one son behind the throne, one son beyond the world. One wears the crown. One was carried out of history entirely, his name struck from every record, his memory taken with it, so that no scrying, no divination, no hungry god could ever find him by looking for a prince.'"

The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft. In the boat above, faintly, Vess's ember-voice said something that might have been "*I'm sorry*" or might have been the water lapping.

Sera turned the page. The next entry was shorter. It was written larger, as if the writer had needed to shout on paper.

"'We could not erase a prince completely. Erasure leaves a wound, and wounds can be traced. So we left one seam. One thread. This register, in this chapel, drowned and forgotten, holds the single true account. Two princes. Born the same night. One name may be spoken: Aurelian, heir to the crown of Vhalen. The other name is written here once and nowhere else in the world, and we have sealed it under the rite of unremembering, so that even we who wrote it cannot recall it. If you are reading this, and the name below means nothing to you—if you look at it and feel nothing, know nothing, remember nothing—then either you are no one, or you are the one the erasure was made for.'"

Below that line, in ink that had faded to the color of dried blood, was a single word.

Isandro read it.

He read it again.

Nothing happened.

That was the horror of it—not recognition, not shock, not the thunderclap of a stolen life returning. Nothing happened at all. He looked at the name written in a dead priest's desperate hand and it meant exactly as much to him as a stranger's name in a stranger's book, and he understood, with a vertigo so total it was almost peaceful, that this was precisely what the entry had promised. *If you look at it and feel nothing, know nothing, remember nothing—then you are the one the erasure was made for.*

He was standing in a drowned chapel reading his own name, and it was a stranger's name.

"Isandro." Sera's hand was on his arm. He hadn't noticed her turn. "Your face. What does it say? What's the name?"

"That's the thing," he heard himself say. His voice was calm in a way that frightened him more than screaming would have. "I don't—I can read it fine. It's right there. And it's nothing. It's *nothing*, Sera. It slides off me. Like reading a word in a language I've decided not to learn." He laughed, one dry note with no humor in it. "Fifteen years ago someone took a baby out of the world and sewed the hole shut behind him, and I've been living in the hole ever since, calling it a life."

"Isandro—"

"No, wait." He pulled the register closer, scanning the final entries, the shaky hand hurrying toward its end. "'The binding holds while the valley holds. The valley holds while the silence holds. If the gods begin to answer again, the seam will fray, and the erased name will begin to leak back into the world. When that happens, the hunger will smell it. It hunts by memory. It eats what is remembered.'" He stopped. "That's why Vess found me. That's why the wardens are asking questions. The moment the old gods woke up, I started *existing* again."

"Then we leave," Sera said instantly. "Now. Tonight. We take the register and we go somewhere—"

"Where?" He gestured at the shelves, the rot, the drowned bell humming its one dead note overhead. "Every record of what I am is in this room, and every one of them says the same thing: there is a king in Ashkarat wearing my family's crown, and something else is wearing *him*, and the only person alive who might be able to unmake that is a man who doesn't know his own name." He closed the register gently, the way you'd close a door on a sickroom. "Vess said the records would tell us how to reach the capital. Let's find that part."

They searched the remaining shelves while the light through the broken tiles went from gray to the color of weak tea. Most of it was ash—parish accounts, hymnals slumped into black lace—but behind a loose stone in the vestry wall Sera found a lead scroll-case, sealed with wax stamped by a dove, and inside it a letter that had survived because lead does not care about water or years.

It was addressed, in the same shaking hand, *To the one who reads this and feels nothing.*

Sera handed it to him without a word.

He broke the seal. The paper inside was thin as onion skin and covered front and back in cramped writing, and he read it by candlelight in a chapel that had been drowned for longer than he had been alive, and the letter began:

*You will not remember your mother, and that was the price, and I agreed to it, and I have regretted it every day since in ways you cannot imagine and I hope you never learn. But if the seam has frayed, if you are reading this, then the fifteen years are spent, and you must know three things.*

*First: you are loved. Whatever else was taken, that was not. Love is not a memory. It is the shape memory leaves behind.*

*Second: your brother did not choose this. Neither did you. Do not let anyone—god, priest, or king—tell you that you were abandoned. You were hidden. There is a difference, and it is the whole difference, and I spent my last strength making sure someone would someday tell you so.*

*Third: the thing that hunts our blood cannot be killed while it wears the crown. It can only be unbound from the one it wears, and the unbinding requires the true heir—blood and voice and name. Your name is in the register. Your name is in you, even if you cannot hear it. Go to Ashkarat. Find your brother. And when you stand in front of him, and he looks at you and sees a stranger—because he will, they took his memories too, differently but just as wholly—when that happens, do not let it break you. Grief is the tax on love. Pay it. Then keep walking.*

There was no signature. There didn't need to be. At the bottom, pressed hard enough to tear the paper, were two words in a woman's hand:

*Mother. Forgive.*

Isandro did not remember crying. He became aware, some time later, that he was sitting on the vestry floor with the letter in his lap and Sera's arm around his shoulders and the candle burned down to a stub, and the drowned bell overhead was still humming its note, and the water in the nave below had begun, very softly, to move—as if something far downstream had shifted its weight.

Vess's flame stood in the dark of the doorway, smaller than before. Gods, Isandro was learning, shrank when they told the truth.

"*Now you know what you are,*" the god said. "*Or at least what was done to you. The rest—the name, the face, the boy who was carried out of the world—he is not lost, storm-caller. He is folded. And folded things can be opened.*"

"How?" Isandro asked. His voice came out scraped raw.

"*In Ashkarat. Beneath the throne. Where the binding was made, it can be unmade. But the road is long, and the hunger has begun to smell you, and there is one in the usurper's service who hunts the god-touched with a patience colder than mine ever was.*" The flame guttered, steadied. "*You should know this before you sleep tonight: the letters, the register, the erasure—they all agree on one point. The unbinding requires the true heir. Blood and voice and name. And there are two princes, and the records do not say which of you the ritual needs.*"

Sera went very still. "What do you mean, which?"

"*I mean,*" said Vess, in a voice like the last inch of a candle, "*that the queen hid one son beyond the world and one son behind the throne, and the binding was made to protect the bloodline, not to distinguish between its branches. The ritual may need the king. It may need the exile. It may need both. And I am not certain the people who wrote these records knew either.*"

Outside, far upriver, thunder spoke once—distant, directionless, a sound with no storm attached to it yet. Isandro felt it in his teeth, in the tremor in his hands, in the place behind his ribs where fifteen years of silence had taught him to flinch.

The valley's quiet was over. The world had remembered him, and the world was coming to collect.

He folded his mother's letter along its worn creases and put it inside his shirt, over his heart, where a name should have been.

"We go to Ashkarat," he said.
