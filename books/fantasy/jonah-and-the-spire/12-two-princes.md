## 12. Two Princes

Gods do not sleep the way mortals sleep. What Vess did in the drowned chapel's dark was older than sleep and stranger: it sank into itself like a stone into deep water, down through its own thinning layers, to the place where it kept what it remembered.

And Vess remembered everything.

That was the cruelty of divinity, if you wanted to call it a cruelty—Vess had never been sure. Mortals got to forget. They woke each morning washed clean by the night, their griefs softened at the edges, their shames worn smooth as river stones. A god woke with every morning it had ever witnessed still burning behind its eyes, undimmed, unblurred, waiting. Fifteen years of silence had not dimmed Vess. Silence had only given it nowhere to put what it knew.

So it went down through itself now, past the candle-flame body Sera had tended all night, past the guttering wick of its attention, down into the memory that had no edges, and it opened the sealed room at the bottom the way a mourner opens a door they have been avoiding for years.

* * *

It began, as so many ruinous things do, with a birth.

Two princes, born on the same night in the same hour of the same labor, in the high white birthing room of Ashkarat's palace while a storm stood over the city like a sentry. Queen Liora labored through the thunder, and the old gods leaned close to watch, because the bloodline of Vhalen had always run thick with sky, and this generation promised to run thicker than any before it.

Vess had been there. Not in the room—gods of Vess's small order were not permitted in royal chambers—but in the storm outside, riding the lightning down the palace spire, tasting the air above the birthing room. It had felt both children arrive into the world within minutes of each other, two small flames kindled from the same fire, and it had felt something else besides, something it had not understood then and understood too well now: the way the storm bent toward them. The way the sky took notice.

The elder twin, by four minutes, was Aurelian. The younger was—

Here Vess paused, even inside itself. Even alone in its own memory, it hesitated over the name, because the name had been taken out of the world, and to speak it carelessly was to risk the binding noticing, the way a splintered bone notices pressure.

The younger prince had been named for the river that ran beneath the palace. His mother called him a name that meant *little rain* in the old tongue, and his father, King Aldric, had laughed and said he would grow into storms or drown trying. He had his brother's eyes and none of his brother's patience. At three years old he had climbed the spire stairs alone, all hundred and forty steps, and been found at the top laughing in the wind with lightning crawling harmlessly along the balustrade like a cat come to see him.

Vess remembered the whole court loving him. That was important, Vess thought now, sinking deeper. That was the detail everyone forgot when they told the story afterward, the story that became no story at all. They loved him. The usurper had not been able to kill him precisely because killing him would have broken something in the realm that could not be mended—the affection of ten thousand servants and soldiers and minor lords who had watched a small boy laugh in thunder.

So instead they had done worse.

* * *

The coup came in autumn, when the king rode north to treat with the hill clans. Vess had seen the shape of it gathering for months—it was hard not to; ambition has a smell, and the man who would take the crown had been marinating in it for years—but seeing a thing gather is not the same as being able to stop it. Vess was a god of hearth-flames and votive candles, of the small fires mortals lit when they wanted to be heard. Its power was attention. And attention, in the end, had not been enough.

The regent—Vess still could not think his name without flinching, though flinching was an odd thing for a god to do—had not acted alone. That was the second detail lost to the rewriting. Half the court had known. Half the court had chosen, in that gray way courts choose, to know nothing. The king returned from the north to a palace that had already turned against him, and the gates closed behind him like a mouth.

What happened to King Aldric, Vess had never learned. The records said fever. Records said whatever their keepers wished them to say. But Queen Liora had understood within a day what her husband's death meant and what would come next, and she had done the only thing left to a mother whose enemies owned every gate and every guard: she had gone looking for a god who owed her a favor.

She found Vess in the chapel of small flames, kneeling—or appearing to kneel; gods and posture are complicated matters—before the votive rack where a single candle burned for the safe return of travelers. She had no offerings. She had no time. She had two sleeping boys and a palace full of knives, and she knelt on cold stone and prayed with a directness that Vess had rarely heard from royalty and never forgotten since.

*I don't ask you to save us,* she had said. *I ask you to help me save them. Tell me what it costs. I'll pay it.*

Vess had told her. Godhood keeps few rules, but one of them is old and absolute: a bargain must be stated plainly before it is struck. No hidden clauses, no fine print, no weasel words. Vess had laid out the ritual in full, the desperate improvised thing that three frightened theologians and one fading god had assembled from fragments of older rites—the Binding of the Twin Paths, it had been called once, in a tongue that predated Vhalen, a rite designed not for hiding princes but for hiding gods from things that ate gods.

That should have been the first warning. Vess saw that now, with fifteen years of silence in which to see it. They were using a weapon built against predators to hide children from men. The rite had never been tested on mortal blood. The rite had never been tested at all, not in living memory, perhaps not ever. But the queen had said *I'll pay it*, and the knives were coming up the stairs, and Vess had been young, in the way gods measure youth, and it had believed that intention could cover for ignorance.

The binding required two anchors. That was its architecture: split the thing to be hidden across two vessels, so that neither vessel held enough of the pattern to be recognized. One child bound to stillness—a valley where magic could not follow, where scrying slid off like rain off glass, where the boy would grow up nameless and safe and slowly forget why he was afraid of thunder. The other child bound to the throne—the visible heir, the one the usurper needed alive to legitimize his regency, wrapped in a binding that would keep the sky-magic in his blood dormant until the realm was safe to wake him into.

Two paths. Two anchors. Each twin hidden from half the world.

And the price, stated plainly as the law demanded: the memories. Not just the children's memories of each other and of who they were—the ritual drank those as fuel—but the queen's memories of what she had done, and the god's own memory of performing it, folded away into the binding itself, held in trust, to be returned only when the binding came undone. Everyone involved would forget the crime in order to make the crime survivable. The queen would wake in the morning not knowing which son she had buried in a valley. The god would wake not knowing what it had carried.

Vess had agreed. Vess had spoken the words. And here, in the sealed room at the bottom of itself, Vess made itself look at what happened next, because a god that will not look at its own worst hour does not deserve the word *god*, only the word *survivor*.

* * *

The rite had gone wrong in the third movement. Or rather—it had gone right, exactly as written, and the writing itself had been the wrong. The Binding of the Twin Paths had been designed to anchor divinity, and divinity, however diminished, was what ran in the princes' veins. When the words took hold, they reached for the divine substance in the two small bodies the way roots reach for water—and they found more than expected. Far more. The storm-sent bloodline of Vhalen, concentrated in twins born under a standing storm, was a reservoir where the rite's designers had planned for a well.

Something noticed.

Vess had never been able to name what the something was. In the moment of the binding, in the white roar of the working, there had been a presence at the edge of the circle—not summoned, not invited, not one of the old gods and not one of the new. Hungry. Patient in the way that only very old and very empty things are patient. Later, thinking back through the memory a thousand times during the long silence, Vess had come to call it the thing that eats prayers, because that was what it seemed to be doing at the edge of the circle: feeding. Drinking the overflow of divine substance that the binding could not hold, lapping at the spill like a beast at a butcher's floor.

And when the binding snapped shut around the two princes—when the younger twin's existence folded away into a valley and the elder's folded away into a crown—something slipped through the gap. Something followed the elder anchor home.

The binding was meant to wrap the true king in dormancy, a sheath of forgetting, until such time as the words were spoken again in reverse. Instead it wrapped the true king in dormancy *and* seated something else in the space the binding hollowed out. The usurper thought he wore the crown. The court thought the regent ruled. But Vess, watching through fifteen years of enforced silence, had come to understand the truth with a horror that had nearly extinguished it a dozen times:

No one wore the crown. The crown wore them. The parasite had taken the regent as its glove the way it later took the king as its mask, and it had spent fifteen years doing the one thing it knew how to do: eating. It had eaten the old gods' voices out of the world—that was the silence, that was the fifteen years of dead altars and unanswered prayers, one vast feeding. It had eaten the records, the names, the witnesses. It had eaten, slowly and with great subtlety, the memory of the younger prince, scrubbing him from registers and chronicles and the minds of everyone who had ever smiled at a boy laughing in thunder.

Everyone except a god who kept its memories in a sealed room at the bottom of itself, and a queen who had hidden her final testimony where nothing that eats could reach it.

* * *

Vess rose through its own layers then, back up through the dark water of itself, and found the present waiting where it had left it: the drowned chapel, knee-deep in black floodwater, the records spread on the one dry ledge, Sera asleep sitting upright with a wet book in her lap, and Isandro standing at the chapel's broken doorway with the last light going out of the sky behind him, looking at Vess the way a man looks at a doctor who has been reading his chart too long.

"You've been somewhere," Isandro said. "For hours. You went somewhere."

"I went back," Vess said. Its voice, when it used voice, was the sound of a match striking. "To the beginning. You asked me what the records mean. The two names. I owe you the whole of it, and the whole of it is worse than the part you've guessed."

Sera woke at the sound of the god speaking, the way she always did—completely, instantly, a woman whose sleep had been trained out of her by years of temple bells and then years of having no temple. She set the book down carefully, as if it might feel neglected, and said nothing. She had learned, in the days since the ferry, when to let a god finish.

"Tell me about the brothers," Isandro said.

And Vess, who had rehearsed this confession through fifteen years of silence, found that rehearsal had prepared it not at all. It flickered. Sera, watching, moved the nearest candle closer without being asked, and the god steadied on the borrowed flame like a swimmer on a rope.

"There were two princes," Vess said. "Born the same night, an hour apart, under a storm that stood over Ashkarat like a promise. The elder was Aurelian. The younger—" and here it did the thing it had dreaded, spoke the erased name aloud into the flooded dark, quietly, the way you speak near a sleeping animal— "was named Maerin. After the river. His mother called him little rain."

Isandro did not move. That was the terrible thing, Vess thought. A man learning he may be a stolen prince should stagger, should deny, should laugh. Isandro stood in the doorway with river water dripping off his coat hem and simply *listened*, the way he did everything—carefully, braced, as if he had spent fifteen years practicing for bad news and had gotten very good at receiving it.

"The kingdom fell to a coup while they were children," Vess went on. "Their father died. Their mother had one night and one favor owed to her by a small god of candles, and she spent both. There was a rite. It was meant to hide them both—one into stillness, one into the throne—until the realm was safe to wake them. It was meant to cost only memory." Vess's flame bent, though there was no draft. "It did not go as written. Something was waiting at the edge of the working. Something hungry. And when the binding closed, it slipped inside the elder anchor's hollow and has been wearing the crown ever since—first the regent, then the king himself, like a glove, like a mask. Your king is real, Isandro. He is asleep inside his own body, behind a door that was built to protect him and became a prison. And the thing outside the door has spent fifteen years eating the world's memory of his brother."

The water lapped at the chapel walls. Somewhere far off, a fish jumped, or a rat, or something with more intentions than either.

"So the records," Isandro said slowly. "The two names. One wears the crown. One is erased."

"One was bound into a valley where magic cannot follow," Vess said. "One was bound to a throne that magic cannot leave. And I have crossed the world to find the one in the valley, because the words of unbinding require the true heir, and because I believed—I *knew*—that the valley held Maerin."

Isandro's face did something very small and very controlled. "But."

"But," Vess said, and hated itself with a thoroughness that surprised even a god, "the binding was corrupted. The anchors were meant to be clean. They were not. The rite drank deeper than it should have, and spilled, and fed things it should never have touched, and in fifteen years of silence I have tried a hundred times to trace which anchor holds which prince, and the traces will not resolve. The valley-child carries memories that belong to the throne-child. The throne-child dreams dreams that belong to the valley. The corruption braided them. I came to Stillwater following the resonance of the younger anchor, and I found you, and your storm-calling is the strongest I have tasted in three hundred years, and I *believed*—"

"Say it," Isandro said. Not cruelly. Just plainly, the way you pull a splinter.

Vess gathered itself. Around it, every candle in the drowned chapel leaned toward the god as if listening.

"I do not know which twin you are," Vess said. "You may be Maerin, the hidden prince, stolen twice—once by the binding, once by the rewriting. Or you may be Aurelian, the true king, misplaced by a corrupted rite, while the man on the throne is a stranger entirely. The binding knows. The binding will tell us, when it comes undone, and not one breath before. I am a god of small fires, and I have led you across the free cities on the strength of my certainty, and my certainty is ash. Forgive me. I do not know who you are."

The silence after that was the kind that has weight. Sera had gone very still, her hands flat on the wet ledger, her eyes moving between the god and the man with the expression of someone watching a load-bearing wall develop a crack.

Isandro looked down at his own hands. Turned them over. Studied the palms as though the answer might be written there, in lines, in scars, in the faint static charge that lived in his skin now that he had crossed the rim and let the sky back in. Fifteen years of a life—Stillwater, the ferry, Odile's shrine, a name he had chosen for himself out of thin air because no truer name was offered—all of it suddenly provisional, a costume he might have been sewn into at birth.

"That's why the valley," he said at last, quietly. "That's why the wardens. That's why I've been afraid of thunder my whole life—it wasn't fear. It was recognition." He laughed, one note, with no humor in it at all. "I don't even know if I'm mourning my own life or someone else's."

"You are whoever you choose to be," Sera began, the priestess's reflex, the comfort she had dispensed a thousand times in a thousand burned temples—

"No." Isandro's voice was gentle but it stopped her like a hand. "Don't. Don't give me the sermon version. I've spent fifteen years being nobody on purpose, and it turns out nobody was a job I was hired for before I could walk." He looked up, and his eyes had the flat bright quality of sky before hail. "The binding knows. Fine. Then we ask the binding. Where?"

Vess felt the question land in the deepest part of itself, where the sealed room stood open and the memory of the rite still glowed like a brand. It knew the answer. It had known since the drowned chapel's first ledger, since the diagrams of the original working, since the words *binding chamber* had swum up out of the flood-stained ink.

"Ashkarat," Vess said. "Beneath the throne room. Where it was done is where it can be undone."

Isandro nodded once, slow, a man accepting terms. Outside, over the black ribbon of the river, clouds were assembling out of a clear night sky, gathering low and deliberate, and the first distant mutter of them rolled down the valley like something clearing its throat.

Neither of them said what all three were thinking. None of them had to.

If the binding could not say which brother it held until it was broken—then the only way to learn who Isandro Almeida truly was, was to walk into the heart of the thing that ate the world, and unmake the one spell keeping everyone alive.
