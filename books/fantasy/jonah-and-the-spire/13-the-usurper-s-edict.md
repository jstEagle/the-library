## 13. The Usurper's Edict

The candle in Inquisitor-General Maren's office had burned down to a stub by the time the courier arrived, and she did not light a new one. She had learned, over nineteen years of service to the Crown's Office of Inquiry, that darkness was not an obstacle to work but a discipline of it. A mind that required perfect light to think was a mind that had already surrendered to comfort.

She heard the courier on the stairs before she saw him—the particular arrhythmic tread of a man who had ridden hard and then walked slowly through the corridors of the Bastion of Order so as not to appear as though he had ridden hard. Cowardice announced itself in a hundred small ways. Maren catalogued it the way other women catalogued embroidery patterns: without effort, without judgment, and without mercy.

"Enter," she said, before he could knock.

He came in wet from the Corvane rain, which had been falling for three days without pause—a fact that would have meant nothing to him and a great deal to her, though she did not yet know how much. He carried a dispatch case chained to his wrist, black lacquer, sealed with the sigil of the Regent's private chancery. Not the Office of Inquiry. Not the army. The private chancery.

Maren set down her pen. "Name."

"Tollen, ma'am. Second rider, eastern relay."

"You left Ashkarat when?"

"Six days past, ma'am. I made Corvane in four and lost a day at the ford."

"Four days for three hundred leagues." She looked at him properly then—long enough that he shifted his weight from one foot to the other. "You killed two horses."

"I—" His throat worked. "Yes, ma'am."

"The Crown reimburses the relay stables for horses lost in urgent service. You will file the claim yourself, with your own name on it, and you will not inflate the animals' value." She extended her hand. "Because if you do, I will know, and there is no punishment in Vhalen more instructive than being caught in a small lie by someone who has read every letter you have ever written."

Tollen went pale in the way of men who had assumed the Inquisitor-General was a figurehead, a gray terror spoken of in barracks and never actually encountered. He unchained the case and gave it over with both hands, like an offering.

"That will be all. Get dry. Sleep. You ride back tomorrow with my reply."

He bowed too deeply and left too quickly. Maren waited until his footsteps faded, then turned the case over once beneath the guttering candle, reading the wax seal the way a physician reads a face. The seal was intact. The wax was the Regent's own gray-violet, pressed with the crowned tower—and pressed *hard*, harder than protocol required, the impression slightly cracked at one edge where the signet had bitten down with more force than ceremony demanded.

Someone in the private chancery had been afraid.

Maren broke the seal and read.

*To the Inquisitor-General, Office of Inquiry, Corvane District. By order of the Regent-who-wears-the-crown, in the name of the peace of Vhalen.*

*It has come to the attention of this office that a practitioner of forbidden arts has manifested within the free city of Corvane. Witnesses describe lightning called down upon a rooftop in the Tanner's Quarter, unbidden, lethal, and precise. One man dead. The practitioner escaped.*

*You are commanded to locate this individual and deliver them into the custody of the Crown alive and unharmed. Alive is not a preference. It is the substance of the command. Any harm coming to the prisoner prior to delivery will be accounted a failure of the highest order.*

*Further: all persons registered under the God-Touched Statute within Corvane are to be re-interviewed. All shrines, licensed or otherwise, are to be searched. The temple tunnels beneath the old quarter are to be mapped and sealed.*

*The Regent's interest in this matter is personal. Do not delegate it. Do not report through channels. Correspond directly with this office.*

*There was no signature. There never was, from the private chancery. But beneath the final line, in ink slightly different in shade—as though written later, by the same hand, after a pause—three words:*

*Bring them breathing.*

Maren read the letter twice. Then she sat back in her chair, laced her fingers over her stomach, and considered the rain against the window for a long time.

She was fifty-one years old. She had entered the Office of Inquiry at seventeen because it was the only institution in Vhalen that promised what she wanted, which was not power, exactly, and not safety, exactly, but something rarer than either: *legibility*. A world in which causes had effects, in which guilt could be established rather than guessed, in which the strong did not simply take and call their taking destiny. Her father had been a ferryman on the Sarn who drowned with eleven passengers when a storm-caller's duel tore the sky apart over the river crossing—an act of god-touched arrogance, two gifted children playing at divinity while ordinary people drowned below. She had watched it from the bank. She had been nine years old. She remembered the lightning standing in the water like white trees, and the silence afterward, and the way the survivors had looked at the sky with gratitude instead of horror, because the storm-callers were beautiful, and beauty excused everything.

That was the day Maren had understood the truth she would spend her life enforcing: that miracle was merely violence wearing better clothes. That every god-touched man and woman was a storm-caller's child grown older and no wiser, convinced that the rules of cause and effect bent around them because they were special. The old gods had gone silent fifteen years ago, and good riddance; the world since had been quieter, fairer, *countable*. The Regent-who-wears-the-crown had built an order out of that silence, and Maren had helped build it, district by district, registry by registry, until god-touched citizens were known quantities—filed, interviewed, monitored, harmless.

And now lightning had fallen on a rooftop in the Tanner's Quarter, and the private chancery was afraid.

She did not yet connect the rain to the letter. That connection would come later, and when it came it would arrive not as a thought but as a splinter working its way toward her heart. For now she did what she always did: she began to build the case.

* * *

By dawn she had cleared her desk of everything else. The smuggler guilds, the counterfeiting ring in the Saltmarket, the missing tithe-collector—all of it delegated, sealed, and sent downward to subordinates who would handle competently or be replaced. The Office of Inquiry did not have a hierarchy so much as a filtration system, and Maren was the membrane through which only certain things passed.

Her lieutenant arrived at the sixth bell: Doss, a heavyset former soldier with a face like a closed door and the best memory for detail she had ever employed. He found her already standing at the great map of Corvane that occupied the eastern wall of her office, pins and thread scattered across the Tanner's Quarter like a surgeon's sutures.

"Report," she said.

"Witnesses from the rooftop death, ma'am. Six credible. The dead man is one Ferro Vance, a runner for the Kellin smuggling family, though nothing was found on the body to prove it." Doss consulted his notebook without opening it; he never opened it in front of her, a courtesy she appreciated. "Struck by a single discharge, entering through the crown of the head, exiting at the heel. The cobbler across the street says the flash lit his whole workshop blue. The body was burned through in a line about as wide as a finger."

"As wide as a finger." Maren touched the map, tracing the streets of the Tanner's Quarter. "Not a broad strike. Not a forked strike. A line."

"Yes, ma'am. And here's the part I don't like. Every witness agrees the lightning came *down*, obviously, but three of them say it curved. Bent around the chimney pots. Like it was thrown rather than fallen."

"Thrown." She let the word sit. Natural lightning fell. Called lightning could be aimed—but aiming took training, and training took institutions, and every institution that had ever taught storm-calling in Vhalen had been burned or buried fifteen years ago. She knew; she had signed the requisitions for the burning. "Age of witnesses?"

"Various. None drunk. Two of them god-touched themselves, both registered, both terrified, both insisting they felt *nothing* before the strike. No warning hum, no pressure in the ears. Ma'am—if another caller had been fighting nearby, they'd have felt it. They feel each other. Everyone knows that."

"So either the caller masked themselves completely, which requires a discipline that hasn't existed in this country for fifteen years—" She stopped. Her own voice sounded strange to her, one degree too even. "Or the caller didn't know what they were doing. Instinct. Raw talent, untrained, firing under fear."

Doss frowned. "Untrained talent kills itself usually. Or burns a house down. It doesn't put a hole the width of a finger through a man's heel from across a street."

"No," Maren said quietly. "It doesn't."

She looked at the map again, and now she did the thing that made her terrifying—not cruelty, not ruthlessness, but patience. She began placing pins. The shrine where the god-touched girl had been registered last month. The chapel in the old quarter that had applied for a license and withdrawn the application two weeks ago, which meant it had something to hide. The tenement where a registered weather-sense had reported feeling "pressure like a held breath" three nights running. Thread between them, red thread, and the pattern that emerged was not a circle but a *line*—a path moving through the city from the river gates inward, unhurried, deliberate, like something following a road only it could see.

"They're traveling," she said. "Not hiding. Traveling. Toward the city center, or through it." She pinned the last point and stepped back. "Find me everyone who saw a stranger in the Tanner's Quarter in the week before the strike. Not god-touched strangers—any stranger who made someone nervous. Nervousness leaves records, Doss. Complaints to watchmen, disputes with landlords, a shopkeeper who remembers a face. Fear is the most honest informant in any city, and it files reports constantly."

"And the re-interviews? The chancery wants all registered god-touched brought in again."

"Start with the weather-senses. All of them. If a caller is working in Corvane, the sensitive ones will be having nightmares." She paused. "And Doss. The order says alive. Read the order."

He read it. She watched his eyes move down the page and stop at the last three words, and she watched him decide not to comment on them, which was the correct decision, because commenting would have required both of them to acknowledge what those words implied—that the Regent feared this person, needed this person, and that fear and need, in the private chancery, wore the same gray-violet wax.

"Ma'am," he said instead. "Why would the Regent want a storm-caller breathing?"

"I don't know," said Maren truthfully. It was the first true thing she would fail to say aloud for many months, and she said it cleanly, because she believed that an inquisitor who lied to herself about small things would eventually lie about large ones. "But I intend to find out. And I intend to find them first."

* * *

The rain continued. On the fourth day of the hunt, Maren walked the Tanner's Quarter herself, because she had never trusted a report she hadn't verified with her own senses, and because there was a question the witnesses' statements raised that no subordinate would think to ask.

She stood in the rain-soaked alley beneath the roof where Ferro Vance had died. The scorch mark was still visible on the slate—a thin dark line, precise as a pen stroke, running down the slope of the roof and ending where the body had ended. She studied it for a long time, letting the rain run off her hood, ignoring the watchman who hovered at the mouth of the alley wanting very much to be useful and knowing better.

Then she asked the watchman the question. "When the lightning struck—was there thunder?"

He blinked. "Thunder? I—yes, ma'am. Must have been. Whole street heard it."

"After. How long after the flash?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. "I don't rightly recall, ma'am."

"Of course you don't. No one does. Go and ask the cobbler. Ask all six witnesses. Ask them whether the thunder came *with* the flash or after it, and make them think carefully, because the difference matters." She turned up her collar. "If the thunder followed the flash by even a few breaths, then the bolt was called from far away—from outside the city, perhaps—and traveled. But if the thunder came *with* it, simultaneous, impossible as that sounds—"

She stopped herself. Even saying it aloud felt like superstition, and Maren did not deal in superstition. Except that she had grown up on the Sarn, and she had seen a storm-caller's duel at nine years old, and some knowledge lived in the body deeper than doctrine. Thunder was the sky's voice. Lightning was the sky's hand. And in every account of the old calling she had ever read—in the confiscated texts she kept locked in her own office, ostensibly as evidence, in truth as reference—there was one consistent law:

*A caller commands the hand. No caller commands the voice.*

Unless the voice chose to come.

She dismissed the watchman and stood alone in the alley a moment longer, rain drumming on her hood, looking up at the gray ceiling of cloud that had hung over Corvane for six unbroken days. Six days of rain, arriving the same week the lightning struck. Weather followed storms; storms followed callers; and callers, if half the old testimony was true, could feel one another across distances that made maps meaningless.

Which meant that somewhere in her city, or passing through it, was a person whom the sky itself was attending—and the sky, apparently, had opinions.

Maren found that she was not afraid. This surprised her a little. She examined the absence of fear the way she examined all data, and concluded that it was genuine: whatever stood at the end of the red thread, it had killed one smuggler and fled, which was the behavior of a fugitive, not a conqueror. Fugitives could be taken. She had taken forty-one god-touched offenders in her career, and forty-one times the machinery of inquiry had ground forward, patient and total, and forty-one times it had closed.

The machinery would close again. She was certain of it, the way she was certain of the rain, and her certainty was the most dangerous thing about her.

* * *

That evening, in her office, she wrote her reply to the private chancery. It took her two hours, because every sentence was weighed twice. She reported progress without speculation, findings without interpretation, and requested—carefully, in language that conceded nothing—clarification on a single point:

*This office notes with respect that the subject is to be delivered alive. This office requests guidance as to whether the subject's identity, once established, is to be confirmed against existing registries, or whether the subject may be an individual unknown to the Crown's records. The distinction materially affects method.*

It was a probe disguised as procedure. If the chancery answered *check the registries*, then the storm-caller was somebody already filed, already known, and the hunt would be short. If the chancery answered anything else—if it hesitated, if it evaded, if it told her that identity did not matter—then the Regent was hunting someone whose name it already knew and did not wish spoken, and Maren would add that fact to the growing ledger of small wrongnesses she kept in the back of her mind, unexamined, the way a careful woman keeps a key she cannot yet find the lock for.

The reply came back in two days, faster than any correspondence had ever crossed three hundred leagues, which told her the chancery had a rider waiting at every relay for her words specifically.

*Identity is not your concern. Deliver the subject. Bring them breathing.*

No signature. Same cracked-wax force in the seal.

Maren read it twice, then fed it to the candle flame and watched it curl. Around her, Corvane settled into curfew—bells, boots, shutters closing, the city holding its breath. Somewhere out in that wet dark, a frightened, powerful, untrained stranger was moving along a line of red thread toward a destination she could not yet see, attended by a sky that had broken fifteen years of silence to follow them.

Maren opened her ledger of cases, dipped her pen, and wrote at the top of a fresh page, in her small exact hand:

*Subject: the Storm-Caller of Corvane. Status: at large. Disposition: to be taken alive.*

Then, after a pause she would never explain to anyone, she added a second line—one word, unasked-for, the kind of notation an inquisitor makes when the evidence has begun whispering:

*Alone.*

She blotted the page, closed the ledger, and rang for her lieutenant, because somewhere in the Tanner's Quarter a cobbler was about to remember that the thunder had come with the flash, and the hunt had just become something no one in Vhalen had conducted in fifteen years.

Outside, over the peaks beyond the city, the rain thickened, and the first distant grumble of a gathering storm rolled down from the mountains like a name being called.
