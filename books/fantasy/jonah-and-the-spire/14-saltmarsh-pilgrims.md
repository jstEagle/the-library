## 14. Saltmarsh Pilgrims

The road to the coast was a ribbon of gray mud stitched with the feet of the faithful.

Isandro had never seen so many people moving in one direction for no reason anyone could name. They came out of the free cities and the farmsteads between them, out of river towns where the water ran brown with spring melt, carrying everything they owned on their backs or in carts pulled by donkeys too old for the work. Pilgrims, they called themselves. They walked toward a coastal shrine that had been a ruin for fifteen years—a tumbled ring of salt-eaten stones where, until recently, no prayer had been answered in living memory.

Now the shrine wept fresh water into the sea, and the faithful had noticed.

"Keep your hood up," Sera murmured beside him. "And stop looking at the sky like you're expecting it to look back."

"It usually does," Isandro said.

She elbowed him, not gently. Three weeks on the road together had worn away some of the wariness between them, but not all of it—Sera kept her edges sharp the way other people kept knives. She wore her defrocking with a strange pride, the faded brand on her wrist half-hidden under a pilgrim's wrap, and she had opinions about everything: the quality of the bread, the honesty of the ferry tolls, the way Isandro held his shoulders when a storm built over the marshes to the east.

"You're doing it now," she said.

"Doing what?"

"Listening." She glanced at him sidelong. Her eyes were the color of tea left too long in the cup, and they missed nothing. "You do it when you think no one's watching. Your head tilts. Like a bird."

Isandro pulled his hood lower and said nothing, because she was right. The sky over the saltmarshes had been restless all morning—not gathering, not yet, but *considering* it, the way a dog considers a door. He could feel it the way he'd once felt nothing at all. Fifteen years in the Valley of Stillness had taught him silence so thoroughly that the world's weather now roared in his ears like a crowd.

They had joined the pilgrimage two days ago, at Sera's insistence. "Soldiers watch roads," she'd said. "But no one watches a thousand pilgrims. We'll be invisible in the middle of them." She'd been right about that, mostly. The usurper's patrols had grown bolder as the army moved south—checkpoints at bridges, registration wagons with iron cages on their beds—but the pilgrim road wound through marsh and fen where wagons couldn't follow, and the soldiers who did come this far were young and bored and more interested in the wine casks than in searching gray-cloaked walkers for god-touched heretics.

What Sera hadn't said, what Isandro had worked out on his own somewhere around the second night of sleeping in ditches, was that she also wanted time. Time to teach him. Because whatever else he was—fugitive, liar, the man who had killed a warden on a rooftop in Corvane with a bolt of lightning he hadn't meant to throw—he was also a storm-caller with no discipline, and every day he went untrained was a day closer to the next accident.

*Accident.* That was the word they'd agreed on. It was kinder than the alternatives.

* * *

They practiced at dusk, in the hollow places the marsh offered—old peat cuttings flooded with black water, reed beds tall enough to swallow a kneeling man, stretches of empty salting where the only witnesses were herons and the ghosts of drowned villages.

"Again," Sera said.

Isandro knelt in the wet grass with his palms flat against the earth, which was a thing she'd taught him. *Ground first,* she said. *The old callers didn't summon storms, they asked. You can't ask anything if you're not standing on something.*

He reached, carefully, the way he'd learned to reach for a hot pan—with respect for what it could do to him. Above the marshes, three miles off, a cloud bank had been sulking all afternoon, heavy-bellied and gray. He touched its edge the way you'd touch a sleeping animal's flank.

It flinched. Static crawled down his arms; his hair lifted inside his hood. Somewhere in that cloud, pressure was building toward discharge, and every instinct he had—every raw, fifteen-years-starved instinct—screamed at him to *take* it, to pull the lightning down and feel it crack through him like the answer to a question he'd been asking since the valley.

"Not yet," Sera said quietly. She knelt across from him, hands on her knees, watching his face. "Breathe. What is it doing?"

"Gathering."

"Why?"

He frowned. The question shouldn't have been hard, but it was. "Because... clouds gather before rain."

"That's what clouds do. Why is *this* cloud gathering? Feel it." Her voice dropped into the register she used for scripture, low and certain, though she claimed to have no faith left. "A storm isn't a weapon, Isandro. It's a laborer. It has work it wants to do—it wants to spend itself, to rain, to breathe out. When you call lightning without asking what the storm needs, you're not commanding it. You're robbing it. And robbed things bite."

The words settled into him strangely, because they were almost the opposite of everything he remembered—or thought he remembered—about storm-calling. His memories of before the valley were smoke. He knew he had been trained once; his hands sometimes moved through patterns he couldn't name, and in dreams he heard a woman's voice saying words like *discipline* and *debt* in a language he no longer spoke. But the knowledge itself had been taken from him along with everything else, and what remained was power without grammar.

"Ask it," Sera said. "Out loud if you have to. What do you need?"

Isandro closed his eyes. The cloud hung there, vast and patient, full of a tension that wanted release. He made himself small inside his own skin and put the question into the space between himself and the sky:

*What do you need?*

The answer came not as words but as pressure—a shape of wanting. Rain. Just rain, first, a long slow unburdening over the cold salting. And after, if the air allowed it, one clean stroke to equalize what the friction of the world had built up. Nothing wasted. Nothing furious.

"It wants to rain," he said, and heard his own wonder. "That's all. It just wants to rain."

"Then let it."

He opened his hands—not grasping, only opening—and the cloud bank sighed. Rain came down over the eastern marshes in a silver curtain, three miles wide, falling exactly where the dry ground needed it most. No thunder. No violence. The herons didn't even startle.

Isandro sat back on his heels, shaking. Not with fear this time. With something that had no better name than recognition—as if he'd spent fifteen years mute and had just spoken a single true sentence.

Sera was smiling. She tried to hide it and failed. "There," she said softly. "That's what you were made for. Not rooftops. Not killing wardens. *This.*"

"I killed a man," Isandro said. The words came out flat. They always came out flat; that was how he could say them at all. "Three weeks ago. With less control than I have right now."

"Yes." She didn't look away. She never looked away from that. "And the difference between then and now is the difference between a flood and an irrigation channel. Same water. One drowns the village. One feeds it. Keep your hands open, keep asking, and someday the man you were on that rooftop will be someone you pity instead of someone you are."

He wanted to believe her. He was learning, slowly, to want things again—the valley had taught him to want nothing, because wanting nothing was safe—and wanting frightened him more than the wardens did.

Above the rain-washed marshes, the spent cloud drifted apart into rags of evening light.

* * *

The child's name was Tam, and he was seven years old, and he prayed the way other children breathed—constantly, unconsciously, and entirely without discretion.

Isandro noticed him on the third day of the pilgrimage: a thin, quick boy walking beside a woman Isandro took for his aunt, both of them refugees from a river town whose name Isandro had already forgotten. The boy collected things—feathers, interesting stones, a dead crab with one perfect claw—and he talked to them. And on the second evening, sitting on a milestone while the camp around him cooked barley porridge, he held a candle stub in his cupped hands and said, clearly, in the carrying voice children use when they think the whole world is listening:

"Vess, please keep my mama's boat from sinking. Amen."

The candle flame stood up straight and burned blue for the length of a heartbeat.

No one else saw it. Or no one else admitted to seeing it. But Isandro felt it go through the camp like a current through wire—a hush, a turning of heads, a woman two fires down pulling her shawl tighter. And he saw, in the way the boy's aunt's hands went still over the porridge pot, that she had seen it, and that she was afraid.

Sera saw Isandro seeing. She crossed the camp to him with two bowls and sat down too close, the way travelers do when they're talking privately. "Don't," she said, without preamble.

"Don't what?"

"Don't get involved. A god-touched child on a pilgrim road is sad. A god-touched child *noticed by us* is a target painted on all three of you."

"He's seven years old."

"He's seven years old and the usurper's inquisitors pay informants by the head." Sera stirred her porridge without eating it. "I've seen what happens to registered children, Isandro. I burned in a temple with people who tried to protect them. I'm telling you as someone who loved those people: you cannot save everyone, and trying will get us all caged."

Isandro looked across the firelight at the boy, who had finished his prayer and was now showing the dead crab to another child with tremendous authority. The candle stub was tucked behind his ear like a carpenter's pencil.

"I'm not going to save everyone," Isandro said. "I'm going to save him."

Sera followed his gaze and was silent for a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had changed—flatter, older. "That's what my abbot said. Right before the temple burned. *We're not going to save everyone. We're going to save them.*" She set down her bowl. "Fine. Then we do it smart. Tomorrow we walk near the front of the column, away from the stragglers the soldiers bother. And you—" she pointed her spoon at him, "—you practice being boring. Can you be boring?"

"I lived fifteen years in a valley whose entire purpose was boredom."

"Good. Be that again."

* * *

The soldiers came at midday, at the place where the pilgrim road crossed a tidal creek on a bridge of packed earth and old ship timber.

There were eight of them, mounted, wearing the black-and-gilt of the Regent's coastal levies, and they had set up their checkpoint with the lazy efficiency of men who had done this a hundred times: two at the bridgehead, the rest spread along the line of walkers, pulling people out of the column at random. Not searching—*sampling*. A woman's bundle dumped into the mud. An old man's papers held up to the light and found wanting. The registrations wagon wasn't here, but everyone knew what the questions led to: *Name. Village. Have you heard voices? Has anything impossible happened to you? Do you pray, and to whom?*

Isandro kept his hood up and his eyes down and walked, and it was working, it was fine, right up until the soldier on the bay horse found Tam.

Later, Isandro would take the memory apart a hundred times, looking for the moment it became inevitable, and never find it. The boy had simply stopped—stopped dead in the middle of the bridge to gape at the horses, the way seven-year-olds do—and the soldier had leaned down out of the saddle with the easy contempt of a man addressing furniture.

"Where's your mother, boy?"

"Auntie's there. Mama's home. Mama's boat—"

"Hold still." The soldier's gauntlet closed on the boy's shoulder, and Tam squirmed, and the candle stub fell from behind his ear into the mud of the bridge. And the soldier, glancing down at it with the idle attention of a man cataloguing trash, saw the wax had been burned blue.

Isandro felt it happen from forty yards away: the small pause, the recalibration. The soldier's hand shifted from shoulder to collar. "Well, well. Praying to the old gods, little man? Who taught you that?" He raised his voice, casual, bored, terrible. "Sergeant! Got a live one here."

The column around them did what columns do—flowed faster, eyes down, the ancient arithmetic of the powerless calculating distance from danger. Tam's aunt was screaming something and being held back by a second soldier. The boy, bless him, fought like a wildcat, kicking and twisting, and the soldier laughed and shook him once, hard, the way you shake a rug.

Something in Isandro's chest went very quiet and very clear.

He was moving before he decided to move—pushing through the column, hood back now, and Sera's hissed *no* behind him reaching him and meaning nothing at all. The sky over the marshes, which had been building all morning the way skies do in spring, turned its attention toward him like a great eye opening. He felt the storm's weight settle onto his shoulders, familiar now, three days familiar, and he did the thing Sera had beaten into him in the peat hollows.

He did not seize it.

He stood in the middle of the bridge, between the soldier and the boy, and he asked.

*What do you need?*

The answer rolled through him like a tide: *to speak. To be seen. To remind the small creatures below what walks above them.* Thunder, then—not as a weapon but as a voice, a single enormous syllable of it cracking directly overhead, so close and so loud that every horse on the bridge screamed and every man flinched as if struck. In the same instant Isandro pulled the storm's static down through himself and out—not as a killing bolt but as a corona, a cage of crawling blue light that wrapped the bridge timbers and the eight soldiers in a web of harmless, singing fire. It raised every hair on every head. It made the bay horse rear and dump its rider into the tidal mud. It did not burn. He held it the way you hold a note, the way you hold a breath, sweat freezing on his spine, and through the roar of it he heard his own voice, thin and steady:

"The boy walks. All of you look at the sky and count to ten. Then we're gone, and you'll tell your sergeant you were scattered by weather, because that's the truth, and no one will believe you anyway."

For three heartbeats nobody moved. The soldier holding Tam stared at the lightning crawling an inch from his visor, close enough to taste the ozone, and Isandro watched understanding arrive in the man's face—not the understanding of a scholar but of an animal, the old mammalian knowledge that something above the food chain had just spoken.

He let go of the boy.

Tam hit the bridge planks running, straight into his aunt's arms, and the column broke around them like water around a stone—pilgrims flowing past the frozen soldiers, past Isandro with his arms full of borrowed thunder, flowing east toward the coast and safety. Sera appeared at his elbow with her hand clamped on his sleeve, steering, muttering, *walk, don't run, walk*, and Isandro walked, letting the corona gutter and die behind them, letting the storm spend itself at last in a soft, exhausted drizzle over the salting.

His legs gave out at the first bend in the road, behind a windbreak of gorse, and he sat down hard in the wet grass and discovered he was laughing.

Sera stood over him, arms crossed, soaked to the skin, wearing an expression that was fury and awe fighting to a draw. "You absolute *idiot*," she said. "You walked into a checkpoint. You threatened eight armed men. You—" she gestured helplessly at the drizzling sky, "—you *negotiated*."

"They let him go."

"They let him go because you made eight grown soldiers afraid of the *weather*." She dropped to her knees in front of him, gripped his jaw, and turned his face side to side, checking him the way she checked him after every practice session—eyes, pulse, the tremor in his hands. Finding nothing worse than exhaustion, she released him and sat back on her heels. "Do you understand what you just did? Not the lightning. Anyone can make lightning—that's what got the warden killed. You *asked*. Under pressure, with a child's life on the line, you asked instead of taking." Her voice cracked, very slightly, on the last word, and she covered it with roughness. "Fifteen years they buried you, and it's still in there. The discipline. Whoever trained you before the valley—"

She stopped. They both stopped, the way they always did at the edge of that particular silence—the stolen childhood, the erased name, the letter in his pack that read *the crown lies.*

Somewhere behind them, faint with distance, a horn blew twice: the checkpoint, reporting. By nightfall every patrol on the coast would know a storm-caller walked with the pilgrims. By tomorrow, so would whoever the reports reached. Isandro thought of Maren—the name Sera had spoken of with a hatred that sounded almost like fear—and felt the net drawing in around them even as the rain washed the road clean.

Tam found them at dusk. Of course he did; children always found the people who saved them. He marched up to Isandro with the dead crab held out in both hands like a knight presenting a sword.

"For you," he announced. "Because you're god-touched. Auntie says don't tell anyone, but I'm telling *you*, because you already know." He lowered his voice to a conspiratorial whisper. "Vess talks to me. Does Vess talk to you?"

Isandro took the crab, because refusing seemed unthinkable. "Sometimes," he said. "Mostly it begs."

The boy nodded gravely, satisfied, and ran back to his aunt, and Isandro sat holding a dead crab in the last light, feeling—for the first time since the valley's silence broke—something that was not dread.

Purpose, maybe. Or the beginning of it.

Sera watched him from across the gorse, and when she spoke, her voice was quiet. "Delmare's council meets in six days. If we're fast, we can be there when they vote on resisting the Regent." She paused. "After today, they might even listen to you."

Far to the south, over the direction of Ashkarat, the horizon flickered—once, twice—like something signaling. Or like something answering.
