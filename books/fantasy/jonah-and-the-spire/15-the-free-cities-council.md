## 15. The Free Cities Council

Delmare announced itself by smell before sight: salt and tar and fish offal, the brackish breath of a city that lived by the tide. Then the walls rose out of the morning haze—gray granite furred with barnacles to twice a man's height, crusted with the white nests of gulls, older than any kingdom Sera had ever prayed in.

She stood at the rail of the river barge with her hands folded to keep them from shaking, and she watched the free city come toward her like a promise she was afraid to collect on.

*You wanted this,* she reminded herself. *You begged every silent god for a sign, and when they finally answered, you got a storm-caller who lies about his name and a candle-god that burns down to embers every time it sneezes.* It was not the answer she'd prayed for. But it was an answer, and after eleven years of nothing, she had learned not to look a miracle's gift-horse too closely in the mouth.

Beside her, Isandro watched the walls with an expression she was beginning to know too well—the flat, careful stillness of a man counting exits. He had been doing it since Corvane. Since the rooftop. Since the night the lightning came out of him without his leave and wrote his name across half of Vhalen in burning letters: *a storm-caller lives.*

"They'll have heard," he said quietly. The wind off the water pulled at his hood. "About Corvane. If the council has any eyes at all."

"Then we won't have to convince them you're real." She kept her voice light. "That's half the battle, isn't it?"

"And the other half?"

"The other half is convincing them that fighting is better than kneeling." She turned to face him fully. Under the hood, his eyes were the gray-green of deep water before a squall, and there were new lines around them that hadn't been there two months ago, on the pilgrim road, when he'd still pretended to be a farmer's son named Aldous with no business knowing how rain behaved. "I've knelt before, Isandro. I watched my temple burn while I knelt. I'm not asking them to do anything I haven't already failed at once."

He looked at her for a long moment. Whatever he saw made him reach out—not quite touching her shoulder, hovering near it, the way he did when the old courtesy of his valley manners warred with something rawer. "You haven't failed," he said. "You survived."

"In my experience those words get used interchangeably by people who weren't there."

The barge bumped the quay. Somewhere above them, a watchman called the hour, and the sound rolled out over the tiled roofs, and Sera thought: *eleven years ago I stood in a temple just like this one and heard bells, and believed they would always ring.*

---

They entered Delmare as pilgrims, because pilgrims were the one class of traveler even a frightened city hesitated to turn away. Sera had stitched the faded sun-of-the-nine sigil back onto her sleeve where the defrocking had torn it off; Isandro carried their bundle and kept his head down and let the crowd's noise swallow the particular quality of silence that followed him everywhere, the hush in which small sounds seemed to hold their breath.

The city was afraid. That was the first thing, and Sera read it in everything: the harbor chain half-raised though no enemy fleet showed on the horizon; the granaries guarded by hired spears; the shrines shuttered—not destroyed, Delmare was too old and too proud for that, but *shuttered*, doors closed, offerings left outside like letters to a friend who might be dead. Children chalked the nine-pointed star on doorposts where their parents couldn't see. A fishwife argued prices with a knife in her fist over nothing. Fear moved through the streets the way damp moves through stone—slowly, invisibly, into everything.

And under the fear, everywhere, the other thing. Sera felt it against her skin like static before lightning: god-touched folk, dozens of them, hiding in plain sight. A lamplighter whose flames leaned toward him like flowers toward sun. A girl selling shells who never spoke but whose silence made people tell her truths. They passed Sera in the street and their eyes met hers and flicked to the sigil on her sleeve, and what she read in their faces was not hope.

It was the look of drowning people watching someone swim past.

*Later,* she told them silently, meeting each gaze for half a heartbeat. *Hold on until later.*

The Council Hall of Delmare had been a temple once, in the generations before the free cities decided gods were expensive. Its dome still stood, painted with constellations gone brown with incense smoke, and beneath it the nine ruling families had built their dais like a ship's deck, so that the councilors sat in a curved row above the petitioners like officers regarding passengers. Sera had heard it described all her life—her order had kept records of the free cities, back when records seemed worth keeping—and none of the descriptions had prepared her for how *small* it felt. How human. Nine chairs, nine families, nine sets of interests, and beyond the harbor the whole drowning world.

They waited four hours in the antechamber among petitioners—a wool merchant disputing a tariff, a widow suing a shipwright, a delegation of eel-farmers—and Sera used every minute. She coached Isandro low-voiced: *the Councilor for the Saltworks hates the Regent personally, his brother was taken in the Corvane registries; the Councilor for Grain will trade with anyone; old Veyra of the Deepmoor family remembers the old gods and pretends she doesn't; whatever you do, don't let them make you perform. They'll want to see the lightning. Don't give it to them. Power that performs is power that can be priced.*

"I thought you wanted me to prove myself," Isandro murmured.

"I want them to *need* you," Sera said. "There's a difference. Proof can be dismissed. Need has to be fed."

When the chamberlain finally called the name Sera had given—"petitioners on matters touching the succession"—the hall went quiet in a way that had teeth in it.

---

Councilor Veyra spoke first, because of course she did. She was seventy if she was a day, wrapped in deepmoor wool the color of wet slate, and she looked at Sera the way a surgeon looks at a wound she intends to open.

"A priestess," she said. Her voice filled the dome without effort. "Or the ruin of one. Defrocked by your own order, if the reports from the south are true. Branded heretic by theirs." A thin hand indicated the usurper's colors on a banner hanging in the hall's shadowed end—black iron crown on white field, hung there, Sera realized with a chill, not as an enemy's standard but as a *caution*, the way one hangs a portrait of a plague. "And you bring us a man who will not give his name, whom the Regent's own inquisitors have named as a storm-caller, and you ask the free cities to go to war. Forgive me, daughter. I buried three sons in the last war the cities fought. I am slow to buy another one with petitions."

"Not war," Sera said. Her voice held. Eleven years of speaking to empty pews had taught her that much: the voice holds when there is nothing left in it to lose. "Not yet. I ask for something smaller and harder. I ask you to decide that the Regent is not inevitable."

Murmurs. Councilor Brann of the Saltworks—broad, red-faced, grief worn openly like a scar—leaned forward. "The Regent holds Ashkarat, the heartland, and now the passes. His armies ate Corvane's independence in a season. What exactly does the priestess propose we decide, and with what fleet, and whose sons?"

"With truth," Sera said, "and with time, and with this man."

Every eye in the hall went to Isandro. He stepped forward out of the shadow of the pillar, and Sera watched the councilors take his measure the way merchants take the measure of goods: hood pushed back, valley-pale, hands calloused from rope and oar, nothing about him that said *kingmaker*. Nothing that said anything at all, which was its own kind of eloquence.

"You are the storm-caller of Corvane," Veyra said. Not a question.

"I'm a man from a valley where nothing happens," Isandro said. "Until recently."

A few of the younger clerks laughed before they could stop themselves. Old Veyra did not. "Corvane says otherwise. Corvane says a warden of the Stillness died on a rooftop with his heart boiled dry by lightning, in front of forty witnesses, in a rainstorm that arrived out of a clear sky. The Regent's criers say you're a demon. Our own merchants say you're a weapon looking for a hand. Which are you?"

"Neither." Isandro's voice was quiet, and the hall bent toward it. "I'm a man who spent fifteen years being told the sky was empty. And then I learned it wasn't. And neither is anything else." He paused, and Sera saw him choose the next words with the care of a man stepping onto ice. "Your shrines are shuttered. Your god-touched are registering with the inquisitors or disappearing. You're debating tariffs while the thing that silenced the gods tightens its grip on the throne of Vhalen. I'm not here to sell you a weapon. I'm here to tell you the king lives."

The word landed in the dome and rang there. *King.* Sera watched it move through the councilors' faces like current through water—hunger in some, terror in others, and in old Veyra's eyes something older than either: the expression of a woman hearing a hymn she'd been told was forbidden.

"That," Veyra said slowly, "is a very large claim to carry into this room with no army behind it."

"It's the only claim worth making."

"Claims are cheap." Councilor Brann again, arms crossed. "The last man who claimed the king lived sold indulgences in the Fishmarket until we hanged him. Show us. Show us the king is alive, show us he can be freed, show us *how*, or thank the nine families for their hospitality and be gone by the tide."

Sera opened her mouth—and Isandro's hand touched her arm. One finger. *Wait.*

He looked up at the painted dome, at the constellations gone brown with smoke, and when he spoke again his voice had changed. Sera felt the change on her skin before she heard it, the fine hairs of her forearms lifting, the lamplight in the great hall steadying as if listening.

"You want proof," he said. "Here is the only proof I have. My life."

"Isandro—" Sera breathed, too late.

"There's an old form," he said to the council. "Older than the nine families. My grandmother's people used it, in the valleys, when a quarrel mattered more than property. Storm-oath. You swear a thing on the sky itself, and the sky keeps the ledger. If I speak true, I live to make it true. If I lie—if the king is dead, if he cannot be freed, if I knew it and swore anyway—" He spread his hands, palms up, and Sera saw they were trembling, and saw him let them tremble, let the council see it. "Then the sky takes back what it lent me. Here. In front of all of you. And you'll have your proof, one way or the other."

Silence, total, the kind Sera had only heard in temples at the moment before the bell.

Veyra's eyes narrowed. "Storm-oath is a shepherd's superstition."

"So is crowning a king," Isandro said. "Most of what holds the world together is. But it holds."

Brann laughed, short and harsh. "Let him. If he's lying, we lose a fugitive the Regent wants anyway. If he's telling the truth—" the big man's jaw worked, "—then we've been kneeling to a regicide for a year, and I'd rather know."

"Swear him, then," said a young councilor Sera didn't know, from the far end, and the motion went down the curved row like fire down a fuse, and Veyra, after a long moment, inclined her head perhaps half an inch.

"Then swear," the old woman said. "And may whatever listens to shepherds also listen to councils."

---

They cleared the center of the hall. Someone brought a brazier; someone else, at Veyra's dry instruction, opened the high windows on the harbor side, and the sea-wind came through, cold and smelling of kelp and distance, stirring the dust of a decade of politics.

Isandro walked to the middle of the floor alone. Sera stood at the edge of the cleared space with her fists clenched inside her sleeves, and she wanted to shout at him—*you fool, you beautiful fool, you don't know what you're promising, you don't know what the binding will ask of you, you don't even know what you are*—and she said nothing, because she had watched enough faith to know that a vow interrupted is a vow broken before it's made.

He raised his face to the open windows. To the light, and the wind, and whatever else was up there beyond the smoke-browned stars.

"I am called Isandro Almeida," he said, and his voice carried to the rafters without effort, as if the air itself had agreed to help. "Fifteen years ago I was put into the Valley of Stillness by someone who loved me enough to erase me. I don't remember why. I don't remember who I was. But I know what I've learned since: that the king of Vhalen, Aurelian, son of Liora, lives. That he is bound—held prisoner in his own body by a thing that wears his crown and feeds on his silence. And I swear that I will reach Ashkarat, and I will unbind him, or I will die on the road between here and there. This I swear on the storm. Let the sky keep the ledger."

The lamps guttered—all of them, every flame in the hall leaning toward him at once, the way flowers lean at noon.

And the thunder came.

It rolled in off the sea, out of a sky that had been clean blue all morning, one long peal that shook the dust from the rafters and rattled the harbor chain and set every gull in Delmare screaming, and it passed directly overhead, close enough that Sera felt it in her sternum, in her teeth, in the old hollow place where her faith had lived and died and now, unbearably, was waking again.

Then it was gone. The blue sky stayed blue. The lamps righted themselves.

But everyone in the hall had felt it. Everyone had felt the ledger open.

Sera found she was crying, silently, and did not trouble to hide it. Eleven years of silence, and the sky had *answered*. Not for her—for him, for a farm-voiced man from a nowhere valley—but the sound of it went through her like a bell through bronze, and somewhere in the ringing she understood that she had never stopped believing, not really. She had only stopped being allowed to.

On the dais, old Veyra sat very still, her wet-slate shawl rising and falling. When she finally spoke, her voice was different—smaller, and larger, both at once.

"Well," she said. "Well. So the sky *is* keeping accounts." She looked at Isandro for a long moment, and then at Sera, and something in her ancient face shifted, settled, hardened into decision. "Councilor Brann wanted proof the king lives. He has it, or as near as makes no matter. Now hear my price, storm-swearer, because the free cities do not fight for promises alone." She leaned forward on her carved chair, and behind her the black-iron banner stirred in the sea-wind like something waking. "You will have ships' passage, safe houses, and the Deepmoor seal. And in exchange, when you free your king—you will bring him *here*. To Delmare. To stand in this hall, in front of these nine families, alive and crowned and *free*, where the whole coast can see him. Because if you fail, storm-caller, if that thing wearing his crown comes for our walls instead—"

She gestured at the windows, at the blue, innocent sky.

"—we will need to know that the sky keeps ledgers for kings as well as shepherds."

Isandro bowed his head. "Agreed," he said. "So sworn."

"So witnessed," said nine voices, raggedly, and then, gathering strength, together: "*So witnessed.*"

Outside, over the harbor, the gulls were still wheeling and crying, and far away to the east—so far it was more felt than seen, a pressure on the horizon, a darkness like a bruise on the edge of the world—the first outriders of the Iron Crown's army were breaking camp at dawn.

None of them in the hall knew it yet. But the storm did. Sera saw it in Isandro's face as he turned from the dais, in the way his eyes went unbidden to the eastern windows and stayed there, and in the low, almost inaudible growl of thunder that answered, from beyond the sea, like a ledger closing.
