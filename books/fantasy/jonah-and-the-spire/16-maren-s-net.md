## 16. Maren's Net

The road into Delmare was a river of the broken, and Inquisitor-General Maren walked in it like a stone that had learned to float.

She had dressed the part carefully, because she did everything carefully: a widow's gray shawl, a bundle of someone else's linens tied to her back, ash rubbed into the seams of her knuckles so her hands looked like hands that had worked. She had left her white-and-iron coat folded in a chest aboard the courier barge at Vell's Crossing, along with her seal, her writs, and the four soldiers who made her visible from a hundred paces. What remained was a woman of perhaps fifty with a face built for forgetting—plain, weathered, unremarkable—and a memory that missed nothing.

Around her, the refugees told their stories to anyone who would listen, because grief demands an audience and terror demands a witness. Maren listened to all of it. A cooper from Harrow Fen whose son had been taken by the registry men for praying aloud. A girl who had watched the sky over Corvane split open on a night of rain, one white branch of lightning striking down through the curfew-dark like the finger of an angry god. An old man who swore he'd seen the storm-caller himself on the pilgrim road, walking with a priestess, his eyes full of weather.

*Eyes full of weather.* Maren turned the phrase over as she shuffled forward in the queue at Delmare's Tide Gate, letting the crowd's press carry her. People said foolish things about what they'd seen. But they said them in patterns, and patterns could be read the way clerks read ledgers, and Maren had spent twenty years reading the ledgers of human fear.

"Name?" asked the gate clerk, not looking up.

"Maren Ostreva," she said, using the dead woman's name she kept for such work—a real name, attached to a real woman who had died of fever two winters past in a village no one visited. Fabrications frayed; borrowed truths held. "Widow. From upriver."

"Purpose in the city?"

"Work," she said, and let her shoulders slump just enough. "Laundry, if anyone will have me. The war took my husband's trade."

The clerk stamped her paper without ceremony. That was the first weakness of Delmare, noted and filed: the Tide Gate processed four thousand souls a day and checked none of them against anything but its own exhaustion. A city that believed its walls were its safety had already mistaken the door for the house.

Inside, the free city roared around her—fish markets and rope walks, shrine-candles burning openly in windows, which would have been unthinkable in Corvane, where god-touched meant registered and registered meant gone. Maren bought a bowl of eel stew she did not eat and sat where she could watch the harbor, and began, methodically, to build her net.

* * *

By the fourth day she had a room above a chandler's shop, a reputation as a quiet widow who mended lace for coin, and a map of the council's fractures drawn in her head more precisely than any cartographer could have managed.

Delmare was governed by a Council of Nine, and Maren had interviewed—or rather, listened to—representatives of six factions in six taverns without once revealing herself. The pattern was old and simple and always fatal to free cities: they agreed on nothing except the imminence of their disagreement. Three factions wanted to treat with the Regent while treating was still possible. Two wanted to fight but could not agree who would pay for walls. One wanted simply to be left alone, as if neutrality were a country one could emigrate to. And threaded through all of them, growing louder each week as the Iron Crown's army crawled south, was the question that had brought Maren here:

*The storm-caller. Omen or weapon? Where is he? Whose side is he on?*

She found the witnesses the way water finds cracks.

The first was a roofer named Tammas who had been repairing slates near the Lantern Quarter on the night of the rain. Maren paid him for a lace repair she didn't need, then let him talk, because men like Tammas had been waiting weeks for someone to ask. He described the lightning in loving detail—the way it had come down twice, the second strike finding a man on the rooftops, the smell afterward like burnt copper and something sweeter, "like the air after a temple burns." He described the figure who had fled across the ridgelines: tall, moving wrong, too fast, "like the storm itself was carrying him."

"Did you see his face?"

"No, lady. But I'll tell you what I saw." Tammas leaned close, lowering his voice as though the Regent's spies hung in the smoke above them. "When that bolt came down, every candle in the street went out. Every one. And when it passed, they lit again on their own. Like the fire knew him. Like it *greeted* him."

Maren thanked him, paid him double, and walked back through the evening streets composing the report she would never send, because reports traveled through channels and channels had ears, and there were things the Regent's own chancellery must not learn before she learned them herself.

The second witness was harder: a defrocked acolyte of the drowned chapel, a boy of nineteen who had been on the temple steps when the lightning fell and had prayed, out loud, to Vess—the small god, the ferry-god, the ember that the registries listed as *extinguished, confirmed*. The boy wept while he told it. Not from fear. From joy. "He answered," the boy kept saying. "After fifteen years of nothing, he answered, and he came *here*, to us, and the caller came with him. It's a sign. It has to be a sign."

Maren wrote in her ledger: *Vess active. Manifestation: flame, small scale. Attached to target. Target protects the faithful.* She underlined the last phrase twice, because it was tactically significant and because it troubled her in a way she could not yet name.

A killer protected the faithful. A fugitive stopped to save a child at a saltmarsh shrine—she had that report too, thirdhand, from a soldier's letter home that a garrison clerk had copied for coin. The storm-caller's path, laid end to end on her mental map, did not look like the path of a weapon. It looked like the path of a man being dragged somewhere against his nature, leaving mercy behind him like footprints in snow.

And the Regent wanted him alive.

That was the splinter working under Maren's skin. She sat late that night in her narrow room with the lamp trimmed low and turned the order over for the hundredth time. *Bring the storm-caller alive. Unharmed. Unquestioned by local authorities. Deliver to Ashkarat under seal.*

She had hunted god-touched for six years. She had taken pyromancers and bone-readers and a woman in Corvane who could hear the thoughts of the dying, and the orders had always been the same ones any state gives: contain, register, remove. Efficient. Impersonal. *Order over miracle*, her first mentor had taught her—not cruelty, only the refusal to let chaos dress itself in wonder. Miracles were how tyrants were born and how mobs were fed. A kingdom that feared its gods was a kingdom that thought for itself.

But this was different. The Regent did not want the storm-caller contained. The Regent wanted him *brought*, the way a man wants a key brought to him—urgently, secretly, by the one hand he trusts. And the Regent feared the little god. Maren had seen the internal dispatches, the ones marked for eyes that no longer included hers: *Vess is not extinguished. If Vess reaches the capital—* and then the sentence simply stopped, redacted mid-thought, as though whoever wrote it could not bear to finish.

Fifteen years ago, the gods had gone silent all at once, on a single night, in every land. Maren had been a young officer then, and she remembered the official explanation: a natural attenuation, a doctrinal error, the old faith collapsing under its own weight like a rotten roof. She had believed it because believing it was orderly.

But roofs do not rot everywhere at once. Silence does not fall on a hundred temples in a single hour unless someone *built* that silence. And now the silence was cracking, and the thing in Ashkarat was afraid—not of rebellion, not of the free cities, not of any army Maren could imagine—but of a candle-flame god and a man who called storms.

*What are you?* she asked the redacted line in her memory, as she asked it every night. *And what did you do fifteen years ago that you cannot say aloud?*

The lamp guttered. Outside, far off over the sea, thunder muttered—harmless, ordinary, the sky clearing its throat. Maren found she had stopped writing. Her pen had left a single ink blot spreading on the page like a wound.

She blotted it, closed the ledger, and told herself, as she always did, that doubt was a luxury for people who could afford to be wrong.

She was beginning to suspect she no longer could.

* * *

On the ninth day, the net began to close, and Maren learned how fine a thread she needed.

It came through the lace. The widow Maren Ostreva mended lace for the wives of harbor clerks, and harbor clerks' wives talked, and one of them—chatty, kind, hopeless at secrets—mentioned that the Council had a guest. A priestess. Defrocked, from the burned temple in Corvane, come to plead the cities' cause. "Sera, they call her. Though between us, half the council thinks she's a spy and the other half thinks she's a saint, and my husband says both halves are wrong, she's just a woman with no temple left who won't stop talking."

Maren's hands did not pause on the lace. "A priestess traveling alone?"

"Oh no, dear, that's the scandal of it. She has a companion. Big quiet fellow, keeps to himself. They say—" the woman dropped her voice, delighted with it— "they say he came out of the Valley of Stillness. Can you imagine? Fifteen years nobody comes out of that place, and now one does, right when the sky starts breaking open again. My husband's cousin works the ferry records. There's a name in the ledgers. Isandro Almeida. Registered out of Stillwater village not two months past."

*Isandro Almeida.*

Maren set down the lace. She smiled at the woman, thanked her, paid her, and walked home through streets that seemed suddenly to have rearranged themselves around a single point of light.

Because she knew the name. Of course she knew the name. Every warden record, every border registry, every scrap the Stillness wardens had surrendered to the Regent's chancellery over fifteen years had passed across her desk in the last month, and one file was thinner than all the others—one man who had entered the valley as a boy of seventeen with no history and no magic, and lived fifteen years in perfect, obedient nothing, and then walked out the week the gods woke, and crossed two hundred miles of Vhalen leaving a wake of answered prayers and dead wardens behind him.

Isandro Almeida. Ex-storm-caller, if the old warden's notes were to be believed—trained, then exiled, then erased so thoroughly that even the valley forgot to fear him.

And now he sat in Delmare, in the same city as her, sheltered by a disgraced priestess, petitioning a council too divided to protect him.

That evening she stood at her window above the chandler's shop and watched the harbor lights come up one by one, and she allowed herself, briefly, the pleasure of a finished thing. She had reconstructed his entire route without ever seeing his face: the ferry at Stillwater, the pilgrim road, the saltmarsh shrine, the rooftop in Corvane, the smuggler's barge downriver. Every witness fit. Every date fit. Somewhere in this city of ninety thousand souls, a man who could tear the sky open was hiding behind a widow's gossip and a council's indecision, and she had found him with lace and patience and no sword drawn at all.

This was the work. This was why order mattered: not because chaos was loud, but because it was *legible*, if you looked long enough. Even miracles left footprints.

Then, quietly, the other thought arrived, the one that had been waiting for her all day the way a patient animal waits at the edge of a firelight.

*If you are right about everything else,* it asked, *why does your stomach turn?*

She pressed her hand flat against the cold window glass. Down in the street below, a procession of children ran past with paper lanterns, chasing the festival dusk, and one small girl stumbled, and a stranger caught her arm and set her right and laughed, and the whole street laughed with her, and the sound rose up to Maren's window like warmth off a hearth.

In Corvane, that stranger would have been registered. In Ashkarat, the lanterns would have been confiscated. She had signed orders that made such streets quieter, and she had believed quiet was safety.

The Regent wanted Almeida alive. Wanted him delivered in secret, unquestioned, unharmed—to a palace where the king had not been seen in public for three years, where the chancellery's dispatches grew stranger every season, where a sentence about a small god had been redacted by a hand that shook.

*Bring him alive,* the order said. Alive for what?

Maren took out a fresh sheet and began, at last, to write the report she would send—not through channels, but by trusted courier, directly, because some truths were too sharp to pass through many hands. She wrote his name. His route. His location, probable, precise to three streets. Her pen hovered over the final line, the one that would loose the hounds, the one that would bring soldiers into these laughing lantern-lit streets within the fortnight.

She wrote it. She sealed it. She set it on the table beside her lamp.

And then she sat looking at it for a long time, until the candle burned down and the wax softened, and outside, far over the dark sea, thunder rolled again—closer now, or perhaps only closer-sounding—and Maren Ostreva, who had never in twenty years hesitated at the sealing of an order, did not sleep.

In the morning, the report would go. It had to go; that was what she was; the net did not decide which fish it caught.

But that night, walking once more through the festival streets to clear her head, she passed the Tide Gate and saw, in the shadow of the customs house, a council messenger hurrying out with a sealed packet and a hired horse—and she recognized, by his livery and his haste and the direction he rode, the faction that had argued loudest for treating with the Regent.

Riding north. Riding toward Ashkarat. Carrying, if Maren's instincts were worth twenty years of service—and they were—something worth more than any letter she could write.

Someone in Delmare was selling the storm-caller already.

And Maren understood, with the cold clarity she trusted more than faith, that whatever happened next would happen fast, and that her net—so patiently woven, so precisely cast—was about to be dragged through waters she had not charted, by hands that were not hers.
