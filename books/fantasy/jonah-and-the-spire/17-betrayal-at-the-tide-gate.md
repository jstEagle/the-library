## 17. Betrayal at the Tide Gate

The summons came at dawn, written on council paper and smelling faintly of salt and ink.

*By order of the Free Cities Council of Delmare, the storm-caller known as Isandro Almeida is requested to attend the Tide Gate at first light, there to demonstrate before the assembled delegates the power he has sworn by oath to place in the city's service.*

Sera read it twice, her lips moving. Then she looked up at him across the narrow room they'd been given in the council house—a room with one window, one bed, and a lock that turned from either side.

"Requested," she said. "Not summoned. Requested."

"They wrote it politely," Isandro said. "That's what worries me."

He had slept badly. He had slept badly every night since the oath, since he had stood before forty squabbling delegates and sworn his life against his promise, and felt the storm-oath settle into his bones like a second spine. It did not hurt. That was the strange part. It simply *was*, a weight of consequence threaded through him, tightening whenever he thought of running, loosening when he thought of Ashkarat. He had made a bargain with the sky inside himself, and the sky kept excellent accounts.

Sera dressed in the dark, moving with the economy of someone who had learned never to trust a morning. She was wearing her traveling coat, not her temple gray. He noticed that. He noticed that she had packed her satchel the night before, and that the forged papers were in the inner pocket rather than the outer one, and that she had put her hair up under a scarf the way pilgrim women did when they expected trouble.

"You think it's a trap," he said.

"I think councils are weather," Sera said. "You can read all the signs you want and still get soaked." She paused at the door, hand on the latch. "But I think if they wanted you dead, they wouldn't have asked you to come to a public gate at dawn with half the harbor watching. Traps are quiet things. This is going to be loud either way."

"Loud how?"

She didn't answer. She opened the door instead, and the smell of the city came in—fish and tar and woodsmoke, the particular brine of Delmare's canals at low tide—and somewhere below, a bell began to ring the hour.

Isandro followed her down.

* * *

The Tide Gate was the oldest thing in Delmare, older than the council house, older than the walls. It was a great stone arch set into the seaward face of the city, through which the tide poured twice a day with a sound like a held breath released, and the people of Delmare had built their whole stubborn freedom around it: *the sea comes in, the sea goes out, no king commands the water.* They had carved it over the arch in letters a hand-span deep. Isandro had read those letters his first morning in the city and thought them brave and slightly foolish, the way free people always sounded to someone who had spent fifteen years hiding.

This morning the square before the arch was full.

Delegates in their chains of office stood on a timber platform built against the seawall. Behind them, the harbor lay flat and pewter-colored under a sky the color of unpolished tin—no wind, no gulls even, as though the whole coast were holding still to watch. Soldiers of the city guard lined the perimeter, but they stood loosely, uncertainly, glancing at the delegates more often than at the crowd. And the crowd itself—fishwives and dockhands and pilgrims who had stayed past the season—pressed shoulder to shoulder behind rope barriers, murmuring, craning.

They had come to see the storm-caller.

Isandro walked toward the platform with Sera at his shoulder and felt their eyes move over him like hands. He had been three weeks in Delmare. In three weeks he had become a story: the man who had called lightning on a rooftop in Corvane, the man who had saved a child at the saltmarsh shrine with a storm that parted around her like water around a stone, the man who had sworn an oath that made the older delegates flinch. Some of them looked at him with hope. More of them looked at him the way farmers look at a sky gone green before hail.

A delegate met them at the foot of the platform stairs—a heavy man with a merchant's chain and a smile like a ledger balance. Lord-Councilor Havel, whose faction had argued loudest for using Isandro and hardest for trusting no one.

"The council thanks you for coming," Havel said, and his voice carried, pitched for the crowd. "Delmare does not ask its guests to perform like mummers. But these are days that ask everything of everyone." He gestured toward the arch, the open sea beyond. "A small demonstration. A show of the power you've promised us. The people need to see it. Hope is a supply like any other, and ours runs low."

"And after?" Sera asked quietly.

"After, the council deliberates further," Havel said smoothly, and Isandro heard it then—the *further*, the door left open, the word doing work in the dark. He looked past Havel's shoulder at the platform. Forty seats. Perhaps thirty occupied. The empty ones clustered together, and he recognized the colors: the shipping families, the grain factors, the faction that had voted last week to send envoys to the Regent asking terms.

They weren't here. Their soldiers were.

Isandro's mouth went dry. "Where are the envoys supposed to be right now?" he asked.

Havel's smile did not change, but something behind it did—the smallest settling, like a man shifting his weight before a lift. "Envoys travel," he said. "It is what envoys do."

The bell rang again. First light came up gray over the harbor, and Isandro stepped onto the platform because there was nowhere else to go, and because the oath pulled at him like a tide, and because some part of him—the part that had spent fifteen years learning exactly how betrayal moved through a room—had already begun counting exits.

There were two. Both blocked.

* * *

He called the storm gently.

That was the discipline of it now, the thing the saltmarsh weeks had taught him: not summoning but *asking*, opening a door in himself and letting the sky lean through. He raised his hands over the Tide Gate and felt the pressure drop, felt the pewter sky bruise and darken, felt the first cold breath of wind come off the sea and move the crowd's clothes all in one direction, like grass under a passing hand.

He gave them thunder without teeth. He gave them lightning stitched along the underside of the clouds, harmless and enormous, a banner unfurled. He gave them rain that fell silver in the new light and stopped at the edge of the platform as though it had manners. The crowd made a sound he had never heard before—not fear, not awe, but both braided together, a low moaning cheer—and for one moment, one whole breath, Isandro let himself believe this was what purpose felt like.

Then he saw the boats.

They came around the breakwater in formation, six long hulls flying no colors at all, which was itself a color—the color of deniability. They were war galleys, lean and black-hulled, and they came on under oars with the terrible smooth patience of things built for exactly this. The crowd saw them a heartbeat after he did, and the murmur turned, curdled, became something frightened.

On the platform, Havel had stopped smiling.

"No," Isandro said. Not loudly. Just the word, into the wind he had made.

"The council negotiated a delay," Havel said, and now the smoothness cracked and Isandro could hear the sweat under it. "Terms. Time. The army is two weeks out—they would have burned the harbor, taken the city, put every god-touched soul in Delmare to the sword or the registry. We bought months. Maybe a year. We bought *time*, storm-caller, and the price was—" He couldn't finish it. He looked at the boats instead.

"The price was me," Isandro said.

"The price was you," Havel agreed, wretchedly honest at the end, the way weak men are. "Alive. Unharmed. Those were the terms. The Inquisitor-General herself is aboard the flagship. She keeps her word—I checked, I *checked*—"

And Sera moved.

Later, Isandro would replay it a thousand times and never make it slower. Sera's hand went into her coat—not for a weapon; she had none—but for the little tin star of Vess she had worn since Corvane, the candle-god's sigil, and she held it up over her head where the crowd could see, and she shouted, in a voice trained by years of liturgy to fill a nave:

"*The old gods answer prayers again! The silence is broken! Look at the sky—he is proof, look UP—*"

And the crowd looked up. Hundreds of faces, wet with his rain, tilted back toward the bruised and burning clouds, and whatever Havel had bought with his treachery, he had not bought the crowd, and for ten seconds—ten glorious, doomed seconds—Delmare believed in something larger than its fear.

Then the galleys fired their ballistae.

The bolts came in high arcs, trailing lines, and they were not aimed at Isandro at all. They were aimed at the platform's supports, at the crowd barriers, at the seawall steps—the geometry of panic, engineered by someone who understood crowds the way Isandro understood storms. The timber shrieked. The barriers went down. Three hundred people surged away from the water all at once, and in that churning confusion the black-hulled soldiers came ashore in longboats, gray cloaks, visored helms, moving in pairs with the unhurried competence of men who had done this in nine cities already.

Isandro reached for the storm.

It came to him gladly—it always came gladly now, that was the terror of it—and he stood in the falling rain with lightning coiling down his arms and he could have emptied those boats. He could have split the breakwater. He could have made the harbor remember why no king commands the water. The power sat in him like a shout waiting to be shouted, and every instinct he owned screamed at him to use it—

—and the crowd was between him and the boats. Three hundred people, trampling each other in the smoke and rain, and every bolt he threw would pass through them, and every wave he raised would drown the fishwives who had cheered him thirty seconds ago. He had learned that much. He had learned it at the cost of a dead warden on a Corvane rooftop, and the lesson held even now, even here, his hands full of sky and nowhere safe to put it.

"SERA!" 

He saw her then, twenty feet away, being folded into the press of gray cloaks—saw her go down fighting, saw her drive her elbow into a visor and get her wrist caught and twisted, saw her turn her head once, deliberately, and find him across the chaos. She wasn't screaming. She was *looking* at him, hard, deliberate, with an expression he had seen on her only once before, at the ford, when she'd told him *don't you dare die stupidly*—and he read it now, read it in the half-second she allowed herself:

*Run. Don't be a fool. RUN.*

Then a hood went over her head, and she was gone into the gray of them, and the storm in Isandro's hands turned to hail for one wild uncontrolled heartbeat, stinging the backs of the fleeing crowd, before he wrestled it down, down, down, choking on it.

Something small and bright flickered at his elbow.

Vess.

The god was barely a god anymore—barely a candle, barely a coal, a shivering scrap of gold light no bigger than his thumb, guttering in the rain that should have killed it outright. Since Blackreach, since it had burned itself nearly to nothing covering his escape, Vess had lived in his pocket like a dying ember, speaking in words of four syllables when it could speak at all. Now it rose beside his ear, and its voice was thin as paper and steady as bedrock.

"*Left. Arch. Under.*"

"I'm not leaving her—"

"*Dead, you save nothing. Left. NOW.*"

The soldiers had seen him. Of course they had seen him; he was standing in a circle of unnatural calm with lightning crawling on his skin, and a horn sounded from the flagship, one long note, and the gray cloaks in the square stopped chasing civilians and started converging with the single-minded hunger of hounds that have finally been given the scent. Maren's soldiers. Maren's precision. Somewhere on that flagship, he knew, the Inquisitor-General was watching through a glass, cataloguing everything: his position, his hesitation, the exact shape of his mercy.

*She'll learn from this,* he thought, with a clarity that felt like ice water. *Every second I stand here, she learns.*

"*Under the arch,*" Vess whispered. "*I have one miracle left. Let me spend it.*"

He ran.

* * *

The Tide Gate's arch was hollow at its base—the old engineers had left maintenance galleries in the stone, passages where the tide itself passed through twice a day, slick-walled and black and utterly unmapped by any soldier's briefing. Vess's flicker of light led him down a ladder slick with weed, along a gallery where the water ran thigh-deep and cold as guilt, while above him, muffled by tons of stone, he heard the square being systematically dismantled: shouts, orders in the usurper's cadence, the creak of the platform being torn apart board by board.

They were searching for him. Methodically. Door to door, boat to boat, and every report crackling back to the flagship would refine the net.

In the black throat of the gallery, thigh-deep in the sea's cold pulse, Isandro finally stopped running and let himself feel it.

Sera. Taken. Because she had stood up in front of three hundred people and named the truth, because she had spent her faith on him one more time when she had every reason to hoard what was left. He could see her still—her chin up, her voice ringing off the seawall, the tin star lifted high—and he could see the hood coming down, and the two images would not separate, would not blur, would not soften. They just alternated. Her courage. Her capture. Her courage. Her capture.

And beneath the grief, colder, the other thing. The arithmetic.

Havel had sold him to buy time, and the worst part—the part that would keep him awake on every road from here to Ashkarat—was that Havel might even be right. Two weeks until the army arrived. A year, maybe, purchased with one fugitive. What was one fugitive, weighed against a city? What was one man's freedom against three hundred lives that hadn't drowned today because the ballistae fired at timber instead of flesh?

The oath stirred in his chest, patient and pitiless. *You swore,* it reminded him. *Ashkarat. The king. Your life against your promise.* The oath did not care that his allies were gone. The oath did not care that he was alone. It simply held him to the shape of what he'd promised, the way the tide holds to the moon.

He laughed—one short, ugly sound that echoed strangely off the wet stone—and Vess flickered beside him, concerned.

"*Grief later,*" the god said gently. "*Rowing now.*"

"There's no boat."

"*There is always a boat. Look.*"

Around the next bend of the gallery, where the passage opened into a flooded cistern chamber, an old skiff sat bobbing at a rotted mooring—fishermen's cache, probably, hidden from the harbor taxes for a generation. Isandro hauled himself over the gunwale, shaking, and found the oars where Vess indicated, and pushed off into the dark.

The cistern had one outlet: a grate, rusted through at one corner, wide enough for a skiff if you angled it, opening onto open water beyond the breakwater. Beyond the reach of the search. Beyond, if he angled south and east and did not stop, the sea road to Ashkarat.

He rowed. His arms hurt. Everything hurt. The rain found him again as soon as he cleared the grate, and the gray hulls of the galleys were visible far off against the lighter gray of the harbor mouth, lanterns swinging, boats lowering, the net drawing closed over a city that had sold him and would learn soon enough what the Regent's gratitude was worth.

Vess rode in the bow, a coal wrapped in cloth now, dimming by the minute. Isandro could feel it happening—the way you can feel a fire dying in a room, a warmth withdrawing. The god had spent nearly everything at Blackreach. This morning's guidance had cost it the rest of almost.

"Vess." His voice came out scraped. "How much of you is left?"

A long pause. Rain on canvas. Far off, a gull crying over water that had gone back to pretending nothing had happened.

"*Enough,*" the god said, which was what gods say when the true answer is *not enough*, and Isandro rowed.

* * *

He rowed through the morning and into the afternoon, alone on a pewter sea, and the loneliness came down on him like the sky had come down in the valley fifteen years ago—total, patient, familiar. He had been here before. Not this water, but this exact place inside himself: the moment after the door closes, when you understand that everything you were protecting others from has found you anyway, and the only person left to pay is you.

No. Not the only one. Sera was paying too, somewhere behind him, in a gray cloak's custody, on that flagship, in front of Maren. He knew what Maren did with god-touched prisoners. He had heard the stories in Corvane's underworld, told in whispers: the questioning that wasn't torture, the truths offered like knives, the way people came out of her interviews *changed*. Sera would not break quickly. Sera might not break at all. But she would pay, hour by hour, for the crime of believing in him, and he could do nothing—nothing but row south, and east, toward the capital where her captors' master waited, toward the one act that might make any of it mean anything.

Unbind the king. Free the thing wearing Aurelian from the thing wearing him. End it.

Or die on the sea road, alone, having lost everything except the promise.

The storm-oath tightened in his chest as the light failed, not painfully, but unmistakably—a reminder, a tether, a hand closing gently around his heart. *South,* it said. *East. Keep rowing.*

And then, as the first stars came out over the water and his shoulders shook with exhaustion, Vess spoke one last time from the folds of cloth in the bow—so faint now that Isandro had to stop rowing to hear it.

"*Blackreach gaol,*" the god whispered. "*She will be taken there. Maren keeps her prizes close. And storms, storm-caller—storms can reach anywhere the sky touches.*"

Isandro stared at the little dying ember in the bottom of his boat.

"What are you saying?"

"*I am saying,*" Vess breathed, and the light gutters, steadied, guttered again, "*that you are not as alone as you feel. And that the sky owes you favors. Go to the lighthouse keeper on the cape north of the river mouth. Tell him the ember sent you. He owes me a debt older than your exile.*"

The light sank to a pinpoint. Held. Dimmed.

"*And Isandro—when you call the storm for her—call it kindly. She will know your voice.*"

The ember went out.

For a long time Isandro sat in the drifting skiff, alone on a dark sea, with a dead god's last words cooling in his ears and an oath burning in his chest and a name he didn't yet understand—*the lighthouse keeper, the ember sent me*—turning over and over in his mind like a coin he couldn't read.

Then he took up the oars.

South. East. The sea road to Ashkarat, and somewhere behind him a prison, and somewhere ahead of him a lighthouse, and between him and both of them, nothing at all but water, night, and the storm gathering slowly in his blood.

He rowed into the dark, and the dark, for the first time in fifteen years, felt less like a refuge and more like a road.
