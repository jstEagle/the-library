## 18. What Sera Endures

They took her shoes first.

Sera noticed that, in the first hour of her captivity, and understood it was deliberate. Bare feet on cold stone kept a body honest. It was the kind of small cruelty that presented itself as procedure—the gaolers of Blackreach had a hundred such refinements, none of which left marks. The usurper's inquisitors had learned, over fifteen years of hunting god-touched, that bruises healed and scars faded, but a woman who could not feel her toes after three days remembered why she'd lost them every time she stood.

The cell was clean. That was the worst part, Sera thought, sitting with her back against the wall and her knees drawn up beneath the rough gray shift they'd given her. No straw, no filth, no rats. A cot with a blanket folded at its foot. A bucket behind a screen. A single candle—unlit—in an iron sconce, placed where she could see it but not reach it.

Someone had studied her order. Someone knew what an unlit candle meant to a priestess of the old gods: a vigil never begun, a prayer never offered, a god who might be listening and might not, and no way to know which.

She had been in the cell for what she judged to be two days when the door opened without a sound on oiled hinges, and the Inquisitor-General Maren came in carrying a tray.

Not chains. Not instruments. A tray, with bread and hard cheese and a clay cup of watered wine, which she set down on the floor between them with the care of a woman laying out surgical tools.

"Sister Sera," Maren said. "Formerly of the Temple of the Ninefold Dawn, Corvane chapter."

"I'm no one's sister anymore," Sera said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "My order burned me off their rolls before the temple smoke cleared. You should have your facts straight if you're going to interrogate me."

"I have my facts very straight." Maren sat down across from her, cross-legged, unhurried, her gray traveling cloak still damp from the rain outside. She was younger than Sera had expected—not much past forty—and plain in the way of women who had decided long ago that their faces were for work. Her eyes were the color of slate and about as warm. "That is rather the point of me. Eat. You'll want your strength; I intend to talk for a long time."

"I'm not hungry."

"You are. Two days of prison bread will do that." Maren poured the wine herself, into one cup, and drank from it first—a small ritual courtesy, showing it wasn't drugged. Then she set it down again. "I'm told you refused to eat this morning. I'm also told you asked the gaoler whether the candle was a test or an insult. Which was it?"

"Both," Sera said. "It depends on whether you light it."

Maren almost smiled. Almost. It moved her face like weather moving over stone. "You're going to be difficult."

"I'm going to be *honest*," Sera said. "There's a difference. Difficult people waste your time. Honest people tell you exactly how much of it you're going to spend."

* * *

The first day, Maren asked questions.

They were good questions—Sera gave her that much. Not the bludgeoning demands of a soldier or the circular riddling of a zealot, but precise, patient inquiries laid one against another like masonry. Where had she met the storm-caller? On the pilgrim road, she said. What did he call himself? Tam, she said, and it wasn't even a lie she had to construct; he'd been using the name since Corvane. Where was he now?

"Gone," Sera said.

"Gone where?"

"Gone." She wrapped her hands around her knees. "You've read the reports, Inquisitor-General. A man calls lightning off a rooftop in Corvane in front of forty witnesses and then walks out of a city under curfew. If I knew where he was, do you honestly think I'd be sitting here?"

"Yes," Maren said simply. "Because you're a believer, and believers trade anything for the chance to convert their captor. It's the oldest gambit in the faith. Saint Veyra talked her way out of a Dalmiran dungeon by converting three gaolers and a warden. You've been reciting her litany under your breath since I walked in. *Veyra of the gates, open what is shut.*"

Sera went cold. She hadn't known she was doing it. Fifteen years of silence, and the old prayers still lived in her body somewhere below thought, surfacing the way a drowning woman's hands surface.

"You were at the temple," Maren said, watching her. "When it burned."

"You know I was."

"The Corvane chapter house. Eleven years ago. You were a novice then—nineteen. You carried four people out through the smoke. Two of them lived." Maren's voice didn't change at all. It was the flatness that made it terrible—that she had learned these details not out of cruelty but out of thoroughness, the way a cartographer learns coastlines. "Your mentor, Prelate Anselm, died on the steps. He was singing when the arrows took him. Do you know what song it was?"

Sera said nothing.

"*The Lament for the Sleeping Gods*," Maren said. "Which is interesting, isn't it? Because the gods weren't sleeping then. They were silent. Your whole order spent fifteen years insisting there was a difference, and the crown spent those same years insisting there wasn't. And then—" she spread her hands slightly, "—a man calls lightning out of a clear sky, and suddenly everyone has to decide what they believed."

"What do you believe?" Sera asked.

"I believe in order," Maren said. "Miracles are just weather that hasn't been assigned a purpose yet."

* * *

On the third day, Maren stopped asking questions.

That was when Sera understood the shape of the thing being built around her. The interrogation wasn't a wall to break against. It was a room being constructed brick by brick, and each answer she gave—or didn't give—was another brick, and Maren was in no hurry at all, because the room would close eventually regardless. Hunger did its quiet work. Sleep came in thin slices, broken by the gaoler's boots in the corridor at irregular hours, so that time itself began to lose its mortar. On the fourth day Sera caught herself talking to the unlit candle, explaining something to it, apologizing to it, and stopped mid-sentence with her heart hammering, because she had seen this happen to others in the temple's history—faithful women worn smooth as river stones until whatever was left inside them slid out easily.

*They will not torture you,* Anselm had taught, in the last year of the temple, when the crown's attention had begun to turn toward them. *Torture makes martyrs, and martyrs make converts. They will offer you comfort instead. Comfort is worse. Comfort asks you to betray yourself gradually, in installments.*

He had died on the steps singing, and his teaching had died with him, and now Sera sat in a clean cell with a tray of food she wouldn't touch and understood that Anselm had been right about everything except the ending. There was no ending. There was only the next installment.

On the fifth day, Maren brought a chair.

It was a plain wooden chair, and she set it in the middle of the cell, and she sat in it facing Sera with her hands folded in her lap, and she said: "I'm going to tell you a story. You don't have to respond. You don't even have to listen. But I've found that stories are more comfortable than questions, for both of us, and I think you're tired of questions."

"I'm tired of you," Sera said. Her voice was rougher than she liked. Five days of thin sleep and half-rations had sanded her edges down; she could hear it herself.

"That too passes," Maren said. "Everything passes. That's rather the doctrine I serve." She leaned back. "Once, there was a kingdom with two princes."

The candle in the sconce seemed, absurdly, to lean toward her.

"Twins," Maren continued. "Born the same night, in the same hour, to Queen Liora and King Aldric. One was named Aurelian, and he was loud and golden and everyone loved him. The other—" she paused, and for the first time something flickered behind the slate eyes, something that might have been distaste, "—the other has no name. Not because he died. Because it was taken. Every record of his birth was amended. Every portrait repainted. Every servant who'd held him was reassigned or retired. There is a boy who existed for six years in the palace of Ashkarat, who had a nurse and a tutor and a favorite horse, and there is not one document in the royal archive that proves it."

Sera kept her face still. Inside, something had begun to fall, slowly, like a tower leaning.

"Why would someone do that?" Maren asked the ceiling. "Erase a child? It's an enormous amount of work. Paper doesn't burn itself. And the answer, historically, is always the same: either to hide him, or to unmake him. And here's what interests me, Sister Sera. Fifteen years ago, the binding rituals of the old covenant were performed in the palace underground—this is in our own records, sealed ones, which I have read, because reading sealed things is my entire personality according to my colleagues. Those rituals required a blood price. And in the same season, a storm-caller appeared in the Valley of Stillness, a valley whose wardens report directly to the crown, a valley that does not appear on any map printed after that year."

The falling thing inside Sera hit bottom.

"No," she said.

"It's a story," Maren said mildly. "Stories don't require belief. But consider the shape of it. A hidden prince, bound out of the world to keep him safe—or to keep the world safe from him. A king on the throne who has not aged visibly in fifteen years, whose own generals describe him as 'distant,' whose confessor resigned without explanation and was pensioned into silence. And now a storm-caller walking the free cities with a defrocked priestess, moving with remarkable purpose toward the capital, leaving lightning behind him like footprints." She tilted her head. "What would you call that pattern, if you saw it in a ledger?"

"I'd call it a story you want me to believe so I'll give up a name."

"I already have the name," Maren said. "I've had it since Corvane. Isandro Almeida. Ferryman's foster-son, Stillwater village, entered the valley fifteen years ago with no history and a warden's escort. What I don't have is certainty. And certainty, Sister, is the one thing I cannot manufacture. So I'm offering you a trade. Not your freedom—I can't authorize that, and we both know you wouldn't take it. Truth for truth. You confirm nothing. You deny nothing. You simply listen to the rest of the story, and afterward, if you still think I'm lying, you may tell me so, and I will leave you alone until the trial."

"There won't be a trial," Sera said. "People like me don't get trials."

"People like you get exactly the trial you're owed," Maren said, and for a moment—for just a moment—her voice cracked along some seam Sera couldn't see, "which is more than most people get. Listen."

* * *

What Maren told her, over the next hour, was not a story. It was evidence.

She produced documents—actual documents, drawn from oilcloth cases, laid out on the floor between them with the same surgical care as the tray. Sera looked at them because refusing to look was its own kind of defeat, and because Anselm had also taught her that a believer who fears evidence has already lost the argument with herself.

A copy of a palace census, fifteen years old, with two lines struck through in different inks. A page from a midwife's ledger, the second entry scraped away so thoroughly the vellum had thinned—but parchment remembers pressure, and under the raking light of the candle Maren had finally permitted lit (she lit it herself, with a taper, watching Sera's face as she did), the ghost of letters rose: *tw*. Twins.

And then a letter. Sealed with the signet of Queen Liora, intercepted—so Maren said—at a border crossing eleven years ago, never delivered, catalogued and forgotten in an inquisitor's archive until Maren herself had pulled it three weeks ago, following the thread of a rooftop lightning strike backward through time.

Sera read it twice. The queen's hand was elegant and hurried:

*To whoever finds this: if my son lives, tell him the binding was not punishment. Tell him his mother chose the lesser grief, and that the greater grief was chosen for her, and that she would choose both again. Tell him the crown lies, and the throne hungers, and that what sits upon it must never be fed what it asks for. Tell him—*

The letter ended there. The rest had been cut away, cleanly, by someone who knew what they were doing.

"Intercepted correspondence is routinely truncated," Maren said. "Standard practice. But I want you to notice what wasn't cut. *The crown lies. The throne hungers.* This is the queen of Vhalen, writing in cipher-plain, warning her own son away from the seat of power. Why would a loyal queen warn her child off her own husband's throne?"

"Perhaps the king was ill."

"Perhaps." Maren gathered the documents with unhurried hands. "Or perhaps the Regent—who is not, incidentally, the king; note the title, note how carefully the court uses the title—knows precisely what sits on that throne, and fears it, and fears even more the one bloodline that could contest it. Perhaps the orders I receive—to bring the storm-caller alive, alive, at any cost, while every other god-touched soul in Vhalan goes to the pyre—are not the orders of a man protecting his realm. Alive is a strange mercy from a butcher, Sister. Ask yourself what a frightened man wants with a living storm-caller. Ask yourself what a hungry thing wants with him."

Sera's hands had gone cold. "You're trying to turn me."

"I'm trying to *inform* you," Maren said. "Turning comes later, if at all. I have served the crown for twenty-two years. I am not in the habit of sedition, and I want you to understand that everything I've shown you is in the crown's own archives, gathered by the crown's own servants, including me. I am not offering you heresy. I'm offering you the possibility that the heretics were right, and that the truth is worse than either side's story." She stood, smoothing her cloak. "One more thing, and then I'll go."

She paused at the door.

"The unbinding rites. Your order preserved fragments—we know, we've collected them for years, incomplete, contradictory. Every version agrees on one point, and it's the point your storm-caller may not know, or may be hiding from you, or may be hiding from himself. The binding that holds the king holds *two*. Whatever was done fifteen years ago split a burden in half, and when the knot is cut, the debt comes due on both halves. Unbind Aurelian, and whatever was bound with him comes home. All of it. The memories. The exile. The cost." Her slate eyes rested on Sera with something that was almost, terribly, kindness. "If your storm-caller is the erased prince, then freeing the king means giving him back everything he is—including the brother who was traded to buy his life. And if he isn't—if he's only a ferryman's son with stolen thunder—then the rite will still collect from whoever performs it. Either way, Sister, someone pays. Either way, someone is destroyed by the saving. I'd like to know, before I write my report, whether you're guarding a liberator or a sacrifice. Because from where I sit, it looks remarkably like both."

The door closed behind her without a sound.

* * *

Sera did not sleep that night.

She sat with the lit candle—Maren had left it burning, the final refinement, comfort offered and withdrawn in the same gesture—and she watched the flame and turned the letter over and over in her mind. *Tell him the crown lies.* Anselm's voice in her memory, and Maren's voice over it, and underneath both, faint as a pulse, the memory of a man on a rain-soaked rooftop calling lightning out of his own skin with his eyes wide and terrified, wilder than any trained caller, wilder than anything in the temple's oldest accounts—wild as something that had never been taught because it had never been allowed to exist.

*Two anchors,* the fragmentary rites said. *The knot binds twain; cut one, loose both.*

She had translated that line herself, seven years ago, in a scriptorium that no longer existed, and had assumed it meant the king and the parasite. King and hunger, bound together, freed together. It had never occurred to her—why had it never occurred to her?—that *twain* might mean two sons. Two princes born the same night. One crowned, one erased. One bound to the throne, one bound out of the world.

And the valley. The valley that forgot. The valley that magic could not follow, that maps omitted, that wardens guarded—a perfect vessel for a binding, if what you needed to bind was not a man but a *name*.

"He knows," she whispered to the candle. "Oh, gods, he has to know. He wouldn't—he wouldn't do this without telling me."

But even as she said it, she saw him: the ford, the wound in his side he'd laughed off, the way he'd gone quiet when Vess spoke of the unbinding's price, the careful blankness that came over his face whenever anyone asked what the ritual would cost. She had watched people lie for faith all her life. She knew the architecture of a concealed truth, and Isandro built his beautifully, with load-bearing silences and walls of deflection, and she had walked through his house of secrets for weeks calling it honesty because she wanted it to be.

The candle guttered. Somewhere above her, through layers of stone, the night wind moved off the sea, and Sera thought she heard—impossibly, faintly, the way you hear a word spoken in a dream—thunder. Far off. Directionless. Or perhaps not directionless at all. Perhaps circling. Perhaps a man with stolen lightning pacing the coastline of a prison, looking for a door.

Her certainty, which had carried her through fire and silence and fifteen years of praying into a void, did not break. Certainty, she discovered, was not a wall but a rope, and ropes fray strand by strand. She could feel each one going: the strand that said Maren lied—frayed, because the documents were real, she'd touched them, she'd read the queen's own hand. The strand that said Isandro would tell her everything—frayed, because she had watched him hide the price and say nothing. The strand that said the gods' return meant hope—frayed, hanging by threads, because what if the gods returned only to be eaten, what if the throne had been feeding on them for fifteen years, what if the silence had been the only mercy left?

One strand held. She tested it the way you test any rope: with all your weight.

Did she believe that Isandro Almeida—Tam, ferryman's son, storm-caller, whatever name the truth finally gave him—would walk into Ashkarat and trade himself for a brother who would never remember him, and let her find out afterward?

Yes.

Gods help her, yes. She had known him six weeks and she knew it the way she knew the litany, below thought, in the body: he would do it smiling, and apologize for the mess.

So the question was no longer whether to be loyal. The question was what loyalty meant now. Loyalty as silence—keeping his secret, guarding his sacrifice, honoring his choice? Or loyalty as the older kind, the harder kind, the kind Anselm had died too early to teach her: loyalty that refuses to let the beloved destroy themselves alone, that breaks trust to save the truster, that stands in the doorway of the sacrifice and says *not without me, and not like this.*

In the morning, Maren came again. She brought no tray this time, no documents, no chair. She stood in the doorway with her slate eyes shadowed, and she looked at Sera sitting upright on the cot with the candle burned to a stub beside her, and something passed over her face that in any other woman Sera would have called doubt.

"Well?" Maren said. "Liberator or sacrifice?"

Sera lifted her chin. The frayed rope held. It would hold, she decided, not because it was strong but because she would wrap her whole self around it and refuse to let go.

"Neither," she said. "You've got it wrong, Inquisitor-General. He's not going to free the king, and he's not going to die for him." She smiled, and it hurt her face, and she did it anyway. "We're going to do both. Together. And when you write your report, write this down: the heresy you should have been afraid of was never the old gods coming back."

Maren waited, motionless, a hunter hearing a sound in brush she'd thought empty.

"It's that they answered *us*," Sera said. "And we said yes."

For a long moment the corridor behind Maren was perfectly silent. Then, quietly, in a voice stripped of everything but the question itself—as though she were asking it of the archive, of the crown, of the fifteen years of sealed documents stacked in her own memory—Maren said:

"And if the price of saying yes is the man you love? What then, priestess?"

Sera opened her mouth to answer—

—and far above them, beyond the gaol roof, beyond the clouds rolling in black off the sea, thunder cracked once, sharp and close, like a knock at a door.

Both women looked up.

Maren's hand went to the keys at her belt. Sera's went, helplessly, uselessly, to her heart.

"That," Maren said softly, "is impossible. There's no storm forecast within sixty miles."

Sera began, despite everything, despite the cold cell and the frayed rope and the five days of hunger, to laugh—a low, exhausted, unbreakable sound.

"That's not weather," she said. "That's him. He's come for me."
