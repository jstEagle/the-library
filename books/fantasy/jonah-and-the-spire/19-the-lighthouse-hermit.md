## 19. The Lighthouse Hermit

The lighthouse stood on a fist of black rock at the edge of the Saltreach cliffs, and Isandro saw it before he understood what he was seeing—a needle of pale stone thrust up out of the sea-mist, its lamp long dark, its galleries crusted with the white salt-bloom that gave the coast its name. He had been walking for six days since Delmare, sleeping in ditches, hood drawn, flinching from every cloud. His boots had given out two days ago; he had wrapped his feet in sacking and told himself it was a disguise.

He was not following any road anymore. He was following the weather.

It had started as a feeling at the base of his skull, a tugging like a fisherman's line set deep in his flesh. When he turned south, the feeling slackened. When he turned north, toward the cliffs, it pulled. By the third day he had stopped pretending it was exhaustion or grief or the ghost of Vess's dying ember still lodged behind his eyes. Something on this coast wanted him to come, and after everything—after Sera's hands being forced behind her back by Maren's soldiers, after the god burning itself to cinders to buy him ten seconds of running—he no longer had the pride to refuse.

So he climbed the cliff path as the light failed, and the wind came off the water smelling of iron and old storms, and the lighthouse door stood open.

Not unlocked. *Open.* As if someone had been watching the path for hours and grown tired of waiting.

Inside, the stair wound upward into darkness, and from somewhere above came the smell of woodsmoke and fish stew, and a voice—dry as driftwood, unhurried—said:

"You track mud like a man who's never been taught better. Come up, then. The stew won't improve for your hesitation."

* * *

The old man at the top of the lighthouse was blind. Isandro knew it before the man turned his face toward him, because the eyes were the color of skimmed milk, filmed and still, fixed on nothing. He sat in a chair by a small iron stove with a blanket over his knees and a wooden spoon in his hand, stirring a pot without once looking at it, and the room around him was immaculate—coils of rope hung by size, brass fittings polished, charts rolled and tied with their labels facing outward. A blind man's order. An order built by touch over decades.

"Sit," the old man said. "You'll want the bowl more than the questions, whatever you tell yourself."

Isandro sat. The stew was fish and kelp and something rootlike he didn't recognize, and it was the best thing he had eaten since Stillwater, and he hated how much that showed on his face.

"I'm looking for no one," Isandro said. "I'm just traveling."

"Mm." The spoon tapped the pot's rim twice, precisely. "And I'm just an old man who leaves his door open for gulls." The milk-white eyes turned toward him, and though they could not see, Isandro felt seen anyway—the way he had felt seen by wardens, by inquisitors, by the thing in Corvane's chapel that wore candle flames. "Your name is mud, boy, but your *weather* isn't. You've been dragging a storm behind you for six days like a bride dragging a train. Every scryer between here and Ashkarat has felt you pass. You might as well have walked the pilgrim road ringing a bell."

Isandro set down the bowl. His hands were not quite steady. "Then why open the door?"

"Because I've been waiting for it." The old man leaned back. Up close, Isandro could see the map of him: sun-cured skin gone gray at the jaw, hands knotted like rootwood, a faded brand on the inside of one wrist—the mark of the Caller's Guild, struck through with a single deliberate line. Defrocked. Or retired. Or exiled, in the way that men who survive their own guilds are exiled. "Sixty years I kept this light. Forty of them I also kept the storms off this coast, because the Guild said a caller's first duty is to the ships. Then the gods went quiet, and my magic went with them, and then—" he smiled, thin and dry, "—then fifteen years ago, the sky over this coast went strange. Flat. Like a table with the cloth pulled too tight. And I thought: someone has been bound away somewhere, and the world is holding its breath."

His head tilted. Listening to something Isandro could not hear.

"And now the breath comes out," the old man said. "And here you come up my stairs trailing thunder like a wet dog trails water."

"My name is—"

"Don't." The word cracked like a whip, and for half a heartbeat the stove flame stood straight up, rigid, and the shadows in the room went sharp as knife cuts. Then the old man breathed out, and the flame swayed again, ordinary. "I don't want your name. Names are what got you hunted. What I want to know is whether you're going to kill yourself and half this coastline before winter, or whether there's enough left of you to be taught."

"Taught." Isandro laughed, and it came out ugly. "Taught what? How to run faster? How to hide better? They took Sera. They took—" He stopped, because his voice had begun to do something dangerous, and the stove flame was standing up straight again, and the old man was watching it with his blind eyes like a man reading a page.

"There it is," the old man said softly. "There's the crack in the dam. Feel that? That heat behind your breastbone when you think of her? That's not grief, boy. Grief sits still. That's *weather*. It wants somewhere to go, and if you don't give it somewhere, it will take somewhere. It will take a rooftop in Corvane. It will take a warden's life you didn't mean to end. It will take a valley full of people who never did you harm."

Isandro's throat closed. "How do you know about—"

"I know about every caller who was never taught," the old man said. "I buried three of them. Two by lightning and one by his own hand, because he couldn't live with what the lightning did while wearing him." He set down the spoon. "My name is Edran. I was the last master of the Caller's Guild before it ate itself. And you will stay the night, and tomorrow we begin, and you will hate me by midwinter. Those are the terms."

Isandro should have said no. Should have remembered that staying anywhere meant danger pooling around him like water around a keel. But the fire was warm, and the stew sat in him like an anchor, and somewhere below them the sea worked at the rocks with the patience of something that had all the time in the world.

"All right," he said.

* * *

The first lesson came at dawn, and it was not what Isandro expected.

Edran did not take him outside. He took him to the lamp room at the top of the tower, where the great lens sat dark under its dust cover, and made him sit cross-legged on the cold floor, and said: "Tell me what you feel."

"The wind," Isandro said. "The sea. Cold floor."

"Liar. Try again."

Isandro closed his eyes. And once he actually listened—once he stopped clamping down the way he had clamped down for fifteen years in the valley, the way he had clamped down on the road, the way he had been clamping so long he'd forgotten there was anything underneath—the world opened like a chest lid.

The pressure systems lay along the coast like sleeping animals. A trough of low air sat offshore, heavy with moisture, turning slowly. Wind moved in layers, fast rivers over slow ones, and where they sheared against each other the air hummed with charge. And far to the south, past the horizon, something vast and slow was breathing—in and out, in and out—a storm the size of a country, weeks old, wandering the open ocean like a beast with nowhere to be.

"Oh," Isandro said.

"Yes," Edran said. "*Oh.* Now here is what the Guild teaches, and here is what ruined us. The Guild says a caller summons. Calls down. Commands. Command language, command thinking—and the sky is older than kings, boy, and it does not *obey*. So the callers strain, and force, and the storm gives them what they demand and adds interest, and the interest is what kills people." He tapped his own temple. "A storm is not a weapon. It is a creature with a nature, like a horse or a river or a hungry dog. You do not summon a storm. You *negotiate* with it."

"Negotiate," Isandro repeated flatly. "With weather."

"With weather. You find what it wants—it wants to equalize, to discharge, to move its weight from the sea to the land—and you offer it a shape. A path. A courtesy. You ask it to spend itself *here*, on the empty water, instead of *there*, on the fishing fleet. And because you asked with respect, and because you offered it something true, it consents. Most of the time." The blind eyes glinted. "Sometimes it doesn't consent, and then you learn humility very quickly or not at all."

For fifteen years Isandro had treated his power like a disease. In the Valley of Stillness he had starved it; on the road he had feared it; in Corvane he had used it exactly twice, both times in terror, both times like a man swinging an axe in the dark. The idea that it was not a disease but a *relationship*—that the pressure behind his breastbone and the pressure offshore were the same thing, speaking the same language—was so large that he could only sit with it, blinking, while the gulls cried outside the glass.

"First exercise," Edran said. "Feel the small squall east of the point. The little one, building over the warm water. Don't touch it. Don't push it. Just... introduce yourself."

Introduce himself. To a cloud.

But Isandro reached out—not with his hands, not even with his mind, but with whatever it was that had been pulling him up this coast for six days—and touched the edges of the small storm, the way you'd rest fingertips on a stranger's shoulder.

The squall noticed him.

It was not thought, exactly. It was attention. A vast diffuse *noticing*, like the whole sky turning one eye toward him, curious and slightly annoyed, the way a cat regards a hand extended toward it. Isandro held very still. Offered nothing. Asked nothing. Just let it look at him.

After a long time—it might have been minutes, might have been an hour—the squall's attention withdrew, satisfied, the way a cat decides the hand is harmless. And in its wake it left something behind: a faint sense of... acknowledgment. Of *room made*. Like a seat pulled out at a table.

Isandro opened his eyes. He was shaking, drenched in sweat, grinning like an idiot.

"Well," Edran said dryly. "Either you've just made your first friend in the sky, or you've insulted it badly and it's going to rain on you specifically for a week. We'll know by supper."

* * *

The days took on a shape.

Dawn: the lamp room, sitting with the weather, learning its moods the way a shepherd learns a flock. Isandro learned that high thin clouds carried gossip from a thousand miles away; that the sea breathed charge up into the air at dusk; that the great southern storm had a rhythm to its wandering, a patience, almost a personality—old and slow and not to be trifled with, the elder of the coast.

Midday: work. Edran ran him up and down the lighthouse stair until his legs burned, made him haul rope, split wood, mend the salt-eaten shutters. "The body is the ground the lightning walks through," Edran said. "A weak ground dies. You want to be a good conductor of everything, including labor."

Evening: hard conversation, which was the real curriculum.

"Why did they bind me?" Isandro asked one night, staring into the stove. "Fifteen years. Why not just kill me?"

"Because killing a bound heir makes a martyr, and martyrs make rebellions," Edran said, too casually, and Isandro's head snapped up.

"What did you say?"

The old man stirred his stew. Said nothing.

"Edran. What did you say?"

"I said," the old man replied, with the mildness of a man discussing tide tables, "that whoever bound you knew their craft. Splitting a binding, hiding a bloodline, silencing the gods themselves—that's not usurper work. Usurpers burn things. This was *weaving*. Someone loved you very much and was very frightened, and those two things together make the strongest bindings in the world." He looked up, milk-eyed, unerring. "Eat your stew."

Another night: "You flinch from thunder," Edran observed. "Why?"

"Because it's mine," Isandro said, before he could stop himself. "Because every clap of it, some part of me thinks—I did that. Even when I didn't. Even in the valley, where nothing of mine could reach. Some part of me has always believed the storms were my fault."

Edran was quiet a long moment. "Good," he said finally. "That's honest. Now unlearn it. Thunder is not guilt, boy. Thunder is the sky clearing its throat. You'll forgive yourself someday or you won't, but either way the storms will keep happening, and you can meet them as a condemned man or as a kinsman. Choose."

* * *

The training changed him in ways he did not notice until they were already done.

He learned to walk *with* the pressure instead of against it—to feel a squall building and, rather than clamping down, open a channel, offer a shape: *spend yourself there, on the water, not here, on the cliffs.* The first time a storm agreed—actually turned aside, its rain falling silver on empty sea while the lighthouse stood dry—Isandro sat down on the gallery stones and laughed until he cried, because it had never once occurred to him that the sky could be kind.

He learned negotiation as etiquette. You greeted the weather before asking anything. You thanked it after. You never demanded what it had not offered, and you never, ever lied to a storm—they knew, somehow, the way animals know, and a lie cost you a season of trust.

He learned that his power had a signature, and that signatures could be softened. Edran taught him to wrap his workings in the coast's own ambient static, to speak to the sky in the local accent, so that the scryers hunting a rogue storm-caller would hear only weather being weather. "You cannot vanish," Edran said. "But you can become difficult to pick out of a crowd. Be the fourth wave, not the fifth."

And slowly—so slowly he didn't mark the day it happened—Isandro stopped fighting his own nature. The heat behind his breastbone stopped feeling like a fever and started feeling like a pulse. When grief for Sera rose in him now, he did not clamp it down; he opened a channel and let it move through him and out, and the sky took it and spent it over the water, and he was emptied and steadied, and the storm was fed and satisfied, and nobody died.

One evening in the fourth week, a genuine gale came up the coast—nothing historic, but honest, a proper autumn blow—and Edran said, "Yours tonight," and Isandro walked out onto the gallery into screaming wind and negotiated with the whole of it for four hours: easing the gusts that would have torn the fishing boats from their moorings in the cove below, spending the worst of the rain offshore, letting the storm keep its thunder because thunder harms no one. At dawn the cove's boats all floated, and the fishermen on the quay would say for years afterward that the storm had turned aside as if remembering something.

Isandro slept fourteen hours and woke up hungry and calm, and realized he could not remember the last time he had woken up calm.

* * *

It was near midwinter when Edran finally spoke of her.

They were in the lamp room at dusk, the sea gone pewter and rose, and Isandro was practicing the deep listening—sitting with the whole coastal weather laid out in his awareness like a chart—when Edran said, without preamble:

"She used to sit exactly like that. Spine straight, chin level, listening to the far storms. Your mother."

Isandro's eyes opened. The coastal weather flickered and blurred, and he had to consciously release it, gently, the way you'd set down something fragile.

"Queen Liora," Edran said. "Though I never called her that. To me she was Liora of the East Reaches, and she was the finest natural listener I ever trained, and I include myself. She didn't call storms, exactly. She *conversed* with them. The Guild elders said she'd ruin the discipline, talking to weather like it was company." He smiled at some private memory. "She was right, of course. It was company."

"You trained her." Isandro's voice came out strange. "You knew her."

"I trained her mother before her. Three generations of East Reach women, all listeners, all sent to me because the Guild didn't know what to do with listeners—they want callers, shouters, men who wrestle the sky for glory. Liora used to sit where you're sitting and say the storms were lonely." The old man's face worked. "Lonely. Fifteen years I've kept this light with no magic in me at all, and I think about that word. Lonely. And I think about what it costs, to silence something that was only ever trying to talk."

The wind moved through the gallery rail, soft, almost tentative.

"She wrote to me," Edran said. "Twice, after the binding. Letters smuggled out of the palace by means I never asked about, because asking is how people get killed. The first letter said: *If a boy ever comes to you with thunder in his bones and fear in his eyes, feed him.*" The old man's voice roughened. "The second letter came eleven years ago. It said: *If he asks about me, tell him the choice was mine, and I would make it again, and I am sorry for the price.*"

Isandro could not speak. The sea worked at the rocks below, patient, endless. Somewhere far south, the great old storm breathed in and out, in and out, indifferent and magnificent.

"Did she—" His voice broke. He tried again. "Did she say anything else? Anything about—about who I am? About my brother?"

Edran turned his blind face toward him, and for a moment the old man looked ancient beyond counting, a keeper of a light whose lamp had gone dark years ago and who tended it anyway.

"She said you'd ask that," Edran said quietly. "And she said the answer wasn't hers to give anymore. It's written down, boy. All of it. The coup, the twins, the bargain she struck. She paid a smuggler's fortune to have it carried to this coast, and I have kept it fifteen years against the day you climbed my stairs." He rose from his chair, joints cracking like ship's rigging, and laid one knotted hand on Isandro's shoulder—the first time he had ever touched him. "Tomorrow," he said. "Tonight you eat, and sleep, and let the weather hold what you can't. Tomorrow I give you her letters."

That night Isandro did not sleep. He sat in the lamp room with the dust cover off the great lens for the first time in decades, moonlight falling through the glass in a long white blade across the floor, and he listened to the sea, and the far southern storm, and the small honest sounds of an old blind man moving below, and he let the sky hold what he could not.

At dawn, Edran set a bundle in his hands: oilcloth wrapped around paper, tied with cord gone soft with age, and on the topmost letter, in a hand Isandro did not know but recognized the way you recognize your own reflection—

*To my son, if the sky remembers him.*

Below, far to the south, the great storm had turned. It was coming north along the coast, vast and patient, and every scrying eye in Vhalen would see it coming—and see, riding ahead of it on the wind, a lone figure walking toward the capital with letters from a dead queen in his pack and, for the first time in his life, no fear of thunder at all.
