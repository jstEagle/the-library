## 20. Mother's Letters

The lighthouse smelled of salt and old smoke, and in the three days since Isandro had arrived, he had learned its rhythms the way he had once learned the rhythms of Stillwater: the groan of the stairs at dawn when the old man climbed to polish the lamp, the whistle of wind through the broken shutter on the north side, the way the sea went quiet an hour before dusk as if it, too, were listening for something.

The hermit—Aldous, though he told Isandro not to use the name, said names were hooks and he'd spent sixty years pulling them out of himself—had begun his lessons at first light each morning. *Storms are not summoned,* he repeated, standing at the rail with his blind eyes turned toward the horizon as though he could see it anyway. *They are negotiated. You do not take from the sky. You ask, and you offer, and you keep your word.*

Isandro was learning. Slowly, painfully, with failures that left scorch marks on the rocks below and once blew the shutter clean off its hinges. But he was learning, and something in him that had been clenched for fifteen years was beginning, grudgingly, to unclench.

It was on the fourth evening, after a lesson that had gone well—a small squall offshore that Isandro had coaxed into bending around the lighthouse instead of through it—that Aldous did not climb to the lamp room. Instead he sat by the cold hearth in the lower chamber, his hands folded over a wooden box on his knees, and waited.

Isandro knew the posture. It was the posture of a man about to hand someone a weight.

"You've stopped flinching at thunder," Aldous said.

"Mostly."

"Mostly." The old man's mouth twitched. "Sit down, boy. There's something I've kept longer than I've kept this lighthouse, and my hands are getting too old to carry it much further."

Isandro sat. The box was plain wood, unremarkable, the kind fishermen used for tackle or tobacco. But when Aldous lifted the lid, Isandro saw that the inside was lined with oilcloth, and beneath the oilcloth lay a bundle of letters tied with ribbon gone gray with age, and beneath the letters, wrapped separately, a small locket of white gold on a snapped chain.

"Where did you—" Isandro began.

"A woman came to me fifteen years ago," Aldous said. "Rain-soaked, half-dead, riding hard enough to kill two horses between the capital and this coast. She wouldn't give me her name. She didn't have to." His blind eyes fixed on Isandro with terrible gentleness. "I knew Queen Liora when she was a girl. I taught her mother's brother the first shapes of storm-calling before the gods went quiet. I would have known her voice anywhere, even broken as it was."

Isandro's mouth had gone dry. "Queen Liora is dead."

"She is." No softening, no hesitation. Aldous had the honesty of men who had outlived the need for comfort. "She died within the year, here, in this room. But before she died, she made me swear an oath on salt and storm that I would keep these until a storm-caller came to my door who flinched at thunder but could not stay away from it." He pushed the box across the space between them. "She described you, Isandro. Though she never once spoke your name aloud. She said names were dangerous. She said—" the old man paused, and his voice roughened, "—she said the valley took your name along with everything else, and that was the price, and she was sorry, she was so sorry, every day she was sorry."

Isandro did not touch the box. He couldn't. His hands lay in his lap like things belonging to someone else.

"The valley," he said carefully. "You know about the valley."

"I know what she told me. I know what I felt the day the binding went through—the whole coast felt it, every caller still living felt it, the sky itself held its breath for nine minutes and then forgot why." Aldous leaned forward. "I don't know everything, boy. She didn't tell me everything. Some of it she didn't know herself. But I know this: those letters are yours. They were always meant to be yours. And I am an old man who has carried another family's grief for fifteen years because it was the last thing she asked of me, and I would very much like to set it down."

* * *

He read them by lamplight, alone, because Aldous had taken his cane and gone up to the lamp room without being asked, and Isandro understood that this too was a kind of kindness.

There were eleven letters. They were written in a fine, slanted hand that grew progressively less steady as the years advanced, and they were all addressed the same way:

*To my son, whoever he has become.*

The first was dated barely a month after Isandro's arrival in the valley—he knew this because Odile had once mentioned the year of the great silence, and the dates matched—and it began:

*I do not know if these will ever reach you. I do not know if there is a you left to reach. They tell me the ritual worked, and the word "worked" makes me want to break things. My sons are alive. That is what they say when they mean: we erased them.*

*You were born seven minutes before your brother. Seven minutes. Your father used to joke that you would spend your whole life waiting for Aurelian to catch up, and now I sit in a palace where no one will say either of your names, and I think: seven minutes. That is all the universe needed to make one of you the king and one of you the price.*

Isandro stopped. Read it again. *Your brother.* The words sat on the page like stones.

He had known, of course. Vess had told him—two princes born the same night, one bound to the throne, one bound to the valley. Sera had said it more gently. But knowing a fact and reading your mother's handwriting saying *your brother* were different animals entirely, and the second one had teeth.

He read on.

*You and Aurelian shared everything. A cradle, until you were two and nearly smothered each other reaching for the same toy. A language—I never taught it to you; you invented it between yourselves, that clicking, laughing speech no tutor could decipher. When one of you cried at night, the other woke, even across the nursery. The physicians called it twin-sense. I called it proof that souls can be split and still know each other.*

*When they came to take you both away to be bound, you reached for each other. Do you understand? They had to pry your hands apart. You were four years old, and you already knew, some animal part of you knew, that whatever they were doing would take him from you, and you fought them like a storm fights a seawall.*

*And then it was done, and you looked at me—you looked straight at me, my darling, my heart—and you did not know me. Four years old, and your eyes were a stranger's eyes, and I have lived every day since in the country of that look.*

The lamplight flickered. Or perhaps it was Isandro's hands shaking. He set the letter down and pressed the heels of his palms against his eyes and breathed, the way Sera had taught him to breathe before calling weather—in through the grief, out through the grief—until he could pick the pages up again.

* * *

The middle letters were harder, because they were happier, and happiness written by someone mourning is worse than grief.

*Today I watched Aurelian hold court for the first time. He is twelve and sits the throne like a coat that doesn't fit yet, and when the ambassadors bowed he glanced sideways—to the empty space beside the throne, to the place where you should have been standing—and for half a heartbeat something moved behind his eyes. Then it was gone. The binding holds. Sometimes I hate how well it holds.*

*He still reaches for you in his sleep. The nurses tell me he talks in a language no one recognizes. Clicking, laughing. I sat outside his door last night and listened and I would have given anything—anything—to be able to answer him in it.*

Isandro's chest ached in a way that had nothing to do with the wound at his shoulder, healing now but stiff. He thought of the dream that had haunted his first years in Stillwater: a clicking, laughing sound, a small hand gripping his, a voice saying *don't let go* in words that weren't words. He had assumed it was a dream. He had built his whole careful, quiet life on the assumption that the hollow feeling in him was just exile, just loss of magic, just the ordinary homesickness of a man without a past.

But it wasn't a home he'd been missing. It was a person.

*The Regent's men searched my chambers again today. They are looking for letters. So I write these anyway, because a queen who cannot protect her sons can at least refuse to forget them, and forgetting is the one weapon they have that I will not surrender.*

*The bargain was mine. I want you to know that, wherever you are, whoever you've become. They came to me with a choice: both sons die tonight, or the binding proceeds and one lives hidden and one lives caged. I asked what the binding would cost. They lied to me—they said memories fade naturally, gently, like a scar fading. They did not tell me it would take everything. If I had known—if I had known what it would feel like to look at my own child and see a stranger—*

*No. I would have done it anyway. Forgive me. I would have done it anyway, because dead is dead and forgotten is only almost, and I chose almost. God forgive me, I chose almost.*

* * *

The last letter was different. Shorter. The handwriting had deteriorated badly, and there were water stains on the page that Isandro chose, deliberately, not to interpret.

*My son,*

*They are coming for me soon. I have known for months; a queen learns to read the weather in a court the way your people read it in clouds. I have made arrangements. The letters go to the coast, to a man I trust with the only thing I have left to trust. The locket goes with them—it was yours and Aurelian's, split down the middle the day you were born, each half holding the other's portrait. The portraits are gone now. Burned, on orders. But the lockets remain, and I am told the metal remembers what was pressed into it, if one knows how to listen. Perhaps a storm-caller knows how to listen.*

*So here is what I need you to understand, the whole of it, plainly, because I will not get another chance:*

*The binding was meant to hide you both. One son behind the veil of the valley, one son behind the veil of the crown. Two halves of one protection. But bindings are doors, and doors open from both sides, and something on the other side noticed. Something hungry. The priests who performed the rite are all dead now—all of them, every one, within a year—and I believe the ritual did not merely hide my sons. It opened a wound, and the wound has been wearing the crown ever since, and the thing that wears my son wears him the way a glove wears a hand.*

*Aurelian is still in there. I have seen him—twice, in fifteen years, twice I have seen my elder-by-seven-minutes son surface like a drowning man breaking water, and both times he looked at me with recognition and terror, and both times it lasted less than a minute before the other thing pulled him under. He is in there. Whatever else is true, however deep, he is in there.*

*And you—my darling, my seven-minutes-elder, my storm-child—you are free. That was the other half of the bargain, the part I paid for with everything: you got to live a life. Small, perhaps. Quiet, perhaps. But yours. Do not let anyone—not a god, not a priestess, not a ghost of your mother—tell you that you owe that life back. If saving your brother costs you the self you have become, then the binding wins twice, and I will haunt every one of you.*

*Live, Isandro. I will risk the name once, at the end, because a mother should get to say it: live, Isandro. Not for the crown. Not for the blood. Live for the ferryman's daughter whose name I hope you know, for the village that fed you, for the man you became in the quiet. That man is not the price of the ritual. That man is the reason it was worth doing.*

*All my love, all my love, all my love,*
*Mother*

Isandro read the signature three times. Then he read the whole letter again, slowly, and when he finished the second reading he found that his face was wet and he had no memory of starting to cry.

* * *

He wept the way storms come in off the sea at that latitude—not all at once but in waves, each one seeming like the last until the next arrived. He wept for the woman who had died alone in this room listening to the sea, carrying the guilt of a choice that had saved everyone and cost everything. He wept for the four-year-old whose hands had been pried apart. He wept for the boy in the palace who still talked in his sleep in a language only one other person in the world had ever spoken, and who would not remember him, would never remember him, because remembering was the one thing the binding had made impossible.

And underneath all of it, quieter and crueler than the rest, he wept for the life in the letters. The shared cradle. The invented language. The empty space beside the throne where a twelve-year-old king had glanced sideways, looking for a brother he couldn't name. Fifteen years of birthdays in Stillwater, fifteen years of mending nets and hauling the ferry line and learning the river's moods—and every single day of it had been happening at the same time as another life, a parallel life, a life that should have been braided together with his and instead had been cut clean at the root.

He mourned the unlived life the way you mourn a drowned stranger whose face you suddenly recognize as your own.

Aldous found him near dawn, still in the chair, the letters spread around him like fallen leaves. The old man said nothing. He built up the fire, set a cup of something hot in Isandro's hands, and lowered himself into the opposite chair with the sigh of a man whose joints had opinions.

"She was right, you know," Aldous said eventually. "About the locket."

Isandro looked down. At some point he had picked it up—the two halves of white gold, cool against his palm. He hadn't consciously tried anything. But when he held them now, he could feel it: a faint resonance, like a struck bell heard through a wall. Metal remembering. Pressure and heat and the tiny weight of two infant heads bent close together over a painter's table, and something else, older, fainter—a clicking, laughing sound pressed into gold like a fossil into stone.

"I can hear it," Isandro said. His voice was wrecked. "Barely. It's like—like a voice through water."

"Then it can be heard fully, given time and skill." Aldous turned his blind face toward the window, where the sky was going gray at the edges. "Which brings me to the harder conversation, boy, and I'll not soften it: the sky over the capital has been wrong for three days. Wrong the way it was wrong nine minutes fifteen years ago. Something big is gathering there, and it isn't weather. Whatever your mother wrote about—the wound, the thing wearing the glove—it's getting nervous. Things that wear crowns get nervous when storm-callers start walking toward them."

Isandro closed his fist around the lockets. Through the wall of his exhaustion and grief, something else was rising—something with structure, with direction. His mother had told him to live. She had also, in the same breath, told him the truth about the wound in the world, and she had not for one moment believed those two instructions contradicted each other.

"My brother is in there," Isandro said. "She saw him. Twice. He surfaces."

"He surfaces," Aldous agreed.

"Then I'm going to Ashkarat."

The old man nodded slowly, unsurprised, and Isandro realized he had probably been preparing for this sentence for fifteen years too.

"Then tomorrow we begin the real lessons," Aldous said. "Because the storm over Ashkarat is the largest of the age, and you will not negotiate with it as a frightened man. You'll negotiate as her son." He rose, joints cracking, and paused at the stair. "One more thing. The letters—keep them. But the last one, the short one? Read it once more before you sleep. There's a line in it most men would skim past. She had a reason for putting it there."

After he had gone, Isandro unfolded the final letter again, eyes gritty, and read it line by line, hunting.

*Bindings are doors, and doors open from both sides.*

He read it a third time. A fourth. And there—at the end, tucked into the closing blessing, so small he had missed it twice—was a single sentence that had not sounded like the others:

*And a door held by two hands opens differently than a door held by one.*

He stared at it until the lamplight guttered, and somewhere far off across the dark water, thunder rolled—distant, patient, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

Two hands. He didn't know yet what it meant. But his mother had buried it in a blessing the way smugglers bury messages in grain shipments, and mothers, Isandro was learning, did not waste their dying words.

Outside, the sea whispered against the rocks, and the storm over the distant capital gathered, and Isandro sat holding his brother's half of a broken locket, memorizing the shape of a door.
