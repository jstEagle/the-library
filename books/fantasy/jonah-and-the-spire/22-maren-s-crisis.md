## 22. Maren's Crisis

The interrogation transcripts arrived at dawn, carried by a courier whose hands shook so badly he spilled wax on Maren's desk.

She noticed the shaking. She noticed everything now; it was a habit she could no longer switch off, the way a woman who had once lived beside a river could not stop listening for water. The courier was young, gray-liveried, and terrified—not of her, precisely, but of what he carried. She had seen that look before, in the weeks since Corvane, spreading through the Inquisition's lower ranks like damp through plaster. Something had happened in that rain-soaked city. Something had come out of the sky.

"Leave them," she said, and the boy fled as if released from a snare.

Maren poured herself tea that had gone cold in the pot and did not bother to heat it. Cold tea kept the mind sharp; hot tea invited comfort, and comfort invited error. She had learned that lesson at fourteen, in the cloister-schools of the Order of the Iron Crown, where the sisters taught that conviction was a blade and doubt its rust. She had believed it then with the whole clean weight of her heart. She had built her life on it. Fifteen years of service, three hundred god-touched registered, forty-seven taken into custody, and not one night's dream she could not explain to herself by morning.

She unrolled the first transcript and began to read.

* * *

The prisoner—Sera Vane, formerly Sister Sera of the Temple of the Ninefold Dawn, defrocked, branded, listed—had been in custody six days. Six days, and the transcript ran to ninety pages.

Maren read it the way she read everything: twice, once for content and once for what was absent. The content was familiar enough. The priestess was defiant in the way educated believers were defiant, quoting scripture back at her interrogators, reframing her captivity as martyrdom, wearing her brand like a medal. Maren had broken tougher subjects than this. What unsettled her was not the priestess's resistance.

It was the questions the priestess asked.

Page thirty-one: *"You keep asking me about the storm-caller. But you never ask me about the king. Why is that? If your Regent serves the crown, why does no one in this building say the king's name?"*

Page fifty-four: *"Your own clerks flinch when I mention him. I've watched them through the grate. What are you all so afraid of remembering?"*

Page seventy-eight, the last exchange of the sixth day: *"Inquisitor-General. You've read the old rites. You know what a binding looks like on paper. So tell me—when your Regent gives you orders about the storm-caller, does he tell you why? Or does he only tell you to be afraid?"*

Maren set down the page.

The lamp guttered. Outside her window, Ashkarat lay under curfew, its ten thousand lamps reduced to a few hundred permitted ones, the city holding its breath the way it had held it for fifteen years—though until recently, Maren had thought the breath was peace.

She read the transcript again, the second reading, hunting absences. And they were there, plain as watermark once you knew to look. Her own subordinates' questions grew shorter as the days passed. By day four they were asking almost nothing. Not because the prisoner had exhausted them—her file showed her sharp as ever—but because they did not want to hear the answers. Because somewhere around day three, the questions themselves had begun to feel dangerous.

Questions about the king felt dangerous.

Maren sat very still in the gray light and made herself finish the thought, because a blade that cannot be drawn in daylight will cut you in the dark.

The Regent's standing orders regarding the storm-caller were precise to the point of strangeness. *Take him alive. Do not harm him beyond necessity. Do not let him speak publicly. Do not let him be seen by crowds.* All standard. But there was a second clause, sealed, delivered by the Regent's own chamberlain, that Maren alone among the Inquisitor-Generals had been permitted to read: *If capture proves impossible, deny his existence. No proclamations. No wanted bills bearing his description. The cities are to learn of him only by rumor.*

She had signed off on it without thinking. Denying an enemy publicity was sound strategy; panic was a weapon anyone could pick up. But now, turning the clause over in the cold morning light, she heard what was actually inside it.

They were not hiding the storm-caller from the people.

They were hiding him from the idea of him. From the memory of what a storm-caller was. From the fifteen-year-old silence that the whole kingdom had agreed not to examine—the silence where the gods used to answer, the silence where, if you dug down far enough, other silences lay stacked beneath it like coffins.

And beneath even those, one more silence, that no one in the palace ever named.

The king's.

* * *

She went to the archive that afternoon, which required no permission because she was the third-highest authority in the Inquisition and the archive answered to her. It occupied three floors beneath the Hall of Registers, and it smelled of dust and tallow and the particular sourness of paper that had been locked away from air for a long time.

Fifteen years ago, Maren had been a junior inquisitor newly sworn, and she remembered—she had always prided herself on remembering—the week the gods went quiet. She had been stationed in Delmare then, cataloguing shrine-seals. One morning the seals simply stopped warming. Prayers stopped arriving. The great machinery of divine response, which had turned for a thousand years as reliably as tide, halted between one hour and the next, and the Order had declared it a Purification: the age of miracle ended, the age of order begun. She had helped burn temples. She had told herself every time that she was saving people from dependence on voices that no longer existed, that a god who abandons you is worse than no god, that order was mercy.

She had never once, in fifteen years, asked why the silence had fallen on a single night rather than fading like a tide going out.

Silences that fall all at once have causes. Tides have none.

The royal chronicles were kept in the deep vault, and access to the years surrounding the Silence required countersignature from the Regent's office. Maren had signed such denials herself, for lesser officers, a dozen times. She stood now before the iron door with her own seal warm in her hand, and she found that her hand hesitated—a small tremor, half a heartbeat, nothing—and that hesitation frightened her more than anything the priestess had said in ninety pages.

Because she was not afraid of being caught. She was afraid of what she would find.

A true believer fears only two things: losing her faith, and learning it was misplaced. For fifteen years Maren had assumed those were the same fear. Standing at the vault door, she understood, with the clarity of cold water, that they were entirely different—and that only one of them was dishonorable.

She signed the request. Her seal bit into the wax cleanly. The archivist, an ancient man who had served three reigns and pretended to serve none, looked at the signature a moment too long.

"The regnal years?" he asked. "Which span?"

"Fifteen years back," Maren said. "The year of the Silence. Through the present."

The old man's face did something very small. Not disapproval. Recognition, perhaps—the look of a man watching another traveler arrive at a crossroads he himself had passed long ago.

"I'll need the full chain of custody," he said mildly. "Countersigned."

"You have my signature."

"And I'll need the Regent's." He said it without malice, the way one states that water is wet. "For those years, General. Always has been. Since the week after."

"Since the week after," Maren repeated.

"Since the week after," the archivist agreed, and turned to his ledgers, and gave her nothing else, and gave her everything.

* * *

She petitioned that evening. She composed it carefully, in the formal register of the Inquisition, stripping out every word that could be called disloyal and leaving only what could be called procedure:

*The prisoner Sera Vane makes repeated reference to the person of His Majesty King Aurelian. Standard counterintelligence practice requires verification of any claim touching the sovereign's person or condition. This office therefore petitions for direct examination of the King's health and lucidity, by qualified physicians of the Order, with results to be sealed and reported solely to the Crown.*

It was impeccable. It was the kind of petition she had approved from others a hundred times. Any officer of integrity would have filed it without a second thought, and she signed it with a steady hand and sent it up through channels, and then she waited.

The reply came in nine hours. That speed itself was an answer; routine petitions took weeks.

It came not as a document but as a visit. The chamberlain arrived at her offices at the hour when the lamps were lit, with two silent attendants, and declined her offer of a chair, and stood looking at her bookshelves as though taking inventory.

"Inquisitor-General." He was a soft-spoken man, graying, meticulous, the kind of servant who had outlasted four ministers by being furniture. "Your petition has been reviewed."

"And?"

"Denied."

"On what grounds?" She kept her voice level. "The prisoner's claims touch the sovereign. Verification is elementary."

"The King's condition," the chamberlain said, "is not a matter subject to verification."

Maren heard the sentence twice—once as nonsense, and once, underneath, as exactly what it was.

"Every matter in this kingdom is subject to verification," she said. "That is the entire principle of the Iron Crown. That is what we tell the cities. That is what we *are*. Order over miracle because order can be checked, measured, confirmed. If the King's condition stands outside verification, then we have not replaced miracle. We have become it."

The chamberlain turned from the bookshelves. His face was mild and empty as a closed door, and yet for one instant—one flicker, gone as soon as it came—he looked at her with something that was almost pity.

"You have served well for fifteen years," he said. "Continue to serve well. The Regent values certainty in his officers. Certainty, General, is a discipline. Like any discipline, it requires that certain questions simply not be asked. Not forbidden—" he raised a hand, gentle, reasonable— "merely unnecessary. A good surgeon does not ask the body why it heals. She lets it heal."

"The King is not healing," Maren said quietly. "Is he."

The silence that followed lasted four seconds. She counted them, because counting was what she did instead of trembling.

"No one," the chamberlain said at last, "has claimed otherwise. Good evening, General. Your diligence is noted in your file."

He left. She stood in her office among her orderly books and her orderly reports and her fifteen years of orderly service, and she understood that she had just been weighed, and measured, and found—for the moment—still useful. And that the noting in her file cut both ways. Every officer knew what it meant when the palace began keeping a file on you with kindness in it.

* * *

She did not sleep. She told herself it was the tea, and then she stopped telling herself things, which was itself a kind of surrender—or a kind of waking; she could no longer have said which.

Instead she walked the corridors of the Hall of Registers in the dead hours, past the sleeping clerks, past the registry rooms where god-touched citizens were recorded in ledgers as calmly as grain shipments, and she let herself think the thoughts she had been refusing for six days, one after another, like drawing knives from a wound.

The Regent feared remembrance. Not rebellion—rebellion she could have understood, rebellion was weather, rebellion came and went with harvests and taxes. But the priestess had put her finger on it in a cell with a grate: the palace did not fear what the storm-caller could *do*. Armies could handle what a man could do. The palace feared what he was, and what his existence reminded people of. A living storm-caller meant the gods' silence was not natural law but event. An event has a cause. A cause has agents. And agents can be named.

And the sealed clause—deny his existence—was not strategy. It was the same gesture, repeated at scale, that someone had made fifteen years ago with the chronicles, with the regnal records, with whatever lay behind the iron door of the deep vault. Someone had erased something once, thoroughly, and the whole architecture of the last fifteen years—the Silence, the Purification, the Registry, the curfews—was not order at all.

It was maintenance.

It was a bandage, changed daily, over a wound that had never been allowed to close.

Maren stopped walking. She was standing before the portrait gallery of the Hall, where the founders of the Order gazed down in their painted severity, and beside them, in the place of honor, hung the state portrait of the Regent—painted, per protocol, in the first year of the Regency, and never renewed, because protocol also decreed that the Regent's likeness was not to be attempted again until the King's restoration to public life.

Fifteen years. No new portrait. No public appearance of the King. No physician's report. No voice. No name spoken in the palace above a whisper. The throne occupied by a Regent who ruled in the King's name and forbade, by sealed order, any inquiry into the King's condition—and the Inquisition, her Inquisition, the blade of order and verification, had enforced that prohibition for fifteen years without one officer, one clerk, one archivist asking aloud the question a defrocked heretic had asked through a cell grate on her fourth day of captivity.

*What are you all so afraid of remembering?*

Maren looked up at the Regent's painted face—young then, handsome then, painted with the flat confidence of official art—and she saw it now, the way one sees a figure hidden in a pattern once someone points: the eyes. Painted eyes that did not look at the viewer. Painted eyes that looked slightly past, slightly down, the way a man looks when he is listening to something behind the wall.

Or wearing it.

The word arrived in her mind uninvited, from some deep shelf of her childhood faith that the Order had never quite managed to burn: *god-eater*. The old stories, the ones the Purification had classified as superstition—the hunger that wore kings, the silence that fed. She had burned books containing that story. She remembered the smell of them.

Her whole life, she had believed she served the wall between order and chaos. Standing in the dark gallery, she felt the truth move through her like a change in pressure before a storm: she had served the thing that needed the wall. The chaos was not outside it. The chaos paid for it. The chaos gave the orders.

And the King—

The King might be alive. Might be in the palace tonight, behind doors no one verified, in a condition no one examined, wearing—or worn by—whatever had silenced the gods in a single night fifteen years ago while a junior inquisitor in Delmare catalogued shrine-seals that had gone cold.

Maren pressed her palm flat against the cool stone wall and breathed, once, twice, the way the sisters had taught her to breathe before a hard interrogation. Steadying herself. Preparing herself.

Except that for the first time in her adult life, she could not tell which side of the interrogation she was on.

* * *

Dawn found her at her desk, and the courier found her there—an older one this time, trusted, discreet—with a single sealed letter from the Regent's office. She broke it expecting reprimand, transfer, disgrace.

It was neither. It was orders. New orders, urgent, marked with the chamberlain's seal rather than the Regent's, which was itself irregular, and she read them twice with a stillness that would have frightened her clerks had any of them dared look.

*The prisoner Sera Vane is to be transferred from the Hall of Registers to the palace undercroft at the next change of watch. Escort detail minimal. Documentation of transfer to be destroyed upon completion. Inquisitor-General Maren will personally oversee the transfer and will make no further record of the prisoner thereafter.*

No charge. No trial conclusion. No destination stated beyond *the palace undercroft*, which held no cells, no courtrooms, no lawful function whatsoever except the one everyone knew and no one wrote down.

They were going to disappear the priestess. Quietly. The way, she now understood, other things had been disappeared—names from pages, years from archives, a king from his own kingdom.

Maren read the order a third time. Then she took a blank sheet of the Inquisition's own paper, and in her precise, unimprovable hand, she copied the order out word for word, including the instruction to destroy documentation, and she folded the copy into the false spine of her personal breviary, where she kept the only two things she had ever taken from a burning temple: a psalm-page, and a child's drawing of a god with a candle's face.

Then she sat back and looked at the original, and at the wax seal, and at the fifteen years of her life arranged around it like iron filings around a lodestone, all pointing one way, all of it suddenly, visibly, arranged.

A true believer beginning to see does not stop believing, Maren thought. She changes what she believes in. It is the most dangerous thing that can happen to her—and to whoever taught her to believe wrongly.

Outside, far to the west, over the hills beyond Ashkarat, thunder rolled. The season's first real storm, the clerks would call it, and mark it in the weather ledger, and think nothing of it.

Maren stood at the window and watched the lightning walk the horizon, and thought: *he is coming here. Whatever he is, whoever he is—he is coming to the palace, and the palace knows it, and that is why the priestess must vanish tonight.*

She looked at the transfer order in her hand.

The change of watch was at midnight. She had eighteen hours to decide what an Inquisitor-General of the Iron Crown did with an unlawful order—and whether, after fifteen years, she was still the crown's blade, or something sharper, and truer, and hers.
