## 23. March of the Iron Crown

The road out of Delmare had never been so wide.

It was wide with people. Ten thousand of them, twenty—no one counted anymore—flowing east along the pilgrim road in a river of carts and children and bleating goats, all of them fleeing the same thing: a dust cloud on the western horizon that did not move like weather. It moved like intention.

Isandro stood at the roadside and watched it come.

* * *

Maren rode at the head of the vanguard, and the vanguard rode through mud.

She had not wanted to look at the mud. Mud meant rain, and rain meant the sky had opinions again, and she had spent her whole career believing the sky had none—that the world was a ledger, that order was arithmetic, that every miracle was simply an equation no one had bothered to solve. Fifteen years ago the gods had gone silent and the ledger had balanced, and Maren had been one of the clerks who made it balance. She had registered god-touched children. She had burned temples whose only crime was empty pews. She had done it cleanly, without cruelty, because cruelty was inefficiency, and she had slept well.

Now her horse sank to the fetlock in a puddle that reflected a bruise-colored sky, and somewhere behind her, thirty thousand soldiers sang a marching song about the Iron Crown, and somewhere ahead of them the free cities were building walls out of fishing boats and desperation, and Maren found herself doing something she had not done since she was a girl in her grandmother's kitchen.

She was listening for thunder.

"General." A courier reined up beside her, breathless, holding out a dispatch tube sealed with black wax. "From the Regent's own hand."

She cracked the seal. The Regent's handwriting had changed lately. It used to be precise, small, economical—a clerk's hand, an accountant's hand. Now the letters leaned. Now they pressed so hard into the paper that some of them tore through. *Take the storm-caller alive,* the dispatch read, for the fourth time. *Alive. Alive. Alive.* And beneath it, in a different pressure of ink, as if written by someone else wearing the same hand: *Do not let him reach Ashkarat.*

"Do not let him reach Ashkarat," Maren said aloud, testing the words.

"General?"

"Nothing." She rolled the dispatch closed. Alive, but do not let him arrive. Bring me the man, but keep him from the place where the man might matter. Her superiors had issued contradictory orders before; bureaucracies breathed contradictions. But this one had a smell to it, a wet-rot smell, like fruit going bad from the inside.

She looked west, where the storm-caller was supposed to be, and east, where the capital waited under its gilded curfew, and she thought: *I am being marched between two sentences that cannot both be true.*

"Column will make the Saltmarsh crossroads by nightfall," she said. "Double the outriders. If anyone sees weather that shouldn't be there, I want to know before the weather knows we've seen it."

The courier saluted and galloped off. Maren sat in the mud-smell and the singing and listened, very carefully, to the sky.

It said nothing.

That frightened her more than thunder would have.

* * *

Three miles ahead of the army, on the same road, a woman named Odessa carried her grandmother in a wheelbarrow.

The grandmother was ninety-one and weighed less than the wheelbarrow. She had been born in a village that no longer existed, married in a temple that was now a tax office, and buried two sons in wars fought over crowns that were not theirs. She had refused to leave when the first riders came through shouting about evacuation. She had refused to leave when the second riders came through shooting. She left now because her granddaughter had stopped asking and started loading.

"They'll burn Delmare," Odessa said, pushing. The wheel squealed on every rotation, a small dying animal of a sound. "They always burn the cities that say no."

"The cities that say yes get eaten slower," her grandmother said. "Same ending. Better table manners."

Around them the road flowed: a tinker with his cart full of dented pots that chimed like a mad belltower; twin boys driving geese with sticks, solemn as priests; a priestess of no particular god walking barefoot because she'd given her shoes to a pregnant woman three miles back and hadn't told anyone. Nobody spoke much. When ten thousand people are afraid in the same direction, silence is the only language everyone shares.

Odessa's grandmother turned her head against the wheelbarrow's slats and looked back, the way old people do, at the thing coming for all of them.

"I remember the last time the gods went quiet," she said. "Everyone prayed harder. That was the mistake. You can't shout at a deaf house." She paused. "This time I hear they're answering."

"Grandmother, please."

"I'm just saying. If a god finally picks up, somebody ought to be polite enough to still be listening." She settled back. "Push straighter. You're veering into the ditch."

* * *

In Delmare, the siege works rose like coral.

The city had no walls worth the name—it had never needed them; its defense had always been the sea and the council's talent for being useful to everyone. Now carpenters swarmed over the harbor chain towers, welding iron links the size of a man's torso. Fishermen dragged their hulls ashore and sank them in the shallows to break landing barges. The glassblowers' guild had produced four hundred bottles of burning oil in six days, and the smell of pitch hung over the terraces like a second fog.

Councilor Brannoc stood on the Tide Gate and watched a crane lower a scorpion bolt thrower onto the eastern bastion, and felt the specific despair of a merchant watching his warehouse become a fortress.

"We have grain for forty days," said Councilor Imre, who had brought the figures because Imre always brought the figures. "Fifty if we ration hard. The fleet can evacuate perhaps eight thousand before the harbor closes."

"And how many are left after eight thousand?" Brannoc asked.

Imre didn't answer. That was the answer.

Below them, on the parade sand that had once hosted the spring regatta, a hundred volunteers drilled with pikes. Most of them were fishermen. Some were children dressed in their dead fathers' gambesons. An old sergeant walked the lines correcting grips, and his voice carried up to the wall, patient and terrible: *"Point it at the horse, not the man. The man has a mother. The horse doesn't care about you either way."*

"You still believe it," Imre said quietly. "That the king lives. That he can be freed. That one man and one defrocked priestess and—" he searched for the word, "—one *weather event* can undo fifteen years."

Brannoc watched the volunteers drill. He watched a boy of maybe twelve drop his pike, pick it up, and set his feet again.

"I believe the oath," Brannoc said. "Storm-oath. I watched a man bind his life to a promise with lightning in his eyes, and I have been a merchant long enough to know that everything else in this world can be forged except that."

* * *

They traveled at night and slept in ditches, and Isandro learned the shape of exhaustion.

It had a geography. There was the flat plain of the first day, when tiredness was just weight. Then the hills, where the body began negotiating—*we can go another mile if you stop thinking*. Then the cliffs, where thought itself eroded and only motion remained, one foot and then the other foot, the road unspooling beneath them like thread from a spindle that would eventually run out.

Sera kept pace beside him. She had stopped talking somewhere around dawn of the second day, which frightened him more than anything the usurper could send, because Sera not talking meant Sera calculating, and Sera calculating meant the numbers had gone bad.

At dusk on the third night they crested a low ridge and saw the Saltmarsh road below them, and the refugee column, and beyond the column, far to the west, the dust.

Not a storm-cloud. Storms breathed. This did not breathe. It hung over the horizon like a held breath, brown and straight-edged, and Isandro understood that he was looking at thirty thousand men and the supply train of an empire that intended to erase the word *free* from the free cities.

"How far?" Sera asked. Her voice was gravel.

"Four days to Ashkarat if we push. The army reaches Delmare in five. Maybe six." He did the arithmetic he'd been refusing to do. "We beat them by a day. One day. And then we have to get inside the most guarded palace in Vhalen, find a binding chamber neither of us has ever seen, and perform a rite that requires—" he stopped.

"Say it," Sera said. "Requires your blood and your voice, while you're also on a spire keeping the largest storm of the age off our backs." She rubbed her face with both hands. "And the price, which you still won't—"

"Sera."

"No. All right." She dropped her hands. In the last light her eyes were very steady. "But understand what I'm doing, Isandro. Every hour I don't ask, I'm spending trust I can't earn back. Keep the ledger."

They went down into the marsh road, into the flow of the displaced, and became two more faces in a crowd that smelled of woodsmoke and fear.

A child ran past them chasing a hoop, laughing, and Isandro flinched—not at the child, but at the sound. Laughter sounded wrong out here, like a bird singing in a mine. Then he caught himself and almost smiled, because the child was wearing a little wooden pendant of a candle flame, carved crude and lopsided, and around them, he realized, he had been seeing them for miles without seeing them: candle-flame charms on wrists and cart-yokes and goat collars, hundreds of them, whittled by hand along the road.

The refugees were praying to Vess. Not in words. In wood.

"He's spreading," Sera murmured, following his gaze. "The little god. Gods do that when they're dying—they scatter. Seeds." She touched the charm on a passing cart, briefly, like touching a wound. "Or maybe when they're being born. I honestly don't know anymore."

* * *

Vess knew.

Vess knew the way a candle knows the draft—by flickering. The god was smaller now than it had been at the ferry, smaller than the chapel in Corvane, smaller even than the moment it had burned itself nearly to nothing at the Tide Gate. What remained of it rode in the hollow of Isandro's pack, wrapped in a scrap of altar cloth, and it was less a presence than a warmth, less a voice than a pressure against the inside of his skull, like a word waiting to be remembered.

But it knew.

It felt the army the way a field feels locusts. Thirty thousand hearts beating in rough synchrony, drummed into step, and threaded through them—this was the part that hurt—the residue of devoured gods. The parasite did not march. It did not need to. Its appetite soaked into the Regent's banners, into the officers' oaths, into the marching song about the Iron Crown, and everywhere the army passed, Vess felt small holy things go out like lamps: a blessed spring turning brackish, a healing shrine forgetting how to heal, a village's guardian spirit, weak and half-starved already, simply ceasing.

*He is coming to eat the free cities,* Vess thought, in the wordless way gods think. *And after the cities, the sea. And after the sea—*

It pressed against the cloth and sent what it had left up into Isandro's sleeping mind: not words, just urgency, just the image of a knot and a knife and a door closing.

Isandro woke in the dark with his heart slamming and Sera's hand already on his shoulder.

"Dream?"

"Reminder," he said.

* * *

On the fourth night, the sky finally broke.

It had been gathering for days behind the eastern peaks—a vast, patient accumulation, the century-storm assembling itself the way a cathedral assembles, stone by stone—and when it came it came all at once, and the whole refugee column stopped to watch lightning walk the mountains like something enormous waking up.

Isandro stood in the rain and felt it call his name.

Not Vess's name for him. Not the erased prince's name. His name—the storm's own word for what he was, a sound like a hinge, like a door, like *come*. Fifteen years of Stillness had taught him to dread that call. Three weeks of running had taught him to fear it. But standing here in the downpour with an army at his back and a brother in chains ahead of him and one day of margin between salvation and nothing, Isandro Almeida discovered that fear and readiness can wear the same face.

"It's early," Sera shouted over the rain, beside him, soaked through, not afraid—or afraid, but standing in it anyway. "The century-storm. The hermit said it wouldn't break for another week. Why is it early?"

Isandro watched the lightning. It wasn't random. It was falling in patterns—in arcs, in spirals, in shapes too deliberate for weather. The storm was not breaking. It was *arranging* itself. The way troops arrange. The way a blade arranges itself in a hand.

"Because it knows," he said. "Whatever's wearing my brother's crown—it ate the silence of the old gods. This storm is what the sky does instead of gods. They've been enemies for fifteen years, and the parasite can feel it coming the way Maren's dogs feel an earthquake." He turned to her, and rain ran off his chin, and for the first time since the valley he did not look like a man running. "It's not racing us to Ashkarat, Sera. We're all racing each other to the same room. The army, the storm, us, the thing on the throne. Everything converges."

Sera looked at the arranged lightning, and at the brown line of the army behind them, and at the dark road ahead where the capital lay glittering under curfew like a jewel in a closed fist.

"One day," she said.

"One day."

They walked on. Behind them the thunder rolled east, patient and enormous, keeping pace.

And in Ashkarat, in a throne room lit by a thousand candles that never guttered, something ancient lifted its head from the body it wore and tasted the wind off the mountains, and smiled with the king's mouth, and said to the empty hall, in a voice like silk drawn over teeth:

"Soon."
