## 24. The God's Last Ember

Gods do not sleep. This is the first truth every god learns and the last one it forgets: there is no dark behind the eyes to rest in, no border between hours where a small god might set down its weight. Vess had been awake for eleven thousand years, and for most of them it had been awake *enough*—fed on candle-smoke and whispered petitions, on the particular sweetness of a child's prayer said wrong on purpose because the child liked the sound of its own voice. A god of thresholds and small mercies, of doorways and the last light left burning for someone coming home late.

Now Vess was awake the way a dying man is awake: entirely, and against his will.

It crouched in the hollow beneath the riverbank willow, three days downriver from Delmare, and took inventory of itself the way a miser counts coins he knows will not last the winter. Once it had been a flicker the size of a candle-flame and that had been wealth enough. Then, in Corvane, in the drowned chapel under the curfew-dark streets, it had gathered itself from forty-seven flames at once—had poured through wax and wick and oil-lamp until it stood tall as a child again, bright enough to speak a mission into being. That spending had been necessary. Necessary things are how gods die.

What remained of Vess now would not have filled a thimble. It glowed like the last coal in a banked fire, orange fading to grey at its edges, and when it moved—and movement cost it; everything cost it now—it shed embers that did not rise but fell, drifting down into the mud like snow that had given up on heaven.

*Isandro,* it thought, and the name alone was an expenditure. *Where is Isandro.*

Not far. It could feel him the way it felt all its faithful—not by sight but by the small tug of attention, the thread of a mind turned toward it in hope or need. Isandro's thread was the strongest Vess had ever held. It was also, Vess understood with a clarity that only the nearly-dead achieve, the thread that was going to kill it.

*Good,* Vess thought, and meant it, and the meaning cost it another ember.

* * *

They came to the willow at dusk, as Vess had asked—or rather, as Vess had begged, in a dream so thin Sera would later say she couldn't tell if she'd dreamed it or invented it. The river ran low and brown-gold with the season's first leaf-fall. Isandro came first, always first now, moving with the careful weight of a man who had learned in the last month that his body was a weapon and had not yet made peace with it. Behind him came Sera, hood back, her defrocked priestess's brand visible at her wrist—the old gods' spiral, cut deep enough to scar, the mark her own order had given her before they burned her temple and called it doctrine.

Vess loved them both. This was not sentiment; gods love the way rivers run, structurally, without deciding to. But it watched them come down the bank—watched Isandro scan the trees twice before allowing Sera to stop walking, watched Sera check the sky for weather that wasn't there yet—and it felt the specific grief of a thing that has read the last page of a book its friends have only begun.

"You're smaller," Isandro said.

Of all the things he might have said. Not *are you well*, which gods cannot be, nor *what happened to you*, which Vess could not answer honestly without lying about other things. He looked at what remained of a god older than his people's oldest city and measured it against what it had been, and grieved the difference out loud, plainly, the way he did everything now—as if plainness were a discipline he'd taken up late in life and practiced daily.

"I am concentrated," Vess said. Its voice came out like paper turning over. "All of me that is left is in less space. You should be flattered."

Sera knelt in the mud without hesitation, without checking whether kneeling would cost her dignity among the refugees upriver. She had been doing that lately—spending herself the way Vess spent itself, and calling it faith. "You shouldn't have called us here if this is what's left of you. Rest. We can find you after Ashkarat."

"There is no *after* for me, priestess." Vess let a little of the old bell-tone into its voice, the tone that had once filled temples, and immediately regretted the extravagance—a whole ember gone for rhetoric. "There is only what I carry, and whether I give it to you before I gutter or after. After is worse. After, it burns with me and no one reads it." It turned its failing light toward Isandro. "Come closer. Closer than courtesy allows. What I have to say is for one ear, and even then I would prefer it were not heard by the air."

He came. He knelt too, mirroring Sera, and Vess saw him glance at her and then deliberately angle his body between her and the ember of godlight—a small motion, unconscious, protective. Vess had watched mortals for eleven thousand years. It knew concealment when it saw it, even concealment not yet fully formed. Even concealment still lying to itself.

*Oh,* Vess thought. *He already suspects.*

That changed nothing. But it made the giving easier, somehow, the way a confession is easier to make to someone who has already guessed.

"The binding chamber," Vess said, "beneath the throne room of Ashkarat, is real. I told you this in Corvane and you believed me because believing me was cheaper than the alternative. Now I will tell you the rest, and belief will cost you more."

"Tell me."

"Fifteen years ago, two princes were born in one hour of one night. The ritual that hid them did not hide one boy. It hid two. One was bound to the valley—stillness, silence, a life with the storm scraped out of him. The other was bound to the throne—to the crown itself, so that whatever wore the crown would wear *him*, and anything that came for the crown would find a king already inside it, locked, defended by his own absence." Vess felt its edges flutter. It steadied itself. Honesty, for a god, is a physical act. "The binding was made by your mother. Queen Liora. With blood, and voice, and the help of four gods who are no longer with us because the thing in the palace ate them. I am the fifth. I ran. I have been running for fifteen years, and I am telling you this now because I have almost stopped being able to run."

Isandro's face did what faces do when they receive news they had already built a room for. Nothing collapsed. Something settled.

"And the words," he said quietly. "The unbinding. You said in Corvane you carried them."

"I carry them. They are the last of my hoard, storm-caller, and they are heavier than gold." Vess drew itself up—all of itself, the whole thimbleful—and began, in the old tongue, the tongue that predated the valley and the usurper and the very concept of crowns, to speak.

The words of unbinding are not long. That is the horror of them. Mortals imagine such things must be vast, libraries of syllable, towers of incantation. They are nine lines. Nine lines, each one a hinge, each one requiring the true heir's blood opened onto stone and the true heir's voice speaking the line whole—no stammer, no doubt, because the binding listens for doubt the way a lock listens for the wrong key. Vess spoke them slowly, folding each line into Isandro's memory the way one presses a flower into a book, and watched him go white, and watched him *hold*, and thought: *there is the spine Liora trusted. There is why she chose this family.*

When the ninth line settled, Isandro sat back on his heels and breathed like a man who had been underwater.

"That's all?" he managed.

"All. Blood and voice and the knot cut while the storm holds the parasite's attention. Sera can conduct it—I have seen her work; she remembers more of the old rites than her order ever knew she remembered. Maren can hold the door, if she is truly ours. You can hold the sky." Vess paused. Ember-light is not good for nuance, but it tried anyway. "And the price—"

"Yes." Too fast. Too smooth. "The price. You mentioned a price in Corvane. The essence you gave me—" He stopped himself, recalibrated, and Vess watched him do it, watched the lie assemble itself with the same competence he brought to storms. "The price. Tell me the price."

And here was the moment. Here was the fork in the road, and Vess, who had lied to kings and comforted thieves and once talked a plague out of a city by promising it a better host, found that it could not do the small easy thing. Perhaps because it was dying. Dying things lose their talent for the long game; everything in them turns toward the true.

"The unbinding returns what the binding took," Vess said. "All of it. From everyone it touched. Aurelian will wake with fifteen years stolen from him—every memory the parasite suppressed will come home, and they will arrive *at once*, and he will have perhaps ninety seconds of being himself before he drowns in his own returned life. That is survivable. Kings have survived worse mornings." It let its light dim deliberately, drawing the moment taut. "But the rite requires a conductor who stands inside the circle. And the circle returns what the binding took *from the conductor as well*. Whatever the binding removed from the one who speaks the ninth line—that comes back. Into them. At the moment of cutting."

Sera, ten feet away, had gone very still. She was pretending to keep watch on the river. She was listening with her whole body. Vess gave her nothing to hear—dropped its voice into the register below human speech, the register of prayer, private as a heartbeat.

"What did the binding take from me?" Isandro whispered.

"I don't know," Vess said, and it was the truth, and the truth was unbearable, so it kept talking. "Your mother split the working. Half the theft went into the valley's making, half into the crown's. What was taken from the son in the valley may not match what was taken from the son on the throne. But hear me, Isandro Almeida, hear me with the ears you use for thunder: whoever cuts that knot gets back everything the binding stole from them. Every memory. Every year. Every name. Fifteen years ago, someone paid to have certain things forgotten. The rite does not care who paid or why. The rite *returns*. And what returns may be the reason you were hidden in the first place."

The river made its small sounds. Somewhere upbank, a nightjar called twice and thought better of it.

"If I cut the knot," Isandro said, very evenly, "I get back whatever I was before Stillwater."

"Or Sera cuts it, and gets back whatever *she* lost—which may be little, or may be the faith she was born with, returned whole and undiluted into a woman who has learned to live without it. Or Maren. Or any heir's-blood hand we can find, and there are none, because the twins were the last of the line." Vess felt itself gutter, actually gutter, a full second of darkness, and fought its way back to brightness with something like anger. It was not done. Gods are not done. "I tell you this so that you choose with open eyes. I do not tell you so that you choose tonight. Sleep on it. Storm-callers are supposed to be patient; it's the one virtue your kind is granted."

Isandro was quiet for a long time. Long enough that Vess, whose perception of time had gone strange and syrupy as its essence thinned, wondered briefly whether it had blacked out and missed his answer.

Then he said: "Thank you. Truly. Go to sleep now, Vess. If gods sleep."

"We don't," Vess said. "But we pretend, sometimes, for the benefit of the living."

* * *

It watched him walk up the bank to where Sera waited, and it watched him lie to her.

There was no drama in it. That was what struck Vess, in its last clear interval of thought. He simply became, for the span of thirty heartbeats, a slightly simpler man—he reported the words of unbinding, the requirements of blood and voice, the role each of them would play, all of it true, all of it delivered in his new plainness—and he omitted exactly one thing, the way a cartographer omits a single valley, and the map still looks complete. Sera asked questions. He answered them. His answers were honest. His silence was not.

She believed him. Vess could see her believe him—not because she was fooled but because she wanted to be, because trust, once extended, is expensive to retract, and she had already spent so much of hers on this man, on this mission, on the stubborn article of faith that the gods had sent her a purpose when they had really only sent her a errand. She touched his arm. He covered her hand with his. Above them the first stars came out, indifferent and ancient, and the two of them stood in the last light of a dying god and planned a rescue, and one of them was carrying a secret shaped like a coffin.

*He means to cut the knot himself,* Vess thought. *He means to stand in the circle and speak the ninth line and take back whatever was taken from him, and he has decided that whatever it is, it is a fair price for his brother's freedom. And he has decided she must not know, because she would try to stand in his place, and he cannot bear that, and so he lies—not from cowardice, which is the usual ore of lies, but from love, which refines much worse metal.*

Vess had watched mortals for eleven thousand years. It had seen love start wars and end them, seen it build shrines and burn them, and it had never, in all that watching, quite reconciled itself to the fact that love and deception shared so much plumbing.

Should it tell her? It considered this with the slow thoroughness of the nearly extinguished. It could spend its final embers on one more act: flare bright, call her over, undo his omission in front of him. Justice, of a kind. The god's prerogative.

But it looked at him instead—at the set of his shoulders as he checked the treeline a third time, at the way he positioned himself, always, between Sera and the dark—and it thought about Queen Liora, who had been offered one son's life for the other's erasure and had refused the arithmetic entirely, splitting the binding rather than choosing, paying whatever interest the years would charge rather than sacrifice either child to the equation. Stubbornness like that runs in blood. So does its opposite.

"He will learn what was taken from him," Vess murmured to the mud, to the roots, to the darkening river. "At the moment of cutting, he will learn it, and it will be too late to refuse, and that is its own cruelty—but it will be *his* cruelty, chosen with open eyes, which is the only kind mortals get to own." Its light sank lower. "And she will forgive him. Not soon. But she will. I have seen her kind before."

* * *

Near midnight, Isandro came back alone.

Vess had banked itself down to almost nothing, conserving, and it felt him approach by the tug of his attention before it saw him—a thread pulled taut across the dark. He crouched at the edge of the hollow, and for a while neither of them spoke, and the river spoke for both of them.

"I need to ask you something," he finally said, "and I need you not to soften it."

"Softening costs energy I no longer have."

"When the memories return—to whoever cuts the knot. Does it hurt?"

Vess considered lying. It had just finished admiring his lie; it knew the craft intimately now, had watched it worked at close range. And it found, examining the option, that it had no strength left for it. Dying simplifies.

"Yes," it said. "Imagine fifteen years of yourself arriving in a single breath. Everything you were before the valley, everything the binding filed away—your mother's voice, your brother's hand in yours, whatever name you answered to before Almeida. It does not trickle back. It *floods*. Some conductors of lesser rites have survived it. Some have not. The old texts call it the drowning-in-reverse." It paused. "You should know that I do not think it will kill you. You negotiated with a century-storm on a rooftop without training. You have a talent for surviving arrivals."

He laughed—one short, cracked note, surprised out of him—and Vess thought: *there. That is the sound of a man who has already decided, hearing confirmation and finding it bearable.* Mortals did this constantly. They asked questions whose answers they needed only for courage, not for choice.

"One more thing," he said. "If you're gone—if you gutter before Ashkarat—is there anyone else who knows the rite? Anyone who could conduct it if—" He stopped. Started again, more carefully. "If I'm unable."

*Liar,* Vess thought, with enormous tenderness. *You are asking whether the secret dies with me. Whether she could ever learn what you've withheld.*

"No one else carries the words," Vess said aloud. "I am the last archive. When I go out, the rite lives only where I have planted it—in you, and in the memory of what you choose to share." It let that settle, and watched him understand it, and watched him accept the burden with a nod so small it might have been a shiver. "Guard it well, storm-caller. Words of unbinding are like embers. They want to be spent. They will whisper to you to hurry, to spend them early, to spend them on smaller fires. Do not listen. Carry them to the knot."

"I will."

"And Isandro—" The name cost it. Everything cost it now. It spent anyway. "Whatever comes back to you, in that chamber, in that moment—you will not face it alone. Even if you arrange the world so that you do. She will be standing there. I have seen her stand in worse places for less worthy men."

His jaw tightened. For a moment Vess thought he would argue, would defend the architecture of his secrecy, and it braced itself to spend its last argument on him.

Instead he reached out—one finger, careful as a man touching a moth—and steadied the trembling edge of Vess's light where it guttered against the cold air. A gesture with no theology in it at all. Just warmth offered to a small dying thing.

"Sleep, Vess," he said. "Pretend, for the benefit of the living."

And he rose and walked back up the bank toward the fire where Sera waited, and Vess watched him go, and felt the last of its inventory shift and settle, and understood suddenly—with the terrible clarity available only to things at the very end—that it had one ember left. One. Enough for a word, or a warning, or a blessing. Not enough for all three.

It spent it on none of those.

It spent it reaching, thread-thin, along the tug of Sera's sleeping mind, and planting there—not the words of unbinding, never the words, it had promised itself that much—but a single image, seed-small, patient: a circle of stone, a figure standing within it, and the figure's shadow cast long and wrong upon the wall, a shadow holding something the body did not. An image that would mean nothing to her tonight. And nothing tomorrow. But somewhere on the road to Ashkarat, when she woke from a dream of circles and shadows with the taste of a question in her mouth, she would look at Isandro across the morning fire and find herself, for no reason she could name, unable to stop wondering what he was carrying that weighed so much.

Gods do not sleep. But they leave messages for the ones who do.

The ember went out. The willow leaves came down. Upstream, a man lay awake beside a woman he loved, guarding a secret he believed was mercy, while the river carried the last light of a god away toward the sea—and ahead of them both, past the marshes and the fords and the iron march of the usurper's army, the towers of Ashkarat waited in the dark like teeth.
