## 25. Skirmish at the Ford

The river came down out of the hills the color of old pewter, swollen with spring melt, and where it bent around a stand of black poplars it spread wide and shallow over a shelf of pale stone. A ford. The pilgrims had been calling it *the Mercy* since morning, because it was the last crossing before the road split—one branch running east toward the coast and safety, the other climbing north into the hill country where the usurper's outriders were said to be burning farms.

Isandro stood at the water's edge and felt the storm before he saw it.

It was still far off—two days west, maybe three, hanging over the passes like a bruise that hadn't yet decided to bloom—but he could feel it the way a man feels a hand resting on the back of his neck. Not threatening. *Waiting.* Since the lighthouse, since the hermit's blind eyes and patient voice, storms had stopped being things that happened to him. They were conversations now. This one was saying, in its slow rolling way: *I am coming. Will you have need of me?*

*Perhaps,* he answered, in the wordless grammar the hermit had taught him, and turned back to the refugees.

There were perhaps two hundred of them. More than half were children or too old to walk fast, which was most of what made them refugees rather than soldiers. They had come out of the free cities' eastern marches in ragged processions, and Sera had gathered them the way she seemed to gather everything—by standing in front of them and refusing to be moved. For three days she had walked at the column's head with her defrocked priestess's staff wrapped in cloth so it wouldn't be recognized, and for three days she had talked farmers into walking one more mile, mothers into sharing bread, a drover with a broken cart into abandoning it without weeping over it.

Now she stood beside him at the water's edge, sleeves rolled, watching the far bank.

"They'll come from the north," she said. "Outriders don't chase a column this size unless they mean to pen it against the river."

"You've fought before?"

"I've buried people who fought badly." She squinted at the poplars. "The ford's narrow. Two hundred people can cross it in an hour if they go in order. Less if there's panic, and panic is what outriders are *for*." She looked at him then, and her eyes did the thing they always did when she was about to ask something she already knew the answer to. "Can you hold them?"

He looked at the water. At the storm waiting on the horizon like a dog told to stay.

"I can hold them," he said.

* * *

They crossed in the order Sera chose: the oldest first, then the children with their mothers, then everyone else, and the wagons last because wagons could be abandoned and people couldn't. Isandro waded out to the middle of the ford and stood on the shelf of stone where the water broke white around his knees, and he opened himself to the storm two days away.

It came to him not as lightning but as *attention*. He felt it lean across the distance like a great animal turning its head. And he spoke to it—not words, nothing so crude—the way the hermit had taught him, offering it shape. A channel. A purpose. *Come to me along the cold front. Come high, come quiet, come grey. Bring your rain but keep your thunder leashed until I call for it.*

The sky began to change.

It happened slowly enough that the pilgrims didn't notice at first—the light going pewter, the wind swinging around into the west, the temperature dropping so that breath showed. But the animals noticed. The drovers' oxen lowed and pulled at their ropes. Somewhere in the column a child started crying, and its mother hushed it, and Isandro stood in the middle of the river feeling the vast pressure of the storm settle over the valley like a hand, and he thought, not for the first time: *the hermit was right. It was never about power. It was never about summoning anything. It is about being worth trusting.*

The first outriders appeared an hour later.

They came down the north road at a trot—maybe forty of them, then sixty, then more behind them, lances up, banners dark. Not the whole company yet; the scouts first, fanning out along the bank to count what they'd caught. Isandro watched them take positions with the bored efficiency of men who had done this many times, penning cattle, and his hands went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the weather.

*Two hundred civilians,* he thought. *One narrow ford. One man in the middle of it.*

Sera's voice carried from the near bank, calm as a market-day greeting: "Keep moving. Eyes forward. Nobody stops, nobody runs." And the column kept moving, wading into the shallows, children shrieking happily at the cold water because children didn't know what the banners meant.

The first arrow came out of the north bank while the first fifty pilgrims were still mid-crossing.

It fell short, splashing into the current, but it was a message, and everyone understood it. The column rippled. Someone screamed. And Isandro, standing in the middle of the ford with the storm coiled above him like a held breath, made his choice.

He raised his arms, and he negotiated.

*Not destruction,* he offered the storm. *Precision. Show me the riders. I will show you where to strike.*

The world went white.

* * *

Afterward, he would remember it in pieces.

Lightning came down the channel he had carved for it—six strikes, seven, each one placed like a surgeon's stitch, hitting the north bank where the outriders massed. Horses screamed. Men scattered. The banner-pole split lengthwise with a crack like the world breaking open, and the burning halves drifted down onto the water and floated downstream past the pilgrims like black ribbons.

But precision was hard, and fear made it harder, and the eighth strike went wide—not by much, by the standards of the sky, but by the standards of men standing thirty feet from where it hit. The thunderclap knocked Isandro sideways off the stone shelf. The river took him.

The current was stronger than it looked. It rolled him under, slammed him against submerged rock, and for a long disorienting moment he didn't know which way the surface was. When he finally broke through, coughing, the far bank was upstream of him and receding, and someone was shouting his false name—"Corvan! *Corvan!*"—and a rope splashed into the water beside him.

He caught it. Hands hauled him in. He knelt on the stony bank spitting river water, and above him the storm raged on without him, unguided now, throwing lightning at the north bank in great indiscriminate sheets—and it was still working, because terror works even better than precision, and the outriders were falling back from the water in complete disorder.

"Corvan!" Sera dropped to her knees beside him. Her hands were on his face, turning it, checking his eyes. "You're bleeding."

"I'm fine."

"You're bleeding from the *ear*, Corvan."

He touched his ear and his fingers came away red. Something had clipped him—a stone, a splinter of the banner-pole, he didn't know. His left side ached where the river had hammered him against the rocks. None of it mattered. What mattered was the column, which had frozen at the first thunderclap and was now standing in the shallows like sheep, and what mattered was that panic hadn't started yet, and wouldn't, as long as someone gave them something to look at.

So Isandro got up. He waded back out to the middle of the ford, bleeding from the ear, ribs screaming, and he raised his arms again, and he took hold of the storm a second time.

*Steady,* he told it. *Steady. We are not finished.*

* * *

The crossing took four hours.

For four hours Isandro stood in the middle of the river while the storm wheeled overhead and the pilgrims waded past him, and every time the outriders probed the banks he struck—not the killing sheets of the first exchange, but short, sharp, *placed* bolts that hit the ground ten feet from the nearest horse and made the point better than any speech. Twice he had to catch a child who lost footing in the current. Once a wagon tipped and he held it against the flow with lightning-split timber bracing until men could right it. By the third hour his legs were shaking, his vision kept graying at the edges, and the wound in his ear had soaked the collar of his shirt red, and still he stood there, because the moment he stepped out of the water the arithmetic changed and the riders would know the crossing was only defended by one exhausted man.

The last of the pilgrims reached the south bank as the light failed.

The outriders withdrew north in the dusk, unhorsed, bannerless, carrying their wounded. They would report back. Everyone on both sides of the river understood what that meant: whatever came next would not be forty men probing. It would be the army itself, or Maren's inquisitors, or worse.

Isandro walked out of the river on legs that barely obeyed him.

Sera met him at the waterline. She didn't speak. She put his arm over her shoulders and took most of his weight without asking permission, and together they climbed the bank to where the pilgrims were making camp—small fires, shared bread, the exhausted silence of people who had been saved and knew it. As he passed, an old woman pressed something into his hand. A dried fig, wrinkled and sweet. He ate it without stopping, because it seemed important to her that he did.

* * *

They treated his wounds by firelight, apart from the camp, where the pilgrims couldn't see how bad it was.

It was bad. Not mortal—he'd known men hurt worse and lived—but the ear was the least of it. The river had driven a shard of stone deep into the flesh below his ribs, and Sera dug it out by candlelight with a needle and steady hands while he bit down on a roll of leather and stared at the stars. Then she stitched him, cleaned everything, bound it all in linen, and sat back on her heels and looked at him for a long time without saying anything.

He knew that look. He'd seen it in the drowned chapel, in Delmare, at the tide gate. It was the look she wore when she was deciding whether to say the thing she'd been carrying.

"Say it," he said.

"You almost died today." Her voice was very quiet. "In the water. You went under and you didn't come up, and I counted—I counted, Isandro, I counted to five before you surfaced, and I have stood at enough deathbeds to know what five counts means." She took a breath. "And you got up and walked back into the river anyway. So here is my question, and I want you to look at me when you answer it. Vess gave you the words of unbinding. The ritual needs blood and voice and a price. What is the price?"

The fire cracked. Somewhere behind them a child laughed in its sleep.

"Sera—"

"Don't." Her voice didn't rise, but it hardened, the way iron hardens. "I have watched people die for faith my whole life. My temple burned with forty of my sisters inside it because someone decided their deaths were an acceptable price for something. I will not stand in a binding chamber and learn afterward that you knew—*knew*—what it would cost, and let me walk in believing we were saving a king." Her hands were shaking now, and she pressed them flat against her thighs to stop them. "Tell me the price."

And Isandro looked at her—at this woman who had lost everything once already and followed him anyway, who ferried strangers across rivers and believed in gods that had gone silent, who had earned the truth a hundred times over—and he heard himself say:

"The price is memories. The binding takes the performer's bound memories. That's the cost. That's all."

It was true. It was also a lie, because it wasn't *all*, and he watched her search his face for the rest of it, and he gave her nothing else to find.

She studied him for a long moment. Then she nodded slowly, and some of the iron went out of her shoulders, though her eyes stayed on his.

"Memories," she repeated. "Whose?"

"The ones the binding holds. Mine."

"And after?"

"After, the king wakes. The parasite tears loose. Ashkarat stands." He managed something that was almost a smile. "That's the whole ledger, Sera. Blood, voice, memories. Nothing hidden."

She held his gaze a beat longer than was comfortable. Then she leaned forward and pressed her forehead briefly against his—an old priestess's blessing, he realized, one of the few rites her burned order had left her—and whispered, "Then we finish it together. Whatever the ledger says."

She rose to check on the pilgrims, and he sat alone by the fire, and the lie sat in his stomach like swallowed river-stone.

Because the journal had been clear, and he had read it three times. The binding must be re-made with someone inside it. Someone had to anchor the parasite, forever, and the ritual would return everything the binding took—including the memories of whoever performed it. Memories returned meant the valley, the fifteen years, Stillwater, the ferryman's daughter, the whole erased life flooding back—and whoever anchored the thing wearing his brother's crown would spend eternity holding it shut.

He had already chosen. He had chosen the night he read the journal, alone, and told no one.

Westward, beyond the hills, the true storm was still coming—the largest of the age, gathering over the passes, and Ashkarat lay between him and it like a city balanced on a knife's edge. Two days, maybe three. He closed his eyes and listened to the distant thunder, and thought: *Hold on, brother. Whoever you are now. Hold on.*

Behind him, unnoticed, Sera paused at the edge of the firelight and looked back at him for a long, long moment—reading his face the way she read scripture, line by line, searching for the verse he had left out.

Then she turned and walked into the camp, and the river ran on in the dark, carrying the smell of rain toward the sea.
