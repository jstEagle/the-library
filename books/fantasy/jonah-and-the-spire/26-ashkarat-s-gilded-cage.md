## 26. Ashkarat's Gilded Cage

The tunnel smelled of myrrh and old stone and, beneath both, of something that had been breathing in the dark for a very long time.

Sera had not walked these passages since she was nineteen years old, a novice with ink-stained fingers and a voice still soft enough to echo. The Temple of the Ninefold Dawn had kept its underways the way a body keeps veins—unseen, essential, threaded through everything. She had memorized them as an act of devotion: which stair descended behind the reliquary, which corridor ran beneath the Processional Way, where the lamps were lit at dusk and left to gutter by dawn. Fifteen years of silence had passed over her faith like water over a stone, wearing it smooth, and she had believed herself past grief.

Then she put her hand on the first familiar wall, felt the grooves of the dawn-spiral carved there by hands three centuries dead, and understood that grief does not wear away. It only waits for you to touch it again.

"Which way?" Isandro whispered behind her.

She held up one finger. *Wait.* Above them, muffled through six feet of earth and marble, the city was singing vespers—or what passed for vespers now, the sanitized liturgy of the Concordance, all incense and no gods. Ashkarat's evening song drifted down through ventilation shafts like light through dirty glass. Sera closed her eyes and listened past it, the way her teachers had taught her to listen past her own heartbeat, and found what she was listening for: footsteps. Two pairs, unhurried, crossing the transept above. Then nothing.

"Now," she said, and lifted the grate.

* * *

They came up into the crypt of Saint Vessane the Unlettered, a saint so obscure that even the Concordance's purges had not bothered with her bones. Dust lay on the sarcophagus like snowfall no one had disturbed. Sera lit no lamp; fifteen years ago she could have navigated this dark blindfolded, and it seemed her body had kept the map her faith had lost. Left at the pillar with the chipped falcon. Down three steps. Through the door that stuck unless you lifted while you pushed.

Isandro moved close behind her, close enough that she could feel the static clinging to him like a second cloak. He had learned to dampen it on the road—the old hermit's lessons, negotiated storms, breath and patience—but in enclosed spaces it leaked anyway, a faint prickling against her skin, the air before lightning. She had stopped finding it frightening somewhere around the ford. Now it was almost comforting, the way a hearth is comforting. Almost.

"The tunnels run under the whole temple district," she murmured when they reached the first junction. "The Concordance sealed the public entrances years ago—they didn't want anyone remembering there had ever been another way in. But they never mapped them properly. The maps burned with the motherhouse."

"You're sure?"

"I'm sure I spent four years scrubbing these floors and dreaming about burning the place down." She heard rather than saw his smile. "I know every inch of them."

They climbed toward the upper galleries, moving between the temple's bones. Through hairline cracks in the vaulting, candlelight bled down in thin gold threads, and with it came sound: the shuffling of registrars' boots, the low murmur of clerks, once—a sound that made Sera's stomach clench—the flat, procedural voice of a man reading names from a list. *God-touched registry, eastern annex.* They were still doing it. Still pulling people out of their homes and their shops and their prayer-circles, writing their names in ledgers, and then—Sera had seen the empty houses in Delmare, had heard the refugees' accounts—writing them out again.

Ashkarat had been beautiful once. Even now, glimpsed through the high slit-windows as they crossed beneath the nave's northern arm, it was beautiful: the terraced districts falling away toward the river in cascades of pale stone and copper roofs, the great spire rising at the city's heart like a needle drawn through fabric, the palace crowning the highest terrace with its thousand windows catching the last red light. Beautiful the way a held breath is beautiful. Beautiful because everyone in it was afraid to move.

She saw it in the streets below. People walked quickly and did not look up. Market stalls stood half-shuttered at the dinner hour. A child laughed somewhere and was hushed—not scolded, *hushed*, a hand pressed gently over a small mouth, the instinct of a city that had learned its sounds might be reported. On the temple steps, two gray-clad registrars checked papers with the bored efficiency of men who had done this ten thousand times, and the line of citizens waiting to be processed stood in perfect, terrible order.

"They're not rioting," Isandro said quietly. He had gone still beside the window, storm-grey eyes tracking the streets. "An army at the gates, a thing on the throne, gods coming back—and they queue."

"Order is easier than hope," Sera said. "Hope gets you registered."

* * *

They made their camp that night in the undercroft of the old novices' dormitory, a room Sera had slept in for one winter before being promoted to the scriptorium. Someone had stored ritual vestments here once; the moth-eaten remnants hung from hooks like the skins of shed selves. There was a ventilation shaft that breathed warm air down from the temple kitchens above, and the smell of bread came with it, absurdly domestic, and Sera found her eyes stinging at the smell of bread, of all things, because the last time she had smelled it from this shaft she had been twenty-two and the gods still answered and her whole life had been ahead of her like an uncut bolt of cloth.

She did not sleep. She sat against the wall with her knees drawn up and let herself feel the city.

That was the discipline Vess had taught her, back when the god was more than embers and a voice: *a priestess is a tuning fork, little Sera. You do not make the music. You resonate.* She had thought the gift died with the gods' silence. It hadn't. It had only had nothing to resonate *with*—fifteen years of playing to an empty hall. But Ashkarat was not silent. Ashkarat hummed.

And the hum was wrong.

It took her hours to find the shape of it, lying in the dark with her eyes open, letting the city's frequencies pass through her. Prayer should have been part of the hum—fearful, furtive prayer, but prayer nonetheless, the old gods returning and desperate people whispering to them in cellars and closets. She could hear that thread, faint and frightened, scattered through the lower districts like sparks. And beneath it, the ordinary human drone of a hundred thousand sleeping bodies. And beneath *that*—

Beneath that, something was feeding.

Not metaphorically. Not the way ambition feeds or fear feeds. Something in the direction of the palace, at the very top of the city's resonance, was drawing the hum upward and inward, drinking it the way a root drinks, slow and constant and without any feeling she could name except *appetite*. It wasn't greedy the way men are greedy. Men want things. This simply consumed, the way a fire consumes, without wanting, without hating, without end. And wrapped around that hunger, threaded through it, held in it like a fly in amber, was a mind. Human. Small compared to the hunger. Screaming, perhaps—or perhaps long past screaming. She couldn't tell. That was the worst part. She pressed her awareness against it and got back nothing but appetite, and somewhere inside the appetite, dim as a face behind frosted glass, a king.

Her hands were shaking. She realized she had pressed her palm flat against the floor, as if to push the sensation away through the stone.

"Sera." Isandro's voice, low, from across the room. He'd been awake too; of course he had. "You've gone white as the vestments. What is it?"

She made herself answer plainly, because he deserved plainness, because they had built this thing between them out of plain words spoken in bad inns and worse weather. "The binding," she said. "Everyone assumes—I assumed—that the ritual went wrong. That the usurper's sorcerers tried to bind a king to obedience and botched it, and something slipped in through the tear." She swallowed. "That's not what happened."

"How do you know?"

"Because I can feel it." She pulled her knees tighter. "A corrupted binding would be a wound. Ragged edges. Leakage. This—" she searched for the word and hated it when she found it— "this is *seamless*. Whatever is wearing Aurelian fits him. It has had fifteen years to fit him. And it isn't feeding on his pain or his fear, Isandro. It's feeding on the *gods*. On their returning voices. Every prayer answered in this city goes up and gets drunk before it reaches heaven. That's why the gods are weak. That's why Vess is embers. It's not that the parasite blocks them." Her voice dropped. "It eats them. It ate the silence itself. Fifteen years of silence wasn't the gods abandoning us—it was this thing swallowing every word."

The kitchen-smell of bread drifted down the shaft. Somewhere above, a bell rang the small hours.

Isandro came and sat beside her, his shoulder warm against hers, his static prickling faintly along her arm. For a while neither of them spoke. Then he said, "If the binding was seamless—if it worked—then someone built it on purpose. To contain it."

"Yes."

"But the records at the drowned chapel said the binding hid a bloodline. Hid *me*." His jaw tightened; she saw the muscle jump in the lamplight they dared not light. "My mother traded our memories to save our lives. That's what the letters said. That was a bargain with the parasite, or with whatever power the loyalists had left. So either they knew what they were containing and paid that price knowingly—"

"—or they thought they were binding a king's obedience," Sera finished softly, "and discovered too late what they'd actually caught." She turned the horror over, examining its faces. "Fifteen years I've cursed the ones who performed that rite. Burned their names in effigy in my head, every defrocked night of every defrocked year. *They silenced the gods. They broke the world.* And now I'm sitting in their cellar realizing they may have been the only reason there was a world left to break." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "Do you understand what that means? It means the heresy I was branded for—the old gods, the true rites, the restoration—none of it was stolen from us. It was *rationed*. Kept from us the way you keep medicine from a patient who would overdose. The silence was a bandage over something that would have eaten the whole limb."

"And now the bandage is coming off," Isandro said. "Because the gods started answering again."

"Because the gods started answering again," she agreed, "and every answer is a meal."

He was quiet a moment. When he spoke again, his voice had the careful flatness he used when he was most afraid—the valley-boy habit of pressing terror flat so it wouldn't thunder. "You said it fits him. Seamless. Sera—if we cut it out, if the unbinding works—"

"Then we tear a seam that's held for fifteen years," she said. "And I don't know what bleeds."

* * *

They went up into the temple proper the next evening, because Sera needed to see the sanctuary.

She told herself it was reconnaissance—the sanctuary connected to the underways, and the underways connected to the palace hill by the old processional tunnel, and if they meant to reach the binding chamber they needed the route. All true. But there was a truer thing underneath, and she let herself admit it as she lifted the final grate into the ambulatory behind the high altar: she needed to stand in her god's house one more time before she died in it.

The sanctuary had been redecorated. That was the Concordance's way—not demolition, but renovation, erasure dressed as improvement. The ninefold dawn mosaic behind the altar had been plastered over and painted with the Regent's sunburst sigil, gold on white, clean as a coin. The old altar itself remained, too massive to move, but it had been draped, covered like furniture in a dead relative's house. No candles burned at the shrines of the Nine. Instead, brass registration stands stood in their places, neat rows of them, each with its ledger and its inkwell and its little sign: *Declare all experiences of voice, vision, or warmth.*

Warmth. They had a category for warmth.

Sera stood in the shadow of the ambulatory columns with Isandro's hand light on her shoulder—he'd reached for her without seeming to notice he'd done it—and looked at the place where she had given her life, and felt almost nothing, and then felt the almost-nothing crack open into everything.

Because the sanctuary was not empty. Not spiritually. Under the plaster and the drapes and the brass stands, the old consecration still lived in the stones the way heat lives in a hearth after the fire dies. She could feel it faintly, a ghost-warmth, the residue of ten thousand dawns of worship. And she could feel the parasite's attention resting on it too—not feeding, not yet, but *aware*, the way a man who has eaten his fill is aware of the pantry. It had not bothered to scour the sanctuary because the sanctuary was already dead. It saved its appetite for living prayers.

"It knows we're here," Isandro breathed.

"No." She shook her head slowly. "It knows *something* is here. It tastes the temple's memory waking up. Fifteen years and these stones remember me, Isandro. Maybe that's enough to wake other things." She looked up at the plastered-over dawn, at the gold sunburst stamped over it like a seal over a mouth. "Or maybe I'm just tired and hopeful and seeing omens in dust."

"Vess started as dust and embers," he said. "Omens have a way of being literal lately."

Despite everything, she smiled. Then the smile died, because the great doors of the sanctuary opened at the far end, and lamplight spilled in, and voices came with it—clerks, closing up the registration stands for the night, their footsteps echoing in the vast hollow space. Sera pulled Isandro down behind the column, into the dark, and they crouched together among stones older than any kingdom, listening to the ordinary sounds of an occupied church, and she felt his heart hammering through his sleeve and knew he was thinking what she was thinking:

Three days. Perhaps four. Then the siege, or the unbinding, or the thing upstairs turning its head and tasting storm-magic in the air.

* * *

They found the processional tunnel at midnight.

It began behind the reliquary of Saint Vessane—which Sera noted, with a tired kind of wonder, was the second time the obscure saint had sheltered them—and descended in a long spiral cut through the living rock of the palace hill. The loyalists had built it, the drowned chapel's records implied, as an escape route for the royal family; the priests had maintained it afterward as a private way to carry the king to worship unseen. Sera walked it with her hand on the wall and her heart climbing into her throat, because every yard brought them closer to the source of the hum, and the hum was getting louder, and it was not a sound. It was a *pull*.

By the third turning of the spiral she could feel it in her teeth. By the fifth, Isandro had stopped walking altogether, braced against the wall, his breath coming fast, and she realized the pull was worse for him—because the thing upstairs had tasted storm-magic once already, from the rafters of its own throne room, and some part of it was leaning toward him even now, the way a plant leans toward a window.

"It feels you," she whispered.

"I know." His voice was ragged. "It's like—standing upstream of something enormous. Like the whole river wants to turn around." He laughed, breathlessly, horribly. "The hermit taught me to negotiate with storms. Nobody taught me how to negotiate with *this*."

"Then don't. We go quiet, we go slow, and we don't give it anything to taste." She took his hand—his skin crackling faintly against hers, static and sweat—and squeezed it. "Isandro. Look at me. We are two people in a hole in the ground, and that thing thinks it owns the sky. Let's keep it thinking that a little longer."

They went on. The tunnel leveled, ran straight, and ended at a wall of fitted stone with an iron door in it, unmarked except for a single worn relief at eye height: the ninefold dawn, half obliterated by a chisel, but recognizable—she recognized it, her chest tightening, the way you recognize your own face in a stranger's photograph. The old priests' mark. The door of those who had served the crown and the gods both, before serving both became impossible.

Sera laid her palm against the iron. Beyond it, muffled by stone, she could hear the palace breathing—guards shifting, torches hissing, the vast organism of a court settling into its uneasy sleep. And beneath all of it, closer now, clearer: the hum. The appetite. And threaded through it, that dim shape behind frosted glass, the king, the brother, the boy in the letters her hermit friend had carried across a kingdom—

She froze.

Because the hum had changed. Just slightly. Just for a moment. The endless, mindless draw of it had stuttered—like a great throat swallowing, and then pausing, mid-swallow—as though something on the far side of the iron door had noticed, at last, after fifteen years, the taste of a priestess's faith and a storm-caller's blood seeping through the seams of the world.

Above them, through fifty feet of rock, faint as a heartbeat, something in the palace turned over in its sleep.

And began, very slowly, to wake.
