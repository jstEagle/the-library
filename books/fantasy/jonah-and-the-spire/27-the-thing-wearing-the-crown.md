## 27. The Thing Wearing the Crown

The tunnel smelled of dust and old incense, of stone that had not been disturbed in years, and beneath it all—faint as a memory of a memory—the scent of myrrh. Sera had gone still three paces ahead of him, her hand pressed flat against the wall, her lips moving in something that was either a prayer or a curse.

"This is the Passage of Voices," she whispered. "I walked it every morning for six years. The acolytes called it the spine of the temple, because it runs the whole length of the palace like a bone." She swallowed. "We were taught it was holy ground. We were taught the kings above us could hear our prayers through the stone."

Isandro put his eye to the grate.

Below him, the throne room of Ashkarat opened like the interior of a gilded bell. He had expected opulence—he had grown up, after all, on stories of the capital's splendor—but nothing had prepared him for the way light moved in this place. A hundred lamps burned behind alabaster screens, and their glow did not fall so much as *pool*, gathering in amber shallows across the marble floor, deepening to shadow at the walls. Courtiers stood in loose constellations around the room, jeweled and silent, their faces turned toward the dais with the fixed attention of people watching a fire they could not look away from.

And on the dais, on the throne of Vhalen, sat the Regent.

He was beautiful. That was Isandro's first thought, and his second, and it took him a long moment to understand why the beauty unsettled him so deeply. The man wore the face Isandro had seen in Sera's smuggled portraits—a strong jaw, dark hair threaded with silver at the temples, eyes the color of honey held up to the sun. Aurelian's face. His brother's face, or close enough that his chest ached looking at it. But the face was too still. It was beautiful the way a painted mask is beautiful, the way a corpse dressed for burial is beautiful: everything correct, everything composed, and nothing behind it moving.

*That is not my brother,* he thought, and then corrected himself, because he had no right to the word yet. *That is not the king.*

"Steady," Sera breathed beside him. She had come up to the grate now, her shoulder warm against his in the dark. "Watch. Learn its rhythms. We may only get one chance to see how it moves."

The Regent rose from the throne, and the court bowed as one body, a field of wheat bending before wind. Isandro watched him descend the three steps to the floor, watched him pause before an elderly lord who trembled slightly despite his fine robes, and heard the Regent's voice drift up through the bronze grate—smooth, warm, pitched perfectly to carry without effort.

"Lord Casserin. Your petition concerns the grain tariffs of the eastern provinces, I believe."

"It—it does, Majesty. The war levies have been—"

"The war levies are necessary." The Regent smiled, and Isandro felt his skin crawl, because the smile reached every part of the face except the eyes. "But I am told your daughter has taken ill. Fever, is it? In this season?"

"Yes—yes, Majesty, she—"

"Then go home to her." The honey-colored eyes softened into something almost tender. "Grain can wait. Daughters cannot. Go."

The old lord bowed again and again, weeping openly with gratitude, and withdrew. Isandro's hands had curled into fists against the grate. Because he had seen it—in the half-second before the Regent spoke, the man's gaze had flicked upward, toward some point beyond the ceiling, the way a man checks a ledger. As if the fevered daughter were an entry there. As if the mercy were arithmetic.

"He knew," Isandro murmured. "About the girl. How could he know about the girl?"

"Spies," Sera said, but her voice lacked conviction. She had gone pale in the lamplight leaking through the grate. "Every court has spies."

"That wasn't spies. That was—" He searched for the word and found it in the oldest stories, the ones the shrine keeper used to tell children in Stillwater, back when telling them was still allowed. "*Omniscience.* Small omniscience. The kind you buy."

They watched for another hour. Petitioners came and went; decrees were signed with a flourish that never varied; twice the Regent laughed at a courtier's joke, and both times the laugh was identical, note for note, like a recording played back. And slowly, as the audience hall emptied toward the midday recess, Isandro began to see what he had come to see—and wished with all that was left of his courage that he hadn't.

It happened when a servant crossed the far edge of the room carrying a candelabrum. Nothing remarkable. But one of the candles guttered as the servant passed beneath a draft from the high windows, and for an instant the flame bent sideways, stretched thin—

—and the Regent's head turned to follow it.

Not his eyes. His *head*, rotating with a smoothness no human neck possessed, past the comfortable arc of muscle and bone, until his face pointed at the candle flame like a compass needle finding north. He held there for perhaps two seconds. Then the head rotated back, the beautiful mask resumed its warmth, and the Regent said something gracious to the chamberlain beside him while the court laughed obediently.

Sera made a small sound beside him, and Isandro realized his own breath had stopped.

"You saw it," she whispered.

"I saw it." His mouth was dry. "Gods below, Sera. I saw it."

* * *

They did not speak again until they had retreated four hundred paces down the Passage of Voices, to a junction where an old ventilation shaft let in a blade of gray daylight and the sound of the city—cart wheels, gulls, the distant hammering of siege preparations on the outer walls. Even here Sera kept her voice below a whisper.

"A god-eater," she said. "The heresiarchs wrote about them. The oldest texts, the ones the order kept chained in the undercroft—I copied fragments when I was a novice, before I understood what I was copying." Her hands were shaking, and she clasped them together to stop it. "There were things older than the gods, Isandro. Things that fed on divinity the way worms feed on fruit. The gods didn't go silent fifteen years ago because they abandoned us. They went silent because something was *eating them*. One by one. Starting with the smallest and working up."

"Vess," Isandro said quietly. "Vess came to me as an ember. A candle-flame god, barely alive."

"And it told you the binding was corrupted. That something slipped into the king during the ritual." Sera pressed her back against the stone, closing her eyes. "I've been thinking about it wrong. All of us have. We assumed the parasite got in by accident—that the binding tore, that something opportunistic crawled through the wound. But what if the binding wasn't corrupted?" Her eyes opened, and there was horror in them, and beneath the horror, a terrible clarity. "What if the binding was *containment*? What if Queen Liora and whoever helped her weren't trying to hide a bloodline from assassins. They were trying to cage a god-eater using the only bait it couldn't resist—a crown, a kingdom, a king's body to wear. And the price of the cage was the twins' memories, because a binding that size needs to be paid in *selfhood*."

Isandro leaned against the opposite wall. The blade of daylight fell across his boots. Somewhere above them, muffled by forty feet of stone, the court was resuming its afternoon session, and he imagined the thing up there wearing his brother's face, turning its head too far to watch a candle flame, tasting the air.

"Tasting," he repeated aloud. "You said the texts—what do they say about how they hunt?"

"Scent. Hunger. Divinity leaves a residue, and they track it." Sera hesitated, and he watched her arrive at the same conclusion he was arriving at, watched her face go from pale to gray. "Isandro. You crossed the valley rim five weeks ago. You leveled a ridge with raw storm-power in front of every scrying eye in Vhalen. You've been calling lightning over free cities ever since. Storm-calling isn't just magic. In the old theology, the storm was the voice of the sky-gods. When you call lightning, you're—"

"—wearing their voice," he finished. "Resonating with dead gods. Leaving a trail of divine residue across half a continent."

"And you're standing forty feet below the creature that ate them."

For a long moment neither of them moved. The gray blade of light crept along the floor between them. From above came the faint, rhythmic sound of the Regent's voice, rising and falling through petitions, patient as tide.

"We leave," Isandro said finally. "Now. Tonight if the tunnels allow, now if they don't. Back down the spine, out through the cistern, and we don't stop until—"

He never finished the sentence.

It began as a change in the air pressure, so subtle that at first he mistook it for the ventilation shaft shifting with the wind outside. Then the hairs on his arms lifted. Then the blade of daylight seemed to thicken, the gray light taking on a faint amber tinge, as though somewhere above them a hundred lamps had all flared at once.

And through the stone, through forty feet of marble and granite bedrock, Isandro felt a *attention* settle over the palace like a hand pressing down on a drumhead.

It was not hearing. It was not sight. It was older and cruder than either—a vast, slow *tasting*, the way a serpent tastes the air with its tongue, drawing the world in across some inner palate and sorting it. Isandro felt himself being tasted. Felt the attention slide over Sera, find her ordinary, move on—and then stop. Come back. Linger.

Because he was resonating. He understood it suddenly, completely, the way he understood things now only when it was too late: the storm-magic in his blood answered the dead gods' silence the way a struck bell answers a tuning fork, and he was standing in the belly of the thing that had devoured every god whose voice he echoed. To the creature above him, he was not a man hiding in a tunnel.

He was the smell of an unfinished meal.

"*Run,*" Sera said.

They ran.

The Passage of Voices stretched ahead of them, dim and endless, and Isandro took the lead because his night vision was better and because running forward was easier than running away from the thing he could feel descending through the palace above them—not physically, not yet, but as attention, as pressure, as a taste growing stronger and more interested with every heartbeat. Behind them, far back along the tunnel toward the throne room, he heard a sound that stopped his blood: a soft, wet, unfolding noise, like a glove being pulled inside out.

The grate they had entered through was ahead, iron bars set in ancient stone, and beyond it the vertical shaft that dropped to the temple undercroft where their route began. Sera had the rope coiled at her shoulder. Forty feet of descent, then the undercroft, then the long crawl west to the cistern and out. It had taken them six hours to come in. It would take twenty minutes to get out if nothing went wrong.

Something went wrong.

The lamp they had left burning at the junction—their marker for the return journey—was out. Not guttered. *Out*, its wick cold, its oil unspent, as though the flame had simply been *taken*. And standing in the junction where the lamp should have been, robed in the gray livery of a palace servant, was a man.

No. Isandro's stomach dropped through the floor. Not a man. The proportions were wrong in a way his mind kept sliding off of, the way a dream figure is wrong—shoulders a fraction too wide, hands hanging a fraction too long, the face above the servant's collar smooth and generic as an unfinished sculpture. Only the eyes were finished. The eyes were the Regent's eyes, honey-colored and warm, and they were warm now, warmly regarding them both with an expression of mild, courteous hunger.

"You shouldn't be here," the thing said. Its voice was the Regent's voice, perfect down to the gentle sibilance. "This part of the house is closed."

Sera's hand found Isandro's wrist and gripped hard enough to bruise. He felt her trembling, and beneath the trembling, feel her faith doing what it had always done—hardening instead of breaking.

"The undercroft," the thing continued, tilting its head with that same wrong smoothness he'd seen through the grate. "So few remember the undercroft. So few remember *anything* anymore. Isn't that wonderful? Forgetting is such a gift. I gave this kingdom such a gift." The honey eyes settled on Isandro, and the warmth in them curdled into something vast and cold and infinitely patient. "But you. You're full of voices, little echo. Full of everyone I've eaten. Do you know what that's like, from my side of the table? It's like hearing old songs hummed in a house where all the singers are dead."

Isandro's power rose in him unbidden—storm-light crawling up his forearms, blue-white and wild, the air crackling with ozone in the sealed stone dark. Every instinct screamed at him to strike, to burn the thing standing between them and the rope, to level the tunnel the way he'd leveled the ridge on the day he fled the valley.

He didn't. Fifteen years of Stillness had taught him at least that much: power spent in panic was power wasted, and this thing had eaten gods. What was one storm-caller's lightning to a creature that had swallowed the silence they died into?

Instead he did the hardest thing he had done since crossing the rim. He banked it. Pulled the storm down into his bones, smothered the resonance, wrapped his whole screaming nature in the mental equivalent of wet cloth—because if the thing tracked him by the taste of divinity, then the only weapon he had left was to stop tasting like anything at all.

The honey eyes narrowed. The head tilted further, further, past any angle a neck should allow, and for one endless moment the creature simply *listened* to him, the way he had once listened to the valley's silence, hunting for the crack in it.

"There you are," it said softly. "Hiding in the quiet. Clever. The valley taught you that, didn't it? I looked for you there, once. Fifteen years ago. Such a well-made silence." Something that was almost a smile touched the unfinished face. "But silences end, little echo. All of them end. And when yours does—"

A bell rang above them. Distant, urgent, the palace alarm—and the creature's head snapped upward, its attention torn away for a single heartbeat toward whatever disturbance the servants' quarters had produced. Maren, Isandro thought wildly. Or the siege. Or a kitchen fire. It didn't matter. It was a heartbeat, and heartbeats were currency.

Sera threw the rope. The grapnel caught the grate's bars with a clang that echoed like doom, and they were climbing, hand over hand, Isandro shoving Sera up ahead of him while the thing below didn't run but simply *unfolded* upward, limbs extending, the servant's livery splitting along seams that had never been sewn, its form pouring itself into the vertical shaft after them like smoke given intent.

Light burst below them—honey-warm, devouring, the light of a thing that ate candles and gods alike—and Isandro felt it rise past his feet, tasting, searching, and finding nothing, because he was holding himself shut, holding himself silent, holding the storm behind his teeth with a discipline that cost him more than the lightning ever had.

They came out of the undercroft into the night behind the temple, gasping, filthy, alive. Behind them, the tunnel entrance stood open and unguarded. It didn't matter. They both knew without saying it: the Passage of Voices was lost. The creature knew the route now, knew the undercroft, knew the smell of their passage through its house. Six hours of careful infiltration, Sera's lifetime of knowledge of the temple's hidden ways—all of it burned in twenty minutes of flight.

Sera leaned against the temple's outer wall, shaking, laughing the thin hysterical laugh of the nearly-dead. "It talked to us," she said. "It *talked* to us, Isandro. Gods don't talk like that. Things that eat gods don't need to talk."

"It wasn't talking to us." Isandro stared back at the palace, at the lit windows of the throne room floating above the dark city like amber lanterns. His arms were still trembling—from the climb, or from the effort of staying silent, he couldn't tell. "It was introducing itself. It wanted us to know it knows exactly what we are and exactly where we stand." He exhaled slowly. "It called me *little echo*, Sera. It said it looked for me in the valley. It's known about me for fifteen years."

Above the palace, clouds were gathering—the leading edge of the great storm system rolling in off the sea, the century-storm the hermit had promised, the storm he would have to negotiate with on the spire when the time came. Lightning flickered inside the cloud-bellies, silent and far, like something pacing behind a door.

And in the highest window of the throne room, Isandro saw the Regent standing alone, motionless, its head tilted back at that impossible angle, watching the coming storm with an expression he could read even at this distance, even across the dark:

*hunger.*

* * *

They did not go back to the safehouse. Sera forbade it before Isandro even suggested it—if the creature had tasted them, it might have tasted their trail, and the safehouse was the first place its servants would look. Instead they walked, hooded and anonymous, through streets already thick with siege-fear: families stacking sandbags against cellar doors, priests of the new quiet temples nailing shut their own shrines, children being carried indoors against the coming rain. The city smelled of tar and terror and the green metallic promise of the storm.

They ended, by unspoken agreement, at the one place in Ashkarat that had ever felt like sanctuary: the drowned chapel where Vess had first reconstituted itself, down in the flooded quarter near the river gate. The water had risen another foot since their last visit, lapping at the base of the altar, and the chapel's broken dome let in the storm-light, turning the flood below them into a sheet of restless silver.

Vess was waiting. Or rather, Vess's ember was—a knot of candle-flame hovering above the waterline, smaller than Isandro had ever seen it, no larger now than a moth. When they waded in, the flame shivered and brightened by a fraction, the way a dying man brightens at the arrival of friends.

"You met it," Vess said. Its voice was thinner than Isandro had ever heard it, a whisper of a whisper. "I felt it turn. I felt it *taste* you. Oh, little storm. Oh, foolish, brave little storm."

"It knows the tunnels," Sera said. "It knows the undercroft. Everything we built the plan on—"

"I know what it knows." The ember sank lower, nearly touching the black water. "Listen to me, both of you, because I am spending strength I do not have. It let you go."

Isandro went very still. "What?"

"It could have taken you in the shaft. It was faster than you. It is always faster than its prey." The flame guttered, steadied. "But it didn't. Because it wants you to come to the spire. It wants you to call the storm. A god-eater grows fat on divinity, and there has been no divinity in this world for fifteen years except scraps—except embers like me. But a storm-caller standing on the highest spire in Vhalen, negotiating with the largest storm of the age, channeling the voices of every sky-god it ever devoured?" The ember trembled, and Isandro realized with a lurch that the trembling was laughter, dry and bitter and ancient. "You wouldn't be a meal, little echo. You'd be a *feast*. It has been starving politely for fifteen years, wearing a king and signing decrees, waiting for exactly this. Waiting for the sky to answer someone again."

The storm-light pulsed overhead, closer now. Thunder rolled across the drowned quarter, and the flood-water shivered into rings around Isandro's knees.

"So we don't go to the spire," Sera said flatly. "We find another way. There's always another way."

"No," Isandro said.

Both of them turned to look at him. He was staring at the water, at his own reflection fractured across its surface, and the reflection was calm in a way he did not entirely feel.

"It expects me to be afraid," he said. "It introduced itself so I'd be afraid. It let us run so I'd know it could have kept me. Every piece of tonight was designed to make me choose hiding." He looked up, and the storm-light caught his face, and Sera would tell him later that in that moment he looked less like a fugitive than like weather deciding where to fall. "But it made one mistake. It thinks I resonate with the dead gods because I can't help it. It thinks my power is a scent it can follow to its feast." His hand closed around the storm in his chest—not smothering it now, but *greeting* it, the way the hermit had taught him, the way you greet an old and dangerous friend. "It doesn't know I've learned to negotiate. It doesn't know the storm and I have terms now. And it certainly doesn't know," and here his voice dropped, quiet and terrible, "that I'm not going to the spire to feed it."

"Then why?" Sera whispered.

"Because the binding chamber is directly beneath the throne room. Because the anchor is there. And because when the century-storm breaks over Ashkarat, every eye in the palace—every *taste* in the palace—is going to be turned upward, at the light, at the thunder, at the little echo on the spire giving it the show of its hungry life." He waded toward the chapel door, toward the dark and the rain and the impossible days ahead. "It wants a feast. Let it look up at the table."

Behind them, Vess's ember flared once—bright, brief, almost hopeful—and went out like a candle pinched between wise fingers.

Above the drowned quarter, the storm arrived.
