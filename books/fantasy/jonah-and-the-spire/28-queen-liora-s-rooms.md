## 28. Queen Liora's Rooms

The corridor behind the tapestry had not been opened in fifteen years, and the air proved it.

Isandro stood at the mouth of it with a shuttered lantern in one hand and Sera's hand in the other, and the breath that came out to meet them was dry as old paper, sweet with the ghost of some flower that no longer grew anywhere in Ashkarat. Dust hung in the lantern light like snow that had forgotten how to fall. Somewhere behind them, deep in the palace's stone throat, the parasite's presence pressed against his senses like a thumb against a bruise—patient, vast, tasting the air for the storm it had sensed in the rafters two nights past.

"It knows we're in the walls," Sera whispered.

"It knows *something* is in the walls." He eased the tapestry back into place behind them, cutting off the faint glow of the temple tunnel. "It doesn't know *what*. Not yet. That's the only coin we have left."

The corridor sloped gently upward, its walls paneled in pale wood gone gray with age. He counted doors as they passed—servants' passages, he guessed, the hidden veins of the palace, the routes a queen would have used when she wanted to move unwatched through her own home. The thought settled in his chest with a strange, aching weight. *Her own home.* He was walking through the house of a woman he could not remember, a woman who had died—or worse, been made to unexist—so that her sons could live, and the walls felt no more familiar to him than the walls of any stranger's house.

Except they did, a little. In the way a song half-heard in childhood feels familiar without being known.

At the corridor's end stood a door of white wood, and across it, in a script that made Sera draw a sharp breath, a seal of silver had been laid in a continuous unbroken line—no lock, no keyhole, only the seal, and beneath it three words burned into the grain in the old high script of Vhalen:

*FOR THE FORGOTTEN ONE.*

"Vess said the binding hid each of you from half the world," Sera murmured. She traced the air above the words without touching them, the way priests touched relics they weren't worthy to hold. "The king was hidden from the gods. Hidden from *memory*—from everything that might remember him truly. And the other twin—"

"Was hidden from the palace." Isandro's voice came out flat. "From this. From her."

He raised his lantern. The silver seal caught the light and did not glint so much as *listen*, the way old god-work listened. He thought of the drowned chapel, of the record with two princes' names where one had been scraped away, and he understood, with the dull certainty of a man reading his own death warrant, that the seal had been waiting fifteen years for a hand like his.

He pressed his palm flat against the door.

The seal did not break. It *melted*—silver running like water into the grain of the wood, and the door swung inward without a sound, and the breath of the dead queen's rooms rolled over them both.

* * *

They were beautiful. That was the first thing, and it hurt more than ruin would have.

High-ceilinged chambers hung with silk the color of a storm's first hour—blue-gray, violet-shadowed. A writing desk beneath a tall window, its glass still whole, still looking out over a city that had forgotten it had ever loved its queen. A cradle, small and white, pushed into the corner as if someone had meant to move it and never come back. Two chairs by a cold hearth, set close together, angled toward one another, the posture of a conversation interrupted mid-word.

And everywhere, faint as breath on glass, the residue of warding. Sera felt it before he did; he saw her sway and caught her elbow.

"It's still active," she whispered. "Fifteen years, no priest to renew it, no god to feed it—and it's still *holding*. Isandro, this isn't ordinary work. This is the binding itself. These rooms *are* the ritual."

He crossed the threshold and the warding recognized him. He felt it the way a lock feels its key: a shiver through the floor, a softening in the air, and then the room *exhaled*, and every candle stub on every shelf lit itself at once with flames the color of dawn.

Dust did not burn. It simply drifted down, released, as though the room had been holding it, holding *everything*, waiting.

On the writing desk lay a journal bound in storm-blue leather, and on its cover, in a woman's careful hand, one word: *Isandro.*

His name. His true name, the one Vess had spoken on the riverbank, the one no one in Stillwater had ever known. Not the ferryman. Not Odile. Not the valley itself, which forgot on principle.

He could not move for a long moment. Sera put her hand between his shoulder blades, warm and steady, and said nothing, and that was its own kind of mercy.

He opened the journal.

* * *

The early pages were ordinary, and that ordinariness was a knife. A queen recording her sons' first words—*Aurelian said "light" before "mama" and was insufferable about it for a month.* Two boys in one cradle because neither would sleep apart. A sketch, clumsy and loving, of two infants holding hands in their sleep. Isandro read with his jaw clenched so hard it ached, mourning a childhood he had no memory of, grieving a mother he had never known he'd lost.

Then the hand changed. The entries grew shorter. The ink grew darker. And the dates began to cluster, day upon day, in the last month of the old world.

*The silence has come. The gods do not answer. Vess was the last, and Vess came to me weeping, if a god can weep, and told me what is in the palace. What has been in the palace since before the coronation. It wore a counselor first, then a general, and now it means to wear my husband's crown on my son's head, and it cannot be killed. It can only be starved, and it feeds on remembrance.*

*It came to me tonight. It has my husband's voice now, which is how I know my husband is gone. It offered me a bargain, and it was honest, which is the most obscene thing about it. It does not lie. It only prices.*

He had to stop there. He set the journal down on the desk and walked to the window and looked out at Ashkarat glittering under curfew, at the distant smudge of the usurper's siege fires on the eastern hills, and he breathed until his hands stopped shaking. Sera read over his shoulder—she had been reading over his shoulder, he realized, and he found he did not mind. There was no longer any part of this he could carry alone.

He picked the journal up again.

*It will spare one son. One. It says the binding requires a life's shape to close around, a whole existence to feed into the knot, and it will take whichever I choose. It prefers Aurelian—the heir, the crown, the richer meal. It says if I give it Aurelian, it will leave Isandro whole, unmarked, free to live and never know what he lost. It says this as though it were mercy. It is not mercy. It is a merchant showing me both goods and asking me to pick which child I can bear to sell.*

*I told it no.*

*I told it no, and it laughed with my husband's mouth, and it said: then I will take both, and the dynasty, and the gods' silence will be forever, and no one will ever have been here to mourn.*

*So I made my own bargain. I am the queen of Vhalen and the daughter of a storm-caller, and I know the old arithmetic better than any hungry thing from before the world. The binding wants a whole life. Very well. But a life can be divided. A river can be split around a stone.*

*I will not feed it one son. I will feed it half of each.*

He read that line three times. The candle flames leaned toward the page, as if they too were reading.

*Two rituals, one knot. Aurelian I will bind away from the gods—from every prayer, every miracle, every divine eye—so the thing wearing the crown will starve on him, find nothing divine to eat. He will sit on the throne and be a glove with no hand in it, and the parasite will keep him, because a starving beast guards its larder. And Isandro I will bind away from the palace, from the blood, from the memory—from every mind that might look at him and see a prince. The valley of Stillness has stood empty since the old wars; its stillness will hold him like water holds a stone. Each boy hidden from half the world. Each boy half-erased. And the knot that holds the parasite will be woven from the seam between them—two halves that were never meant to be separate, strained apart forever. The tension itself will be the lock.*

*It will not know. It thinks it has won a whole kingdom and silenced the sky. It will wear my son like a glove and feel nothing inside the fingers, and it will not understand why it is always hungry.*

*And my sons will not remember each other. That is the price I cannot soften. For the binding to hold, they must not remember. Not each other, not me, not what was taken. If either of them remembers, the seam tears, and the thing gets out, and everything I have done becomes the instrument of its freedom.*

*So I write this and then I will forget it, and that is the last cruelty. I am about to unmake my own motherhood. I am holding my sons' whole childhood in my hands and I must burn it to keep them alive.*

*If anyone ever reads this—if you, whoever you are, one of my sons, if it is one of my sons—then the binding has failed or is failing, and you are reading this in the last hour of the world or the first. I am sorry. I am so sorry. I would do it again. That is the worst thing I know about myself and I am leaving it here for you to find: I would do it again, and I would not hesitate, because you were both breathing, and I could not choose.*

*But you can choose. That is the part no one will have told you. The binding can be undone, and it can be undone in either direction, and it cannot be undone in both.*

*Unbind Aurelian, and the crown wakes, and the king returns, and everything the binding took from him comes back—except what was given to the other knot. The other knot stays. The other knot holds. Someone must sit in the stillness forever so that the king can stand in the storm. Unbind Isandro instead, and the same is true in reverse.*

*One of you, or the other. The knot must be re-made with someone inside it. That is the arithmetic. I did not write it. I only had to live it, and now, so must you.*

*Forgive me. Choose with love. It is the only thing I know that the hunger cannot eat.*

*—Liora, Queen of Vhalen, in the last days of the world that remembered her*

The last candle guttered out on its own, as if the room had finished speaking.

* * *

Isandro did not know how long he stood there. The window had gone from night to the gray bruise of false dawn. Sera had read the journal twice more while he stood unmoving; he could tell by the way she had turned the pages, by the small sounds she made—not sobs, nothing so generous as sobs, just the involuntary noises of a body absorbing a blow.

"Say it," he said finally. His voice sounded scraped. "Say it out loud. I need to hear it in someone else's voice, because in mine it doesn't sound real."

She closed the journal gently, as though it were sleeping. "The unbinding can restore the king. Or it can restore you. Not both. One of you stays in the knot—stays bound, stays hidden, stays *half*—forever. And whoever performs the rite—" she hesitated.

"Whoever performs it pays with their own bound memories. Vess told me that much. Everything the binding took comes back to the performer." He laughed, and it was an ugly sound in that beautiful dead room. "So if I do it—if I unbind Aurelian—I get back fifteen years of a stolen life I don't remember, a mother, a childhood, a *brother*. I get everything. And he gets a crown and a kingdom and a stranger who saved him, and I go back into the stillness. Into a valley, or a knot, or whatever shape the re-made binding takes. And he will never know me. He *can't* know me. If he remembers me, the seam tears."

"And if we unbind you instead—"

"Then I remember. All of it. And he stays a glove with no hand in it, and the thing keeps wearing him, and Vhalen keeps a king who is a larder." He turned from the window. "Sera. The free cities are marching to die for a king who can be freed. The council swore because I swore. Maren is hunting us because she half-believes the king can be woken. Every road between here and the sea is full of people betting their lives on Aurelian. And the arithmetic says I can buy him with myself, or I can buy myself with him, and there is no third line in the ledger."

Sera set the journal down and crossed to him and took his face in both hands, and her eyes were fierce and wet and absolutely unyielding.

"Your mother split a whole in half rather than choose," she said. "She wrote that she could not choose, and then she found a way not to. Fifteen years ago, a woman with no army and one dying god refused the arithmetic. And you're standing in her rooms telling me there's no third line in the ledger." Her thumb brushed his cheekbone. "I watched my whole faith burn because everyone in my temple believed the silence was permanent. I will not watch you believe this is."

"The journal is her hand, Sera. It's not a riddle. It's a will."

"It's a will *and* it's a door." She turned back to the desk, pulling the journal toward her, flipping to the final pages with sudden urgency. "She split a binding in half once. She wove a lock out of the seam between two brothers. A woman who could do *that*—who could divide a river around a stone—she would not leave her sons a choice with only one cruel answer. She'd have hidden something. She'd have—" Her finger stopped on the page. On the last page, beneath the signature, there was a line he had taken for a smudge, a stray mark of ink.

It was not a smudge. In the candle-light, in Sera's steadying grip, the mark resolved into the old high script—three words, small as a secret, pressed so lightly into the paper that only someone looking for a hidden thing would ever find them:

*Ask the hermit's daughter.*

Isandro felt the floor tilt under him, or perhaps it was only fifteen years of certainty finally giving way.

"Sera," he said slowly, "the lighthouse hermit—the one who's going to teach me the storms—he told me he once knew my mother."

Sera looked up at him, and outside, far to the east, the first thunder of the besieging army's war-drums rolled across the hills like the sky clearing its throat.

"Then we know where the third line is," she said. "And we have about as much time to find it as your mother had to write this."

Behind them, in the cradle in the corner, something that had been waiting fifteen years in the shape of a folded blanket stirred—just once, softly, like a child turning in its sleep—and went still.
