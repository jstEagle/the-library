## 29. Sera Finds Out

The queen's chambers smelled of dust and roses that had died fifteen years ago.

Sera stood in the doorway while Isandro knelt at the writing desk, his shoulders curved around a leather-bound journal like a man sheltering a wound. Candlelight moved across the sealed room—the shutters had not been opened since Queen Liora's last day, and the air held the particular stillness of a place where grief had been the last occupant. A hairbrush still lay on the vanity, silver-backed, with two long hairs caught in its bristles. A child's wooden bird perched on the windowsill, its paint faded to the ghost of blue.

Isandro had been reading for an hour. He had not turned a page in twenty minutes.

Sera knew what avoidance looked like. She had spent eleven years watching the faithful avoid the truth of their empty temples—smiling too quickly, praying louder as the silence deepened, insisting the gods were only resting, only testing, only waiting. She knew every posture of a person holding something terrible very carefully so it would not spill.

"Isandro." She kept her voice gentle. "The others will be back within the hour. Maren wants to move before the night watch changes."

He didn't answer. His thumb was pressed flat against the page, hard enough that she could see the whiteness of the nail even from the door.

She crossed the room. The floorboards did not creak; even the palace's bones had learned to be quiet here. When she reached the desk and looked down over his shoulder, he flinched—a whole-body flinch, the kind a man gives when touched by someone he has forgotten how to be touched by—and his hand came up too late to cover the page.

Too late.

Sera read.

*The parasite came to me in the form of mercy,* the queen had written, in a hand that started elegant and grew progressively less controlled as the entries went on. *It said: give me one son entire, and I will let the other live whole. It said this as if it were generosity. As if I were choosing which child to save rather than which child to erase.*

Sera's eyes went down the page. The words seemed to darken as she read them, though it was only candlelight and her own pulse.

*I refused. So we split the binding instead. One twin hidden from the world of men. One twin hidden from himself. The ritual can be completed either way: restore the king, or restore the brother. Not both. The anchor must hold one soul entire.*

Below that, in a hand gone nearly illegible:

*If you are reading this, my son, then I am sorry. I am sorry for the arithmetic I forced upon your life. But know that whichever of you stands here now, I loved you both with the whole of a heart that was never permitted to be divided, whatever the ritual demanded of it.*

And below *that*, in fresh ink, in Isandro's handwriting, pressed so hard into the paper it had nearly torn through:

*I choose him. I have always chosen him. Let the anchor take me.*

The room went very quiet. Somewhere far below them, the siege drums had begun—not the assault yet, only the testing rhythm, the army telling the city it was patient.

"You wrote that," Sera said. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears. Flat. "Just now. You read the journal and you wrote your decision, and you were going to fold this book closed and carry it to the binding chamber and never say a word."

"Sera—"

"When?" She heard her own voice rise and could not stop it. "When were you going to tell me? At the spire? After? Were you going to stand on top of Ashkarat and negotiate with a century-storm and come down into the dark and step into that thing and let me find out afterward, when I went looking for you and found only—" Her breath caught. "Found only weather."

"I was going to tell you."

"No." She stepped back from him, and the distance felt like a country crossing. "No, you weren't. You were going to manage me. You've been managing me since Corvane. Since Vess gave you the words of unbinding and you went quiet for a full day and told me it was nothing. You've known the price since the ford—I asked you, Isandro, I begged you at the ford, bleeding, with an arrow-graze across your ribs, and you lied to my face with rain running down it."

"It wasn't a lie. It was a deferral."

"A deferral." She laughed, and the laugh came out broken, and she hated it. "You sound like a temple accountant. You sound like the elders who burned our library and called it *prudence*."

He rose from the desk. He was taller than her, broader, and usually that meant nothing between them, but tonight it felt like a wall he was building in real time. "What would you have had me do? Tell you, and watch you try to talk me out of it? Tell you, and spend the last days I have left arguing? There is a war outside these walls. There is a thing beneath us wearing my brother like a glove. Someone has to go into that chamber and hold the anchor, and there is exactly one person whose blood fits the lock, and that person is me."

"And you decided that alone. In a dead woman's room. With a dead woman's pen." Sera's hands were shaking. She looked down at them, surprised, as if they belonged to someone else—some other priestess, some other life. "Do you know what I did for eleven years, Isandro? Do you know what my whole ordination was?"

He said nothing. Outside, the siege drums kept their patient time.

"I watched people die for faith," she said. "That was my vocation. Not miracles—Vess never once answered me, not in thirty years of prayer, not until the valley broke open. My vocation was standing beside people who believed the gods wanted their deaths. Pilgrims who walked into the sea at Saltmarsh because a voice told them the tide-god was hungry. Mothers who gave up sons to the temple because the old rites demanded a tithe of blood. I held their hands. I told them their sacrifice was holy. And every single time, some small honest part of me screamed that it was a lie—that faith should not require the faithful to burn, that any god who demands a life is either starving or lying."

"Then you understand why—"

"*No.*" The word cracked out of her like the ice on Stillwater's river. "You don't get to use my grief as permission. That is exactly what I'm saying. You are doing what they all did. You have found a theology of one—you have made yourself the tithe, and you have dressed it up in your brother's name so it looks like love instead of suicide, and you expect me to stand in the binding chamber and conduct the rite with clean hands and call it holy."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The candle guttered. Down in the city, a bell began to ring—not alarm, just the hour, indifferent as water.

"I watched my mother do it too," Sera said, quieter now. The fury was draining out of her and leaving something worse behind, something rawer. "Did I ever tell you? Before the temple took me. She gave herself to the plague-shrines when the gray fever came through our district. Walked in healthy, walked out in a shroud in nine days. Everyone said how brave. Everyone said how holy. I was seven. I stood at the pyre and I thought: *she chose the god over me, and everyone clapped.*"

She looked up at him. "I will not clap for you, Isandro Almeida. I will not stand in that chamber and conduct your funeral and call it a coronation. If you step into that anchor, you do it over my objection, with my curse in your ears, and I will spend the rest of my life knowing I failed to stop you the way my whole order failed to stop every pilgrim who ever walked into the sea."

"Sera." His voice had gone rough. "There is no other way. The journal says—"

"The journal says *either or*. The journal says *one soul entire*." She crossed to the desk, snatched up the book, and turned pages with fingers that trembled but did not fumble. She had read half of it in those first stolen seconds over his shoulder, and grief had made her fast. "But queens do not write journals for one reader. Listen. Here—Liora again, three entries later:"

*The binding was built for one because the parasite is greedy—it cannot share a meal. What it cannot devour wholly, it cannot digest. Two souls braided at the anchor would choke it. This is theory. The original rite-masters feared it. They feared it because it might fail, and because it might work.*

"There." Sera set the book down between them like a card laid on a table. "A shared binding. Two anchors. Braided souls. The thing beneath us eats whole—devours, consumes, takes everything into itself. It has eaten gods, Isandro. It ate the silence itself. But it has never once in its long hunger had to share. Feed it two lives tangled so tightly it cannot swallow one without swallowing the other, and it chokes. Or it fails entirely. Either way, no one is erased."

"That's theory," Isandro said. "Written by a desperate woman who was guessing."

"Yes." Sera smiled, and there was nothing kind in it. "Welcome to faith. Everything worth doing is a desperate guess written down by someone with no better option. I have eleven years of professional experience distinguishing between the guesses that are madness and the guesses that are prayer, and I am telling you this one is prayer."

"It could kill us both."

"It could. So could the siege. So could Maren's former colleagues, who are hunting her through the palace corridors as we speak. So could the storm you intend to negotiate with on that spire—do you think the century-storm cares about your plans? Everything we do from this hour forward could kill us both. The only question is whether we die *separate* or *together*." She drew a breath. "And I know which I choose."

He was silent. The candle flame steadied, as if the room itself were listening.

"You planned to leave me," Sera said. "Not cruelly. You'd have said it was kindness. You'd have called it sparing me. But you planned to walk out of my life into that knot of devoured god-stuff and let me conduct the rite that ended you, and you thought I would thank you for it afterward, in whatever years I had left, the way the temple taught me to thank every martyr." She shook her head slowly. "I stopped thanking martyrs a long time ago. I started burying them."

"Sera—"

"Love stated plainly, then, since subtlety has failed us both." She took his face in her hands. His skin was cold from hours in the sealed room; his eyes were storm-colored and exhausted and afraid, more afraid than she had ever seen them, afraid not of the parasite or the siege or the dying but of being seen this clearly. "I love you. Not the way the faithful love their gods, from a kneeling distance. Not the way martyrs love their causes, from the far side of a pyre. I love you the way a person loves another person—with hands and temper and terrible timing, in sieges and forgeries and river crossings, in the specific weight of your shoulder against mine on the road to Delmare. And I will not perform your sacrifice. I will share it, or I will fight you in front of Maren and the wardens and the old gods themselves."

His throat worked. For a moment she thought he would argue again—he was a man who had spent fifteen years learning to carry things alone, and fifteen years is long enough to make solitude feel like virtue.

Then his forehead came down against hers.

"The rite-masters feared it," he said at last, barely above a whisper. "If it works, we're bound together for whatever remains of our lives. Every residue, every echo, every voice of every returning god—we'd feel all of it, forever, each other always in the edge of the mind. No privacy. No ordinary life. Never."

"I've had an ordinary life," Sera said. "It was called the temple, and it burned."

A wet, disbelieving sound escaped him—half laugh, half sob. His arms came around her, and she felt the trembling in him that he would never have allowed her to see an hour ago, the fear he had been carrying alone since the ford, since Corvane, maybe since the valley.

"If it fails," he murmured into her hair, "we both go into that thing. Together."

"Together," she agreed. "And if it works, we come out diminished and entangled and unable to live ordinary lives, and we grow old feeling ghosts at the edge of every prayer."

"Doesn't sound like much of a bargain."

"No," she said. "It sounds like a marriage. Those are always terrible bargains. People make them anyway."

He laughed then, really laughed, brief and cracked and human, and she held onto the sound of it because the hours ahead would not contain many such sounds.

Outside, the siege drums changed rhythm—from patience to preparation. Below them, deep beneath the throne room, something ancient shifted in its sleep and tasted the air, tasting storm and candle-smoke and two hearts beating in one small sealed room, and began, slowly, to wake.

Sera lifted her head from Isandro's shoulder and looked toward the door, where torchlight was climbing the corridor wall.

"Maren's back," she said. "And we have a rite to rewrite."
