## 30. Maren Turns

The petition had been denied in eleven words.

*Access to His Majesty's person is not granted at this time.*

Eleven words, written in the Regent's own hand, delivered by a chamberlain who would not meet her eyes. Inquisitor-General Maren had read a thousand denials in her career—denials of clemency, denials of appeal, denials from frightened officials trying to hide incompetence behind formality—and she knew every species of them the way a falconer knows the moods of birds. This one was different. This one was afraid.

She stood in the antechamber of the eastern wing for a long moment after the chamberlain withdrew, the folded paper crackling slightly in her grip because her hand would not quite relax. Around her, the palace of Ashkarat went about its evening: servants with candles, guards changing at the hour-markers, the distant groan of siege preparations echoing up through the floors like the palace itself was clearing its throat. Outside the high windows, the sky over the free cities' road glowed faintly with cookfires and war-camps, and above it all the clouds had begun to gather—the first true storm of the season, or perhaps something more than a season's worth.

She had served the crown for twenty-two years. She had burned temples when the law required it and spared them when mercy served order better. She had interrogated priests until they wept and executed nobles whose only crime was ambition, and she had slept soundly after each of it, because every act had been *procedural*, every cruelty a load-bearing wall in the architecture of peace. Order was not a comfort to her. It was a discipline, the way prayer was a discipline to the faithful, and she had never once needed it to feel good in order to believe it was right.

But eleven words.

Three times now she had petitioned to verify the king's condition directly. Three times denied. And the reasons given were worse than no reason at all: that His Majesty's health was delicate; that his audiences were suspended for his protection; that the Inquisitor-General's duties lay elsewhere. Elsewhere. As if hunting a storm-caller across half of Vhalen were a chore to be shuffled between clerks.

And beneath all of it, the thing she had been refusing to say aloud even to herself: the Regent did not fear rebellion. She had seen fear of rebellion before—it looked like doubled guard rotations and grain hoarding and nervous edicts. This was something else. The Regent feared *remembrance*. Every order that had crossed her desk in the last month pointed the same direction: burn the chapel records again, double-check the archives, seize the genealogical rolls from Delmare, silence anyone who spoke the old gods' names in public—not because worship bred sedition, but because worship made people *remember what praying felt like*.

Maren folded the denial into her breast pocket, beside the others, and walked back toward her offices without deciding anything at all.

That was the trick of it, she told herself on the stairs. She wasn't deciding anything. She was only going to walk past the king's wing on her way to bed. There was nothing improper in walking through one's own palace.

* * *

The eastern wing at night was a held breath.

Two sentries at the first landing, both of them hers once, both of them reassigned three months ago to the Regent's personal detail—a reassignment she had approved herself, without thinking twice, and which now sat in her memory like a stone swallowed wrong. They saluted. She returned it. Neither asked why the Inquisitor-General was walking the royal corridor at the hour of deep bells, and their not-asking told her everything: they had been instructed not to see things. The whole wing had been instructed not to see things.

The doors to the royal apartments were oak, iron-banded, older than the dynasty that slept—or did not sleep—behind them. She had passed them a hundred times in her career and never once thought about what happened on the other side. Kings were ill sometimes. Kings were private. The machinery of state ran whether the king turned its gears or not, and Maren had built her life on trusting the machinery.

She stopped before the doors and listened.

There was a sound. Faint. Rhythmic. Not snoring—she knew what sleeping royalty sounded like, had guarded enough of them in her years as a young officer. This was wetter. Slower. Like something breathing through cloth, or through water, or through a throat that was not entirely its own.

Her hand went to the keys at her belt before her mind authorized it. Twenty-two years of service bought certain inheritances: the master ring, the emergency set, the keys that opened what protocol said should never be opened except in extremity. Her fingers found the oldest one, black iron, unmarked, and she stood there holding it against the lock without turning it, feeling like a woman standing at the edge of a frozen lake, testing the ice with one foot.

If she opened this door and found a sick old man asleep, she would have broken faith for nothing, and she would resign in the morning, and order would be preserved.

If she opened it and found something else—

The breathing hitched. Then resumed. Deeper. Hungrier.

Maren turned the key.

* * *

The antechamber was cold in a way that had nothing to do with the season. Candles burned in every sconce—dozens of them, more than any household needed—and yet the light seemed to fall *into* the room rather than fill it, as though the walls drank it. The furniture was shrouded in white sheets. Dust lay thick on everything except a single path through the center of the floor, worn clean by footsteps, leading toward the inner bedchamber.

She followed it. Her boots made no sound on the stone, and she could not decide whether that was her own training or the room itself declining to echo.

The bedchamber door stood open.

King Aurelian of Vhalen sat upright upon his throne-bed, crowned, robed in full regalia at midnight, facing the window. The storm clouds beyond the glass had thickened while she climbed the stairs; lightning flickered somewhere far off over the hills, and each flash lit the room for a heartbeat, and in those heartbeats Maren saw what she saw, and the rest of her life divided cleanly along that seam.

The king's face was wrong.

Not aged, not ill, not mad—*wrong*, the way a mask is wrong when you realize it has no face behind it. The skin moved too smoothly over the skull. The eyes were open and fixed on the distant storm, and they were the color of deep well-water, and they did not blink, and around their edges something dark shifted like oil under ice. His mouth hung slightly open. From it, from his very breath, came threads of pale luminescence—thin as spider silk, drifting upward, coiling toward the ceiling where they gathered in a slow-turning haze that pulsed with each of the king's long, drowning breaths.

He was being fed upon. He was being fed upon *from inside*, and whatever remained of Aurelian sat behind those well-water eyes like a man locked in a cellar beneath his own house, listening to strangers walk across his floors.

Maren had interrogated heretics who claimed the gods were real. She had burned men who swore they had seen angels, and women who wept that they had heard the dead. She had catalogued every variety of human delusion and found them all, in the end, to be fear wearing costume. She had believed—had built her entire self on believing—that the world was matter and law and procedure, and that miracle was simply a name for what hadn't been audited yet.

Nothing in her catalogue covered this.

The king's head began to turn.

It turned slowly, joint by joint, with a sound like rope tightening, and when the well-water eyes found her standing in the doorway with a black key still in her fist, the thing behind them *looked at her*. Not with curiosity. With appetite taking inventory. She felt it move across her the way a butcher's thumb moves across a carcass, weighing cuts.

Then, from somewhere impossibly far down inside that face, a voice—not spoken so much as surfaced, like a drowning man's hand breaking water:

"*Run.*"

Lightning flashed. In the white instant, the king's expression was human—terribly, briefly human, young and terrified and *familiar* in some way she couldn't name—and then the dark closed over it again, and the mouth curved into a smile that belonged to no living thing, and the voice that came out was layered, many-throated, patient as erosion:

"The Inquisitor-General honors us. Come in, come in. We have been meaning to speak with you about the storm-caller."

Maren did not remember leaving. She remembered the stairs, and the night air hitting her face like a slap, and finding herself in the courtyard with her hands shaking so badly she had to press them flat against her thighs to make them stop. Above the palace, the distant storm grumbled over the hills, and for the first time in her life the sound of thunder did not seem like chaos to her.

It sounded like an answer.

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat in her office with the lamps unlit and let the truth assemble itself piece by piece, the way she had once assembled cases, because method was the last thing she owned that still worked.

Everything she had served was appetite. The silencing of the gods fifteen years ago—not decay, not abandonment, but *consumption*: the thing in the king had eaten them, had drunk the prayers of a generation and left the world dry. The purges of the god-touched—not security, but husbandry. You cull the herd's strays so the herd doesn't notice what's missing. The orders to take the storm-caller alive—not justice, but *hunger*. A creature that fed on divinity had smelled a mortal who could call the sky, and wanted him brought close.

And the Regent—she had met the Regent a hundred times, had taken orders from that smooth, courteous face, and now she understood that she had never once met the Regent at all. There was no Regent. There was a glove, and something wore it.

Twenty-two years. Burned temples. Broken lives. A lifetime of discipline poured like water into sand, and the sand had been hungry all along.

She laughed once, quietly, in the dark office, and the laugh had no humor in it at all. It was the sound of a foundation cracking.

What broke in her was not faith—she had none to break. What broke was simpler and more total: the belief that the system she served contained anything worth serving. She had always known individuals were corrupt. She had built her career on catching them. But she had believed the *structure* was sound, that above the rot there was a crown, and above the crown a principle, and that if she climbed high enough she would find someone, something, worth the climbing.

She had climbed. At the top there was a mouth.

Dawn came gray through the shutters. When it did, Maren rose, washed her face, dressed in her plainest uniform, and began, with the same procedural calm she had brought to every task of her life, to commit treason.

* * *

They took Sera from the Tide Gate cells two nights later—Maren learned of it from the garrison reports, and understood immediately what it meant: the storm-caller was loose, alone, oath-bound, and heading for Ashkarat with nothing left to lose. The Regent's orders sharpened accordingly. Bring the caller alive. Bring the priestess alive. Kill everything else that had seen either of their faces.

Maren spent a day compiling what she carried. Not in her offices—the Regent's spies watched her now, she was sure of it; she had been summoned twice for conversations that were really audits. She compiled it in fragments, in her head, in cipher, written out only once, late, by candlelight in a rented room near the river gate:

The palace keys. All of them, including the black iron and the older set below it—the tunnel keys, the ones that opened the service ways dug before the dynasty, the ones the Regent's people didn't know she possessed because they'd been inherited, not issued.

The troop dispositions. Where the garrisons stood, where they'd be thinned when the siege lines tightened, which gates would be held and which sacrificed, the rotation schedules of the palace guard down to the quarter-hour. She wrote it all in the flat, exact hand that had filled twenty-two years of reports, and she noted, with a bitter precision that surprised even her, exactly which units still answered to the crown rather than the Regent—which captains still trained their men in the old oaths, which sergeants still wore the king's badge under their collars where the Regent's inspectors couldn't see. There were more of them than the thing in the palace knew. Loyalty, it turned out, was harder to eat than gods.

And one page more. A single sheet, sealed separately, containing the only thing she would ask in return.

* * *

Finding Isandro Almeida was easier than it should have been, and that frightened her more than the thing on the throne had. A man who called lightning should be hiding in cellars. Instead, the whispers led her upward and outward—to a lighthouse on the sea road, to rumors of a blind hermit, to fishermen who spoke of storms that bent around a single point on the coast like water around a stone. And then, on the third night, in the wreck of a wayshrine outside Blackreach where refugees huddled against the coming frost, she heard it: thunder where no storm front stood, precise as a hammerfall, and the crowd pressing back from a tall figure in a traveler's cloak who stood in the shrine yard with rain beginning to gather out of a clear sky around him like a congregation.

Maren walked into the rain.

Every instinct she had honed in two decades of hunting told her this was suicide. She was unarmed—she had left her sword in her rooms, deliberately, because she knew herself well enough to know she might need the excuse to use it. She walked with her hands visible and her head bare and the satchel of treason heavy against her ribs, and the rain thickened around her with every step, and she felt it watching her, the weather itself, weighing her the way the thing in the king had weighed her, and finding her, apparently, acceptable.

Isandro Almeida turned to face her when she was ten paces out.

He was younger than the reports suggested, and more tired. Rain ran off his jaw. Behind him, a defrocked priestess—Sera, alive, thinner than her wanted posters, with a fresh scar at her hairline—stepped halfway in front of him with a knife already in her hand, and Maren noted with detached professional approval that she held it correctly.

"Inquisitor-General Maren," Isandro said. His voice was quiet under the rain. "You've been following me since Corvane."

"I have."

"You killed my friend's people. You burned the temple in Delmare's lower city with forty god-touched inside it."

"I gave the order," Maren said. "I did not light the fires, but I gave the order, and I will not insult you by pretending otherwise." She knelt then, slowly, in the mud of the shrine yard, in the rain, in full view of the refugees and the priestess's knife and the storm gathering in the young man's eyes, and she set the satchel on the ground between them and opened it.

"Keys," she said. "Tunnel keys, palace keys, the black iron that opens the king's own door—I have stood on the other side of it, Almeida. I have seen what wears him." Her voice did not shake. She had drilled it steady the way soldiers drill a line, and it held. "Troop dispositions. Gate rotations. The names of every officer in Ashkarat who still serves the crown instead of the thing on the throne. Everything you need to get inside, and everything you need to survive once you're there."

Sera had come closer, circling, never quite lowering the knife. "Why?" she said. "People don't do this. Not your kind. What do you want—pardon? Position? The council won't—"

"Nothing." Maren looked up at her, rain streaming off her chin. "Not pardon. Not position. I have read your movement's pamphlets, priestess—they want the old gods back, and I understand why, and I am not asking to be forgiven for helping burn them out of the world the first time." She reached into the satchel one last time and drew out the single sealed page, and held it up between two fingers, offering it. "One thing. That is all. When this is finished—whatever is finished, whoever sits the throne, whatever gods answer or don't—this gets recorded. Written into the histories, the trials, the chronicles, whatever survives. Every order I gave. Every fire. Every name. Mine above all of them. No pardons, no expungement, no gentle phrasing. Let it be written down that Maren of the Inquisition did these things, and let no one ever again be able to say it was impossible, that no one knew, that order could not become this." Her hand, at last, was not quite steady. "I served appetite for twenty-two years and called it law. Record it. That is my price. That is my only price."

The rain fell. Somewhere over the sea, thunder rolled, long and low, and Isandro glanced toward it the way a man glances toward a voice calling his name.

He took the page. He read the seal, and then he looked at her kneeling in the mud—at the most feared hunter in Vhalen, soaked and unarmed and offering her life's work like a confession—and something in his face shifted, some judgment postponed rather than granted.

"If we win," he said, "you'll stand trial with the rest of them."

"Yes."

"And if we lose, you'll die in the palace with us."

"Yes," Maren said. "I assumed that was the arrangement."

Sera laughed—a short, disbelieving bark, quickly bitten off—and lowered the knife at last. "Gods help me," she muttered, "the heretics are everywhere."

Isandro extended his hand and pulled the Inquisitor-General of Vhalen to her feet. Her joints ached from the cold ground; her satchel was empty; she felt lighter than she had in twenty-two years, and more damned, and both sensations fit her like a uniform cut to measure.

Above the coast road, the storm was building—great gray towers of cloud stacking themselves against the horizon, marching inland toward Ashkarat with a purposefulness that no natural weather had ever shown. Isandro watched it, and Maren watched him watch it, and she understood suddenly that she had not merely changed sides tonight.

She had walked out of a burning house and discovered the fire had an owner.

"We move at dawn," Isandro said, turning back to her, rain still beading in his hair, lightning flickering far off like a signal only he could read. "Through the tunnels, straight under the walls. You'll lead us."

"I will," Maren said.

And far away, in the palace on its hill, in a shrouded room full of drinking candles, something ancient lifted its head from its feeding and tasted, on the edge of the coming storm, the unmistakable flavor of a servant who had stopped belonging to it.
