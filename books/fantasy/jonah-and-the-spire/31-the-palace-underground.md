## 31. The Palace Underground

The keys Maren carried made no sound.

Isandro noticed that first, before the torchlight, before the smell of cold stone and older things. A ring of iron and brass and one key of black glass, and yet when she lifted them from her belt they hung silent against her hip, as if even metal had learned to hold its breath in this place. She had been an inquisitor of the Regent's court for eleven years. Her keys had opened every door a person could be locked behind. They had never once been permitted to jingle.

"Three turns below the throne room," she said, not looking back at him. "The stairs are cut from the mountain itself. The palace was built on top of them like a lid."

Sera walked between them with her hood down—Maren had insisted on that, said hoods read as guilt in corridors where guilt was currency—and her hands were steady around the unlit candle stub she carried. Not for light. For Vess. The god rode in her pocket now as a pinch of ash wrapped in waxed linen, small enough to lose, precious enough to end everything if they lost it.

"You've been down here," Isandro said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Once." Maren's voice stayed flat, professional, the voice of a woman reading charges aloud. "In my first year. I escorted an archivist who was cataloguing the Regent's seals. We were given four minutes in the antechamber and told we had seen all there was to see." A pause. "There was more to see. I have spent ten years pretending I didn't know it."

"And now?"

"Now I am done pretending." She stopped at a door of blackened oak banded with bronze, and fit the black glass key into a lock that had no visible mechanism. "Now I am a traitor, and the truth is the only thing I have left to carry."

The lock did not turn so much as *yield*, like a jaw unclenching after years of being set. Beyond it, stairs went down into dark that swallowed their torchlight three steps at a time.

* * *

They descended for what felt like an hour. The walls changed around them as they went—the dressed stone of the palace giving way to rougher blocks, then to stone that had never been blocks at all but had been *shaped*, flowed like taffy by hands or forces that predated masonry. Old carvings surfaced in the torchlight and sank again behind them: spirals, storms, figures with raised arms whose faces had been deliberately smoothed blank. Sera touched one as they passed and pulled her fingers back as though burned.

"These are prayer-figures," she whispered. "From before the Silence. This is how the old gods were called—the worshipper's face erased so the god could look through it instead of at it."

"Then someone built this place to be looked through," Isandro said.

"No one built it," Maren said quietly. "That is what the archivist understood in his four minutes, and what got him reassigned to a coastal parish within the month. The palace was built *around* this. Whatever is below us was here first."

The air grew warmer. That was wrong, Isandro knew—it should have grown colder, deeper, further from sun and hearth—but the warmth was there, faint and wet, like breath on the back of the neck. And beneath it, so low he felt it in his teeth rather than heard it, a pulse.

He stopped on the stairs.

Sera bumped into him. "What—"

"You feel it too?" he asked her.

Her eyes had gone wide in the torchlight. "It's like standing next to a heart."

"It is a heart," Maren said. "Or what's left of one. Keep moving. The guards change at the bottom of the hour, and I would rather not explain why three people are walking toward the thing the Regent has killed men for mentioning."

* * *

The binding chamber announced itself first by its door—or rather, by the absence of one. Where the stair ended, the corridor simply opened into a cavernous round room, and Isandro understood in a single vertiginous glance that the room had not been carved out of the mountain; the mountain had been persuaded to open here, long ago, the way a mouth opens.

Torchlight reached perhaps thirty feet and gave up. Beyond that, the darkness had texture. It moved.

At the center of the room, suspended in nothing—a cradle of pale light that came from no source he could name—hung the anchor.

It was smaller than he had expected. That was the horror of it, he realized later; he had braced himself for something vast, a leviathan pinned mid-thrash, and instead it was hardly larger than a man's head. A knot. A tangled knot of matter that was not matter, woven from strands of grey and gold and a color he had no word for, the color of the moment before lightning strikes, and it turned slowly on itself the way a sleeping animal turns toward warmth. With each turn, it pulsed. With each pulse, the whole room breathed.

And it was *eating*. Even now, even diminished, even bound, he could see the fine filaments trailing off it into the dark like root-hairs, and where they touched the shadows, the shadows dimmed a little more. It had devoured the old gods' silence, Vess had told him, drunk fifteen years of unanswered prayer down like wine. It was still drinking. There was very little left to drink, and it drank anyway, patiently, because patience was the only appetite it had ever needed.

"That's it," Sera breathed. "That's what's wearing him."

"That's what's wearing *everything*," Maren said. "Look up."

Isandro looked. The ceiling of the cavern was veined with the same filaments, hundreds of them, thousands, running upward through the stone like the roots of an inverted tree—and he understood with a lurch that they ran up through the throne room floor, through the throne itself, into the man who sat upon it. Fifteen years of feeding. Fifteen years of growing into the shape of a king the way ivy grows into the shape of a wall, until you could not say where the wall ended and the ivy began.

"The unbinding cuts the knot," Sera said, and her voice shook only slightly. "Vess gave me the words. Cut the knot, and everything it took comes back—the gods' voices, the king's crown, the memories the binding ate."

"And everything it *is*," Maren said, "goes somewhere. That is the part your god did not emphasize, priestess. I have read the inquisition's oldest files. The ritual was performed once before, fifteen years ago, by people who thought they were saving two princes. Something slipped in during the cutting. Ask yourself what else might slip in during ours."

"I ask myself every hour," Sera said. "I do it anyway."

Before Maren could answer, a sound came from the corridor behind them—the measured ring of boots on shaped stone—and torchlight bloomed at the chamber's mouth.

Guards. Six of them, in the Regent's black and silver, halberds grounded, faces hidden behind visors that reflected the pale cradle-light back at them in fragments. Their sergeant stepped forward, a broad woman with a scarred jaw, and her voice came out flat and rehearsed.

"This chamber is sealed by order of the Regent. None may enter. Inquisitor-General Maren—" a flicker of surprise in the flatness, quickly mastered, "—your presence here is outside your warrant. Withdraw, all of you, and this will be reported as an inspection."

Maren did not move. "Sergeant. Your name."

"Halvard. Third Company, Crown Guard."

"Crown Guard," Maren repeated, and let the words hang. "Not Regent's Guard. Crown. Do you know the difference, Sergeant Halvard?"

The silence stretched. Isandro watched the woman's visor tilt, fractionally, toward the pulsing knot of light at the center of the room, and something in the set of her shoulders changed—he had spent fifteen years learning to read stillness, and he recognized the tremor beneath it.

"Every soldier swears two oaths," Maren said, walking forward slowly, hands open and empty. "One to the crown, and one to whoever holds it. When those are the same oath, life is simple. But they are not the same now. Look at what sits in this room. Look at what it has been *doing* while you stood post above it, guarding the door of its own prison from the inside." She stopped an arm's length from the sergeant. "You have felt it. Don't lie to me—I taught half your officers how to detect a lie. You've felt the dreams. The hunger that isn't yours. The way you sometimes wake with the taste of smoke and can't remember any fire."

The sergeant's gauntlets creaked around her halberd shaft.

"I am asking you," Maren said, quieter now, "whom you truly serve. Not whom you were ordered to serve. Whom. Remember."

For a long moment nothing moved except the knot, turning, pulsing, patient.

Then Sergeant Halvard did something no soldier of the Regent's guard had done in fifteen years, as far as Isandro knew. She turned her head—not to Maren, not to the door, but to the pale cradle at the center of the room—and she bowed. Shallow, stiff, almost involuntary, the bow of a woman remembering a gesture her body had learned in another life. An oath to a crown, not a wearer.

"Stand aside," she said to her five soldiers.

Two of them obeyed immediately. One hesitated, and Isandro saw the war happen behind his visor—saw the moment the memory won, whatever dim, buried, half-eaten memory it was, some grandfather's story of a coronation, some childhood prayer to a god whose name had been scraped from the temples. He grounded his halberd and stepped back with the others. The last soldier, the youngest, stood frozen, and it was Sera who moved to him—Sera, defrocked and branded, who put her hand flat on his breastplate over his heart and said, gently, "You swore to protect the king. He is down here. He has been down here for fifteen years. Let us go to him."

The young soldier's breath came out ragged behind the visor. He stepped aside.

"Third Company will hold this stair," Halvard said, and her voice had found something it hadn't had before—not flatness now, but weight. "Whatever happens below. Whatever the Regent sends. We hold the stair."

"Maren," Isandro said, "they'll be executed for this."

"They'll be remembered for it," Maren answered. "There is a difference, and it is the only difference that has ever mattered."

* * *

Beyond the guards, the chamber narrowed into a passage that the torchlight barely survived, and the pulse grew stronger until Isandro could feel it in his sternum, keeping time with his own heart and then, horribly, correcting it—his heartbeat stumbling, stuttering, falling into step with the thing's rhythm like a drummer boy overtaken by the column. He clenched his fists and forced his breath slow. *Storms are not summoned but negotiated.* The hermit's voice, dead these many weeks and still teaching. *And hearts, apparently,* he thought wildly, *are also negotiated.*

The passage opened into the inner sanctum, and there, at last, was the king.

Or the shape of the king. Aurelian sat—not slumped, not chained, but *seated*—on a plain stone chair at the center of a ring of runes worn nearly smooth, and he sat the way furniture sits, upright and perfect and empty. He wore no crown down here. His head was bare, his hair grey-streaked though he could not have been past forty, and his eyes were open and fixed on the middle distance with the mild, terrible patience of a man watching weather he cannot influence. Filaments ran from the base of his skull into the floor, into the walls, up toward the knot above, dozens of them, silver-pale, throbbing gently with each pulse.

"He's alive," Sera whispered. She was already moving toward him, kneeling, checking the pulse at his throat with fingers that trembled and steadied. "Alive. Heart strong. God, he's just—*sitting* there—"

"He sits because it lets him sit," Maren said. She had stopped at the ring of runes and would come no closer; Isandro watched her knuckles whiten on her sword belt and understood that the inquisitor-general, who had stared down kings and heretics without blinking, was afraid. "I told you. It wears him. This is what's underneath the wearing."

Isandro approached slowly. Up close, the king's face resolved out of the gloom, and Isandro's breath caught—not at the resemblance, though the resemblance was there, the same jaw, the same brow, a face like his own seen through water and fifteen years of frost. What caught him was the eyes. They were not empty. Empty would have been merciful. They were *occupied*—something behind them looking out, using them, the way a hand uses a glove, and when Isandro stepped into the ring of runes the gaze slid toward him with a smoothness no tired human eye could manage.

The king's mouth opened.

"Storm-child," it said.

The voice was Aurelian's voice, warm and deep, and it was not Aurelian's voice at all—it was layered, doubled, a chord where there should have been a single note, and the second note underneath was old and hungry and amused. The filaments brightened. Across the chamber, the knot pulsed hard, once, twice, turning faster.

"You brought the storm to me," the thing wearing the king said, smiling with the king's mouth. "How thoughtful. How *nourishing*. I tasted you on the wind at the ford. I tasted you on the spire. Do you know how long it has been since anything with sky in it came down my stairs?"

Isandro held his ground. His skin prickled with gathering charge; the storm-magic woke in him unbidden, answering fear the way it always had, and he wrestled it down, down, negotiated it to stillness. "I'm not here to feed you."

"Everything that enters feeds me." The king's head tilted, birdlike, wrong. "Even defiance. Especially defiance. It has such a bright flavor." The eyes narrowed, and for one instant the doubled voice thinned to something almost curious. "But you are strange. There is a hole in you where a name should be. I remember holes. I made most of mine myself." A pause, and then, softer, silkier: "Would you like to know what your brother dreams of, down here in the dark? He dreams of a garden. Two boys in a garden. He cannot see either of their faces. Isn't that the cruelest mercy you have ever heard?"

"*Stop*," Sera said, and her voice cracked across the chamber like a whip, and in her cupped hands the candle stub flared—flared white, impossibly, and in the flame Isandro saw a shape gather, small and guttering and furious. Vess. The last ember of a god, burning itself brighter than it had burned since the ferry.

"You do not speak to him," Vess said, in a voice like a struck bell heard through water. "You do not *taste* him. You have eaten a generation of prayers and you will eat no more. The rite begins tonight."

The thing laughed with the king's throat, and the laugh shook dust from the ceiling, and far above them—muffled by stone and distance but unmistakable—came the first distant boom of siege artillery finding its range against Ashkarat's outer wall.

The siege had begun. The hour had arrived whether they were ready or not.

Sera looked at Isandro across the seated king, across the breathing dark, and her face in the candlelight was terrified and certain in equal measure. "The words need blood and voice," she said. "Yours. Are you ready?"

Above them, the knot turned faster, and faster, and began—for the first time in fifteen years—to pull against its bindings.

* * *

Isandro drew the knife.

It was a small thing, a pilgrim's knife with a bone handle, bought in Delmare for three coppers, and it was about to open the oldest wound in the world. He pressed the blade to his palm and found his hand was steady, which surprised him; he had expected shaking, had expected the valley-boy's terror, the exile's flinch. Instead there was only a strange calm, the calm of a man who has finally walked far enough that there is no road left but through.

"One condition," he said, and looked not at the parasite but at the face above it—at the hollowed, haunted architecture of his brother's features, searching for anything behind the occupation. "When this is done. When he wakes. He doesn't owe me anything. He doesn't owe me recognition, or gratitude, or grief. He gets his crown and his city and his own mind back, clean. Whatever I am to him—whatever I was—that stays buried with the rest."

The thing wearing the king smiled wider than a face should allow.

"Oh, storm-child," it said, almost tenderly. "Do you think the binding kept things *from* you? It kept things *for* you. Wait until you remember what your mother traded. Wait until you understand what you agreed to forget." The filaments blazed. The knot screamed soundlessly against its cradle. "Cut, little twin. Cut, and we shall all find out together what was under the ice."

Somewhere above, the wall of Ashkarat cracked like a struck bell, and the whole mountain shuddered, and Isandro opened his hand.
