## 32. Aurelian, Briefly Awake

The passage beneath the throne room narrowed until Isandro had to walk with his shoulders turned sideways, the stone pressing close as a held breath. Maren went first, her stolen keys chiming softly against her hip, each lock surrendering to her with a small iron click that sounded, in the dark, like a countdown. Sera came behind him with a candle stub cupped in her palm, and its light trembled—not from her hand, he thought, but from the air itself, which had grown thick and strange the deeper they descended.

He could feel it too. Not with his skin, not exactly. With whatever part of him the storm lived in.

There was a wrongness below them, patient and vast, the way a river is wrong beneath ice—everything smooth on the surface, everything moving underneath. Fifteen years in a valley without magic had taught him what silence felt like when it was natural: the hush of Stillwater at dawn, snow settling on the rim, Odile's shrine with its cold hearth. This was not that silence. This was silence the way a strangled throat is silent.

"Three more doors," Maren said quietly. "Then the old guard post. After that, we're inside the king's keep proper."

"And after that?" Sera asked.

"After that," Maren said, "we are either saviors or traitors, and there will be very little time to discuss which."

Isandro said nothing. He had been saying nothing for hours, saving his words the way a man saves his last coins. His hands would not stop being cold. He kept thinking of the binding chamber they had passed through an hour ago—the knot of devoured god-stuff still pulsing in its cradle of black stone, beating like a second heart under the palace, feeding upward through veins of corrupted ritual into the man who sat the throne above them. He had felt it taste him as they crossed the chamber. A slow, wet attention, turning over his storm-magic like a tongue finding a cracked tooth.

It knew he was here now. There was no pretending otherwise. Every step deeper was a step it permitted, and he could not decide whether that made him brave or bait.

The third door opened onto a gallery hung with portraits, and Isandro stopped walking so suddenly that Sera nearly collided with his back.

The faces on the walls were his own face.

Not exactly—the way a reflection in disturbed water is not exactly your face—but close enough that his body understood before his mind did. A young man with dark hair and storm-grey eyes stood in gilded frames beside a woman with Liora's jaw, beside an older man whose smile Isandro did not know and could not bear to study. And in the largest portrait, at the gallery's end, crowned and robed in white and gold, a king looked down at him with eyes that were, unmistakably, his own eyes.

*Aurelian.*

His brother. The word arrived without permission, whole and certain, as if it had been waiting fifteen years in some sealed room of him for the door to crack.

"You didn't tell me you'd seen these," Sera murmured.

"I hadn't." His voice came out rough. "I've never seen my own face before. Not truly. Mirrors in Stillwater were—" He stopped. *Small, cracked, avoided.* "I didn't know I looked like anyone."

Maren glanced back at them, and something flickered across her disciplined face—not pity, nothing so soft, but recognition. She had spent her life serving order; perhaps she understood what it cost to learn you had been filed under the wrong name.

"The king's apartments are beyond," she said. "We should move."

But Isandro stood one moment longer, looking up at the painted version of the life that should have been his—or should have been Aurelian's; he still did not know which twin he was—and felt the grief arrive early, ahead of schedule, like weather from a horizon with no clouds yet.

* * *

The doors to the king's chambers were white oak banded in silver, and they were guarded.

Two men of the palace watch stood there in the livery of the crown—*the true crown*, Maren had called it, distinguishing it from the Regent's black-and-iron—and when she stepped into their view with her inquisitor's seal raised high, Isandro watched her perform the finest lie of her long career: that she had come on direct orders to inspect the king's condition personally, that the storm-caller and the priestess were prisoners under escort.

One guard hesitated. The other did not. And in that hesitation, Isandro saw what Vess had promised in its dying embers—that the old loyalties were not dead in this place, only sleeping, and that they woke at strange angles. The hesitant guard's eyes moved from Maren's seal to Isandro's face, and widened, and his hand drifted toward his sword not in threat but in something like awe.

"By the Spire," the young guard whispered. "You have the king's face."

"Stand aside, Torvald," the older guard said—but his voice shook too, and his spear did not come up. "Stand aside and forget you saw it."

They let them pass. Neither guard met Isandro's eyes again, and he understood that they had both just committed treason in the space of a breath, and that they would carry it alone, silently, the way soldiers carry everything.

Then the doors were open, and then closed behind them, and Isandro was standing in the king's bedchamber, and the thing wearing the king's crown was looking at him from across the room.

* * *

Aurelian sat by the window in a chair of pale wood, dressed simply, as if he had chosen his own clothes—which, Isandro would understand later, he almost never did. He was thin. Thinner than the portrait, thinner than a king should be, worn down like a coastline, and his hands lay open on his knees in a posture of terrible patience, the patience of a man who has been waiting inside his own body for someone to notice he is home.

And his eyes—

His eyes were grey. Storm-grey. Isandro's grey. But they were wrong the way a house is wrong when something else lives in it. Behind them, far back, something vast and hungry watched through the glass of the king like a fisherman watching through ice.

"Well," said the thing in the king's voice, mild and pleasant and utterly alien. "The valley sends its regards at last."

Sera's candle guttered. Maren's hand went to her sword.

But Isandro found, strangely, that he was not afraid. Or rather—he was afraid the way a man is afraid standing before a great wave, which is to say completely, and also unable to look away. Because underneath the parasite's pleasant voice, underneath the hunger wearing his brother like a glove, there was something else. Faint. Flickering. A candle in a draft.

A man, knocking on the inside of his own skull.

"You can hear me," Isandro said. Not to the parasite. Past it. Into the deep water where the real king waited. "I know you can. I felt you feel me in the binding chamber. You tasted my name before I ever spoke it."

"The binding holds beautifully," the parasite said, tilting Aurelian's head with a small, curious grace. "Fifteen years, and the little brother comes home all on his own feet. We hardly had to call. Do you know how rare that is? How *delicious*? Most meals require hunting."

"We are not meals," Sera said, stepping forward, her faith rising off her like heat off stone. "We are the old gods' answer, and you are a thief who ate their silence and called it a kingdom."

"Old gods." The parasite smiled with Aurelian's mouth, and it was a beautiful mouth, and the smile ruined it. "We ate those. Slowly. Over years. You cannot imagine what a god tastes like from the inside, little priestess. Like drinking a thunderstorm through a straw. Your Vess burned itself down to embers trying to remember what it was. We remember *everything* we eat."

Isandro took one step forward. Then another. The parasite watched him come with the lazy attention of a cat watching a moth, and Isandro let it watch, because every step closer to the window was a step closer to the light, and the light was doing something to the king's face. The afternoon sun came through the glass and struck Aurelian's cheek, and where it touched, the wrongness thinned. The eyes behind the eyes flinched from it.

Sunlight. Small things. The parasite had eaten gods, but it had never learned to love anything, and love leaves doors open.

"Aurelian," Isandro said.

The name dropped into the room like a stone into a well.

For a heartbeat, nothing. The parasite's smile held. Maren's breath stopped. Somewhere far above, siege drums began—faint, rhythmic, the usurper's army announcing itself to the city, though none of them had known it until this moment. The army had come. The clock they had been racing had run out while they walked through tunnels.

And then Aurelian blinked.

It was a small thing. A human thing. The parasite's gaze slid away for half a second, distracted by the drumbeat, tasting the fear rolling off the city in waves it could drink—and in that half-second, the man behind the glass pressed both hands against the ice.

"*Lio.*"

The word came out of Aurelian's mouth broken, rusted, a hinge unused for fifteen years. His eyes changed. The grey warmed. The fisherman's gaze vanished and in its place was a boy—a grown man, yes, thirty years old and hollowed by captivity, but underneath it, suddenly, unbearably, a boy of fifteen looking at his brother across a nursery full of toy ships and paper storms.

"Lio," Aurelian said again, and his hands came up off his knees, reaching, trembling. "Lio, you *came*. Mother said—I couldn't remember what she said, they took it, they took everything, but I knew, I always knew someone was coming, I wrote it on the wall of myself where it couldn't reach—"

Isandro could not move. Could not breathe. The sound of his own childhood name—*Lio*, a name no living person knew, a name that had died with Queen Liora's letters—went through him like lightning goes through a mast, lighting every dark timber of him at once.

"I don't remember you," Isandro heard himself say, and his voice shattered on it. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I don't remember any of it. The nursery. You. None of it."

"It doesn't matter." Aurelian was crying now, silently, the tears of a man who has forgotten how to do it loudly. "It doesn't matter, don't you see? You came. That's the memory. That's the only one I need. Lio—listen—they told me I was the king, and then they told me I wasn't, and then they stopped telling me anything at all, and I held on to one thing, I held on to—"

The drums stopped.

All of them. At once. As if the whole army had drawn one breath.

And the temperature in the room fell.

"No," Aurelian whispered, and his face began to change, the warmth draining out of it like color out of a sunset. "*No, not now, please, I just got—*"

"*Mine*," said the thing wearing the king.

It did not shout. It did not need to. The word filled the chamber the way floodwater fills a cellar, from the bottom up, and the candle went out, and the sunlight itself seemed to curdle. Aurelian's spine straightened against his will. His hands lowered. His face smoothed into the parasite's pleasant blankness, and the grey of his eyes frosted over, and the door—the door that had been open for ninety seconds, ninety seconds Isandro would count and recount for the rest of his life—closed with a finality that rang in the stone.

The king looked at Isandro with polite, distant interest.

"How touching," the parasite said. "Brothers. We had forgotten the flavor of family. So rare, these days. So *sweet*."

"Get out of him," Isandro said. Quietly. Which was worse than shouting, and he knew it, and the parasite knew it too, because for the first time its pleasantness flickered.

"You cannot unmake what your mother helped make," it said. "She split the binding herself, did she tell you? Clever woman. Dead woman. Her cleverness bought you fifteen years and it will buy you nothing now. Come to the spire, little storm. Come and see what we make of callers."

It rose from the chair. It walked past them—past Maren's drawn sword, past Sera's shaking faith, past Isandro frozen at the window-light—and paused at the door, wearing his brother's body like a borrowed coat, and looked back once.

"He will remember this," it said gently. "When we let him surface again. We will make sure of it. We want him to know exactly what his coming home cost."

Then it was gone, and the guards outside did not stop it, because it was the king, and kings go where they wish.

* * *

For a long time, nobody spoke.

Sera relit the candle with hands that were not quite steady. Maren stood with her sword still drawn, staring at the empty doorway, and Isandro saw her recalculating everything—her career, her god, her order—in the space of that silence.

"Ninety seconds," Isandro said finally. His voice sounded like someone else's. "Maybe less. I counted."

"Isandro—"

"He called me Lio." He turned to face them, and Sera flinched at what was in his face, because it was not grief anymore, or not only grief. It was grief that had been heated past burning and come out the other side as something with an edge. "Fifteen years. Fifteen years of a valley that forgot me, a letter that said *the crown lies*, a god that burned itself to embers to find me, a road, a gaol, a ford, a lighthouse, a war—and I got ninety seconds. One name. One moment of my brother knowing me."

Sera reached for his arm. He let her take it.

"And it will take even that away," he said. "That's what it meant. When it surfaces him next, it will show him this room empty. It will poison the one memory I have. Unless—"

"Unless we do it first," Sera finished. "Tomorrow night. The rite. The storm."

"The storm," Isandro agreed.

He walked to the window. Far below, Ashkarat lay under a sky the color of a bruise, and along the southern horizon, the usurper's army was making camp, ten thousand cookfires kindling in the dusk like a fallen constellation. Above it all, far to the west, over the mountains, a storm was gathering—the largest he had ever felt, a century-storm rolling toward the capital like something summoned, like something answered, like something that had been walking toward this exact window for fifteen years.

It felt him looking. He felt it look back.

"Hold on, Aurelian," he said to the glass, to the city, to the brother behind the ice. "Ninety seconds was enough. I'll make it enough."

Behind him, Maren sheathed her sword and said, in the flat voice of a woman reading her own death warrant: "The spire. It wants us at the spire. Then that's where we'll end it."

Outside, the first drops of rain struck the palace windows, and the storm began to answer.
