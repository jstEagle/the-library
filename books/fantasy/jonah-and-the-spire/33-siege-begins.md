## 33. Siege Begins

The storm had been building for three days.

Isandro felt it the way a sailor feels a change in tide—not in his eyes or ears but somewhere deeper, in the marrow of him, in the place where the sky and his blood had learned to speak the same language. It sat over the Ashkarat mountains like a crouched animal, vast and patient, gathering itself out of the moisture of two seas and the heat of the lowlands. The largest storm of the age, the old hermit would have called it, and then he would have said, *And it will not come to you, boy. You will go to it.*

He stood on the palace roof with the wind pulling at his coat and looked north, past the gilded domes and the temple spires, to where the horizon had gone dark in a way that had nothing to do with nightfall. The usurper's army was coming up the river road, and even at this distance he could see it—not the soldiers themselves, not yet, but the dust of them, a brown smear against the green of the floodplain, like a bruise spreading under skin.

Behind him, on the roof tiles, Sera knelt over an open satchel, checking the rite's components for the fourth time that hour. Candle stubs. A bowl of river water. The scrap of vellum bearing Vess's last ember-words, copied out in her careful hand because the god itself had burned down to something small enough to live in a lantern. She moved with the economy of someone who had stopped being afraid and started being useful instead, and Isandro loved her for it in a way he had no time to examine.

"Third candle's cracked," she said without looking up. "It'll burn uneven. I need another."

"There are no more candles," Isandro said. "There hasn't been a working chandler in this city since the curfew."

"Then I'll use the cracked one and compensate." She set it down among the others, adjusted its angle by a degree, and Isandro understood that she was not really fussing over candles. She was keeping her hands busy so they wouldn't shake. "How far?"

"Two days' march. One, if they push through the night."

"They'll push through the night." Sera closed the satchel. "Maren says the Regent doesn't rest. Whatever wears that crown doesn't sleep, doesn't eat—or eats things we don't want to think about—and it wants you badly enough to march an army through the flood season." She looked up at him then, wind whipping a strand of hair across her face, and her eyes were steady and terrible. "It knows what we're going to do, Isandro. It has known since the ford."

"Then we do it before it arrives."

"That's what frightens me." She rose, joints cracking in the cold. "Not that we fail. That we succeed too fast, and get it wrong."

Below them, Ashkarat glittered in the last light like a jewel held over a fire. Beautiful and terrified, Sera had called it weeks ago, and she had been right, and now the terror had a shape: watchfires kindling on every wall, the great bronze bells of the temple district hanging silent because no one dared ring them, streets emptying as families who could afford it barred their doors and families who couldn't huddled in cellars and prayed—to the new gods, to the old gods, to anything listening. Some of those prayers were being answered now. Isandro could feel them, faint as moths against glass, the returning voices of gods still weak from fifteen years of silence, whispering comfort into cellars they could not otherwise enter.

One of those voices was Vess, guttering inside its lantern beside the roof door, barely taller than a thumb-flame.

"I remember sieges," Vess said, and its voice was thin as paper. "I remember the siege of Old Vhalen, before your grandmothers' grandmothers. Three years. The dead were stacked like cordwood outside the gates until the crows grew fat enough to walk instead of fly." A flicker. "I say this not to frighten you. I say it because I have forgotten how to be comforting, and honesty is all I have left."

"Thank you," Isandro said gravely, because he had learned that with Vess, gravity was kindness.

"The storm," Vess said. "You will speak with it tomorrow?"

"At first light. When the army is in sight of the walls. Not before—the hermit said a negotiation opened too early collapses under its own weight."

"The hermit was wise."

"The hermit was terrifying."

"Those are often the same thing." The little flame bent toward the northern dark. "Isandro. When you stand on the spire tomorrow, and the sky comes down to meet you—remember that it is not your enemy. It never was. Even in the valley, even in the silence, it waited for you. Fifteen years, it kept your seat warm."

Isandro did not answer. There was nothing to say to that which would not crack something open in him that needed to stay whole until the work was done. He only put his hand flat on the roof tiles, feeling the city's warmth bleeding up out of ten thousand hearths, and made himself a promise shaped like a vow: *no one else pays my price.* He had made that promise once before, leaving Stillwater, and broken it within a week. Tomorrow he intended to keep it if it killed him.

Which, he thought, looking at the storm-light beginning to pulse behind the peaks like a slow heartbeat, it very well might.

* * *

Maren heard the bells of the outer wall at the second hour after midnight, and knew the sound for what it was: not alarm, but accounting. The vanguard had been sighted. Somewhere below her, a clerk was marking a ledger, and somewhere beyond the walls, forty thousand men were making camp in the mud of the floodplain, and somewhere above all of it, the Regent was awake in the high tower, and smiling.

She knew because she had seen him smile. Once. At night, through a door she should never have opened, when the thing wearing King Aurelian fed, and Maren's whole ordered world had come apart like wet paper.

Now she walked the palace corridors with a lamp in one hand and a stolen ring of keys in the other, moving through the service passages the way she had once moved through interrogation cells—with total command of the geography and none of the authority. Her former colleagues were hunting her. She had heard them at dusk: Inquisitor Dace's voice, flat and precise, asking a scullery maid whether the traitor-general had passed; the soft ring of halberds against stone as soldiers took up positions at the stairheads. They would find her. Maren was good, but she was tired, and the palace was full of eyes, and sooner or later competence lost to arithmetic.

What mattered was whether she found the binding chamber first.

She descended. Down past the wine vaults where her lamp threw shadows like gallows, down past the old cistern where the water breathed cold and black, down the stair that appeared on no plan she had ever been shown—the stair Maren herself had pried out of a dying archivist's memory three nights ago, trading the man a painless end for a map drawn in his last breaths. She had told herself it was mercy. She was no longer sure she believed in mercy, only in debts paid promptly.

At the bottom was a door of black iron, older than the dynasty, worked with symbols worn nearly smooth. Two locks. Her keys fit both.

She did not open it. She stood before it with her lamp raised, listening, and beneath the silence she heard it—the pulse. Slow, deep, rhythmic as a buried heart. The knot of devoured god-stuff that anchored the parasite to this place, to the crown, to the king. It pulsed faster than it had when she'd first heard it, days ago. It was excited. Something above was coming that it wanted, and the thing wearing the crown was hungry for it the way she had once been hungry for truth, back when she'd believed truth and order were the same thing.

"You were wrong about me," she said quietly, to the door, to whatever listened behind it. "You offered me certainty once, and I took it, and I built a life on it. But certainty isn't order. Certainty is just appetite wearing a uniform. I know, because I wore the uniform."

The pulse stuttered. Just slightly. As if something vast had turned its attention toward her, considered her, and dismissed her—a moth again, a nothing, a door-knocker at the feast.

Good, Maren thought. Underestimate me. Underestimation was the one weapon the powerless were issued by default, and she had learned to use it better than any blade.

She relocked the door, marked the passage in her memory with the precision of fifteen years' training, and climbed back up into the darkening palace, where her colleagues' lamps were already moving through the corridors above, closing like a net.

Let them close, she thought. By tomorrow night, either everything she had served would be ash, or everything she had believed would be. She found she could survive either outcome. That, more than anything, told her how far she had fallen—or risen. She had stopped being able to tell the difference, and it was the freest she had ever felt.

* * *

Sera could not sleep, so she went down to the chapel.

The underground chapel had become crowded in the last week—refugees from the river districts, god-touched folk who had fled the registries, a handful of wounded soldiers from the city garrison who had decided, quietly and without orders, that if the walls fell they would rather die defending a chapel than running from one. They slept in rows on the stone floor, and in the darkness their breathing rose and fell together, and above them the candles burned, and in the candles Vess lived, distributed now across forty small flames because the god could no longer hold a single shape.

Sera knelt at the altar and laid out the rite.

She had performed ceremonies her whole life. First as an acolyte, then a priestess, then—after the burning—as nothing at all, a woman with vestments in her memory and ash where her faith used to be. She knew liturgy the way sailors know rope: in her hands, without thinking. But this was different. This rite had no congregation, no tradition, no precedent in any text she had ever studied. It came from a god's dying memory and a dead queen's journal, and if she got it wrong there would be no second attempt, no penance, no rebirth. Just a parasite unloosed on the world with a king's body for a glove and an army at its back.

*Unbinding requires the true heir's blood and voice,* Vess had said. *And it returns everything the binding took—to whoever performs it.*

She had read Queen Liora's journal twice more since Isandro confessed what he'd been hiding. The third path, the shared binding, was there in the queen's hand, cramped and desperate, half theology and half mathematics: *two anchors split the burden; two anchors double the risk; the knot must be tied from inside.* The queen had never attempted it. The queen had chosen instead to split her sons between two hiding places and spend fifteen years dying slowly in sealed rooms, writing letters to children who would never read them until it was too late for the reading to matter.

Sera understood her now. That was the worst part. Grief made scholars of us all.

"You're doing the sums again," said a voice behind her—one of the wounded soldiers, a young woman with a bandaged arm who had appointed herself Sera's unofficial guard. "Your face does a thing when you're doing sums."

"Go back to sleep, Tavi."

"Can't. Too loud." Tavi nodded upward, at the ceiling, at the city, at the distant mutter of an army making ready. "Everyone's awake. Whole city's lying there listening. You can feel it, can't you? Like before a wave breaks."

Sera could feel it. She had spent years learning to sense the movements of gods, and lately the whole world had begun to feel like a temple at the moment before invocation—that held-breath silence, that pressure, that sense of something immense leaning in. Tomorrow, when Isandro climbed the spire and spoke to the storm, that pressure would break, one way or another.

"Tavi," she said. "If I don't come up from under the palace—"

"You'll come up."

"If I don't. Someone has to tell the story afterward. The true one. All of it—the twins, the binding, the thing in the crown, what the Regent really was. Maren wants it recorded too; she gave me her testimony, written and signed. Keep it safe. Truth is the only weapon that survives a siege."

Tavi was quiet a moment. Then, gruffly: "You sound like a woman planning to die."

"I sound like a priestess. We plan the funeral before the wedding. It's how we stay honest." Sera smiled, and found, to her surprise, that the smile was real. "Fifteen years ago the gods went silent and I thought I would never feel them again. Tonight I can feel Vess in every candle in this room, and the storm waiting over the mountains like a promise, and a king down in the dark who is going to wake up screaming and free. Whatever happens tomorrow, Tavi—I am owed nothing, and I have been given everything. Go to sleep."

Tavi went. And Sera stayed kneeling at the altar, arranging cracked candles, doing her sums, praying—not for safety, not anymore, but for accuracy. Let my hands be true. Let the knot be cut clean. Let them both live, the brothers who cannot remember each other, and let one of them at least learn to smile again.

Somewhere above, the first fire arrows arced over the outer districts, earlier than any projection, because the Regent did not wait and did not rest, and the war arrived at Ashkarat a full day ahead of schedule.

* * *

Isandro woke to the smell of smoke and knew, before he opened his eyes, before he reached the window, before the bells began—the arithmetic had failed. The army had forced the march through the flooded lowlands, losing men to the marshes rather than lose the element of surprise, and now the vanguard stood before the outer walls while the city slept.

He ran.

The corridors of the palace were full of shouting. Servants streamed one way; guards in crown-livery—*true* crown-livery, the old colors, waking to loyalties they had half-forgotten—streamed the other. Through a window he saw the eastern districts lit orange, fire arrows lodged in thatch and timber, bucket-lines forming in the streets below with the grim practiced speed of a city that had drilled for this and prayed never to need it. Smoke smeared the stars. Somewhere a child was crying, and somewhere else, incredibly, someone was singing—a low, defiant hymn to the old gods rising from a cellar grate, and Isandro felt the answering stir of weak divine voices gathering around it like hands cupped around a match.

On the roof, Sera was already there, lantern in hand, hair whipping in a wind that had teeth in it now.

"They're early," she shouted.

"I know!"

"The rite needs the storm. Your storm has to hold the parasite's attention while I cut the knot—if it turns on me mid-rite, everything fails—"

"Then the storm holds." He turned north.

And there it was.

The century-storm had come down off the mountains overnight, drawn by the burning city or by him or by its own ancient appetite for drama—it was impossible to say, and the hermit would have said the question didn't matter. It filled the entire northern sky, a wall of cloud from earth to heaven, lit from within by pulses of lightning so deep and slow they seemed less like flashes than like thoughts. Rain fell from its leading edge in gray curtains, marching toward the walls. Beneath it, the usurper's army looked like toys scattered on a table—forty thousand men, siege engines, banners, the iron crown standard glinting in the fires—and above it all, Isandro could feel the parasite turning its attention upward, away from the walls, away from the burning districts, tasting the air the way it had tasted it from the rafters that day.

It knew the sky was answering someone. And it was coming to deal with him personally.

"Isandro." Sera caught his arm. Her face was soot-streaked and calm. "Whatever happens up there—"

"Don't." His voice cracked. "Don't say it like a farewell. Say it like a promise."

She held his gaze, firelight and lightning fighting across her features. "Come back down," she said. "That's the promise. Come back down, and finish this, and then—for once in either of our lives—we figure out what comes after."

He kissed her, brief and fierce, and pulled on the hermit's oilskin coat, and took up the iron rod the old man had pressed into his hands at parting—"not a weapon, a *talking stick*, boy; you don't shout at storms, you *converse*"—and went up.

The great spire of Ashkarat rose from the heart of the old palace, a needle of white stone older than the dynasty, built—Queen Liora's journal had hinted, and Vess had confirmed—in the age when kings still spoke with the sky as neighbors speak across a fence. Its summit platform was barely wide enough for two people. The stairs inside were choked with debris and pigeon filth and fifteen decades of neglect, and Isandro climbed them in the dark, lungs burning, while below him the city burned and the bells finally, finally began to ring—all of them at once, bronze voices cracking the night open, and he understood that someone in the temple district had decided that if the city might die tonight, it would die loud, and calling its gods home.

At the top, the wind hit him like a wall.

The storm filled half the world. Up close it was not weather but presence—an intelligence of pressure and charge, vast beyond sense, and every instinct he owned screamed at him the way he had screamed at thunderstorms as a boy in Stillwater, face pressed to the window, afraid of the noise. Fifteen years of silence had taught him fear. One autumn with a blind hermit had taught him something better.

He stepped to the edge of the platform, planted the iron rod, and did not summon.

He knocked.

*Here,* he said, in the language that was not language, the grammar of pressure and potential the hermit had beaten into him through a hundred freezing dawns. *Here is a mortal. Here is a city burning. Here is a thing that ate the gods, and it is climbing toward me, and I am asking—not commanding, asking—will you help me hold the door?*

The storm went still. The whole vast front of it, miles of boiling cloud, hung motionless over the battlefield, and the silence underneath the lightning was the deepest Isandro had ever heard—even deeper, he thought wildly, than the Valley of Stillness. Every scrying eye in Vhalen was fixed on him now; he could feel them like insects on his skin. Below, on the plain, the army had stopped. In the high tower, the thing wearing the crown had risen from its throne.

And the storm leaned down, and considered him, and answered.

Lightning struck the spire—not striking him, striking *around* him, a cage of white fire, and inside the cage Isandro Almeida, exiled storm-caller, forgotten prince, stood in the loudest quiet in the world and began, carefully, the negotiation on which everything depended.

Below him, unseen in the burning dark, Sera took up her candles and started down the stairs toward the binding chamber, toward the pulse in the deep, toward the knot.

Above him, the storm gathered its first argument.

And out on the floodplain, at the head of forty thousand men, the thing wearing King Aurelian smiled its borrowed smile, spread its arms to the rain, and began to climb.
