## 34. The Storm Over Ashkarat

The wind found him first.

It came up the palace stairwell in a long, low moan, the way a hound noses at a door it has been shut behind for years. Isandro stood at the top of the Spire of Bells with his hand on the cold iron rail and felt the whole city breathe beneath him—Ashkarat's ten thousand rooftops, its burning districts, its walls where fire arrows fell like orange rain—and above all of it, the sky.

The century-storm had been building for three days. He had felt it gathering beyond the mountains while he was still underground, still crawling through the binding chamber's dark, still learning the words that would cost him everything. It was a vast, slow thing, older than the war, older than the usurper, a storm that had circled the high peaks of Vhalen since before the gods went silent, feeding on its own patience. The old callers had names for storms like this. They did not summon them. You could no more summon this than you could summon the sea.

You negotiated.

*Remember,* the hermit had said, blind eyes turned toward a sea he could not see. *A storm is not an army. It is an animal. And animals can be spoken to—if you speak honestly, and if you are willing to be changed by what they say back.*

Isandro closed his eyes and let the wind take his hair, his cloak, his breath. Below him, Sera was descending into the earth with Maren at her side, carrying candle-flame and knife and the last ember of a god. Somewhere beneath his feet, his brother slept inside a monster. Somewhere beyond the walls, forty thousand soldiers waited for the gates to break.

And here, at the top of the world, there was only him and the weather.

He opened himself the way he had learned to—not flinging the doors wide as he had on the valley rim, not the desperate tearing-open that had leveled a ridge and killed a man in Corvane—but slowly, deliberately, like opening a window in a house long shuttered. His magic rose to meet the air. Fifteen years of silence, two months of discipline, one lifetime of fear: all of it went out of him into the wind like smoke from a doused fire.

The storm noticed.

It was not a sound or a sight but a pressure, a turning of vast attention, the way a sleeping mountain might notice an ant crossing its shoulder. Isandro felt himself weighed. Measured. Found very small.

*Good,* he thought, and made his voice steady even inside his own skull. *I am small. That is rather the point.*

* * *

The first lightning struck the spire itself, and he did not flinch.

It came down the length of the bell tower like a white tree growing in reverse, root in heaven, crown in stone, and it passed through him—or he passed through it; the distinction stopped mattering—and for one blinding instant he saw the storm as it saw itself. Not rage. Not malice. A hunger for form, a century of undirected power circling the peaks with nothing to do but rain on empty rock. Storms, the hermit had told him, were prayers that had never been answered. This one had been praying for a hundred years.

*I have work for you,* Isandro said, into the thunder, into the space between heartbeats. *If you will take it.*

The wind screamed around the spire, and in the scream he heard something that was almost language—the way he had once heard almost-language in Vess's flickering voice, in the creak of the ferry rope, in every half-sacred thing the valley had tried to silence. The storm asked its question. It was the same question every storm asked, the same question the child pilgrim had asked with her small upturned face, the same question Sera had asked him on the road when she thought he was asleep:

*Why should I answer you?*

Isandro laughed. It came out ragged, half terror, and entirely true.

"Because everyone else who ever called me wanted a weapon," he said aloud, shouting into the gale because some things deserved a human voice. "The usurper wants my lightning to break gates. The parasite below wants my power because it eats gods and I taste like one. Even I wanted you for killing, once—I killed a man with your cousin, a little squall off the Corvane rooftops, and I have regretted it every day since." He gripped the rail, knuckles white, rain beginning now, fat and warm and electric. "I'm not asking you to be a weapon. I'm asking you to be architecture."

The storm went quiet around him. Not calm—quiet, the held-breath quiet of something enormous listening.

Below, the city burned. He could see the eastern districts glowing, the siege fires ringing the walls, the tiny scurrying lights of Maren's former colleagues hunting her through corridors he would never see. Every second he spent talking to the sky, men died down there. He felt each one like a stone dropped into water.

But you did not rush a negotiation with a century-storm. That was the first lesson. Rushing was what got the ridge leveled. Rushing was what got the warden killed. Rushing was how boys became weapons instead of men.

"I will give you shape," Isandro said. "A hundred years of rain falling on nothing, a hundred years of lightning with nowhere to go. I'm offering you a purpose. There is a thing under this city that ate the silence of the gods—it drank a hundred years of unanswered prayer, and it grew fat on it. It is the reason you were never answered. It is the reason *I* was never answered." His voice cracked, and he let it. Honesty, the hermit had said. Always honesty. "Every prayer that ever went up from this valley, from those burned temples, from every mouth the usurper silenced—that is what fed it. And tonight, if you help me, every drop of you falls somewhere it matters."

The wind turned. Slowly, majestically, the whole rotating body of the century-storm swung its attention toward Ashkarat, toward the spire, toward the small wet man standing on top of it with his arms open.

*And the price?* the storm asked, in the voice of distant thunder deciding whether to come closer. Storms always asked about price. They were honest that way. More honest than queens, more honest than gods.

Isandro thought of the journal in Queen Liora's sealed rooms. *The ritual can be completed either way: restore the king, or restore yourself. Not both.* He thought of the words of unbinding sitting in his chest like swallowed coals, waiting for Sera's hands below. He thought of the price he had already agreed to pay, alone, without telling her—though she had seen through him, because she always saw through him, and had refused to let him carry it by himself.

"The price," he said, "is mine. Whatever it turns out to be. You may take your toll from me and no one else."

The storm considered him for a long moment. Rain streamed down his face. Far below, a section of the western wall lit orange as fire arrows found the timber hoardings, and he heard—even up here, even over the wind—the thin sound of ten thousand voices crying out at once.

Then the century-storm answered.

* * *

Lightning became architecture.

That was the only word for it, afterward, though few survived to describe it and fewer believed them. The great bolt did not strike the spire—it *built* upon it, branching outward in perfect load-bearing arcs, a cathedral of white fire assembling itself around the bell tower in the space of three heartbeats. Ribs of light arched over Isandro's head. Pillars of plasma rooted themselves in the stone and held. The storm poured itself down the spire like water finding a channel it had been carved for, and Isandro stood in the center of it, in the eye of the design, and felt the raw power of a hundred years pass through him and *obey*.

Not because he commanded it. Because he had offered it what it had always wanted, and it had accepted.

He raised his hands, and the lightning-architecture spread. Arcs leapt from the spire to the neighboring towers, and where they landed, they did not burn—they *shielded*, forming luminous vaults over the streets where refugees ran, turning the fire arrows to steam a hundred feet short of the rooftops. Rain fell in ordered sheets, drowning the siege fires along the western wall, hissing into the arrow-slits, filling the moat-ditches until the mud swallowed the battering rams. Thunder rolled across the city in great measured strokes, and Isandro realized, with a wonder that made his chest ache, that the storm was *timing itself* to the bells—to the silent bells of the Spire of Bells, giving them back their voice in the only language left to them.

The people of Ashkarat looked up.

He saw them from his perch in the heart of the lightning—faces in windows, faces in doorways, soldiers on the walls dropping their cranks and crossbows to stare. A hundred years of fear, and now the sky itself had come down wrapped around a single figure on the highest point of the city, blazing like a saint in a window, and the oldest people in Ashkarat began, in cracked and disbelieving voices, to say the old words. Prayers. Real ones. The kind that had gone unanswered so long that saying them had become a crime.

Above the clouds, in whatever place storms kept their hearts, Isandro felt the century-storm drink those prayers like rain after drought.

*This,* it seemed to say, in the rolling voice of the thunder. *This is what I was for.*

"Yes," Isandro whispered, tears cutting tracks through the rain on his face. "This is what we were both for."

* * *

The parasite felt it too.

He knew the moment it happened, because the lightning shuddered—a tremor running through the white architecture like a crack through glass. The storm's attention, which had been spread wide over the whole city in a posture Isandro could only call joy, snapped inward, focused, wary. An animal scenting an older animal.

Down in the palace, in the throne room, the thing wearing Aurelian lifted its head.

Isandro felt it the way he had felt it from the rafters days ago: a coldness, a hunger, a wrongness that predated the usurper's ambition by centuries. It had eaten the silence of the gods. It had drunk a hundred years of unanswered prayer. And now, for the first time since it had crawled into the world and put on a king like a glove, it felt the sky answering a mortal's call—and it recognized what it saw. The hermit had hinted at it. The records in the drowned chapel had named it. The sky was the parasite's ancient enemy, the one power it had never managed to devour, the vast unkillable thing above every temple it had ever hollowed out.

And someone had just taught it to answer.

The palace doors burst open far below. Isandro could not see it from the spire, but he felt it—felt the parasite pour out of the throne room like ink poured into water, abandoning the walls, abandoning the siege, abandoning the army that served it, because none of that mattered anymore. The Regent-who-wore-the-crown had spent fifteen years hiding from remembrance, hunting god-touched children, burning temples, erasing names from pages. All of it, Isandro understood suddenly, completely, had been fear. The parasite had not been building an empire.

It had been building a wall between itself and the sky.

And the sky had just come through the wall.

"It's coming," Isandro said aloud, though there was no one to hear. His hands were shaking. The lightning-architecture hummed around him, magnificent and fragile, and he knew—he knew with the certainty of the storm speaking through his own bones—that every arc, every pillar, every shield over every street now hung from a single thread, and that thread was him. If the parasite reached the spire before Sera finished below, it would not simply kill him. It would take the storm. It would take the city's new hope. It would take his brother, forever, and wear him into the ground until nothing was left to free.

The wind shifted. Down in the dark of the palace, something vast began to climb.

Isandro set his feet against the wet stone of the spire's crown, spread his arms into the roaring architecture of light, and spoke to the storm one last time—not a negotiation now, but something closer to a vow.

"Hold with me," he said. "Whatever comes up these stairs, whatever tears itself out of that palace—we hold until she cuts the knot. One hour. One minute. One heartbeat. However long it takes."

Thunder answered, rolling across the burning city like a promise.

And out of the darkness below, up through the guts of the palace, along the stairwells and the rafters and the ancient stones, the thing wearing the crown began to rise toward him—hungry, patient, and very, very old—while beneath Isandro's feet, deep in the binding chamber, a disgraced priestess lifted her knife over the knot of a stolen god and prepared to spend the only price either of them had left.
