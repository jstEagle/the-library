## 35. Unbinding

The binding chamber breathed.

Sera felt it the moment Maren's key turned in the last of seven locks and the door swung inward on air that had not moved in fifteen years. It was not stale, as a sealed room should be. It was *alive*—a slow, tidal exhalation from the dark below, carrying the smell of cold iron and old incense and something sweeter underneath, something rotten in the way honey goes rotten, thick and golden and wrong. The stairwell descended in a spiral so tight that the walls brushed her shoulders on both sides, and the stones were warm. Not sun-warm. Body-warm. The whole shaft was the throat of something that had been swallowing for a very long time.

"Stay behind me," Maren said, and it was such an absurd thing to say—Sera had no weapon but a satchel of chalk, candle-stubs, and a god's dying words memorized against her heartbeat—that Sera almost laughed. Almost. Her throat was too tight for laughter. She had been rehearsing this descent in her mind since Delmare, since the tide gate, since the first night she'd watched Isandro flinch from thunder in a refugee camp and thought: *there is a man who has been locked away from himself.* She had imagined fear. She had not imagined this—the sense of walking down into a wound.

The stairs ended at a circular chamber, and the chamber was older than the palace above it. That was the first thing she understood, standing on the threshold while Maren lifted her lantern high. Ashkarat was three hundred years old; its foundations were laid by merchant-kings who had bought their thrones with grain fleets. But this room predated them all. The stones were fitted without mortar, each one carved with script so old that only fragments survived in the liturgies Sera had been branded a heretic for preserving. She knew these characters. She had copied them by candlelight in a burned temple's cellar, believing they were metaphor.

*The sky remembers. The blood answers. What was split must be made whole.*

They were instructions. They had always been instructions.

At the center of the floor lay the knot.

She had expected a symbol, a sigil, something drawn. Instead it was a mass—a tangled sphere roughly the size of a curled sleeping child, suspended a hand's width above a basin of black stone, turning slowly on no axis she could name. It looked like rope made of shadow and lightning frozen mid-strike, like a nest woven from the hair of drowned things. Light came out of it in pulses, and each pulse was a color that hurt to look at directly—not because it was bright but because it was *old*, because her eyes kept trying to file it under colors that existed and failing. When it pulsed, the warmth in the walls pulsed with it. When it dimmed, Sera felt the room inhale.

"That's the anchor," she said. Her voice came out flat, professional, the voice she had used over sickbeds and funeral pyres. "That's what we cut."

"It's been there my whole career," Maren said quietly. "I've walked over this floor a hundred times. There were always guards at the door below the throne room. We were told it was a cistern." A pause. In the lantern light her face was gray, and there was fresh blood on the sleeve of her stolen officer's coat where a loyalist blade had found her in the corridors above. She had bound it herself, tightly, without comment, the way she did everything. "I believed them. Fifteen years of orders, and I never once asked what was under the throne."

"You're asking now."

"I'm asking now." Maren set the lantern on the basin's rim, and the shadows leaned away from it as if the knot disliked being seen. "How long do you need?"

Sera knelt at the edge of the circle carved into the floor—an outer ring of unbinding-script, worn nearly smooth, and inside it a second ring, and inside that a space exactly the size of a kneeling person. Two spaces, actually. She noticed that only now. Two kneeling-places, side by side, cut into stone that had waited fifteen years for someone to fill them. Her stomach turned over. Isandro had read the journal. Isandro had known about the second place, and he had said nothing, and she would deal with that later, if there was a later, with fury or forgiveness or both at once.

"As long as the storm holds," she said. "As long as he holds."

Above them—far above, up through the throne room and the gilded halls and the spire that crowned the palace like a needle through the city's heart—Isandro Almeida was walking into the largest storm in a century. She could not see him. She could feel him, faintly, the way you feel a fire in the next room: a pressure behind her sternum, a prickling along her arms where the old gods' returning voices gathered thickest. Since the shared-binding clause had passed between them in the queen's chambers—her words, plain and unforgiving, *you will not do this alone*—she had been able to feel him the way she once felt the presence of Vess. Small and flickering and stubbornly alive.

The siege had begun at dusk. Fire arrows were falling on the harbor districts even now; she had seen the glow through the tunnel grates, orange against the underside of the clouds. Somewhere out there the usurper's army was throwing itself at walls held by soldiers who were only just beginning to remember whom they served. And somewhere above her, a man who had spent fifteen years afraid of thunder was negotiating with the sky.

"Then we begin," Maren said, and drew her sword, and turned to face the stairwell. "No one passes me. Pray quickly."

* * *

Sera knelt in the first hollow and began.

The rite was not complicated. That was the horror of it, when she thought about it afterward—all those years of hidden liturgy, of whispered heresies, and the actual work of unbinding a king came down to chalk lines renewed, candles set at the four quarters, and words spoken in order without stopping. Vess's last gift had been simple almost past bearing: *the true heir's blood, the true heir's voice, spoken over the knot until it lets go of what it holds.* The complication was everything else. The complication was that the true heir was a mile above her, hanging his life on a hook of lightning. The complication was that the words required her to speak continuously, and every minute the parasite poured more of itself down the spire toward him, and every minute the knot above the basin turned faster, straining like an animal scenting freedom.

She opened her palm with the small ritual knife and let her blood fall into the groove cut around the inner ring.

"The sky remembers," she said. "The blood answers."

The knot stopped turning.

For three full seconds the chamber went utterly still—the candles' flames stood straight as needles, the air stopped breathing—and then the pulse of light from the tangled mass changed rhythm. Slower. Deeper. Like a heart waking from long sleep, or like an eye opening somewhere behind the world.

"What was split must be made whole," Sera continued, and her voice steadied as the liturgy took her the way a river takes a swimmer. This was the part they could never burn out of her, the part the temple had planted too deep: the words themselves were a kind of shelter. Whatever happened in this room tonight, whatever tore loose or poured down or died screaming, she would speak the old words correctly, in order, to the end. Faith had hollowed her out once. Tonight it filled her back up, and she hated how much it felt like coming home.

The blood in the groove began to move. It crept along the carved channels, thin and bright, tracing the outer ring, then the second, then spiraling inward toward the basin, and where it touched the stone the ancient script woke—filling with light the color of dawn through storm-clouds, gold shot through with gray. The two kneeling-hollows lit last. The empty one beside her glowed softly, patiently, waiting for a body that might never come to fill it.

And the knot screamed.

There was no other word. Sound came out of it that was not sound—pressure, mostly, a subsonic groan that rattled her teeth and made the candle flames lie flat—but threaded through it was something like a voice, something that had learned language by eating the people who spoke it. It did not use words. It used memories. The air in front of Sera filled suddenly with her temple: the cedar smoke, the choir loft, her teacher's hands folding hers over a hymnal, all of it rendered so perfectly that she reached out before she could stop herself—

It was feeding. Showing her the things she loved so it could drink the wanting.

"No," Sera said, and cut the memory off with the next line of the rite, speaking louder, pressing her bleeding palm flat to the floor. "What was taken, return. What was hidden, reveal. What was bound, be loosed."

The false visions shredded like smoke. The knot convulsed, and the whole chamber shuddered, dust sifting down from a ceiling that had held still for fifteen years, and Sera understood with sudden clarity that the thing knew exactly what was happening. It had eaten gods. It had worn a king. It was not going to sit in its nest and be cut loose politely.

Far above, through the stone, through the palace's bones, she felt the sky answer Isandro.

It came as light first—blue-white, flooding down through the spiral stairwell, strobing the walls—and then as sound, thunder arriving late and enormous, and then as a vibration in the floor that had nothing to do with the knot. The storm had reached the spire. He had reached the storm. And the parasite, feeling its oldest enemy answer a mortal's call, did exactly what she had known it would do, what Isandro had counted on it doing, what terrified her more than anything else in this room:

It poured itself down after him.

She felt it go. There was no seeing it—the knot remained above the basin, still tethered—but a great dark current ripped out of the chamber anyway, up through the stone, streaming toward the spire like a river reversing, and the knot dimmed to half its brightness, and the pressure in the air dropped so fast her ears popped. Most of the thing wearing the crown was leaving to deal with the man on the roof of the world. What remained in the anchor was the root of it, the oldest part, the part that had actually gripped Aurelian's soul fifteen years ago and never let go.

Which meant the king was here. Which meant the king was *hers.*

"Aurelian," she said—to the knot, to the room, to whatever fragment of a man was wrapped inside that tangle of devoured god-stuff. "Aurelian, son of Liora, true-born of Vhalen. Your brother calls the sky for you. Your mother wrote your name in hiding for fifteen years. I am Sera, defrocked of the temple of the Nine, and I have come to cut you free."

The knot's pulsing stuttered. And beneath the groan of the anchor, faint as a voice heard through a wall, something answered. Not words. A rhythm. A human rhythm, arrhythmic and desperate, nothing like the parasite's tidal pull.

A heartbeat. His heartbeat. Still in there. Still fighting.

Sera laughed—one broken, sobbing note—and renewed the chalk line at the eastern quarter, and kept speaking.

* * *

Maren killed the first soldier at the top of the stairs.

She heard him before she saw him—boots on stone, the particular careful tread of palace guard moving in pairs—and she stepped back into the alcove beside the doorway and let the lantern-light hide her and put her blade through the gap beneath the first man's jaw as he passed. He folded without a sound. The second had time to draw half a breath for a shout; Maren took it from him with a knee to the throat and the pommel across his temple, and lowered both bodies gently, because noise traveled down spiral stairwells, and downstairs a woman was performing surgery on the soul of a kingdom.

Her arm was bad. She admitted it to herself in the clinical way she admitted everything: the corridor wound had opened, the binding was soaked through, and the sword arm was losing perhaps a third of its strength with each minute. She recalculated. She had held doors against worse odds with better arms. What she had never done was hold a door for a miracle.

Inquisitor-General Maren had spent her adult life hunting god-touched, and she had done it with a clean conscience, because order was worth any price and miracles were chaos wearing a kind face. Then she had seen the Regent unmasked at midnight, seen what fed at the root of the throne, and the clean conscience had shattered along a fault line she hadn't known was there. Now she stood in a stairwell reeking of old rot, guarding a heretic priestess with her own dwindling blood, and the terrible thing—the thing she would carry if she lived—was that she felt *relieved*. Fifteen years of orders, and the first order she had ever chosen for herself was the only one that had ever felt clean.

Boots again. More of them. They had found the bodies.

"MAREN!" A voice she knew—Captain Ossler, twenty years in the palace guard, a man she had drunk with, a man whose daughter she had once pulled from a well. "Maren, stand away from the door! The Regent commands—"

"The Regent is dead," Maren called back, steady, buying seconds, watching the torchlight grow at the turn of the stair. "What wears him is not. Come and see, Ossler. Come and see what you've been guarding."

Silence. A long one. Long enough that she knew the words had landed somewhere soft and unguarded, because every soldier in this palace had felt the wrongness at the top of the tower and told themselves it was drafts.

Then the torchlight surged, and they came anyway—because orders were orders, and that was the whole tragedy of Maren's life in a single sentence—and she met them on the narrow stair where numbers meant nothing and her blade had nowhere to go but forward.

* * *

Below, Sera was losing the race.

She could measure it precisely, which was somehow worse. The rite had stages—she could feel them the way she'd once felt the hours of a vigil—and she was in the fifth of nine, and the knot was unraveling faster than the liturgy could loosen it, tearing rather than untying, because the parasite's greater mass was gone up the spire and the anchor was panicking, thrashing, trying to drag the king's soul deeper instead of releasing it. The heartbeat she'd found beneath the groan was stuttering now. Skipping. Being smothered.

"No you don't," Sera snarled, and did the forbidden thing. The journal had hinted at it; Vess's dying words had named it plainly; the shared-binding clause that Isandro had hidden from her and she had torn out of him with both hands. Two anchors. The second hollow, glowing patiently beside her, waiting.

She moved into it. Knees in the carved stone, palm still bleeding onto the script, she spoke the sixth stage with her own name added to the old words—*and Sera, defrocked, stands surety*—and the chamber *changed*. The light doubled. The load halved. She felt the knot's grip on the distant heartbeat slacken, felt the king's soul slide a hand's-width freer, and felt, simultaneously, something settle into her own chest that had not been there before: a second weight, a tether, thin as spider-silk and strong as iron, running from her sternum up through the stone, out the spire, into the storm.

Into him.

*Oh,* she thought, dizzy. *Oh, that's what he's been carrying. That's what it feels like to be tied to the sky.*

The knot shrieked. The remaining mass of the parasite convulsed upward, trying to follow its greater self, trying to abandon the anchor entirely—and Sera slammed the seventh stage of the rite down on it like a lid. "You don't get to leave," she said through her teeth, blood running freely now from a nose she didn't remember injuring, the words of the liturgy blurring wetly but not stopping. "You ate a god. You ate a king's years. You wore his crown and called it mercy. You don't get to leave. *What was taken, return.*"

The knot began, at last, to come apart.

Not tearing now—untying, strand by strand, each severed thread dissolving into light that fell upward, and beneath the unravelling mass a shape emerged that made Sera's breath catch: a man. Small, curled, translucent as glass, wrapped in the last coils like a fly in amber. Fifteen years folded small. A king the size of a sleeping child, and around his glassy throat, the final strand—the root, the oldest grip, the part that had slipped into the binding fifteen years ago and made the whole ritual corrupt.

Cut that, and the king wakes. Cut that wrong, and everything dies.

Above her, distantly, impossibly, she felt Isandro scream—not in pain, in *defiance*—and the storm answered with a sound like the world's spine breaking, and the whole palace lurched, and dust fell like snow, and Maren's sword rang against someone else's somewhere far above, and Sera raised the ritual knife over the final strand with a hand that shook and did not hesitate.

"The blood answers," she whispered. "Be loosed."

She cut.

The sound the king made when the thing wearing him tore loose was not a sound a human throat should hold. It started below hearing and climbed through every register at once—grief and rage and fifteen years of muffled screaming all released in a single note—and the last coil of the parasite whipped free of him, lashing, burning, streaming up the stairwell after its greater self with a hunger so vast and so suddenly *frustrated* that the stones it passed blackened. Sera threw herself over the small glass-bright figure of the king as it passed, and the edge of it caught her shoulder, and the pain was white and total and strangely cold, like being touched by the space between stars.

Then it was gone. Up. Toward the spire. Toward Isandro.

Toward the two anchors it now had no way to understand.

The knot was ash. The basin cracked down the middle with a sound like ice giving way in spring. And in the center of the ruined circle, unfolding, expanding, filling out from glass-small to man-sized in a rush of returning years, King Aurelian of Vhalen drew his first free breath in fifteen years and woke screaming.

Sera caught him. She was crying and laughing and bleeding from the shoulder and the nose, and she caught him as he came apart with terror, a grown man sobbing like a child, hands scrabbling at her sleeves, eyes wild and blind with light.

"It's over," she told him, holding his face, making him see her. "It's over, it's out, you're free. You're free, your majesty. Breathe. Just breathe."

His eyes focused, slowly, on her face. On the burned temple brand hidden under her collar. On the blood. On the knife.

"Where—" His voice was ruin, fifteen years unused. "Where is my brother?"

Sera's heart stopped.

Because he wasn't looking at her anymore. He was looking past her, up the blackened stairwell, toward the spire, toward the storm, toward the man holding the sky above their heads—and his face, raw and new-woken and stripped of everything the parasite had built over him, was breaking with recognition.

"He's here," Sera managed. "He's here, he came for you—"

"No." Aurelian was already pushing himself up, swaying, gripping the cracked basin, dragging himself toward the stairs on legs that hadn't borne weight in fifteen years. "No, you don't understand, I can hear him, I've *always* been able to hear him—" He looked at her, and his eyes were full of a grief so old it predated his imprisonment, and he said, in a whisper that carried through the ruined chamber like a bell:

"He's about to give it everything. I know that look. I grew up with that look. *Stop him.*"

Above them, the storm went silent.

And then the sky began to fall.
