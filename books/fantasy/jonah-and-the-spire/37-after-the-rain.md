## 37. After the Rain

The rain stopped at dawn, and Aurelian woke on the floor of his own throne room with the taste of iron in his mouth and a stranger's name caught between his teeth.

He did not know whose name it was. That was the first thing he understood about himself: that he was carrying something that belonged to someone else, the way a man carries a stone in his shoe long after the stone has been shaken out—the ache remaining, shaped exactly like the thing no longer there. He lay on cold marble among broken braziers and the black scorch of lightning that had come *down* through the roof, down through the spire itself, as though the sky had reached into the heart of his palace to take hold of something. His hands were clean. His hands were shaking.

*Isandro,* said the name in his mouth, silent, shapeless, a word he had never learned and could not forget.

He sat up. The great doors of the throne room stood open, and beyond them the palace corridors were full of light—not torchlight, not the gray gleam of the thing that had lived behind his eyes for fifteen years, but ordinary morning sun, washed thin by rain, falling through windows he had walked past ten thousand times without once seeing them. He could not remember walking past them. He could not remember most things. His memory began three days ago, or seemed to; everything before was a corridor of closed doors, and behind each door something breathed.

But he knew this much, the way he knew how to breathe: he had been asleep. Not resting—*asleep*, sealed, worn. Something had sat inside him the way a rider sits a horse, and it had looked out through his eyes at his court, his city, his kingdom, and it had smiled with his mouth, and everyone had believed the smile because what else is a king's smile for?

Aurelian put his hand against the floor and pushed himself upright. Somewhere below him—beneath the marble, beneath the foundations, down in the dark where the old binding chamber still smoked—he felt two small bright knots of presence, like coals banked for the night. They pulsed faintly, in time with each other. When he listened too closely they made his teeth ache, so he stopped listening, and filed the feeling away with all the other things he would have to understand later.

Later. There would be a great deal of later. The city was burning.

* * *

He walked out onto the high balcony above the Processional Way because some part of him, older than his memory, knew that this was where kings stood when cities needed to see them.

Ashkarat lay below him steaming in the early sun, and the sight of it stopped his breath. The siege had lasted six days. He knew this the way he knew his own name now—fragments arriving without provenance, dropped into his mind like coins into a well. Six days of fire arrows over the river districts. Six days of the usurper's—no. Not the usurper's. *His own* army, his own banners, the Iron Crown marching on its own king, commanded by men who believed they served the crown while the crown ate its wearer from inside. Six days of walls breached and held and breached again, of the temple quarter alight, of refugees packed into the undercrofts singing hymns to gods who had only just remembered how to listen.

And then last night, the storm.

He had felt it even through the parasite's grip—or perhaps the parasite had felt it and he had felt the parasite flinch, which amounted to the same confession. A storm the size of an age had come off the mountains and stood over Ashkarat like a judgment, and then it had not fallen. It had *built*. Lightning in architecture, in arches and buttresses and vaulted ceilings of white fire, a cathedral of weather erected over the spire, and within it a single figure that the whole besieged city had seen and the whole besieging army had seen, standing where no man could stand, negotiating with the sky.

The storm-caller. He knew that much. The storm-caller had come for him.

Below the balcony, the Processional Way was filling. It filled slowly at first—a handful of ash-streaked faces looking up, then dozens, then hundreds, pouring out of doorways and smoke-holes and the mouths of the undercrofts, drawn by some word already running through the streets faster than any crier could carry it: *the king is awake.* They came out into the wet morning blinking like creatures after a long winter, and they looked up at him, and they waited.

Aurelian gripped the balustrade. His knuckles were white. He had, he realized, no speech prepared, no speech remembered, no idea what a restored king says to a city half in ruins on the first morning of his actual life. Everything he might have said had been said before, by the thing wearing him, in his voice, and every one of those words was poisoned now.

So he said the truest thing he had.

"I don't remember you," he called down, and his voice cracked on the second word, and carried anyway, the way voices carry in the hush after storms. "I don't remember your names, or your streets, or the laws I signed, or the faces I've betrayed by sleeping through it. Fifteen years, and I was not in them. Whatever wore me wore me well enough that none of you saw the seams—I know you didn't, because I can't find them either." A murmur moved through the crowd like wind through wheat. He let it move. "I won't stand here and claim a restoration I can't feel. What I can tell you is this: I am awake now, and I intend to stay awake, and I will spend the rest of my life learning what I lost—and the first lesson is that I did not free myself."

He turned, deliberately, so the whole square could see him look toward the spire.

"A man stood on that spire last night," he said, "and asked the sky for help, and the sky said yes. I am told his name is Isandro. I am told he crossed half the world to reach a king he'd never met. I am told—" and here his voice did fail him briefly, because the phantom limb ached, the stone-shaped absence in his shoe, the name-shaped hole where a brother should be— "I am told he paid more than any of us will ever fully know. So hear my first decree as a waking king: whatever he asks of this crown, the answer is yes before he finishes asking. And if he asks for nothing—then we owe him everything anyway, and we will spend our lives finding out how to pay a debt to a man who won't name it."

The silence that followed was not empty. It was the silence of several thousand people deciding, together, to believe him. Then somewhere in the crowd a woman began to sing—one of the old hymns, a god-song, banned for fifteen years and sung anyway in cellars and kitchens—and the song spread the way fire spreads through dry grass, until the Processional Way was full of it, ragged and off-key and unmistakably alive, rising past the balcony like incense.

Aurelian stood in it and wept, quietly, in front of everyone, and found that this too was something a king could do.

* * *

They came to him that afternoon in the ruined map room: the survivors of the night, the ones the songs were already being written about.

Sera came first—a priestess, defrocked, her order burned, her faith visibly reassembled around her like scar tissue. She walked with a limp she tried to hide and a bandage at her collarbone she didn't bother to, and when she bowed to him she did it the way one bows to an equal who has been foolish, which he suspected was accurate. Behind her came a tall woman in the gray-and-black of the Inquisition, out of uniform, unarmed, carrying her former office like a wound she had chosen to keep open. Maren. He knew the name from fragments: the hunter who had hunted him, who had seen the thing wearing him unmasked and turned rather than look away. And behind her, half in shadow, a young man with storm-grey eyes and burn scars along one arm, moving carefully, as though the air around him were a horse that might startle.

Isandro.

The name landed in Aurelian's chest and did not land anywhere else. No memory rose to meet it. No face from the corridor of closed doors stirred behind its hinges. And yet—

It was like standing in a house he had lived in all his life and finding one room bricked over. He knew the room was there. He could feel the dimensions of it, the way sound changes near a hidden space. When Isandro stepped into the light of the map room's shattered window, something behind Aurelian's sternum leaned toward him like a plant toward sun, and Aurelian had to grip the edge of the table to keep from reaching out—to touch what? He didn't know. A stranger's shoulder. A brother's. The absence didn't say.

"You're staring," Sera said gently.

"Forgive me." He made himself stop. "You'll have to forgive a great deal, all of you, for a long time."

"We're told that's the job now," said Maren, dry as old paper, and something in her tone made clear she had decided, provisionally and permanently, that she would hold him to it.

They gave him the account in pieces, correcting one another, finishing one another's sentences—the valley, the god Vess, the letters, the gaol, the ford, the tunnels, the spire—and through all of it Aurelian watched Isandro, and Isandro watched him back with an expression Aurelian could not read and could not stop trying to. There was grief in it. There was something that looked almost like peace. There was, underneath both, a patience like deep water, as though the young man had already mourned something and was waiting, courteously, for Aurelian to catch up to the funeral.

At the end of it, Aurelian asked the question he had been holding since the balcony.

"Why doesn't the binding release? You split it—you and the priestess—you should both be free."

Isandro glanced at Sera. Some whole conversation happened between them in that glance, years' worth, compressed. "The parasite isn't dead," he said. "It's starved. Split between two anchors, it can't gather enough of itself to wear anyone—it just leaks. Weather, mostly. Odd warm winds. Rain that falls upward if you're unlucky." A ghost of a smile. "We're the corks in the bottle. We stay corked."

"For how long?"

"For good, probably." Isandro shrugged, and the shrug cost him something visible. "It's not nothing. We feel it. Every prayer in Vhalen comes through us now like water through a channel—we're how the gods get their hearing back, apparently. But it's not chains, either. It's—" he searched— "it's work. Worth doing."

"And you?" Aurelian said, and heard his own voice go quiet and careful. "What does it cost *you*?"

The pause stretched. Sera's hand found Isandro's sleeve—not restraining, just present—and Isandro covered it with his own and looked at the king with those grey eyes full of everything he was not going to say.

"Less than it would have," he said at last. "That's what she's for."

Aurelian nodded slowly. He wanted to demand more. He wanted, with a ferocity that startled him, to seize the young man by the shoulders and shake loose whatever was bricked over between them—*tell me what I don't remember, tell me whose childhood name you know, tell me why the word 'brother' sits in my mouth like a coin under the tongue whenever you enter a room.* But he had spent fifteen years being worn by a thing that took what it wanted without asking, and he would sooner cut off his own hand than begin his true reign the same way. So instead he did the only kingly thing left to him, the thing the crowd on the Processional Way had already taught him.

He trusted.

"The council will want to give you titles," he said. "Lands. A place at court—they'll fight over who gets to stand nearest you. Refuse all of it if you like; I'll sign the refusal myself. But I ask one thing in return, and it isn't gratitude, and I want you to understand the difference." He came around the table, slowly, favoring a stiffness in his legs he couldn't explain—fifteen years of a body used by something else, and the body remembering its own use the way a scar remembers the blade. "Gratitude is a debt discharged. Trust is a debt *kept*. I'm asking you to let me keep it. Let me write to you. Let me learn you the way I'd have learned you if the world hadn't been stolen from us both—from whichever of us it was stolen. Let me be wrong about you for years, if that's what it takes, rather than right about you never."

For the first time, Isandro's composure cracked—not into grief, but into something younger, something that might have been a boy in a palace nursery laughing at nothing, gone before it could be named.

"That," Isandro said, "is the first thing anyone's offered me in fifteen years that I actually want."

* * *

Evening came, and with it the first report of the parasite's dissolution, delivered by a breathless temple acolyte: all across Ashkarat, the wrongness was draining out of the air. The cold spots in the palace corridors were warming. The dogs that had howled at the throne room for fifteen years had stopped. And the weather—Maren herself brought this news, almost smiling, which those who knew her would count among the age's genuine miracles—was turning *ordinary*. A soft south wind. Clouds doing nothing but drifting. Over the harbor, witnesses swore, a small squall had formed, rained politely on one fishing boat, apologized, and dispersed.

Starved and split, leaking through two anchors into the wide air, the god-eater was becoming weather. Nothing more than weather. Rain on roofs. Wind in laundry lines. The most dangerous thing Vhalen had ever known, reduced at last to the common business of the sky—which was, Aurelian thought, watching the sunset gild the smoke still rising from the temple quarter, perhaps what it had always secretly envied.

He stood alone on the balcony again as the lamps of the city came alight, district by district, a constellation reassembling itself after a long eclipse. Below, crews were already pulling rubble from the Processional Way. Somewhere a smith's hammer had started up again, absurdly, undiscouragably, beating metal in the dark as if tomorrow were a certainty. Perhaps it was. He was beginning to suspect that certainty was simply what people built when they refused to wait for proof.

Behind him, in the doorway, a servant coughed respectfully. "Your majesty. A letter has come up from the guest wing. The storm-caller's party departs at first light—he wished you to have this before they went."

Aurelian broke the seal. Inside, a single line, in a strong, careful hand:

*You'll want to know what you forgot. Come to Stillwater someday, when the roads are safe. Bring nothing. Ask for the ferryman's daughter.*

He read it three times. The words did not unlock anything—but they *leaned*, the way the hidden room leaned, the way the bricked-over space behind the wall leans when you press your palm flat against it. Stillwater. A valley he had never heard of, in mountains he could not picture, waiting for him like a letter addressed in handwriting he almost recognized.

Aurelian folded the note and put it inside his shirt, over his heart, where the phantom limb ached.

"Have my horse saddled at dawn," he said to the servant. "Not to ride. Just so I remember I can."

Far to the north, over a small green valley where the wardens had gone home and children were already daring each other to fly kites in the spring storms, thunder rolled—gentle, unhurried, almost fond—and the rain that followed fell soft on the roof of a ferryman's daughter's house, where two travelers slept the dreamless sleep of people whose watch had finally, finally ended.
