## 38. Two Anchors

The dreams came every night now, but they were not nightmares.

Isandro had expected them to be. For fifteen years the Valley of Stillness had kept his sleep as flat and gray as its water, and when the binding was remade—when he and Sera had stepped together into the anchor and let it close around them like two hands clasped—he had braced himself for a lifetime of the parasite's hunger whispering through his dreams.

Instead he dreamed of weather.

Not storms, exactly. Not the century-storm he had negotiated over Ashkarat's spire, lightning becoming architecture around him while the god-eater poured itself up the stone toward him like a tide of black glass. That memory lived in him too, always would, the way a scar lives in skin. But beneath it, threaded through it, ran something gentler: rain falling on far-off fields that needed it. Wind turning someone's mill wheel in a village whose name he did not know. The slow, patient breathing of pressure systems crossing the sea.

The residue of the parasite, Sera called it. The part of it that had starved down to nothing but appetite for *weather itself*—for the old conversation between sky and soil—and which, split between two anchors, could no longer devour, only listen.

"You're smiling," Sera said from across the fire.

Isandro opened his eyes. They were camped in the ruins of a roadside shrine three days east of Ashkarat, one of hundreds the war had emptied and one of dozens they had already begun to put back in order. Sera sat cross-legged on the fallen lintel, mending a strap of her pack by candlelight, her defrocked priestess's braid coming loose the way it always did by evening. The candle flame leaned toward her, the way flames did now. Toward both of them, really. Gods returning to the world found anchors the way water found low ground.

"I was dreaming about rain," he said. "In Vessany, I think. The wheat country."

"Vess is there this week." She said it lightly, but Isandro heard what underlay it—the same thing he felt every time they spoke of the small flickering god who had come to a riverbank begging help and burned itself nearly to nothing covering his escape. Vess was recovering. Candle by candle, prayer by prayer, the little god was rekindling. But gods did not forget what they had spent, and neither did the people who loved them.

"It sent word?" he asked.

"A farmer's prayer. It answered with a good growing season and then complained to me in a dream that farmers only ever pray about weather." Sera bit off her thread. "It said you'd understand. It said you were the only mortal alive who'd ever negotiated with a storm and lost the argument."

"I didn't lose the argument."

"You gave it *hail*."

"The storm wanted hail. I wanted no deaths. We compromised on hail and no deaths. That's not losing, that's diplomacy."

Sera laughed—that real laugh, unguarded, the one he had first heard on the pilgrim road when she'd caught him lying about his name and decided to pretend to believe him anyway. Fifteen years of silence in the valley, and he had forgotten what laughter sounded like until she'd given it back to him somewhere between Corvane's curfew streets and the drowned chapel where he'd learned his own name had been erased from history.

He still did not know which twin he was.

That was the truth the shared binding had left him, the price within the price. The unbinding had returned everything the original ritual took—but it had been split, diluted, divided between two anchors the way a river divides around a stone. He had his memories back: Queen Liora's voice reading to two boys in a palace garden, the smell of orange blossom, a brother's hand in his. But the memories came to him like light through deep water, and some of them belonged, perhaps, to Aurelian. Some nights he dreamed of learning to ride in the palace paddocks and could not say whether the boy on the pony had been him or the brother who wore the crown. The binding had been made to blur them; even undone, it left its smudge.

Aurelian remembered nothing at all. And yet—

*You feel it too,* Isandro thought, looking east, toward the city whose spires were visible on clear evenings as a gray-blue serration against the sky. *You feel the absence like a phantom limb. You honored two strangers with trust instead of gratitude because something in you knew.*

* * *

The summons had come a month after the siege ended, delivered by a herald in royal blue who rode out to find them at the shrine they were restoring outside Delmare.

His Majesty King Aurelian requests the presence of the Storm-Caller and the Lady Sera at court, it read, in script so formal it might have been carved. There followed a list of titles and honors so long that Sera read it aloud twice just to laugh at it—*Defender of the Realm, Bearer of the Century-Storm, First Friend of the Crown*—and then a single handwritten line at the bottom, in a different hand, less practiced:

*I am told we have met before. I regret that I do not remember it. Come anyway. — A.*

They had gone. Of course they had gone. Isandro had walked the gilded corridors of a palace he half-remembered from dreams that might not be his, past guards who straightened as he passed—not because of his titles, he realized, but because some dim, buried loyalty in their bones recognized whom they truly served. He had stood in the restored throne room, beneath the spire where lightning had become architecture, and watched a young king who looked like a stranger and like a mirror at once descend the steps to meet him.

"Ninety seconds," Aurelian had said quietly, before the heralds could announce anything. "I'm told I was awake for ninety seconds, once, and knew you. I don't remember it. But I've had a year of people telling me who I should be grateful to, and none of them make my chest hurt the way your name does when the criers shout it." He had looked at Isandro with eyes the exact color of their mother's—of *their* mother's—and said, "So I won't pretend. I'll just ask. What do you need?"

What Isandro needed was impossible to put in a herald's language. He needed his brother to remember orange blossoms. He needed the smudged memories sorted into their rightful owners. He needed the fifteen stolen years back, and the mother whose letters he had wept over in a lighthouse, and the childhood name only Aurelian had ever spoken.

None of that was available. So he had asked for smaller things: wardens withdrawn from the valley of Stillness forever. Pardons for the god-touched registered during the curfew. Rebuilding funds for the free cities' shrines. And a stipend, modest, unremarkable, drawn annually, for "two travelers who tend holy places along the roads."

Aurelian had granted all of it without hesitation, and then, as they turned to leave, had said the thing Isandro carried now like a smooth stone in his pocket:

"My physicians say the absence will never fill. They call it grief without an object." The king had almost smiled. "I've decided to stop calling it that. I've decided it's a door, and doors are meant to be waited beside. Go do whatever it is you two do. But come back sometimes. Knock. Even if I never open it all the way—I'd rather know it's there."

* * *

Now, a year later, they were on the road again, and the road was full of voices.

That was the other thing the shared binding had done. Anchors to the remade binding sat at the hinge point between the world and the returning gods, and the gods, finding their voices again after fifteen years of silence, had a great deal to say. Most mortals prayed and hoped something heard. Isandro and Sera *heard*—not words, usually, but pressures, weathers of meaning, the way you can hear distant surf through a shell. A shrine that wanted tending hummed like a struck bell. A god arriving in a region announced itself as a change in atmospheric pressure. Grief, unanswered for a decade and a half, pooled in certain valleys like cold air.

"We're going to be busy for the rest of our lives," Sera had said once, early on, surveying a map dotted with silent shrines. She hadn't sounded dismayed. She'd sounded like a woman whose faith, hollowed out and refilled, had finally found its shape.

Tonight the nearest voice was a small one. Isandro had felt it since sundown—a thin, persistent plucking at the edge of his awareness, southeast, maybe four miles off the road. Not a god. Something younger. The binding translated it for him as best it could, and what came through was less a prayer than a child's stubbornness: the spiritual equivalent of someone knocking on a door because they'd been told no one was home.

"There's a caller," he said. "Southeast. Young. Untrained—they're pushing at the weather without knowing what they're doing. If a squall comes through tonight, they'll catch it in the teeth."

Sera was already rolling her map closed. "How young?"

"Ten? Eleven. The voice feels—" He searched for it. "Lonely. Practicing in secret. Someone told them it was wicked."

"Someone told *you* it was wicked, once. The whole valley told you." She kicked dirt over the candle. "We'll reach them by midnight if we cut through the olive terraces."

They walked by moonlight, and Isandro thought about how far the road bent from where anyone had planned it. He had crossed the valley rim intending to hide, or to die, or possibly both. Sera had fled a burned temple intending never to hope again. Vess had arrived at a ferry as a guttering ember intending, probably, nothing so grand as saving a kingdom—it had only known a name and a debt. And now here they were, two anchors walking through olive groves in the dark to find a frightened child, carrying between them a god's last ember, a queen's letters, an inquisitor's testimony, and a king's unfinished grief.

Maren's book was in his pack. She had died at the binding chamber door—her wounds had accumulated past bearing, Sera said, using the clinical phrasing Maren herself would have preferred—and her final act had been to press her journal into Isandro's hands with the instruction that the truth be recorded, all of it, including her own years of service to appetite wearing order's uniform. He had copied it fair in the winter months, by firelight, in shrine after shrine. Someday it would go to the palace archive. Not yet. Truth, he had learned, needed to ripen before it was served.

* * *

They found the child at midnight in the hollow below an abandoned watchtower, standing in a circle of scorched grass with her arms raised at the sky, arguing with a cloud.

She was small and furious and soaked to the bone, and when she saw them she flinched so hard she nearly fell, throwing up a wall of wind that knocked Isandro back a step—he let it knock him back; startling a child caller into panic was how villages burned—and shouted, "I'm not doing anything wrong! Nobody's supposed to be here! Go away!"

"Squall's going to ground-strike inside the hour," Isandro said calmly, over the wind. "You're holding it above yourself because you think if you keep it up there it can't hurt anyone. It can't hold. When it breaks, it breaks on you."

The girl's eyes went wide. The wind faltered. "How do you know that?"

"Because I did the same thing for fifteen years, in a valley where the sky couldn't answer me, and when it finally could, I leveled a ridge by accident." He knelt slowly, palms open, rain beginning to spot the dust around them. "My name is Isandro. This is Sera. We teach callers. Would you like to learn how to *ask* the storm instead of fighting it?"

The child stared at him. Rain ran down her fierce little face. Above them the squall muttered and shifted, feeling, Isandro knew, the presence of two anchors and one untrained spark, the way a dog feels a strange dog at the edge of its yard.

"It talks back?" she whispered.

"It negotiates," said Sera. "Storms are vain and reasonable, like priests. You give them shape and purpose and they give you their strength. But nobody teaches it anymore, because everyone forgot the gods were listening." She held out her hand. "We're remembering."

The girl looked at the offered hand, at the sky, at the hand again. Then she took it, and the squall overhead, sensing a new student, rumbled what Isandro chose to interpret as approval.

Her name was Tavi. Her village was two miles south. Her grandmother had told her the old prayers when the gods were silent, and Tavi had prayed anyway, every night, to a god she'd never seen, and one evening the candle on the shrine had leaned toward her, and she had screamed, and hidden it, and practiced in secret ever since.

"Which god?" Sera asked gently, as they walked her home through the rain.

"The little one. The candle god." Tavi frowned. "Vess. It says it knows you. It says—" she cocked her head, listening to something Isandro could only feel as a faint warm pressure at the edge of the binding, "—it says to tell the tall one that the wheat in Vessany came in *magnificent*, and that he still owes it an apology about the hail."

Isandro laughed until his ribs hurt, there in the rain, with a child caller grinning up at him and Sera shaking with laughter beside him and the whole night sky negotiating politely overhead.

Peace, he understood then, was not a place like the valley. It was not silence. It was this: work worth doing, love that told you the truth, a burden shared until it became a shape you could carry. Earned, not given. Paid for, and worth the price.

They reached Tavi's village at dawn. Her grandmother wept and fed them bread. By noon, three more children had crept out of the fields to stare at the strangers who talked to weather, and Sera had cleared a space in the granary for lessons, and Isandro stood in the doorway watching rain fall kindly on wheat and thought: *this is the life. This is enough.*

Then the eastern horizon flickered.

Not lightning. He knew lightning intimately, had negotiated with it, had built architecture from it. This was different—a pulse in the pressure of the world, a tremor running through every anchor-hinge in his chest, and beside him Sera went rigid, her head snapping east, her face draining pale.

"What is it?" Tavi asked, frightened. "What's wrong?"

Isandro stared east, toward Ashkarat, toward the spire where bells had begun to ring for reasons no one yet understood, and felt the binding pull taut as a bowstring.

"The sky remembers something," he said quietly. "And it's afraid."
