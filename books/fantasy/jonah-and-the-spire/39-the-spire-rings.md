## 39. The Spire Rings

The bell in the great spire of Ashkarat had not rung in fifteen years. It had hung there all that time, green with verdigris, its rope cut and coiled at the base of the tower like a snake someone had decided was better off dead. Children grew up beneath it without ever hearing its voice. Old men forgot it had one. The city learned to measure its hours by the drums of the garrison and the cries of the fish-sellers, and if anyone remembered the bell at all, they remembered it the way one remembers a dead relative's handwriting—with a small ache, quickly set aside.

Then, on a morning in the first spring of the new reign, King Aurelian of Vhalen climbed two hundred and forty steps on his own feet, took the rope in both hands, and pulled.

The sound went out over the terracotta roofs like a wave going out over sand. It rolled down the Street of Lanterns and into the temple quarter, where masons were already at work unbricking doors that had been sealed since the Silence. It crossed the river and woke the gulls on the breakwater. It reached the poor districts first and last, as bells do, and there an old woman named Katrin—who had prayed to Vess every night for fifteen years into a silence so complete she had begun to suspect her own madness—set down her water jars in the middle of the street and wept without shame while her neighbors pretended not to see, because everyone in those streets had someone to weep for, and pretending not to see was the courtesy they had perfected during the long quiet.

The bell rang, and the gods heard it.

That was the miracle no chronicle quite managed to capture: not that the bell rang, but that something answered. A flicker of candlelight in the shrine of the Hearth-Mother where no candle had been lit. The smell of rain arriving half a day ahead of the rain itself. In the rebuilt chapel beneath the Street of Weavers—the drowned chapel, raised and drained now, its records copied by careful scribes—a small flame stood upright on the altar with no wick beneath it, and when pilgrims came it inclined, very slightly, the way a person inclines their head to acknowledge a friend across a crowded room.

Vess was smaller than it had been. Everyone who served there knew it and did not say it aloud, the way one does not say aloud that a grandmother has grown thin. The god had burned most of what it was covering a fugitive's escape through the Tide Gate, and then given away nearly all the rest on a night of storm above Ashkarat, pressing its last ember into the hands of a man standing on a spire with the sky coming down around him. What remained was a candle-flame's worth of divinity, and yet it persisted, and it listened, and slowly—gods are patient even when they are small—it began to grow again, fed on prayers that were finally being answered rather than merely spoken.

The new prayers were strange ones, at first. For fifteen years the faithful had prayed the old prayers out of habit, into a silence, expecting nothing; now that expectation had to be unlearned, and learning to hope again turned out to be harder than learning to despair had been. Priests of the restored orders stumbled through rites reconstructed from smuggled fragments and half-burned books. There were arguments—there would be arguments for a generation—about which prayers were true prayers and which were nostalgia wearing prayer's clothes. But the gods, it seemed, were not particular. They answered the clumsy prayers and the elegant ones alike. They answered the child who asked for rain on the barley and the widow who asked only to be heard. What they seemed to want, more than incense or precision, was simply to be addressed again—to be treated as present.

King Aurelian understood this better than his counselors did, though he could not have said why. He had come back from whatever far country the parasite had kept him in with gaps in him the shape of a life, and he had learned to live among the gaps the way a sailor learns to live with a missing finger: compensating, adapting, occasionally reaching for something that wasn't there and closing his hand on air. He did not remember a brother. He did not remember a childhood name whispered in a binding chamber ninety seconds before the world slammed shut. But some mornings, waking, he felt an absence beside him in the bed like the impression left by a body that had risen early, and he would lie still a moment, honoring it, before calling for his attendants.

He honored other absences too. When the council of free cities sent their delegates to Ashkarat to negotiate the new compact, the king received them not as a conqueror receiving petitioners but as a debtor receiving creditors, and everyone in the hall understood that the debt was real even though no ledger recorded it. Two names came up often in those negotiations, always casually, always attached to some impossible thing: the storm that had held the ford at Serel while four hundred refugees crossed; the lightning that had walked the walls of Ashkarat like a mason inspecting bad work and finding none of it worth striking. *The storm-caller,* people said. *And the priestess who walks with him.* The king never claimed to know them. But when the Delmare delegation proposed a new office—a Warden of Storms, charged with teaching weather-craft to a new generation—he approved it before his ministers finished explaining it, and he endowed it richly, and when asked why, he said only: "Some debts you pay forward."

* * *

Far to the north, in a valley cupped between mountains like water in a stone hand, the wardens had gone home.

They had gone quietly, as they did everything. One autumn morning the gray-robed figures who had walked Stillwater's lanes for a generation, mute and watchful, enforcing the stillness with their mere presence, were simply absent from their stations at the ferry landing and the shrine path and the rim road. Their quarters stood open, swept clean, their robes folded on their bunks. On the boundary stone at the valley's mouth, where for fifteen years a carved sigil had drunk magic out of the air like a drain drinking water, someone had chiseled a single deep line through the sigil's heart, breaking it, and the valley had exhaled.

What rushed in was everything the stillness had held out. Birds sang louder—or perhaps the villagers had forgotten how loud birdsong was. The river, which had run with a kind of dutiful flatness, developed opinions: eddies, whitecaps, a low constant conversation over its stones. And the sky, that most changeable of things, remembered that it was allowed to change.

The first storm came in the second week. Odile, the old shrine keeper, stood in her doorway and watched the clouds pile up over the western peaks with an expression the village had never seen on her face before, and afterward several people swore it had been fear, and several others swore it had been joy, and Odile herself, when asked, said only: "It was about time," and went inside to put her kettle on.

She was nearly eighty now. Her hands shook when she lit the shrine candles, but they did not shake when she taught, and she taught every evening now, in the shrine's back room, to whoever came—which was, increasingly, everyone. Because the valley that had forgotten how to hold magic had not forgotten everything else, and it turned out that fifteen years of enforced stillness had pressed certain knowledge down into the deep sediment of the place, the way floodwater presses gold into a riverbed. Old recipes for salves that worked better than they should. Prayers that, spoken correctly, made the fire burn steady in wind. Small craft, hedge-craft, the humble domestic miracles that had lived in the valley before the wardens came and would live in it now that they were gone. Odile taught it all without ceremony, and when children asked her where she had learned such things, she said, "From a letter I kept too long," and refused to elaborate, and the children learned that some questions were doors better left closed until they opened on their own.

The ferry ran again under a new ferryman—the daughter of the old one, broad-shouldered and sun-brown, with her father's easy laugh and her mother's stubborn chin. Mira Eastwick had been a girl when the storm-caller left the valley; she remembered him as a tall, quiet man who mended nets and flinched at thunder, who had once carried her, feverish, four miles upriver through the rain to the shrine because the ferry was grounded and he hadn't waited to argue about it. She remembered that he had hated storms. She thought about that sometimes, poling her ferry across a river that laughed and argued with itself, watching her own children play on the bank.

Because her children played in storms now. That was the thing visitors found hardest to believe, when traders began coming up the rim road again after years of nothing: the children of Stillwater flew kites in weather that would have emptied the streets of any other town in Vhalen. It had started as a dare, the way these things do—one boy, the cooper's son, running out into the first spring storm with a kite stitched from an old flour sack—and the lightning had come down and the whole village had frozen, and then the lightning had *gone around*, bending wide of the boy as though the storm itself had stepped aside to let him pass, and the kite had climbed, and climbed, wild and glorious at the end of its string, while thunder applauded overhead.

No one could explain it. Odile, pressed on the matter, offered a theory she shared with no one but her kettle: that a storm is a guest, and remembers hospitality. That somewhere in the world, a man who had once feared thunder had learned instead to speak with it, and had taught it manners, or been taught them, and that storms, being creatures of habit once they find a pattern they like, extended the courtesy to the valley that had sheltered him. Whether this was true no one could say. But the kites flew, year after year, and the mothers of Stillwater stopped calling their children in from the rain, and the valley that had been famous for its silence became famous instead—for a while, anyway, until fame moved on as fame does—for the sight of a dozen paper dragons dancing in the teeth of a thunderhead while their owners shrieked with laughter below.

On the shrine wall, in a place of honor that no one had ordered and no one dared remove, hung a framed scrap of parchment behind glass: a single line in a firm hand, *The crown lies*, above a royal seal. Pilgrims sometimes came just to look at it. Odile would tell them the story if they asked, and embellish it freely, and the story grew a little each year, as stories do, until the quiet net-mender who had once lived by the ferry became in the telling a giant-slayer, a dragon-speaker, a king in exile. Odile knew better. She also knew that the truth needed no embossing: that an ordinary frightened man had walked out of safety into the teeth of the world because staying would have cost others their freedom, and that this, in the end, was the whole of heroism, and rare enough to be miraculous.

* * *

And somewhere on a road—this is the part the chronicles get wrong, or leave out entirely, because chronicles prefer thrones to roads—two travelers heard thunder and smiled.

It was late afternoon in the hill country south of the Saltmarshes, that country of grey stone and yellow gorse where the road runs high along a ridge with the whole sky laid open above it. The storm had been building since noon, walking up from the sea in slow purple ranks, and any sensible traveler would have looked for shelter. These two did not hurry. The woman walked with a slight hitch in her stride that rain always worsened—an old wound from a gaol cell, though she spoke of it as lightly as she spoke of everything—and the man matched his pace to hers without appearing to, the way he had done for years, the way one does when the matching has become involuntary.

Sera saw the lightning first. She always did; she claimed the gods tipped her off, and Isandro claimed she simply watched the horizon more than he did, and neither of them had ever conceded the point, because arguing about it was one of the pleasures of the road.

"East ridge," she said. "Big one. Feel it?"

He closed his eyes. Fifteen years of silence, then five years of listening, and still the feeling arrived fresh every time: a pressure behind the sternum, a taste like coins, the sense of something vast turning over in its sleep and beginning, slowly, to wake. "Feel it," he said. "It's angry about something. Dry summer. It wants to give and nobody's asked it properly."

"Then ask it properly." She smiled sideways at him. "You're getting soft. Five years ago you'd have been up that ridge already, negotiating terms."

"Five years ago I leveled a mountain because I sneezed near it." He shrugged. "I've learned patience. Mostly from you."

"Mostly from the storms. I take no credit." But she was pleased; he could tell; after all these years he could read her the way he read weather, in small pressures and shifts of light.

They found the farmhouse an hour later—a low stone place with sheep in the dooryard and a family on the porch watching the sky with the wariness of people who had heard what the storm-caller could do and hoped, without daring to say it, that rumors traveled. Sera did the talking, as she always did. By the time the first fat drops fell, she had learned the farmer's wife's sister was ill with a fever the local remedies wouldn't touch, and had promised to look in on the way back; by the time the thunder arrived, the children had been promised a story; and by full dark, with the storm roaring over the hills like a congregation finally allowed to sing, Isandro stood in the farmyard with his face turned up to the rain, his arms slightly out from his sides, doing the work no one watching would have seen at all.

It did not look like power. It looked like attention. He listened to the storm the way one listens to an old friend who talks too much and means well, nodding here, murmuring there, and where his attention rested, the storm gentled—not tamed, never tamed, but *considered*. The lightning that might have struck the barn struck the lightning rod instead, precisely, almost politely. The hail that might have shredded the barley fields spent itself on the stony pasture where nothing grew. Once, briefly, the clouds parted above the dooryard, and the children on the porch screamed with delight at the moon, and Isandro laughed—a sound the storm seemed to catch and carry, rolling it away eastward like a gift.

"You're showing off," Sera said, when he came in dripping, shaking rain from his hair like a dog.

"I'm being polite."

"You're showing off *politely*." She handed him a towel. Their fingers touched around it and stayed touching a moment longer than the towel required, an economy of gesture they had refined over years until it said everything and cost nothing.

Later, when the farmhouse slept, they sat in the kitchen by the banked fire and did what they always did on nights like this: felt along the edges of the binding. It was quieter now. The parasite—what remained of it, split between two anchors, starved of the god-stuff it had fed on for fifteen years—had dwindled to something little worse than a chronic ache, a cold spot in the chest that stirred only when great wrongs were done nearby. They felt it now, faintly, both of them at once, the way two people sharing a blanket feel the same draft. And beyond it, past the ache, the other thing: the voices. The gods, returning one by one to the world like lights being relit in the windows of a dark house, some of them familiar, some of them strange, all of them hungry to be addressed.

"Vess sent word," Sera said. "Through the chapel. It's grown another inch, it says. It can light a whole candelabra now without resting after."

"It'll be burning braziers by the time we're old."

"It says—" she hesitated, and he waited, because he had learned that her hesitations were load-bearing—"it says the hearth-shrine in Stillwater answered forty-one prayers last month. Forty-one. From one valley. It says the valley is *loud* now, Isandro. Loud with the small stuff. Kettle-prayers and lambing-prayers. It says that's the healthiest sound a god can hear."

He was quiet a while, watching the fire. "Do you ever miss it?" he asked. "The silence?"

"No." Then, honestly: "Yes. Some nights. The silence was simple. You could be tired in it. Now there's always someone asking." She leaned her head against his shoulder. "But I was hollow in it. I'd rather be tired than hollow. You taught me that."

"I didn't teach you anything. You marched me out of hiding with a stick and a forged passport."

"And you've thanked me for it exactly once, and I'm saving it up." 

Outside, the storm was moving off eastward, grumbling its way toward the coast, satisfied. Somewhere under it, in towns they would never see, people were sleeping soundly through weather that would once have terrified them, because word had spread the way good word spreads: not as decree but as rumor, as story, as a child's report that the lightning goes around if you ask nicely. The world was not fixed. They both knew that better than anyone. The parasite's kin were still out there in the deep places, older hungers than hunger; the free cities squabbled; the king ruled wisely but slept badly, reaching in the night for a presence that was not there; and every prayer answered anywhere was a door opened that something, someday, might try to walk through again.

But that was tomorrow's weather. Tonight there was a fire, and a storm retiring honorably over the hills, and two travelers on a road with no destination more urgent than the next shrine, the next fevered sister, the next child who needed to learn that thunder was not a monster but a neighbor with a loud voice and a good heart.

"The ridge shrine tomorrow," Sera said, drowsily, against his shoulder. "The one with the broken statue. Someone's been leaving offerings. We should see who."

"We should," Isandro agreed, and meant it, and would mean it tomorrow, and the day after, for all the days the road gave them.

Above the hills, very far off, the last of the thunder rolled away like a bell being rung in some enormous tower, and if you listened—if you were the sort of person who had learned to listen—you could almost hear what it was saying, which was nothing wise, nothing portentous, only the oldest message the sky knows:

*I am still here. I remember you. Sleep well.*
