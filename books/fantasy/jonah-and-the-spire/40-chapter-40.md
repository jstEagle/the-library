## 40. Chapter 40

The road east of Delmare smelled of rain that had not yet fallen.

Isandro felt it the way he now felt everything—through the binding, through the thin bright thread that ran from his chest to Sera's and down into the deep places of the world where the parasite's residue still pooled like silt in a riverbed. Fifteen years in the Valley of Stillness had taught him silence. Three years since Ashkarat had taught him that silence was never empty; it was only the sky holding its breath.

He walked beside a cart of shrine-stones, one hand resting on the rail, and watched the clouds gather over the hills with something that was no longer fear.

"You're doing it again," Sera said, without looking up from the ledger balanced on her knee. She sat beside the driver, sleeves rolled, ink on her fingers—the defrocked priestess turned keeper of records, the heretic turned founder of an order that had no name yet because she refused to give it one. "Reading the weather like a letter addressed to you."

"Sometimes it is."

"Sometimes it's just weather." She glanced at him then, and the corner of her mouth betrayed her. "But not today. Today it's showing off for you."

The storm was still two valleys away, but he could feel its shape the way a musician feels a chord before it is struck: pressure systems stacked like voices in a choir, warm air shouldering up through cold, the whole vast apparatus waiting—not for command, he had learned that lesson at the lighthouse, kneeling before a blind old man who had made him apologize to the wind—but for *negotiation*. Storms did not obey. They agreed. And what they asked in return was never what you expected.

The cart creaked. Behind them, the pilgrims they had gathered along the road sang a walking song with too many verses, and among them moved three young people whom Isandro had been teaching for a season: a ferryman's son who could make river fog sit still when she hummed; a boy from the saltmarshes whose prayers to Vess were answered so promptly that the god had taken to visiting him in dreams, complaining of the noise; and a silent girl from the mountains whose gift was not weather at all but memory—who could hold a thing remembered so steadily that others could see it too.

That last one worried him, sometimes. Memory was a country with borders that shifted. He knew that better than anyone alive.

* * *

They made camp that night in the ruins of a wayshrine—one of the hundreds Sera had catalogued across the free cities, each one a small archaeology of the years when the gods had gone quiet. This one had been dedicated to a hearth-god whose name survived only in a cracked lintel: *VARETH*, or possibly *VARETHA*; the stone had crumbled at the decisive letter.

Sera built the fire with the economy of long habit while Isandro walked the perimeter out of an instinct he no longer questioned. There was no danger. Maren's letters—which arrived by courier every few months, sealed in gray wax, written in a hand that had grown almost gentle—reported that the last organized cells of the old regime had dissolved a year past, that the inquisition had been formally disbanded and its archives opened, that the Regent-who-had-been was remembered now mostly as a fever the kingdom had survived. The letters always ended the same way: *The truth is recorded. That was all I asked.*

But Isandro walked the perimeter anyway, because the binding meant he could feel things other people couldn't, and tonight, at the edge of the ruined shrine, he felt something small and stubborn and familiar pushing up through the ash of the old altar like a green shoot through pavement.

A candle flame. Burning with no candle under it.

"Well," he said. "You're a long way from Corvane."

The flame flickered, steadied, and gathered itself into the shape he knew—a figure no taller than a child, translucent as smoke, flickering like a candle in a draft. Vess had never fully recovered from burning itself nearly to nothing at the Tide Gate. The god that traveled with them now, when it traveled at all, was a fraction of what it had been: a votive light where once there had been a beacon. But it was *here*, and it was *itself*, and after everything, Isandro had come to think that this—smallness, persistence, the refusal to go out—might be what divinity actually looked like when you stripped away the terror.

"The boy prayed," Vess said. Its voice was still that strange doubled sound, like wind through a keyhole. "The saltmarsh boy. He prayed for rain for his mother's garden, and I answered, and then I could not stop being here. It is very strange, being an answer." The little god tilted its head. "You are going to the valley."

It was not a question. Isandro looked at the fire, at Sera's silhouette against it, at the young ones asleep in their bedrolls with the unguarded faces of people who had not yet learned what their gifts would cost.

"In the spring," he said. "We promised the council we'd see the coastal shrines restored first."

"You have been promising the council things for three years." Vess's flame guttered, amused. "I remember Stillwater, Isandro Almeida. I remember a man on a riverbank who would not say his own name. I remember begging him." The god's light dimmed, softened. "I would like to see what became of him."

Isandro said nothing for a while. Down in the camp, Sera had begun the evening rite—not the old liturgy, which was lost, but the new one she had written herself, plain words spoken plainly: *Whatever watches, watch kindly. Whatever remembers, remember us gently.* The ferryman's daughter murmured it along with her, word-perfect.

"When the sky remembers," Isandro said finally. "That's what Odile told me. Open the letter when the sky remembers." He laughed, quietly, without much humor. "I spent fifteen years thinking that meant some catastrophe. Some breaking. And it just meant—" he gestured at the fire, at the sleeping students, at the small god standing in the grass, "—this. It meant the sky started answering again. That was all."

"And are you sorry?" Vess asked. Gods asked direct questions; it was one of the things mortals never got used to. "For leaving the valley. For the price. For the anchor you carry in your chest that will never come out."

Isandro considered lying, considered deflecting, considered all the old habits of a man who had spent half his life being no one. Then he thought about the ninety seconds on the palace floor—Aurelian's eyes clearing, his brother's voice saying a childhood name, *Drossa, Drossa, you came*—and how even that stolen moment had been worth fifteen years of silence, and how the shared binding had given them both back to the world diminished but alive, two anchors splitting one burden, forever feeling the weather in each other's bones.

"No," he said. "Ask me on a bad day and I might lie to you. But no."

* * *

Spring came late to the mountains that year, and when they finally crested the pass above the Valley of Stillness, Isandro had to stop and simply stand there, letting the sight of it move through him like weather.

The valley lay green and gold below, the river running high with snowmelt, Stillwater village strung along its banks like beads on a wire. The ferry was crossing. Smoke rose from chimneys. And over the fields—over the shrine where a prayer had once cracked the silence like ice, over the boundary stone where he had leveled a ridge with raw grief and power—children were flying kites.

In a thunderstorm.

Not a dangerous one; he could feel it from here, feel its mood, a big soft-fronted spring storm full of static and show, the kind of storm that liked attention. But lightning walked inside it nonetheless, and the children ran beneath it shrieking with joy, kites trailing tails of ribbon, and none of them flinched when the thunder rolled.

"They went home," Sera said softly beside him. "The wardens."

"I can tell." The valley's magiclessness was gone—he had felt it lift the moment they crossed the pass, the way you feel a hand removed from your shoulder. Whatever compact had held the Stillness together had dissolved with the old regime, or perhaps with the parasite, or perhaps simply because nothing made of silence can survive forever. The wardens, Maren's letters said, had filed no report and offered no resistance. They had folded their gray robes, laid down their staves, and walked out of the valley in single file, and no one had ever learned where they went.

Odile met them at the shrine.

She was older—of course she was; everyone was older—but her spine was as straight as the boundary stone, and her eyes went straight past Sera and the students and the cart and found Isandro as if fifteen years and a war were a curtain she could see through.

"You look terrible," she said.

"You should see the other guy," Isandro said. "Actually, you shouldn't. He's a king now. It went to his head."

Something moved behind Odile's stern face—amusement, or its close cousin—and then, unexpectedly, she embraced him. Briefly, fiercely, the way a fortress embraces: all stone, all at once. When she stepped back her composure was restored, but her hands stayed on his shoulders a moment longer than necessary.

"The letter worked, then," she said.

"It worked."

"The seal held?"

"Fifteen years in a dry box. Not a crack in it." He reached into his coat and drew it out—the letter he had carried across half of Vhalen, through curfews and sieges and the end of the world as it had been, and which he had never thrown away, because some things you keep not for what they say but for what they cost. *The crown lies,* it had said, in one line, under a royal seal. It seemed very small now. Most true things did.

Odile took it, weighed it in her palm, and then handed it back. "Keep it. Shrines are for sacred objects, and that's yours, not mine." She turned to Sera and inclined her head with the precise courtesy of one keeper acknowledging another. "The village has questions. We've had questions for three years. The sky answers now, and half my congregation thinks it's a miracle and the other half thinks it's a military strategy, and I am too old to adjudicate."

"That's why we've brought students," Sera said. "Teach them enough that they can answer for themselves."

"Mm." Odile's gaze swept the three young people, lingering on the silent mountain girl, and something in her expression suggested she had already adjudicated several things privately. "There's room at the ferry house. And the shrine needs sweeping. Sacred work is mostly sweeping; nobody tells you that part."

* * *

He went down to the river at dusk, alone, the way he had gone down to the river a hundred evenings in another life, when his whole ambition had been to remain unremarkable until death made it official.

The ferryman's daughter—no longer a daughter, a woman grown now with children of her own—was pulling her boat in for the night. She saw him on the bank and stopped, oars dripping, and for a long moment neither of them spoke.

"You came back," she said.

"I said I would."

"You said it three years ago." But she was smiling as she said it, and she shipped the oars and let the boat drift to the bank, and climbed out, and stood in front of him studying his face with the frankness of someone who had known him before he had a history. "Mara asks about you. My youngest. She wants to know if the stories are true—that you talked to the century-storm, that lightning built itself into towers around you over Ashkarat, that a god died for you and got better."

"What do you tell her?"

"I tell her the man I knew was afraid of thunder." Her smile gentled. "And then I tell her the rest, and I tell her that's the whole point of the story."

Downstream, the first stars were coming out over the water, and the spring storm had drifted closer, grumbling amiably along the peaks like an old dog settling by a fire. Isandro felt it lean toward him—not demanding, not pleading, just aware of him, the way the sea is aware of the moon. He sent back along the binding-thread a feeling he had no word for: acknowledgment, greeting, the nod exchanged between neighbors.

The storm rumbled contentedly and began, very softly, to rain.

* * *

On the last night, they lit the shrine properly for the first time in living memory.

Sera led it, of course—she had become, to her own continuing bewilderment, something between a priest and a librarian, keeper of rites and records both. The villagers filled the little stone building and spilled out onto the steps. The students stood at the front, nervous and straight-backed. Vess burned on the altar, a small steady flame, and when the saltmarsh boy prayed aloud—for rain in due season, for the ferry to run safe, for the king's health in far-off Ashkarat—the god answered with light rather than words, and everyone saw it, and no one screamed, and that alone was worth the whole long road.

Afterward, when the crowd had thinned to embers and echoes, Isandro stood at the back of the shrine with Sera's shoulder against his, and felt the twin threads of his life pull taut and sing: the binding-thread to Sera, worn smooth as a river stone with three years of use; and the fainter, stranger thread that ran north and east, over mountains, to a throne room where his brother sat wearing a crown that fit him properly now.

Aurelian did not remember him. The unbinding had returned everything except that—the queen's bargain had carved the twins apart too thoroughly, and some prices, once paid, stay paid. But the letters came anyway, twice a year, in a strong careful hand: reports of harvests and treaties, of the school for god-touched children established in the capital, of a bell cast for the great spire and named, at the king's insistence, *Drossa*. Aurelian did not know why he had chosen the name. He wrote that he dreamed of a boy in a reed boat on a brown river, and woke with the dream's warmth still on him like sunlight, and had decided that whatever the dream was, he would honor it.

In the latest letter, folded now against Isandro's heart, there had been one line that he had read forty times:

*I am told I had a brother. I cannot grieve someone I cannot remember, and yet I find I have set a place for him at every table since the coronation. If he ever reads this: the seat is not ceremonial. Come home whenever you like. It will be like coming home to someone I have always known, because I suspect that is exactly what it will be.*

"He'll write again in autumn," Sera said, watching his face. "He always does."

"I know."

"And someday you'll answer with more than a formal note about shrine restorations."

"Someday." Isandro smiled. Outside, the spring storm had settled in earnest over the valley, rain silvering down through the dark, and the children's kites were long since folded away, and the river rose gently against its banks the way rivers do when the sky is generous. "When I can write it without weeping on the page. A king shouldn't have to read tearstains. Bad for the dignity of the crown."

"The crown," Sera said, "has endured worse than your tears."

Outside the shrine, Vess's small flame lifted from the altar and drifted out into the rain, which did not extinguish it—it never did—and hung there above the wet village like a lantern looking for its door. The god turned slowly, taking in the valley that had forgotten and now remembered, the shrine with its candles, the two figures in the doorway bound to each other and to the deep machinery of the world.

Then it flickered—its version of a bow—and went out, unhurried, the way a day goes out: not ending, only moving somewhere else, where someone was praying, and needed an answer, and would be astonished, and comforted, and never quite the same.

The rain fell on Stillwater, soft and endless and full of light.

*The End*
