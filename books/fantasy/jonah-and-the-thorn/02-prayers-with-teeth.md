## 2. Prayers with Teeth

The trouble began, as most troubles did in Coldharbour, with someone asking for something they couldn't afford.

Sable heard it before he saw it — a voice rising through the floorboards of the Gilded Debt's counting house, thin and cracked and carrying that particular desperation that made the hair on his arms stand up. He had spent thirty years learning to read voices the way other men read ledgers. This one was a beggar's voice. A beggar's voice, raised not in pleading but in *prayer*, and there was all the difference in the world between the two.

He set down his pen. The nib left a small black star on the blotter, and he noted, distantly, that he would have to scrape it out later. Order mattered. Order was the only wall he had ever trusted.

Below him, in the guild's courtyard, the beggar was still speaking.

*"O Thrice-Buried One, who sleeps beneath the roots of the mountain, hear me. I have nothing. I have less than nothing. But you are old, and old things remember hunger, and I am hungry—"*

Sable crossed to the window.

The courtyard of the Gilded Debt was a place designed to be unimpressive: grey flagstones swept clean, a single ironwood tree kept ruthlessly pruned, a fountain that trickled just enough water to seem prosperous without seeming rich. Clients came here to trade away their worst days and their sweetest ones, their first kisses and their griefs, and the courtyard's very blandness reassured them that what they sold would vanish into something equally forgettable.

The beggar knelt at the center of it, a bundle of rags with frost in its beard, and around him the morning light had gone strange.

It pooled. That was the only word for it. The grey winter sun, which had been falling flatly on everything in Coldharbour since Sable could remember, now gathered itself into a column above the kneeling man, warm as honey, thick as poured gold. And out of the flagstones — out of the *pruned and orderly flagstones* — something was growing.

Roots. Pale as bone, fine as spider silk, threading up between the stones in patterns that hurt to look at directly. They spread toward the beggar like fingers opening after a long sleep, and where they touched the frost, the frost bloomed into tiny white flowers.

Sable's hand found the windowsill and gripped it.

*Old gods don't answer,* he told himself. *Old gods are dead. Old gods are the reason the Gilded Debt exists at all — because when the gods went silent, prayer became a market, and markets need brokers.*

But the god beneath the mountain had never been dead. Sable knew that better than anyone alive. He had sold something to its court, once, and the court did not deal in corpses.

The beggar lifted his face into the golden light, and he was weeping, and he was laughing, and the sound of it came up through the glass like a knife finding a seam.

"Thank you," the man whispered. "Oh — oh, thank you, thank you—"

And then, because he was a man of Coldharbour, and men of Coldharbour understood debt the way fish understood water, he added: "What do I owe?"

The light flickered.

For one heartbeat the whole courtyard went dark as a closed eye, and every root in the stones shivered, and Sable felt — actually *felt*, through the soles of his feet, through the bones of his hands — something vast turn its attention upward, past the courtyard, past the counting house, past the careful grey anonymity of his life, and pause.

Looking. Listening. *Reading.*

Then the light withdrew, the roots sank, the flowers crumbled to frost again, and the beggar sat alone in an ordinary morning, holding something in his cupped palms that glowed faintly, like a coal wrapped in cloth.

Sable did not wait to see what it was. He was already moving, taking the back stairs two at a time, because whatever happened next in that courtyard, it would happen to the ledger-keeper first. It always did. Records were the guild's skin, and when the skin was pierced, everyone bled.

* * *

By noon, the courtyard was full of clients, and none of them were calm.

They stood in clumps by the fountain, wrapped in furs and suspicion, and their voices carried the particular pitch of people whose worldview has developed a crack down the middle. A grain merchant named Osk Vell was insisting, loudly, that his grandmother's memory of the sea-god's festival — which he had sold to the Debt eleven years ago — had come back to him in the night, "whole and wet and *singing*." A widow demanded to know whether her purchased forgetting of her husband's debts might also unravel. Two brothers who had traded away their shared childhood were standing very close together, staring at each other as if seeing each other for the first time, which perhaps they were.

"The books," said Guildmaster Corvane Ash from the top of the stairs, and the word dropped through the hall like a stone into a well.

Corvane was a tall man built like a folded letter — all creases and angles, sealed shut. His silver hair was oiled flat; his rings were modest; his voice never rose because it never needed to. He looked out over the frightened clients with the expression of a man watching weather he intended to personally disapprove of.

"The books are sound," he continued. "Every contract holds. Whatever the old ones do above ground is above ground, and does not touch the Gilded Debt. Go home. Come back tomorrow, when you've remembered how to be reasonable."

They went, mostly. But Sable, watching from the ledger-room doorway, saw the way they looked back at the building. Not with greed or gratitude, as clients should. With *fear*. And fear, in Coldharbour, always curdled eventually into torches.

"Sable." Corvane didn't turn. He never had to; he always knew where Sable stood, which was nowhere, which was everywhere, which was exactly forgettable enough. "My office."

* * *

The guildmaster's office smelled of ink and cedar and, beneath both, something older — the faint mineral cold of deep stone, as if the mountain under the city leaned in through the walls to listen. Corvane poured two cups of tea and pushed one across the desk, which he did only when something was badly wrong.

"You saw the courtyard," he said.

"I saw it."

"And?"

Sable turned his cup a quarter-turn, aligning the handle with the desk's edge. "I saw a god answer a prayer in our own yard. In daylight. Without a contract, without a broker, without a fee." He paused. "That last part is the problem. Everything else can be explained as weather. Free grace cannot."

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes — not surprise; confirmation. "The Diviners in Vhale are reporting the same. Wells answering questions. Statues weeping real salt. A shrine in the Lowmarket that hasn't taken a coin in forty years found full this morning." He laced his fingers. "The old ones are waking, Sable. And a waking creditor is worse than any debtor, because a debtor wants only what he contracted for. A waking god wants *everything he was ever owed.*"

"So what do you want from me?"

"Erase them." Corvane slid a folded list across the desk. "Every entry in every ledger that touches the divine. Contracts paid in prayers. Debts denominated in devotion. Names offered to shrines as collateral. All of it. Burn the pages, strike the lines, and if the ledgers resist—" he tapped the list, twice, "—you know the deeper arts. You wrote half of them."

Sable took the list without looking at it. "Erasure leaves scars, Guildmaster. Even my kind of erasure. Anyone who knows how to read the gaps will know something was removed, and will wonder what."

"Let them wonder. Wondering is cheap. Owed is expensive." Corvane rose, which ended meetings the way a guillotine ended conversations. "Begin tonight. And Sable—" For the first time, the guildmaster hesitated, and the hesitation was more frightening than anything he'd said. "If you find your own name in those pages... bring it to me before you act on it. Do you understand? Before. Not after."

"I understand," said Sable, who understood nothing except that his hands had gone cold.

* * *

The ledger room at midnight was a cathedral of small sins.

Shelves rose to the ceiling in ranks, each volume bound in leather dyed the color of its contents — grey for mundane debts, blue for memories, black for the trades no client ever spoke of afterward. Sable worked by lamplight, moving down Corvane's list with the steady rhythm of long practice: find, verify, excise. A prayer-contract from a fisherman, struck. A devotion-collateral from a dying woman who'd pledged her afterlife against her daughter's dowry, burned. Page by page, the divine was scraped out of the Gilded Debt's history like mold from bread.

It was going well. That should have warned him.

He reached the third shelf of the black volumes — the personal archive, the entries too sensitive for the general stacks — and pulled down Ledger Nine, the book of soul-prices, and opened it to the section marked *Court Beneath the Mountain.*

The entry was glowing.

Not metaphorically. Not in the way ink sometimes caught lamplight. The letters themselves emitted a soft amber radiance, the same honey-warmth he'd seen pooling over the beggar, and they had *changed*. Yesterday — he remembered yesterday with terrible clarity, because remembering was his profession — yesterday this page had held a simple transactional record:

*Received: one (1) soul, first-self, intact. Rendered by: [name struck]. Consideration: full remission of terminal affliction, Maren Blackwood. Status: held in perpetuity, Court Reserve.*

Now, beneath it, in ink that shimmered like light through deep water, new words had written themselves:

*Debt called due. The sleeper wakes. The account must be balanced by the hand that opened it.*

*Sable Coldharbour, debtor.*

His name. His actual name, in letters of living light, in a ledger he had kept himself, in a transaction he had buried so deep even he only visited it in dreams — and he had trained himself, over years of disciplined effort, to stop having those dreams.

The room tilted. Or he did. He put his palm flat on the desk and felt the wood humming, faintly, like a plucked string that refused to stop.

*Invisible,* he thought. *I made myself invisible. Thirty years of being no one, of walking through rooms like smoke, of keeping my face so plain and my name so quiet that even the gods' agents skimmed past me. That was the whole architecture. That was the wall.*

And the wall had a crack in it now, shaped exactly like four words in glowing ink.

Because here was the thing about being unforgettable-by-design: it only worked while no one was *looking for you*. Anonymity was a lock, not armor. And somewhere beneath the mountain, something vast had woken, had opened its ledgers — older and deeper than his — and run one pale finger down the column of names until it stopped on his.

The lamp guttered. In its flicker, Sable could almost see the boy he had been, the one he'd sold — the one who remembered their mother's face, who had laughed easily, who had loved without calculating interest — waiting somewhere in the dark below, patient as a held note.

*The account must be balanced by the hand that opened it.*

His hand trembled as he reached for the page. Every instinct screamed at him to scrape the entry out, burn the volume, salt the ashes, go back to being smoke. That was survival. That had always been survival.

Instead, Sable Coldharbour picked up the ledger, closed it gently, as one closes a door on a sleeping child, and carried it toward the door of the guildmaster's office — because Corvane had asked to see it first, and because some part of him, some hollowed-out and echoing part, already suspected that no fire in Coldharbour would touch that ink.

Behind him, on the empty desk, the lamplight settled — and for just a moment, in the shape of the shadows it threw, it looked like roots, reaching up through the floorboards, following him.
