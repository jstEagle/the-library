## 3. Wren at the Threshold

The Gilded Debt kept its doors open even in the hour before dawn, and Wren understood why. A guild that traded in memory could not afford the dark; darkness was where forgetting happened naturally, and a thing that happened naturally could not be sold.

She stood across the cobbled square from its entrance, her grey traveling cloak pulled tight against the river damp, and watched the lamplight spill out onto the stones like something poured rather than shed. The guildhouse had been a counting-house once, then a temple to a god whose name had been scraped off the lintel so thoroughly that the stone itself seemed embarrassed by the absence. Now it was neither and both. Above the door hung the Debt's sign: a set of scales wrought in gilt iron, one pan holding a coin, the other holding nothing at all.

*Nothing weighs more than you think,* her mother used to say of such signs. Her mother had said many things, all of them lost now to the fever that had carried her off in Wren's twelfth winter, back when the gods were still sleeping and prayers were just words spoken into a pillow.

That was before the waking. Before the beggar in the courtyard. Before the letters came.

Wren touched the medallion at her throat—the waking god's sigil, an eye half-opened, rendered in hammered tin because her order was poor and honest about it—and made herself cross the square. The cobblestones were slick with mist. Somewhere behind her, the city coughed itself awake: cart wheels, a fishwife's cry, the groan of the tide-gates.

She had been sent because she was young and therefore expendable, though Mother Cassia would never have said it in those words. *You have a gift for seeing what people have removed,* the old priestess had told her, folding the letter of introduction with hands like birch bark. *The guild deals in removals. Go and see what they've taken, and whether any of it belongs to heaven.* And then, quieter, in the voice she used for truths too heavy for the novices' ears: *And Wren—be careful what you see. Seeing is how they get their hooks into you.*

The interior of the guildhouse smelled of tallow, old paper, and beneath it all something faintly sweet, like fruit going over. Memory, she thought. Spent memory must smell like this—ripe past its keeping. The entry hall was wainscoted in dark oak, and clerks moved through it with the hushed efficiency of hospital orderlies, carrying ledgers instead of basins. No one looked at her twice, which surprised her until she remembered that no one looked at anyone twice here. That was the whole business model.

"I'm expected," she said to the man at the receiving desk, and laid Mother Cassia's letter on the counter. "Sister Wren, of the Order of the Opening Eye."

The clerk read the letter without expression. "The guildmaster has assigned an auditor to assist you." He rang a small brass bell. "He keeps our books. If there are divine debts outstanding, he'll know where they're buried."

"Buried," Wren repeated.

"It's a figure of speech." The clerk's eyes flicked up, and for a moment there was something in them—not fear exactly, but the shape fear leaves behind when it moves through a room. "Mostly."

* * *

They put her in a reading room on the second floor, a narrow chamber with one window overlooking the square, and it was there that Sable Coldharbour came to her.

She knew his name before he gave it, because everyone in the building spoke it the way sailors speak of weather—constantly, warily, without ever quite looking in its direction. He was not what she expected. She had imagined the keeper of the Gilded Debt's ledgers as some ancient spider of a man, all knuckle and squint. Instead he was perhaps thirty, unremarkable in the way that takes real work to achieve: brown hair, brown eyes, a face you would struggle to describe five minutes after leaving the room. His coat was good wool gone soft at the cuffs. He carried a ledger under one arm the way other men carry infants or weapons.

"Sister Wren." He set the ledger on the table between them but did not sit. "I'm told you want to inspect our accounts for entries touching the divine."

"The gods are awake," Wren said. "When they wake, they collect. My order needs to know what your clients owe them."

"And if I tell you we keep no such accounts?"

"Then I'll know you're lying, and I'll say so in my report, and the first prayer answered in this building will be someone praying you hadn't."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of one, haunting the place where a smile used to live. "You're younger than I expected."

"You're forgetter than I expected."

That earned her a real look—a flicker of surprise, quickly banked, like a match struck and smothered. He sat down across from her, opened the ledger, and turned it to face her. "Then let's not waste each other's morning."

The pages were beautiful. That was the first thing she noticed, and it unsettled her more than ugliness would have. Columns in ink the color of dried blood, each entry in a hand so precise it looked typeset: names, sums, dates, and in the final column, brief descriptions of collateral. *One summer afternoon, aged nine, with the smell of cut grass.* *The name of a childhood dog.* *Six years of a marriage, retained by the husband, surrendered by the wife.* Page after page of lives disassembled into tradeable parts.

Wren had grown up on scripture about the sin of division—cutting the soul into pieces to sell the pieces. She had always assumed the verses were metaphor. Here was the metaphor with a decimal column.

"These are memories," she said.

"Memories, impressions, habits, griefs. Anything a person can carry, we can carry for them. Or take entirely, at fair market value." He turned a page. "The divine accounts, if they exist, would be segregated. Sealed. I'd need the guildmaster's key."

"Then get the key."

"In a moment." He was watching her now, and there was something new in his attention—not suspicion, exactly. Curiosity, maybe, or its poorer cousin. "May I ask you something, Sister? Off the record?"

"You may ask."

"When you pray—to your waking god—what does it feel like? When he answers."

The question caught her off balance, which she suspected was its purpose. "It feels like being known," she said finally. "Completely. It's terrible and wonderful and I wouldn't trade it."

"Terrible," he repeated softly. He looked down at the ledger, and his finger came to rest on the margin of the page, beside an entry she couldn't quite read upside-down. "Yes. I imagine it would be."

And that was when she felt it.

It came through her fingertips first, resting on the table's edge, the way cold comes through iron. A wrongness in the air between them, a silence with edges. Wren had spent three years training her gift—Mother Cassia called it *the sight of absences*, the ability to perceive what had been taken out of a person, the way you can hear the missing note in a song you know. Most people carried small absences: a forgotten language, a dead friend deliberately unremembered, a shame excised. Little holes, little shadows.

What sat across from her was not a hole. It was a *hollow*. A vast, clean-walled emptiness where a whole person should have been, carved out with surgical care and packed with nothing. It was like standing in a cathedral and realizing the cathedral had been built around a void—that the architecture existed only to disguise the fact that its heart had been removed.

She gasped before she could stop herself.

Sable went very still. "What is it?"

"You—" Wren pressed her hand flat against her own sternum, as if to steady something inside herself. "There's a hollow in you. A large one. Someone cut you, and recently enough that the walls haven't scarred."

For a long moment he said nothing. The lamplight found the planes of his face and made them strange, a mask worn by someone practicing at being a face. Then, quietly: "Everyone in this building has been cut. It's a professional hazard."

"Not like this." She leaned forward. "This isn't a trim. This isn't a bad year removed. This is—" She searched for the word and hated the only one that fit. "*Excavated.* There was a person in that space. A whole self. And it's gone, and what's left is—" she gestured at him, at the careful blankness, the practiced unremarkability, "—what's left is the scaffolding."

His jaw tightened. "You don't know what you're looking at."

"No," she admitted. "But I know what it costs. I've seen men who gave up single memories to your guild, and they walk crooked ever after, leaning away from the missing part of themselves. You lean in every direction at once. You've made yourself into a shape with nothing inside it." She heard her own voice drop, go gentle, go dangerous. "Why would anyone do that to himself?"

"Why does anyone do anything?" He closed the ledger with a soft, final sound. "Survive."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer that's ever true down here." But he didn't leave. He sat with his hand flat on the ledger's cover, as if holding something down, and after a while he spoke again, slower. "My order—you pray to a god who wakes up and starts collecting. Do you fear him?"

The question landed somewhere deep. Wren thought of the night in the chapel when the god had first answered her—not with thunder but with *attention*, the crushing, total attention of something vast turning its gaze upon a girl kneeling in the dark. She had wept. She had never told anyone that she had also, for one shameful instant, wanted to run.

"Yes," she said. "Every day. He knows everything I am. There's nowhere in me he can't see." She laughed, short and unsteady. "I serve him anyway. Because being known is the only thing worse than being alone, and the only cure for it."

Sable looked at her then—really looked, the way no one had looked at him in years, perhaps decades—and she watched something shift behind his brown eyes, some door opening a crack onto the hollow she had named.

"I built myself so nothing could find me," he said. "No debts visible. No history traceable. Nothing for the gods to collect." His thumb traced the ledger's edge. "And yesterday morning, an entry in my own book began to glow. New ink. Old debt. My name in the debtor's line."

Wren felt the words settle over her like cold water. "A divine debt. Yours."

"So it seems." He rose, tucked the ledger under his arm, and stood there a moment, a man-shaped absence lit from behind. "The guildmaster wants all records touching the divine erased by nightfall. Including, I suspect, mine."

"Erasing won't help you," Wren said, standing too. "If the old gods have woken, they don't need paper to remember. Paper is for us—for people who've forgotten how." She hesitated, then did the thing Mother Cassia had warned her against, the thing that got hooks set into you: she reached out and touched his wrist, lightly, where the pulse showed thin and quick beneath the skin. Through the contact she felt the hollow again, vast and echoing, and at its far wall—faint as a heartbeat through stone—something that was not empty at all. Something waiting.

"But there's still something in there," she whispered. "At the bottom of the hollow. It's not gone, whatever you sold. It's *kept*."

Sable stared at her hand on his wrist. He did not pull away.

"Where would I find it?" he asked, and his voice was the voice of a man asking directions to his own grave—or his own cradle, and not knowing which.

Wren held his gaze and felt her vows tremble like a struck bell.

"Beneath the mountain," she said. "And I think—I think you already knew that."
