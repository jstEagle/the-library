## 4. What the Sister Paid

Maren lived in a house the color of weak tea.

Sable had chosen it for her himself, six years ago—a narrow townhouse on Candlewick Row with good light in the mornings and neighbors too dull to gossip. He had paid three years of ledger-work to secure it, and he had never once visited without first standing across the street for an hour, watching the windows, making certain no one else was watching him watch them.

Today he did not wait. Today his hands shook too badly for patience.

The door was unlocked, which was wrong. Maren locked her door the way other women said their prayers—faithfully, hourly, without thinking. He stepped into the front hall and the air inside was stale, still, like a room that had been breathed in but not lived in for a long time.

"Maren?"

"In here." Her voice came from the kitchen, and it was her voice, and yet it was not—flattened somehow, like a bell wrapped in wool. "You're early. You're always early."

He found her at the table with a cup of tea going cold before her. She had been pretty once in the fierce, angular way of their mother; now she was pretty the way a pressed flower is pretty, all the color remembered rather than present. Her hair was the brown of old paper. Her eyes were gray where they had been green—he was almost sure they had been green—and when she looked up at him, her gaze slid off him twice before it caught, as though he were slightly out of focus even to family.

"You look terrible," she said.

"You look—" He stopped. He had prepared a dozen openings on the walk over, each one a careful key cut for a different lock: concern, casualness, brotherly teasing. All of them fell away now, because she was fading in front of him the way ink fades in rain, and there was no clever way to ask about that. "Maren. When did you last go outside?"

"Tuesday." She frowned, reaching for the date. "Or perhaps not Tuesday. The days have been... soft lately. Like fruit past its season." She smiled at him, and the smile was an heirloom, worn smooth by handling. "Sit down, Sable. You'll wear a hole in my floor."

He sat. The tea was cold. Everything in the room was very slightly cold, very slightly dim, as if the house were being viewed through smoked glass. And beneath it all, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, he could feel the pull of the glowing entry in his ledger—the new ink that bore his own name, drawing at him like a tide.

"I've been reading old records," he said. "Guild records. There's a transaction from nineteen years ago. A cure purchased from the court beneath the mountain. For a girl of nine with lung-rot." His voice stayed level. He was proud of that, in a distant, hollow way. "The payment listed is redacted. Not struck through—redacted. In my own hand, or a hand so like mine I can't tell the difference."

Maren's cup stopped halfway to her lips.

"You went to Mother," she said quietly. "She told you."

"Mother's been dead eleven years. She told me nothing." He leaned forward. "Who told me what, Maren? What is it you think I know?"

For a long moment she only looked at him, and he watched something move behind her faded eyes—something struggling toward the surface through deep water. Then she set the cup down with a small, precise click, and when she spoke again her voice had some of its old iron in it, the iron that had kept her alive when the physicians had given her a season.

"Nineteen years ago I couldn't breathe," she said. "Do you remember that much? Do you remember anything from before?"

"Of course I remember. I remember the winter. I remember carrying you to the physician's door because you'd gone blue around the mouth."

"Do you remember *me*?" she asked, and there was no cruelty in it, only a terrible gentleness. "Not the sick girl. Me. Before. Our mother's face—can you see it?"

He opened his mouth to say yes.

And found nothing.

There should have been a face. Every man has his mother's face filed somewhere behind his eyes, worn smooth by ten thousand lookings. Sable Coldharbour, who could recite the debts of four hundred clients from memory, who could walk the Gilded Debt's vaults blindfolded, reached for his mother's face and found a smooth white wall where the memory should have been. Not a gap—the gaps he knew, the clean surgical absences he carved in others and occasionally in himself, and those had edges, texture, scar tissue. This was different. This was a wall built from the inside, load-bearing, ancient, so seamlessly part of the architecture that he had lived his whole life against it without once wondering why the room was smaller than it should be.

"No," he heard himself say. It came out very small. "No, I can't."

"Oh, love." Maren reached across the table and took his hand, and her fingers were cool as river stones. "Then sit down properly, because I'm only going to tell you this once, and then we're both going to wish neither of us knew it."

* * *

"The physician gave me until spring," she began, staring into her cold tea as though the story were written there. "I remember the room. I remember you standing behind Mother's chair—you were fourteen, all elbows and shadows, already learning how to be nobody. And I remember a man coming who wasn't a physician. He wore gray, and his shadow pointed the wrong way, and Mother let him in anyway because she was past caring about shadows."

"A broker," Sable said. His mouth had gone dry. "A broker of the court beneath the mountain."

"You know them better than I do now." She glanced up. "Yes. One of theirs. He looked at me the way you look at ledgers—I remember that, even through the fever—and then he looked at *you*, and he smiled, and I hated him instantly and completely. He said the court could cure me. The court's cures always take; that's what makes them worse than any disease. But the price—" Her voice caught, steadied. "The price wasn't coin. It wasn't memories, either, whatever you've been telling yourself all these years. Memories are currency, little brother. Everyone knows that. Even you."

"What was the price?"

"He called it your *first self*. Your soul's opening chapter. Everything you were before you became what you are." She turned the cup slowly in her hands. "I didn't understand it then. I was nine and drowning. I signed nothing; you signed everything. I remember you signing. You didn't hesitate. You didn't even read it. You just wrote your name like you were glad to be rid of it."

The room tilted. Sable gripped the table's edge and held on.

Nineteen years. Nineteen years he had believed he understood his own bargain—that he had sold memories, hours, seasons, the ordinary tax every desperate family pays. He had built his whole self upon that understanding. *I am hollow because I chose to pay,* he had told himself in the dark watches of a hundred nights. *The hollowness is mine. I bought it honestly.*

But he had not sold hours. He had not sold seasons.

He had sold the boy who owned them.

"That's impossible," he said, and heard how young his voice sounded. "The soul isn't divisible. Even the guild's law says—"

"The guild's law," Maren said softly, "was written by men who trade in souls and would prefer their customers not examine the goods too closely. Of course it's divisible. You're sitting here, aren't you? Breathing, scheming, keeping your books. Whatever they took, they left enough of you to walk around in." She hesitated. "But you've been walking around in a coat with no one inside it, Sable. That's why you made yourself forgettable. That's why you're the best thief in the city and no one can describe your face. There's less of you to notice. You've been living in the negative space ever since."

He thought of Wren—the priestess with the storm-gray eyes who had looked at him across the guild's interview table and flinched, subtly, as though peering into a well and finding no bottom. *There's a place in you,* she had said, *where something was cut away. It's not empty. Empty things don't ache. It's occupied. Something is missing and something else moved in to fill the dark.*

He had thought her mad. He had thought her dangerously perceptive. He had not, even for one breath, imagined she was simply accurate.

"The entry in my ledger," he said. "The one that glows. It names me as debtor. Nineteen years of interest on a debt I didn't know I carried."

"The old gods are waking," Maren said. "Mother used to say they'd wake someday, and when they did, every bargain made in the dark would come up for audit. She prayed they wouldn't live to see it." Her mouth curved, not quite a smile. "They didn't. We did."

"Why tell me now? Why not years ago?"

"Because you never asked. Because asking would have hurt you, and hurting you was the one thing I swore I'd never do—you'd already paid enough." She looked down at her hands, and he saw that they trembled, faintly, like candle flames. "And because until this week, I could keep the secret easily. Secrets are heavy, Sable, but I've had practice carrying mine. Lately though..." She lifted her eyes to his, and he saw with a lurch that her irises had gone paler even since he'd arrived, green-gray washing toward silver. "Lately I feel like I'm the one being erased. Like the court's price is coming due in installments, and I'm the collateral. The glow you saw in your ledger—I've seen it too. In the mirror. My reflection has new writing on it, behind my eyes, where I can't read it."

The cold in the room suddenly made sense. The staleness, the soft days, the door left unlocked because locking it required a certainty about tomorrow that she no longer possessed.

"They're collecting," he said. "Through you. Because the debtor can't be found."

"You made yourself unfindable," Maren agreed. "Very well done, brother. The finest invisibility in the city. Unfortunately, debts don't need to find the debtor. They only need to find what the debtor loves." She reached out and touched his cheek, and her hand was so cold, so terribly cold. "So. Now you know what I paid for my life. The question—the only question—is what you intend to do about it."

What did he intend?

Every instinct he had honed over nineteen years answered in a chorus: *nothing*. Burn the page. Falsify the entry. Move Maren somewhere the court's clerks would never think to look, change her name, change his own, sink deeper into the safe gray water of anonymity and wait for the gods' waking madness to pass, as all madness passes. Survival was a discipline. He was its master.

But the chorus sounded thin tonight. It sounded like a boy's voice doing an old man's impression.

Because he had finally understood the shape of the theft, and it stole his breath: he had spent his whole life mourning a sister he saved, and never once mourned the brother he murdered. Some gray-clad clerk had walked out of their mother's house carrying the true Sable Coldharbour—the boy who remembered summer, who remembered their mother's laugh, who had signed his name gladly because he loved his sister more than he loved himself—and Sable had built a career, a reputation, a *self* on top of the wound and called the scar a face.

He had robbed the only person he'd never suspected: himself.

And the court beneath the mountain still had the goods.

"Maren," he said, rising, and his chair scraped back like a drawn blade. "I'm going to need to borrow your candlesticks."

She blinked. "Whatever for?"

"Forgeries," said Sable Coldharbour, and for the first time in nineteen years, the name fit him like a key turning in a lock. "I'm going to rob the old gods, little sister. And I'll need a priestess who owes me a favor—one who knows the way down."

Behind him, faint as a heartbeat, the ledger in his coat began to glow through the leather.
