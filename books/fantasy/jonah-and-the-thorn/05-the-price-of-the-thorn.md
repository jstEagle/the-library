## 5. The Price of the Thorn

Wren had been taught that prayer was a door, and that a priestess knocked politely and waited.

She had never been taught what to do when the door opened before she finished knocking.

She knelt in the shrine of the Waking God at the hour before dawn, when the candles burned low and the incense had gone sweet and heavy, and she did not pray. She *listened*. That was the first vow broken, though it was such a small one—listening without asking, opening herself like a window instead of speaking through the proper forms—and perhaps that was why the god came so easily, sliding into her like water finding a crack in stone.

*You have been walking in a den of thieves,* the god said, and its voice was not a voice but a warmth behind her ribs, a hearth-fire kindling in a cold room.

"They keep ledgers of souls," Wren whispered. Her hands were pressed flat against the cold mosaic floor, the tiles depicting the god's thousand open eyes. "One of them sold himself to the court beneath the mountain. I felt the hollow in him, Holiness. It goes all the way down."

*And you wish to fill it.*

"I wish to understand it." The lie caught in her throat like a fishbone. She swallowed it down. "I wish to help him."

The warmth in her chest pulsed once, twice, like a heart deciding whether to beat. Then, gently, terribly, the god laughed.

*Little wren. You have flown into the thornbush and wonder why you bleed.*

She lifted her head. In the candlelight the mosaic eyes seemed to shift, all thousand of them turning toward her at once, and she understood suddenly why the old rites demanded worshippers avert their gaze. To be seen completely was not comfort. It was flaying.

"Tell me how to free him," she said. "Tell me the law of it. The court beneath the mountain keeps its contracts; even the gods honor them. There must be a way."

*There is always a way.* The god's warmth spread up through her chest, into her throat, until her own voice seemed to hum with it. *But hear me, daughter, and hear me well, for I will say this only once: wholeness has a price, and the price is a thorn. Not pain inflicted—pain freely chosen. Pain embraced as a bride is embraced, with open eyes and an open hand. What he cut away, he must take back whole, and everything he cut away will cut him in return. Every grief he refused. Every love he abandoned. He will feel all of it, forever, and there is no anesthesia in honesty.*

Wren's breath went shallow. "Forever?"

*Forever.* The word settled over her like snowfall. *That is the law beneath the mountain, and older than the mountain: nothing given freely may be taken back cheaply. If you lead him to this, you lead him to suffering. Weigh it, priestess. Weigh your vows against his agony, and tell me which is heavier.*

She thought of Sable Coldharbour's face—the careful blankness of it, the way he moved through rooms as though apologizing for occupying space, the ledger-keeper's precision with which he kept even his own soul filed away and balanced. She thought of the hollow she had touched in him during the interview, that vast smooth absence where a boy should have been, and how he had looked at her afterward with something like terror, as though she'd found the one door in him that didn't lock.

"He is already suffering," she said quietly. "He just doesn't know it. That seems worse."

The god was silent for a long moment. When it spoke again, the warmth had gone strange and soft.

*You sound like my first priestess. She also argued with me. She also won.* A pause, and then, almost tenderly: *Go, then. But know this, Wren of the Ashen Order: if you walk this road, you walk it out from under my roof. The order will call it apostasy. They will take your vestments and your name from their rolls. And I will not stop them, because even gods must let their birds fly.*

"Then I'll fly," Wren said, and heard her own voice steady in a way that surprised her. "But I'm asking anyway, Holiness—one more thing. Not for the law. For me."

*Ask.*

"When it's done—when he's whole and hurting—will he thank me? Or will he hate me?"

The god's laughter again, warm and sorrowful at once, like sunlight on a grave.

*Yes,* it said, and withdrew, leaving her kneeling alone among the thousand eyes with tears she had no memory of starting.

* * *

She told him three days later, in the guild's archive, surrounded by shelves of stolen pasts.

Sable was working by lamplight, balancing columns in a ledger whose entries murmured faintly when you got too close—the memories whispering to themselves like sleepers talking in their dreams. He didn't look up when she entered, but she saw his pen pause, just slightly, and knew he'd marked her footsteps. A man who made himself forgettable noticed everything; it was the only armor he had left.

"The god answered me," she said.

Now he looked up. Lamplight caught the flat gray of his eyes, eyes that had been sanded down to nothing, and she watched him arrange his face into polite attention the way other men arranged their coats. "Priestess. That's... fortunate for the guild, I imagine. The master will want a full report."

"Forget the report." She stepped closer, close enough to see the fine tremor in his hand that he immediately stilled. "I asked about you. About the debt in your name. And the god told me the truth of it, Sable. Wholeness has a price. The court beneath the mountain doesn't release what it holds—not for coin, not for trickery. Only for the thorn-price. You would have to take back everything you cut away. All of it. The grief, the fear, the—" she hesitated "—the love. Whatever you were willing to amputate to save your sister, you'd have to feel it all again, every day, for the rest of your life."

The lamplight hissed. Somewhere in the archive, a ledger entry whimpered.

Sable set down his pen with exaggerated care, the way a man sets down a blade he very much wants to throw. "And your god told you this. Your god, who woke last month after a thousand years of silence and started answering beggars in courtyards." His voice was dry as old paper. "Why would it tell you anything true?"

"Because gods don't lie about prices. Only men do." She held his gaze. "You've spent your whole life learning what things cost, haven't you? Coin, memory, years. You keep the books for people who trade in impossible things. So look me in the eye and tell me I'm lying about this one."

For a long moment he said nothing. Then, so quietly she almost missed it: "What did it cost you? Asking your god this."

The question landed harder than any accusation could have. Wren thought of her order, her sisters, the Abbess's stern kind face, the rolls where her name was written in ink that could be scraped away. She thought of the vow she'd taken at fifteen, hand on the mosaic of eyes: *to serve the waking world and no single soul in it.*

"A great deal," she said. "Possibly everything I have."

"Then don't." He rose from his chair, and up close she could see the exhaustion carved into him, the hollowness worn like a garment so long it had become skin. "Whatever this is—pity, duty, some priestly compulsion to rescue lost things—don't spend yourself on me. I'm a bookkeeping error, Wren. An entry someone forgot to balance. The kindest thing you can do is close the book."

"That's not what you are."

"It's exactly what I made myself." His mouth twisted. "It took me twenty years to become invisible. Do you know what that's worth? Safety. No one collects debts from a man they can't remember. No one loves a man they can't see. It's the only fortune I have, and you're asking me to burn it."

"Yes," Wren said simply. "I am."

"And why?" His voice cracked on the word, just barely, a hairline fracture in the ledger-keeper's calm. "Why does it matter to you whether I'm whole or hollow?"

Because I touched the hollow in you and it felt like standing at the edge of a well, she thought. Because I have served a god of waking things and never once felt awake myself until you looked at me as though I were the first real thing you'd seen in twenty years. Because somewhere under all that careful gray there is a boy who remembers his mother's face, and I cannot bear that he is locked in the dark while you walk around above ground wearing his absence like a coat.

"Because I choose to," she said aloud. "Not my order. Not my god. Me. And I'm telling you now, Sable Coldharbour, so there are no false ledgers between us: I have broken my vows for you. When we go down beneath the mountain—if we go—I go as an exile, not a priestess. My god warned me what it costs to help you, and I'm paying it anyway. So don't you dare tell me what I should or shouldn't spend."

The silence between them stretched taut as wire.

Then Sable laughed—a short, broken sound, nothing like humor. "You realize what you're describing. You're describing a man who feels everything. Grief, fear, shame, all of it, uncorked, forever. I built myself into this shape because feeling was killing me." He shook his head slowly. "You're asking me to unmake the only self I have left."

"No," Wren said, stepping closer still, near enough now that she could see the boy ghosting underneath the gray, faint as a watermark. "I'm asking you to meet him. The rest is yours to decide."

His hand rose—hesitated—then touched her cheek, feather-light, as though checking whether she too might be hollow. His fingers were ink-stained and cold and trembled like a man holding a candle in wind.

"If I do this," he whispered, "I won't be able to hide from you anymore. From anyone. I'll be *visible*, Wren. Everything I am, out in the light, all the time. It's the one thing I swore I'd never be."

"I know," she said, and turned her face into his palm. "That's rather the point."

* * *

They left the city at dusk, two days later, when the guildmaster's summons finally came and Sable read the orders in it—erase the divine debts, burn the pages, bury the truth—and understood there was no longer any place for him above ground either.

Wren walked beside him on the mountain road in her plainest clothes, her vestments folded in her pack like a shroud. Behind them the city smoked gold in the dying light; ahead, the mountain stood black against the stars, its roots going down and down to the court where a boy waited in the dark with his mother's face still bright in his memory.

At the first bend in the road, Sable stopped and looked back once. Wren watched him take inventory of his life the way he took inventory of ledgers—every column summed, every debt noted—and saw him arrive at the total and find it wanting.

"You'll be exiled," he said. "Your god said so. Your order will scrape your name from the rolls."

"My god also said," Wren replied, taking his hand, "that even gods must let their birds fly."

He looked at her then with those sanded-gray eyes, and for the first time she saw something kindle in them that wasn't fear—a small stubborn flame, banked deep, the ember of the boy he'd been.

"The gates below take tolls," he said quietly. "Memories. Pieces of yourself, paid willingly. I know the rates; I wrote half of them." His grip tightened on her hand. "When we get there, Wren—when it comes time to pay—don't bargain for me. Promise me. Whatever the court offers, whatever it asks, you let me settle my own accounts."

"I make no promises I intend to break," she said.

And far below them, deeper than roots, deeper than memory, something ancient turned a page in its own great ledger and smiled, waiting to see what they would bring to the scales.
