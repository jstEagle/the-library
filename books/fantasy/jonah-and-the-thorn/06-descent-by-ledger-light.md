## 6. Descent by Ledger-Light

The mountain's mouth was a door that had never learned to be shut.

Sable stood before it at dusk with Wren beside him and his ledger wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, the way other men carried infants or bombs. Behind them, Greyhollow's lamps were coming on one by one, small and stubborn against the dark. He found he was memorizing them. He caught himself doing it—counting the lights, fixing each one in place—and understood, with a miser's cold clarity, why.

He was saying goodbye to things he might not get back.

"The gates take memories," he said. It wasn't the first time he'd said it. Saying it again made it no more survivable. "That's what my own entries say. Toll of remembrance, one per gate. The court beneath doesn't want coin. Coin is what you use when you can't pay in something real."

"And you've counted how many gates?"

"Seven." He had counted them three times, in three different hands, hoping to be wrong. Seven gates between the mountain's mouth and the court where a boy who remembered their mother's face sat as a favorite among old gods. Seven pieces of himself, if he paid honestly. More, if he paid badly.

Wren touched his sleeve. She had left her vestments behind—grey traveling wool, her hair bound back, the waking god's sigil hidden under her collar where it still burned faintly, like a coal under ash. "You could lie," she said. "You're good at it."

"I'm *excellent* at it." His mouth did something that was almost a smile. "That's the problem. I know every trick for shorting a toll, because I invented half of them. And the court wrote the law that binds its own judgment. If I cheat at the gates, I forfeit the claim. My debt stands. My name stays in the book." He looked up at the black shoulder of the mountain, where snowfields caught the last red light like embers. "So for once in my life, Jude help me, I have to pay full price."

* * *

The first gate was only a mile inside, where the tunnel narrowed and the dark began to have texture. It wasn't a door so much as a decision—a place where the stone seemed to lean in and listen. A basin of black glass sat in a niche at its center, and above it, carved in letters older than any alphabet Sable knew, words he could nonetheless read because they were written in ledger-script: *PAY WHAT YOU REMEMBER.*

He unwrapped the ledger anyway. Habit. The glowing entry with his name pulsed gently, patient as a creditor.

"You don't need the book," Wren said softly. "It's not asking for ink."

"No." He set the book down. "But I want to record what I give. That's the whole point of me."

He knelt at the basin. The surface showed him nothing—not his reflection, not the dark, just a smooth waiting absence, and he thought, absurdly, *it looks like me.* Then he reached into himself the way he'd been trained to reach into others, fingers of will hooking into the soft architecture of a memory, and chose.

Not the mother. Not Maren. Not anything load-bearing. He took a small thing, deliberately: the smell of the bakery on Cooper's Lane on winter mornings, the way the bread-warm air rolled out the door and made the whole street feel briefly forgiven. He held it up in his mind like a coin between two fingers.

And hesitated.

Here was the thing no one told you about being a thief of memories: giving them away was easy when you took them from someone else. There was distance in it. Craft. But this was his, earned across forty winters of cold mornings, and his whole life he had hoarded such things precisely because they were his, because everything taken could be taken back but everything given—

"Sable," Wren said, and put her hand over his where it hovered above the glass.

He let go.

The memory left him the way blood leaves a cut—warmth first, then the strange cool absence. The basin drank it. Somewhere far below, something vast turned over in its sleep, satisfied. And Sable knelt there feeling a hole the exact shape of a bakery doorway, and discovered, to his horror and wonder in equal measure, that the hole did not hurt the way he'd expected.

It hurt the way an opened hand hurts after years of clenching.

"It's gone," he whispered. "I gave it away and I'm still—" He didn't finish. Still here. Still myself. The arithmetic of his entire life had always been subtraction equals safety, and just now, for the first time, the sum had come out different.

Wren helped him up. Her eyes were bright in the dark. "Six more," she said.

"Six more," he agreed, and hated how much lighter he felt, and hated hating it.

* * *

The second gate asked for a sound. The third, a color—the particular grey-green of Maren's eyes before her illness washed them pale, which cost him more than he'd budgeted and left him leaning against the stone, breathing, while Wren held his shoulders and murmured fragments of a liturgy he suspected she was improvising.

By the fourth gate he had stopped trying to choose cheaply.

That was the change, and he felt it happen the way you feel ice shift underfoot. At the fourth gate he stood before the basin and did not sort his memories by expendability, as he had sorted everyone else's all his life. Instead he asked himself a question he had never once asked: *what deserves to be given?*

He gave the memory of teaching Maren to swim in the quarry pond, her shrieking laughter echoing off the rock. He gave it whole, unshorted, with the sunburn and the leeches included, and the gate accepted it with a sound like a struck bell, and he walked through weeping without shame because Wren was weeping too, and neither of them pretended otherwise.

"A miser learning generosity," she said later, at their fireless camp in a widening of the tunnel where pale lichen gave off light like drowned moons. "That's what you look like. Like a man counting coins and finding out they multiply when you spend them."

"Don't romanticize me. I'm terrified."

"I know. So am I." She sat close enough that their shoulders touched. "But terror and wonder are neighbors, Sable. My god woke up after ten thousand years of silence, and do you know what the first answered prayer sounded like? A beggar in your guild's courtyard, getting exactly what he asked for, and screaming. Everyone ran. He stayed on his knees." She looked into the lichen-light. "Wonder is just terror that decided to stay."

Sable turned that over the way he turned stolen goods, looking for the flaw, the seam, the trick. He couldn't find one. That frightened him most of all.

* * *

The fifth gate was guarded.

Not by a creature—by a clerk. That was somehow worse. It sat on a stool of fused bone before the basin, grey-robed, faceless beneath its hood except for a mouth, and the mouth said, in the flat cadence of every auditor Sable had ever feared: *"State your entry."*

"Sable Coldharbour," he said. "Debtor. One soul, sold to the court beneath, payment received: the life of Maren Coldharbour, restored from death's threshold. I've come to redeem."

The mouth smiled. It was not a comforting arrangement. *"Redemption requires payment in kind. The soul you seek is your first self. What will you tender?"*

Sable's hand went to the ledger at his chest. Every instinct he owned screamed the answer: *anything else. Offer the book. Offer the guild's secrets. Offer Wren—*

No. Not that. Never that; the thought arrived already finished, and he marveled at it even as it appalled him.

"I'll tender this," he said, and reached deep, deeper than the bakery smell, deeper than the pond, down to a memory he had kept locked in the innermost strongbox of himself since he was nineteen years old: the night he first stole a memory from a living mind. The alley. The man's face going slack and grateful, emptied of his grief for a dead wife, and Sable walking away rich with someone else's mourning, telling himself it was mercy, knowing it was appetite.

He gave the gate the whole truth of it. The pleasure. The shame. Both together, inseparable, the way they had actually lived in him.

The clerk's mouth closed. For a long moment the tunnel was silent. Then: *"Paid in full. Honest weight."* And the gate opened onto stairs going down forever, and Sable realized his knees had buckled and Wren was holding most of his weight, and that somewhere in the transaction he had begun, quietly, to become a different man—or rather, to stop being the carefully hollowed thing and start being whatever was underneath.

"That one nearly broke you," she whispered on the stairs.

"It should have. It was the truest thing I own." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand. "Two gates left, Wren. And I'm running out of sins to pay with. After tonight I'll be paying in virtues, and I don't know if those weigh enough."

"They weigh more," she said. "They're denser. Nobody guards them, so nobody ever spends them down."

* * *

The sixth gate took his name.

Not the alias—his actual name, the one their mother had given him, the one he hadn't spoken aloud in thirty years. He gave it freely, and the moment it left him he felt it go the way you feel a tooth go: wrongness, whistling space, tongue returning again and again to the gap. Wren said his name to comfort him and he heard only a pleasant sound, meaning nothing, attached to no one.

"This is how I did it to myself," he said as they descended. His voice sounded strange in his own ears, unowned. "All those years ago. This is exactly what it felt like, gate by gate, until there was nothing left to collect. I built my invisibility one toll at a time and called it survival." He laughed, and the laugh had no floor under it. "I've been descending this mountain my whole life, Wren. Tonight I'm just doing it honestly."

She stopped on the stair and faced him, and the lichen-light made her look carved, ancient, certain. "Then hear me," she said. "One gate left. Whatever it asks—and it will ask for the biggest thing remaining, because courts are creditors and creditors are greedy—you will not pay it alone. Whatever piece of you goes into that basin, I will hold the shape of it. I'll remember it for you. That's what my vows were supposed to mean, before I understood them. Hold the shape of what people lose, until they can carry it again."

Sable looked at her—at this woman who had broken heaven's rules to walk into the dark with a thief—and felt the last unspent treasury of himself open, unbidden.

"Wren," he said, and then, because his name was gone and hers was not, he spent it recklessly: "When this is done, whatever's left of me—I want it to be yours to find. If you still want it."

The seventh gate was ahead of them now, and it was not stone.

It was a page. Vast, vertical, hanging in the tunnel's end like a sheet of vellum stretched across the world, and written across it in ledger-script that glowed like the entry bearing his name: *FINAL TOLL. PAY WHAT YOU CANNOT BEAR TO LOSE.*

Below the words, in fresh ink still wet and shining, the ledger at Sable's chest grew warm against his heart—and a new line began writing itself, letter by letter, in a hand he recognized as his own:

*Sable Coldharbour owes the court beneath one memory: the reason he sold his soul.*

He had no memory of writing it. But it was true. And the gate was waiting to be paid.
