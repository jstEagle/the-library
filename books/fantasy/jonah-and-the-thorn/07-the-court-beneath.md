## 7. The Court Beneath

The last gate had taken Wren's memory of her mother's voice. She had paid it with her chin up and her eyes dry until the moment the toll was drawn out of her—Sable had watched it leave, a silver thread of sound pulled from between her lips like a splinter of light—and then she had wept for exactly eleven steps before squaring her shoulders and saying, *Don't look at me like that. I'd rather lose her voice than you.*

He had not known what to do with that. He still didn't. He carried it now in the hollow of his chest where his stolen self used to sit, a warm coal in a cold stove.

Beyond that final gate, the mountain opened.

Not gradually—the way a cave opens, throat by throat—but all at once, the way a cathedral opens when you push through its doors mid-mass. The passage spat them out onto a ledge of black glass, and below them, impossibly far down and impossibly close at once, spread the Court Beneath.

It was lit from within. That was the first thing Sable understood: every stone of it glowed faintly, veins of gold running through basalt like ink through water, so that the whole cavern seemed to breathe light rather than merely hold it. Pillars rose in ranks, each one carved into the likeness of a standing figure whose face had been deliberately left blank—a thousand smooth ovals, featureless as eggs, watching without eyes. Between them moved shapes that might have been courtiers and might have been prayers given legs. And at the center, on a dais of fused bone-white stone, sat the judges.

There were nine of them. Sable knew this the way he knew arithmetic—not because anyone had told him, but because some ledger deep in the world's keeping had recorded it long ago and never been corrected. The old gods. Not the waking god Wren served, who was young and hungry and answered prayers like a merchant answering letters, but the elder things, the ones who had held the first accounts: debt and payment, price and worth, the terrible mathematics underneath mercy.

They were not large. That was the horror of them. They sat like tired clerks behind a counter that stretched the width of the dais, robed in colors Sable's eyes kept sliding off—colors that existed only in memories he no longer owned—and their faces were the same blank ovals as the pillars, except that each one bore a single mark, incised deep: a sigil, a seal, a name worn down past reading.

"They're judging," Wren whispered beside him. Her hand found his in the dark. "Listen."

And beneath the vast breathing silence, he heard it: voices. Hundreds of them, thin as reeds, rising from somewhere below the dais. Each voice spoke a single sentence over and over—I paid, I paid, I paid, or I owe, I owe, I owe—and after each sentence, one of the nine gods would tilt its blank head, and somewhere in the cavern a small light would go out, or a small light would be born.

"Every soul that ever traded here," Sable said. His voice came out flat, bookkeeper's-flat, which was the only way he could get it out at all. "Still being weighed. Some of these debts are older than the city above."

"Then yours is here." Wren turned to him. In the gold-veined dark her face was half shadow, half ember, and she looked, he thought, like exactly what she was: someone standing at the edge of everything she'd sworn to avoid. "Somewhere down there is a boy who remembers your mother's face."

*Our* mother's face. He almost corrected her. He stopped himself, because the correction was the whole wound: there was no *our* anymore. There was only a man made of gaps, and a boy made of everything the gaps used to hold.

"Stay close to me," he said. "In a place like this, they'll weigh whatever we carry. Don't let them weigh your vows."

"My vows are already spent," she said quietly. "I broke them for you three gates ago. All I'm carrying now is you."

He had no answer for that either. It was becoming a habit, this woman leaving him wordless, and he suspected—if he ever got himself back—he would find that the man he'd been had always been bad at answers too.

They descended.

* * *

The Court received them the way a ledger receives an entry: without surprise.

No herald called their names. No guard barred their way. They simply walked down the ramp of black glass, and the blank-faced courtiers parted around them like water around two stones, and when they reached the floor of the cavern proper, the nearest of the nine gods lifted its head.

Sable felt the attention of it land on him physically, a weight between the shoulder blades, and heard—not with his ears but with the part of him that had spent thirty years balancing other people's sins—an opening of a page.

**Coldharbour,** said a voice that was not a voice but a sum being done. **Debtor. One soul, first-formed, sold in the year of the Grey Frost against the life of Maren Coldharbour. Payment rendered. Debt discharged. What does a discharged debtor want in the Court Beneath?**

"To steal," Sable said.

The word fell into the silence like a coin into a well. Around them, the courtiers went still. Far off, one of the pleading voices faltered mid-*I owe*, then resumed.

The god tilted its blank head. **Theft is our oldest trade. We honor it. But the entry is closed, little clerk. Closed entries are sacred. You of all people should know—we learned bookkeeping from watching men like you.**

"I know how closed entries work," Sable said, and stepped forward, and did the bravest and most foolish thing he had ever done: he opened his coat and drew out the Gilded Debt's master ledger, the one he'd carried through six gates, the one whose pages had begun to glow with ink that wrote itself. "And I know that closed entries can be reopened. When the gods woke, your judgments started collecting themselves. Your law is moving again. Which means your law has loopholes again. I've spent my life finding loopholes in laws older than me. Yours are just older than most."

For a long moment nothing moved except the slow pulse of the gold in the walls, brightening and dimming like something breathing in its sleep.

Then the god laughed. There was no mouth to laugh with, but the sound came anyway, low and grinding, tectonic, and the other eight heads turned toward Sable with the smooth synchronized motion of things that had never needed faces because they had never needed to lie.

**A thief who argues contract law.** The voice was almost fond. **Very well, Coldharbour. The Court will hear your petition. But first—you should know that your soul is not in a vault.**

Sable's stomach dropped. "Where is he?"

**Favorites are not kept in vaults.** The god gestured with one grey hand toward the far side of the dais, toward a colonnade hung with lamps of colored glass. **Come. See what you sold.**

* * *

He was playing dice with a courtier made of smoke.

That was how Sable saw him first: a boy of perhaps ten years, sitting cross-legged on the warm stone beneath the colored lamps, shaking a cup of knucklebones with the quick, greedy delight of a child who has never once lost anything that mattered. He wore clothes of the Court's weaving—grey and gold, fine as spider silk—and he was laughing.

Sable stopped walking. Wren's hand tightened on his arm and he barely felt it.

Because he knew that laugh. He had heard it last in a sickroom in Salt Row, thirty years ago, when a girl with fever-bright eyes had told a joke about a neighbor's goat and a boy of nineteen had laughed until his ribs hurt, because the fever hadn't taken her yet, and hope had still been cheap.

The boy looked up.

His face was Sable's face. Not the way a mirror is—it was younger, rounder, unlined, the nose not yet broken in a warehouse brawl, the jaw not yet set into its careful neutral mask—but underneath, unmistakably, it was him. It was the face that had existed before the carving. Before the guild. Before he had taught himself, cut by patient cut, to be a man shaped like a gap in a crowd.

And the boy's eyes went wide, and he dropped the dice cup, and the knucklebones scattered across the stone, and he said:

"You have her hands."

Sable could not speak.

"She used to hold them like that," the boy said, scrambling up, coming closer, fearless, radiant, talking the way children talk when they have waited years for someone to arrive. "Flat and steady, like she was smoothing something. When she braided Maren's hair. When she counted coins. You count the way she counted—I saw you come down the ramp and I thought, *that's Mother's counting.* Do you know her? Is she—" and here the boy stopped, suddenly wary in a way that cracked Sable open along a seam he hadn't known he had, "—is she why you came? Did she send you? Did she finally pay?"

"No," Sable managed. His voice was wreckage. "She died. A long time ago."

The boy absorbed this the way children absorb death: completely, instantly, and with a grief that had no armor on it at all. His face crumpled and reformed, crumpled and reformed, and then he did the thing that undid Sable entirely. He reached out and took Sable's hand—flat and steady, smoothing something—and held it.

"But you remember her," the boy said. "Tell me you remember her. They let me keep her face but they take pieces of it sometimes, when I misbehave at dice. Last winter I lost her voice for a whole month. I had to draw it. I drew it wrong." His eyes were wet and furious and terribly, terribly young. "So tell me you kept her safe. Tell me somebody kept her."

And Sable Coldharbour, who had built his entire self out of not-being-here, who had carved away his own soul to buy his sister's breath, stood in the gold-lit dark holding the hand of the boy he had amputated, and understood at last the full shape of what he had done. He had not sold a soul to save a sister. He had sold a son to save a daughter, and the son had been kept, like a favorite, like a pet, like a lamp that mustn't go out—and all these years the boy had been down here in the warm, remembering everything, while the man walked above in the cold, remembering nothing, calling the emptiness *safety*.

"I kept her," Sable said. It was a lie. It was the truest lie he had ever told. "I kept every piece of her. I came to bring her to you."

Behind them, the nine blank heads had turned. The sum was being done again; he felt it like weather.

**Careful, Coldharbour,** said the god, mild as frost. **Promises made in the Court Beneath are entered. And entered debts collect themselves.**

The boy was still holding his hand. The boy was smiling up at him with Sable's own unbroken face, saying, *You'll stay for supper? They always give favorites supper.*

And across the cavern, in the shadows of the colonnade, Sable saw Wren go very still—because she had seen what he had not: that the smoke-courtier the boy had been playing dice with had risen silently, and was drifting toward the dais, and had begun, in a voice like paper tearing, to whisper to the nine.

Whispering, Sable would learn soon enough, about a priestess who had broken her vows to walk into the dark.

Whispering about bargains.
