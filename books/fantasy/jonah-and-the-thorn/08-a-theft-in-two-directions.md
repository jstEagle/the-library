## 8. A Theft in Two Directions

Wren had prayed in a hundred postures—kneeling on cold flagstones, standing hip-deep in the river at dawn, face-down in the dust of the pilgrim road—but she had never prayed while committing theft. The old gods, she suspected, would find it refreshing.

The court beneath the mountain did not sleep. That was the first thing she had learned in the three days since they descended, and the thing that made her task nearly impossible. Light came from no source she could name; it seeped from the walls themselves, pale as bone marrow, and beneath that light the court moved in its endless slow business of judgment. Debts were read aloud in voices like water dripping through stone. Debtors wept or laughed or went silent forever. And through it all, threading between the pillars of black basalt, walked the boy.

He was smaller than Sable had been at that age—or so Sable guessed, having no memories of his own childhood to consult. He wore clothes of grey silk that never wrinkled, and his hair was cut in the fashion of a city Wren had never seen, and he carried everywhere a small carved bird that he turned over and over in his fingers as though checking that it still existed. The court favored him. They let him sit near the throne of the eldest god when judgments were rendered. They fed him sweetmeats shaped like moths. They called him *Little Ledger*, because he was an entry made flesh, a debt kept alive for its beauty rather than its value.

And he was Sable. Not metaphorically, not mystically—he *was* the man Wren loved, the part of him cut away and sold: every memory of their mother's face, every summer before the sickness, every unguarded laugh. When the boy smiled at the court's cruelties with a child's easy delight, Wren saw Sable's mouth. When he flinched from the eldest god's shadow, she saw the flinch Sable still carried in his shoulders without knowing why.

Tonight, while the court convened to hear the false ledger Sable had prepared—a masterpiece of forgery, forty pages of invented debts so baroque and binding that even the old gods would need hours to untangle them—Wren would take the boy back.

She crouched behind a pillar of basalt, in the one pocket of shadow the marrow-light could not reach, and she prayed the way her order had taught her: not with words but with attention. *You who wake. You whose name I must not speak here. Look at what I am about to do, and forgive me, or don't.*

The waking god did not answer. It hadn't, not clearly, since they passed the third gate. But warmth had pooled in her chest ever since she broke her vows—since the night she chose Sable over her order—and she had learned to read that warmth the way sailors read weather. It was there now. Faint. Watchful. The god was watching her steal.

*Good,* she thought. *Let it watch. You taught me that all debts must be paid. This is me paying one.*

* * *

Across the chamber, Sable stood before the dais with the false ledger open across both hands, and Wren marveled, as she always marveled, at how completely he could disappear into a performance. His voice had changed—not louder, but denser, weighted with the boredom of a professional reading terms to amateurs. "—and here, my lords, the matter of the Weeping Duke of Corvane, whose grief was purchased secondhand and has appreciated considerably—" The eldest god leaned forward on its throne of fused vertebrae. Two of the lesser gods began arguing over a clause. A clerk-thing with too many hands reached for the book.

Now.

Wren moved along the pillar's base, soft-footed, her stolen court-cloaks deadening every sound. The boy sat apart from the proceedings on a step of the dais, bored, turning his carved bird. Children, even soul-children, even thirty-year-old children preserved in amber, grew restless during long readings. She counted on it.

She was ten feet away when he looked up and saw her.

She froze. He tilted his head. And then he did the thing that undid her: he put a finger to his lips.

*Shh.*

He knew. Of course he knew—the moment Sable had entered the court, the boy had known, the way a scar knows rain coming. He had been watching Sable for days with an expression Wren couldn't name, hunger and terror braided together. And now, watching her creep toward him with theft in her hands, he was helping.

Her eyes stung. She crossed the last distance and knelt beside him, and up close he smelled of moth-sweetmeats and something older, something like the inside of a house where someone is baking bread, and she understood suddenly why Sable had sold this and not something else. This was the smell of being loved before he learned to hide.

"I'm taking you home," she whispered.

"Home isn't a place," the boy said. His voice was light, unbroken, a voice Sable no longer owned. "It's a person. Mama said." He looked toward the dais, where Sable's voice flowed on, smooth as oil. "Is that him? Is that what I became?"

"You didn't become anything," Wren said. "You're what he was."

"Then he's very quiet now." The boy turned the bird in his fingers. "I remember everything. He remembers nothing. Which one of us is real?"

"That question," said a voice like a mountain settling, "is precisely the sort this court exists to answer."

* * *

They had not seen the eldest god move. That was the horror of the old ones—they did not move so much as *arrive*, the way dread arrives, already present before you notice it. The eldest stood above them now, vast in its robe of stitched names, and the marrow-light bent toward it as if ashamed.

The reading stopped. Sable's head came up across the chamber, and for one naked instant Wren saw his face without its performance—saw the terror of a man watching the only two things he loved stand in a god's shadow.

"The Little Ledger attempts departure," the eldest god said. "The priestess assists. How tidy. Theft, in this court, is merely a debt incurred in the wrong direction." It extended a hand like a root system, and Wren felt herself lifted—not dragged, simply *relocated*, her feet leaving the floor with terrible gentleness. "But we are just. We are old law, and old law offers bargains. Hear ours, daughter of the waking god."

"I'm not—" she started.

"You pray to a god whose name you will not speak even here, where names are currency and yours is worth less than the boy's." The eldest god's regard settled on her like snowfall. "Interesting. Your order believes names are sacred. We believe names are *payable*. So. Here is our offer, priestess, and we make it once."

The court drew close. Even Sable moved forward, helpless, held at the edge of the gathering by two clerk-things with too many hands.

"Your thief's debt," the eldest god said, "the soul sold by Sable Coldharbour to buy Maren Coldharbour's life—that debt is thirty years old, compounding, and the interest alone would strip him to a whisper. We could forgive it. Entirely. The boy walks free, the ledger entry burns, your thief walks out of this mountain whole and owing nothing." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat against skin. "In exchange, you give us the name of your god."

The silence that followed had texture. Wren felt it press against her eardrums.

To give a god's true name to the old courts—to the creditors of souls, the collectors, the beings who had woken hungry after an age of sleep—was not sacrifice. It was apocalypse in miniature. A named god could be summoned. Bound. *Billed.* Everything her order had built, every ward and vow and whispered prayer, existed to keep that single syllable out of hands like these. If she spoke it, the waking god would wake fully—and be owned.

And if she refused, Sable would remain hollow forever. Half a man, walking out of the mountain lighter than he entered, safe and empty until he died.

Duty and love did not argue in her chest. That was the strange thing. They simply stood together, shoulder to shoulder, and waited to see which hand she would raise.

"Wren." Sable's voice, cracked open. "Don't. Whatever it costs me—don't. I've been half a man for thirty years. I know how to live in the missing parts."

"That's exactly what frightens me," she said, and her voice did not shake, which surprised her. "That you've gotten *good* at it."

The boy tugged her sleeve. He had not been lifted or restrained; the court seemed to have forgotten him, or was watching him the way one watches a lit fuse. "Priestess," he said, using the word carefully, tasting it. "If you say the name, does everyone get to keep their memories? Even the sad ones?"

"Yes," Wren said.

"Then why do they look so scared?" He pointed at the court—at the gods, the clerks, the whole rotting grandeur of them—and Wren saw it then, the thing the boy saw with a child's terrible clarity: they *were* scared. The eldest god's offer was not confident. It was desperate. The old courts ran on debts, and debts required ledgers, and ledgers required that names stay written and fixed. A god whose name entered these books would be collateral—but collateral cuts both ways. Something in those ancient bindings would snag. She could see it in the eldest god's posture, the slight forward lean of a creditor who needs the payment more than it admits.

"You want more than the name," Wren said slowly. "You *need* it. Why?"

The marrow-light flickered. For the first time since she had entered the court, the eldest god was silent a beat too long.

"We woke," it said at last, "owing."

The words dropped into the chamber like stones into a well, and every lesser god flinched. *We woke owing.* The old gods had risen—and rising had costs, and somewhere above or beyond them sat creditors older still, and the courts beneath the mountain were not judges at all. They were debtors, dressing their desperation as law.

The boy laughed. It was a bright, awful, wonderful sound, Sable's laugh, the one he'd lost. "They're not the bank," he whispered to Wren. "They're behind on payments."

And in that moment Wren understood the shape of the game, and understood also that she could not play it alone. Because the eldest god was watching her with new calculation, and Sable was watching her with love and dread in equal measure, and the boy was turning his carved bird over and over, faster now, like a heart beating in a small wooden fist.

"Here is my counter-offer," Wren said, lifting her chin. "You keep your bargain on the table. I'll consider the name. But first—" she looked at Sable, and let him see everything she intended, "—your court should hear what my god says when I ask it directly. Right here. In front of your ledgers."

Because she had realized something the old gods, in all their ages of accounting, might have forgotten: a god who wakes answering prayers does not do it for free either. And she was very much afraid—no, she was very much *certain*—that the waking god had been waiting, all this time, for someone to bring its name down into the dark.

The eldest god's many-jointed fingers curled slowly closed.

"Speak, then," it said. "And let us see what your god charges."

Wren closed her eyes, found the warmth behind her sternum, and opened her mouth to pray aloud for the first time in her life—in the one place in the world where prayer had teeth.
