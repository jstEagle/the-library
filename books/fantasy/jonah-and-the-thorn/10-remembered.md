## 10. Remembered

The sun was an insult.

That was Sable's first thought, standing in the guild courtyard with the ledger open against his forearm and the morning pouring down on him like something spilled. For thirty years he had arranged his life to slip beneath light—stood in doorways, kept his voice at the pitch of furniture, worn colors the eye declined to hold. Now the light found him everywhere. It lay across his shoulders like a hand. It warmed the scar at his throat where the thorn had gone in, and it would not be argued with.

He ached everywhere. That was the price, and he had known it would be, and he had paid it anyway: every memory he had carved out of himself to become invisible had come home, and they had come home *loud*. His mother's face, which he had not been able to summon for three decades, now arrived unbidden each time he closed his eyes—the flour on her knuckles, the way she hummed off-key when she thought no one listened. The boy he had been stood inside him again, small and stubborn and grieving, and Sable carried him the way one carries a sleeping child: carefully, forever, with arms that would never be allowed to put him down.

It hurt. It hurt the way weather hurts, the way hunger hurts, the way being *known* hurts. He would feel all of it for the rest of his life. He had chosen it in the dark beneath the mountain, with the old gods watching from their thrones of debt and bone, and he would choose it again, and that certainty was the strangest thing of all—stranger than the waking gods, stranger than the glowing ink, stranger than the fact that he could now walk through the Gilded Debt's great hall and have every clerk look up and *see* him.

They were looking at him now. Word had come up from below ahead of them, as word always did in Coldharbour: the ledger-keeper had gone down with nothing but his own book and come back whole. The clerks lined the colonnade in their grey robes, and none of them pretended not to stare, and Sable found—to his considerable surprise—that he did not mind. Let them look. Let them learn his face. A man who is seen can be loved; a man who is invisible can only be missed by accident.

Wren stood beside him at the brazier. She had changed out of her priestess whites into plain travelling wool, because there was no order left to dress for. The Temple of the Waking God had cast her out two days after their return—*consorting with a debtor of the elder courts*, the writ had said, as if love were a line item—and she had read the writ standing in the temple doorway, and then she had laughed, actually laughed, and folded it into a paper boat and set it sailing down the gutter.

"They'll send someone to fetch the brazier," she said. "Guild property. They're very particular about guild property."

"Let them send someone." Sable turned to the ledger's final page. "I'm very particular about what I burn."

The entry glowed still, though fainter now, its claim spent. *One soul, first-formed, sold to the court beneath the mountain by the hand of Sable Coldharbour, in payment for the life of Maren Coldharbour. Status: RECLAIMED.* Beneath it, in ink that had appeared only this morning, in a hand that was unmistakably his own and unmistakably not—a child's round letters grown up into a thief's slanted script, both at once, because he was both at once now:

*Paid in full.*

He held the page over the coals.

For a moment—old habit, older than habit—he hesitated. Thirty years of keeping books had taught him that a ledger is a kind of law, that what is written persists, that debts do not die but only sleep. To burn a page was to unmake a truth. And some frightened cellar of him whispered that if the entry burned, maybe the reclamation burned too; maybe the boy would go back into the dark; maybe he would wake tomorrow hollow again, safe again, nothing again.

Wren's hand covered his where it gripped the page. Her fingers were warm. Her fingers were always warm; it was the first thing he had noticed about her, back when she sat across the interview table asking her careful questions and he had felt her attention pass over the hollow in him like a lamp passed over an empty room.

"It's already true," she said quietly. "Burning it doesn't make it true. Burning it just stops the book from arguing."

"You sound like your god."

"My god sounds like me. We've been over this." She smiled, and the ache in his chest that was grief and the ache that was joy leaned into each other and became, briefly, indistinguishable. "Burn it, Sable. You've paid enough interest."

He let the corner touch the coal.

The page caught slowly, the way important things burn, the glow of the ink flaring gold before the flame took it, and for one heartbeat Sable saw the whole entry written in fire—his name, Maren's name, the court's cold seal—and then it was smoke, rising straight up through the morning air, unwinding like a thread pulled from a knot. Somewhere far beneath the city, he felt the court beneath the mountain register the loss of its claim, felt the old gods turn over in their long attention like sleeper shifting under blankets. One of them, he suspected, was smiling. The Thorn had liked him. The Thorn had said so, in its voice like roots splitting stone: *You will hurt beautifully.*

He let the last scrap go and closed the empty binding.

* * *

Maren was waiting in the herb garden behind the house on Candlemaker's Row, because Maren had decided, in the way of sisters everywhere, that if the world was ending or beginning she would meet it sitting down with tea.

She had been faded for years—that was the word Sable had finally learned to use, after the confrontation that had cracked his life open. Faded. Like cloth washed too many times, like a face behind fogged glass. The cure he had bought her had saved her body and starved her spirit, feeding it drip by drip on the interest of his stolen self, and she had lived those years half-present, forgetting mid-sentence, drifting off in the middle of her own laughter. He had told himself it was the illness. He had known, somewhere he refused to look, that it was the bill coming due.

Now she looked up as they came through the gate, and Sable stopped walking, because seeing her was like seeing color for the first time after a long grey illness.

She was *bright*. Not healed-bright, not the brittle brightness of the cured—she was present, all of her, every year of her forty-one years assembled and accounted for, her eyes sharp and brown and entirely her own. She had freckles he'd forgotten she had. She had her mother's exact frown, deployed at once upon seeing him.

"You look terrible," she announced.

"I feel wonderful," Sable said, and his voice broke on it, and he didn't try to hide that either, because hiding was over, hiding was finished, hiding had cost him everything and bought him nothing but time.

Maren set down her cup and crossed the garden and took his face in her hands the way she had when he was nine and had fallen out of the pear tree, and she studied him with the fierce, inventorying gaze of someone checking a brother for damage, and whatever she found made her eyes fill.

"There you are," she said. "There you *are*. I used to talk to you and it was like talking down a well. I'd get echoes." Her thumb traced the scar at his throat. "Does it hurt?"

"Constantly."

"Good," she said, and kissed his forehead, and he understood that she meant it—that in their family, in their blood, pain freely chosen was the only honest currency, and she would rather have a brother who ached and stayed than a ghost who drifted. "Sit down. The tea's going cold, and I spent actual money on it, which you of all people should respect."

He laughed. It came out of him whole and unguarded, loud enough that the neighbor paused at her window, and Sable met the neighbor's eyes and nodded to her, and she nodded back, having seen him, and the world went on.

Wren poured the tea. Maren watched her pour it with the frank appraisal of a sister who has already decided how this is going to go, and Sable felt the heat climb his neck—another novelty, blushing at thirty-eight, his body apparently determined to make him feel everything including embarrassment.

"So," Maren said. "You're the priestess who got exiled for him."

"I'm the priestess who got exiled *with* him," Wren corrected. "There's a difference. Exile's much nicer with company."

"And you're staying?"

"Maren—" Sable began.

"I'm asking her, not you." Maren sipped her tea, serene as an empress.

Wren set down the pot and looked at Maren directly, and Sable watched two women who loved him conduct, over his head, the negotiation of his entire future. "I gave up my vows," Wren said. "Not my faith. My god woke up, and it turns out the waking god cares a great deal less about cloisters and a great deal more about what you choose when the knife's at your throat. I chose him. I'd choose him again with the knife already in. So yes. I'm staying. If he'll have me, which he's been too much of a coward to say out loud, so I'm saying it for him."

"She's right," Sable said hoarsely. "I have been."

Maren clapped her hands once, decisively, the sound of a verdict rendered. "Then it's settled. Wren takes the back room. Sable, you're going to stop sleeping in the shop above the guild, because I've seen that cot, and I want it burned next."

"The ledger first," Sable said. "Then the cot. I'm reforming gradually."

* * *

Evening came, and with it the bells—not the guild's bells, with their flat accounting clang, but the temple bells, and then, underneath them, bells no one had installed: a resonance in the stones themselves, in the bones, in the ground. All across Coldharbour the old gods were answering prayers tonight. Sable could feel it through the soles of his feet, a vast slow turning, the machinery of heaven grinding awake after centuries of disuse. Down in the streets people were kneeling in doorways, shouting into the sky, bargaining, thanking, cursing. The age of impossible things had reopened its ledgers, and every soul in the city was suddenly a debtor or a creditor, and someone—someone would have to keep the books.

Sable stood at the garden wall with Wren's shoulder warm against his, watching the first stars come out over the mountain. The thorn in him pulsed gently with the evening, aching, alive, his.

"You know what they'll ask," Wren said. "The guild. The temples. Everyone. A man who went into the elder courts and came out whole—they'll want him keeping accounts. Divine ones. Debts between gods and mortals." She turned to look at him, and the starlight caught the smile she was trying and failing to suppress. "They'll want *you*, Sable Coldharbour. Loudly. Publicly. By name."

He considered it. The old reflex rose—shrink, dim, decline, survive—and he let it rise and watched it find nothing to hold onto, no hollow to hide in, no darkness left to be. The boy inside him, the first-formed self, the one who remembered their mother humming off-key, tugged at his ribs like a child at a sleeve, wanting to see.

"Let them ask," Sable said. "I've decided to start saying yes to things."

Far below the city, deep beneath the mountain, something ancient opened a fresh page, dipped a pen in light, and wrote—at the top, in waiting ink—a single new entry bearing two names side by side, and the words: *to be collected, gladly, in full.*
