## 1. Small Charms

The fish market woke before the sun did, the way it always did in Saltcross, and Coral Halloran woke with it, because a man who sold luck could not afford to sleep past the hour when luck was most needed.

He set up his stall in the blue-grey dark with hands that knew the work without being asked: the folding table with its one short leg shimmed by a wedge of driftwood, the canvas awning strung between two pilings that smelled of tar and old herring, the little wooden tray where he laid out his wares in rows as neat as teeth. Luck-knots first—three-strand braids of grey cord, each knot tied seven times, each tie accompanied by a word he mouthed and never spoke aloud. Then the fever-wards, small pouches of salt and dried eelgrass stitched shut with red thread, hung from loops so they could be nailed above a sickbed door. Then the sea-charms for the boats, flat discs of bleached wood carved with waves that were not quite waves, if you looked at them too long.

None of it was magic. That was the point. None of it, to any eye that might be watching, was anything but the honest craft of a charm-seller, the kind of trinket every harbor town had sold since before anyone could remember, harmless as a horseshoe over a door. The knots were just knots. The wards were just salt and grass. If a buyer wanted to believe a bit of cord could turn the tide of fortune, well—that was their faith, not his craft, and no law in Saltcross had ever been written against cord.

The law had been written against everything else.

Coral worked the seventh knot on the first braid of the morning and let his lips shape the word beneath them—*veil*—the way a man might touch an old scar without thinking. Five years since he had spoken it aloud. Five years since the god had spoken back.

"Morning, Coral." Old Maren from the oyster boat, hauling her baskets past his stall, her knuckles swollen like galls on a branch. "Give us a look at those fever-wards. My Tam's got the shivers again."

"Fever-wards are three coppers, Maren, and I'll tell you what I always tell you—boil the water, keep him dry, and don't let your sister-in-law near him with her remedies."

Maren laughed, a sound like a gull with opinions, and counted out her coppers into his palm. Her fingers brushed his as she took the ward, and Coral felt the small familiar ache of it—the warmth of a stranger's trust placed in something he could not honestly sell. Because the truth, the truth he kept folded up inside himself like the grimoire leaf he would burn page by page soon enough, was that the wards *had* worked once. Had worked beautifully. Had worked because when Coral tied the red thread he whispered to a god who lived beneath the water and the world, and the god had listened, and the fever had broken like a wave on sand.

Five years ago. Before the silence.

He tied another knot. Mouthed the word. Felt nothing come back, the way he had felt nothing come back five hundred mornings running, and kept tying anyway.

* * *

By midmorning the market had filled with its usual riot: fishwives crying the catch, gulls conducting their endless larceny overhead, the reek and shine of the day's haul spread on beds of ice-chipped kelp. Coral sold a luck-knot to a young man about to ask a question of a certain girl; sold a sea-charm to a trawler captain who gripped his shoulder and said, "My father bought these off your mother, gods rest her. Good to see the trade stay in the family." Sold nothing at all to a woman who stood at the edge of his stall for a long moment, looking at the fever-wards with an expression he had learned to read the way sailors read clouds, and then walked away too quickly.

That expression was getting more common. He pretended it wasn't.

It was midday when the riders came through—four of them in the crown's grey-and-white, moving slow along the market's spine, and behind them a clerk on a mule with a bucket of paste and a sheaf of broadsheets under his arm. The market's noise changed pitch, the way water changes sound when something large passes beneath a boat. Conversations didn't stop so much as lower themselves, bending toward whispers.

Coral watched them post the edicts. He made himself watch. A man who looked away looked guilty, and a guilty man was a man the hunters remembered.

The broadsheets went up on the customs house wall, on the fish-weighing post, on the corner of the chandlery where everyone queued. Big black letters on white, the crown's seal stamped at the bottom in wax the color of old blood:

*BY ORDER OF THE LORD JUSTICE OF SALTCROSS: A CENSUS OF THE AFFLICKED SHALL BE TAKEN IN ALL QUARTERS OF THIS CITY AND ITS HINTERLANDS. ALL PERSONS POSSESSED OF UNLAWFUL GIFTS, OR HARBOURING, AIDING, OR CONCEALING SUCH PERSONS, ARE BOUND BY LAW TO PRESENT THEMSELVES OR MAKE REPORT TO THE OFFICE OF PURIFICATION WITHIN THIRTY DAYS. REWARD OFFERED FOR TRUE INFORMATION. PENALTY FOR CONCEALMENT: AS FOR THE CRIME ITSELF.*

*As for the crime itself.* Which was fire. Everyone knew what the penalty was. They'd burned the tanner's daughter on Merrow's Green in the spring, and the ferryman in autumn before that, and last month—last month they'd burned a family out past the weir, a whole family, though the official broadsheet had only ever said *a practitioner and accomplices*, as if the children had been accomplices too.

Coral went back to tying knots. His hands were steady. He had trained them to be steady the way other men trained themselves not to flinch at loud noises, and they obeyed him even when the rest of him wanted to shake.

"You seen this?" muttered the eel-seller from the next stall over, jerking his chin at the broadsheet. "Census. Means they'll be knocking on doors."

"Means they'll be counting," Coral said mildly. "Counting's not knocking."

"Counting's how knocking starts." The eel-seller spat into the gutter. "My gran used to say there was a time nobody needed a paper to tell them what a gift was. Said the god himself—" He stopped. Looked around. Looked at Coral, and something passed across his face, quick and unreadable, before he turned back to his eels. "Never mind what my gran said. Gran talks rubbish."

But he didn't meet Coral's eye for the rest of the day, and Coral lay awake that night wondering which of them the eel-seller thought needed watching, and whether the answer was both.

* * *

The shrine was down a dead-end lane behind the ropewalk, through a door that looked like it led to a collapsed cellar and actually led to a stair, and down the stair to a chamber that had been, a very long time ago, part of something much larger. Coral had found it his first winter in Saltcross, following a damp draft and a feeling in his teeth like the moment before a storm. The room was barely bigger than a fishing skiff's cabin, its walls weeping mineral stains in long pale curtains, its floor always faintly wet no matter the weather above. Seawater seeped somewhere in the dark and pooled in a natural basin of stone, and it was in that basin that Coral kept his shrine.

It was a poor thing by any honest measure. A bowl of the basin's own water. Three shells arranged in a curve. A stub of candle he lit only when the fog above was thick enough to swallow the light leaking up through the grate—which was never, quite, but he told himself it was. And at the center, half-submerged in the pool, a piece of green sea-glass worn to the shape of an eye, which was not holy and had never been blessed and was only sea-glass, except that on the night he'd found it, five years ago, the last night the Veil had ever spoken to him, the god had said: *keep that one.*

So he kept it.

Coral came at night, after the market stalls were struck and the taverns had drawn their first full houses and the watch had made its first round and would not make another for hours. He came down the stairs with his boots in his hand so the steps wouldn't ring, and he knelt on the wet stone, and he lit the candle, and he talked.

"I sold eleven knots today," he said. "Twelve, if you count the one I botched and gave to the gulls. Maren bought a fever-ward for her boy. You remember Maren? No—of course you don't. Of course you don't." He laughed, softly, and the laugh came out wrong, the way laughs do when they're carrying more than laughter. "I keep forgetting that's the arrangement now. I talk. You listen. Or you don't even listen. Maybe I'm just talking to water. Maybe the water's the honest one between us, at least it moves."

The candle flame leaned in a draft that wasn't there—or was it? He had spent five years learning the difference between a draft and a presence, and in five years the difference had been: drafts happened, presences didn't.

"They posted the census today." His voice dropped, the way voices drop in churches, in sickrooms, in the presence of things too large for ordinary volume. "Thirty days. Reward for informing. You understand what that means? It means my neighbors are going to price me. Not out of wickedness—most of them, not out of wickedness. Out of need. Coin is coin, and the winter's coming hard, and the crown has made it so that loving your neighbor is a luxury. That's clever. That's the cleverest cruelty I know. They didn't have to make people hate us. They only had to make hate affordable."

The pool moved. Just the seep, just the tide working far below, breathing through cracks in the world. Just water doing what water does.

"I'm not angry," Coral said, and then, because lying to a god—even a silent one, even maybe a nonexistent one—seemed a poor way to pass the hours, he corrected himself. "I am angry. I'm furious. But not at you. That's the thing I can't make anyone understand, because there's no one to explain it to. Everyone thinks the faithful must feel abandoned, and abandoned men turn bitter, and bitter men are exactly what the hunters say we are. But I don't feel abandoned. I feel—" He searched for it, kneeling in the wet dark with his candle guttering. "I feel like a man standing outside a house where the lights have gone out. I don't know if you've gone. I don't know if you've been taken. I don't know if you're asleep, or wounded, or listening from some room I can't see, unable to answer. All I know is the house is dark, and I'm not leaving the doorstep. Someone should be on the doorstep. In case."

His knees ached. He was thirty-one years old and his knees ached like his father's had, and his mother's grimoire sat wrapped in oilcloth under the floorboards of his rooms, and every day he thought: *tonight I'll start burning it,* and every night he thought: *one more day.* One more day of a god who might still be there. One more day of a faith that cost nothing but loneliness, which was, he was beginning to suspect, the cheapest price it would ever ask him.

"I'll come tomorrow," he said, rising, stiff-jointed, gathering his boots. "And the day after. And the day after that. That's the whole of my theology now, I suppose. Showing up."

At the top of the stair, pausing with his hand on the cold door, he spoke the word aloud for the first time in longer than he could precisely reckon—not the god's name, which he had never dared, but the word, the small word, the one he mouthed into every knot.

"Veil."

Silence. The particular silence of a city at night, layered and indifferent, gulls and rigging and distant drunk singing and the great slow breath of the tide.

He pulled the door shut behind him and went home through streets where new broadsheets gleamed pale on every wall, thirty days ticking down in black ink, and told himself, as he told himself every night, that tomorrow the god might answer.

Tomorrow. Or the day after.

He was still telling himself that when the knock finally came—but that was weeks away yet, and it was not the god who knocked.
