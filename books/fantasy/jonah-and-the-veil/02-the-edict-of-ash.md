## 2. The Edict of Ash

The edicts came on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays were for mending nets and mending quarrels; Tuesdays were when the fishwives forgave each other for Saturday's shouting. The crown should have chosen a colder day, a day without meaning, but crowns never understood that a city keeps a calendar of its own, stitched from small mercies, and the crown had never once been invited to the sewing.

Coral Halloran heard them before he saw them—the wet slap of paste-brush on brick, the squeak of the broadsheets going up, the low murmur of the market crowd gathering the way gulls gather over a gutted fish. He was setting out his luck-knots on the trestle, arranging them in their careful descending rows, when the first crier's bell rang out from the Customs House steps.

"By decree of the Crown Regent, in the matter of public health and spiritual safety—" the crier's voice was young and too loud and enjoying itself, "—a census shall be taken of all persons afflicted, suspected of affliction, or harboring the afflicted. Thirty days. Informants compensated. Refusal to declare constitutes concealment, punishable as affliction itself."

*Harboring the afflicted.* Coral's hands did not stop moving. That was the first discipline his mother had ever taught him, before she taught him a single charm: *the work goes on, love, whatever the sky is doing. The work goes on, or the work owns you.* So he straightened the luck-knots, blue thread for travelers, grey for the grieving, and he did not look at the broadsheet nailed to the fishmonger's post, though its black ink seemed to pull at the corner of his eye the way a hook pulls at a cheek.

He had known this was coming. You did not survive five years in Saltcross by being surprised when the tide came in. There had been the edicts against unlicensed physicking, then the edicts against charms of any kind, then the burning of the charm-seller Old Nan Pell in the Grainmarket square two winters back—she had been eighty-one and half-blind and they had still made her climb the steps of the pyre herself, because mercy was not in the hunter's inventory. After Nan Pell, the city had gone quiet in a way that had nothing to do with peace. It was the quiet of a house where someone has died and the family is deciding, day by day, whether to say so.

And now the census. Now the counting. Now the crown asking every neighbor to weigh every neighbor, and setting a price on the scale.

Copper, Coral had heard. A copper a name. Less than a herring. That was the arithmetic of betrayal in Saltcross this season: cheaper than supper, and it would feed a family for a week.

He sold four luck-knots that morning. A dockhand with a ship leaving for the ice-fisheries. Two old women buying fever-wards for grandchildren, though the fever had passed through their street in the spring and was not expected back until autumn—wards bought the way umbrellas are bought, against a sky that might turn. And a boy of maybe ten, who counted out his coins one at a time with the gravity of a treasurer and asked, in a whisper, whether the knot would keep his mother from crying at night. Coral had looked at the boy for a long moment and then given him two knots for the price of one, the second one grey, and told him the grey one was for the crying and the blue one was for the morning after, and that mornings were stubborn things and usually won.

The boy had nodded solemnly, as if this were doctrine, and gone.

It was good work. It was honest work, or honest-adjacent, which was the best Saltcross offered. None of it was true charm-work—no breath of the Veil in any knot or ward, only thread and salt and the old wives' confidence that a thing made with care works better than a thing made without. His mother had called it *scaffolding*: the little crafts that hold a shape while the real building goes on somewhere out of sight. He had not done the real building in five years. The real building required a god on the other end of the rope, and his god had gone quiet as a shut door.

By midday the market had digested the edict the way a body digests a poison—slowly, with small shudders. He watched it happen in the faces around him. The fishwife Marta, who had shouted him a good morning every day for three years, suddenly very busy with her scales. The rope-seller Doss, whose eyes went down the row of stalls the way a man's eyes go down a row of horses he's been asked to value. A pair of clerks in crown grey moving along the stalls with a ledger, asking cheerful, terrible questions. *And who lives above you, master charm-seller? And how long? And do they keep late hours?*

"Early hours," Coral said. "Early to bed, early to the boats. Nothing late in my house but the tide."

The clerk smiled without smiling and wrote something down. It did not matter what. It would have been the same word whatever he said.

* * *

That night he burned his mother's grimoire.

He had known he would have to. He had known since Nan Pell, if he was honest, and he had not been honest with himself about it, because a man can carry a lie like a warm stone in his pocket and only notice the weight when he has to set it down. The census made it simple. The census made it arithmetic. A book like that, found in a charm-seller's house—a book with the Veil's old sigils pressed into its boards and its pages written in his mother's quick slanting hand, with marginal notes, with little sketches of her laughing at her own jokes in the margins, with a pressed sprig of sea-lavender on the page where she had written his name for the first time in ink because a naming was too important to trust to memory alone—

They would not just burn the book. They would burn the house it was found in. They would burn whoever was standing nearest the house, on the principle that fire is economical and spreads.

So. Arithmetic.

He waited until the fog came in off the harbor, the thick grey wool of it that muffled sound and made the streetlamps into smudged moons, and then he built the fire up in his hearth until it was hot enough to take paper cleanly, and he sat down on the floor with the grimoire in his lap like a child on a knee.

It was a fat book, much mended, its spine stitched twice with waxed cord. The cover was driftwood-board, and the sigils on it had been carved by his grandmother and refreshed by his mother, and the whole thing smelled the way his childhood had smelled: salt, tallow, dried lavender, the particular smoke-smell of a house where charms were worked daily and the god was a presence like a second parent, felt in every room.

*Page by page,* he told himself. *Not all at once. She would want it read out, at least. She would want the words to go into the fire saying themselves, not silent.*

He began at the beginning, where his grandmother's hand gave way to his mother's halfway down the first leaf, the way one tide gives way to the next. A blessing for the launching of boats. He read it aloud, quietly, in the fog-muffled room, and then he laid the page on the coals and watched the words go—watched the blessing curl and blacken and lift, its ash rising up the chimney toward a sky that had no god listening in it, or none that would say so.

The fire took each page the same way, and he learned the rhythm of it: the paper browns first at the edges where the words are thinnest, then the heat finds the ink, and the ink goes last, the letters holding their shape in the flame a heartbeat longer than the paper around them, so that for one breath every charm he had ever known hung burning in the air, legible, luminous, and gone.

He read them all aloud. The fever-breaking. The stilling of a bleeding. The calling of a lost child home through fog—*that one he read twice, because his mother had written in the margin, "for Coral, who was always the lost child, though he never went further than the end of the lane."* The blessing over the dead, that eased them through the Veil's dark water and set them on the far shore. The prayer of the covenant itself, the old words every charm-worker learned first and forgot last, the words that were supposed to be a rope with a god holding the far end: *Breath to breath, tide to tide, what was sundered in the making, let the making mend.*

His voice cracked on that one. He let it crack. There was no one to hear, and a man is allowed one crack per book.

Somewhere past midnight, near the end, his hands began to shake, and he discovered that grief, when you have disguised it as practicality for long enough, will wait. It will sit in the corner like a patient creditor. And when the last page but one is in the fire it will stand up and name its terms.

He kept the last leaf.

He had told himself he would burn everything, and he had meant it, and then his fingers closed around the final leaf—the salt-stained one, the oldest, his grandmother's own hand, the leaf that had been in the book since before his mother was born, stained dark at one corner where seawater had found it decades ago—and his hands simply declined the instruction. He held it over the fire. The heat came up against his knuckles. The fire leaned toward the paper like a dog toward a dropped crust.

He could not.

He folded the leaf small, small as a coin, small as a secret, and pushed it down inside his boot, against the arch of his foot, where it beat against his pulse like a second small heart.

The last of the ash went up the chimney. The fire settled into orange coals. Coral sat on the cold floor with an empty lap and a full throat, in a house that suddenly smelled of nothing at all, because a house where the charms have been burned smells of nothing, that is the whole point of burning them, that is the crown's whole doctrine in a single sensory fact: a house without magic smells of nothing, and a house with it smells like lavender and tallow and salt, and every hunter in Saltcross knows the difference.

He was still sitting there, in the grey hour before dawn, when the knock came at his door.

* * *

It was a small knock. That was the first thing. Hunters knocked like they were knocking a door down in advance, three blows with the heel of the fist, but this was two soft raps, apologetic, the knock of someone who hopes no one is home.

Coral opened the door with his hand already curled around the iron poker, and found Wren on his step.

She was thirteen, or thereabouts—street children in Saltcross did not keep exact accounts of their own ages—and she was soaked to the knees with fog-dew, and she was holding her elbows the way she always held them, as if she were her own prisoner under escort. She had been hanging around his stall for the better part of a season, first as a customer with no money, then as an assistant with no wages, sweeping his stretch of cobbles, shooing gulls, fetching thread. He had never invited her. She had simply accrued, the way barnacles accrue, and he had found he did not mind.

"Wren," he said. "It's not even light."

"I know." She did not come in. That was unusual. She usually came in the way weather comes in, without asking. She stood on the step and looked past him into the room, at the hearth, at the drift of ash on the stones, and Coral watched her read it—the little heap of grey, the smell of burnt paper, the way a man sits on a floor at dawn—and he watched her decide not to have read it.

"I couldn't sleep," she said. "The fog's wrong tonight. It's got a sound in it." Her eyes came back to his face and held there, and they were steady in the way of a child who has practiced steadiness until it set like plaster. "I thought you'd want to know. Before the bell."

"Before what bell?"

"The counting clerks." She said it flatly, a fact like the tide table. "They start at the Tidewater end. Marta's already given them names—I saw her at the Customs House with a purse, Coral, I saw the copper in her hand. And Doss was queuing." She swallowed. "They're asking about you too. I heard a clerk say your name. He said, *the charm-seller on Rope Lane, check his stock, check his house.*"

The fog moved behind her, slow as a held breath. Somewhere out in the harbor, a bell buoy clanked its idiot note, and Coral thought: *the fog has a sound in it. She's right. The fog has a sound in it tonight, and the sound is a city turning over in its sleep and selling itself.*

"Thank you," he said. It was not enough and they both knew it and neither of them said anything more about it, because that was the local dialect, the Saltcross tongue: gratitude spoken in the space where the real words should be, both parties fluent.

"You'll be careful," she said. It was not a question. Thirteen-year-olds who have buried things do not ask questions they cannot bear the answers to.

"I'm always careful."

"You're not." But there was no edge on it. She looked at the hearth again, at the ash, and this time she let herself be seen looking, and then she looked back at him, and what passed between them in that doorway was a whole conversation conducted entirely in the grammar of things unsaid: *I know what you burned. I know what you are, or I know enough. I will not say it. Saying it would be a copper in someone's hand. I am not Marta. I am not Doss.*

Coral had spent five years making sure no one knew him, and had succeeded so well that he had begun to believe it himself, and now a soaked child on his doorstep had undone the whole architecture with a look and a silence, and he found—to his considerable surprise—that the feeling in his chest was not fear.

It was something older. It was the feeling of a lamp being carried toward him down a dark street by someone who has no reason to carry it except kindness.

"Go home," he said gently. "Get dry. Don't come to the stall tomorrow. Come the day after."

"Why the day after?"

"Because tomorrow I'm going to be very boring, and you shouldn't have to watch." He managed half a smile. "Day after, bring your elbows and your opinions."

She almost smiled back. Then she went, quick and quiet, swallowed by the fog, and Coral shut the door and stood with his back against it and his hand still curled around nothing, and after a while he took the folded leaf out of his boot and unfolded it on his palm.

*Breath to breath, tide to tide,* said his grandmother's faded hand, in ink gone brown as old blood. *What was sundered in the making, let the making mend.*

The bell buoy clanked. The fog pressed at the windows like a congregation.

He was still holding it when the second knock came—and this one was not small, and it was not soft, and it was not Wren.
