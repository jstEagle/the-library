## 3. Wren

The girl came back on the third morning after the burning of the grimoire, standing at the edge of Coral's stall with her hands folded like a penitent and her eyes fixed on the luck-knots as though they might bite.

"You said you'd think about it," she said.

"I did say that." He had said it hoping she would not return. Saltcross was no place to teach a child anything but how to be small, and Wren—Wren Ashby, thirteen years old, all elbows and watchfulness—had already been small enough for too long. Her grandmother's house smelled of camphor and boiled mutton; her shoes had been resoled twice with leather that didn't match; she had the particular hunger of a child who had learned to eat last and ask for nothing. Coral knew that hunger. He had worn it himself, once, in a fishing village up the coast where his mother had taught him knot-work with one hand and prayer with the other, until the prayer became the dangerous half.

"You thought about it," Wren said. It was not quite a question. She had a way of speaking that made statements out of things other people left hanging, pulling them down like fruit within reach.

"I thought about it." He tied off the cord he was working—a fever-ward, blue wool over red, nothing in it but patience and the suggestion of protection—and set it on the board between them. "Sit. Hands where I can see them."

She sat on the upturned crate he kept for exactly this purpose, though no one had ever used it before, and put her hands flat on the stall's scarred planks. They were good hands, he noted despite himself. Steady. A little salt-cracked at the knuckles, the way harbor children's hands went by ten years old and never recovered. There was a scab on her left thumb from picking at something, or from knots pulled too tight.

"First lesson," Coral said, "is what I don't do."

Wren frowned. "That's not a lesson."

"It's the only one that matters." He held up the fever-ward between two fingers, letting it turn slowly in the gray light off the harbor. The fog had come in early that morning, lying against the market like a cat against a warm wall, muffling the fish-cries and the gulls into something soft and far away. "Anyone can sell you this. Half the stalls here sell some version of it. Blue wool, red wool, three knots, a word said over it—not a spell, just a *suggestion*, a thing your mother whispers when she tucks you in so you'll sleep. That's the craft. That's all the craft there is, legally, in Saltcross, and if you learn it well you'll never go hungry and never hang. Do you understand me?"

"No," Wren said honestly.

"Good. Honest apprentices are worth two clever ones." He laid the ward flat and began, slowly, to untie it. "The difference between this and what gets people burned is—" His hands stopped. Not because he wanted them to. Because they stopped, the way they sometimes did, as if his mother's voice lived in his fingers still and had clamped down hard. Five years since the Veil had spoken. Five years since he had let himself speak to it. And still his hands flinched from the memory of real work, the deep tide-pull of it, the sense of putting his hand through the world's skin and touching something warm and enormous on the other side.

"The difference is intent," he finished. "This is a comfort. The other thing is a *claim*. You knock on a door and ask it to open. Understand?"

"Whose door?"

Coral looked at her. She looked back, unblinking, the way gulls look at you when you're holding bread—patient, certain, absolutely without shame. Thirteen years old and she already asked the questions that adults in Saltcross had trained out of themselves at the cost of their souls.

"Doors you don't want to know about," he said. "Pick up the wool. We start with the three-knot bind."

* * *

He taught her the way his mother had taught him, which was to say cruelly slowly and with total attention. The first knot for memory: *what goes in stays in*. The second for binding: *what is joined holds*. The third for release: *what is let go is gone*. Three gestures, three breaths, a rhythm any fisherman's wife could learn in an afternoon, and none of it magic—only craft, only habit, only the body's own superstition dressed in wool. But Wren learned it the way water learns a channel. By the second day her fingers moved without instruction. By the fourth she was correcting his tension on the crosswise pass, and he let her, because she was right.

"You've done this before," he said, watching her tie a luck-knot for a crab-wife who had lost two sons to the winter swells and wanted, desperately, to believe the sea would give the third one back.

"My brother taught me rope-work." Something crossed her face then, quick as a fish under a boat, and was gone. "Tam. He was going to be a rigger."

"Where is Tam now?"

The pause lasted one breath too long. "Gone," Wren said, and bent over her knot, and Coral did not press, because pressing was a hunter's trick and he had sworn off hunters' tricks even the useful ones.

The market around them went on with its ordinary commerce of herring and rumor. The edict-bills were everywhere now, pasted to walls and doorposts and the flanks of the customs house, black letters on ash-gray paper: *CENSUS OF THE AFFLICTED TO COMMENCE AT MONTH'S END. ALL CITIZENS POSSESSING UNNATURAL GIFTS MUST PRESENT THEMSELVES FOR ASSESSMENT. INFORMERS WILL BE COMPENSATED.* Compensation. That was the new cruelty, the crown having learned that fear alone made men stubborn but coin made them prompt. Old Marta who sold eels had taken to crossing herself with a fish-bone when she thought no one watched, an old country gesture, harmless, and Coral had seen a customs clerk mark something in a ledger when she did it. Marked it. Wrote her name down for a twitch of the hand.

So he kept the lessons godless. Strictly, scrupulously godless. No words over the wool but counting-words. No gestures that traced anything older than arithmetic. When Wren asked why the third knot needed a breath let out and not in, he told her it was balance, and it was, and it also wasn't, and he hated himself a little each time he lied by telling the truth sideways.

On the sixth day she brought him a charm she'd made alone—her first unsupervised work—and it was wrong in a way that stopped his breath.

It was a fever-ward. Blue wool, red wool, three knots. But she had added a fourth knot, low on the cord, tight and strange, and when Coral picked it up his palms prickled with a sensation he had not felt in five years: the nearness of deep water. Not the harbor's shallow gray. Deep water. Cold and vast and patient, the feeling of the sea a mile down where no light has ever been.

"Where did you learn the fourth knot?" he asked, and his voice came out level, which cost him.

"I didn't learn it." Wren shrugged. "My hands did it. It felt right. Like the cord wanted it."

*Cords don't want*, he almost said. Cords don't want, wool doesn't want, and girls of thirteen don't stumble backward into the old grammar of the drowned faith without something reaching up through them to guide the stitch. He set the ward down on the board very gently, the way you set down a sleeping animal you are afraid will wake.

"We'll call that a mistake," he said. "Unpick it. Fourth knots aren't part of the craft."

"But it worked." She wasn't arguing, exactly. She was puzzled, the honest puzzlement of a child confronting an adult's obvious stupidity. "I gave it to the net-mender's boy—the one with the cough—and his cough broke that night. Everyone says so."

Coral looked at the little ward sitting on his boards among its lawful siblings, four-knotted, quietly impossible, humming with the cold of fathoms. Somewhere under the city, under the harbor silt, under the foundations of the customs house and the assize hall and every law-stone the founders had ever raised, something turned over in its sleep—or did not, because it could not, because it was bound—and yet its breath still found its way up through a child's fingers like water finding a crack in a wall.

"Unpick it," Coral said again, softer. "Please, Wren."

And because she was thirteen and he was the master and the world had already taught her obedience, she unpicked it. But she watched him while she did, and her eyes had changed. They had been a child's eyes, hungry and direct. Now they were something older, and sadder, and they did not stop watching him for the rest of the day.

* * *

The fog came thick that evening, the kind the fishermen called the widow's veil, rolling up off the water in slow white shoulders until the lamplight of the quay was only smudges and the masts of the moored boats were suggestions of suggestions. Coral was closing the stall, wrapping the unsold wards in oilcloth, when Wren appeared out of the whiteness at the edge of his lantern's reach.

She should have gone home an hour ago. Her grandmother would be worried, or would pretend to have been worried, which amounted to the same ritual. But there she stood, hands jammed in the pockets of a coat two sizes too big, chin tucked, and Coral knew before she spoke that whatever she had carried down to the harbor in secret was about to be set down between them, and that once it was set down neither of them could pick it back up.

"I need to tell you something," she said. "And you have to not send me away."

"Wren—"

"You have to promise." Her voice cracked on it, and he saw that beneath the steadiness she was terrified, had been terrified for days, maybe years, and that coming here tonight had cost her more than the walk through the fog. "Because everyone says it's grief. My gran says it's grief. The physicker said it's grief, and gave me tincture of valerian that tastes like pond water, and it doesn't help because it isn't *grief*."

Coral set down the oilcloth. "Tell me."

Wren took a breath that shuddered going in and came out steady, the way breath does when the body has finally decided to spend its fear. "Tam sings," she said. "In the fog. Every time the fog comes in, I hear him singing, out past the mole, where the water goes deep. He sings the hauling song he used to sing on the boats—*low, my love, the tide is turning, low, my love, and lay her down*—and he never sang it better than he does now, and he's dead, Coral. He's been dead two years. Drowned off the Kettle Rocks when the *Merrow's Pride* went down. I heard him drown. I heard him singing while the water took him, and I thought I'd imagined it because I loved him, and then I kept hearing him, every fog, ever since, getting closer, getting clearer, and nobody hears him but me, and it isn't grief." Her eyes were huge in the lantern light, wet and blazing. "Is it?"

The fog pressed close around the lantern's little sphere of yellow. Somewhere out on the water a bell buoy tolled, once, twice, patient as a heartbeat. Coral stood very still inside himself, the way he had learned to stand still during the hunts, during the burnings, during the long silence of his god—stillness as armor, stillness as lie.

Because he knew. Of course he knew. He had known since the fourth knot, since the prickling cold in his palms, since the first day he'd watched her tie wool and seen the old grammar move through her young hands like a tide through a channel. What she described was not madness and not mourning. It was the hearing. The oldest gift of the Veil's faithful, the gift the drowned god gave to those born half in his element: the ability to hear the dead who had gone into the water, whose souls the Veil had gathered to itself in the covenant days, keeping them close, letting them sing to the living across the thin place where sea met air. His mother had had it faintly—she used to say the harbor murmured in its sleep. Coral himself had never had it; his gift ran the other way, toward the making, toward the binding and blessing. But he had grown up among hearers. He had buried hearers. He had watched the crown's lists, and *hearer* was written at the top of them in letters like gall, because a hearer could testify to what waited on the other side of the Veil, and testimony was the one thing the founders' law could not survive.

If Wren was a hearer, and she was, then she was already dead. She simply didn't know it yet. The census would find her. An informer would find her. A hunter's ear, trained by the assize to detect the particular stillness of someone listening to a voice no one else could hear, would find her in a market, in a chapel, in her grandmother's kitchen, and then there would be a wagon, and a stake, and a crowd taught to bring their children.

"Coral?" Wren's voice, small now. "You went somewhere."

He came back. He made his face do what faces in Saltcross must do: gentle, reasonable, reassuring, false.

"It's grief," he said. "A strong kind. Some people who lose someone in the water carry them so close that the mind keeps them sounding. It fades. Give it a year, maybe two, and the fog will be only fog."

He watched her take it in. Watched hope and disappointment fight behind her eyes, hope losing, because she had known—he could see that she had known—that he would say this, had perhaps come here half-hoping to be contradicted and half-knowing she wouldn't be. Adults always said it was grief. Adults always said it faded. And every adult who said it looked, while saying it, exactly the way Coral looked now: like someone holding a door shut against something on the other side.

"Two years," Wren repeated. "Tam's been dead two years, and it's gotten louder."

"Fog plays tricks. Sound carries strangely over water—"

"He knows things." The words came out of her fast now, tumbling, the dam breaking all the way. "Last month he sang where the old pilings are, off Tidewater Lane, and I followed, and there was a locket down there in the mud, Gran's locket, lost off the ferry dock when I was six, and it was *there*, exactly where his song stopped. He led me to it. Grief doesn't lead you to lockets, Coral. Grief doesn't—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped to almost nothing, swallowed by the fog. "Grief doesn't harmonize. Last week there was another voice with him. A woman. Singing rounds with him, like they do on the boats. Two voices, Coral. Dead people don't make harmony unless there's two of them."

The bell buoy tolled again out in the white dark. Coral's hands, wrapped in the shadows of his sleeves, had begun to tremble, and he stilled them the way he stilled everything, with practice and denial, and heard his own voice say the lie one more time, gentler than before, which somehow made it worse:

"It will fade, Wren. I promise you it will fade."

She looked at him for a long moment. Fog beaded on her hair like the sea had reached up to crown her. And then she nodded—slowly, obediently, the nod of a child accepting a verdict she has already decided to appeal—and turned, and walked into the white, and the fog closed behind her the way water closes over a stone.

Coral stood alone with his lantern and his lies and listened until her footsteps were gone entirely, and then listened longer, to the bell buoy, to the lap of black water against the pilings, to the enormous silence underneath everything, the silence of a god five years drowned in his own temple.

*It will fade,* he had promised. He was a charm-maker. Promises were his trade. And every promise he had ever tied into wool and cord was true, and this one, the one that mattered most, was the first he had ever made knowing it for a lie—and knowing, too, as he banked his lantern and walked home through the widow's veil, that the lie would not hold. Lies about the dead never held. The dead were patient, and the fog was coming in thicker every night, and somewhere out past the mole, in the deep cold dark where the drowned keep their songs, Tam's voice was getting louder—and it was not calling Wren to grief.

It was calling her toward the water, and the old spire that slept beneath it, and Coral fell asleep that night with his mother's salt-stained leaf under his pillow and dreamed, for the first time in five years, of doors.
