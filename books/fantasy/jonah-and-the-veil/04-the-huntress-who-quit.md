## 4. The Huntress Who Quit

The Gull's Gullet smelled of tar, spilled ale, and the particular despair of men who had spent too many years breathing salt air and calling it freedom. Sable Vane sat in the corner booth with her back to the wall—old habit, hunter's habit—and drank her fourth cup of the evening with the mechanical dedication of a woman trying to drown something that refused to float.

The cup was chipped. Everything in the Gullet was chipped: the cups, the tables, the patrons, the promises. She ran her thumb along the crack in the glaze and thought about the Halloran family's kitchen table, which had not been chipped at all. It had been scrubbed white with lye, set with bread and salted fish, and around it had sat a mother, a father, and two small children—the youngest still nursing—who were guilty of nothing except a fever-charm stitched into the hem of a curtain.

*Burn them,* Captain Ilex had said. Not *arrest.* Not *question.* The edict of ash had made the word simple. *Burn.*

She had said no. Fourteen years of service, four hundred arrests, three commendations from the Crown Marshal himself, and she had said no in front of six hunters and a weeping constable, and then she had walked out of the Halloran house into the grey Saltcross drizzle and kept walking until her feet gave out at the docks.

They'd let her go. That was the worst part. Ilex had looked at her across that clean white table with something like pity and said, "Go home, Vane. Sleep it off." As if fourteen years could be slept off. As if what she felt was a hangover rather than a fracture running through the whole architecture of herself.

The family would burn anyway. Another squad had been sent by morning. She knew it the way you know weather coming over the water.

Sable signaled for another cup. The barkeep, a one-eyed ex-whaler named Brack, poured it without comment. He never commented. That was why she drank here, or one reason; the other was that the Gullet's corner booth sat beneath a beam where someone had carved a knotwork pattern decades ago, worn smooth now, and if she tilted her head just so, in the lamplight, the carving looked almost like—

No. She put the cup down. She was not going to start seeing Veil-work in tavern beams. She was not going to start seeing anything at all.

But her hands. Her hands had been shaking since the Halloran kitchen, and no amount of ale stilled them. They shook when she held the cup. They shook when she counted coin. They shook most terribly at night, when she lay in her rented room above the chandler's shop and listened to the foghorn moaning out past the harbor chain, and they shook as if they were trying to remember some motion her mind had forbidden them to make.

Fourteen years ago, on her first burning, an old hedge-witch had cursed her—not with fire, not with any power the crown's law-stones could detect, but with words. "Your hands will remember," the woman had said, spitting blood onto the wet cobbles. "Long after your head forgets why."

Sable had laughed then. She was twenty-two and armored in certainty, and she had believed the curse was nothing but a dying woman's spite.

Her hands remembered.

* * *

It was past midnight when the smuggler came in.

Sable noticed him the way she noticed everything, even drunk, even broken—hunter's eyes didn't retire, they just started cataloguing for different reasons. Young man, maybe twenty-five, lean as a gaff hook, moving wrong. Favoring his left side. A dark stain spreading through his coat below the ribs, fresh enough to shine in the lamplight. He came in the back way, which told her he wasn't stupid, and he went straight to Brack, which told her he trusted this place, and he spoke low and fast, which told her he was desperate.

Brack glanced toward Sable's booth—one flicker of his single eye, apologetic almost—and jerked his chin toward the storeroom door. The smuggler limped after him.

And Sable Vane, former witch-hunter of the Crown Marshal's Third Company, should have done exactly what fourteen years of training demanded.

A wounded man, bleeding in a dockside tavern, asking after a healer at midnight. In the old days—in three weeks ago—that equation resolved itself neatly. Wounds like that came from knife fights with other smugglers, mostly. But sometimes they came from elsewhere. From charms gone wrong. From wardings flaring. From magic, which was outlawed, which was plague-tainted, which burned.

She finished her cup instead of moving. Then, hating herself with a thoroughness that felt almost comfortable by now, she stood up and followed.

The storeroom smelled of brine and rope. Brack had lit a single lamp, and by its light Sable saw the smuggler stretched on a barrel-top, coat open, shirt soaked red, and kneeling beside him—a figure she recognized at once, because half of Saltcross knew the charm-seller from the fish market. The quiet one. The one who sold luck-knots and fever-wards and never bargained hard and always seemed to be listening for something behind the world.

Coral Halloran—or whatever his true name was; hedge-folk traded names like counterfeit coin—had his hands pressed flat against the wound, and he was doing something with them.

Sable's breath stopped.

He wasn't stitching. He wasn't packing the wound with moss or binding it with linen, though both waited ready on a cloth beside him. His palms were flat against the torn flesh and his eyes were closed, and he was murmuring—low, rhythmic, in a cadence that rolled like tide over shingle—and between his fingers, faint as moonlight under water, a soft grey luminescence gathered. It moved like current. It breathed.

Veil-work.

Sable had seen a thousand spellcrafts burned out of a thousand screaming practitioners. She had studied the classifications: ember-craft, storm-song, root-binding, blood-reading. But the drowned god's work was different—it was rarer, older, and the manuals described it only in warnings. *Grey light. Tide-rhythm speech. Do not engage alone.*

Here it was. Here it was, glowing softly between the fingers of a market charmer, knitting shut a smuggler's wound while the god it belonged to had been silent for five years, and Sable stood in the doorway with her hand resting on a knife she no longer had any right to use, and she did not move.

The grey light sank into the wound and faded. Coral sagged, pale, sweat standing on his brow, and reached for the linen anyway—to bind it properly, cleanly, the mundane craft layered over the miracle like sacking over silver. He worked with the tenderness of long practice.

"You can come in," he said quietly, without turning. "Or you can stand there deciding. Either way, he'll live."

"I should arrest you." Her voice came out rougher than she intended. Ale and shame in equal measure.

"Yes," he agreed mildly, tying off the bandage. "You probably should."

"The penalty is burning." She stepped into the room. Brack had drifted toward the door—discreetly, deliberately—and the lamp flame bent in the draft. "You know that. You must know that. Every corner post in this city has it nailed to it in letters tall enough to read from horseback."

"I read them every morning," Coral said. He finally looked up at her, and his eyes were tired and steady and utterly without fear, which frightened her more than begging would have. "I also read the tides. Both are written by powers I don't control."

"Don't." She heard the edge enter her voice, the old hunter's blade sliding home. "Don't philosophize at me. I've burned people who philosophized."

Something flickered across his face—not fear. Recognition. "Yes," he said slowly. "I know. I've seen your face on the warrant postings. Sable Vane." He said her name without venom, which somehow made it worse. "Though I hear you're not posting warrants anymore. I hear you refused the Hallorans."

The room tilted. Sable gripped the edge of a shelf and found the wood solid, unhelpfully solid, real in a way she currently was not. "You hear a lot, for a fish-market charmer."

"I hear everything," Coral said simply. "It's part of listening to a god who's stopped speaking. You get very good at hearing other things."

Silence stretched. The smuggler on the barrel groaned and shifted, and Coral checked his bandage with gentle hands, and Sable stood there in a dockside storeroom at one o'clock in the morning, fourteen years of doctrine warring against one grey glow of light she couldn't stop seeing behind her eyes.

*Burn them,* Ilex had said. And she had said no, and they had burned anyway, and here was proof—living, breathing proof—that everything she had spent her career destroying might be the only thing in Saltcross worth keeping.

"Why?" The question tore out of her before she could dress it in hunter's formality. "Why heal him? Why risk the pyre for a smuggler you've probably never met?"

Coral considered the question seriously, the way he seemed to consider everything. "Because he was hurt," he said at last. "And because the Veil taught that no breath should end unwitnessed. Even a smuggler's. Even—" and his gaze touched her, brief and weightless, "—even a huntress's."

"My hands shake," she heard herself say, and had no idea why she was telling him this, why the truth was climbing out of her throat like something surfacing from deep water. "Since my first burning. An old woman cursed me with it. *Your hands will remember.* Fifteen years and they haven't stopped. Not once. Not ever." She stared at them, those traitorous, remembering hands. "I used to think it was guilt. Then I thought it was sickness. Now I don't know what it is."

"It's prayer," Coral said quietly.

She looked at him sharply.

"That tremor. When the body wants to make an offering and the law forbids the hands." His voice was gentle, and it was the gentleness that cut her. "I've seen it in others. People who pass our shrines and touch the stone without knowing why. People whose fingers knot cord in patterns they were never taught. The covenant runs deeper than edicts, huntress. You can burn the practitioners, but the faith is in the bone."

"The faith got us the plague," she said automatically. Doctrine, rote, dead words. They died in her mouth even as she said them.

"Did it?" Coral asked. And that was all. Just that. Just *did it?*, soft as a wave withdrawing, leaving her standing on suddenly bare sand.

She had helped bind the last free priests eleven years ago. She had believed—Ilex had believed, they had all believed, because the Marshals told them and the Marshals read from law-stones and the law-stones did not lie—that the hedge-mages' unsanctioned workings had bred the Grey Fever that emptied three districts of Saltcross. She had stood in the rain outside the Tide Chapel while its priests were taken, and she had watched an old man bless his captors with seawater, and she had reported it in her log as *defiance* rather than what it was.

Grace.

Her hands were shaking again. She shoved them under her arms.

"How much?" she said.

Coral blinked. "What?"

"For the healing. For him." She fumbled at her belt pouch with unsteady fingers. "You did work. Work gets paid. I won't owe a—" she stopped herself from saying *witch*, the word catching like a fishbone, "—a charm-maker. I pay my debts."

"You arrested people for paying debts like mine."

"And I'm asking to be one of your customers now, so either take the coin or throw me out." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she hated it, hated all of it, the shaking hands and the cracked voice and the fact that she, Sable Vane, terror of the Third Company, was standing in a smelly storeroom begging a hedge-witch to let her buy her way toward something that might possibly, someday, resemble forgiveness.

Coral studied her for a long moment. Then he took the coin—three coppers, fair price, no more—and pressed into her palm a small object.

It was a knot. Simple cord, grey-blue, tied in a pattern she almost recognized from somewhere deeper than memory. It was warm. That was the impossible thing about it—it sat in her palm like a living thing, warm as a held hand.

"What is it?"

"A calm-knot." He began gathering his tools, not looking at her, giving her the mercy of being unseen. "For the shaking. Hold it when the hands won't stop. It won't fix anything. Nothing fixes anything. But it helps you bear the bearing of it." He paused at the door. "It's not a cure, Sable Vane. It's company."

She stood holding it after he left, after the smuggler was carried out the back by Brack's silent cousins, after the lamp burned low. She stood there until the tavern went quiet, and then she opened her fist.

The trembling slowed. Not stopped—slowed, eased, like a tide finding its level. Warmth pulsed faintly in the cord, in time, she realized with a jolt, with her own heartbeat. As if the little knot were listening. As if it cared.

Sable Vane walked home through the pre-dawn fog with the charm closed in her fist and tears cutting tracks through the grime on her face, and she did not report anyone, and she did not sleep, and for the first time in fifteen years she prayed—to whom, she didn't know, to whatever was left—to let her hands learn something new to remember.

Behind her, in the fish market, the new edicts went up with the morning light: *CENSUS OF THE AFFLICTED. ALL GIFTED PERSONS TO BE DECLARED. REWARD FOR TRUE REPORTING.*

She passed one on the chandler's wall. She read it twice.

Then she went inside, packed her kit, and sat by the window with a calm-knot warming in her palm, watching the fog roll in off the harbor, waiting to find out what kind of person she was going to be.
