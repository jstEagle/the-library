## 5. What the Fog Remembers

The song came at dusk, the way it always did.

Wren was minding the charm stall while Coral counted out fever-wards for a fishwife who haggled like she was defending her last coin, and the song slid up through the cobbles and into her teeth before she even knew she was listening. Tam's voice. Not the way he'd sounded in life—no boy's voice could carry that far—but the way fog sounds when it has words in it: low, wet, patient.

*Come down to the water, little sister. Come down where the tide forgets.*

She'd learned not to flinch. Flinching made Coral look at her sideways, and Coral looking at her sideways made her feel like a jar with a crack in it, something that might spill if anyone tilted it wrong. So she kept her hands busy, passing knots and wards across the stall boards, and let the song pool in her chest like cold water finding its level.

*Come down to the water.*

"Three coppers," Coral said to the fishwife, "and I'll throw in a luck-knot for free, because you'll only come back tomorrow and argue me down to two anyway."

The fishwife laughed, gap-toothed and unrepentant, and took her wards, and the market went on around them—the slap of fish on scales, the gull-cries, the bell of the harbor watch ringing the half-hour. Saltcross smelled of brine and tar and frying onions, and underneath all of it, faint as a stain, the smell of ash from the edict-posts, where the crown had nailed its census notices and where someone had already burned one down to a black lace of paper.

Wren watched Coral smile at his customers. It was a good smile. It reached all the way to his eyes if you didn't know him well enough to see that it never quite started there. She'd been his apprentice four months now, long enough to learn the safe techniques—how to braid a ward without asking anything of any god, how to knot luck into cord with nothing but patience and good intentions—and long enough to notice that everything he taught her was a wall built around a door he never opened.

*Come down to the water,* sang her brother. *It remembers you. It remembers us both.*

That night, after Coral banked the shrine-coals behind the false wall in the back room—he thought she didn't know about the shrine; she heard him talking to it every night, low and hopeless, like a man writing letters to someone who'd moved away without leaving an address—Wren lay on her pallet and listened to the fog roll in off the harbor.

Fog came to Saltcross most nights this time of year. It came up the streets like a slow animal, filling gutters, muffling footsteps, turning lamplight into soft drowned stars. The other children were afraid of it. The grown-ups said the fog carried sickness, or smugglers, or worse. But Wren had known since Tam died that the fog was just the sea learning to speak, and the sea had been trying to tell her something for months now, and tonight the song was different.

Tonight it wasn't *come down to the water.*

Tonight it was *come now.*

---

She waited until Coral's breathing went long and even behind his curtain. She left by the window, barefoot on the cold sill, and dropped into the alley where the mud swallowed the sound of her landing. Her grandmother slept three rooms away and would sleep until morning; grief and age had made her sleep deep as a well. Wren told herself she'd be back before dawn. She told herself a lot of things on the way to the harbor, and none of them held, but she kept telling them because they were the only rope she had.

The fog was thick as wool at the docks. It ate the lamps whole so that each one hung in the air like a yolk without an egg. Ropes creaked somewhere close and invisible. Water lapped at pilings with a sound like slow applause.

And under it all, the song.

Tam had drowned two years ago, pulled under by a mooring line in a squall, and they'd found him three days later washed against the weir, and their grandmother had wailed and the neighbors had brought bread, and Wren had stood very still and realized she could still hear him singing. Everyone said grief did that. Everyone said it kindly, the way you'd pat a dog. *It will fade,* they said. *Give it time.* Even Coral had said it, gentle and certain, and Wren had nodded because nodding was easier than saying what she actually knew, which was that grief didn't sing in tune. Grief didn't harmonize with itself. Grief didn't know the words to songs Tam had made up and never sung aloud to anyone living.

She followed the harbor road east, past the fish-stalls shuttered for night, past the customs house with its new edict-board gleaming wetly in the fog, past the last of the lamp-posts where the city gave up pretending to be a city and became mudflats and broken stone. The ebb was far out. She could feel it in the way the air changed, the exposed flats breathing their cold mineral breath, acres of dark sand and weed-slick rock laid bare beneath the fog.

Her feet knew the way even when her mind didn't. That was the strange part. She'd never come this direction before—children were warned off the flats, and Wren had always been obedient in the ways that mattered least—but her feet picked between stones and pools as if she'd walked it a hundred times, and the song grew clearer with every step, and it wasn't coming from everywhere anymore.

It was coming from ahead.

*Little sister. Come and see what they buried.*

The fog thinned near the waterline, drawn thin like breath on glass, and Wren stopped at the edge of a tidal pool wide as a courtyard, and looked down.

At first she thought it was a wreck. There were wrecks all along the flats, old hulls the sea had chewed and spat, ribs of black wood sticking up like the fingers of drowned giants. But this wasn't wood. This was stone—cut stone, dressed stone, stone carved in patterns that made her eyes slide off and then snap back, unwilling. A spire, or the top of one, rising from the sand like a tooth from a gum of mud. It broke the surface maybe the height of two men, crusted with barnacle and weed, streaming water down its sides in bright threads that caught the fog-diffused moonlight.

And it was singing.

Not Tam. Or—yes, Tam too, Tam woven through it, but the song was older than Tam, older than the city, older than any voice Wren had ever heard. It came up through the soles of her feet and out of the stone itself, a vast slow note like the inside of a bell, and when she knelt and pressed her palm flat against the wet rock, the note climbed her arm and settled behind her sternum and *recognized* her.

That was the only word for it. Recognized. The way a house recognizes your key.

Images moved behind her eyes—not pictures exactly, more like weather. A hall full of people standing in seawater to their knees, holding hands. A shape vast and gentle moving beneath them, vast the way the sea is vast, gentle the way the tide is gentle, giving and taking and giving again. Voices—hundreds, thousands—saying words together that Wren couldn't hold onto, that dissolved like salt in water, except for one phrase that repeated and repeated until she thought her heart would crack along it:

*We share our breath. We share our breath. We share our breath.*

Then the vision folded shut like a hand closing, and Wren was kneeling on a freezing tidal flat with her palm on a drowned spire and tears running hot down her face, and she didn't know why she was crying except that she was homesick for a place she'd never been, grieving for a faith she'd never been allowed to have.

The fog had closed in again. Somewhere behind her, the city was a rumor of light. And Tam's voice, small now, satisfied, almost sleepy:

*Told you. Told you it was real. Now go tell the man with the knots. He knows. He's forgotten, but he knows.*

Wren sat back on her heels. The spire streamed and dripped. Far out in the dark, the sea turned over and began, slowly, patiently, to come home.

She stayed until the water licked the toes of her bare feet, and then she ran.

* * *

Coral was already awake when she climbed back through the window. She knew it the way you know a room is occupied—by the quality of the silence. He sat at the worktable with a single candle lit, and his face in that light was terrible: not angry, which she'd have known how to survive, but frightened, and his fear made her own rise up in her throat like floodwater.

"You went to the flats." His voice was quiet. Quiet was worse than shouting. "I heard the window. I've been sitting here deciding whether to come find you."

"I followed the song." There was no point lying. Lying to Coral felt like lying to a lockpick about which way the tumblers went. "Tam's song. It's been getting louder for weeks, and tonight it said *come now*, and I went."

"Wren." He said her name the way you set down something heavy. "You can't do that. You can't follow voices into the fog at night. The hunters patrol the east flats on ebb tides—they're mapping the shoreline for the census, Sable told us—"

"Sable isn't here yet. And Coral—" Her hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against her thighs. "There's a spire. Out past the last pools, where the flats drop toward the deep channel. Stone, cut stone, older than anything in Saltcross. It sings. When I touched it, I saw—"

"Stop." He was on his feet. The candle guttered with his movement, though there was no draft. "Describe it to me. Don't say *sing*. Describe it."

So she did. The carved patterns that made eyes slide and return. The note like the inside of a bell. The hall of kneeling people, the vast gentle presence, the phrase worn smooth as sea glass: *we share our breath.* She watched his face as she spoke, and what she saw there frightened her more than the fog had. Because it wasn't disbelief. Disbelief she could have argued with. This was recognition—recognition arriving like a blade, like bad news, like a debt called in.

"The drowned temple," he said. His voice had gone strange and thin. "The covenant site. My mother's grimoire spoke of it—half a page, water-stained, everyone assumed it was myth. The founding texts say the temple was destroyed. Pulled down stone by stone when the first king outlawed the gift. They built the law-stones from its rubble, they said. They said—"

"They said it so you'd stop looking," Wren said. The certainty rose in her from somewhere below thought, from the place the song lived. "But you can't destroy a thing like that. You can only bury it. And the tide comes in twice a day, Coral. Twice a day, every day, for hundreds of years. The sea's been keeping it."

He stared at her. In the candlelight his eyes were wet, and he blinked hard and turned away, gripping the edge of the worktable, and for a long moment the only sound was the fog pressing softly against the windows and the distant bell of the harbor watch.

"If you found it," he said finally, "they can find it."

"Coral—"

"No. Listen to me." He turned back, and the fear in his face had hardened into something colder and more disciplined, the face of a man building a wall brick by brick in front of a door. "You don't understand what you've done tonight. For five years I have kept my head down and my hands clean and my god locked behind a false wall, and it has worked, it has kept everyone safe, because the Veil is silent and silent things are forgotten things. And now a twelve-year-old girl has walked out of the fog having touched the one place in the world the crown cannot ever allow to exist. Do you know what the law-stones run on? Do you know what happens if anyone learns the founders' law is fed by a chained god? Every edict, every burning, every hanging—it all becomes a lie with a body count. They will drown those flats in blood before they let one person stand on that spire and remember."

Wren's throat hurt. "I saw people sharing breath with it. Holding hands in the water. That's what the covenant was, isn't it? That's what they stole."

"Yes." The word came out ragged. "Yes, that's what they stole."

"And it recognized me." She hadn't meant to say it. It came out anyway, small and blazing. "When I put my hand on the stone, it knew me. It's been sending Tam to me for months. Maybe longer. Maybe since the day he drowned. The dead aren't haunting me, Coral. They're *calling* me. All of them. The fog is full of them, everyone the crown burned or drowned or worked to death, and they've been singing in the harbor fog for years and nobody alive could hear them except me, and now I know why. Somebody has to hear them. Somebody has to be the ear."

Coral crossed the room in three strides and took her by the shoulders—not rough, but firm, the grip of a man holding onto a railing in a storm—and knelt so their faces were level, and his fear was so naked now that it looked almost like love, and perhaps it was.

"Wren. Listen to me very carefully. If the hunters find those tracks—if anyone sees where you walked tonight, if the fog lifts wrong, if one fisherman finds wet footprints leading to a spire that should not exist—then everything ends. Not just for us. For your grandmother. For Sable. For every soul in this city with a drop of the gift. You have given me the most wonderful and the most terrible knowledge I have ever held, and I need you to understand that both things are true at once."

Outside, far off across the flats, the tide was turning. Wren could feel it in her teeth, in the scar of the song behind her sternum—a slow gathering, a patient returning, the sea beginning its long walk home to the buried door.

"I understand," she said.

And then, because she was twelve and honest and could not help herself, she added: "But Coral—the tide goes out again tomorrow. Lower than tonight. Tam says it's the lowest ebb in ten years. And he says—" she faltered, because the next part still trembled in her like a struck bell. "He says the spire isn't the temple. It's the roof. And the door is open at the bottom, and something inside has been waiting five years for someone to answer."

Coral went white. The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft, as if the room itself had drawn a sharp breath.

"He says," Wren whispered, "that it knows your name."
