## 6. Betrayal in the Market

The morning of the raid began like any other, which Coral would remember afterward as the cruelest part of it—the way ordinary things refuse to warn you. The gulls were loud over the fish market, screaming their arguments about scraps. The tide came in gray and slow beneath the pilings. A woman bought three fever-wards from him and haggled him down to two coppers, though the wards cost him nothing but knot-work and a whispered word he no longer believed in.

"Keep it dry," he told her, tying the cord at her wrist. "And don't thank me out loud. Luck spooks easy."

She laughed and moved on into the crowd, and Coral stood behind his little stall of knotted cords and salt-charms and felt the familiar ache settle into his chest, the one that had lived there five years now, dull as an old scar. He had made forty luck-knots that week. Each one had taken a thread of something true—a breath held just so, a name spoken backward into the wool—and each one was a small crime under the new edicts, though no one knew it but him and the silent god he'd stopped expecting answers from.

Wren was late. She was always late, and always for the same reason: the harbor fog. He could see her from the stall's edge, down where the cobbles went dark and slick toward the water, standing in that thin morning mist with her head cocked like a bird listening for worms. Listening for him. Her dead brother sang in the fog, she'd told Coral once, in a voice so small he'd wanted to lie to her forever.

*It will fade,* he had said. *Grief does that.*

He had known even as the words left his mouth that they were false, and that someday she would know it too, and that knowing would be his fault.

"Coral!" She came pelting up the row now, thirteen years old and all elbows, her braid coming loose, cheeks pink from the cold. "You should have seen it—there was a seal on the steps this morning, right by the fish-cleaning tables, and old Marta said it was an omen and Brannick said it was just a seal—"

"Just a seal," Coral agreed, and handed her the basket of knots to sort. "Omens are cheaper than seals anyway."

She grinned at him, gap-toothed still, and set to work with the fierce concentration she brought to everything, separating the fever-wards from the luck-knots by touch alone because she'd decided looking was for amateurs. Coral watched her a moment longer than he needed to. Then he let himself look away, because watching Wren had become a kind of prayer, and prayers, in Saltcross, were dangerous things to be caught at.

That was when he saw the posters going up.

A man in crown livery was pasting them to the market post at the far end of the row, smoothing each sheet with a flat wooden paddle, unhurried, thorough. Coral had seen edicts before—there had been edicts every season since the census was announced, each one more precise about what the crown meant to take. But this one was printed on ash-gray paper, and ash-gray paper meant urgency, and urgency meant coin.

He crossed the row without hurrying, without meeting anyone's eyes, and read:

*By order of the Crown Wardens: any citizen harboring knowledge of an afflicted person is bound by law to report such knowledge at the nearest warden-post. Reward: five crowns silver. Failure to report is itself affliction.*

Five crowns. Enough to eat for a month. Enough to buy a grandmother out of the census wagons' path, if a girl were desperate enough and frightened enough—and everyone in Saltcross was both, these days.

Coral walked back to his stall slowly, doing sums he didn't want to do. He thought of the neighbors who bought his charms. He thought of how many of them knew—or half-knew, or suspected—that the hedge-charmer's knots worked better than knots had any right to work. He thought of Wren sorting cords by touch, humming.

"We should close early today," he said.

Wren looked up. "Why? It's not even noon."

"The weather." It wasn't a lie exactly; there was weather coming. He could feel it in his teeth the way he'd felt it since he was a boy, since his mother first put a salt-stained grimoire leaf in his hand and told him the sea remembers everything we'd rather forget. "Storm off the water. You can feel it too."

"I can't feel anything," Wren said, with the flat honesty that was her worst quality and the one he loved most. "Except the fog stuff. And you always say that's grief."

"It is grief," Coral said, and heard how tired the words sounded, how worn, like a coin passed through too many hands. "Sort your knots."

But the storm didn't come off the water. It came up the hill road, in gray cloaks and iron-shod boots, six of them moving through the market gates in a wedge, and the sound they made was worse than thunder because it was quiet—the particular quiet of people parting crowds who already knew to part. Somewhere a woman began gathering her goods with shaking hands. Somewhere a child started crying and was hushed too fast.

Coral's hands went still over the luck-knots.

They weren't here for a general sweep. General sweeps came loudly, with drums and proclamations, theater for the crowds. These six moved like men with an address written on a slip of paper in the lead hunter's glove. They moved like men following a tip.

*Anonymous*, Coral thought, absurdly, uselessly. *Whoever it was, they'll get their five crowns.*

The lead hunter stopped four stalls down and turned a slow circle, reading faces the way Sable Vane had once taught hunters to read faces—Coral had heard the stories, everyone had, back when Sable Vane was a name instead of a warning. This one was young, though. Young and careful, with a scar through one eyebrow, and he held a folded paper in his hand and consulted it between glances at the crowd.

Then his eyes found Coral's stall. Found Wren.

Found Wren, and stayed.

"No," Coral breathed, though no one heard him, though it wasn't a word meant for anyone but himself and whatever god might still be listening beneath the harbor. "No, no—not her—"

Because he understood then, with the terrible clarity that comes only after it's too late to matter, what the tip had been. Not a description of a charm-seller. Not a report of hedge-magic. A description of a *girl*. A girl who stood in fog and listened to the dead. A girl whose name someone had traded for five crowns silver and a grandmother's safety, or maybe just for the five crowns, or maybe out of fear, which spends the same as greed in the end.

The young hunter started toward them.

"Wren." Coral's voice came out steady, which surprised him; inside, everything was falling. "Take the basket. Go home by the tanner's lane, not the hill road. Don't run. Walk."

"What? Why—"

"*Walk.*"

She saw his face then. Whatever she found in it made her go pale and gather the basket with clumsy hands, and she turned, and she walked, fast, toward the mouth of the row where the crowd was thickest—and the young hunter changed direction to follow her, casual as a shopper, hand resting near the long knife at his belt. Not the sword. Hunters used the long knife for afflicted, close work, quiet work. Everyone in Saltcross knew what the long knife was for.

Coral did not think. That was the truth he would carry afterward, the thing he would turn over in the dark in smugglers' cellars and drowned temples and all the days that came after: *I did not think.* Five years of hiding, five years of small safe godless knots, five years of talking to a silent god in a hidden shrine—and in the moment it mattered, thought had nothing to do with it.

The stall beside Wren was old Brannick's, and Brannick's awning-frame had been rotting since spring. Coral put his palm against the crossbeam and spoke the word his mother had died rather than surrender.

Not a whisper this time. Not a knot, not a trick, not a hedge-charm. The full word, open-handed, the way it was meant to be spoken, and the power came up through him like a tide answering the moon—five years of dammed river bursting its banks—and the rotten frame *moved*, groaning, swinging wide, and the whole stall came down across the row between Wren and the hunter in a crash of canvas and splintering wood that scattered the crowd screaming.

Under the cover of it, Coral seized Wren's arm.

"Down the fish-gutters' stairs," he said. "Now. Don't look back at me. Whatever you hear, don't look back."

"But you—your hands—they're *glowing*—" Her eyes were huge, fixed on his fingers where the light of the working still crawled, blue-white as foxfire, unmistakable, undeniable, the mark of the afflicted written plain for every eye in the market to see.

"Yes," Coral said. "That's done now. Run."

She ran. And Coral turned to face the hunters, because someone had to hold the door, and he was the door.

The crowd had become a wall of backs and screams, and through it the hunters came, the young one first, knife already drawn, face gone from careful to cold. "Afflicted," he called out, and the word carried the way a name carries, the way a sentence carries. "In the crown's name—"

"In the Veil's name," Coral answered, and lifted his hands, and let them see the light.

He had imagined this moment for five years, usually at three in the morning, usually with sweat soaking the pallet. In the imaginings he always broke. He always begged, or ran, or denied his mother and his god and the salt-stained leaf. What he had never imagined was the calm. It filled him like the tide fills a channel, and he understood that this, too, was the Veil—not the silence, not the absence, but this: the stillness of deep water before the storm crosses it.

The first hunter rushed him. Coral spoke the word for *weight*, and the man dropped as if the sky had settled on his shoulders, gasping into the cobbles. The second circled wide, clever, and Coral's counter-charm caught him mid-stride and turned his boots to anchors in the mud. But there were six, and Coral had spent five years rationing himself like a miser, and the power came slower now, each working costing more than it should have, his vision graying at the edges.

A thrown net took him across the shoulders. Weighted hooks bit through his coat. He went down hard, and the cobbles were cold and smelled of fish blood and old rain, and above him the young hunter raised the long knife with the paper—the tip, the five crowns' worth of betrayal—still clutched forgotten in his other fist.

"Any last words, afflicted?"

Coral looked past the blade, past the gray cloak, to the mouth of the row where Wren had vanished, and found that yes, he had last words after all, and they were not for the hunter.

"She sings," he said. "Her brother sings to her in the fog, and someday the whole city will know why that matters, and you will wish you had asked me more questions."

The hunter frowned. "What—"

The awning of the sailmaker's shop, directly overhead, chose that moment to come down.

It did not come down on its own. Coral felt the working leave him like blood leaving a wound—he had nothing left, truly nothing, and so he took the last of himself, the final hoarded coin of five years' hiding, and gave it all at once. Canvas and spars and coiled rope buried the hunter and his companions in a white avalanche, and Coral rolled clear on pure animal instinct, and then a hand closed on his collar and hauled him upright and a voice he knew said, close to his ear:

"You absolute *idiot*."

Sable Vane. Disgraced huntress, dockside drunk, buyer of one fever-ward she'd never explained. She had a knife in her free hand and a hood up and the eyes of a woman doing arithmetic she hated.

"Can you walk?" she demanded.

"Barely."

"Good enough." She cut the net from his shoulders with two strokes. "Cellar under the rope-walk. Move *now*, before the sixth one stops being unconscious."

"The girl—"

"Is ahead of us, and smarter than both of us. *Move.*"

They went through the fish-gutters' gate, down stairs slick with scale and brine, into the warren beneath the market where the city kept its oldest secrets: the sewers, brick-throated and black, running with the tide's leavings, stinking of everything Saltcross preferred to forget. Sable moved through it like a woman reading a book she'd memorized in another life, calling turns before they came, her lantern shuttered to a slit.

Behind them, muffled by stone and distance, horns began to sound. The alarm. More hunters. Dogs, maybe. Sable cursed with the fluency of long practice and pulled him down a side-channel where the ceiling dripped and the water ran shin-deep and cold.

"They'll sweep every grate by nightfall," she said. "We need to be three districts away and underground the whole time. Can you do another working?"

"No." His voice cracked on it. "I'm empty. Worse than empty. I took out loans I can't repay."

"Then we do it the old way." She glanced back at him, and in the lantern's slit of light her face was grim and older than her years. "Walking. Hiding. Staying alive until we're somewhere they can't follow."

"Why?" The question tore out of him before he could stop it. "You're a huntress. Were. You could turn me in right now and buy back your name with interest. Five crowns for the tip, a fortune for the catch. Why, Sable?"

For a long moment she said nothing, just waded on through the dark, and the water talked around their legs, and somewhere far off a rat moved through the dark like a rumor.

"Because I've burned people," she said finally, not turning. "Because I stood in circles with torches and told myself the law was the light. Because last month I watched a family burn and couldn't make my hands move, and I've been trying to make my hands move ever since." She stopped at a junction, checked marks on the bricks that Coral couldn't read, and turned left. "Your ward worked, charmer. The one I bought. First night I slept without dreaming of smoke. So either you're a fraud, or everything I burned was real, and I have to know which before I die."

"That's a terrible reason to save someone."

"It's the only honest one I've got."

Ahead, faint but unmistakable, came a sound that stopped them both: singing. Thin, wavering, echoing down the sewer channels—a child's voice singing a harbor song, the kind fishermen's wives sang to call boats home, except the words were wrong, older, in no language Coral knew, and yet he understood them anyway, the way you understand weeping.

Wren. Sitting on a dry ledge at the channel's edge with the basket hugged to her chest, singing into the dark as if she expected an answer.

And the horrible, wonderful thing—the thing that made the hair rise on Coral's arms—was that the dark seemed to be considering one.

She saw them and scrambled up, and for one heartbeat she looked at Coral's still-faintly-lit hands, at the ruin of the life they'd both known, and then she threw herself at him and held on with all thirteen years of her strength.

"You came back," she said into his coat. "You told me not to look back and I didn't, and you *came back*."

"I came back," Coral said, and found his eyes were burning, and let them.

Sable stood apart, lantern raised, watching the channel mouths with a huntress's patience. When she spoke, her voice was flat and practical and utterly at odds with the softness of the moment.

"We can't stay. By dawn they'll have dogs down every access grate in the fish district. There's a cellar under the rope-walk—a smuggler's, sealed since the embargo—dry, hidden, one entrance. We can reach it before the tide turns if we stop talking." She looked at Coral, and then at Wren, and something in her jaw tightened, some decision being made that she clearly resented. "Both of you. If you're coming, come."

Wren took Coral's hand. Her fingers were cold and salt-sticky and trembling, and she held on like the tide itself might separate them.

"Coral," she said quietly, as they waded into the dark after Sable's shuttered light. "When your hands were glowing. In the market. It was beautiful."

He almost laughed. Almost wept. "It was the end of hiding, little fish. That's all it was."

"No," Wren said, with that flat honesty of hers, the one he loved most and feared most, the one that heard dead brothers sing in fog. "It was the beginning of something. I heard it."

"Heard what?"

"The god." She squeezed his hand. "Under the water. When you said the Veil's name out loud—it *moved*, Coral. Down there. Under the harbor. Like something turning over in its sleep."

Coral stopped walking. The sewer water swirled cold around his knees. Far above, muffled through fathoms of brick and stone and the whole weight of the law-bound city, the horns were still sounding—but beneath them, beneath everything, so faint he might have invented it, so faint he would spend the rest of his life unsure, he thought he heard it too.

A long, slow exhalation. The sigh of something imprisoned, hearing, for the first time in five years, its own name spoken in the open air.

"Come on," Sable called back, impatient, afraid, brave. "Tide's turning."

They went.
