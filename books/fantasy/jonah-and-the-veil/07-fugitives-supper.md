## 7. Fugitives' Supper

The cellar smelled of brine and old rope and the particular sweetness of apples rotting in the dark—smugglers' stock, abandoned when the tide of the law turned against them. Sable Vane had known a hundred such cellars in her years hunting the gifted: places where contraband charms were stashed between the kegs, where fugitives crouched with their breath held while her boots crossed the floorboards overhead. She had never once been on the underside of those boards before tonight.

It fit her like an old coat that had been let out wrong.

She sat with her back to the cold stone wall, one knee drawn up, watching the two of them by the light of a single tallow stub she'd found wedged in a niche. Coral Halloran sat cross-legged near the stairs, his hands moving over the loot they'd carried down from the market raid's chaos—a wheel of hard cheese gone waxy at the rind, half a loaf black with rye, three apples, a jar of pickled herring sealed under cracked wax, and a jug of small beer that sloshed hopefully when shaken. Wren knelt opposite him, arranging their stolen feast on a flour sack with the solemnity of a girl setting a shrine.

They had not spoken since the sewer grate clanged shut behind them. An hour of silence, maybe more. The harbor fog pressed against the high little window like something listening.

Sable broke the seal on the beer first. It seemed as good a way to begin as any, and she was tired of beginning nothing.

"Ration it," she said, pouring into a tin cup that smelled faintly of lamp oil. "We don't know how long we're down here."

Coral took the cup without meeting her eyes. He drank, passed it to Wren, who drank like someone remembering she was thirsty only after the cup touched her lips. Then he tore the loaf—not evenly, Sable noticed; he gave the larger piece to the girl—and began to eat with the mechanical patience of a man feeding a body he'd forgotten he owned.

The candle guttered. Somewhere above, water dripped off the eaves of the fishmongers' row, keeping slow time.

"You knew," Sable said finally. Not a question. She'd been turning it over all through the dark miles of the sewers, the way you turn a coin you suspect is clipped. "In the market. When the stall came down—you moved before it fell. You were already reaching for her." She nodded at Wren. "You knew where the hunters would strike."

"I've watched raids for five years," Coral said. His voice was flat, scraped clean of anything. "You learn where they put their blades."

"That's not what I mean, and you know it isn't."

He set down his bread. In the candlelight his face was all hollows and salt-gray stubble, a face built for being overlooked, which was perhaps why he'd survived so long in Saltcross. But his eyes—his eyes had been open in the market in a way Sable recognized from the other side of a hundred arrests. The look of a man who has decided hiding costs more than burning.

"They had a tip," he said. "A specific one. They went straight for my stall. Straight for *her*." A flick of his chin toward Wren. "Someone told them where to find a charm-maker and his apprentice. Someone who knew us both."

The cellar went very quiet. Even the drip above seemed to hold its breath.

Wren's hands stopped moving over the apples. She was thirteen, maybe fourteen—all elbows and chapped knuckles, hair the color of wet kelp cut short around her ears. Sable had seen a thousand children like her in the census lines, in the holding wagons, in the ash-queues outside the burn-yards. Children learned early in Saltcross that fear made people do arithmetic: one life weighed against another, grandmother against charm-maker, and the scales always tipped toward whoever was closest to your own bed.

"I did it," Wren said.

Her voice didn't crack. That was the worst part, Sable thought later. It didn't crack at all. It came out level and small, like a stone dropped down a well, and they all heard it hit bottom.

"The day after you burned your mother's book, sir—I mean, Coral. I followed you home. I saw where you kept the leaf you saved." Her fingers found each other in her lap and gripped. "Two men came to our room. Not uniformed. They said they knew about my brother. They said—" She swallowed. "They said dead boys don't stay quiet unless someone helps them along. They knew Gran's name. They knew she takes the census rolls for the parish, and they said if I didn't tell them what you were, they'd write her name in the afflicted column instead of mine. They said a child's affliction could be... corrected. Reeducation. But an old woman who keeps books for the parish, an old woman who *knew* and hid it—" Her breath shuddered once and steadied. "They were very clear about what happens to old women who hide things."

The candle flame leaned as if in wind, though there was none.

"So I told them," Wren finished. "I told them you were Veil-sworn. I told them about the knots you sold that weren't just knots. I told them everything I knew, and then I went home and Gran asked me why I looked like I'd been crying and I said it was the fog." She lifted her chin, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'd do it again. I'd do it again tomorrow, if it was her or you, and I hate that I'd do it, but I would, and you should know that about me before you decide anything else."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

Sable watched Coral. She had spent eleven years reading faces across interrogation tables—the flinchers, the freezers, the ones who went soft with pity and the ones who went hard with contempt. Coral did none of these. What moved across his face was slower and sadder than any of it: grief arriving late, finding its seat, settling in.

"Your brother," he said quietly. "Tam. He sang in the fog."

"Yes."

"And you hear him still."

"Yes."

Something in Coral's jaw worked. "I told you that would fade."

"I know you lied," Wren said. "I've known since the second week. Dead things don't fade, sir. They just get quieter until somebody listens."

Coral closed his eyes. When he opened them again, he looked at the girl for a long moment—and then away, out at the dark wall, and Sable understood that he was not able to look at her yet. That looking cost too much. Five years of careful, cramped, terrified living, undone in an afternoon; a stall smashed, a name spoken aloud in a crowded market, magic flaring from his hands like a struck match for the first time since she'd been a hunter fresh enough to still believe the crown's catechisms. All of it because a child had done the arithmetic every child in this city was forced to do.

He picked up his bread again. Tore it. Did not eat it.

"I need you to understand something," he said, and his voice was low and even and worse than shouting. "I took you on knowing what you were. Gifted. I taught you the safe craft anyway, the godless craft, because I thought—if I can keep one of them hidden, one of them whole, then these five years weren't just cowardice. They were worth something." A breath. "And the first time it mattered, the first time the choice came, you sold me for your grandmother."

"Coral—" Sable started.

"No." He raised a hand, not angry, just final, like a door closing gently. "Let me finish. I'm not saying I wouldn't have done the same. Maybe I would have. My mother—" His voice caught on the word and he pushed past it. "My mother died in a wagon like the ones they'll put your gran in, and I ran, and I have hated myself for running every day since, so I know exactly what that arithmetic feels like. I know it in my teeth." He finally looked at Wren then, and Sable saw that his eyes were bright and wet and furious. "But knowing doesn't make it hurt less. You were mine to protect. I chose you. And you chose her, and she's worth choosing, and I am *still* sitting here with a hunter's brand on my name because of it, and I don't—" He stopped. Started again, softer. "I don't forgive you yet. I want to be honest about that, because everyone in this city lies to everyone else to be kind, and it's rotted us all from the inside. I don't forgive you. Maybe I will. Right now I'm just tired, and hungry, and I'd like to eat this bread before I decide what I feel."

Wren nodded. Two tears slid down her face, quick and businesslike, and she wiped them with her sleeve and reached for an apple as though nothing had happened, which was its own kind of bravery, Sable thought. Or its own kind of wound. At thirteen they were often the same thing.

* * *

They ate. It was not a comfortable meal, but it was a meal, and hunger is honest company—it makes even enemies pass the cup.

Sable carved the cheese with her belt knife, cutting wedges with the precision of a woman who had once rationed supplies on three-week warrants in the marsh country. The pickled herring was fierce with vinegar and dill, better than it had any right to be, and Wren ate hers with her eyes closed in something close to bliss, and for a few minutes the cellar was only a cellar and they were only three people eating in the dark.

It was Sable who spoke next, because someone had to, and she had spent enough of her life letting silence do the crown's work.

"When I was nineteen," she said, "I had a partner named Doss. Good man. Dull as ditchwater, loyal as a dog. We pulled a family out of a fen cottage one winter—mother, two sons, all hedge-gifted, nothing dangerous, charms for calving and weather-sense, that sort of thing." She turned her cup slowly on her knee. "Protocol said separate them for questioning. Doss said protocol. I said we'd question them together, one room, save the wagon-space. So we did. And while we had them all in one place, the younger boy panicked, and there was a scuffle, and the mother stepped between Doss's blade and her son."

The candle flame stood very still, as if it too were listening.

"She didn't die," Sable said. "That would have been cleaner. She lived, and the trial took four days, and I testified to what I saw, and the crown's advocate used my testimony to hang all three of them, because I confirmed the gift was real. My word was good. My word was always good. That was the whole reason they'd made me a hunter at nineteen—because I didn't lie." She laughed, short and airless. "Eleven years, and I never lied once. I just kept believing the lie they'd already told me. That the gift caused the plague-years. That burning it out of the blood was mercy. That every family I broke was a fire I was putting out."

"You quit," Coral said. It wasn't quite a question either.

"Last month. They ordered me to burn a weaver and her daughters in Millrow. The girls were eight and eleven." Sable looked at her own hands, broad and scarred, steady now, always steady, that was the joke of it—they'd only ever shaken when she was drunk enough to stop pretending. "I put my warrant on the table and walked out. And I've been drinking in the Gull's Rest ever since, telling myself I was the only honest woman left in Saltcross." She looked up, and met Coral's eyes, and held them. "Then a smuggler bled out on the tavern floor and the famous luck-knot man stitched him with thread that glowed like deep water, and I sat there knowing exactly what I was looking at, and I had one job left in me—one instinct left—and it wasn't to report you. It was to buy a fever-ward off you like some dockside widow. Because I wanted to carry a piece of the thing I'd spent my life breaking. Does that make sense? It barely makes sense to me."

"It makes sense," Coral said.

"Does it?" She set down the cup. "Because here's what I actually think, Coral Halloran, and you can throw the herring jar at me if you like. I think you're sitting over there deciding whether that girl betrayed you or saved you, and the truth is she did both, and that's the only kind of loyalty anyone gets in this city. Nobody comes to anybody clean. Not her, not me, not you with your five years of watching neighbors get dragged past your window while you sold luck-knots and kept your head down. Family isn't earned clean. It's earned the way a road through marsh is earned—bad ground, both feet sinking, everybody hauling on everybody else. If you're waiting for her to be worthy of your forgiveness before you give it, you'll wait until the tide stops."

"Sable." His voice had an edge now. "You've known her a day."

"I've known *people* eleven years longer than that. I've read more confessions than you've tied knots, and I'll tell you what I know about them: nobody confesses like that unless they mean to carry it. She didn't excuse herself. She didn't cry her way out of it. She told you she'd do it again—that's not a plea, that's a map of who she is, handed over so you can decide with your eyes open." Sable leaned forward. "That's worth more than clean, Coral. Clean is cheap. Anyone can be clean if they've never been afraid."

Wren had gone very still, apple forgotten in her lap, watching them trade over her head like a coin.

Coral was silent a long time. The drip above counted out the seconds. When he finally spoke, it was to the girl, and his voice had lost its edge and gained something wearier and more dangerous: honesty.

"I'm not going to pretend tonight," he said. "I can't forgive you yet. My hands still shake when I think about the market—about what would have happened if I'd been a heartbeat slower. But." He exhaled. "But you came back. After you informed, you came back to my stall the next morning and swept up glass without being asked. I kept asking myself why. Now I know. You were trying to stand in the doorway of the thing you'd broken." He picked up the tin cup and poured the last of the beer and held it out to her. "Drink. And tomorrow we figure out how to get your grandmother out of this city before the census wagons roll. Because if I'm going to be burned for a betrayal, I'd rather it be for undoing one."

Wren took the cup with both hands, the way you take something holy, and drank, and her chin crumpled and reformed twice before she mastered it.

"There's a thing," she said, when she could speak. "I haven't told you. About the fog."

* * *

Later—much later, when the candle had burned to a thumb's width and Wren slept curled in her cloak against the flour sacks, breathing the deep uneven breaths of a child who has cried herself empty—Sable sat beside Coral at the foot of the stairs, sharing the last apple in slices, passing the knife back and forth.

"She'll sleep till midday," Sable murmured.

"Let her." Coral turned his slice of apple over in his fingers without eating it. "What she said. About the fog. About following Tam's song at low tide and seeing a spire break the water out past the mole." His voice dropped further. "There's no spire on any chart I've ever seen. No temple, no ruin marked. But the old prayers—the ones my mother whispered, the ones that got her killed—half of them are addressed to 'the house beneath the harbor.' I always thought it was poetry." He looked at Sable, and in the dying candlelight his face was stripped of everything except the question underneath. "If the drowned temple is real, and a gifted child can find it by following her dead—then the hunters can find it too. Or worse, the men who wrote the edicts already know it's there. Already know what's under this city. And everything they've done for two hundred years—the laws, the burnings, the census—everything might be built on top of it like a lid on a pot."

Sable chewed her apple slowly. Eleven years of warrants and route-maps and sealed orders moved behind her eyes, little anomalies she'd never been cleared to question: the survey teams that mapped the harbor floor and burned their own charts; the law-stones in the magistrate's court that hummed on windless days; the standing order, older than any living officer, that no vessel anchor beyond the mole at ebb-tide.

"I know a man," she said at last, "who owes me his life and hates it. He runs charts out of the Gull's Rest. Restricted ones." She held out the knife, handle-first, the way you offer a hand. "Tomorrow, while the girl sleeps, you and I are going to find out what the crown has been sitting on. And then we're going to decide whether we run from it—" she smiled, and it was the first smile in months that didn't taste like drink, "—or whether we go down and knock."
