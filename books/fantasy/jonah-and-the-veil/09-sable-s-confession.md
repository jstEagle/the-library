## 9. Sable's Confession

The smuggler's cellar smelled of brine and old rope and the sweet rot of apples gone soft in their barrels, and Sable Vane had not slept in it so much as lain awake inside her own skull, listening to the tide breathe through the walls.

Three nights now since the market. Three nights since she had pulled a hedge-charmer and a child through the sewer grate with hands that would not stop trembling, and had told herself it was the cold, the exertion, the years of drink sitting in her blood like silt. She knew better. Her hands had been shaking for eleven years, ever since Greymouth, ever since the pyres, and no amount of ale or arrack or raw harbor spirit had ever once quieted them. The drink was just a way of not watching them shake.

Above her, muffled by floorboards, Coral moved through his small rituals. She could hear him teaching Wren the safe craft—the knot-work, the salt-measures, the little godless sleights that any cunning woman might sell in any market without fear of the stake. His voice was low and patient. Wren's voice answered, quick and hungry. Between them, like a third presence, the silence of the drowned god sat in the cellar and pressed on Sable's chest.

She had killed people for that silence. That was the truth she had carried up from Greymouth like a splinter working toward her heart, and it had finally worked its way through, and there was no pulling it out now except to speak it.

She climbed the stairs.

* * *

They had made a home of the smuggler's loft in the way that fugitives do—out of blankets and crates and the stubborn animal need to be near warmth. Wren slept curled against a barrel with her brother's song still sometimes moving her lips. Coral sat by the single tallow candle, the saved grimoire leaf flattened between two boards of glass he'd scavenged from a broken lantern, studying it the way a man studies a letter from someone who died angry at him.

Sable lowered herself onto the crate across from him. The candle guttered in the draft off the stairwell. He looked up, and his eyes were tired and kind, and that kindness was the worst part, because she had seen that same quality of mercy in the faces of the priests they dragged to the wagons, and mercy was the thing she had least deserved and most remembered.

"I can't sleep," she said.

"Neither can I." He set the leaf down carefully, as if it were a sleeping thing. "The god keeps poor company."

"You talk to it." It wasn't an accusation. She'd heard him, some nights, murmuring into the dark the way sailors murmur to saints they aren't sure are listening. "Five years of nothing, and you still talk to it."

"It's the only conversation I've ever been sure is real."

Sable turned her hands over in her lap. In the candlelight the tremor was plain—a fine, ceaseless shivering, like water remembering a stone dropped into it long ago. Coral watched her hands. He didn't say anything. He was good at not saying things; she had learned that about him in the weeks since the tavern, since she'd bought a fever-ward from him with coin she should have used to report him, and begun this slow walk away from everything she had been.

"When I was nineteen," she said, "I could hold a bow steady enough to put an arrow through a sparrow's eye at forty paces."

Coral said nothing. Waiting. People were always waiting on her, back when waiting on her meant something terrible.

"The plague came to Greymouth the winter I turned twenty. You'd have been—what, a boy in Saltcross, learning knots from your mother. You wouldn't have seen what it did. It didn't kill fast. It unmade people. A fisherman would take to his bed with a cough and by the week's end he'd be talking to the walls, saying words nobody knew, drawing spirals on the plaster until his fingers bled. Whole streets of it. And the crown sent criers through every town: *the gift is the plague. Magic rots the mind. Report the afflicted and be spared.*"

"We heard the criers here too," Coral said quietly. "My mother nailed our shutters closed and burned tallow for a month."

"They were lying." Sable heard her own voice go flat, the way it went flat when she gave orders, back when giving orders was her whole self. "Or rather—they were telling a lie built on top of something true, which is the only kind of lie that ever works. The gift doesn't rot the mind. But grief does. Terror does. When the plague came, the gifted felt it coming through their gifts before anyone else did—the Veil-touched felt the dead gathering, the charm-makers felt the luck curdle—and they went mad with foreknowledge, some of them, trying to warn, trying to ward, and the crown looked at a city full of screaming prophets and called it contagion." She stopped. Swallowed. "And I believed them. Gods help me, Coral, I believed them with my whole heart, because believing them meant the world was simple and my work was holy."

The candle flame leaned. Somewhere above, gulls argued over the night harbor.

"I was good," she went on, because stopping now would be a mercy to herself and she had forfeited mercies. "Not competent—*good*. Captain Ilex and I, we were the pair they sent when a town couldn't manage its own burning. I knew every rite in the Protocol. I knew how to read a person's gift out of their shadow, how to bind a tongue so a witch couldn't curse us with her last breath, how to build a pyre so it burned clean, without smell." Her hands shook harder. She let them. "Greymouth was the last free priory in the north. Not hedge-folk, not market charmers—an actual priory, with bells, with a ledger of oaths going back six hundred years. They'd kept the old rites alive under license, the way you keep a fire banked in a hearth you're not supposed to have. The crown decided licensed faith was still faith. Ilex drew the warrant. I carried the chains."

"And you bound them." Coral's voice was very soft. "The priests."

"Fourteen of them. I bound nine myself." She met his eyes because looking away would have been the coward's portion and she had eaten enough of that. "There was an old woman, the prioress. Mother Osla. When I put the binding-cord on her wrists she didn't struggle. She looked at my hands and she said—" and here, eleven years later, in a smuggler's loft over a drowned temple, Sable Vane's voice finally broke, "—she said, *child, whose breath is holding yours?*"

Silence. The tide breathed. Wren stirred in her sleep and murmured a fragment of melody.

"Afterward," Sable said, steadier, because the worst was out and the rest was only geography, "my hands started shaking. Not that night. Three days after, when we were back in the garrison and I was cleaning my kit, and I picked up my whetstone and it chattered against the blade like a tooth. I thought it was nerves. Nerves pass. This didn't. Eleven years, Coral. Every morning I wake up and my hands are already going, like they're trying to tell me something my head won't say. So I drink, because drunk hands shake slower, and everyone in the garrison assumed Sable Vane was just another soldier rotting herself, and I let them assume it, because the truth was worse. The truth was that my body had been keeping a ledger my mind refused to open." She laughed, short and ugly. "You know what the healers call it? Tremor of unresolved oath. There's a name for it in the hunter's manual. We wrote the manual. We knew exactly what we were doing to ourselves."

Coral reached across the space between them—not touching, but near, his scarred charm-maker's hand hovering over her shaking ones like warmth offered without claim. "Why tell me this now?"

"Because of the leaf." She nodded at the grimoire page under glass. "Because you've been reading it for three nights and getting paler, and I know what's in it, or the shape of it, because I helped write the other version. The founders' law isn't just law, is it. It's a siphon. The law-stones in the magistrate halls, the oath-stones every official swears on—they're not inert. They run on something. And the something has been running out for five years, which is why your god went silent, which is why the edicts got crueler as the stones got hungrier. Am I close?"

He stared at her. "How could you possibly know that?"

"Because I've stood in the vaults under the Citadel where the first law-stone sits, the Founder's Stone, and I've seen what feeds it." Her mouth tasted of ash. "It's not a stone, Coral. It's a heart. Or it was one. And every witch we burned, every priest we chained, every shrine we smashed—we weren't stamping out magic. We were clearing the field around a prisoner. Cutting the god off from everyone who might hear it crying out."

Wren sat up in the dark. Sable hadn't heard her wake, but there she was, eyes huge in the candlelight, grandmother's ring on its cord glinting at her throat. Neither of them told her to go back to sleep. Some things a child should hear, if the child already carries the dead in her ears.

"So the plague," Wren whispered. "The plague that started all of it—"

"Was real," Sable said. "Grief and terror and a sickness that preyed on minds already stretched thin by listening. But it wasn't the gift's doing. The crown needed it to be, because the founders' covenant—theirs, the false one, the one written in chains—only holds while the true one is forgotten. You can't steal a god's breath and leave witnesses who remember the god was free." She looked back at Coral. "I helped make the forgetting. Nine priests. Fourteen, counting the ones Ilex handled. And a hundred hedge-witches besides, over eleven years, whose names I wrote in warrants with these hands." She held them up, trembling, offering them like evidence at a trial. "So. That's my confession. Judge me however you need to. But hear the rest first, because judgment can wait and the hunters can't."

Coral's face had gone through shock and grief and arrived somewhere she recognized from her own mirror: the place where anger and understanding sit down at the same table and refuse to leave.

"Say the rest," he said.

"I know the Protocol," Sable said. "All of it. The binding rites, the counter-songs, the failsafes the founders built into their theft in case the god ever strained its chains. I know the hunter routes along the coast, the watch rotations, the signal-codes, the tide-tables the patrols use. I know which captains will follow a bad order and which will hesitate, and I know—Ilex." The name cost her. "If you're going to the drowned temple—if that leaf says what I think it says—you'll be walking into the one place the crown cannot afford to let you reach, guarded by rites I helped perfect. You need someone who knows where the locks are." She took a breath. "I'm offering you the traitor's knowledge, Coral. Use it. Hate me while you use it. Both are fine. But don't leave it behind, because it's the sharpest thing I own."

For a long moment Coral didn't answer. He picked up the grimoire leaf instead, tilting it to the candlelight, and Sable saw the salt-stained ink catch fire—lines of old script, and beneath them, half worn away, a diagram she recognized with a lurch: the seven-pointed seal of the original covenant, the one from the priory murals at Greymouth, the one the crown had spent a century teaching people to see as heresy.

"My mother wrote this," Coral said. "Before the silence. She wrote that the Veil's voice was being *carried away*, not lost. I thought it was a grieving woman's fancy." He looked up. "You're telling me she was right."

"I'm telling you the founders built their kingdom on a stolen heartbeat," Sable said, "and I spent eleven years guarding the thief's door."

Wren had come closer without either of them noticing, the way children and cats move through candlelight. She knelt beside Coral and put her small hand flat on the glass over the leaf, over the seven-pointed star.

"My brother sings," she said softly. "Down in the fog. He's been singing louder since the market. Since you did magic in the open, Coral." She looked between them, solemn as a tiny priestess. "Maybe the dead know the chains broke a little. Maybe that's why they're singing."

Sable felt the hair rise on her arms. Beside her, Coral had gone very still, the stillness of a man hearing a door open somewhere in a house he thought empty.

"Then we'd best hurry," he said at last, and began rolling the leaf into a waterproof twist of oilcloth with careful, steady hands—steadier than hers would ever be again, though perhaps, she thought, watching him, steadiness was not the only kind of worth a broken instrument could have. "Because if the dead can feel it, so can the living. And the living have hounds."

Sable rose to check the stairwell, out of habit, out of the old discipline that was the only honest thing left of her old life. Below, the tide was turning. She could feel it in the walls, in the slow exhale of the harbor, in the faint far-off tolling of a bell buoy marking the ebb.

And beneath all of it—so faint she might have imagined it, so faint she chose not to decide—something vast and patient shifted in the flooded dark under Saltcross, like a sleeper turning toward a voice it had waited five years to hear.

She went back and told them: "Get your boots. I know a road the patrols don't."
