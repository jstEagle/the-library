## 10. The Tide Road

The song woke her before the gulls did.

It came the way it always came now—not through her ears but through some thinner place behind them, a thread of sound pulled taut between the world of breathing things and whatever country her brother lived in now. *Come down to the water,* it said, the way it had said every night since the harbor fog. *Come down where the stones remember.*

Wren lay in the smuggler's cellar with her grandmother's ring on its cord against her sternum and listened to Sable's snores, deep and soldierly, from the cot by the wall. Coral slept badly—she could tell by his breathing, which never quite settled into the slow tide of true sleep but kept snagging, like cloth on a nail. He dreamed of burning pages. She was almost sure of it. Some nights she heard him murmur words in a language that wasn't Saltcross speech, and the words tasted of salt even across the dark room.

She rose without waking anyone. This was the hour her brother's voice came clearest: the dead hour, the one the living skipped over, when the tide turned and the whole harbor seemed to hold its breath between exhale and inhale.

*Ebb comes early tomorrow,* the song said. *Lowest water in ten years. Come down. Come see what I've been keeping for you.*

Up the ladder, through the trapdoor, out into the blue-black cold of the alley behind the fishmongers' row. The stars were hard as salt grains. Wren wrapped her arms around herself and walked toward the smell of the sea, and the song walked with her, patient as weather.

---

She didn't mean to wake Coral. But when she reached the top of the seawall stairs and looked back, he was there, barefoot on the cold stone, his coat thrown over his nightshirt, his face gray with a fear he was trying very hard not to show her.

"You went alone," he said. Not a question. An accusation aimed mostly at himself.

"The song said the ebb—" She stopped. His fear was a wall, and she kept walking into it. "Coral. It's not grief. I told you before and you lied to me, and I forgave you because you were scared, but it's *not grief.* Tam is singing me somewhere."

"Dead don't sing people places." He said it flatly, the way men say things they've decided to believe so the world stays shaped right. "They linger. They echo. They don't—"

"He's not lingering." Wren heard her own voice come out steady, older than her years, and it surprised her. "He's *guiding.* There's a difference. Lingerers stay where they died. Tam keeps moving. He's been moving since the fog, always just ahead, always singing louder when I go the right way and quieter when I go wrong. That's not an echo, Coral. Echoes don't care if you follow."

The wind off the water pushed at them both. Below the seawall, the flats were emerging from the retreating tide like something vast turning over in its sleep—mudflats gleaming, channels braiding silver, and far out, where no honest boat would ever ground itself, a dark shape breaking the surface of the low water. The spire. She'd seen it once already, in the fog, and run home shaking. Now she looked at it the way you look at a door you've finally decided to open.

"That's temple ground," Coral said quietly. "Or it was. Before." His hands were doing the thing they did when he was frightened—turning over and over, as though warming themselves at a fire only he could feel. "Wren, if you found it by following a song, then hunters can find it by following *you.*"

"Then we'd better get there first."

He stared at her. And then—this was the thing about Coral, the thing she'd loved even before she betrayed him and loved him after, which was harder—he laughed. A small, cracked, exhausted laugh, but real.

"When did you get brave?" he asked.

"I was always brave," Wren said. "I was just scared longer."

---

They woke Sable, who took one look at the sky, one look at the spire, and started swearing with the fluency of a woman who had once been paid by the crown to know exactly how much trouble a thing was worth.

"Lowest tide in a decade," she said, lacing her boots. "Which means every hunter in the eastern reach who can read a tide table knows tonight's the night the flats open. They'll have watched that spire for years waiting for a walkable road to it. We go now, we move fast, and we do not stop to admire the view." She paused at the cellar door, looking back at the two of them, and something moved behind her eyes—the old shame surfacing and being shoved down again, like a body held under water. "And I need my old seal."

"Your what?" Wren asked.

"Hunter's credential wax. Ilex thinks I burned mine when I quit." Sable smiled, and it was not a nice smile; it was the smile of a woman sharpening something. "I lied. You don't burn the tools of your trade. You keep them for exactly this kind of morning."

---

The tide road was not a road. It was a promise the sea made twice a day and broke twice a day—a mile of wet sand and shining mud between the seawall and the spire, stitched with channels that filled faster than a person could wade them. The three of them went out onto it as the light came up gray and pearl, and Wren felt the city fall away behind them like a coat taken off.

Tam's song grew stronger with every step.

It wasn't words anymore, or not only words. It was direction. When she drifted left, toward a channel mouth, the song thinned, went distant, the way a voice does when someone turns away. When she corrected, it swelled warm against the thin place behind her ears, and her chest ached with a gladness so sharp it was nearly pain. *This way. Yes. This way.* Her brother had been a boy who loved maps, who'd drawn the harbor on slate until their grandmother scolded him for wasting chalk, and now he was a map himself, drawn in the ink of the dead, and Wren followed him across the sea's own floor.

"There," she said, pointing. "The channel bends east. If we cross at the bend while it's still shallow—"

"How do you know that?" Sable demanded.

"I know it the way you know your routes," Wren said. "Someone taught me. Someone who's walked here before." She didn't say *who*. She didn't have to. She saw Coral's throat move as he swallowed.

They crossed at the bend. The water came to their knees and was cold enough to make their bones sing, and on the far side the mud firmed, and the spire rose ahead of them, black and barnacled, dripping weed from a height no wave should reach. Up close Wren could see carvings under the crust of the sea—figures, dozens of figures, human shapes and shapes that were not human, their arms raised together toward something above them, mouths open. Singing, maybe. Or breathing. It was hard to tell the difference, carved in stone.

"They're holding hands," she said softly. "Look. All of them. The god-shapes and the people-shapes. Nobody's kneeling."

Coral stood before the carvings a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice was rough. "That's not how they teach it in the schoolrooms."

"No," Wren agreed. "It isn't."

---

Sable heard the horses before either of them did—or rather, she heard the absence, the sudden hush of the gulls lifting off the flats all at once, wheeling inland.

"Down," she breathed, and pulled them both behind a shelf of rock crusted with mussels. From the seawall, small in the paling morning, riders were coming onto the flats. Six of them. Seven. Wren counted, and stopped counting, because the number kept getting worse. At their head rode a figure straight-backed in a gray oilskin cloak, and even at this distance Wren knew the set of those shoulders, because Sable had gone rigid beside her in a way that had nothing to do with cold.

"Ilex," Sable said.

"You're sure?"

"I'd know that self-righteous posture anywhere. He sat a horse like that through the plague winters too. Like the horse should be grateful." She was already moving, pulling things from inside her coat: a stub of red sealing wax, a stamp worn smooth, a wallet of papers soft with age. "Right. Listen fast. They'll ride the main channel line—it's the only firm going. So we give them a reason to be elsewhere." She pressed the wax against a flat stone and softened it with a match, working quickly, her hands suddenly steady in the way Wren had learned meant the opposite of calm. "I'm going to leave them a trail. Reports, sealed with my mark, dated three days back—scouting notes saying the fugitives were sighted heading north along the coast road, toward the ferry at Grayhaven."

"They'll check the dates," Coral said.

"They'll check the *seal*," Sable said. "Ilex trusts wax more than he trusts people. Always did. It's his whole tragedy." She worked fast, folding, pressing, tucking the false reports into a oilskin pouch, and then—this was the part Wren watched with her heart in her teeth—Sable stood up out of cover, in plain view of the riders, and walked fifty yards north along the flats, casual as a woman gathering shellfish, and dropped the pouch beneath a leaning stake that some long-dead fisherman had driven into the mud. Then she strolled back, unhurried, and folded down behind the rocks again.

"What if they saw you?" Wren whispered.

"Then they saw a disgraced huntress picking up whelks," Sable murmured. "Half of them still think I'm a cautionary tale. The other half think I'm a coward. Cowards and cautionary tales don't lead fugitives to drowned temples." Her jaw was tight. "They'll find the pouch. Ilex will read it twice. And then he'll split his riders, because he never could resist a chance to be thorough, and thorough men are slow men."

Through the gap between the rocks Wren watched the riders come on. The wind carried snatches of their voices, flattened by distance. Then a shout—one of them had found the stake, the pouch—and the little knot of horsemen bunched and argued, and the gray-cloaked figure at their head raised an arm, and four riders wheeled away north along the tideline at a canter while three continued toward the spire.

Three. Sable exhaled slowly through her nose. "Better odds than I expected from him."

"It's still three," Coral said.

"It's always three," Sable said. "The trick is choosing which three."

---

They went into the spire's shadow while the riders were still arguing over the false trail, and found the door.

It shouldn't have been there. That was Wren's first thought—that a door in the side of a drowned tower, below the tideline, below every watermark, should have been full of sea. But the stone around it was dry. Dry as a hearthstone. Dry as bone left in the sun. And when she put her palm against it, the song didn't just swell—it *broke open*, all of it at once, every night of listening pouring through her at full volume, and Wren staggered, and would have fallen except that Coral caught her arm.

"Wren? What is it? What do you hear?"

"Not one voice." She was crying and hadn't noticed starting. "Coral, it was never just Tam. It's—I thought it was one song because it sounded like one voice, but it's a *choir*, it's hundreds, it's everyone the Veil ever—" She pressed both hands over the thin place behind her ears, which helped nothing at all. "They're all in there. All the dead whose prayers got cut off. And they're not haunting me. They never were." She laughed, wet and disbelieving. "They've been *waiting* for someone who could hear them. Five years the god's been silent and the dead have been standing at the door of the world, singing, hoping somebody alive would hear and come."

Coral had gone very pale. "The god's been silent five years," he repeated, like a man testing ice.

"And the dead have been singing five years," Wren said. "You thought nobody answered your prayers. But somebody answered *mine*, Coral. Somebody's been answering mine every single night, with the only voice it had left." She wiped her face with her sleeve, fiercely, and looked up at the dry door in the drowned wall. "I'm not haunted. I never was. I'm *called.*"

Above them, faint across the flats, came the creak of harness and the strike of hooves on firm sand. Three riders, closing.

Sable drew the knife she'd sworn off drawing. "Door first," she said. "Feelings after. Alive is the whole strategy."

Coral put his hand on the stone beside Wren's, and spoke—not the safe, godless charm-words he used at market, but the other language, the salt-tasting one, the one he murmured in his sleep. And the door, which had waited under the sea for longer than Saltcross had had a name, opened for them both.

Beyond it, stairs went down into dry darkness, and out of the darkness rose breath—the sense of breath, vast and slow and held for five long years, like a lung finally allowed to fill.

Wren stepped through first. Behind her, hooves thundered on the flats, and Sable swore, and the door began, gently, to close.
