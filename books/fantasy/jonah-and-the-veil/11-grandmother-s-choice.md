## 11. Grandmother's Choice

The census wagons came to Tidewater Lane on the morning the gulls went quiet.

Coral felt it before he saw it—the way the harbor held its breath, the way even the fishmongers' cries faltered and died mid-word, as though the whole of Saltcross had been dipped in wax. He had learned to read silences the way other men read weather. This one had iron in it.

They were three days out from the weir, hidden in a cooper's abandoned drying shed above the flats, when Wren came running with her face gone white as bleached shell.

"They're taking her," she said. "The wagons. They're on our street."

Sable was already moving, pulling her old hunter's coat over her shoulders like a woman putting on a knife. "Which street?"

"Tidewater. The blue door with the—"

"I know it." Sable didn't look at Coral, but her jaw set in that particular way that meant she was bracing for an argument she intended to lose or win without discussion. "It'll be a collection detail. Two wagons, six men at most, a clerk with a ledger. They sweep the low streets first because the poor don't argue."

"Then we go now," Coral said.

"We *can't*—" Sable began, and stopped, because he was already looking at her, and whatever she found in his face made her close her mouth on the rest of it.

He understood what she wanted to say. Three fugitives walking into a census collection was not rescue; it was delivery. Every hour they stayed unseen was an hour bought with Wren's brother's ghost-song and Sable's forged trails and Coral's own five years of swallowed breath. To spend all of it on one old woman who might refuse to come anyway—that was not strategy. It was madness dressed as love.

But Coral had spent five years learning what happened to people who let prudence do their choosing. Prudence had kept him alive. Prudence had also kept him kneeling at a silent shrine, talking to a god that could not answer, while the city around him filled up with ash. There were worse things than dying foolishly. He had watched them done to other people, from behind a market stall, with his hands folded and his gift hidden, and he had told himself every night that survival was its own kind of faithfulness.

He no longer believed it.

"Wren stays with you," he said to Sable. "I'll go alone."

"You'll go *nowhere* alone," Sable said flatly. "You walk into a hunter cordon without me, you're dead inside a minute. I know their formations. I *trained* half of them." She said it bitterly, but her hands were already checking the knife at her hip, the weighted cord at her wrist—the tools of a trade she'd sworn off and could not seem to stop carrying. "We go together. We get the grandmother if she'll come. We're gone before the bell rings noon. And if any part of this sours, we run and we do not look back."

"Agreed," Coral said.

Wren grabbed his sleeve. "I'm coming."

"No."

"She's *my* grandmother—"

"And they know your face now, little wren." He gentled it as best he could. The girl had been the informant; he had not forgiven her; and still, looking down at her stricken face, he felt the strange stubborn tenderness that had gotten him into this whole ruin of a pilgrimage. "If they see you, everything Sable risks becomes nothing. You stay here. You sing your brother's song and listen for anything the dead will tell you about those wagons. Can you do that?"

Her chin trembled. She nodded once, hard, the way children nod when nodding is the only alternative to breaking.

* * *

Tidewater Lane smelled of tar and low tide and woodsmoke from cookfires hastily smothered. The census wagons sat crooked where the lane narrowed, two of them, canvas-topped, wheels greased against screaming. Six hunters in grey-and-ash livery. A clerk with a ledger chained to his wrist, ink-stained fingers moving down a list of names like a man counting coins.

And at the blue door with the chipped paint, arms folded, spine straight as a mast, stood Maren Odile—Wren's grandmother, eighty-one years old, built like the sea-cliffs she'd been born under.

"—not afflicted, not listed, not going," she was saying, in the voice of a woman who had buried a husband, raised two grandchildren alone, and outlived three kings. Her voice carried the length of the lane. "My name is on no list. My granddaughter is dead of fever, God keep her, and my son is dead of the same, and there is nothing in this house for your wagons but grief, which you cannot tax."

The clerk did not look up. "Maren Odile. Widow. Listed by informants as harboring a gifted child. The girl is to be taken for assessment regardless of—"

"The girl is *dead.*"

"The girl was seen in the fish market eleven days ago."

Coral's blood went cold and then colder. Eleven days ago was the day of the raid. The day he had thrown open his magic like a struck match to shield Wren from a falling stall and a hunter's blade. Someone had seen her. Someone had named her.

Sable moved beside him, and he realized they had drifted apart in the crowd of onlookers—dockhands, washerwomen, a knot of children too hungry to be afraid—each of them watching the way Saltcross watched everything now: hungrily, calculatingly, wondering what a neighbor's name might fetch in coin.

"Six men," Sable murmured, barely a breath. "Two at the wagons, two flanking the clerk, two at the door. The clerk is soft—Ilex's people call him Quill, and Quill has fainted at two burnings that I know of. The door pair are the danger. The tall one is Bracken. Good with a blade. Bad with hesitation."

"And the other?"

"Other one's mine." Something flickered across her face—shame or resolve, he could no longer tell the difference in her. "Aldous Reeve. Nineteen. I taught him to tie his bootlaces. He'll hesitate when he sees me. That hesitation is our whole plan."

"What *is* our whole plan?"

She almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, worn thin. "I'm going to arrest you. Loudly. In front of everyone. And then we're going to walk out of this lane like a hunter and her prisoner, and we're going to collect the old woman on the way, because nobody argues with a transfer in progress. It's the oldest trick in the book. I wrote half the book."

"And if someone recognizes you?"

"Then we improvise." She rolled her shoulders back, and Coral watched something extraordinary happen: the drunkard's slouch fell away from her like a shed coat, and underneath it stood the crown's finest witch-hunter, boots squared, chin level, authority rolling off her like heat off a forge-stone. Five years of shame, and the posture still lived in her body, waiting. "Walk behind me. Look sullen. You've had practice."

Before he could answer, she stepped out of the crowd.

"HOLD!" Her voice cracked across the lane like a whip. "Hunters! By order of the Census Edict, stand away from the detainee!"

The lane erupted into murmurs. The hunters turned—and Coral saw Aldous Reeve's young face go through recognition, disbelief, and terror in the space of a heartbeat, exactly as Sable had promised. Bracken's hand went to his blade and stopped, uncertain whether he was witnessing an arrest or interrupting one.

"Sable Vane," Bracken said carefully. "Captain Vane. We thought—you were relieved of—"

"I was *reassigned*, you great lump, which is more than you'll be able to say if this prisoner escapes." She strode forward, seized Coral's arm above the elbow, and gripped hard enough to bruise. "This man is Coral Halloran. Charm-seller. Afflicted. Confirmed gift-work, witnessed by myself. I've been tracking him since the market raid. Where's your clerk?"

Quill looked up from his ledger with the expression of a man who hoped very sincerely to be elsewhere.

"Log him," Sable snapped. "Transfer to the temple holding cells. I'll take him personally—orders from Ilex himself, if you want to waste an hour sending word up-chain and explaining to the Captain why you delayed his prize."

The name worked like salt in a wound. Quill paled. Bracken stepped aside. And Maren Odile, standing in her doorway, looked directly at Coral—looked through the rags and the sullen droop he affected—and her eyes narrowed with the terrible clarity of the very old.

"Well," she said. "About time somebody came for me."

* * *

Getting her to leave took longer than getting past the hunters.

That was the thing Coral had not counted on—not the blades, not the ledgers, not the risk, but the sheer granite immovability of an old woman who had decided her house would not fall while she stood in it.

They made it four streets, Sable marching them along like a procession, Reeve trailing at a distance with his hand nowhere near his sword and his conscience visibly eating him alive—before Maren planted her feet outside a shuttered chandlery and refused to move another step.

"This is far enough," she said. "You'll take the ring and you'll take the message, and then you'll put me down and go."

"Grandmother—" Coral started, and she rounded on him with such ferocity that he actually stepped back.

"Don't you *grandmother* me, boy. I know what you are. I've known since you sold me that fever-ward last winter that smelled of my mother's kitchen and no shop in this city." Her voice dropped, fierce and low. "I knew what you were when I told the hunters my granddaughter was dead of fever, may God and the Veil both forgive the lie. You think I don't know why you've come? You think I don't know whose song has been leading my Wren about the fog like a pied piper?"

Coral went still. "*You* know about the singing?"

"I know everything that happens under my roof, including the parts that happen after my bedtime." For just a moment, something in her face cracked—not weakness, but the ache beneath strength, the grief that the strength existed to carry. "My Tom used to sing too. Before the fever took him. Same tune. I used to think it was a family song. Then I got old enough to understand that some songs aren't taught. They're inherited." She held out her hand. "Ring. Now. Before I lose my nerve, and believe me, boy, I am *very* close to losing my nerve."

"It's a wedding ring," Coral said stupidly. "Gold. It won't feed anyone, it won't buy passage—"

"Look at it properly before you tell an old woman what her own property is worth."

He took it. It was heavier than gold should be, worn smooth on the inner band by fifty years of a living finger. And as he turned it in the grey light, he saw the marking—fine as a hair, cut into the metal in a script he had only ever seen once before, on the single salt-stained leaf he had saved from his mother's grimoire.

His breath left him.

"That's—" His voice failed. He tried again. "That's covenant-work. Pre-covenant script. This ring is older than the law. Older than the *crown*."

"My husband's line goes back to the tide-priests," Maren said simply. "They married into fisherfolk and hid what they were until hiding became breathing. That ring was sworn at a shore-altar when the temples still had names. It was passed to me, and I was meant to pass it to my son, and my son is ash on the wind, and my granddaughter is—" She stopped. Steadied. "Well. My granddaughter is standing in a cooper's shed somewhere, listening to her dead brother, wearing a charm you made her that I have *never* once seen fail."

"Grandmother, come with us." All his careful distance was gone; he heard his own voice crack like a boy's. "Come with us to the temple. Come and see it. Please. You can't stay here—they'll come back for you, they'll—"

"They'll burn me, yes." She said it the way she might have said *it'll rain*. "Probably. Eventually. But I have buried a husband in that house and birthed a daughter in that house and I will not die anywhere else, and I will certainly not die *running*, because then I'd die a stranger to my own walls, and I have been many disappointing things in my life but never that."

"Maren—"

"*Listen* to me." Her hands closed over his, over the ring, pressing his fingers shut around it. Hers were cold and strong as mooring-line. "I am giving you the only thing I have left to give, and it isn't gold, whatever it's made of. It's a *charge*. Do you understand? My mother-in-law gave it to me with these same words: *keep it until there is someone worth keeping it for.* Fifty years I waited. I thought it would be Wren grown, maybe. Wren wed." Her grip tightened. "Instead it's you. I don't know what you mean to do at that drowned temple and I don't want to know—old women sleep better ignorant—but I know a debt-collector's face when I see one, and yours has been collecting on behalf of a god for five years. So. Take the ring. Take the girl. Keep them both alive. And when you see my Tom over there in the fog-country, you tell him his grandmother kept the faith, even when the faith forgot to keep her."

Coral could not speak.

He had imagined, in the long dark of the past weeks, many versions of what rescue would feel like—triumph mostly, or relief, or the hot clean burn of having chosen rightly. He had not imagined this: standing in a shuttered lane holding a piece of a faith older than law, being handed the weight of generations by a woman who intended to stay and burn.

"You're asking me to take her and go," he managed finally.

"I'm telling you that you're going to, whether I ask or not, because you're a good man and good men always do what dying old women tell them." The ghost of a smile. "It's how we get things done."

Behind them, Sable cleared her throat—once, sharply, the sound of a woman reminding the world that hunters were still hunting. Down the lane, the census wagons had finished loading their other cargo: a lamplighter with shaking hands, a washerwoman's son who couldn't have been fifteen. The clerk Quill was scratching entries. Noon was coming. Somewhere across the rooftops, the temple bell began to count it.

Maren Odile straightened her shawl. Kissed her thumb and pressed it briefly to Coral's forehead, an old gesture, a priest's gesture, one he hadn't seen since his mother died.

"Tell my granddaughter," she said quietly, "that the ring fits the left hand, third finger, and that she is to wear it until *she* finds someone worth passing it to. Tell her the dead sing because they love us, not because they haunt us. And tell her—" For the first time, her voice wavered, a single thread pulled loose from all that rope. "Tell her the blue door opens from the inside, always, wherever I am. She'll understand."

Then she walked back up the lane toward her burning-to-be house with her spine straight as a mast, and Coral stood in the shadow of the chandlery awning and let himself, for three full seconds, break apart and reassemble.

When he turned, Sable was watching him with an unreadable expression.

"We have to go," she said gently. Gently—from Sable, it landed like absolution.

"I know."

"Coral. The girl. When she hears where we left her grandmother—"

"I know." He closed his fist around the ring until the old script bit into his palm. He thought of Wren in the cooper's shed, singing to a brother only she could hear, an orphan twice over by nightfall—first to fever, now to a choice made on her behalf by people who loved her. He thought of his own mother's grimoire, page by page by page, and the one leaf he'd kept, and how inheritance so often arrived as ash with a single live coal in the middle of it.

*I will not let her carry this alone,* he swore—to the ring, to the fog-country, to the silent god who had finally begun, in small ways, to stir. *Whatever it costs. Whatever I have to become. She will not stand in the gap by herself.*

* * *

Wren knew before they told her. Of course she did. She met them at the shed door with her brother's song already dying on her lips, and she looked at Coral's empty hands—at the absence where a grandmother should have been—and she did not cry. That was the worst of it. She simply went very still, the way water goes still before it freezes.

"She wouldn't come."

"No."

"But she gave me something." Not a question. Wren's eyes dropped to his closed fist. "Through you."

Wordlessly, Coral opened his hand. The ring lay in his palm, catching the last of the afternoon light, the pre-covenant script glimmering like frost.

Wren reached out. Stopped. Looked up at him with a question in her face that was older than her years: *am I allowed? Is this real? Am I the kind of person things are passed to?*

"It fits the left hand," Coral said, his voice rough. "Third finger. And you're to wear it until you find someone worth passing it to—which, for the record, I intend to be a very long time from now, because I have plans for you involving approximately forty years of unpaid apprenticeship."

A sound escaped her—half laugh, half sob, entirely seventeen years old. She slid the ring onto her finger. It fit perfectly, of course it fit; blood keeps its own measurements. She stared at it, turning her hand in the light, and then she crossed the shed floor and put her arms around Coral and held on like a drowning swimmer, and after a moment—stiffly, awkwardly, having forgotten how—he held on back.

Over her head, Sable caught his eye. She said nothing. She didn't need to. Family wasn't earned clean; she'd told him that herself, over a stolen supper in a smuggler's cellar, and he was beginning—slowly, unwillingly, completely—to believe her.

But outside the shed, far off across the flats, a horn sounded. Once. Twice. A third time, closer.

Sable's face went grim as slate. "That's a pursuit signal. That's *Ilex's* signal." She was already moving, kicking sand over the cold hearth, gathering their few belongings. "Someone talked. Reeve, or Bracken, or the clerk—I don't care which. We have to be at the tidal gate before the ebb turns, or we miss the road to the spire for another month."

Wren lifted her head from Coral's shoulder. On her finger, the old ring seemed to drink the fading light—and faintly, impossibly, from somewhere out beyond the harbor fog, the dead began to sing.

All of them this time.

Not one voice. Hundreds.
