## 12. Hunters at the Weir

The river spoke to Sable before she saw it.

She'd learned its voice in her first year of service, wading reed-beds after a charm-witch who drowned rather than be taken. The weir ahead made a sound like a congregation murmuring through a closed door—water worrying itself over the stone lip, patient, endless, indifferent to what came to stand on its banks. She had stood on many banks like this one. She had always been the one with the lantern and the drawn blade.

Now she was the thing in the dark that other people's lanterns were looking for.

"Down," she breathed, and dropped into the sedge, pulling Wren by the sleeve as she went. Coral folded himself beside them without a sound, which she still hadn't gotten used to—a grown man vanishing into shadow like spilled ink. Three days out from Grandmother Odell's cottage, two days since the census wagons, and Sable had begun to understand that hiding was a craft too, one Coral practiced the way she'd once practiced sword-drills: daily, humbly, until it lived in the hands.

Wren crouched against her side, small and warm and smelling of woodsmoke and river mud. The girl's eyes were fixed on the water. Listening. She did that constantly now—head tilted, lips parted slightly, as if the world were a room where someone was always singing just past hearing. Sable had heard her humming the dead boy's tune at odd hours, half-asleep, walking. It should have frightened her. It only made her tired, in some old deep place, of everything she had ever burned.

"The ebb turns within the hour," Wren whispered. "He says—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening. She still caught herself saying *he says*, then flinched, as if naming the habit might make it worse. "The flats will open up. We can cross before the hunters reach the ford."

"He," said Coral mildly, from the dark. He never pushed. That was his particular cruelty, if it was cruelty—patience that left you nowhere to hide.

Wren didn't answer. Her hand found the cord at her neck, the wedding ring beneath her shirt, and held it the way other children held amulets. Maybe that was exactly what it was. Sable thought of the old woman pressing it into her granddaughter's palm, refusing the wagon, choosing her own ground. There were kinds of courage that looked like stubbornness right up until the moment they looked like faith.

Sable scanned the far bank. The weir was old work, crown-cut stone spanning the river in three steps, the spillway at its eastern end choked with foam. Beyond it the tidal flats spread silver-gray under a thinning moon, miles of shining mud cut with creeks that filled and emptied with the sea's slow breathing. Once the ebb began, they could walk out onto the exposed flats and be gone—no roads, no tracks, no cover either, but distance enough.

It was a good plan. It would have worked, on any other night, with anyone else behind them.

That was why, when she saw the lantern-light kindle along the top of the weir—three points, then five, then nine—her stomach did not lurch so much as settle. As if some part of her had been waiting all week for exactly this.

"Hunters." Her voice came out flat and professional, and she hated how easily the old tone returned, like a uniform she couldn't stop wearing. "Nine, maybe ten. They're not searching. They're waiting."

"They know we have to cross here," Coral said quietly.

"They don't *know*." But even as she said it, she knew it wasn't true. False trails cost time, and time was the one coin they'd been spending freely since the market. Someone had read the pattern of their flight better than she had. Only a handful of people alive could have. She named none of them aloud.

* * *

They watched from the sedge while the hunters arranged themselves with terrible competence. Lanterns spaced along the weir-top. Two men moving down toward the ford, unhurried. A tall figure standing at the center of the span with his arms crossed, making no attempt at concealment, because concealment was for people who feared being seen.

Even across the water, even in the bad light, Sable knew the set of those shoulders. She had slept back-to-back with those shoulders through eleven winters of campaigns. She had trusted him to watch her blind side through the plague years, through the purges, through the night they burned the Ferrow family and Ilex had stood closest to the pyre and said nothing at all.

Captain Ilex. Her partner. The last person in the crown's service whose opinion of her had ever mattered.

"Sable." Coral's voice was very soft. "Who is that?"

"You already know."

"I want to hear you say it."

She made herself look at the tall figure instead of at Coral. "His name is Ilex. He taught me how to track a witch through standing corn without bending a single stalk. He carried me four miles on his back when I broke my ankle at Harrow Ford. He—" Her throat closed. She swallowed it down like something bitter. "He was my friend. Before friend became a word I stopped being allowed to use."

Wren's small cold fingers found hers in the dark. Sable let them stay. She had spent twenty years learning to keep her hands steady around blades; it had never occurred to her until recently that there were other things hands could hold.

"What do we do?" Wren asked.

What they did not do was run. Running confirmed pursuit, and pursuit brought horns, and horns brought every garrison between here and the coast. What they did not do was split up; she'd seen what happened to fugitives who scattered, and it involved long ropes and longer fires. What they did was wait, because the tide was turning and the flats were opening and Ilex, whatever else he was, was not a man who wasted men on mud that would swallow them to the thigh.

He was waiting for the water to give them up. So they would give themselves up first—or they would go over the spillway, which no sane person attempted at night, which was precisely why it might work.

"I'm going to talk to him," Sable said.

Coral turned his head. In the dying moonlight his face was all planes and shadow, but she could feel his attention land on her like a hand. "No."

"Yes."

"He'll take you."

"He won't kill me. I'm worth more breathing—there are questions with my name on them in three garrisons." She almost smiled. "And he hesitated once already, Coral. At the Ferrow farm. I watched him hold the torch an extra breath before he lit it. Men like Ilex don't hesitate unless something in them is already bleeding."

"And if he doesn't care about your hesitation? If he takes you and comes for us anyway?"

"Then you'll be gone over the spillway, and it will have bought you the time to do it." She stood, brushing mud from her knees, and felt the old posture settle over her—spine straight, chin level, the stance of a woman who had once walked into plague-houses without flinching. "Keep the girl low. When I raise my hand flat, run for the spillway. Don't wait for me."

"Don't wait for you," Coral repeated. His voice was gentle and terrible. "You know I won't agree to that."

"I know," Sable said. "But you'll do it anyway, because you're the only one who knows the way, and she follows you." She looked down at Wren, whose face was pale and set and much too old for its years. "Both of you. Promise me nothing. Just live."

She stepped out of the sedge before either of them could answer, walking down to the waterline with her empty hands visible, the way you approached a spooked horse or a grieving man. The river-mud took her boots to the ankle. She didn't slow.

A dozen yards out, the nearest hunter shouted, and lanterns swung, and the whole arrangement of armed men along the weir-top shifted like a machine engaging. Then the tall figure at the center raised one hand—not a command to attack, but a command to *wait*—and Sable's heart did something complicated and painful in her chest, because he had waited for her, even now, even knowing what she was.

She stopped at the edge of the shallows, close enough to see his face by lantern-light. Older. Gray coming in at the temples like frost on iron. The same scar splitting his left eyebrow, the one she'd given him with a practice blade when they were nineteen and laughing.

"Ilex," she said.

For a long moment he simply looked at her, and the lanterns hissed, and the river kept up its murmuring behind him like a crowd that wouldn't disperse.

"Sable Vane." His voice hadn't changed either. Deep, unhurried, the voice of a man who had never once needed to shout. "Turned informer. Turned fugitive. Turned—what, exactly? Confessor?"

"Turned honest, maybe. Late."

"Honest." Something moved behind his eyes—something that might have been contempt, might have been grief wearing contempt's clothes. "Do you know what they're calling you in the garrisons? *The Vane*. Like a weather sign. Like a warning to the others."

"Good. Let them be warned."

"Three of my hunters died at the tide road," he said, flat. "Not killed. *Lost.* Mud to their chests, screaming, and the flats closed over them like a mouth. And the fisherfolk say the fog sang while it happened." He leaned forward. "Is that your doing? Is that what you've become—one of them? Some fog-calling witch in bare feet?"

"No." And then, because lying to Ilex had always been impossible: "Not yet."

Something flickered across his face. Almost humor. Dead and buried, but recognizable—the ghost of the man who used to cheat at cards and lose anyway.

"Sable." He came down off the weir a step, then another, until he stood at the water's edge opposite her, near enough that she could see the exhaustion carved under his eyes. He had not been sleeping well either, then. Good. Misery loved company; it was the only company she'd offered anyone in years. "Give it up. Give *them* up. The charm-man, the child. Walk in with me tonight and I swear before anything you like that you'll see trial, not a pyre. You were the best of us. They'll remember that, when it matters. Judges remember."

"Judges remembered the Ferrows, too."

He flinched. Actually flinched, a small involuntary motion of the shoulders, as though she'd struck an old wound—and there it was, the thing she'd gambled everything on. He had carried that night too. All these years. She watched him carry it now, visibly, the weight shifting in him like ballast.

"That was law," he said, but his voice had lost its footing.

"That was murder with paperwork." She took one step closer, mud sucking at her boots, close enough to see his pupils contract. "Listen to me, Ilex. One thing, and then I'll say no more. Five years ago I believed what we were told—that the gift caused the plague, that burning it out saved the city. I helped bind priests for that lie. I burned families for that lie. And I have spent every night since with my hands shaking, drinking to make them stop, and they have *never once stopped*, because the body keeps its own ledger and mine is written in ash." She held her palms up between them, open, trembling faintly in the lantern-light. "Look at them. Look at what your law has made of its best hunter. Now ask yourself what it's made of everyone else."

The lanterns hissed. Somewhere upstream a night bird called, twice, and fell silent.

Ilex looked at her hands. He looked for a long time.

Then he said, very quietly, in a voice she had never heard him use—not in eleven winters, not at any pyre, not ever: "Was it worth it? Whatever you found on the other side of all this. Was it worth becoming *this*?"

And Sable, who had rehearsed arguments all week, who had prepared speeches about stolen gods and bound covenants and the founders' theft, found that none of it would come. What came instead was the truth, plain as river water:

"I slept three nights ago," she said. "First time in six years without drink. Because for the first time since I quit, I was doing something that mattered, with people who—" Her voice cracked and she let it. Let him hear it crack. "Yes. It was worth it. It's worth it right now."

Behind Ilex, up on the weir, one of the younger hunters shifted his weight, and the gravel crunched, loud as a shot.

Ilex's eyes moved—just moved, a flicker upward—and in that flicker Sable saw the whole architecture of the man: the partner who wanted to believe her, and the captain who could not afford to, standing in the same body, tearing it apart. She saw him weigh her surrender against her words, his oath against her shaking hands, the Ferrow children's faces against the orders in his dispatch case.

He raised his hand. Not flat, not commanding. Half-raised, hovering, the gesture of a man reaching for something in the dark that might not be there.

"Ilex," she said softly. "Come with us. Don't sound it. Just—stand there. Stand there and count to a hundred, and by then the tide will have taken our trail and you can tell them the flats ate us. Let this one night be the night you hesitated *well*."

His hand hung in the air between them. The lantern-light trembled across his face, and for one suspended, endless heartbeat, Sable Vane—who had bet her life on reading men, and had rarely been wrong—believed she had read this one right.

Then he closed his eyes. Briefly. Like a man shutting a door on something too bright.

When he opened them, the captain had won, and it was written all over him in the set of his jaw.

"I'm sorry, Sable," he said, and turned, and his arm swept down in a hard, clean arc, and the horn sounded—two long notes rolling out across the water, across the flats, waking every garrison and watch-post between the river and the sea.

* * *

She was moving before the second note finished.

"Spillway!" she roared, and the sedge erupted behind her—Coral with Wren's wrist locked in his grip, both of them sprinting for the eastern end of the weir where the water went over in a white, thundering sheet. Behind her, boots hammered the weir-stones; someone shouted a challenge; a crossbow snapped and the bolt went wide, skimming the foam.

Sable put herself between the hunters and the others and ran backward, which was a trick from her campaigning days, keeping her body between the blades and the fugitives as the gap closed. Twenty yards. Fifteen. The spillway roared ahead, a curtain of falling water eight feet high, and beyond it the drop into the churning pool below, and beyond *that* the dark mouth of the mill-race, and beyond that—

"We can't jump that!" Wren screamed.

"We can!" Coral's voice, wild and certain, and there was something rising in it that Sable had never heard before—something that made the hair on her arms lift. "Hold the ring! Girl—hold your grandmother's ring and *think of her*!"

Wren's fist closed over the cord at her neck. And the water changed.

Sable felt it more than saw it—the spillway's roar stuttered, thickened, the falling sheet going strangely slow, like honey pouring, like the world holding its breath. The Veil, working through a dead woman's wedding band and a living girl's grief. Small magic. The god answering small prayers, the only kind it could still manage through its chains.

It would have to be enough.

"Jump!" Coral shouted, and hurled Wren bodily over the falls.

Sable reached the lip a stride behind him, turned once more to fire her parting glance down the length of the weir—at Ilex, standing motionless at its center, watching her go with an expression she would spend the rest of her life trying to name—then leapt into the slow-falling wall of water.

The world went white and roaring.

The pool below hit her like a hammer wrapped in ice. She went down spinning, lungs full of shock, boots dragging, and for one lucid instant understood with perfect clarity that she was going to drown in a mill-pool in the dark, unwept and unmourned, and that this was, all things considered, an appropriate ending for the life she'd led.

Then hands closed on her collar—strong hands, desperate hands—and hauled her sideways into the mill-race's current, and Coral's voice came through the roar, raw and furious: "*Swim*, damn you, swim or I swear I'll drag you!"

She swam. The current took them all—Coral with one arm around Wren, Sable clutching his trailing sleeve, three bodies tumbling together down the black channel while behind them the horn sounded again, closer now, and lanterns swarmed the weir-top like angry wasps.

A bolt struck the water near her shoulder. A second tore through the meat of her upper arm, a line of fire she barely registered—adrenaline was a mercy that way—but felt later, oh, she would feel it later, she already knew that with the certainty of long experience. Blood unfurled from her arm into the dark water, a ribbon of warmth in the cold.

The mill-race spat them out into the lower river, and the lower river carried them around a bend, and the bend hid them from the lanterns, and by then the ebb had begun and the sea was pulling at them, patient and vast, drawing them out toward the flats where the mud would take their footprints and the fog would sing.

Sable got her feet under her in the shallows, coughing, one arm useless and streaming. Coral stood waist-deep beside her, holding Wren above the water, the girl soaked and shivering and gripping her grandmother's ring like a talisman, her eyes enormous in the dark.

"They followed," Wren whispered. "He sounded the horn. He—he *hesitated*, I saw him, why did he sound it?"

"Because he's a good man," Sable said, "who chose a bad master. It happens more often than you'd think. It happened to me for twenty years." She looked down at her arm, at the blood diluting pink in the seawater, and felt the first cold finger of fever-anticipation touch the base of her skull. Later. Deal with it later.

Coral was staring at her arm too. His face, when she met his eyes, was grim in a way that had nothing to do with the wound.

"That needs stitching," he said. "Properly. Tonight."

"It needs nothing of the sort. It needs wrapping and ignoring."

"Coral's right," Wren said, with the brisk authority of a twelve-year-old, and Sable laughed despite everything—a short, cracked, genuine laugh that hurt her ribs and felt, absurdly, like the first honest thing she'd done all night.

They stumbled out onto the flats as the fog came rolling in off the sea to meet them, gray and vast and softly singing. Wren tilted her head toward it, listening, and her expression shifted—fear becoming something else, something steadier.

"This way," the girl said. "He knows a place. Dry. Hidden. Where nobody looks." A pause, and then, softer, almost shy: "He wants to help. Your arm. He remembers fever."

Sable looked at the fog, at the dead boy's unseen presence folded inside it, at the two bedraggled souls beside her—one bleeding, one hunted, both hers now whether she'd agreed to it or not.

"Well then," she said, and started walking. "Lead on."
