## 13. Salt and Fever

The cellar smelled of brine and rotting kelp and, beneath it all, the sweetish wrongness that Wren had come to dread—the smell of a body going bad from the inside out.

Sable lay on a pallet of folded sails near the far wall, where the smugglers' lamp couldn't quite reach. Her skin had gone the color of old candle wax, waxy and yellowed at the edges, and the wound on her side—where the hunter's blade had caught her as they went over the spillway—had swollen into a hot red ridge that wept something thin and gray. Three days ago it had been a clean line. Two days ago Coral had said *it's knitting*. Last night he had stopped saying anything about it at all, which frightened Wren more than any words could have.

She sat beside the pallet with her knees drawn up, her grandmother's ring on its cord around her neck, and she counted Sable's breaths the way she used to count waves when her brother first died, because counting was a thing hands and mouths could do when hearts had nothing.

One. Two. Three. A hitch. Four. Five. A long, wet pause that stretched so far Wren leaned forward with her hand half-raised before Sable dragged in air again and the count began over.

"You're staring," Sable rasped. Her eyes were open a slit, fever-bright.

"I'm counting."

"Counting what?"

"Your breaths."

A ghost of a laugh moved through her, or maybe just pain. "Waste of time. I'm not going anywhere." But her hand slid across the sail-cloth until her fingers found Wren's wrist, and they stayed there, light as mooring rope, for a long while after she drifted back under.

* * *

Coral worked at the little table by the seaward wall, where a crack in the stone let in the sound of the tide if not the tide itself. He had laid out everything he owned of his craft on the boards: the luck-knots, the fever-wards he'd sold for coppers in the fish market, the salt-stained leaf of his mother's grimoire, a bowl of harbor water, a dish of ash, three candles stubbed down to their collars. He was building something out of them—a shape, an argument—and Wren watched him fail at it.

He would set the knots in a ring around the bowl, murmur, wait. Nothing. He'd move one knot a finger's width, murmur again in that low language that wasn't Low Speech at all but something older, saltier, the tongue of people who prayed with their faces to the water. Nothing. His shoulders climbed higher toward his ears each time, until they were nearly touching them, until he looked like a man carrying a roof he couldn't put down.

"It's not working because you're afraid," Wren said.

"I'm not afraid."

"You are. You do the thing with your mouth, the tight thing, like when the hunters came through the market. Your magic hears your face."

That startled a real look out of him—the first one all day. "My magic hears my *face*."

"Mm. Gran says—" She stopped. Gran was behind them now, small and stubborn on her doorstep, shrinking in the census wagon's dust. Gran was a door already closed. "Gran used to say fear is just prayer with the wrong address."

Coral sat back on his stool. For a moment he looked very tired and very young, this man who had hidden a god inside himself for five years and never once let Wren see the seams. Then he laughed, short and cracked, and rubbed both hands over his face.

"All right," he said. "All right. Fear is prayer with the wrong address." He blew out a breath and squared himself at the bowl of harbor water like a man squaring up to a debt. "Then let's try praying properly."

This time he didn't murmur. This time he *sang*—low, almost below hearing, a sound Wren felt in her sternum rather than heard with her ears, the way you feel thunder deciding whether to be rain. The candle flames leaned toward him. The water in the bowl shivered into rings without being touched. And the salt-stained leaf, lying in its dish of ash, curled slowly upright like a hand opening.

The knots darkened. One by one, the fibers drank shadow, and a smell came off them—not brine, not rot, but something cold and clean, the smell of deep water where light has never been.

"That's it," Coral whispered. "That's—that's Veil-work. That's real." His voice shook. "Five years of knots and wards and little lies, and it still remembers me."

He lifted the bowl with both hands, careful as a man carrying soup to a sickbed, and carried it to Sable's pallet. Wren scrambled back to make room.

"What do I do?" she asked.

"What you've been doing." He knelt, set the bowl on the boards by Sable's hip. "Keep vigil. Talk to her. Sing, if you want." He glanced up, and there was something raw and asking in his face. "The dead listen to you, Wren. Maybe the living do too, if someone sings loud enough."

* * *

Her brother's song was a harbor song, the kind fishermen sang hauling nets, except Tam had only ever known four lines of it and made up the rest, so what Wren carried in her chest wasn't really a song at all but a boy's voice looping four true lines into nonsense and laughter:

*Low tide, high tide, the sea keeps what it loves,*
*the sea gives back what it must—*

She didn't remember learning it. She didn't remember a time before it. It lived in her the way the fog lived in the harbor, and since Tam drowned she sang it into every silence the way other people said his name.

So she sang it now, quietly, kneeling by the pallet while Coral worked. Sable's breath hitched and dragged. The gray weeping from the wound had slowed, but not stopped, and the red ridge had begun to map itself outward in thin dark lines, like rivers on no map Wren knew, climbing toward Sable's heart.

Coral laid two fingers in the bowl of harbor water. He closed his eyes. And Wren felt the room change pressure, the way it did before her ghosts spoke—as though somewhere a door had opened onto a much larger space.

"Veil," he said, and the word came out of him like something he'd been holding in his teeth for five years. "Drowned one. Keeper of the threshold between the breath and the brine. I'm asking. I know I haven't asked in a long time. I know I stopped. I thought you weren't listening, and maybe you weren't, maybe you couldn't—but if any scrap of you can reach this room, reach *her*, she bled for us. She bled for a god she doesn't even believe in. So take the poison. Take it through me. I'll hold the door."

Nothing answered. Of course nothing answered. The candles burned straight, the water lay flat, and Wren's chest ached watching him kneel there offering himself to a silence, the way she used to talk to Tam's grave-marker before she learned she could hear him anyway.

But then—

The water in the bowl turned black.

Not shadowed. Not stirred-up silt. *Black*, ink-black, and cold rolled off it like breath off ice in summer, and the dark lines on Sable's side began to move—backward, drawing in, retreating down the rivers of infection toward the wound as though the whole poisoned delta of her were flowing home to its source.

Sable screamed.

It was a terrible sound, torn out of her, and her hand shot out and gripped Wren's arm hard enough to bruise, and Wren held on and kept singing, louder now, her four true lines and her brother's nonsense after them, because Coral had said sing and she would sing until her throat split.

"Tam," Sable gasped between the waves of it. "There's a—there's a boy—in the water—"

"Yes," Wren said. "Yes. That's Tam. He's mine. Hold on to him."

"He's singing—"

"I know."

"He won't stop singing—"

"No," Wren said, and her eyes were burning and she didn't care, she sang harder, "*low tide, high tide, the sea keeps what it loves—*" "he won't ever stop. That's how you know he's got you."

The black in the bowl thickened, swirled, and Coral's face had gone gray with effort, sweat cutting channels through the grime on his cheeks, his lips moving around words too old for Wren's ears. The wound wept one last thread of gray, thinned to water, thinned to nothing. The red ridge faded from river-map to blush to the angry pink of a hurt that means to heal.

And then it was done, all at once, the way tides turn—all at once and completely. The water in the bowl went clear. Sable slumped, breathing deep and even, actually *even*, for the first time in three days. Coral pitched forward onto his hands and stayed there, shaking, spent.

Wren kept singing. Softer now. A lullaby version. She sang until Sable's grip on her arm loosened into sleep, and a little longer after that, because it seemed rude to stop while anyone might still be listening.

* * *

Coral slept twelve hours sitting up against the wall, the empty bowl tipped over beside him like a finished argument. When he woke, the smuggler's lamp had been trimmed and refilled—by whom, nobody asked—and Sable was propped on one elbow, gray-faced but awake, watching him with an expression Wren had never seen on her before.

"You pulled it out of me," Sable said. "Whatever that was. You pulled the rot out through a bowl of harbor water."

"It's called drawing the tide." Coral's voice was hoarse. "It's hedge-craft, mostly. The Veil part is—" He hesitated. Wren saw him decide to stop lying; she saw it happen, a small straightening behind his eyes. "The Veil part is that I asked, and something answered. First time in five years."

Sable was quiet a long moment. "I spent nine years burning people who could do what you just did."

"I know."

"And last night, in the fever—I heard singing. A boy's voice. And underneath it—" Her jaw worked. "Underneath it there were others. A woman. My mother, maybe, or—someone I—" She stopped. Started again, flatter. "People I sent to the pyre don't get to sing to me. That's not how it should work."

"How does it work?" Wren asked.

"I don't know." Sable looked down at her own hands, turned them over as if checking them for soot. "I always thought the gift was the plague's leavings. That's what they taught us. That's why we burned them—to cauterize. But I heard them, Wren. All night. They weren't accusing me. They were just... singing along."

"They're not haunting you," Wren said. The certainty arrived whole, the way Tam's songs arrived, from some place below thinking. "Ghosts don't sing along. Hauntings are lonely things. If they were singing with me, that means they wanted to be in the room."

Sable stared at her. Then at Coral. Then, unbelievably, she laughed—one dry bark, quickly regretted, her hand pressed to her bandaged side, but a laugh nonetheless. "God help me. The child's giving me absolution by way of folk theory."

"It's not folk theory. It's observation." Wren lifted her chin. "I've been observing the dead since I was six."

* * *

They became a household in the way water becomes a tide: gradually, and then all at once.

It began with meals. There was a rusted iron stove in the cellar's corner, vented through a crack the smugglers swore no smoke escaped by (a lie, but a kindly one), and Wren appointed herself cook because the alternative was sitting still, and sitting still meant counting breaths. She made fish stew out of smoked herring and a sack of wrinkled potatoes and a bottle of pickled things whose label had rotted off, and it was terrible, and Coral ate three bowls of it and declared it the finest meal of his life, which was such an obvious lie that it looped all the way back around to being true.

Sable, who could sit up now for an hour at a stretch, took over the washing because lying in a sickbed while a child cooked for you offended some deep hunter discipline. She scrubbed bandages in seawater and wrung them with her good arm and complained about the soap, the water, the company, and the general state of the world, and Wren learned that Sable's complaining was the same thing as Coral's humming: a sound people make when they're too full to say the actual feeling.

At night they told stories. Not on purpose, at first. Coral mentioned offhand that he'd once sold a luck-knot to a bride who was marrying three brothers simultaneously, and Sable demanded the whole story, and then she owed one back—a huntress tale, sanitized halfway and then less and less sanitized as the night wore on—and Wren traded them Tam stories, the eel-fishing disaster, the time he'd convinced half the dock children that gulls were the souls of unpaid sailors, and the room got warm the way rooms do when the people in them stop keeping watch on themselves.

On the fourth night, Wren woke to voices and lay listening in the dark, expecting an argument.

"—shouldn't have carried her over the spillway," Coral was saying. "You should have left me to fight."

"With what, your luck-knots?"

"With anything. With my life. It's cheaper than yours."

"Don't be a fool. Your life is worth ten of mine. Everyone in this room knows it. Even the girl knows it, and she's kind enough not to say."

"She doesn't think that."

"She thinks exactly that. She counts your breaths when you sleep, boy. I hear her doing it. She thinks you hang the moon over Saltcross, and she's not wrong, from where I'm standing."

Silence. Then Coral, very quietly: "I gave five years to a god who wouldn't speak. I forgot what it was like to be part of something." A pause. "Don't die on me, Vane. I've only just started remembering."

Wren lay in the dark with her heart going hard, and after a while she heard Sable say, rough and low, "Get some sleep, charm-seller," and she smiled into the sail-cloth, and Tam's song rose in her unasked, soft as breath, and this time she hummed it instead of fighting it down.

* * *

On the fifth day, Sable walked to the corner and back without help, sweating but upright, and announced that they'd outstayed the smugglers' charity by two days already.

"We move tomorrow," she said, standing at the foot of the stairs that led up toward the docks, toward the flats, toward the drowned spire Wren had seen breaking the fog. "Tide turns at dawn. Lowest water of the month comes day after. If we mean to walk the tide road, we walk it then."

Coral stood beside her, looking up the stairwell as if he could see through the boards to the sky. "The temple," he said. "You believe it's really there."

"The girl found it. I trust the girl." Sable glanced back at Wren, and something passed over her face—too quick to name, gone before it landed. "More than I trust most living people, these days."

Wren touched the ring at her throat. Beneath her ribs, faint but unmistakable, Tam's song had changed key. It had been pulling her for days now, but today it pulled differently—steadier, sweeter, the way a current pulls when you've finally stopped swimming against it.

*Tide road,* she thought. *Dawn.*

And far off, beneath the fog and the city and five silent years, something vast turned over in its sleep, and the water in the cellar's catching-pail trembled once, gently, like a throat clearing before a song.
