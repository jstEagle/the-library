## 17. Ilex at the Door

Wren heard them before anyone else did.

It was not the boots—though the boots came too, a hundred iron-shod heels striking the drowned stone in a rhythm like a hammer on a coffin lid. It was what walked behind the boots. The dead of Saltcross came up the temple stair the way fog comes through a cracked window, and they clung to the hunters like burrs to a shepherd's coat. Wren had spent her whole life hearing one ghost at a time: her brother Tam singing in the harbor fog, an old fishwife muttering about nets, a drowned child counting stones. She had learned to carry them the way you carry water—one cupful, carefully, without spilling.

But tonight the door at the top of the spire groaned open and every cup she had ever held broke at once.

They poured into her.

She was standing near the altar with Sable's knife in her belt and Coral's salt-leaf pressed flat against her sternum where he'd tucked it for safekeeping, and when the dead came in she went down on one knee on the cold mosaic floor, hands over her ears, which had never once helped and did not help now. The murals overhead—the ones showing men and god sharing breath like two swimmers sharing air—seemed to lean closer, as if the painted figures wanted to hear too.

*They've come,* said Tam's voice, clear and bright and terribly close, closer than he had been since the flats. *Wren. They've come, and they brought their dead with them.*

"I know," she whispered.

The others were still in the heart-chamber below, where the Veil hung in its chains of law-stone, where Coral knelt with his palms bleeding salt and his lips moving around words older than the crown. Sable stood at the chamber's mouth with her wounded side strapped tight, her crossbow loaded, listening to the stairwell the way she used to listen for deer in the Weald—head cocked, breath shallow, all hunter again despite everything.

"They're coming fast," Sable said. "Too fast. Ilex doesn't hurry unless he's certain."

"Then he's certain," Wren managed, rising. Her ears rang. The dead were not voices anymore; they were weather. They pressed against the inside of her skull like a tide against a sea-wall, and beneath the pressure she felt something else, something that had been growing in her since the tidal flats, since the night she first understood that the song in the fog was not haunting but calling.

A purpose. A shape. A door.

Sable looked at her sideways. "Stay behind me."

"No," Wren said.

And it was strange how easily the word came out, how little it weighed, when she had spent eleven years being the smallest thing in every room, the girl who flinched from fog, the child who heard things and lied about hearing them because lying was safer than belonging to herself.

*You're going to do it,* Tam said, and his voice was full of something she had no name for—pride, maybe, or its ghost.

"I'm going to do it," she told him.

* * *

They came up the spiral stair in ranks of four, torchlight climbing the walls ahead of them like spilled honey, and Captain Ilex led them the way winter leads—inevitable, gray, without malice and without mercy. Wren knew his face from posters and pulpit-threats: the long jaw, the burn-scarred cheek, the eyes that never softened because softness was a door and doors let things in. He had hunted her family across three tides and a river weir. He had refused Sable's surrender at the weir and sounded the alarm anyway, and Sable still carried the wound from that day under her bindings.

He stopped at the threshold of the antechamber and looked at the girl standing between him and the altar-stair, and something flickered across his scarred face—not hesitation, exactly. Recognition. Hunters always know a witness when they see one.

"Step aside," Ilex said. His voice was quiet, which was worse than shouting. "No one needs to die tonight except the warlock and whoever stands with him."

"There are four of us," Wren said. "You'll need a bigger list."

One of the hunters laughed—a short, startled bark—and Ilex's hand cut the air and the laughter died. Behind him, torches guttered in a draft that should not have existed this deep beneath the sea, and the murals breathed their painted light, and the dead pressed harder against the walls of Wren's skull, wanting out, wanting in, wanting *witness*, and she understood suddenly, completely, the way you understand falling—that this was why she had been given the gift instead of grief. Not to suffer it. To carry it. To be the cup.

"Last chance," Ilex said.

"You first," Wren said, and opened the door inside herself.

* * *

She did not decide so much as stop deciding. She stopped holding the sea-wall. She let the tide come through.

Every ghost she had ever heard spoke at once.

It began as sound and became something past sound—a chord struck on an instrument the size of the world. The fishwife's net-muttering and the drowned child's stone-counting and the fever-dead of the census wagons and the burned families of the purges, hundreds of them, thousands, all the voices Saltcross had tried to drown beneath edict and ash and silence, and they did not scream. That was the terrible part. They did not rage or wail. They simply *spoke*, each in its own ordinary voice, the voice of someone asking a question at a kitchen table:

*Why?*

*Why did you burn my house with me in it?*

*Why did you sign the ledger?*

*Why did you look away?*

*Why did you tell them where we hid?*

*Why, why, why*—and then the chord changed, and Wren felt it change in her own chest, felt the dead turn from her toward the hunters as if she were only a window and they had finally found the glass—

—and the hunters saw their own dead.

She watched it happen. Watched a young hunter with a pike go white as the temple marble as a woman stepped out of the torchlight wearing his mother's face, or the memory of it, or whatever the dead wear when they need to be recognized. Watched him lower the pike not all at once but by degrees, the way a man kneels. Watched another drop his torch, and the flame died in the seawater film on the floor with a hiss like a whispered name. Watched a third begin, horribly, quietly, to weep, saying *"Da? Da, I was following orders, Da—"*

The dead asked why, and the hunters answered, or could not, and either way the blades came down. One by one. Steel kissing stone. The sound rang through the drowned temple like bells rung backward, like a covenant unsworn, and Wren stood in the middle of it with tears running freely down her face and her arms spread wide, not because she was brave but because the dead needed somewhere to stand and she had made herself the ground.

Only Ilex did not kneel.

He stood in the flood of voices with his sword drawn and his jaw set, and the dead came to him too—she saw them, saw a boy of perhaps ten appear at his elbow, saw the captain's eyes track sideways and fix on nothing and everything—and still his blade stayed level.

"I know what you're doing," Ilex said, and his voice shook only a little, which after everything was almost a kind of strength. "Glamour. Warlock's trickery. Voices in the dark." He raised his voice to his hunters, to the kneeling, weeping, disarmed remnant of his command. "It isn't real. None of it is real. Stand up. Take up your arms. *Stand up.*"

"He's not lying," Wren said softly, and the dead hushed around her words the way water hushes around a dropped stone. "That's the worst part, Captain. It isn't glamour. I don't make anything. I only let you hear what's already there. It's been there your whole life. You just built your law thick enough that you couldn't hear it anymore."

"The plague killed them," Ilex said. "Magic caused the plague. That is the law, and the law—"

"—was written on a stolen god," Wren said. "We found him. He's downstairs. He's been under your feet your whole life, holding up your law-stones, and he never once cursed you. Isn't that worth putting the sword down to see?"

For one moment—one long, salt-heavy moment—Wren thought she had him. The boy-shaped dead at Ilex's shoulder leaned close, and the captain's blade dipped a finger's width, and his eyes, those doorless eyes, finally opened a crack, and what came through was not cruelty but exhaustion, decades of it, a man who had carried a lie so long he had forgotten it was heavy.

Then his face closed like a fist.

"My son," Ilex said, very quietly, "died of the fever-year. Along with four hundred others. And I am told a god let it happen while chained under a city—which means your god is either powerless or cruel, and neither deserves my knee." He leveled the sword at her, at the altar-stair beyond her, at everything. "Move, girl. Or be moved."

*Tam,* Wren thought, *he can't hear him. Why can't he hear him—*

And then she understood, and it broke her heart even as it steadied her arm: Ilex could hear his dead perfectly well. He had simply decided, years ago, that answering them would cost more than silencing them. Some men build walls against ghosts. He had built his whole self into one.

"No," Wren said.

Ilex moved.

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way disasters do—too quick for the dead, too quick for thought. Sable's crossbow sang from the shadows of the antechamber and took a hunter's shield-arm; the man spun and fell down the first steps of the altar-stair. Ilex strode through the bolt's wake like a ship through spray, sword coming up, and Wren did the only thing left, the small brave thing, the true thing: she stood her ground and sang.

Tam's song. The harbor-fog song, the one that had led her across the flats, the one the dead had taught her was older than fear. Her voice cracked on the second line and she sang anyway, loud, ragged, defiant, and every ghost in the temple joined her—not in harmony but in witness, hundreds of dead voices carrying one living child's tune up through the drowned dark, and the murals blazed, and far below, through the floor, through the chains, through the very bones of the mountain, Wren felt something answer.

The Veil was waking.

Ilex faltered mid-stride—not from the song, she thought, but from the feeling underneath it, the pressure of a god turning over in sleep—and in that heartbeat Sable was there, placing herself between blade and girl with her empty crossbow raised like a club, snarling, "Not this one, Ilex. Not this time. I've buried enough of my own sins. I won't bury her."

"Sable Vane," Ilex said, "you're under arrest for treason, desertion, and—"

The temple interrupted him.

The whole spire shuddered. Seawater that had hung obediently outside the walls for a thousand years trembled at the windows of the high gallery, and the law-stone chains far below gave off a sound like a bell cracking, and every torch in the antechamber turned blue. The dead roared—not in anger, in *hope*—and the sound lifted Wren's hair off her neck and filled her mouth with the taste of low tide.

Downstairs, Coral screamed—or prayed, or both; from up here they were the same word.

Ilex recovered first, because cruelty recovers quickly, and drove forward—but the defected hunters moved too. Three of them, the ones whose mothers and fathers had come out of the torchlight, threw themselves bodily into his path, not fighting him, just *holding*, arms locked, faces wet, buying seconds with their careers and possibly their lives.

"Go," the young one with the pike gasped to Wren, over Ilex's shoulder, as the captain raged against them like a storm against a jetty. "Whatever he's doing down there—go help him. We've got the door."

Wren hesitated one instant, looking at Ilex over the wall of men who had once been his, watching the last certainty drain out of the finest hunter the crown ever made as his own command dissolved around him into witnesses.

Then she turned and ran down the altar-stair, toward the chains, toward the god, toward Coral, as above her the temple shook again and the first true crack split the oldest chain, and Ilex's voice followed her down the dark, hoarse and furious and, beneath it all, afraid:

"*Stop them! Stop them, in the crown's name—*"

But the crown's name, Wren thought, taking the steps three at a time, the dead streaming past her like a river running home—

the crown's name didn't mean anything anymore.

Below, in the heart-chamber, the Veil opened its eyes.
