## 20. A New Covenant

Sable Vane had spent eleven years learning to read a crowd for fear, and so she knew exactly what she was walking into.

The provisional council had taken the old customs house for its chambers—the same building where, not three seasons past, she had signed the writs that sent three families to the pyre. The long room still smelled of tallow and old paper, of the particular staleness that gathered in places where men wrote down other men's deaths. But the edicts had come down from the walls. She had watched them fall all week, sheet by sheet, like a slow snow that melted where it landed. Children pulled them down for sport. One old fishwife had used a census notice to wrap her herring, and Sable had nearly wept over a wrapped herring in the middle of the market like a madwoman.

Now the benches were full. Fishers and ropemakers, a midwife with ink-stained fingers, two of the harbor priests who had hidden their vestments in flour barrels for a decade and wore them now with the creases still showing. And in the front row, where she could not avoid seeing them: Coral, with his sea-glass calm, and Wren beside him, the grandmother's ring on its cord clutched in one fist like a talisman, and the child's other hand holding Coral's sleeve as though the whole room might try to take him away.

They had come to watch her testify. She wished, briefly and shamefully, that they hadn't. It would be easier to be hated by strangers.

"Sable Vane." The speaker was Magistrate Corren, a gray woman with a voice like a ledger closing. "You stand to give testimony concerning the binding of the god Veil, the founding of the crown's law, and your own service to the Office of Hunters. You are not compelled. You may refuse."

"I know," Sable said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. Eleven years of delivering verdicts had taught her voice to be steady in exactly the rooms where she least wanted it to be. "I'm not refusing."

She had rehearsed this on the walk over, and then thrown the rehearsal away at the door, because rehearsed words were just another kind of writ—language written down in advance so it couldn't bleed. What she owed this room was the bleeding.

"Eleven years ago I took the hunter's oath," she said. "I believed it. That's the first thing you should hear, because it's the thing that makes the rest possible. I didn't do what I did because I was cruel. I did it because I believed the plague came from the gift, that the old covens had poisoned the wells with spellwork, that every charm was a lit fuse. The crown told us that story so completely that I never once thought to ask who had written it. I burned a family in Marsh Hollow. A man, his wife, a boy of nine. I held the torch myself because my captain said a hunter should never ask another hand to do what her conscience won't." She let that sit. Let the room hear the shape of it. "My conscience wouldn't hold the torch. I held it anyway. That's what the oath made of me."

Somewhere behind her a chair scraped. She didn't turn.

"Two years ago I refused an order. A tanner's family, the father gifted, the children not. I put down my torch and my captain put me on half-rations and desk-work until I drank myself quiet. And then I met a charm-seller in the fish market whose knots were Veil-work, real Veil-work, the kind that hasn't functioned since before I was born, and I understood in one moment that everything I had burned had been alive. That the god hadn't abandoned the faithful. That we had chained Him under the city and called the silence proof of His absence." She looked at Coral. She couldn't help it. "The law of this crown is built on a stolen god. Every statute, every edict, every pyre. I helped feed it. I am here to give it back, and to give you the names of everyone I can remember, and to accept whatever this council decides I owe."

Corren's quill scratched. "Do you ask for mercy?"

"No."

"Do you ask for pardon?"

"No."

The gray woman looked at her for a long moment, the way Sable had once looked at prisoners who refused to lie even when lying would save them—with a kind of unwilling respect, and a kind of pity, and the suspicion that both were useless to everyone.

"Then tell us what happened beneath the temple."

So Sable told them. The tide road and the false trails she'd laid with her old credentials, the weir and Ilex's face when she'd offered her surrender, the fever and the song, the airless halls kept dry by a chained god's last strength, the chains breaking, the law-stones going dark across the city like lamps snuffed at dawn. She told it plainly, without ornament, because ornament was a hunter's trick too—make the story pretty enough and no one counts the bodies. She counted the bodies. She counted them out loud, as many as memory would yield, forty-one names in all, and the room grew very quiet around the names, the way a room grows quiet around a grave.

When she finished, an old man in the third row stood up. Harbor-priest, by his vestment. "My daughter was taken in the second year of the census," he said. "You signed the writ. I've seen the signature."

"Yes," Sable said. "That's my hand."

"I have imagined killing you," the priest said, and the room did not gasp, because half of them had imagined it too. "Every night for two years. I want the council to know that I am not standing here because I have stopped."

"I know," Sable said. "I'm not standing here because I expect you to."

He looked at her a long time, and then he sat down, and that was the whole of it. No absolution. No forgiveness. Just the terrible, ordinary arithmetic of a room full of people who had lost things, hearing the person who had taken them say *I did, it was me, it was my hand*—and finding that even that was not enough, and knowing that nothing would be, and choosing to keep living in the same city anyway. That, Sable thought, was the closest thing to peace the world was going to offer her, and she took it the way you take bread in a famine: gratefully, and knowing it would not fill you.

The council deliberated until the light through the high windows went from gold to copper. When Corren read the judgment, her voice was flat as a tide table: Sable Vane was stripped of rank and title, already gone; her property, such as it was, forfeit to the families of the burned; her sentence, five years' labor in the service of the harmed—rebuilding, recording, testifying, tending the graves of the forty-one—and after that, whatever the city chose to make of her.

"Do you accept?" Corren asked.

Sable thought of her mother's house on the hill, long sold. Of the hunters' barracks, which had never been a home, only a kennel with better weapons. Of the cellar on Tidewater Lane where a leaking roof waited for her hands and a child waited for her songs and a charm-seller waited, patient as tide, to teach her what prayer actually felt like.

"Yes," she said. "Gladly."

And she meant it, and the meaning of it frightened her more than the sentence ever could.

* * *

She found Ilex at the harbor at dusk.

She had known she would—had felt it the way you feel weather coming, a pressure behind the scars. He stood at the end of the long pier where the hunter's launches used to berth, in a coat with the rank-braid torn off, the loose threads still visible at the shoulders like the stubble of something shaved. He was watching the water. The drowned spire was visible at low tide even now, black and patient, and the city had not yet decided what it meant that their god had a body and a prison and a name.

"You came to the temple," Sable said, when she was close enough. "Wren told me. You stood in the door with your blade drawn and half your hunters crying, and you didn't strike."

"Ilex the merciful," he said. His voice was sanded down, all the command worn out of it. "Write that on my stone."

"There doesn't have to be a stone."

"Don't." He turned and looked at her, and she saw what the last weeks had done: the man had been hollowed like a gourd, everything poured out, and what remained was just a tired soldier trying to find the shape of the thing he'd defended. "You don't get to offer me redemption, Sable. You burned your forty-one names and got five years of honest labor and a clean conscience by suppertime. I held the torch at Marsh Hollow too. I held it at every pyre you didn't. And I read the founders' charter last week—all of it, front to back, the parts they never taught us—and I found the clause where they wrote *the god's consent is presumed in perpetuity*. Presumed. They stole a god and wrote *presumed* in the margin, and I spent twenty years enforcing the margin."

The tide came in around the pier pilings, soft as breathing. Somewhere out in the fog, faint and clear, a child's voice was singing—Wren, up on the headland, singing her brother's song to the water, and the water, these days, singing something back.

"What will you do?" Sable asked.

"Walk," Ilex said. "North, maybe. There are towns up the coast that never signed the charter, places where the old prayers went underground instead of out. I know every hunter route in the country; I can spend the rest of my life making sure no one ever builds another pyre on one." He laughed, and it was a broken sound, but it was a laugh. "The council offered me a trial. I asked them what the charge would be, and the clerk read out *crimes against the covenant*, and I thought: they've made a new word for it already. A new law with the old god's name in it. And I thought, I can't sit in another room where men in gray robes decide what I am. So. Walking."

"It won't fix you," Sable said. "The walking. I tried the drink and it didn't fix me, and the walking is just the drink with better views."

"I know." He looked out at the spire one last time. The fog was taking it apart piece by piece, spire first, then the shoulders of the drowned halls. "But maybe a man should have to look at what he defended from the outside of it. From a long way off. For a long time." He straightened, and for a moment the old captain's posture came back, and she saw the young man she'd trained beside, before the pyres, before the plague, before the charter's margins ate them both. "The boy in Marsh Hollow," he said. "His name was Tam. I never said it out loud. Twenty years, and I never once said it."

"Tam," Sable said.

"Tam," Ilex said, and his voice cracked clean through, and he did not hide it, and that, she thought, was the first honest thing the Office of Hunters had ever produced.

He walked down the pier and did not look back, and she watched him go because someone should, because that was the last service she could render him—witness. The fog took him at the harbor mouth the way it had taken the spire, gently, completely, and the water kept singing, and Sable stood on the cold planks until full dark and let herself feel all of it: the grief, the relief, the terrible lightness of a debt she could finally begin to carry instead of merely owing.

* * *

The house on Tidewater Lane smelled of woodsmoke and stewed eel when she got home, and the roof was still leaking, because she'd been meaning to fix it for a week and the city kept needing her testimony instead. Coral had the good lantern lit. Wren was at the table doing sums with a stub of charcoal, tongue between her teeth, the ring on its cord swinging as she bent over the slate.

"Well?" Coral asked. He didn't ask *how did it go*. He asked the only question that mattered.

"Five years' labor. Graves and rebuilding and telling the truth until they're sick of hearing it." She hung her coat on the peg that was, somehow, hers now. "The priest whose daughter I took—he wouldn't forgive me. He wanted me to know he hadn't."

"Good," Coral said.

Sable blinked. "Good?"

"You don't want to be forgiven yet. Forgiveness now would be a roof over a house with no walls." He stirred the pot, unhurried, a man who had learned patience from a silent god and then from a loud child. "Let him hate you honestly for a while. It's the most respectful thing you can give him. And when the hate wears thin—because it will, hate is heavy and he's old—there'll be something underneath it that's true. That's the only foundation worth building on. Ask me how I know."

"You gave a god your name," Wren said, not looking up from her sums. "We know how you know."

"I gave a god my name," Coral agreed, "and got it back from a twelve-year-old who out-bargained Him, which should tell the whole council something about where the real power in this city sits."

Wren grinned, fierce and quick, and went back to her sums, and Sable sat down at the table in her own chair, in her own house that wasn't hers, under a roof that leaked, and felt the sentence settle onto her shoulders—and found, to her astonishment, that it fit the way a tool fits a hand. Not comfortably. *Usefully.* Five years of graves to tend, of names to say out loud, of walls to build for people who had every right to turn her away from their doors. She had spent eleven years tearing down and calling it order. Now she would spend five building, and call it what it was: penance, which is just love that arrives too late and stays anyway.

Later, when Wren was asleep and Coral had banked the fire, Sable climbed through the skylight to look at the stars, and the Veil's presence rose up out of the harbor like warmth off a stone—faint, unhurried, everywhere. It didn't speak. It never spoke to her in words. But she felt it take note of her, the way the sea takes note of every river that comes home to it, and she said, quietly, to the water and the god and the sleeping city all at once:

"I'll do the work. Every day of it. You don't have to forgive me either. Just watch."

And beneath the fog, beneath the spire, beneath the foundations of the law itself, something vast and patient and newly free turned its attention toward her—and did not forgive her, and did not condemn her, but *kept her*, the way the sea keeps what is given to it, and that, Sable Vane thought, going down the ladder to her family, was a covenant too. The new one. The one written not in charters but in labor, in names said out loud, in roofs mended and graves tended and children taught.

She slept without dreaming of fire for the first time in eleven years.

And in the morning there was a knock at the door, and Wren answered it, and came back to the table with her eyes wide and the ring clutched tight, and said: "There's a woman here. She says the temple's ledger is open, and the Veil is asking for the faithful by name—and Sable, she says it's asking for *you* first."
