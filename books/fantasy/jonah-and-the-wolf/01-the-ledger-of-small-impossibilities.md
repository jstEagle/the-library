## 1. The Ledger of Small Impossibilities

The rain came down on the Ledgerhouse like a debt being collected, steady and indifferent, and inside the counting room Lior Hartwell did what she did every morning of her hidden, careful life: she opened the ledger and began to write.

*Item the first,* she wrote in her small, precise hand. *One jar of bottled thunder, provenance uncertain, seller claims it was struck from a storm over the Ashfen marshes eleven summers past. Contents agitated. Do not shelve near the mirrors.*

The jar sat on the counter before her, squat and green-glassed, its cork wrapped in lead foil that sweated little beads of condensation even though the room was cold. Every so often—when a cart rattled past outside, when someone dropped something upstairs—the thunder would stir. It made a sound like a word remembered just at the edge of sleep. *Grmmmm.* A low rolling grumble, muffled through glass and years.

Lior had learned not to flinch at it. That was the first thing the Ledgerhouse taught you: nothing here was to be feared, only *recorded*. Fear was for people who didn't know what they were looking at. She looked at everything.

She dipped her pen again. The ink was guild-made, iron-black, faintly warm to the touch, and it never faded—not from paper, not from skin, if you were careless enough to get it there. She was never careless.

*Condition: intact. Weight: heavier on gloomy days. Valuation: three crowns, or one honest apology from the seller, who has clearly been watering down his storms.*

That last part she allowed herself as a private joke, the kind of joke no one would ever read, because no one ever read her marginalia except her. The Guild of Impossible Things employed forty-one people above ground and nobody knew how many below, and every single one of them believed the cataloguing fell to Master Ellery Voss, Keeper of Acquisitions, whose signature appeared—elegant, looping, entirely forged—at the bottom of every page Lior completed.

It had been her idea, three years ago, or rather it had been survival's idea wearing her handwriting. She had arrived at the Ledgerhouse with rain-soaked shoes, an empty stomach, and a face that made people look through her the way light passes through water. Nobody stopped her. Nobody ever stops a girl like that; girls like that are furniture. So she had sat down at an empty desk in the counting room, opened a ledger that wasn't hers, and begun to work, and by the time anyone thought to ask what she was doing there, she had already been doing it long enough that asking would have been more embarrassing than not asking.

So they didn't ask. That was the arrangement she had built out of other people's indifference, brick by invisible brick: she worked, she was paid a clerk's wage into an account under a name that was almost hers, she slept in the narrow cot behind the map cabinets where the smell of old vellum kept the damp out, and in exchange for this small, dry, predictable life, she asked no questions about anyone and gave no answers about herself.

*Where are you from?* — *South.* (True, technically. Everything is south of somewhere.)

*Family?* — *No one living.* (Also true. Also a wall.)

*How did you learn ledger-craft?* — This one she hated most, because the honest answer was *I don't remember learning it*, and honesty was the one coin she could never spend.

The thunder jar grumbled. Lior blew across the wet ink, set her pen down, and lifted the jar into the crook of her arm to carry it to the shelf where volatile items lived—the shelf reinforced with iron bands and old superstition, both equally effective, neither fully trusted. As she crossed the counting room she passed the tall windows, gray with rain, and caught her reflection in them: a thin young woman of nineteen or twenty, brown hair pinned back with a pencil, sleeves rolled to the elbow, ink stains on her fingers like a second set of fingerprints.

A stranger, essentially. Even to herself, some mornings. Especially lately.

She shelved the thunder between the taxidermied griffin hatchling (Item 4471, *does not age, occasionally blinks*) and the crate of left-handed candlelight (*burns shadows instead of wax; keep away from moths*). The jar settled with a last resentful rumble and went quiet, and Lior stood there a moment in the hush of the storeroom, listening to the rain and the building's slow creaks, feeling the particular loneliness that comes not from absence of company but from absence of witness. There were always people around her. None of them saw her.

*Well,* said the practical voice in her head, the one that sounded like a schoolmistress and kept her alive. *You built it that way.*

She went back to her desk.

* * *

By mid-morning the acquisitions began to arrive, because mid-mornings were when the sellers came—those furtive, hungry-eyed men and women who dealt in things the world had agreed not to notice. They came up from the fog-docks by the river stairs, hooded against the rain though rain hid nothing, and they laid their wares on Lior's counter with the reverence of priests and the greed of pawnbrokers.

The first was a fishwife with salt-cracked hands who sold the Ledgerhouse a key that opened yesterday.

Lior turned it over twice before writing anything down. It was small, brass, unremarkable—except that its teeth were worn smooth on one side and sharp on the other, and when she held it up to the lamplight, the shadow it cast on the counter was of a key much older, corroded green with age, as though the shadow remembered a future the key hadn't reached yet.

"It opens any door you walked through yesterday," the fishwife said. "Only yesterday. Only doors. And only once."

"Opens it to what?" Lior asked, pen poised.

"To yesterday." The fishwife shrugged, as if this explained itself, which Lior supposed it did, in the way of all guild goods: perfectly, uselessly clear. "Stand in the doorway tomorrow—no. Today. Stand in a door today that you stood in yesterday, turn the key, and you're standing there again. Yesterday's you walks out and today's you walks in, and nobody's the wiser but the hinges."

"What happens if yesterday's you sees today's you?"

The fishwife's mouth thinned. "Then yesterday gets crowded," she said, "and I'd want my money back, is what happens."

Lior wrote it all down. *Item the fourth. One key of yesterday, brass, single use per door per day. Caution: do not permit user to encounter prior self. Seller advises refunds unavailable after temporal crowding.* She assigned it a valuation of twelve crowns and a shelf in the deep cabinet, third drawer, next to the compass that pointed at whatever you'd lost most recently—which meant, these days, it mostly spun in confused circles, because Lior had checked, once, experimentally, alone, and it had simply lain flat and dead in her palm, pointing nowhere at all.

She hadn't let herself think about that since. She put the key away and did not think about it now either.

* * *

The morning thickened. A peddler brought a birdcage containing a song that had escaped from a dead man's throat, still singing his favorite tune, and Lior catalogued it with steady hands and did not think about dead men or songs. A nervous apprentice from the Alchemists' Concourse sold them a mirror that showed rooms instead of faces, and swore up and down that the rooms were real somewhere, and Lior catalogued it and privately resolved to check whether any of those rooms contained a cot behind map cabinets, and then decided firmly that she would not.

Master Voss came through at the eleventh bell, as he always did—a round, comfortable man with ink on his cuffs and the serene expression of someone who has never once wondered what he'd do if the world required effort. He glanced at the day's entries, which were of course in his own elegant hand, and nodded approvingly at his own diligence.

"Good work, Voss," he told himself, and patted himself on the shoulder, and left.

This was the ritual. This was the whole architecture of her life: a fraud so gentle, so useful to everyone involved, that it had calcified into simple fact. Master Voss got credit for work he couldn't have done if his life depended on it. The Guild got its ledgers kept in a hand far better than his. Lior got wages, a cot, dry socks, and a door that locked.

And in exchange she got to be no one, which was safety, and no one was also loneliness, which was the price, and she paid it every morning without letting herself count the cost, because she was, after all, very good with numbers.

* * *

At noon the rain eased and the sellers thinned, and Lior took her bread and cheese to the window seat at the end of the counting room, where a gap in the shelves framed a slice of the city below: wet rooftops, chimney smoke bending sideways, the gray ribbon of the river. She ate slowly. From here she could see the market square, where a juggler was failing cheerfully at keeping four balls aloft while a dog stole bread from his basket with the calm professionalism of a guild member himself.

She watched the dog. She watched the juggler laugh when he finally noticed. She watched two children chase the dog and fail, shrieking, delighted, and something in her chest pulled tight as a knot drawn wrong, and she looked down at her bread and found she wasn't hungry anymore.

There was a version of her life, she knew, in which she was one of those children—loud, seen, chased by dogs, loved by somebody waiting at a door. She had no memory of it. That was the strange thing, the thing she circled and never landed on: she had no memory of ever being loved, but she had a memory-shaped ache where such memories ought to be, the way an amputated limb itches. Sometimes a smell would do it—woodsmoke, or bread baking, or the green smell of a storm coming—and for half a heartbeat she'd be certain she was about to remember something enormous, something that would explain the hole in the middle of her, and then the moment would pass, and she'd be sitting on a window ledge eating cheese, and the hole would be as round and perfect as ever.

She had learned, over three years, to treat that hole like an entry in the ledger: *Item unknown. Provenance unknown. Valuation: priceless, presumably, or why does it hurt.*

"You're brooding," said a voice above her.

Tamsin, one of the genuine clerks, stood with a stack of folios in her arms and rain dripping off her braid. Tamsin was nineteen too, loud, freckled, chronically curious—the kind of person who collected other people's business the way Lior collected impossible objects. She was also, inconveniently, kind.

"I'm working," Lior said.

"You're eating bread and staring at a dog with the expression of a widow in a ballad."

"The dog was stealing from him. It seemed worth noting."

"Noted where?" Tamsin craned toward the ledger on Lior's lap, and Lior tilted it away with the ease of long practice. "Right. Your secret marginalia. You know I'll find out what you write in there someday."

"You won't."

"One of these days I'm going to sneak in here early and—"

"Tamsin." Lior smiled at her, a small real smile, because Tamsin deserved real ones even if she got few enough of them. "Go file your folios."

Tamsin hesitated, and Lior saw the question come up in her like a bubble in deep water—the question, *the* question, the one everybody eventually asked and Lior always drowned. *Who are you, really? Where did you come from? Why does the compass point at nothing when it points at you?*

But the bubble broke against the surface and dissolved, the way it always did, because Lior Hartwell was furniture, was wallpaper, was the girl at the desk who was always there and never quite there, and Tamsin sighed and hoisted her folios and went, calling back over her shoulder, "Voss wants the volatile shelf inventoried by evening. Thunder and all."

"Inventoried by evening," Lior echoed, and was alone again.

Alone again. She said it to herself sometimes like a spell, like a ward. *Alone again, safe again.* The words fit together so neatly she'd stopped noticing they weren't the same thing.

* * *

The afternoon passed in entries. A bottle of a sea that existed only in a drowned man's dreams. A pair of gloves that made whatever you touched feel forgiven. An egg, unhatched, that dreamed audibly—and oh, how Lior had wanted to hold that one close and listen to what impossibility dreamed of, but rules were rules, and she recorded it as received, weighed it, shelved it, and only after the seller had gone pressed her ear briefly to the shell, hearing something like wind over high grass, and felt, absurdly, like crying, and didn't, and washed her hands, and moved on.

That was the trick of the Ledgerhouse, she thought, dipping her pen for the hundredth time that day. It was a museum of wonder run by accountants. Everything miraculous arrived here and was immediately reduced to weight, condition, valuation, and shelf. Bottled thunder became *three crowns*. A song from a dead man's throat became *storage fee pending*. Wonder was permitted, but only in the margins.

Which was, she supposed, exactly how she lived.

The light was going amber and low when she came to the last item of the day, delivered by a courier who wouldn't give a name and wouldn't stay, who set a plain wooden box on the counter and left before Lior could ask the standard questions—provenance, seller, terms. Inside, packed in wool, was a stone.

Not an impossible stone. That was the first strangeness: it was just a stone, gray and rough-faceted, the size of a fist, utterly ordinary except that when Lior's bare fingers closed around it—

—the ledger room tilted. The lamp guttered though there was no draft. Somewhere very far away and very deep, something *answered*, a resonance like a bell struck underwater, and Lior felt it not in her ears but in her blood, in her teeth, in that memory-shaped hole in the middle of her, which for one terrible, dazzling instant seemed less like a hole and more like a door.

She dropped the stone back in the box. Her heart was going hard. Her hand, when she looked at it, was steady—her hands were always steady—but her eyes stung, and she didn't know why.

*Item the nineteenth,* she wrote, and her pen shook for the first time in three years. *One stone. Ordinary. Gray. Origin: refused. Effect upon handling: [entry incomplete].*

She sat looking at the bracketed blank space for a long time. Outside, the rain had stopped without her noticing. The light through the windows was failing, and with it, oddly, the light in the sky itself seemed to be failing faster than it should—she frowned at the western pane, where the clouds had torn open over the rooftops, where the first stars should have been pricking through.

The sky over the city was black. Not dusk-black, not cloud-black. Black like a page with the words scraped off it.

And in the storeroom behind her, faintly, through glass and lead foil and three years of careful routine, the bottled thunder began—very quietly—to sing.
