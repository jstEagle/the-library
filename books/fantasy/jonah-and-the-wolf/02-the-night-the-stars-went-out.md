## 2. The Night the Stars Went Out

Lior Hartwell had learned to measure time by the sound of ink drying.

Not by the great clock in the Ledgerhouse atrium—that monstrous brass thing with its thirteen hands and its habit of ticking backward on feast days—but by the small, patient whisper of a page absorbing what was written upon it. Ink took four minutes to dry in winter, three in summer, and an unpredictable span whenever the weather turned strange, which in the city of Corvess it did more often than not. She had catalogued this too, once, in a ledger no one else would ever read: *Ink, drying times of. Variable. See also: patience, the practice of.*

Tonight the ink was taking forever.

She sat at her desk in the Receiving Hall, third window from the left, where the lamplight fell just so across her work and the draft from the old casement kept her awake without quite freezing her. Before her lay the evening's intake: a jar of bottled thunder (second one that month; someone in the Tanners' Row was clearly harvesting storms), half a compass whose needle pointed not north but *backward*, toward places its owner had already been, and a single black feather that weighed as much as a hymnal. These were ordinary impossibilities. The Guild of Impossible Things received forty or fifty of them a night, logged them, shelved them, and forgot them, because forgetting was half of what ledgers were for.

Lior dipped her pen. Her hand hesitated over the line.

*Item: thunder, bottled, one jar. Blue glass, wax seal intact. Hums when approached by persons of guilty conscience.*

She wrote it. The letters came out smaller than usual, cramped, the way her handwriting always went when she was afraid—and she was afraid, though she could not have said of what. It had been building all day, a pressure behind her eyes like weather about to break. The cats in the alley behind the Ledgerhouse had been yowling since dawn. The bottled thunder had begun humming before she even unpacked it. And Master Ellery-Quince, who ran the Night Desk and never noticed anything short of a fire, had looked up at the ceiling twice this evening with a puzzled frown, as if he'd heard something walking around up there in the attic of the world.

"Entry complete?" he called from his station by the great doors.

"Nearly." Lior blotted the page. "The feather's resisting classification."

"Everything resists classification. That's rather the point of the place." He chuckled at his own joke, the same chuckle he'd worn for thirty years like a coat gone shiny at the elbows.

She almost smiled. Almost. Instead she found herself glancing at the tall windows, at the strip of night visible between the velvet curtains the guildmasters insisted on keeping drawn—*stars are distracting to precision work,* Master Corvane had declared years ago, and no one had argued, because no one argued with Corvane—and thought, not for the first time, that she missed them. The stars. She had grown up—wherever she had grown up, which was a question she had learned to stop asking herself—under open sky. She was sure of it the way you are sure of a scar's origin: not by memory but by the shape of the absence.

The guild did not ask questions about its apprentices' pasts. That was one reason she had chosen it, or let it choose her, five years ago, when she'd turned up at its doors with no name anyone recognized and no history anyone could find. The Ledgerhouse accepted the unaccountable. It was, professionally speaking, their whole trade. They had given her a cot in the attic, a desk by the third window, and a life measured in entries and intakes, and Lior had built herself into that life the way water builds itself into the shape of its vessel. Routine was a kind of armor. She wore it every day. Most days she could almost forget it was armor at all.

The first star went out while she was logging the compass.

She didn't see it happen. She felt it—a faint wrongness, like a note dropped from a chord—and then through the gap in the curtains she caught the sky's reflection in the brass of the inkwell, and one point of light that should have been there simply wasn't. Not clouded. Not occluded. *Gone*, the way a tooth is gone from your mouth: a hole with edges, aching.

"Lior," she said to herself, very quietly, because saying her own name sometimes steadied her. "You're being silly."

Then the second star went out, and the third, and the silliness curdled into something cold that slid down her spine and pooled at the base of it.

They went in sequence. She understood that much within moments, watching the inkwell's reflection with her breath held, counting. Not random—sequential, west to east, each disappearance following the last with the terrible regularity of a lamp being snuffed room by room down a long corridor. As if something were walking through heaven, closing doors.

"Master Ellery-Quince," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "You should look outside."

He grumbled, set down his pipe, shuffled to the window, pulled the curtain wide—

And stopped.

For a long moment the only sound in the Receiving Hall was the great clock's backward tick. Then Ellery-Quince said, in a voice stripped of thirty years of chuckling, "Fetch the Duty Bell."

"The—the bell? But that summons the full conclave, that's only for—"

"*Fetch the bell.*"

She fetched it. Her legs carried her down the corridor to the alcove where the Duty Bell hung—a bowl of greenish bronze older than the building around it, engraved with constellations she'd always assumed were decorative—and when she struck it with the bone hammer chained beside it, the sound it made was not a ringing but a *falling*, a deep descending note that she felt in her teeth and in the floor and, absurdly, in the scar-shaped absence of her childhood. Every light in the Ledgerhouse came on at once. Every door unlocked itself. Somewhere below, in the vaults she was not permitted to enter, something vast turned over in its sleep.

By the time she got back to the hall, guildmasters were pouring down the central stair in their nightclothes and half-fastened robes, and the stars were still going out.

Through the open curtains now—no one bothered with them anymore—they watched. A dozen masters, a hundred years of combined arcane scholarship among them, standing in a row like children at a funeral, saying nothing. Lior counted the vanished stars past thirty, past fifty. The gaps in the sky had begun to overlap, merging into wounds. In the east, where the constellation the old books called the Shepherd's Crown hung above the border mountains, there was now only darkness shaped like a crown.

"It's the wards," said Master Ondile, the cartographer, and her voice shook. "The border wards. They're failing. They've been *holding* the—" She stopped herself, lips pressed white.

"Holding the what?" Lior asked.

Every head turned toward her. It was the most attention anyone had paid her in five years, and every ounce of it was horror—not at the sky, she realized, but at her question. At what the question revealed.

"You weren't here," said Master Ondile carefully. "You saw nothing tonight. Do you understand me, child?"

"No," Lior said. "I don't."

Corvane arrived then. The Guildmaster did not run; running was for people who could be seen to fear things. He descended the stair at his customary pace, gray robe buttoned to the throat, silver hair perfect even at midnight, and the crowd parted for him the way water parts for a keel. He looked at the wounded sky for exactly as long as it took to assess it, and then he looked away, and the looking-away frightened Lior more than the darkness had.

"Seal the vaults," he said. "Triple wards on the lower levels. No one enters or leaves the Ledgerhouse until I give the word." His gaze swept the hall, snagged on Lior for half a heartbeat—an assessment, brief and cold as a blade laid flat against skin—and moved on. "The night staff will continue normal operations. Precision calms people. Go."

They went. Panic dressed in obedience looks exactly like order, Lior thought, and then wondered where she had learned such a cynical thing, and filed the wondering away with all the other wonderings she did not permit herself.

* * *

Normal operations lasted another hour, which was longer than Lior expected.

The sky continued to empty. By two o'clock the Shepherd's Crown was entirely dark, and the dark had begun to spread southward along the line of the mountains, and the bottled thunder on her desk had stopped humming and started, very softly, to *whimper*. She logged it anyway. *Item: thunder, bottled. Distressed. Cause unknown.* Her pen scratched in the lamplight, and the scratch of it was the only sane sound left in the world, and she clung to it.

That was when the doors burst open.

No knock. No bell. The great double doors of the Receiving Hall—iron-banded oak warded six ways against everything the guild knew how to ward against—swung inward hard enough to crack one of the bands, and the night came in ahead of whatever had forced them: a wash of cold air that smelled of pine and blood and something wilder underneath, musk and snow and old forests. The lamps guttered. The great clock stopped mid-tick, all thirteen hands frozen.

And the stranger collapsed across the threshold.

He was tall—or he had been, before his knees gave; folded up on the flagstones he seemed made entirely of angles and ruin. A cloak of undyed wool, soaked dark. Hair the color of wet ash falling across a face that might have been handsome once and was currently mostly blood. And beneath the torn hem of the cloak—Lior's eyes went there before she could stop them, and her stomach dropped—feet that were not entirely feet. The left was a man's boot, split open. The right was something else: broad, clawed, furred to the ankle, paw and foot both at once, as if two creatures were sharing one body and disagreeing about it.

"Guards!" someone shouted. "Masters! There's been a breach—"

The stranger lifted his head. His eyes found Lior across the length of the hall—found *her*, out of every soul present, the invisible girl at the third window—as if she were the only lit thing in a dark room. They were amber. They were going gray. They were the color of the sky where the stars used to be.

"Ledger," he rasped, and blood came with the word. "Where is—your ledger. Keeper's daughter. *Where is your ledger.*"

Keeper's daughter.

The words went through her like the bell-note had, down into that scar-shaped hollow, and something in there *answered*. Not memory—she had no memory, that was the whole architecture of her life—but something older than memory, something her body knew the way it knew how to bleed. The hair rose on her arms. The ink in her well trembled though no hand had touched the desk.

"Search him," snapped a voice—Ondile, or one of the other returned masters, crowding in now with wards already glowing at their fingertips. "Restrain him. Corvane must see this. *Corvane must see this.*"

Hands closed on the stranger's shoulders. He didn't fight them; he hadn't the strength left to fight a strong wind. But his eyes stayed on Lior, and in them, under the pain, she saw something that undid her utterly: recognition. Not of her face—he couldn't know her face—but of something in her, some scent or signature, and the recognition was threaded with grief, and with relief, and with a desperate, dying hope.

"*Please,*" he mouthed. No sound. Just the shape of it. Please.

The masters dragged him toward the stairs leading down—to the infirmary, she told herself, or to the holding cells, or to wherever the guild took impossible things it didn't want anyone to see. The doors boomed shut behind them. The lamps steadied. The clock remembered how to tick.

Lior stood at her desk with her pen still in her hand, her heart slamming, the stranger's words ringing in that hollow place.

*Keeper's daughter.*

Slowly, not letting herself think, not letting herself be sensible, she opened her personal ledger—the small black one, the one no one else read—and turned to tonight's entry. The bottled thunder. The backward compass. The feather.

The entry was not what she had written.

Her own handwriting looked back at her, letter for letter identical to hers, and it said:

*Item: stars, missing, fifty-one and rising. Item: wolf, wounded, at the door. Note to self: they will tell you you saw nothing. You are the daughter of a keeper. Remember what they made you forget.*

The last line was not in her handwriting at all.

It was in a fine, slanted script she almost recognized—a script that tugged at her the way a melody tugs when you can't name the song—and it read, simply:

*Run, little star. Before they put out yours too.*

Outside the window, over the black mountains, the fifty-second star went out.
