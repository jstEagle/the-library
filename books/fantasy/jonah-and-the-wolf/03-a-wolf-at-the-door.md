## 3. A Wolf at the Door

The stranger did not wake for three days.

Lior learned to work around him the way one learns to work around a sleeping dog—carefully, with an ear always cocked. Master Ellery had ordered him moved from the entry hall to the infirmary loft above the counting room, a narrow space that smelled of camphor and old linen, and had then, in the manner of guild masters everywhere, washed his hands of the entire affair and gone back to arguing about the wards.

Which meant Lior was left with him.

She told herself it was duty. The Ledgerhouse paid her four coppers a day plus board, and board included seeing that no one died in the lofts, because dead men generated paperwork, and paperwork was *her*. She brought water. She changed the bandages on his side where something—claws, she decided, though she never said so aloud—had torn through cloth and skin alike. She noted his breathing in the margin of her ledger the way she noted everything: *Patient sleeps. Fever steady. Wound weeping less than yesterday.*

It was a lie of omission. His wound was not weeping less. It was weeping *strange*—the blood came away dark at first and then, near the center of the gash, almost silver, like ink mixed with moonlight. She had seen impossible things catalogued every working day for three years. Bottled thunder that hummed before storms. A key that opened yesterday. Salt that remembered being sea. But she had never seen blood behave like that, and she had not written it down, because some part of her understood instinctively that once you entered a thing into the guild's ledgers, it stopped belonging to itself.

So she kept the silver-blood secret, folded small beneath her ribs along with all her other secrets, and she tended him, and she watched.

He was young—or he had been young once; there was something in his face that suggested age measured differently for him, the way a river's age is measured differently than a man's. Dark hair, matted with sweat. Hands that were unmistakably hands, calloused and human, but with nails just slightly too thick, too dark at the quick, as if they couldn't quite decide what they wanted to be. When the fever took him he made sounds—not words, exactly, but half-words, syllables bitten off mid-shape, in a language that made the hairs on her arms rise and the lamp flames lean toward the bed as if listening.

And there was the smell.

That was the thing she could not stop returning to, the thing she turned over in the dark hours after her rounds, lying in her narrow cot in the apprentice wing staring at the ceiling beams. He smelled of pine smoke and wet stone and something wilder underneath, something that had no name in any language she knew—and she *knew* it. Not recognized it. Knew it, the way you know your own name, the way your hand knows the weight of its own shadow. It rose off his skin even through the fever-sweat and the camphor, patient and unmistakable, and every time it reached her it pulled a thread somewhere behind her sternum, a thread tied to—

To what?

To dreams. That was the terrible answer, the one she kept trying to refuse. She dreamed of him. Had dreamed of him, or of someone wearing his scent, for years—dreams she had never told anyone, because telling them would have required explaining them, and explaining them would have required admitting they existed, and admitting they existed would have cracked open the careful little box of her life at the Ledgerhouse and let out everything she'd worked so hard not to look at.

In the dreams she was always running through a forest that did not exist anywhere in the human realm—trees too tall, silver-barked, their leaves whispering in a tongue she almost understood. And something ran beside her. Not chasing. *Beside.* Matching her stride for stride, keeping pace with a loyalty so fierce it felt like a kind of love, and she never saw its face, only caught glimpses—a shoulder, a flash of amber eye, the sound of breath that was both animal and laughing—and always, always, the scent of pine smoke and wet stone.

Three years she had filed those dreams under *nonsense*, the way she filed everything inconvenient. Nonsense was safe. Nonsense didn't require explanations about who Lior Hartwell was before she'd appeared at the guild's gates at fourteen with no history and no family and a memory that started abruptly, like a ledger missing its first hundred pages.

Now the nonsense was lying three feet away from her, bleeding silver, smelling like home.

* * *

On the third night, the fever broke.

She was there when it happened—she was always there, though she'd stopped pretending this was accidental even to herself. One moment he was thrashing, muttering those bitten-off syllables, the lamp flames bending toward him like courtiers; the next, stillness rolled over him like a tide going out, and his breathing slowed and deepened, and his eyes opened.

Amber. Of course they were amber. She might have known.

They fixed on her immediately, sharp and unclouded despite three days of fever, and she felt herself weighed in them—measured, assessed, catalogued right back, and there was something in the precision of that gaze that made her straighten her spine without deciding to.

"You," he said. His voice was ruined gravel, barely more than breath. "You're the one who smells like—"

He stopped. His nostrils flared. Lior watched something move across his face that she had no name for—recognition, yes, but recognition with teeth, recognition that hurt, like touching a scar you'd forgotten you had.

"Like what?" she asked. Her voice came out steadier than she felt.

He didn't answer. He pushed himself up on one elbow—the movement cost him, she saw it in the tightening around his eyes, the fresh dark bloom on his bandage—and looked at her properly, head tilting, and the gesture was so purely, so *caninely* curious that it should have been absurd on a man's face and instead was not absurd at all.

"You've been tending me," he said.

"The guild pays me to keep people alive. Dead men generate paperwork." She lifted the water cup from the bedside table. "Drink. Slowly."

He took it, but he didn't drink. He held it, watching her over the rim, and said, "You wrote things down while I slept. I heard the pen."

"It's my job."

"What did you write?"

"That my patient is a trespasser found bleeding at the gates during a ward-collapse, identity unknown, carrying—" She stopped.

"Carrying what?" Something in his voice had gone very quiet and very still, the stillness of a thing deciding whether to be afraid.

Lior set down the pitcher. She crossed to the shelf where she'd put his belongings—the guild had confiscated them, naturally, though they'd been returned to her custody pending inventory, which meant pending her, which meant they were effectively hers to guard. A knife of black metal. A cloak that seemed to drink the lamplight. And wrapped in oilcloth, because she hadn't been able to bring herself to let it touch anything else:

The stone.

Even now, through two layers of wrapping, she could feel it. A faint warmth against her palms when she carried it. A faint pulse, slow as a sleeping heart. And since the second day, a strangeness worse than warmth: whenever her bare skin came near it, the shattered edges *ached*, a deep harmonic ache, like a struck bell heard through walls, like something calling a name it had forgotten how to pronounce.

She set the bundle on the bed between them.

"Fen," he said suddenly.

She blinked. "What?"

"My name. You'll want it for your ledger." A ghost of something that wasn't quite a smile. "Fen. Just Fen."

"Lior," she heard herself say. "Just Lior."

His ears—his ordinary, human-shaped ears—seemed to prick. "Lior," he repeated, tasting it. And then, softer, almost to himself, in that same bitten-off language from his fever, a word that sounded nothing like Lior and yet somehow meant her; she felt it mean her, the way the stone's pulse meant her.

Then he unwrapped the oilcloth.

The covenant-stone lay in the lamplight, and up close Lior could finally see what she'd only guessed at: it had been whole once, a shard of night sky the size of a fist, shot through with veins of light that had gone dark and cold. Now it was broken into five great pieces bound together by cracks of empty air, and the broken faces glittered like geode crystal, and along one of the fractured edges—

Along one edge, the light was not dead.

It flickered. Faint, guttering, but alive, a thread of silver-blue fire running through the crack like a vein remembering blood. And as Lior leaned closer—as her breath touched it—the vein brightened.

Fen went rigid.

"Move back," he said.

"Why? What does it—"

"*Move back.*"

But she didn't. She couldn't. The ache in the stone's broken edges had become a song, and the song had become a pull, and her hand was rising toward it with the helpless inevitability of water finding its level. The silver-blue light flared, spread, raced along the crack-lines toward her fingers, and Fen lunged—too slow, hampered by the wound—and her fingertips brushed the largest fragment.

The world *rang*.

Not sound. Something older than sound, something in the bones and the blood and whatever part of a person remembers being made. Lior saw—felt—knew, all at once and far too fast: a forest of silver bark. Two thrones. An altar that never slept. Ink flowing backward off a page. A woman's hands, her mother's hands, writing in a ledger by starlight, and the stars leaning down to read over her shoulders—

She came back to herself on the floor. She didn't remember falling. Fen was kneeling beside her, gripping her shoulders, saying her name—not the wolf-word, her name, *Lior, Lior*—and his face was white with something beyond pain.

"What did you see?" he demanded.

"I don't—" Her own voice sounded distant. "What *is* it?"

"A covenant-stone." He said it like a confession. "One of three. They bind the border between this realm and mine. Or they did." His jaw tightened. "Two nights ago the wards failed. The stars began going out. This stone broke, and I ran, and I ran here, because—" He stopped. Started again. "Because it wanted to come here. It dragged me across half the world like a fish on a line, and I thought it was mad grief, a dying relic pulling toward its own grave. But it wasn't pulling toward a grave."

His hands tightened on her shoulders. Up close, his amber eyes had flecks of green in them, like sunlight through leaves, and she hated that she noticed.

"It was pulling toward *you*," he said. "It resonates with your blood, Lior Hartwell. Which should be impossible. Only covenant-keepers' blood answers the stones, and the last keeper died fifteen years ago."

The room tilted. Somewhere below, distantly, the guild's night-bell rang the hour, indifferent and ordinary, and Lior clung to the ordinariness of it like a rail in a storm.

"My mother died fifteen years ago," she said quietly.

Fen went very still. The lamplight bent toward them both, and outside the infirmary window the sky hung starless and black, darker than any night she had ever known, and in the silence between heartbeats Lior felt the truth arrive ahead of its explanation—the way you feel rain coming, the way you know, before the letter is opened, that it carries bad news.

"Say her name," Fen said softly. "Your mother. Say it and see if the stone answers."

"I don't—" Her throat closed. "I don't remember her name."

She heard the words leave her mouth and heard them for what they were: insane. Unthinkable. A daughter who did not know her own mother's name, and had never once, in three years of meticulous record-keeping, noticed the gap.

Fen's expression changed. The wariness remained, but underneath it something else surfaced—pity, and beneath the pity, a dawning, grim understanding.

"Then we have a problem bigger than a broken stone," he said. "Someone has been editing you, Lior Hartwell. And I think I know who keeps the pen."

Below them, boots were climbing the stairs. Many boots. Moving fast.
