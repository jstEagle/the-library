## 5. Ink That Remembers

The Ledgerhouse never truly slept. Even in the dead hours between the last bell and the first, ink dried with a sound like breathing, and the shelves settled their weight the way old men settle into chairs—slowly, complainingly, as if they might yet change their minds about staying.

Lior Hartwell sat at her desk in the third-floor scriptorium with a candle burning low and a pot of iron-gall ink going thick at its edges, and she did not know that her life was about to come apart along its seams. She only knew that the entry before her refused to make sense.

*Item 447: One jar of bottled thunder, cracked at the seal, contents diminished by half. Condition: volatile. Provenance: unknown.*

She had written it three days ago. She remembered writing it—the smell of the jar's wax seal, the way the glass had shivered against her palm like something alive and sulking about being shelved. She had a gift for remembering entries. It was, if she was honest with herself, the only gift she had ever bothered to cultivate: ten years in the Guild of Impossible Things, cataloging the cataloged, and she could recite whole ledgers back the way other people recited prayers.

But the entry on the page was not the entry she remembered.

Her hand had been different. That was the first thing. The scriptorium taught a house hand, all disciplined loops and level rules, and Lior's version of it was small and cramped, letters huddled together like children afraid of the dark. The words before her now were written large, confident, with a slight forward slant—and a peculiar habit of crossing the letter *t* with a single sharp stroke, left to right, like a blade drawn fast.

She knew that stroke. She had seen it every day of her childhood, on shopping lists and margin notes and one half-finished letter to a father whose face Lior could no longer summon.

It was her mother's hand.

Lior sat very still and let the candle gutter, because moving felt like the sort of thing a person did when they believed the world still held its shape.

*You're tired,* she told herself. *You've slept badly since the stars went out. Everyone has.* Four nights now, the sky over Grayhollow had hung bare and black where the constellations should have been, and half the city walked around looking like mourners who hadn't been told who died. Of course her mind was playing tricks. Of course her own handwriting looked strange to her. Grief did that. Fear did that.

Except it wasn't grief or fear that made the next thing happen.

It happened while she watched. The ink moved.

Not flowing—*rewriting*. The confident strokes dimmed and faded like breath off glass, and beneath them, rising up through the paper as though it had always been there and had merely been waiting for someone to look properly, came another line in a cramped and huddled hand:

*Item 447: One jar of bottled thunder, sealed. Contents intact. Do not open. — L.H., Keeper.*

Lior knocked her chair over getting up from it.

The sound rang through the empty scriptorium and came back to her wrong—too loud, too slow, as if the room were listening and disapproving. She stood with her back against the shelf of *Class C: Improbable Textiles* (a coat with too many sleeves; a scarf knitted from yesterday) and stared at the ledger until her eyes watered.

*Do not open. — L.H., Keeper.*

Keeper. Her mother had been many things—a seamstress, officially, though Lior had long suspected the seamstress story was the thinnest kind of lie; a woman who hummed in a minor key while she worked; a woman who had died eleven years ago of a fever that no one else in Grayhollow caught. But keeper? Keepers were guild masters, wardens, people with offices and seals and the right to walk into the vaults below without an escort. Keepers were not women in a garret above the tanneries who mended cloaks for coppers.

And L.H. Lior Hartwell. Her own initials. Written in what looked, now that she dared approach again, exactly like her own hand—but dated fourteen years ago, when Lior had been nine years old and could barely write her own name without smearing it.

The candle flame bent sideways. There was no draft.

She should fetch someone. Master Ellery kept late hours in the Archive of Disputed Claims; Corvane himself was said to work by candlelight more often than by sun. There were procedures for anomalous documents—she had filed enough of them. Seal the item in red vellum, note the anomaly, deliver to the second floor, ask no questions you aren't entitled to answers to.

Instead she turned the page.

* * *

The ledger was hers. That much she verified with the desperate thoroughness of a woman checking whether the floor is really there. The binding stamp matched the batch issued to junior clerks eight years ago; the marginalia was her own—the little tally marks she used for condition grades, the doodled key in the corner of Item 312 because she'd been bored. Every page she had personally entered was there, in her hand, exactly as she remembered.

Except that some pages were not.

Between Item 502 and Item 503 there was a gap. Not a torn-out page—the guild punished defacement of records with a week in the dark cells—but a page that had been written on and then *unwritten*, the paper smooth and faintly gray, holding the ghost-shape of lines that had once existed. She tilted it toward the light. Nothing. She breathed on it, the trick the restoration clerks used for faded receipts. Still nothing.

But her fingers knew. She couldn't have said how, any more than she could have said how she knew rain was coming before the clouds formed—a pressure, an ache in the pads of her thumbs, as if the page remembered being touched by her and missed it.

There were four such gaps. Then seven. Then, in the earliest section of the ledger, dating back past her apprenticeship to a time she had no memory of ever holding this book at all, the gaps stopped being gaps and became something worse.

They became entries.

*Entry 1: Received into keeping, one daughter, aged six years. Terms of custody per arrangement of M. Hartwell, Keeper, deceased. Conditions attached. Conditions sealed. To be opened upon the subject's twentieth year, or upon failure of the wards, whichever comes first.*

Lior read it four times. Each time the words rearranged themselves slightly, like a face seen through warped glass, and each time they said the same terrible thing in the same calm bureaucratic voice.

*Aged six years.* She had come to the Ledgerhouse at thirteen. That was the story she had told herself so long it had worn smooth as river stone: orphaned at twelve, a year on the streets, taken in by the guild's charity intake because she had neat handwriting and no family to object. She could remember the streets. She could remember the intake clerk, a sour woman named Mistress Pell who had smelled of vinegar and had pronounced Lior "adequate." She could remember her first day in this very scriptorium, the smell of the ink, the enormous windows going violet with dusk.

She reached for the memory of the year before—the tannery garret, her mother's humming—and found it strangely shallow, like a pond that looks deep until you step in. She could remember the humming. She could remember a kitchen with a crooked blue tile by the stove. But when she tried to remember her mother's *face*—her own mother's face, the face every child is supposed to carry carved behind their eyes forever—she got a blur. A warm brown blur with hands attached. Hands she remembered. A face she did not.

Who forgets their mother's face?

Nobody. Nobody forgets. Unless—

Unless someone took it.

The thought arrived whole, uninvited, and once it arrived it would not leave. Lior stood in the empty scriptorium with her arms wrapped around herself, and the vertigo came up through the floorboards and through the soles of her feet and into her teeth, because if a memory could be taken—if the record inside her head could be edited as casually as a clerk strikes a bad line—then which parts of her were original text? Which parts were forgery? She had spent ten years trusting her own recall the way other people trusted the ground. Now the ground was paper, and someone had been writing on it.

*Conditions attached. Conditions sealed.*

Her hand, almost without her permission, went to her collarbone. There was a scar there, thin and white, a hairline crescent just below the hollow of her throat. She'd had it as long as she could remember—which was precisely nothing, she realized now, precisely no time at all—and she'd assumed, the way you assume things about your own body, that it meant nothing. A childhood fall. An accident nobody mentioned.

A seal.

"Hello?" Her voice came out cracked and too small for the room. "Is anyone—"

The candle went out.

Not guttered. *Out*, all at once, the way a candle goes out under a snuffer, and in the sudden dark the ledger began to glow. Faintly. A cold silver luminescence rising off the ink like moonlight off snow, and Lior saw—or dreamed she saw, she would spend years deciding—that every gap in the ledger filled itself at once. Ghost-lines everywhere, hundreds of them, thousands, the whole book suddenly dense with writing that had been hidden in plain sight, and among the ghost-lines, moving, a single phrase surfacing again and again like a body refusing to stay drowned:

*the wolf remembers. the wolf remembers. the wolf remem—*

The door to the scriptorium opened.

Lior slammed the ledger shut on the light. It leaked out around the edges anyway, a seam of silver, and she stood clutching the book to her chest like a child hiding stolen bread, blinking against the lantern-glow from the corridor.

Master Ellery stood in the doorway, nightcap crooked, spectacles pushed up into his wild white hair. He was seventy if he was a day and he had the mildest disposition of any man in the guild, which was why Lior's heart chose that moment to attempt escape through her ribs.

"You're awake," he said, unsurprised, peering at her. "Good. Good. Thought I heard—" He paused. His gaze dropped to the ledger, to the silver seam glowing between her arms, and something crossed his mild old face that was not mildness at all. It lasted less than a heartbeat. Then he blinked and it was gone, and he was only a sleepy old archivist again, patting his pockets for spectacles he was already wearing.

"Heard the shelves," he finished. "They groan at night. Old buildings do."

"The stars," Lior said. Her mouth was dry. "Master, the stars went out four nights ago, and tonight my ledger started—"

"Yes," Ellery said. "I expect it did."

He said it the way a man says *it's raining*. No alarm. No wonder. And that—more than the ghost-ink, more than the impossible dates, more even than the missing face of her mother—was the thing that turned Lior's blood to well-water. Because Ellery had been the intake clerk's superior. Ellery had signed her apprenticeship papers. Ellery had been *kind* to her for ten years, kindly and consistently, the way a man tends something planted.

"How long," she whispered, "has it been doing that?"

Ellery sighed. He came into the room slowly, joints crackling, and set his lantern down on a desk with the unhurried care of a man who had all the time in the world and wanted her to know it. "Child," he said, "there are ledgers in this house older than the kingdom. Paper remembers what people are made to forget. That's rather the point of paper." He held out his hand. "Give me the book, and we'll say no more about it. You've had a fright. Sleep will mend it."

Every instinct Lior had honed in ten years of being invisible—of watching, of noting, of keeping her head down and her observations to herself—screamed at her not to let go of that ledger. She hugged it tighter. The silver seam pulsed against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

"It's my ledger," she said. "My assignment. My hand."

"It's guild property," Ellery said gently, "as are you."

The word landed harder than he could have known. *As are you.* He probably meant nothing by it—probably meant only that apprentices belonged to the house that fed them, an old formula, a joke even. But standing there in the dark with a book full of her mother's handwriting pressed against her chest and a scar at her throat shaped like a broken seal, Lior heard the sentence the way the ledger would have recorded it, in cold and level ink:

*Received into keeping, one daughter, aged six years.*

"I'll take it to Master Corvane myself," she said. "In the morning. He'll want to see it. Won't he?"

Something flickered behind the old man's spectacles—calculation, quick as a fish turning. "Corvane," he repeated. "Yes. Yes, I suppose he would want to see it." He retrieved his lantern. At the door he paused, and did not turn around. "Lior. When you sleep tonight—and you should sleep—dream of nothing. Some doors are easier closed from the outside."

Then he was gone, and his lantern-light with him, and Lior stood alone in the dark scriptorium with a glowing book and a heart full of splinters.

* * *

She did not go to her bunk. She took the ledger and a stub of candle down to the reading carrel in the farthest corner of the map-room, where the guild kept charts of places that no longer existed, and she locked the carrel's little gate behind her—an antique lock, but locks were locks—and she opened the book to the first page and began, methodically, with the discipline of ten years' training, to build an index of everything that was wrong.

It was better than panic. Panic was a luxury for people whose selves were intact.

*One:* the ledger contained two texts. The visible text, hers, ordinary. And beneath it, surfacing since the night the stars went out, a second text in hands that were not hers—her mother's chiefly, but others too, older hands, stranger alphabets that resolved into common script only when she wasn't quite looking directly at them.

*Two:* the visible text was a forgery in places. Not false entries—true ones, accurate ones, items she genuinely remembered cataloging—but *incomplete*, pruned, each true entry sitting where a larger true entry used to be, the way a stump sits where a tree stood. Someone had cut her record down and left the grain showing.

*Three:* she had gaps. This was the entry she wrote last, pressing hard enough to tear the page, because writing it down was the only thing that kept it real: *I cannot remember my mother's face. I cannot remember arriving here. I cannot remember the years six through twelve. I remember streets I may never have walked and a fever I may never have had. My life begins at thirteen and everything before it is a room with the furniture draped in sheets.*

*Four:* the phrase *the wolf remembers* appeared forty-one times in the hidden text. Forty-one. She had counted twice. And beside the thirty-first occurrence, in her mother's sharp left-to-right stroke, someone had added a single line that made the candle seem colder than it was:

*If you are reading this, little bird, then the wards have failed, and they will come for you the way they came for me. Trust the ledger. Trust the stone. Do not trust the Guild. Above all—do not trust the man with the ledger-ring.*

Lior read that line until she had it by heart. Then, because she was her mother's daughter whatever else had been done to her, she did the most dangerous thing she could think of.

She answered.

She dipped her pen. She turned to the empty space beneath her mother's warning, and in her small cramped hand she wrote: *Who are you? Who am I? What did they take?*

The ink sank into the page and vanished, swallowed whole.

For a long moment, nothing. The map-room ticked and settled around her. Somewhere far below, in the vaults, something heavy shifted its weight—she chose to believe it was a cart.

Then, across the bottom of the page, in ink that rose up silver-bright and wet, her mother's hand replied:

*I am sorry. There isn't time. They know the book is awake—you must feel it already, the pulling, the attention. Listen to me: the man with the ledger-ring wears it on his right hand. Count the rings. You have seen him every day of your life and never counted the rings. Go and count. And little bird—when you find what I hid in your memory, forgive me. It was the only place they would never think to search. They never look at what they consider invisible.*

The silver faded. The page was blank except for Lior's three questions and one answer that changed the temperature of the world.

She sat shaking for the length of ten slow breaths. Then she snuffed the candle, tucked the ledger into her shirt against her skin, and slipped out through the sleeping corridors toward the masters' wing, repeating the instruction under her breath like a charm: *count the rings, count the rings, count the rings.*

Guildmaster Corvane kept his office on the second floor, and his door, famously, was never locked—*a house of records has nothing to hide*, he liked to say, smiling his patient smile. Lior had delivered documents to that office a hundred times. She knew the smell of it, pipe-smoke and sealing wax; she knew the tall window and the model of the Ledgerhouse in its glass case; she knew Corvane's habits, his hours, his gentle voice asking after her health in that way he had that made invisibility feel almost like warmth.

What she had never done—what no apprentice did—was look at his hands.

His office was empty at this hour, or seemed to be. Moonless dark pressed against the window. On his desk lay the night's correspondence, and atop it, gleaming faintly, a ring of keys left out as if the world had arranged itself for her convenience.

Lior crept to the desk. Her heart was so loud she was certain it would wake the whole floor. And there, on the ring of iron keys, woven among them like a thread of midnight, hung a chain of rings—not key-rings, but *rings*, bands of dark metal, dozens of them, sliding freely along the loop, each one etched so fine she had to hold it to the window's starless light to read the engraving.

Each band bore a name.

Hartwell, M. Her breath stopped. Hartwell, M.—a covenant-keeper's ring, the size and shape of the others, hanging on a bureaucrat's key-chain like a trophy tooth.

And below it, fresh-cut, the metal still bright at the edges, waiting: a new blank band. Unengraved. Sized, she noticed with a sickness that reached all the way down through every edited year of her life, for a slender finger.

For hers.

Behind her, without any sound at all, the office door clicked shut.
