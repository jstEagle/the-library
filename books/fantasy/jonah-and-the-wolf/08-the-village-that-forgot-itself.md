## 8. The Village That Forgot Itself

The road to Saltmere wound down through hills that had forgotten how to be wild. Fen smelled the wrongness before he saw it: a hush like snowfall, like the moment after a kill, when everything holds its breath. But nothing had died here. Nothing had bled. The silence was simply *empty*, the way a den is empty after the pack has gone.

He kept Lior behind him without deciding to. It was instinct older than his exile, older than his shame—the body moving to shield before the mind consents. She noticed, of course. She always noticed. She said nothing, only shifted her satchel higher on her shoulder and walked with her chin up, as if being protected were an insult she was choosing to forgive.

"Smoke," she said.

"I smell it."

"But no one tending it." She was right. A thin grey thread rose from a chimney ahead, straight and unattended, the smoke of a fire someone had lit and then wandered away from. "That's not right, is it? Nobody leaves a fire."

"Nobody human," Fen said, and regretted it at once, because her eyes went sharp and wounded in that particular way she had, the way that made him want to take words back like stolen meat.

They crested the last hill together, and Saltmere opened below them like a book whose pages had been soaked in water—all the ink run, all the letters blurred into shapes that almost meant something.

It was a good village. That was the first thing he understood, and the understanding hurt. Thatched roofs mended with care. Gardens weeded to their edges. A well with fresh flowers twined round its posts, though the flowers had begun to brown because no one had replaced them. Laundry hung on lines between houses, shirts and shifts turning slowly in the wind, washed by hands that could no longer remember why they'd washed them or whose they were.

And everywhere, people. Standing in doorways. Sitting on fences. Walking the lanes in slow, aimless loops like animals pacing a too-small pen. Their faces were the worst part. Not vacant—Fen had seen vacancy in the wolf-realm's broken ones, and this was not that. These faces were *searching*. Every person in Saltmere wore the expression of someone who has walked into a room and forgotten what they came for, except the room was their whole life, and there was no leaving it.

A man stood in his garden holding a hoe, working the soil in slow, methodical strokes. He had planted nothing. The rows he turned had already been turned that morning; Fen could see the same footprints pressed over and over, deeper each time, like the grooves a fox wears circling a snare.

"Lior," Fen said quietly. "Stay close. Don't tell them your name."

"Why not?" she whispered back.

"Because I don't think they'd know what to do with one. And neither would you, if you heard yours come back to you from a stranger's mouth."

She frowned at him but obeyed, and something in her obedience sat strangely warm in his chest. He did not examine it. There was no time to examine anything anymore; since the stars had gone out, examination was a luxury of a world that still had a sky.

They moved down the lane slowly. A woman knelt by her doorstep churning butter in a bowl with no cream in it, her hands moving in the old rhythm while her eyes drifted over the rooftops. Two children sat on a wall playing a game with stones—and this nearly undid him—because they played it correctly. Hands remember longer than heads. They tossed and caught and chanted a counting rhyme, and every so often one of them would stop mid-verse, mouth open, waiting for a word that had been taken, and then begin again from the top, hoping the missing piece might have grown back.

Fen had broken things in his long life. He had shattered a covenant and let a border bleed. He knew what ruin looked like when it came with teeth and fire. This was worse. This was ruin with the furniture left standing. This was a theft so complete the victims didn't know they'd been robbed—they only felt the shape of the hole, the way an amputee feels weather in a missing limb.

"They don't know," Lior breathed beside him. "They don't know what happened to them."

"No."

"But they *feel* it." Her voice cracked on the word. "Look at them. They're all grieving something and they can't even—" She stopped. Pressed her fist to her mouth. When she spoke again her voice was carefully level, the levelness itself a kind of grief. "Is this what happens when the wards fall? Is this what's coming for everyone?"

"This is what comes when the covenant starves," Fen said. "The pact wasn't just a wall between realms. It was a promise, and promises are how humans keep themselves whole. Take the promise away and..." He gestured at the churned garden, the dry churn, the stone-game children. "*This.*"

They passed the well. An old woman stood beside it, very still among all the drifting people, the way a stone stands still in a river. She was small and bent and dressed in mourning black gone soft with years of washing, and her hands were folded on her cane with terrible patience, as if she had been waiting a long time for something and intended to go on waiting until the waiting itself became the thing.

As they drew level with her, her head came up.

Her eyes found Lior.

Fen saw it happen—he saw the whole catastrophe arrive in the old woman's face like weather. The blank searching look filled, and filled, and overflowed, and suddenly there was a person behind her eyes where there had been only fog, and the person was *weeping*.

"Oh," the old woman said. "Oh. *There* you are."

Lior froze. "I'm sorry, madam, I think you've mistaken—"

"You've been away so long." The woman's voice climbed, cracked, climbed again. She reached out with both hands, trembling, and took Lior's face between them, and Lior—foolish, soft-hearted Lior, who flinched from touch like a cat from water—did not pull away. "I told them you'd come back. I *told* them. Everyone said the fever took you, everyone said give up, give up, but a mother knows. A mother knows her own."

"I'm not—" Lior's voice was very small. "I'm not your daughter."

But the old woman only laughed through her tears, the way the old laugh at the young, certain of the world's foolishness. "Always joking. Even as a little one, always the jokes. Come inside, love. I've kept your room. All these years I've kept your room exactly as it was, in case, in *case*, and now—" She stopped. Her thumbs stroked Lior's cheekbones, and something in her face flickered, some deep animal part of her that memory cannot reach catching a wrongness. "Your hair is different," she murmured. "You've cut it. You never used to wear it short. And your hands—" She turned them over, frowning at the ink stains, the calluses. "These aren't... these aren't kitchen hands."

"*Mother*," said a voice from the doorway behind her, and a young man stepped out—a big, gentle-faced young man with flour to his elbows, who had surely been baking bread he couldn't remember why he was baking. He put his hand on the old woman's shoulder. "Come away from the road now. You know what Dr. Ellery said."

"He said keep to the house," the old woman agreed placidly, not letting go of Lior. "But my daughter's come home, so the doctor can write whatever he likes."

"Mam, she's a traveler." The son's voice was kind and infinitely tired. "You do this most days now. Most days it's the miller's girl. Yesterday it was the reeve's wife when she was young. You'll be confused again in a minute and it'll hurt worse, and I'll have to sit with you through it, and I don't mind, Mam, I don't mind, but—"

"I am *not* confused." Fury flashed in her, brief and bright as a struck match, and then guttered just as fast, and what was left underneath was so nakedly lost that Fen had to look away. "I'm the only one in this whole village who isn't. Someone has to remember her. If I stop remembering her, who will she be?"

The question dropped into the lane like a stone into a well, and Fen heard it land at the bottom of himself, where it would stay.

*If I stop remembering her, who will she be?*

He thought of the ledger-girl beside him, who had been edited once already, whose mother had carved pieces out of her past with who-knew-what intent, and who did not yet know the size of the hole she carried. He thought: *this is the warning. This is what the gods are showing us, walking us through this town like a lesson written in flesh.* Because the altar would demand payment too. Everything in him that understood covenants understood that much. And Lior had more to lose than she knew, and less idea than anyone alive of how much of her was already spent.

The old woman was still holding Lior's face. Lior was crying silently, tears running straight down past the woman's fingers, and doing nothing to wipe them, and doing nothing to step back.

"What was her name?" Lior whispered. "Your daughter. What was her name?"

"Brenna." The word came out worn smooth as a river stone from handling.

"Brenna," Lior repeated. "Tell me about her. While you hold on. Tell me about Brenna."

And the old woman *told*. It poured out of her like water finding a crack—Brenna who sang off-key on purpose to make her brother laugh; Brenna who could calm a spooked horse with a hum; Brenna who went to the coast for work one winter and the fever came through the harbor town and the letter came after. The son stood by with his head bowed. The wind turned the laundry on its lines. And Lior stood in the road of a stranger's grief, wearing a stranger's face, receiving a stranger's memories like a gift, like a wound, and Fen watched her understand—watched the comprehension move through her body like cold water—that memory is not a possession. It is a person. To lose it is to lose the dead twice, and the living endlessly.

When the story ran out, the old woman's grip gentled. The confusion was returning; Fen could see it rising in her like evening fog. "You will write," she said to Lior, urgent, gripping her wrists now. "You'll write when you're settled. You always forget to write."

"Yes," Lior said. "I'll write."

"And you're eating properly?"

"Yes."

"Good girl." The old woman patted her cheeks once, twice, and smiled, and in the smile, for a heartbeat, she was entirely present and entirely aware, and she said, quietly, in a different voice entirely: "Whoever you are—you hold on to what remembers you. It goes faster than you think."

Then the fog closed over, and she smiled up at the son, and asked him pleasantly who he was, and whether his mother knew he was out.

The son led her toward the house. At the door he paused and looked back at Lior, and nodded—not thanks, not blame, just acknowledgment, one keeper of grief to another—and then the door closed, and the lane was empty, and the laundry went on turning in the wind like patient ghosts.

* * *

They walked out the far side of Saltmere in silence. It was Lior who broke it, three fields on, her voice roughened.

"The guild keeps records of everyone. Births, trades, debts, deaths. Every soul in the Ledgerhouse's territory has entries." She stared straight ahead. "Saltmere's ledger is sitting on a shelf somewhere, and every name in it is still *written down*, and none of those people know who they are. Ink didn't save them."

"No."

"So it's not enough. Writing isn't enough. It's the *keeping* that matters—who holds the book, and what they'd burn it for." She glanced at him sideways, quick and fierce under her red-rimmed eyes. "Corvane holds the books."

"Corvane holds the books," Fen agreed.

"And my mother held me. Or pieces of me." Lior's jaw tightened. "That old woman chose to remember when everyone else forgot. She stood at that well every day choosing it. My mother—" her breath caught, steadied— "my mother *chose the other way*, didn't she. Whatever she did to me. She chose forgetting, and called it keeping safe."

Fen said nothing. He had sworn, somewhere on the moon-road, that he would not be the one to tell her; that truth should come from her mother's ink, not a wolf's mouth. But the silence answered her well enough. He saw her file it away, ledger-fashion, in the growing column of things she would make someone answer for.

They crested the ridge as the sun failed. Below, in a fold of the moorland, stood a ruin crowned with a broken dome—the old observatory, its brass ribs naked against the darkening sky, its great lens shattered centuries ago, glinting in the last light like a spill of frozen stars.

"There," Fen said. "Before full dark. Wolves see well in the dark, but what waits in ruins tonight, I'd rather see coming."

Lior didn't move. She was looking up. Above the observatory, above the whole curve of the world, the sky lay bare and black where stars should have been—except, Fen saw now, following her gaze, it was not entirely bare. In one patch of heaven, faint as breath on glass, a few dim points remained. Not stars. *Cages*, maybe. Held lights. Kept souls, burning low because no one fed them.

One of them, as he watched, pulsed. Twice. Like something knocking.

Like something signaling.

Lior's hand found his sleeve. "Fen," she whispered. "That one—it flickered when we stopped. It's been flickering since Saltmere." Her voice shook. "I think something up there knows we're coming."

He looked from the pulsing dark to the girl beside it, and felt the old dread and the new hope twist together in his chest like rope.

"Then let's not keep it waiting," he said, and led her down toward the ruin as the last light died.
