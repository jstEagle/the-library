## 9. Hollow Stars

The road to the observatory had been eaten by the mountain.

Lior felt it before she saw it—the way the path simply stopped being a path, swallowed by a landslide of black stone and old frost, as though the mountain itself had decided, centuries ago, that no one should come here. Fen picked his way ahead of her through the rubble with the sure-footedness of his wolf-half, but even he moved slowly now, ears pricked, nose working at air that smelled of nothing. That was the strangest part of this whole broken country, she thought: the *nothing*. Since the stars had gone out, the world smelled thinner. Fewer things. As if reality itself were being rationed.

"Careful," Fen said without turning around. He always knew when her footing slipped a half-second before she did. "The scree is loose here. And the wards—" he paused, tasting the wind, "—whatever wards were on this place are dead. Dead a long time. But dead wards leave scars."

"What kind of scars?"

"The kind that itch." He glanced back at her, one amber eye catching the gray light. "Stay behind me until we're inside."

Lior hugged her satchel closer—the ledger inside it warm against her hip in a way paper had no right to be—and obeyed. She was getting better at obeying, or perhaps just better at admitting that Fen usually knew what he was talking about. A week ago she would have bristled. A week ago she'd been a girl who alphabetized impossible goods and kept her head down and told herself that invisibility was a kind of safety. Then Corvane had smiled at her with betrayal dressed up as protection, and guild assassins had put an arrow through Fen's shoulder, and now here she was, climbing a dead mountain in the company of a shapeshifting exile, chasing a rumor about caged souls.

The rumor had come from a peddler woman with silver teeth and a voice like dry leaves, met three days back on the smuggler's moon-path. *"You want to know where the stars went? Climb the Spine to the old watching-house. The humans built it when they still remembered what the sky was for. It's all that's left of them remembering anything."*

Lior hadn't liked how she'd said *humans*. She liked even less how often, lately, she wasn't sure whether the word included her.

* * *

They found the observatory at dusk—or what passed for dusk now, the sky a flat bruised color with not one point of light in it. The building crowned the mountain's shoulder like a broken tooth: a great stone drum, its domed roof split open down the middle, the two halves peeled apart like the rind of a fruit. Through the gap, the ruined telescope's brass skeleton jutted into the dark, a dead flower reaching for a garden that no longer existed.

Fen stopped at the threshold and would not cross it.

"It smells like a grave," he said quietly. "But a grave with something alive in it."

"That's reassuring."

"I didn't say whose grave." His jaw tightened. "Lior. If you go in there and find what I think you might find—I need you to promise me something."

She looked up at him. He was half-shifted again; he did that when he was frightened, she'd noticed. Fur along his forearms, pupils gone wide and round. The Wolf wore his fear on his skin because he'd never learned anywhere safe to hide it.

"Promise me you won't decide anything tonight," he said. "Whatever we learn. Whatever it costs. You keep your head and we walk out and we decide tomorrow, in daylight, on flat ground. That's how my people survived the Long Winter. One night at a time, never deciding anything while the dark is listening."

"That sounds less like wisdom and more like avoidance."

"It's both. We're wolves. We're very good at both."

Despite everything—the ache in her legs, the cold, the dread sitting in her stomach like a swallowed stone—Lior almost smiled. "Fine. I promise. No decisions after dark."

He dipped his head, satisfied, and followed her in.

Inside, the observatory floor was a vast circle of polished basalt, inlaid with a star chart in silver so old it had gone black. Lior's boot scraped across the Bull's shoulder, the River's bend. Around the walls ran galleries and niches, and in every niche stood—

She stopped breathing.

People. Dozens of people, standing in alcoves cut into the stone, motionless, their eyes open. Her hand flew to her mouth before the scream could get out.

Then she saw they weren't people. Not anymore. They were statues—but statues made wrong, made of something between marble and smoke, figures caught mid-gesture: a woman with her arms raised as if holding something aloft, an old man leaning forward as if listening, a child—gods, a *child*—reaching upward with both hands, fingers spread, mouth open in a soundless word.

"They're not dead," Fen said softly beside her. His hackles were up; every hair on him stood like quills. "Look at the surface. It moves."

It did. Up close, the stone-smoke of the nearest figure rippled faintly, like the skin of a pond disturbed by a breath of wind. Like something underneath trying to turn over in its sleep.

"This is a watching-house," Lior whispered. "You watch *from* here. You don't—you don't *become part of the furniture*—"

"No," Fen agreed grimly. "But you might become part of the *sky*."

He nodded upward, through the split dome.

Lior raised her eyes, and for a long moment she couldn't understand what she was looking at, because her mind refused the shape of it.

Above the observatory, where the empty sky pressed down black and lightless over the whole world, there was a hole in the darkness. A ragged circle directly overhead, maybe the size of a coin held at arm's length, through which something *else* showed—not stars, not the friendly scatter of constellations she'd catalogued in her head during lonely nights at the Ledgerhouse, but a dense packed glittering mass, thousands upon thousands of tiny lights crushed together like fish in a net, like bees in a hive, like—

Like souls in a cage.

And each tiny light was moving. Flickering. Fluttering against the edges of whatever held them, in patterns that were not random, that were not the slow wheel of astronomy, that were—

"*They're struggling,*" Lior breathed. "Fen. They're all trying to get out."

* * *

There was a chamber beneath the main floor, reached by a spiral stair worn concave by centuries of feet. Fen found it by smell—a thread of ink and lamp-oil and human sweat, old but preserved by the cold. At the bottom: a keeper's study, untouched, a museum of someone's last ordinary day. A chair pushed back from a desk. An inkwell crusted solid. A cup of tea grown a soft grey fur of mold that decades should have consumed and somehow hadn't, as if time itself had been wound down to nearly nothing in this place.

And books. Shelves of them. Ledgers, journals, observation logs bound in cracked leather.

Lior went to them the way a drowning woman goes to driftwood. Reading was the oldest comfort she had; numbers and entries were the language she thought in. She pulled the nearest volume toward the guttering lamp Fen lit from his flint, blew dust from its cover, and opened it.

*Observations, Year 412 of the Second Covenant. Keeper Maren Voss, Watcher of the Ninth Station.*

The handwriting was small and precise. Lior felt an odd kinship with it immediately—here was someone who understood that the world stayed sane only if somebody wrote everything down.

*Entry 1. The pact holds. The border sings. Tonight I took my turn at the Aperture and saw my predecessor's light steady among the First Choir. She waved. I am certain she waved.*

"Waved," Lior repeated. Her voice came out strange.

"Keep reading," Fen said. He had taken up a post at the stair, but his ears were turned fully toward her, tracking every rustle of the pages.

*Entry 9. The new keeper's training proceeds. I have explained the exchange as gently as I can. She wept, and then she thanked me, which was worse. Entry 10. I have begun drafting my own ascension letter for my daughter. There is no gentle way to write it. When my term ends, my soul goes up to hold the border with the others, and my body goes into the gallery below, and my daughter comes here, and in four hundred years someone will write this same entry about her. We are the mortar between realms. Nobody thanks the mortar.*

Lior's hands had gone cold on the page. "Fen. What is this? What did they do here?"

He came down the stairs slowly, reading over her shoulder, and when he understood, he made a sound low in his chest that she'd never heard from him before—not quite a growl, not quite grief. Something older than either.

"The covenant," he said. "Your guild's precious covenant. The one your masters file paperwork about. Do you know what actually holds the border between our realms? Not stones. Not wards. Stones are anchors. Wards are fences." He touched one long finger to the page, to the words *my soul goes up*. "*Keepers* are the wall. Living souls, given up freely, set into the sky to burn as stars and push against the dark from the other side. Every star you ever wished on was a person who volunteered."

"But the stars went *out*—"

"Because the wall is failing. Because keepers stopped coming. Because somewhere along the way—" his voice roughened, "—somewhere along the way, the humans who ran the stations decided it was easier to hoard relics and collect fees than to feed souls to the sky. The old lights are trapped up there, burning themselves thin, holding a weight they were never meant to hold alone. And when the last of them gutters—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. Liar had seen the wards collapse. She'd seen the stars vanish one by one. She'd seen what happened to the village that forgot itself.

She turned pages faster now, hunting. Entries about the choir, the Aperture, the ascension rite. Diagrams of the telescope—which was not a telescope at all, she realized, but a lens-array built to focus *downward*, to draw a soul up out of a body and set it alight in the cage of the sky. Sketches of the gallery above: the standing figures, each labeled. *Keeper Aldous Renn, 389–402. Keeper Sefa Ilm, 402–417.* And then, near the end of the book, in a different hand, shakier:

*My mother stands third from the north door. Sometimes I swear the stone of her face changes. Sometimes I swear she is trying to tell me something. I have begun to sleep here, under the dome, so I can hear her.*

Lior's breath stopped.

She knew that handwriting.

Not the specific letters—she'd never seen these letters before in her life. But the *hand*. The way the descenders hooked. The way the crossings slanted left. She had spent eleven years filing ledgers; she knew handwriting the way musicians knew pitch.

It was her own.

No—that was madness. This book was older than she was. But the resemblance was so exact it made her scalp prickle, and beneath the prickling rose another feeling, one she'd been carrying since the night the ink on her own ledgers had rewritten itself: the vertigo of gaps. Things she couldn't remember. A childhood with soft edges and missing rooms. A mother whose face she could describe perfectly and yet somehow never see.

"Fen." Her voice was very small. "What happens to a keeper's family? After ascension. Does anyone remember them?"

He was quiet for a moment. "The old rites demanded it," he said at last. "A keeper gives up her soul. Her kin give up the memory of her. It was called the Mercy Clause. The grief of watching someone burn in the sky forever was judged too cruel to leave intact." His eyes flicked to hers, amber and careful. "Why do you ask?"

"Because there's writing in this book that looks like mine. Because my whole childhood feels like a house with locked rooms. Because—" She stopped. Made herself say it. "Because when I look up at that cage of lights, Fen, I'm not horrified. I mean, I *am*, it's monstrous, it's the worst thing I've ever seen. But some part of me looks up there and feels—"

*Hope.* The word sat in her chest like a coal.

"—like I'm looking for someone."

The lamp flame bent in a draft from the stairs. Neither of them moved.

"There are thousands of lights up there," Fen said gently. "That doesn't mean—"

"She was a covenant-keeper. Corvane said so. My mother was a covenant-keeper, and covenant-keepers end up *there*." Lior's hand rose without her permission, pointing up through the floor, through the dome, at the distant imprisoned glitter. Hope and horror tangled in her throat until she couldn't tell which was which. "She might be up there right now. Holding the border. Burning. For twenty years while I filed invoices and didn't even know her name mattered."

"Lior—"

"And the worst part—" her voice cracked, and she hated it, hated the tears starting, hated that she'd promised Fen no decisions after dark because already a decision was building in her like a flood against a gate, "—the worst part is that some of those lights are going *out*. Look at them. Look how many fewer there are than the logs say there should be. They're dying up there, one by one, holding up two worlds, and nobody knows, and nobody cares, except us, and we're standing in a cellar arguing about—"

Light swept the stairwell.

Not lamplight. Not moonlight—there was no moonlight left in the world. It was a cold white glare, sweeping down the spiral stairs in a slow deliberate arc, the unmistakable signature of a guild-lantern run through a focusing crystal.

Fen moved like water. One instant he was beside her; the next he had snuffed the lamp, pulled her into the shadow beneath the desk, and shifted—bones sliding, fur flooding, until a grey wolf crouched where the man had been, lips peeled back from his teeth, one eye fixed on the stairs.

From above, muffled by stone, came a voice Lior knew. Smooth. Patient. Terrible.

Corvane.

"Search the galleries," the Guildmaster of Ledgers called pleasantly to his hounds, as if directing a filing crew. "The Hartwell girl will be here. She was always going to come here—it's in her blood. And be *gentle*, please. She is, after all, going to make such a fine addition to the choir."

Up through the split dome, far above, one of the caged lights flared—brief, desperate, bright—like a soul waving.

Or screaming.
