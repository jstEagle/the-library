## 10. Corvane's Hounds

The rain found them three miles past the observatory ruin, and Lior was grateful for it. Rain meant tracks filled in, scent washed away, and the world went soft at the edges—everything blurred into gray the way she had spent her whole life trying to be. She pulled her cloak tighter and watched Fen pick his way along the ridgeline ahead of her, favoring his left side where the bridge-guardians' teeth had opened him up. He hadn't complained once. Wolves, she was learning, treated pain the way ledgers treated small debts: noted, carried, never spoken aloud.

"Stop," he said.

She froze mid-step. The rain drummed on the bracken around them, on the flat stones of the old drover's road, on everything except the space directly behind her—which had gone suddenly, horribly quiet, the way air goes quiet before lightning.

"What is it?" she whispered.

Fen turned his head slowly, nostrils flaring, and even through the wet she could see the hackles rise along his neck. His eyes had gone amber-bright, pupils narrowed to slits. "Iron," he breathed. "And ink. And something that smells like—" He grimaced. "Like my own blood, but older. Colder."

"Hounds," Lior said. The word left her mouth before she'd decided to say it, dredged up from a memory she hadn't known she kept: a page in the Ledgerhouse's restricted catalog, *Corvane's Hounds, bred of hound and shadow and contract-ink, bound to the scent of those who steal from the Guild.* She had read it on a slow afternoon, filed it under *curiosities*, and thought no more about it. Now the curiosity had a smell, and it was coming down the hill behind them.

"How many?" she asked.

"Three." Fen's voice had dropped to something low and rough, more wolf than man. "Maybe four. They're circling wide. Corvane taught them patience." His lip curled. "I remember these animals from the last war. They don't tire. They don't eat unless he lets them. They just follow, and follow, until there's nowhere left to follow you to."

Lior's hands were shaking, so she did what she always did with shaking hands: she made them useful. She dug into her satchel and drew out the two covenant-stones, now fused to the backs of her fingers like second knuckles of some stranger's bone, warm and faintly humming against her skin. Even through the rain they pulsed with a light too deep to be seen, only felt—a heartbeat in her hand that wasn't hers.

"They're tracking me," she said. It wasn't a question anymore. She'd understood it since the vaults, since the ledger entries rewrote themselves, since Corvane's smiling order of detention. Everything—the wards, the dark stars, the assassins—converged on the girl who carried the pact in her blood. "It's me they want."

"It's the stones they want," Fen corrected. "You just happen to be wearing them." He glanced back at her, and his expression softened by a degree, which for Fen was practically a speech. "There's a hollow under the ridge. Old badger set, big enough for you. I can lead them off—"

"No." The word came out harder than she intended, and she was surprised to find she didn't want to take it back. "No. I'm done being hidden. That's the whole—that's what got us here. Everyone hiding things. My mother. Corvane. Me." She lifted her chin, rain running down her face. "We stay together."

Something flickered across Fen's face—surprise, then something warmer and more frightened than surprise. "You don't know what they do to—" He stopped. Started again. "The last time someone stood between me and the Guild's hounds, I buried what was left in a shoebox because there wasn't enough for a grave."

The confession landed between them like a stone in still water. Lior looked at him—at this exile who carried his shame the way she carried her satchel, everywhere, always—and felt the guilt arrive early, pre-emptively, the way it had been arriving all week. Every mile they traveled together, she cost him something. A wound on the bridge. Blood on the smuggler's moon-path. And if she let him run decoy down this rain-slicked ridge, she would cost him everything, and she would be alive afterward to keep the accounting.

That was the thing about being invisible, she was beginning to understand. You told yourself it cost nothing—that slipping through the world unnoticed was a kind of freedom, a cleverness, a survival. But invisibility was never free. Someone always paid the difference. Her mother had paid it. Fen was paying it. And the bill was coming due in tooth and iron on a hillside in the rain.

"Then we fight smart," she said. "Not apart. Together."

* * *

They made their stand at the ruined tollhouse where the drover's road crossed the stream—a squat stone archway, half-collapsed, with walls thick enough to put their backs against and one narrow approach that any attacker would have to funnel through. Fen shifted as they waited, dropping to four legs in the lee of the wall, his great gray body steaming in the cold. Lior crouched beside him with a knife in one hand and her ledger in the other, which felt absurd, which felt exactly right. Ink and iron. The only weapons she'd ever owned.

They came out of the rain the way nightmares come out of sleep—not walking so much as unfolding. The first hound was the size of a pony and wrong in every particular: too many joints in its legs, hide like wet black paper, eyes that were not eyes at all but smears of guild-seal wax, gold and unblinking. Where its paws struck the ground, the rain itself seemed to flinch. Across its flank, scarred into the flesh in neat clerk's hand, ran words Lior could half-read even at a distance: *Property of the Master of Ledgers. Recover or destroy.*

"Fen," she whispered. "They have contract-words written on them."

"I know." His growl vibrated through the stones beneath them both. "Everything Corvane owns has his handwriting on it. That's how he loves things."

The first hound hit the archway at full speed.

What followed was not a battle so much as a demolition with pauses. Fen met the beast head-on and the impact cracked a lintel loose; Lior heard ribs give on one side or the other and couldn't tell whose. She stabbed at the second hound as it poured through the gap, drove her knife into the joint of its foreleg, and the creature screamed in a human voice—actually screamed, in a register and cadence she recognized, and the recognition made her sick down to her heels. There were people in these things. Had been. Contracted, consumed, repurposed. Corvane's ledger of souls, bound in flesh.

*Recover or destroy,* the scars said, and she understood suddenly that recover referred to the stones, and destroy—

The third hound came over the wall.

She never saw it land. She saw Fen move—saw him abandon the first hound mid-struggle, saw him throw himself across the space between them with a speed that ignored every wound he already carried—and then the world was weight and heat and the terrible wet sound of teeth finding purchase. Fen's body covered hers. The hound's jaws closed on his shoulder, on the muscle that joined arm to torso, and shook him like a terrier shaking a rat, and Lior heard him make a sound she would spend the rest of her life unable to forget.

"No—" She was screaming it, she realized. "No, no, get *off* him—"

She did the only thing she knew how to do. She opened the ledger.

Her hand moved without consulting her, the way it did when the covenant spoke through her—pen out, page flat against the fallen hound's paper-hide, and she wrote with the stub of pencil she kept for margin-notes, fast and fierce, in her own small invisible handwriting:

*Entry 4,417. One (1) hound, property of G. Corvane, returned to sender. Reason: theft of services. This animal was contracted to hunt a thief. It has instead stolen a life — his — and lives, per Guild law, are owed.*

She wrote the word *owed* twice. Underlined it. And pressed the page against the creature's scarred flank.

The hound shuddered. The wax-gold eyes flared. Somewhere in whatever passed for its mind, the old arithmetic woke—the deep guild-instinct that debts must balance, that contracts bind both ways—and the thing released Fen's shoulder, turned, and bolted into the rain, dragging its wounded packmate after it, called home by an entry in a runaway apprentice's ledger.

The silence afterward was worse than the noise.

"Fen." She crawled to him through the churned mud. "Fen, look at me, look—" His shoulder was a ruin. The hound's teeth had gone through muscle and into the joint, and the blood welled out faster than the rain could dilute it, spreading pink and then red beneath him. His breathing had gone shallow and quick, each exhale a thin whine he was clearly fighting not to make. His eyes found hers—still amber, still aware—and in them she saw something that frightened her far more than the hounds had.

Trust. Absolute, unreserved, *earned-at-the-price-of-his-body* trust. He had thrown himself onto her without calculation, without hesitation, the way you catch a falling child, the way you do the obvious thing. As though she were worth it. As though it had never once occurred to him to be somewhere else.

"Don't," she choked out. "Don't you dare look at me like that. I told you—I told you we'd stay together, I thought we had a plan, I—"

He tried to speak. What came out was mostly blood-froth and breath, but she bent close and caught it: *"Worth it."*

"It is not worth it!" The words tore out of her, loud enough to startle her own self, loud enough to echo off the broken tollhouse. "Nothing is worth this. Don't you understand? I've spent my entire life making sure nobody paid anything for me. That was the point. That was the *whole point*. You stay small, you stay quiet, you stay nothing, and everyone else gets to keep what's theirs." Her hands were pressing on his shoulder now, both of them, hot blood sliding between her fingers, and the stones on her other hand pulsed faster as though agitated, as though grieving. "And it turns out that's not safety. It's just debt. It's debt that piles up somewhere I can't see until it comes due all at once, in blood, on a hillside, in the *rain*—"

"Lior." His voice, barely. He had gotten a hand up—his good one—and pressed it weakly against her wrist. Not to stop her. Just to touch. "Listen... to yourself. You think that's why I..." A ragged breath. "I didn't bleed for you because you're small. I bled because you're the first thing in thirty years I've believed in. That's not a debt. That's a choice. Learn the difference."

She stared at him through the rain, and something in her chest broke open in a way that had nothing to do with grief—or not *only* grief.

"We need a hedge-witch," she said finally, voice steadier than she felt. "Stitches, poultice, the works. My mother's cottage—it's two days east, maybe less if we cut through the alder flats. There might be charms there. There might be—" She swallowed. "There might be answers, too. About the stones. About her. But first we stop the bleeding."

His eyes had begun to glaze. She slapped his cheek lightly, once, twice. "Stay with me. You hear me? You don't get to leave. I forbid it. I'm writing it down—you are hereby entered in my ledger as *kept*, and nobody keeps a book better than me."

The ghost of a grin touched his muzzle. *"Bossy."*

"Yes," she said. "Now shift back. I can carry a man better than I can carry a wolf, and I intend to insult you thoroughly the whole way."

* * *

She half-carried, half-dragged him east through the dying light, and the guilt walked beside her the entire way, keeping pace like a fourth hound.

Every stumble of hers that jarred his wound, every time she had to set him down while she scouted ahead, every minute the bleeding soaked fresh through the moss-poultice she'd packed into the torn shoulder—each one added another line to the account she was keeping now, furiously, involuntarily, in her head. *One shoulder. Thirty years of exile. A shoebox grave she'd made him confess. How much more before the column totaled something she couldn't repay?*

And beneath the guilt, quieter and more terrible, the truth she'd been circling for days finally sat down inside her chest and refused to leave: invisibility had never protected anyone but herself. Her mother had hidden the truth and died for it. The village had forgotten itself and hollowed out. She had hidden herself her whole life and won nothing but loneliness—and now the people who dared to see her were the ones who bled.

The rain eased near midnight. Above the black line of the eastern woods, through a tear in the clouds, a single star appeared—the first she'd seen since the wards fell. It burned low and red and steady, and Lior stopped, Fen heavy against her side, and looked at it until her eyes stung.

"That one's new," Fen murmured against her hair. Or maybe she imagined it; he was half-delirious by then.

"No," she said softly. "I think it's an answer. We're close to something."

Through the trees, barely visible in the star's thin light, a crooked chimney rose above a roofline collapsed at one corner—a cottage she had never seen before and knew instantly, the way you know your own name: her mother's house, waiting.

The door, when she reached it, was unlocked. On the threshold, scratched deep into the wood in handwriting she recognized from a thousand rewritten ledger pages, were words that stopped her heart:

*Lior — if you're reading this, the price has come due. Come in. Bring the wolf. We have very little time, and I have so much to tell you about the third stone.*
