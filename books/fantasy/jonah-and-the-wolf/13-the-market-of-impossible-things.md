## 13. The Market of Impossible Things

*Fen*

The market came to them before they found it.

They had followed the salt-road south for two days, past hedgerows gone strange with the ward-fall — blackthorn blooming out of season, hawthorn bleeding sap that glowed faintly green in the dark — when Lior stopped mid-stride and said, "Do you smell that?"

Fen lifted his nose to the wind. Underneath the rot-sweetness of dying orchards, he caught woodsmoke and frying onions, tallow and cardamom, wet wool and hot iron. And beneath all of it, so faint a human would never have marked it: *magic*. Old magic, peddled and haggled over, magic that had been passed hand to hand like counterfeit coin until its edges wore smooth.

"Impossible things," he said.

"You know it?"

"I knew it once." He kept walking, and did not elaborate, and she had learned enough not to press. But he felt her glance slide along his jaw, cataloguing him the way she catalogued everything, and he thought: *she'll have it out of me eventually. She has a ledger's soul. Everything gets an entry.*

The market sat in the hollow of a dry riverbed that no map remembered, which was precisely why maps forgot it. Tents of patched silk in forty colors leaned against one another like drunkards. Lanterns hung from crooked poles, each burning a different color of flame — one blue-white as frostbite, one the deep amber of honey held up to the sun, one that gave off not light but the *memory* of light, so that Fen felt warm looking at it even though the air bit his knuckles. Peddlers cried their wares in a dozen tongues and several that were not tongues at all but birdsong, arithmetic, grief.

*A jar of bottled thunder,* someone called, echoing a phrase Lior had once written in a ledger half a world away, and Fen watched her flinch at the familiarity of it.

"Keep your hood up," he murmured. "And your thoughts down. There are things here that read minds the way you read entries."

"I don't have anything worth stealing," she whispered back.

"Lior." He turned to look at her properly — at the smudged ink on her fingers, the stubborn line of her shoulders, the way she stood in this impossible place with her chin up as if she'd been sent to audit it. "You're carrying two-thirds of the covenant of Vharn under your skin and more courage than anyone I've met in three hundred years. You are the most valuable thing in this riverbed. Act like it."

Color rose in her cheeks. She pulled her hood lower anyway, but her chin stayed up, and Fen found himself smiling behind his scarf, which was an unfamiliar sensation, like finding an old coin in a coat pocket.

The first stall they stopped at sold bottled weather. The second sold names — small glass vials, each holding a name someone had given away, swirling like fireflies. The third sold doors, laid flat and stacked like lumber, each one opening somewhere else entirely if you knocked correctly.

But it was the fourth stall where the trade happened.

The peddler there was ancient in a way that had nothing to do with years. Her skin was brown parchment creased with script, actual writing, moving slowly across her face like weather across a sky. She sat behind a table draped in black velvet, and on the velvet lay a single object: a small brass scale, empty on both pans.

"Keeper's daughter," the old woman said, without preamble, and Lior went rigid beside him.

"How—" Lior began.

"The stones sing, girl. Did you think we wouldn't hear them?" The peddler tapped her own temple. "Everything in this market hears everything. That's rather the point. Sit."

There were no chairs. Lior sat anyway, cross-legged in the dirt, and after a moment Fen settled beside her, close enough that his shoulder nearly touched hers. He told himself it was tactical. He was becoming very good at telling himself things about Lior Hartwell that were not entirely tactical.

"We need information," Lior said, falling into the clipped cadence of negotiation, the guild cadence. "A location. We can pay—"

"With what?" The old woman smiled. Her teeth were tiny and white as rice grains. "Coin? Coin is for people who still believe the world runs on it. Here, dearling, we trade in the only currency that's real." She gestured, and the brass scale between them began, very softly, to glow. "Memories."

* * *

*Lior*

Lior had spent four years in the Ledgerhouse learning what things cost. Ounces of moonwater. Grains of phoenix ash. The going rate for a griffin feather versus a basilisk eye. She had thought herself fluent in value.

She understood nothing until the old woman explained the exchange.

"A memory," the peddler said, "is weighed by what it holds, not how long it lasts. A single afternoon your mother braided your hair might outweigh ten years of drudgery. A moment of terror might be worth nothing at all — fear is cheap, everyone's got plenty. Joy is dear. Love is dearer still." She folded her hands atop the table. "So. What will you give me for the location of the second covenant-stone?"

Lior's mouth went dry. Beside her, Fen had gone very still, in the way he did when something from the wolf-realm brushed against the present — a wariness, a coiling.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you keep everything, and lose the stone, and the stars stay dark, and eventually both realms tear along the seam and everyone loses everything anyway." The peddler shrugged, a motion like pages turning. "I'm not a villain, child. I'm a shopkeeper. The universe set these prices long before either of us was born; I only mind the till."

Lior looked into the scale's empty pans and felt her own memory spread out before her like a ledger awaiting audit. Which entries could she spare?

Her first day at the Ledgerhouse — no. That was load-bearing; take it out and the whole structure of who she'd become might collapse.

The taste of stolen plums eaten on a rooftop with Tam, the other junior clerk, laughing until they choked — she gripped that one tight, then made herself loosen her hold. *Tam.* She hadn't thought of Tam in months. Wasn't that already a kind of selling? A memory left unvisited, gathering dust?

"The night the wards fell," she heard herself say. "When I saw the stranger collapse at the door. I'll trade my memory of that night."

"No," Fen said quietly.

"It's a fair trade. It's recent, it's vivid—"

"It's the night you met me." He wasn't looking at her; he was watching the peddler, but his voice had an edge she rarely heard from him. "You don't get to give that away."

"Why not?" she shot back, stung. "It's *mine*."

"Because—" He stopped. His jaw worked. The lantern-light caught the amber ring around his pupils, the one that appeared when the wolf came near the surface. "Because some things shouldn't be sold cheap just because you don't yet know what they weigh."

The old woman chuckled, low and dry. "Oh, the exile speaks sense. Rare enough to be worth bottling." She turned her moving-script face to Lior. "He's right, dearling. That night is worth more than you think — not to me, but to you. I deal honestly. I won't take an item the seller doesn't understand."

Lior stared at the scale. At Fen. At the lanterns burning their impossible colors above the tents, and beyond them the sky, where the stars should have been and weren't — a darkness so complete it looked less like night than like a page torn out.

"What do you want, then?" she asked the peddler. "What's fair?"

"That," said the old woman, "depends on what you can bear to lose."

* * *

*Fen*

Fen had traded at markets like this before. Three hundred years ago. Back when he'd been someone else — a prince of the Long Winter court, a son, a brother, before he'd broken the covenant and been broken in return.

He knew the arithmetic of the place. He knew that every peddler here carried memories they'd bought from the desperate, wore other people's first kisses and deathbeds stitched into their coats. He knew the temptation too: that you could put down the heavy things, the shameful ones, walk out lighter and emptier and call it healing.

He also knew, with the certainty of scar tissue, what it was to lose the wrong memory. His exile had begun with an edit — his own, self-inflicted, the night he'd tried to carve out his guilt and succeeded only in carving out everything around it.

So when Lior finally spoke, he was braced.

"My mother," she said.

His head turned. "Lior—"

"Not everything." Her hands were shaking. She flattened them against her knees, the way she did when steadying a ledger entry, when making herself write the true number instead of the easy one. "My mother sang to me. Every night. I remember the songs — I think I do — but I can't remember her face anymore. It's been edited, Fen, someone took pieces, and what's left of her in my head is mostly..." She swallowed. "Mostly fog. But there's one clear thing. One evening. Rain on the window. She held my hands and taught me the first line of a counting rhyme, the one keepers use, and she said, *'When you can't remember, count.'*" Lior's breath shuddered out. "That one's clear. Crystal clear. I've carried it through everything. And it's mine to give, isn't it? Even if it costs me?"

The old woman's script-face rippled. For a long moment she simply studied Lior, and Fen watched the scales of some private judgment tipping behind her eyes.

"Yes," the peddler said at last. "That's honest weight. That's love, clearly labeled." Something almost gentle entered her voice. "Are you certain, keeper's daughter? Once sold, a memory doesn't come back. Not here. Not anywhere."

Lior was quiet for a long time. The market noise washed around them — hawkers crying, a fiddle playing a tune that made Fen homesick for a home he'd burned down himself.

Then she said, "My mother gave things up for me. I'm learning that. Piece by piece, I'm learning exactly what she gave." Her chin came up. "I can give one evening back to the current. Maybe... maybe that's how you honor a current. You let it carry you."

Fen's chest hurt. He wanted to argue. He wanted to grab her wrist and pull her out of this riverbed and away from all these smiling thieves of the interior. Instead he heard himself say, "Then I trade too."

"Fen, no—"

"One memory. Equal weight." He faced the peddler. "From the same era. My mother teaching me to hunt. First snow of my ninth winter. She laughed when I fell in the drift, and I remember thinking her laugh was the warmest thing in the whole cold court." His voice roughened. "That one."

The old woman looked between them, and her expression shifted into something that was not quite pity and not quite respect. "Two childhoods," she murmured. "Two mothers' voices. That buys you a location, yes. And a little extra besides — honesty compels me to say so. The stones resonate with sacrifice. They'll feel lighter after this. Whether that's a mercy or not, I couldn't say."

She extended her right hand, palm up. "Hold the memory in mind. All of it, every detail, front to back. Then place your hand in mine and let go."

Lior glanced at Fen — one last sideways look, asking something he didn't have words for. He nodded. *Together,* he meant. *Whatever this does to us, together.*

She closed her eyes. Her lips moved slightly, counting — *one, two, three, the rain, her hands, the rhyme* — and then she reached out and took the peddler's palm.

Fen watched her face as the memory left her. It was like watching a candle go out in slow motion: the softness in her expression dimming, the tears that rose and spilled without her seeming to notice them. When she opened her eyes again they were clear and calm and missing something, and he hated this market, hated every tent and lantern in it, hated himself for leading her here.

Then it was his turn. He summoned the snow. The drift. The fall. The laugh — *her* laugh, bright as struck ice, warmest thing in the whole cold court. He held it as long as he dared, memorizing the shape of losing it, and then he let go.

The absence was immediate and enormous, like a tooth pulled from the middle of his smile. He knew a mother had taught him to hunt. He knew there had been snow. The rest was a smooth blank wall, painted over so carefully he could see the seams only because he'd watched them being made.

"Well," said the old woman briskly, wiping her palm on her apron as if memories left residue, which perhaps they did. "The second stone. Listen close, because I sell directions once."

* * *

*Lior*

"There's a ruin called Hollowstar Observatory," the peddler said, "three days north-east, past the village of Greywater — if Greywater still exists; the ward-fall hit it hard. Beneath the observatory's collapsed dome lies the observatory's heart: a lens the old keepers used to read the sky. And fused into that lens—" she tapped the velvet table, tap, tap, tap, like punctuation— "is your stone. The Second Stone of Vharn. The Stone of Witness. It was taken there eight hundred years ago by a keeper who believed the stars themselves needed guarding."

Lior's blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the wind off the dry riverbed. An observatory. Stars. A keeper who guarded the sky.

Beside her, she felt Fen go still — that particular stillness, the wolf-wariness.

"Eight hundred years," he repeated slowly. "Which keeper?"

The old woman smiled her rice-grain smile. "Now that, exile, would cost extra."

"We don't have anything left to—" Lior started.

"Oh, you have plenty. Everyone does. But no." The peddler waved a hand, rings flashing. "Some answers are better earned at the destination. Consider it... a reason to make the journey yourself." She leaned forward, and her moving-script face rearranged itself into lines that looked almost like warning. "One more thing, freely given, because I liked your mothers — or what I saw of them through what you paid. The road past Greywater is watched. Someone else seeks the Stone of Witness. A man with ink under his fingernails and a ledger in his heart, and hounds that smell like paper and blood."

Corvane.

Lior stood so fast her knees cracked. Fen rose with her, smooth as water pouring uphill.

"Thank you," she managed.

"Don't thank me, dearling. Thank yourselves, in twenty years, if you still remember thanking anyone." The old woman was already turning away, closing up her stall, the brass scale folding itself flat like a moth settling its wings. "Mind the roads. Mind each other. And mind—" a pause, her back to them now— "mind that the most dangerous trades aren't made in markets. They're made in hearts, daily, without receipts. Guard your entries."

They walked out of the riverbed as the colored lanterns guttered one by one behind them, as if the market were folding itself back into the ordinary world, back into the margins of maps. Neither of them spoke for a mile. Lior kept reaching for the counting rhyme — *when you can't remember, count* — and finding, each time, only the instruction and not the voice that had given it. A lesson floating free of its teacher. She counted anyway. It helped. It didn't help enough.

It was full dark when Fen finally broke the silence, his voice low and strange.

"Lior. The keeper who took the stone to the observatory. Eight hundred years ago."

"Yes?"

He stopped walking. Above them the starless sky pressed down like a lid. When he turned to her, the amber ring around his pupils was wide, and for the first time since she'd known him, Fen looked afraid.

"I remember the observatory," he said. "Not from stories. From *before*. Lior — I've been there. In another life, under another name." He exhaled, ragged. "And the keeper who hid the stone in the lens, the one whose ghost still walks that ruin—"

The wind moved through the dead orchards, and somewhere far behind them, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of hounds.

"—I think she was a Hartwell."
