## 15. The Bridge of Teeth

The border between the human realm and Vharn was not a line. It was a wound.

Fen felt it before he saw it—a low, grinding ache in his teeth, the old wolf-sense that told him he was walking toward something that wanted to unmake him. Behind him, Lior kept pace with her ledger strapped across her back like a shield and the fused covenant-stones glowing faintly at her wrist where they had sunk into her skin, two knots of pale light beneath the flesh. She had stopped flinching at them sometime on the third day of travel. He had not.

He remembered what it was to carry stones like those. He remembered carrying one for sixty years and then throwing it into the sea.

*Not now,* he told himself, the way he had been telling himself since the Hedge-Witch's cottage, since Lior had knelt in the ruins of his shame and said *absolved*, as if she had any right to absolve him, as if the word were hers to give. But it had landed anyway, the way her words always did—like a stone dropped into still water, ripples going out and out and never quite stopping.

The road ended at a cliff's edge above a valley of fog. Not ordinary fog. This was the breath of Vharn itself, spilling up over the lip of the world like water running backward, silver-white and slow and smelling of pine sap and blood and cold iron. And rising out of it, arcing from the cliff to a jagged promontory lost in cloud, stood the bridge.

It was made of teeth.

Not carved to look like teeth—it *was* teeth, thousands upon thousands of them, fangs and molars and tusks grown together into a span of bone-white arches, roots woven into roots, enamel gleaming dully under a sky the color of an old bruise. Some of the teeth were enormous, each one taller than Fen; some were small enough to belong to a child. They creaked when the wind moved through the valley, a sound like a giant grinding its jaw in its sleep.

"The Bridge of Teeth," Lior said quietly. She had gone very still, the way she did when she was reading something difficult. "It's in my mother's notes. I thought—I assumed the name was figurative."

"Nothing in Vharn is figurative." Fen stepped closer to the edge. The fog below shifted, and for half a heartbeat he saw shapes moving in it—long bodies, low slung, patient. "The bridge is fed. Every wolf who crosses must leave a tooth behind, or be refused. That is the toll."

Lior looked at him sideways. "You've crossed before."

"I built part of it." His jaw ached saying it. He touched one canine with his tongue—the left lower, the gap he had never let heal shut because the ache kept him honest. "When I was young and stupid and believed the covenant would last forever. Every alpha of my line has laid teeth into this bridge since before your kind learned to write. It is the oldest pact-stone in either realm. Older than the Altar. Older than the guild." He paused. "Older than my family's disgrace, which came later, and was entirely our own doing."

"You're doing the thing again," Lior said. "Where you insult yourself before anyone else can."

"It saves time."

"Fen." She turned to face him fully, and the light of the stones under her skin caught in the hollows of her face, making her look briefly like someone from one of her own ledger entries—an impossible thing, catalogued and real. "Whatever happens on that bridge, I'm glad it's you crossing it with me."

He had no answer for that. He had spent sixty years without anyone saying things like that to him, and he had built a whole architecture of silence to survive it, and she kept walking through the walls as if they weren't there. So he said nothing, and began instead to strip off his human shape, because the bridge did not accept men. It accepted wolves.

The change came easier than it had in decades. Fur like storm-shadow, limbs lengthening, senses blooming open until the world became a symphony of scent and sound—the fog below full of watchers, the bridge humming with old oaths, and beside him Lior's heartbeat, quick and steady, steady, steady. She did not step back when he shook himself into his true body. She reached out and set her hand flat against his shoulder, right over the scar Corvane's hounds had given him, and left it there for three heartbeats.

Then they stepped onto the teeth.

* * *

The bridge knew him.

That was the trouble. Fen had expected resistance—he had expected the toll, the guardians, the long cold walk—but he had not expected the bridge to *remember*. With every stride his paws sank slightly into the enamel, and with every stride the bridge whispered to him in the voices of everyone who had ever laid a tooth into it. His grandmother, who had held the eastern passes through the Long Winter. His father, who had died defending the ford at Silvermelt. His sister Wren, whose tooth sat somewhere in the third arch, laid down the day she came of age, six years before Fen broke everything.

*Traitor,* the bridge murmured, in Wren's voice, in his father's voice, in his own younger voice. *Traitor. Traitor. You threw your stone away. You let the wards starve. Traitor.*

"They're not real," Lior said beside him—she had insisted on walking, though the fog below churned and the guardians watched, and the bridge had grudgingly permitted her passage because she carried the stones. Her hand hovered near his shoulder, not quite touching, ready to. "Fen. They're echoes. Old grief caught in the bone. Don't listen."

"They're not wrong, either."

"No," she admitted. "But grief isn't a judge. It's just weather." She said it like she was quoting someone—her mother, maybe, or the hedge-witch's memory-charm—and it steadied him more than he wanted to admit.

They were halfway across when the guardians rose out of the fog.

There were nine of them. Fen knew there would be nine; there had always been nine, one for every broken oath in the history of the realms, which meant there had always been far too many and somehow exactly enough. They were wolves the way a cathedral fire is warmth—wolves scaled up past sense, shoulders higher than the arches, fur like black frost, eyes burning the white-gold of caged stars. Each wore chains of no metal Fen could name, links thick as tree trunks, dragging behind them across the bridge and through the fog, and where the chains touched the teeth, the teeth wept.

The largest guardian planted itself in the middle of the span, directly ahead, and spoke with a voice like an avalanche deciding.

**WHO COMES TO VHARN?**

Fen forced his legs to hold still. Beside him, Lior had gone rigid, her hand now gripping the fur at his neck—not for her own comfort, he realized, but to anchor him. As if he were the one who might bolt. As if she had decided, somewhere on this bridge of dead alphas' teeth, that his courage was hers to keep safe.

"A returning son of the Thornbury line," Fen said, and his voice came out in the growling speech of Vharn, rougher than he remembered, rusted at the hinges. "Come to reforge what I helped break."

The nine heads turned as one. The white-gold eyes fixed on him, and the weight of their attention was physical, a mountain leaning on his spine.

**THE THORNBURY LINE IS ENDED,** the guardian said. **ITS LAST SON CAST AWAY HIS STONE AND HIS NAME. THE COURT OF THE LONG WINTER STRUCK HIM FROM THE ROLLS. THERE IS NO RETURNING SON.**

"I know," Fen said.

**THEN WHAT STANDS ON OUR BRIDGE?**

And there it was. The question he had been running from for sixty years, asked at last by something too large to lie to. Fen looked down at the teeth beneath his paws—at his own contribution, the left lower canine he'd given at sixteen, bright-eyed and certain, believing he would spend his life keeping the borders warm and the stars fed. He had spent it instead in exile, hating humans for their small cruelties, hating himself more, letting the covenant rot because rotting was easier than admitting he had been wrong about everything.

Lior's grip tightened at his neck.

"My name is Fen Thornbury," he said. "I am the last of my line, and my line ended in disgrace, and I am here anyway. Not to take back what I threw away. To pay for it. Whatever the toll is now, I'll pay it."

**THE TOLL HAS CHANGED,** said the guardian, and its chains dragged forward, links shrieking across the enamel. **THE BRIDGE IS STARVING. THE STARS ARE CAGED. THE OLD TOLL WAS A TOOTH. THE NEW TOLL IS A CLAIM.**

"A claim?"

**YOU WILL CLAIM YOUR BIRTHRIGHT OR YOU WILL BE UNMADE. THE BRIDGE DOES NOT CARRY THE NAMELESS. IT NEVER DID. WE ONLY FORGOT, IN THE FAT YEARS, TO ASK.**

The other eight guardians began to move. Not attacking—not yet. Circling, wide and slow, closing the span ahead and behind until the fog swallowed both ends of the bridge and there was only the teeth, the nine vast shapes, and the small furious girl standing at his flank with covenant-light bleeding through her skin.

"Fen," Lior said, low and fast. "What does 'unmade' mean?"

"It means the bridge eats you," Fen said. "All of you. Your teeth go in the span. Your name goes nowhere." He found, distantly, that he was calm. Sixty years of dread, and now that the moment had arrived, it arrived quiet. "I need you to stand back."

"No."

"Lior—"

"I said no." She planted herself squarely beside him, chin up, staring down a guardian whose single eye was larger than her entire body, and she did it the way she did everything: thoroughly, precisely, as if defiance were simply another entry to be correctly recorded. "Your birthright is a claim on your *line*, isn't it? On Thornbury blood and Thornbury oath. Well, I'm holding two-thirds of the original covenant in my arm, and I've been reading your family's history in flashes for days. If claiming means swearing something, swear it. If it means fighting—" her voice didn't waver, but her hand, hidden in his fur, trembled once, "—then we fight, and I'll hit them with whatever a ledger does when you throw it hard enough."

Something cracked open in Fen's chest. It took him a moment to recognize it, because it had been so long.

It was not fear. For the first time in sixty years, standing on the bridge where all his ancestors' teeth accused him, surrounded by nine starving gods with chains, Fen was not afraid. He was angry—angry at the years wasted, angry at the exile he'd chosen because choosing disgrace felt safer than risking failure again. Courage hadn't abandoned him all that time ago. He had simply locked it away with the rest of his inheritance and thrown away the key, and here was this invisible runaway, this keeper's daughter, this *friend*, holding it out to him in her open palm.

He turned to face the great guardian, and he lifted his head, and he howled.

It was not a pretty sound. It was rusty and cracked and it hurt his throat, and it carried anyway—down the span of his ancestors' teeth, out over the fog, into the dark of Vharn beyond, a sound that said *I am still here, I was always still here, come and see.*

The guardians went silent.

Then the great one lowered its head until its burning eye was level with him, close enough that its breath stirred his fur, cold as the space between stars.

**SPEAK YOUR CLAIM, FEN THORNBURY.**

"I claim the alpha-right of my line," Fen said. "Not to rule. To *keep*. I claim the eastern passes and the western ford and the long watch on the border, and I claim the debt my family owes the covenant, and I will pay it with the only coin I have left, which is everything." He bared his teeth—all of them, even the gap, even the ache. "I am the last Thornbury. I am enough."

**THE CLAIM MUST BE SEALED IN BLOOD AND BONE.** The guardian's jaws opened. **AS IT WAS ALWAYS DONE.**

Fen understood one heartbeat before it happened, and he did not run. The guardian closed its teeth around his left foreleg—not biting, not breaking, but pressing, pressure beyond bearing, and then a pain like being written upon, like ink made of ice scoring itself into marrow. He heard himself make a sound he wasn't proud of. He stayed standing. Through the white blaze of it he felt Lior pressed against his side, both hands buried in his fur now, holding him to the world, whispering something rhythmic under her breath—numbers, he realized. Ledger numbers. Counting him through it the way you'd count a fever.

When the guardian released him, the fur of his foreleg bore a mark: a spiral of interlocking fangs, the sigil of the Thornbury line, seared into skin and bone alike. Alive. Burning. *His.*

**THE CLAIM IS HEARD,** the guardian said. **THE LINE OF THORNBURY STANDS AGAIN—ONE WOLF STRONG.** Its head swung toward Lior, and the eight others bowed, slow as continents settling. **AND ONE WHO CARRIES THE PACT. CROSS, KEEPER'S DAUGHTER. CROSS, ALPHA-OF-ONE. VHARN REMEMBERS YOU NOW, THORNBURY. DO NOT MAKE US REGRET IT.**

The fog parted. Ahead, the promontory emerged from the cloud—a ridge of black stone crowned with wind-bent pines, and beyond it, far off under the bruised sky, the faint impossible glow of a court that had known no night in a thousand years, now guttering like a dying lamp.

The Court of the Long Winter, waiting to hear whether humanity deserved to keep existing.

Fen walked on shaking legs to the end of the bridge. Lior kept her hand in his fur the whole way, and neither of them mentioned it, and both of them knew.

At the last arch, he paused and looked back once at the span of teeth—his grandmother's, his father's, Wren's, his own. *One wolf strong.* It should have felt like poverty. Instead it felt like a door finally opening after sixty years of pushing on a wall.

"Lior," he said. "The court will ask why they should believe a human. They'll want proof the guild betrayed the covenant."

She tapped the strap of her ledger. "I have forty years of Corvane's entries memorized. Cross-referenced. Annotated." A thin, dangerous smile. "I've been waiting my whole life for someone to finally read my footnotes."

Beyond the pines, horns began to sound—deep, mournful, many-voiced. The Court of the Long Winter had seen them coming.

And with the horns, Fen smelled something else on the wind, something that froze the blood in his newly claimed veins: wolf-musk and cold iron and, underneath it, unmistakably, the stink of human steel.

Guild blades. In Vharn.

Corvane had not waited for them to reach the court.
