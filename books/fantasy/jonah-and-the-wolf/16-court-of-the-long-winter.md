## 16. Court of the Long Winter

The cold in Vharn did not come from the wind. Lior felt that at once, standing on the ice-slick causeway that led up to the Court of the Long Winter. The cold came from *above*—from the empty places where stars should have been, pouring down a darkness so dense it seemed to press against her shoulders like a second cloak.

Fen walked half a step ahead of her, and even he looked diminished here. His shoulders were squared, his chin lifted, but she could see the tension knotted between his shoulder blades, the way his breath came shallow through his teeth. He had told her on the bridge what waited for him: a court that had named him exile, coward, oath-breaker. A court that had every reason to let him die slowly rather than hear him speak.

But it was not Fen they had summoned. That was the strange thing about it. When the border-guardians of the Bridge of Teeth had finally yielded—when Fen had stood bleeding among the frost-bitten reeds and roared out his claim to the birthright he'd abandoned—the oldest of them had turned its burning eyes not to him but to her, and said, in a voice like ice cracking over deep water:

*Bring the ledger-keeper. The Court will hear the small one.*

So now Lior Hartwell, runaway apprentice, invisible girl, walked into the wolf-court with two covenant-stones fused to the skin of her left hand like a brand of living amber, and an ink-stained ledger bound in grey leather clutched beneath her arm, and nothing else. No title. No protector who would be permitted to speak for her. No mother.

Just the numbers. Just the entries. Just the truth, written in her own careful hand.

The causeway climbed toward gates of black ice taller than any cathedral spire in Callowmere. Through them she could see the court itself: a vast hollowed mountain, its ceiling lost in darkness, its floor a frozen lake so clear she could see shapes beneath it—old bones, perhaps, or older promises. Hundreds of wolves sat ring upon ring around the frozen water, some in their four-legged shapes, vast as oxen, some walking upright in the half-form that Fen wore when he forgot to be careful. Their eyes caught what little light remained and threw it back in gold and green.

At the far end of the lake, on a dais of unmelting snow, sat the Winter King.

Lior had imagined many things on the road north. She had not imagined that the king of Vharn would look *tired*. He was enormous—a wolf in the man-shape, white-furred, silver-crowned with antlers of black ice—but there was a sag to his great frame, a dimness in his eyes, like a fire that had burned all night and found no morning coming. Around him, the court pressed close, and every face wore the same expression she had seen in the Ledgerhouse the night the stars went out: fear wearing the mask of anger.

A herald-wolf threw back its head and howled one long note that shivered the lake ice. Then silence. Then a voice from the ring:

"The human comes."

"She comes," another answered, and the words rolled around the court like a stone dropped into a well. "She comes. She comes."

Fen started forward beside her, and a dozen throats grew low and warning, and he stopped, jaw working. Lior touched his arm once—quick, before she could lose her nerve—and stepped out onto the ice alone.

It held her. Of course it held her. It was waiting for her.

"Approach," said the Winter King.

She walked. Her boots made almost no sound; her breath made small ghosts in the air. Two hundred pairs of eyes tracked her, and she felt each one like the point of a pin. This, she thought absurdly, was what she had spent six years avoiding—the being-looked-at, the being-*known*. In Callowmere she had perfected the art of the unremarkable: the bowed head, the quick step, the ledger held just high enough to hide behind. Corvane had never once learned her name. Most of the guild hadn't known she existed until the night the wards fell.

And now she stood at the center of a court of monsters, and there was nowhere left to hide, and her heart was going like a trapped bird against her ribs.

She stopped ten paces from the dais and bowed—not deeply, not servilely. The way one clerk bows to another across a difficult account.

"You are the keeper's daughter," the Winter King said.

"I am Lior Hartwell." Her voice came out steadier than she expected. "Apprentice of the Guild of Impossible Things. And I'm the one holding your stones, your Highness, so I'd take it kindly if we got the blaming part over quickly and moved on to the fixing part."

Somewhere in the rings, a wolf laughed—one short bark, hastily smothered. The Winter King's ears twitched.

"You are bold for something so small."

"I'm bold for someone who's been shouted at by bigger things than you this month." She lifted her chin. "Your court says humanity broke the covenant. That we let the wards fail. That the darkening of the stars is our doing. I've come to tell you that you're wrong—and worse than wrong, you've been *robbed*, and so have we, by the same thieves. But I can't say it. I have to show it. Will you let me open my ledger?"

The king regarded her for a long moment. Beneath the frozen lake, something vast shifted.

"A ledger," he said, "is a human thing. Ink and paper. We keep our covenants in blood and bone."

"Yes," Lior said. "And how has that gone for everyone lately?"

That laugh again, harder to smother this time. The Winter King's gaze flicked past her—to Fen, standing rigid at the court's edge—and something old and complicated moved across his face.

"Open it, then, small one. You have until the ice beneath you remembers your weight."

Which, she suspected, was a very short time.

* * *

She set the ledger on the ice and knelt beside it, and the stones on her left hand flared faintly warm as if recognizing old friends. She had rehearsed this on the road—in her head, in whispers, drawing diagrams in frost on the inside of their shelter. Ledger-craft, her mother's craft, though Lior hadn't known it then. Not arithmetic. Not bookkeeping. *Argument*, made of evidence.

"Every covenant has terms," she began, pitching her voice to carry. "Terms, conditions, and obligations. Your side: the wolf-realm holds the border, keeps the wild things off the roads, lends strength to the wards. Our side: humanity keeps the ledger, tends the altars, and—" she turned a page, "—I quote, from the original entry, transcribed in the first Keeper's own hand: *each realm shall hold the other's memory, that neither may forget what was promised.*"

Murmurs moved through the rings. She pressed on.

"Now. On the third night of Frostmonth, the border wards collapsed. Not frayed. Not faded. Collapsed—all at once, along nine hundred miles of border, which no natural failure does. Natural failures are like leaks. They start in one place." She turned the page again, and held it up, angling it toward the court. "Here is the guild's own ward-log, copied out in my hand the night it happened. Nine hundred miles. One instant. And look—" her finger found the line, "—the energy didn't disperse. It was *drawn*. Siphoned. Something drank the wards dry."

"And what would a human know of drinking wards?" snarled a voice from the second ring—a she-wolf with a scarred muzzle, eyes like green glass. "Words. Ink. A pup brings paper to a court of teeth."

"Paper," Lior said quietly, "that proves where the wards' power went. Because it didn't vanish, mistress. Power doesn't vanish—it moves. And I traced it." She turned three pages at once, the entries glowing faintly now, the ink remembering itself. "For eleven years, the Guild of Impossible Things has been skimming power from the border wards—a thimbleful at a time, hidden in the margins of impossible goods. Bottled thunder. Keys that open yesterday. Every miracle the guild ever sold was paid for with the covenant's strength, siphoned through the Ledgerhouse vaults, metered so carefully no one noticed. Except ledgers notice everything. That's the whole point of them."

The scarred wolf surged to her feet. "Lies—"

"Then refute the numbers." Lior's voice cracked out sharper than she meant it to, and she didn't care. "You want to blame humanity? Fine. Blame us for the first thousand years of honest keeping. But don't call it a broken covenant when it was a *looted* one. Look at the sums! Cross-check them against your own star-tithes! Your stars haven't failed because humans stopped believing in you—they've failed because every year, more of their light was diverted into guild vaults below Callowmere, hoarded by a man named Corvane, who is right now using the last of it to forge a covenant of his own—with himself as sole keeper, and both our realms as property!"

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

The Winter King rose. It took him a long time, joint by joint, like a mountain deciding to be a landslide, and when he descended from the dais and crossed the ice toward her, the whole court held its breath. He stopped before her, close enough that she could see the frost blooming and dying on his fur with each breath, and he lowered his great head until his eyes were level with hers.

"You accuse the guild," he rumbled. "Show me the margin entries."

"They're here. Pages ninety through one-forty-four. Dated, itemized, initialed." She swallowed. "Initialed C.C. My master's mark. I watched him sign the authorization myself, the night the wards fell, when he thought I was too frightened to understand what I was seeing."

The king studied her. Then, slowly, he extended one claw—black ice, sharp as a guillotine blade—and touched the open page.

The ledger *sang*.

It was a sound Lior felt in her teeth and behind her eyes: a low harmonic chord, the resonance of true accounting, of ink that remembered honestly meeting a judge who could weigh it. The court heard it too. She saw ears swivel, saw hackles lower, saw understanding move through those rings like dawn moving across a valley.

"The ink does not lie," said the Winter King softly. "Human ink. Human hands. Honest." He straightened, and turned to face his court, and his voice filled the whole hollowed mountain. "Hear the judgment of the Long Winter. The failing of the stars is not the sin of humankind. It is theft—the theft of one man, sheltered by one guild, at the cost of two realms. The covenant is wounded, not betrayed. And the wound can yet be closed—if the thief does not reach the Altar first."

Cheers went up—or what passed for cheers among wolves: a rising wall of howls that shook frost from the cavern ceiling. Lior stood in the middle of it, shaking slightly, gripping her ledger like a shield.

And then she realized the king was looking at her again. Looking, really looking, the way no master had ever looked at her, and when he spoke, his voice carried only to her and to Fen at the court's edge.

"When you stood on my ice and opened your book," he said, "you were afraid. But you did not hide. Do you know how rare that is, small one? To be seen entire, and stand anyway?" Something that might have been the ghost of a smile moved in his tired eyes. "My court called you *the ledger-keeper*. Not the human. Not the thief's apprentice. Keeper. Names matter here. Yours has already been written."

Heat prickled behind Lior's eyes, sudden and unwelcome, and she blinked it away furiously because queens of anything did not cry in front of wolf-courts, even not-yet queens, even probably-not-ever queens.

"There's one more thing," she managed. "Corvane took the third stone. From the guild vaults. He'll be making for the Nightless Altar—and he has a head start, and hounds, and enough stolen ward-power to burn anyone who gets in his way."

"Then we ride tonight," the Winter King said.

But Fen was already pushing through the rings toward her, and there was something wrong in his gait, something urgent, and when he reached her he caught her arm and turned her gently aside, and his voice dropped low.

"Lior. The scouts just returned from the southern passes." His golden eyes were grim. "Corvane isn't heading for the Altar through human lands. He's already *in* Vharn. He crossed the Bridge of Teeth two days ago—he bought passage with guild-gold and stolen light. And he's taken hostages to hold the path open." Fen's jaw tightened. "He took the hedge-witch. The one who kept your mother's cottage."

The warmth of the stones on her hand suddenly felt like a fever.

"He knows what you are now," Fen said. "And he means to make sure you never reach the Altar at all."

Above them, through the great gates, the starless dark pressed down like a held breath—and somewhere south, in the frozen passes, a thief was carrying the last piece of the sky toward the one place it could be remade.
