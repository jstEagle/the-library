## 18. The Price of the Altar

The Nightless Altar sat at the crown of the world like a wound that had learned to shine.

Lior had imagined it a hundred times on the long road north—had built it in her head out of candlelight and guild rumor—but nothing she'd constructed came close to the truth. It was not a building. It was not even, properly, a place. It was a bowl of black stone cradled between two mountains, and it glowed with a light that cast no shadows. The stars overhead were still dark—had been dark for weeks now, since the wards fell—but here, above the clouds, the altar's radiance made its own false sky, pale and sourceless, so that everything stood lit from all sides at once and cast no shadow anywhere. Even Fen, standing at the rim of the bowl with his cloak stirring in a wind that seemed to come from beneath the world, looked strangely flattened, as if he were a drawing of himself.

"It doesn't feel right," he said quietly. "Light without shadow."

"Neither does a covenant without cost," Lior said, and was surprised at how steady her voice came out. She'd been practicing steadiness for three days now, ever since the hedge-witch's memory-charm had shown her the last thing her mother ever wrote: *The altar takes what you love best. There is no way around it. There is only the choosing.*

She had not told Fen. She had told him about the rite, about the words her blood remembered, about the way the stones in her palm sang when they crossed the tree-line into Vharn's border country. She had told him everything except the price. And every hour she didn't tell him, the silence grew another layer of stone around itself, until carrying it felt like carrying a second heart, heavy and cold and beating out of time with her own.

Fen glanced at her sidelong. He always knew when something sat wrong in her—he'd said once that she smelled like ink when she lied, which was absurd, but also, she suspected, true. "You've been quiet since the ridge."

"I'm always quiet."

"You're *quiet-quiet*. This is different." His ears—the wolf ones, the ones that appeared when he forgot to keep them hidden—twitched toward her. "Lior."

"We should make camp while there's light," she said, which was a joke between them now, because there was always light here. "Even if the light never ends. My legs think it's midnight whether the sky agrees or not."

He let it go. That was the thing about Fen that still caught her off guard, weeks after the smuggler's moon-path and the village that forgot itself and the bridge of teeth where he had stood bleeding before the border-guardians and refused to kneel until they recognized him: he let things go when she needed them gone, and picked them up again exactly when she could bear the weight. He had a keeper's patience in him, though he'd never kept anything but his own exile.

They made camp at the altar's eastern rim, in the lee of a fallen pillar carved all over with hands—thousands of them, pressed into the stone like leaves, some small as a child's, some broad and clawed. Covenant-keepers, reaching across centuries. Lior ran her fingers over one handprint whose fingers were stained, permanently, with something dark. Ink, maybe. Or blood. Probably both. Keeper's work always was.

The stones in her satchel hummed against her hip. Two fused to the back of her left hand now, sunk into the skin like coins into wax, warm as living things. The third—Corvane's prize—waited somewhere south, and every hour it waited, the man who held it bent it further toward himself. Fen's contacts in Vharn had brought word two nights past: Corvane had reached the Nightless Altar's approach roads with a column of guild-hands and a portable forge, and was building something at the altar's foot. A cage for the rite, they said. A way to hold the covenant open while he alone stood inside it.

They had perhaps a day. Maybe less.

So Lior did what she always did when the world became too large to hold: she took out her ledger.

It was nearly full now. The old entries—the jar of bottled thunder, the key that opened yesterday, the crate of winter delivered out of season—seemed like relics from another girl's life, a girl who had hidden in a counting-house and believed that cataloging impossible things was the same as understanding them. She turned to a fresh page and began, in her small precise hand, to write down what she wanted to remember.

*The way Fen looks at the false sky and scowls, because wolves are owed real darkness.*
*The sound he makes when he laughs, which is half growl and mostly surprise.*
*The night on the moon-path when I fell asleep against his shoulder and he went so still I thought he'd stopped breathing rather than wake me.*
*The smell of the hedge-witch's cottage. Woodsmoke and rosemary. My mother's smell, which is my smell.*
*His name. Not Wolf. Not exile. Fen. He gave it to me on the third day like it was worth giving.*

She wrote until the page was full, and then she read it back twice, and then she folded the corner down so she could find it again, knowing even as she did it that folded corners were how you marked pages you intended never to return to honestly.

"Lior." Fen crouched beside her fire—her fire; he still wouldn't light them himself, some old superstition of his exile—and offered her a strip of dried elk. "Eat. Tomorrow you'll need—" He stopped. Started over. "Tomorrow we finish this."

"Yes," she said. "We do."

And because she could not say the true thing, she said the next truest thing. "Tell me about the first pact. The original one. You were there."

He settled beside her, close enough that his warmth reached her through her cloak, and told it again—not the formal version he'd given the wolf-court, all lineage and grievance, but the human-sized version, the one with details. How the first keeper had been a shepherd-girl who got lost chasing a lamb into Vharn and found herself face-to-face with the Winter King, and instead of running had asked him, furiously, why his realm ate her villages' lambs. How the bargain had been struck over three nights of argument. How the shepherd-girl had paid the altar's price without flinching, though no record remained of what she'd given up, because the price was always the same kind of thing and the same kind of thing leaves no record—it simply isn't there anymore, a shape cut out of a life, edges clean.

"What did she lose?" Lior asked, though she knew there was no answer.

"No one knows. That's the cruelty of it. Whatever she loved best, she lost it and couldn't mourn it properly, because mourning requires remembering." Fen stared into the fire. "The wolves used to say that's why the covenant has held so long. Grief with no name keeps no grudges."

"Grief with no name," Lior repeated softly. "That sounds like something my mother would have written."

"She wrote it. It's carved above the vault door in the Ledgerhouse. You never noticed?"

"I never noticed anything important until it was too late to matter." She smiled so it wouldn't show as anything else. "Tell me about the Winter King. Was he terrible?"

"He was lonely," Fen said. "Terrible and lonely, which is most terrible things, if you look at them long enough."

* *

She woke before him, as she'd planned to.

The false light never changed, but her body kept its own hours, and she lay for a while watching Fen sleep—truly asleep, unguarded, the human line of his jaw softened, the wolf in him banked down to embers. He slept the way exiles sleep, she'd noticed: lightly, facing the door, one hand always near a weapon. Except lately. Lately he'd begun sleeping like a man with a home, which frightened her more than Corvane's column on the road below, and which she would not allow herself to think about, and thought about anyway, thoroughly, the way you press a bruise to prove it's yours.

Then she got up, quietly, and went down into the altar-bowl alone.

Up close the black stone was warm underfoot, faintly pulsing, like the hide of an enormous sleeping animal. The carved hands covered every surface, spiraling inward toward the center, where a basin the size of a wash-tub held something that was not water and not light but both at once—a slow silver turning, like milk stirred by a patient finger. The altar's payment-basin. Her mother had knelt at one of these once. Perhaps at this very one. Had poured out whatever she loved best so that her daughter could grow up unremarkable and unmarked in a guild counting-house, safe behind someone else's edited memories.

*I chose to forget,* her mother's charm had said, in that voice like dry leaves. *Because the ones who love you are the ones they can use. I made myself a woman with nothing to take hostage.*

Lior knelt at the basin's edge and put her hands in the silver.

It was cold, then warm, then it *knew* her—the way the covenant-stones had known her, the way the ledger-ink had rewritten itself under her fingers. Images surfaced and sank: her mother's face. Jonah, the stable-boy from her first year at the Ledgerhouse, who'd shared stolen plums with her and called her Sparrow and died of marsh-fever before she understood she'd loved him. The taste of rain on the Ledgerhouse roof where she used to hide. Fen's laugh. Fen's shoulder. Fen saying *his name*, giving it to her like it was worth giving.

The basin showed her all of it, patiently, the way a merchant lays out wares, and she understood that it was asking her to choose. Not to surrender everything—just what she loved best. One thread, or several, pulled clean out of the weave.

She thought about lying to it. About offering up something second-best, some clever partial payment, the way she'd once haggled a peddler down on a jar of bottled thunder. But the silver turned, and turned, showing her Fen asleep facing the door, Fen going still so she could rest, Fen standing between her and Corvane's hounds with his ribs opening like a book—and she understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the altar could not be cheated because she could not cheat herself. What she loved best was not a thing she could misfile. She was a keeper's daughter. She had spent her whole life among ledgers. She knew true weight when she held it.

"All right," she whispered to the basin. To the hands carved all around her, thousands of keepers who had knelt where she knelt. "All right. But I get to choose the hour."

She climbed back up the bowl's side with the dawn-that-wasn't breaking in her eyes, and found Fen awake, and pretending he hadn't been frantic, badly.

"You left."

"I needed to see it. Alone." She sat down across the dead fire from him and wrapped her hands around her knees. "Fen. If tomorrow goes wrong—if Corvane—"

"It won't."

"If it does." She made her voice the ledger-voice, flat and exact, because feeling would ruin it. "I need you to know that these last weeks have been the only time in my life I haven't been invisible. You saw me when the entire guild looked straight through me. You gave me your name like it was worth giving. Whatever happens tomorrow—whatever the rite costs, whatever anyone pays—you changed what I am. Permanently. That can't be taken back by anything, even by me."

Something moved behind his eyes—the quick animal caution, reading her the way he read weather. "Lior. What does the rite cost?"

"A keeper's dearest memory. That's all the records say. It might be nothing. It might be a smell, a song." She shrugged, and hated how easily the lie wore her face. "Keepers' prices aren't written down. Remember? Grief with no name keeps no grudges."

He watched her a long moment. The wind off the false sky moved through his hair, and for one heartbeat his eyes caught the light and shone amber, wholly wolf, wholly ancient, wholly the creature she had dreamed of before she ever met him—she knew that now, knew those dreams had been her blood calling across the broken wards to the one being alive who would come for her.

"If you're lying to me," he said quietly, "I'll know. Eventually. I always know."

"Then you'll know eventually," she said, and smiled, and the smile hurt more than any wound Corvane's hounds had given her. "Come on. We should sleep. Tomorrow we save two worlds."

He didn't move for a moment. Then he reached across the ashes and took her hand—the one with the stones fused to it, warm as a sleeping bird—and held it, briefly, the way you hold something you already know you're keeping.

"Two worlds," he agreed. "One keeper."

She slept that night curled a hand's breadth from him, not touching, and memorized the sound of his breathing the way she'd once memorized inventory: item, quantity, irreplaceable.

* *

Corvane came at what passed for noon, and he came loudly.

They heard the column before they saw it—iron boots on mountain stone, the groan of the portable forge, guild-hands chanting ward-verses in voices trained flat. From the altar's rim they watched the Master of Ledgers ascend the road with the third covenant-stone carried before him on a pole like a captured banner, and behind him forty men in grey, and behind *them*, lashed to a cart, the great iron frame of his cage-for-the-rite, its bars etched with binding-script that made Lior's fused stones ache.

"He means to stand inside it while he forges," Fen said. "Make himself the only keeper. Cut everyone else out of the covenant forever."

"Then we take the stone before he reaches the bowl." Lior checked her satchel: ledger, chalk, the hedge-witch's charm, the vial of salt-and-ash. Everything accounted for. Except the one thing that wasn't an object at all. "There's a path down the western face. Smugglers' cut. We can reach the bowl ahead of him if we run."

Fen looked at her. Really looked, the way he had on the moon-path the first time he'd decided to trust her, and she saw him weighing her—the flat ledger-voice, the rehearsed calm, the goodbye-shaped things she'd been saying since yesterday like coins counted out in advance.

"Sparrow," he said—Jonah's old name for her, which she'd told him about once, weeks ago, beside a fire very like this one. "Whatever you're not telling me—"

"Is mine to carry until it isn't," she finished. "Keeper's privilege."

And because the moment was too tender and too terrible to survive honesty, she did the last ordinary thing she would ever do with these memories intact: she leaned over and kissed his cheek, quick as sealing a letter.

"For luck," she said. "For tomorrow."

Then she turned toward the western path, and began to run, and did not look back—because looking back was how keepers lost their nerve, and she had a basin of silver waiting at the bottom of the world, and a choice already made, and one night left to love her life before she gave the best of it away.

Behind her, she heard Fen follow. Ahead, the false light burned on, shadowless and pitiless, and the altar waited with the patience of all debts finally come due.
