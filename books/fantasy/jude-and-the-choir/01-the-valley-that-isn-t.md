## 1. The Valley That Isn't

The trouble with Hollowmere is that it's perfect, and everyone knows better than to say so.

I was thinking this at six in the morning with my hand up a goat named Duchess, who was thinking about becoming a mother and had opinions about my technique. Duchess is a large white Saanen with the temperament of a disappointed duchess, which is where she got her name, and she communicates primarily through sighs. Most of the animals here do, actually. Sighs, grumbles, little huffed complaints. It's safer that way.

Because the other thing about Hollowmere—the thing nobody says—is that I can talk to them properly. Words. Sentences. The whole embarrassing vocabulary of it.

"Left," I told Duchess, quietly, under my breath, in the way that isn't words exactly but is close enough that any animal within a mile would understand me. "You're pushing wrong. Left, and then breathe."

Duchess pushed left, breathed, and delivered a kid the color of weak tea into the straw. Then she looked back at me with an expression I chose to interpret as gratitude but was probably contempt. Goats don't really do gratitude. It's one of the things I like about them.

"There," I said out loud, because out loud I could say anything, it was only the other way that got me in trouble. "Beautiful. You did wonderfully."

*I know,* said Duchess, in sighs and a flick of one ear. *Bring me the good hay. Not the brown stuff. The brown stuff is an insult.*

"The brown stuff is what we have."

*Then we will both be disappointed together.* She turned to lick her kid, conversation over. Duchess has never once asked me a question about myself, which I find restful.

I wiped my hands on my apron and stood there a moment in the doorway of the barn, looking out at the valley, because it was morning and the valley deserved a look. That's the thing about Hollowmere—you look at it whether you want to or not. It arranges itself for viewing. The mountains came down on either side like hands cupped around a candle flame, green to the treeline, grey above, and the mist sitting in the low fields the way it always does, patient as a cat waiting for a door to open. The town itself huddled in the middle: two hundred rooftops, one chapel spire, smoke rising straight up from every chimney because there's no wind in Hollowmere worth mentioning. There hasn't been weather here in living memory. There's just *weather-shaped*, the same soft grey-gold day repeating itself like a song stuck in the valley's throat.

It's beautiful. I want to be clear about that. Anyone who came here—and nobody comes here—would call it beautiful. The light falls through the mist like something out of a painting your grandmother would hang over the mantel. The bread smells like bread should smell. The creek runs cold and clean and full of trout that practically apologize when you catch them.

And no road comes in, and no road goes out.

That's not quite true, and I want to be precise, because precision matters when everything else is fog. There IS a road. It leaves the town at the western end, past the Hendersons' place, past the last fence post, up the shoulder of the mountain—and then it stops being a road. Not blocked. Not washed out. It just stops being. You walk up it, and somewhere past the fence post your feet get tired and your thoughts get round and comfortable, and you find yourself thinking, *well, actually, why would I go anywhere, everything I need is right here,* and you turn around, and you're home by supper, and you feel fine. Wonderful, even. Everyone feels wonderful about it.

Old Marta Tulley walked that road once when I was nine years old. Her son had gone before her—I'll come back to that—and she said she'd had enough of feeling fine. The whole town watched her go. She got as far as the bend where the pines start, and then she stopped, and turned around, and waved at us all with a big smile on her face, and called out, "Forgot my shawl!" And she came home, and she made tea, and she never spoke of it again, and three weeks later she couldn't tell you why she'd been standing at the bend of the road waving at anybody. Couldn't remember her son's name either. Nobody could. There was a boy who used to live in the Tulley house, we all agreed, nice lad, gone off somewhere, and wasn't that nice for him, getting out.

I remember him. I remember his name was Edmund, and he had a laugh like a door hinge, and the night before he left he sat on our porch steps and told me, in strictest confidence, that he could hear the mountains breathing. He thought it was wonderful. I didn't have the heart—or maybe I didn't have the courage—to tell him I'd heard them too, all my life, and that lately they sounded less like breathing and more like holding their breath.

Anyway. No one leaves. That's the rule. Nobody wrote it down. It doesn't need writing down. It lives in the valley the way the mist does, and it settles over you the same way, and after twenty-six years I've learned to wear it like a coat that almost fits.

"Coral!" Aunt Verity's voice carried across the yard the way it carries everywhere, with the confidence of a woman who has never once wondered if she should lower it. "Are those kids born yet? The Hendersons are asking!"

"The Hendersons can come ask the goats themselves," I called back, which was rude, and she knew I was being rude, and she let it pass, which meant she wanted something.

Verity appeared at the barn door a minute later, which for Verity was restraint. She's seventy-one and built like the chapel door—narrow, dark wood, and not opening for anyone she hasn't decided to admit. Today she was wearing her good shawl, the blue one, which meant Company Was Expected.

"You're wearing the shawl," I said.

"I'm always wearing the shawl."

"You wear the shawl when you're expecting company. Last time was the census man, and the time before that was when the choir needed new robes and you intended to bully the committee."

She had the grace to look caught. "There's a young man coming today."

"No."

"I haven't said anything yet."

"You put the good teapot on the shelf by the window where the light hits it. You only do that when there's a suitor. And you're wearing the shawl. And you said 'young man' instead of 'the minister' or 'your cousin's letter,' which are the only other subjects that make you put the teapot out." I picked up the kid—weak tea, I'd named her already, privately—and tucked her against Duchess's flank. "No."

"He's passing through," Verity said.

"Nobody passes through."

"He's walking the long way to his sister's in Corrowdale."

"There is no Corrowdale. There's no direction that has anything in it. You taught me that yourself, when I was small and asked what was over the mountains. You said, 'Nothing, darling, nothing at all,' and then you gave me honey cake so I'd stop asking."

Verity's face did something complicated and brief, like a window opening onto a room you weren't supposed to see, and then it closed again. "He'll be here by noon. His name is Jude. He wrote ahead—he stayed the night in Ashford with the Pells, they passed the letter along from the crossroads, don't ask me how it traveled, the mail finds its ways. He seems pleasant. Educated."

"Aunt Verity. He's *passing through*. Men passing through don't court goat farmers."

"This one might. You're very courtable."

"I smell of goat."

"That's never stopped anyone worth having." She came further into the barn, which she never does—Verity regards livestock the way she regards modern poetry, with polite total incomprehension—and lowered her voice, which frightened me more than shouting would have. "Coral. You're twenty-six. You're clever and kind and far too pretty for the life you've got, and you spend all your time with animals because they don't ask you questions. This town has eleven unmarried men under forty and I have personally vetted each one and found them wanting. Along comes a stranger, an actual stranger, with good boots and a letter of introduction, and you want to say no?"

"Yes."

"Well." She straightened the shawl on her shoulders with two sharp tugs. "Wear the green dress anyway."

And she left, because Verity delivers verdicts; she doesn't stay for the appeal.

I stood in the barn with the new kid breathing against my boot and the morning going on outside, perfect and still and suffocating as ever, and I thought about the word she'd used. *Courtable.* As though I were a house for sale. As though the problem with my life were merely inventory.

Here's what I didn't say to her, because I don't say it to anyone: I said yes to things all the time. I said yes to the choir on Thursdays, yes to helping at the harvest festival, yes to mending the Hendersons' fences and reading to old Pell when his eyes went bad. I am, by every measurement Hollowmere keeps, content. Ask anyone. Coral Dunmore, content as a cat in a sunbeam. And it's even mostly true, most days. The lie isn't a hard one to maintain because it's built out of true materials. I do love this valley. I love the goats and the mist and the way the light comes down the mountain at four o'clock in October like honey poured slow. If I could want what I have, I'd be the happiest woman in the world.

But wanting is the one thing the valley can't quite grow here, and I've got a weed of it growing in me that won't die. Some mornings it's quiet. Some mornings it's this loud. Today it was loud, and it sounded like: *a stranger is coming, a real one, someone who has been somewhere else, someone who might know what's over the mountains, and you're going to stand there in the green dress and pretend to be content while you interrogate him for every scrap of elsewhere he's carrying.*

* * *

I spent the middle of the morning lying to the animals, which is harder than it sounds.

Not lying with words—they'd forgive me words. Lying with the other voice. Every animal in Hollowmere knows me the way you know a neighbor whose kitchen window faces yours, and every one of them would hear it if I came out to the paddock humming a different tune than usual. So I did the chores in the ordinary order at the ordinary pace and told myself I wasn't hurrying, and the horses didn't believe me for a second.

*You're going to meet someone,* said Bess, the bay mare, while I brushed her down. She says everything in long slow sentences, like sermons.

"I'm meeting a man for tea. My aunt arranged it. It's nothing."

*You braided your hair.*

"It's how I braid my hair."

*You braided it differently. There's more of it showing at the front.* She flicked an ear at me, insufferably calm. *When the gray goose flew over last autumn, you braided it like that. When the comet came. You do it when you hope.*

Bess is seventeen years old and has known me since I was nine and has never once let me get away with anything, which is why I prefer the goats.

"Go eat your hay," I said.

*The brown hay?*

"...Yes."

*We will both be disappointed together,* said Bess, quoting Duchess, because the animals talk to each other about me, I'm certain of it. I finished with her and went and sat on the fence and let the mist sit around me and tried to have a serious internal conversation, which went approximately as follows:

*It's just tea,* I told myself. *One stranger, one pot, one afternoon. He'll describe the road he came in on—which will turn out, when pressed, to be the western road, the one that stops existing—and he'll talk about his sister in a town that isn't on any map, and by the end of it he'll be rubbing his eyes and saying what a lovely valley this is and how rested he feels, and by tomorrow he'll have forgotten why he was walking anywhere at all, and Verity will be disappointed, and I'll be exactly where I started, which is here.*

And underneath that sensible speech, the weed whispered its whisper, which was: *or.*

Or he's different. Or the valley's grip slips sometimes—I've seen it slip, I've seen the mist thin at the tree line on certain mornings until you could almost see shapes beyond it, roads maybe, or the ghost of roads, or just the idea of a road, the way you can see the idea of a face in firelight. Or a man with good boots and an educated voice walks in from nowhere, from *outside*, from the place that is allegedly nothing, and looks at this valley with eyes that haven't grown up inside it, and for one afternoon I get to borrow those eyes. For one afternoon I get to see Hollowmere from the outside, the way it must look to whatever's out there looking in.

If anything is out there looking in.

Which is a thought I don't let myself have often, because once you start wondering what's watching the valley, you have to wonder why it needs watching. And the mountains hold their breath when I think that way, and I don't like hearing them hold their breath.

* * *

At half past eleven I put on the green dress, because I am my aunt's creature after all, and because the alternative was explaining to her why I hadn't, and Verity's disappointment has more moving parts than her anger. I braided my hair the hopeful way. I fed the chickens, who wished me luck in the manner of chickens—that is, by screaming. I told myself the whole walk down to the house that my heart was pounding from the hill.

The house was full of smells doing their best: bread, cinnamon, the good teapot warming on the stove. Verity had cut flowers. From the garden, yes, but also from the wild patch up behind the well, which blooms out of season and always has, and which she pretends is ordinary the way she pretends everything here is ordinary.

"Sit," she commanded. "Don't slouch. If he asks about your family, mention the Pells are cousins. If he asks what you do, say 'farmer,' not 'goat girl,' it sounds unserious. And Coral—" she fixed me with the look she uses for the choir's altos, the one that means *we have discussed this and the discussion is over* —"be charming."

"I'm always charming."

"You are *wry*, which is different, and men from outside don't always know the difference."

"And you'd know all about men from outside," I said, mostly to needle her, and was rewarded with that same flicker crossing her face, the window opening and shutting, the glimpse of some room in her that I'd never been allowed into. She turned away to fuss with the teapot and said nothing at all, which from Verity was a shout.

The knock came at twelve exactly. Punctual. Of course punctual—no train schedules to blame out here, no coaches, no clock but the sun and whoever kept it. A man who arrives at the minute he promised in a valley where arriving is impossible is either very careful or very strange, and I remember thinking, with my hand on the latch, *please be strange.*

I opened the door.

He was standing in the mist at the edge of the yard with a leather satchel over one shoulder and mud on his good boots, and he was smiling the smile of a man about to say something rehearsed—and then he saw me, and lost the thread of it entirely, and stood there with his mouth open like a fish considering philosophy. Which I confess did wonders for my nerves. There's nothing like watching a confident man misplace his confidence to make a body feel less alone in her own awkwardness.

"Miss Dunmore?" he managed.

"Mr.—?"

"Jude." He said it like he was checking the name fit. "Jude. Your aunt is expecting me."

"She is. She's armed with cinnamon buns. Come in before she comes out here."

And he laughed—startled, real, a door-hinge sort of laugh—and stepped over the threshold into my house, into my valley, carrying the outside in with him like mud on his boots.

Behind him, out in the paddock, every animal on my land had gone absolutely silent. Not calm—silent. Forty heads lifted at once, staring at the stranger in the doorway, and not one of them made a sound.

I'd never seen them do that before.

I told myself it was the weather.

There is no weather in Hollowmere. But I told myself it was the weather, and I smiled, and I brought him in to meet my aunt, and that was the first hour of the last ordinary day I ever had.
