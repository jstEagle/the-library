## 2. A Stranger at the Gate

Aunt Verity had spent the entire morning pretending not to fuss, which in Verity's language meant fussing at a volume normally reserved for house fires.

"You'll wear the blue," she said. Not a suggestion. Verity didn't make suggestions; she issued decrees with the calm of a woman who had never once been contradicted and intended to keep her record intact.

"It's got a hole in the pocket," I said.

"Then you'll put your hand through it charmingly." She set down the teapot with a click that meant *the negotiation is over*. "His name is Jude. He's coming up from—"

She paused. Just for half a heartbeat, but I've spent twenty-six years learning to read the pauses of Hollowmere the way farmers read weather, and that one was a storm front if ever I'd heard it.

"From where?" I asked.

"From away," she said smoothly. "The coach comes through on the mill road at noon."

"The mill road doesn't go anywhere," I said. "It goes to the ford, and the ford goes to trees, and the trees go to more trees."

"Then he'll be very well traveled by the time he arrives." She smiled at me, and it was a real smile, warm as fresh bread, which somehow made it worse. "Coral. Be kind. That's all I ask. Be kind, and be careful, and don't tell him about the goats."

I looked at her. The goats were, arguably, my single most prominent feature. I talked to them. They answered. Any stranger spending ten minutes in my company would learn about the goats whether I told him or not.

"Aunt Verity," I said, "you cannot ask me not to mention the thing I am."

"I'm not asking you not to be it," she said, and her voice went soft in a way that unsettled me far more than the decree about the blue dress. "I'm asking you to let him meet *you* before he meets everything else. There's a difference. You'll understand when you're older."

"I'm twenty-six."

"Yes," she said, "and I'm eighty-one, so do the arithmetic and hush."

So I wore the blue, with my hand in the hole like a woman posing for a portrait of casual indifference, and I walked down to the gate at half past eleven because I was early, obviously, and because the goats had been insufferable about it all morning.

*He smells like paper,* said Marigold, who is the herd's chief gossip and has never once been wrong, which is a burden I would not wish on anyone.

"Everything smells like something," I told her.

*This smells like nothing,* she said. *That's what's wrong.*

I should have listened. Marigold is always right. But I had a hole in my pocket and a decree hanging over my head, and there are days when a person will trade any warning for the possibility of a story, and this was going to be one of those days.

* * *

Jude arrived at noon precisely, walking up the mill road out of the trees that lead nowhere, which should have been the first thing I noticed and instead was merely the first thing I filed away under *later.*

He was tall in an apologetic way, as though height were a condition he hadn't asked for, with dark hair that had clearly lost an argument with the wind somewhere back on the road and brown eyes that did most of his talking before his mouth got involved. He carried a leather satchel and a paper-wrapped bundle, and when he saw me at the gate he stopped, and his whole face did something complicated—a small collapse and rebuild, like a man remembering how to stand.

"Coral?" he said. My name came out of his mouth strange, like he was tasting the spelling of it.

"That depends who's asking," I said, because I am smooth and gracious in all social situations.

"Jude." He offered his hand through the gate bars, then seemed to realize we were separated by a fence like two prisoners in adjacent cells, and laughed—a short, startled laugh, at himself mostly. "Sorry. This is—I rehearsed this. It went better in rehearsal."

"Most things do." I unlatched the gate. "You're early."

"You're earlier."

"My aunt threatened me with the blue dress."

"It's a good dress," he said, and then visibly heard himself, and turned a shade of pink I found, against every intention, rather winning. "That sounded—I mean—it's just a dress, objectively. A good one. Objectively."

"Objectively," I agreed. "Come on. Verity's made enough food for a funeral. If we don't eat some of it she'll take it personally, and she holds grudges the way other people hold mortgages."

He followed me up the path, and here is the thing I want to set down honestly, because later events will try to make me revise it: I liked him. Immediately, stupidly, against my better judgment, I liked him. He had the particular charm of a person who is genuinely surprised to be liked, and there is no defense against that. None.

But the goats knew something first.

As we passed the paddock, Marigold lifted her head—and then lowered it again, slowly, deliberately, the way an old dog lies down when thunder is coming. And the others followed. Eleven goats, mid-morning chaos, all of them simply... stopped. No bleating, no crowding at the fence to inspect the newcomer, none of the usual theatrical outrage at strangers. They stood in the grass like furniture.

"They're shy," Jude said.

"They've never been shy in their lives," I said. "They once held a traveling tinker hostage in that paddock for forty minutes because he smelled interesting."

"Hostage?"

"He wanted his cart back. We negotiated. I did the negotiating."

"With the goats."

"With the goats." I glanced at him sideways. "Is that going to be a problem?"

"No," he said, and something crossed his face too fast for me to catch it. "No, I think it might be the least surprising thing about today."

* * *

Lunch was Verity at maximum hostess power, which is to say she asked Jude four hundred questions and answered at least sixty of them herself. Where was he from? Away. What did he do? He was, he said, "a surveyor"—and I watched Verity's teacup pause an inch above its saucer, just an inch, just long enough.

"A surveyor of what?" I asked.

"Roads," he said. "Mostly roads."

"There aren't many roads here," I said. "There's the mill road, and the church lane, and the loop that takes you around the orchard if you enjoy going in circles."

"That's the thing," Jude said, leaning forward with an earnestness that made my chest do an unauthorized somersault. "That's the thing exactly. Your mill road—where does it come from? Before the ford, I mean. Every road comes from somewhere. Roads are arguments between two places. Someone built them because they needed to get from here to there. But yours—" he shook his head, laughing a little at himself—"yours starts in the middle of nowhere and ends in the middle of nowhere, like someone built a road and forgot to build the places on either end."

"Well," said Verity, "we're a practical people. Why build places you don't need?"

"Nobody needs a road to nowhere," Jude said. "But somebody built one anyway. Somebody laid stone. That's weeks of work. Years. For a road that goes to trees." He looked at me. "Doesn't that strike you as odd?"

I opened my mouth to say something wry and deflecting, and discovered that no—the truth was, it struck me as odd constantly. It struck me as odd the way a loose tooth strikes you as odd: only when you touched it, but oh, how it ached when you did. All my life I had felt the shape of the missing thing at the end of the mill road, the way your tongue finds the gap where a tooth used to be. I'd just assumed everyone felt it and agreed not to mention it, the way Hollowmere agreed not to mention most things.

"It's a valley," I said instead. "Valleys end. That's sort of their whole personality."

"Mm," said Jude, and wrote something in a little notebook, and I decided not to ask about the notebook, and failed immediately.

"What are you writing?"

"Nothing. Habit." He snapped it shut. "Surveyor's habit. We write everything down."

"Why?"

"So it doesn't get lost," he said, and for a moment he looked—not sad, exactly. Looked like a man standing in front of a door he couldn't decide whether to knock on. "Things get lost, where I'm from. People spend their whole lives making sure they don't."

"Where are you from?" I asked again. "Exactly. Not 'away.' Away isn't a place."

And here he did the strangest thing of the whole afternoon, which was considerable competition. He looked at me, and he looked at the window, and he said:

"That's the problem. I keep trying to remember the name of the town I changed coaches in, and it's gone. Just gone. Like a word on the tip of your tongue forever." He smiled, rueful. "Travel does that, I suppose. Wears the names off things."

"Like a road sign left out in the rain," I said.

"Exactly like that." Something in his shoulders unclenched. "Yes. Exactly like that."

Under the table, out of Verity's sightline, his knee bumped mine, and neither of us moved away, and I spent the rest of lunch acutely aware of approximately four square centimeters of my own leg, like a woman possessed.

* * *

After lunch I gave him the tour, because Verity decreed it and because I wanted to, and wanting to frightened me slightly, so I led with sarcasm the way other people lead with lanterns into dark places.

"This is the chapel," I said. "Very old. Nobody remembers how old. The choir sings Thursdays, badly, with conviction."

"It's beautiful," he said, tilting his head back at the stonework, and I watched his face do something complicated again—that collapse-and-rebuild, that flicker of grief or recognition, I couldn't tell which. "Who carved the doorframe?"

"Nobody knows. It's been here since before anyone."

"Someone carved it," he murmured, running his fingers along the stone, and I could have sworn—he paused at a spot near the base, low down where the ivy had grown over, and his hand hovered there like it remembered the way. "Someone always carves it. That's the part people forget."

"You talk like a man reading from notes," I said.

"I talk like a man who reads a lot of notes," he said, and grinned, and I hated how easily I smiled back.

We ended up, eventually, at the top of the pasture where the valley shows you itself whole: the rooftops, the orchards, the smoke rising straight up in windless air, and the hills ringing us all round like a cupped hand. It's a fine view. It's the finest view in Hollowmere. I have stood on that hill a thousand times and thought: *how lucky we are, that no one bothers us, that the world leaves us alone.*

Jude stood beside me and was silent a long moment, and then he said, quietly, almost to himself:

"It's like standing inside a snow globe."

"What?"

"Beautiful," he said quickly. "I mean it's beautiful. Peaceful. I only meant—" He stopped. Started again. "Do you ever leave? The valley, I mean. Do people travel?"

"People go to the orchard," I said. "Some brave souls go to the mill."

"Coral."

"What?"

"Do *you* ever want to leave?"

And there it was, the loose tooth, pressed hard. I felt the ache open all the way down. Because the honest answer was a thing I had never said aloud to another living soul, a thing I barely admitted to myself on the good nights, a thing shaped like a bird beating itself against the inside of a window: yes. Always. Constantly. I wanted to leave the way lungs want air—not as ambition but as function. I wanted to know what was past the hills. I wanted to know why the road ran out. I wanted to know if the world beyond Hollowmere was real, or whether it was like the horizon, a thing that retreated politely whenever approached.

What I said was: "There's nowhere to go. The road ends at trees."

"I know," Jude said. "I walked it this morning. I walked until the trees closed behind me like water, and then somehow I was arriving here anyway." He turned to look at me fully, and his eyes were doing the talking again, saying something urgent and enormous that his mouth wouldn't carry. "How does that happen? How does a man walk away from a place and arrive in it?"

"You took a wrong turn."

"I took three wrong turns. In opposite directions." He laughed, but it wasn't a comfortable laugh. "Coral, I need to ask you something, and I need you not to think I'm mad."

"Too late. Verity vetted you. Madness is practically a requirement."

"The birds," he said. "On the road this morning. There were hundreds of them. Crows, thrushes, a hawk. And they were all silent. Not quiet—silent. Like they were holding their breath. Like they were watching me pass and deciding something." His voice dropped. "They've been silent around me all day. Haven't they."

I opened my mouth to deny it.

Because it was true. All through lunch, all through the walk, the valley's usual noise—the jackdaws on the chapel roof, the wren in the hedge, the endless argumentative commentary of hollowmere's birds—had been absent. I'd registered it the way you register a clock stopping: only now, in the silence of the silence. And the goats. The goats lying down in the grass like the sky had gone heavy.

"They're not afraid of you," I said slowly, because it was true, and truer than anything else I could offer. "Fear makes them loud. They scream at foxes. They scream at the mail cart. Whatever they're doing around you, it's not fear."

"Then what is it?"

"I don't know." I hugged my elbows. The wind moved the grass in long silver strokes, and the hills sat around us, patient as creditors. "Jude, why did you really come here? And don't say the coach. The coach doesn't exist. Don't say a friend. Verity invented the friend. I watched her invent it. Why did you come?"

He was quiet for so long I counted my own heartbeats. Fourteen. Fifteen. Somewhere below us the goats stood in their silent row, watching the gate.

"I came because of a book," he said finally. "An old book. It mentioned a valley that isn't on any map, and a road that ends in trees, and I—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening, and whatever the rest of the sentence was, he swallowed it whole. "I came to see if it was true."

"And is it?"

He looked at me then with such naked, aching attention that I actually stepped back half an inch, my heel catching in the turf.

"It's more than true," he said softly. "Coral, I think you should ask your aunt what she burns after dark."

The words landed cold. "What did you say?"

"Ask her what she burns," he repeated, gentler now, "after dark. And watch her face while she answers."

Above us, for the first time all day, a single crow spoke—one flat syllable, like a dropped stone—and then the whole grey flock of them lifted off the chapel roof at once and wheeled east, toward my aunt's chimney, where the evening smoke was already beginning to rise.
