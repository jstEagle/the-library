## 3. What the Crows Said

The crow landed on the fence post at half past six in the morning and said, with the flat certainty of a tax collector, "The paper man is here."

I was elbow-deep in a bucket of goat feed, which is to say I was already having the kind of morning that makes a person want to lie down in it. The goats were being their usual selves—Bramble screaming about breakfast as though she hadn't eaten since the Flood, Petunia standing on the trough like a monument to poor decisions—and I had exactly zero patience left for prophecy.

"No," I told him.

"The paper man," the crow repeated, tilting his head so one black eye could get a better angle on my soul. He's an old bird, that crow, older than any of us, with feathers gone grey at the edges like a well-worn coat. The animals of Hollowmere have always talked to me. That's the thing I don't say out loud, the thing I buried so deep under twenty-six years of careful silence that some mornings I almost believe it isn't there. But it's there. It has always been there, like a stone in a boot.

"I heard you the first time," I said, keeping my voice low because Mrs. Aldous next door had ears like a bat and a mouth like a town crier. "There's no paper man. There's a Jude, who is a person, who is arriving on Thursday, and he's probably going to be terrible, and then Aunt Verity will have to find me someone else to disappoint."

"Paper," said the crow, and then, with what I swear was pity: "Man."

Crows are not liars. This is the inconvenient truth about crows. Goats will lie to you all day—Bramble once convinced an entire generation of Hollowmere children that she was starving, while visibly rounder than a church bell—but crows deal only in facts they find interesting. And apparently my aunt's blind date arrangement qualified.

"Fine," I said. "If he's made of paper, I'll let you eat him."

The crow fluffed himself up into something resembling satisfaction and stayed for breakfast anyway, which should have been my first clue that he wasn't joking. Crows don't linger for bad jokes. They're busy birds. They have things to peck.

* * *

Thursday came the way Thursdays do in Hollowmere—slowly, and with pie.

You have to understand something about this valley before you understand anything else about me: nothing here arrives unannounced, and nothing here leaves without permission nobody remembers granting. The mail comes Tuesdays. The butcher's van comes Fridays. The fog rolls down off the ridge every evening at seven sharp, punctual as a hymn, and by eight o'clock the whole valley is wrapped up soft and woolly and *kept*, like a thing in a pocket. We joke about it. We've always joked about it. *Hollowmere time*, we call it, when someone's late, and everyone laughs, and no one asks why the roads out are all half-remembered, why nobody's cousin ever visits from beyond the pass, why the maps in the schoolroom stop at the valley walls like the world simply gave up drawing.

I asked about it once, when I was small. I asked Aunt Verity why we never went over the mountain, and she looked at me for a long moment with an expression I couldn't read then and can read too easily now, and she said, "Because everything we need is here, love." And I believed her, because I was six, and because believing her was easier than the alternative, which was noticing that she hadn't actually answered the question.

So: Thursday. Pie. Verity had baked since dawn, three pies, one of them clearly a practice run she'd hidden in the pantry and pretended didn't exist. She'd attacked my hair with more vigor than affection. She'd put out the good tablecloth, the one with the embroidered hollyhocks that her mother—I'm told; I never met her—had stitched before the war. Which war, Verity has never specified. Another door in this house that opens onto a wall.

"He'll be here at four," she said for the eleventh time, straightening a chair that did not need straightening. "His letter said four."

"What letter? I never saw a letter."

"It came." She smoothed the tablecloth where there was no wrinkle. "It came and it said four."

I let it go, because that is what we do in Hollowmere. We let things go. It's practically our regional sport.

He arrived at ten past four, which even then struck me as odd, because in a valley where the fog keeps time, nobody is late. Nobody. The fog doesn't allow it. But there he was at the gate, hat in hand, looking up at the house like a man checking an address against a memory, and something cold walked down my spine wearing small wet feet.

The animals went quiet.

Not quiet like startled. Quiet like *held breath*. Bramble stopped mid-scream. Petunia got down off her monument. Even the hens, who have never once in their lives had a thought worth pausing for, went still on their roosts and watched him with flat orange eyes. And the crow—the old grey-feathered crow—lifted off the fence post and circled the yard twice, high up, saying nothing at all, which from a crow is a scream.

"You must be Jude," I called, because someone had to break the silence and it wasn't going to be the livestock.

"Coral?" He smiled, and it was a good smile, warm and slightly crooked, the smile of a man who has practiced smiling but hasn't yet learned to fake the eyes. "Sorry I'm late. The road—" He paused. He looked back down the lane, toward the pass, toward the place where the road out does its vanishing trick, and his brow creased like paper held too long in a fist. "The road took longer than I expected."

"How long?"

"What?"

"How long did the road take?"

He blinked at me. "That's an unusual question."

"That's an unusual answer you haven't given."

And there it was again, that crooked smile, but slower this time, like a door opening onto a room he hadn't meant to show me. "Longer than it looks," he said. "Longer than it *should*. Doesn't anyone here ever wonder—"

"Wonder what?" said Aunt Verity brightly, appearing at my shoulder with the efficiency of a woman who has spent decades materializing precisely when conversations get dangerous. "Jude! Come in, come in, the pie's just resting. Coral, take his coat."

I took his coat. It was a good coat, city-made, the kind of tailoring that doesn't exist within fifty miles of Hollowmere, possibly within five hundred, possibly anywhere, if I'm honest about the clothes I've seen, which are all variations on the same three patterns like the whole world shares one wardrobe. His coat was different. His coat had traveled.

At dinner he asked questions. That was the first strange thing, how many of them there were. People in Hollowmere don't ask questions; we ask confirmations. *Lovely weather, isn't it? Pie good, is it? Your mother's grave kept tidy, is it?* Questions with the answers built in, load-bearing answers, answers that hold up the ceiling of the world. But Jude asked real ones, open-ended ones, the kind with holes in the bottom that you can fall through.

"Where do your supplies come from?" he asked, over his second slice of pie.

"The butcher's van. Fridays."

"And the butcher gets them from...?"

"From the supplier."

"Which is where?"

I opened my mouth. I closed it. Somewhere in the middle distance of my mind, a fact should have been standing—a town name, a direction, a *somewhere*—and it simply wasn't there. Not hidden. Absent. Like reaching for a stair that turns out to be painted on the wall.

"Aunt Verity," I said carefully, "where does Mr. Tull's stock come from?"

She refilled Jude's tea with great concentration. "Over the pass, love. You know that."

"Do I?"

"Coral." Gently. Gently, the way you'd speak to a dog you feared might bite. "Eat your pie."

Jude watched this exchange the way a man watches ice crack underfoot. And when Verity went out to see about the fog—she always sees about the fog, though there's never anything to see—he leaned across the table and said, quietly, "Your animals. They've been staring at me since I arrived. All evening. Every one of them."

"They do that with strangers."

"No," he said. "They don't. They do that with something." He turned his teacup in its saucer, once, a full slow rotation, and I noticed his hands were trembling very slightly, and I noticed that I wanted, quite badly, to put my own hands over them, and I decided both observations were none of my business. "Coral. Has anything strange ever happened to you here? Anything you couldn't explain?"

The fire popped. Out in the yard, the crow called once, low, and I would have sworn on any book you liked that what he said was *tell him*.

"No," I lied, and the lie tasted like the stone in my boot, familiar and worn smooth. "Nothing strange happens in Hollowmere."

Something moved behind his eyes—disappointment, or relief, or both wearing each other's coats—and he said, "No. I suppose it wouldn't."

When he left, at nine, just ahead of the fog, he shook my hand instead of kissing it, and his palm was dry and warm and shaking, and he held on maybe two seconds longer than manners required, and I stood at the gate watching him go until the fog folded him away like a letter being closed.

"Well," said Aunt Verity, beside me, though I hadn't heard her come out. "What did you think?"

"I think," I said slowly, "that he asked a lot of questions."

"Mm." She was looking down the lane where he'd vanished. Her face, lit by the porch lamp, had gone somewhere young and frightened. "Some men do that."

"Some men do that," I agreed. And then, because the crow's words had been sitting in my chest all day like a swallowed coal: "Aunt Verity. Did you get a letter from him? About today?"

Her head turned toward me very slowly. "Why do you ask?"

"Because you said four o'clock, and you were right, and nobody's right about times here. Because his coat comes from a city I can't name. Because the animals—" I stopped. Because the animals go silent around him, I almost said, and saw her face change, and understood with a lurch that she already knew.

"Go to bed, Coral," she said.

"Aunt Verity—"

"*Go to bed.*"

* * *

I did not go to bed. I am my aunt's niece, after all, and stubbornness in this family skips no generations.

I lay in the dark listening to the house settle, and past midnight I heard it: the back door, easing shut on hinges Verity oils monthly and pretends it's habit rather than stealth. Then voices—or one voice, hers, low, talking to someone, except the yard was empty when I pressed my eye to the glass, empty except for the fog, which lay thick as spilled milk right up to the fence.

Then the smell. Smoke, but wrong—not woodsmoke, not leaf-smoke. Paper burning. That particular sweet-bitter stink of ink giving itself up, of words returning to air.

I went downstairs in my socks.

She was in the washhouse behind the kitchen, the little stone room where the boiler mutters all winter, and she had a tin tub full of flame, and she was feeding it. Papers. Dozens of papers. She'd tied back her hair and rolled up her sleeves like a woman doing laundry, methodical and grim, and the firelight jumped on her face, and I stood in the doorway watching my aunt burn what looked like years.

Letters, mostly. Old letters, browned at the edges, tied in bundles with string gone soft. A ledger of some kind, its cover stamped with something I couldn't read in that light. And—this is the piece I keep coming back to, the splinter under the skin of the whole night—a book. Thick, swollen with age, its leather cracked like drought ground, and she held it a long moment before it went in. Held it like you hold a thing that has cost you. She read something on the page it fell open to. Her lips moved. Whatever was written there, she whispered it back to itself, one last recitation before the sentence.

And the crow—my old grey friend—was on the windowsill. Inside. Which crows do not do, which no bird does, and he was watching the fire with his head cocked, and when Verity dropped the book into the flames he said, softly, in his flat feathered voice:

"She knew."

Verity spun. The poker came up in her hand before I think she'd decided to reach for it, and then she saw it was me and the poker sagged and her face did something complicated and terrible, grief and guilt and fury all trying to fit through the same door.

"Coral." My name in her mouth sounded like a warning label. "How much did you see?"

"Enough. What are you burning?"

"Papers."

"What papers? From whom? Why does the crow know things, Aunt Verity, why do the *animals talk to me*, why did a stranger ask me today if anything strange has ever happened as though he already knew the answer—"

"Stop." Her voice cracked like the ice on the trough in hard winters. She stepped between me and the tub, as if the fire were mine and she were protecting it, or hers and protecting me from it, and honestly it may have been both. "You don't want to know this."

"I have never once in my life wanted less to know something than I do right now, which is exactly why I'm asking."

For a moment—one long, flickering moment—she looked like she might tell me. The truth rose in her face like water in a well; I watched it climb; I watched her put her hand on the rope and hold it down. That's the image I've kept. My aunt, holding down the truth by main strength, the way she'd held down everything else for twenty-six years.

"Go to bed," she said again, and this time her voice broke in the middle of it, and this time, God help me, I obeyed.

But I lay awake until the pale hour, and I listened to the washhouse fire die, and I thought about a man made of paper walking through our gate, and a road that takes longer than it should, and a valley that keeps time better than clocks, and the stone-in-the-boot feeling I have carried since childhood, the whisper I buried alive and pretended stayed buried.

It isn't buried anymore. It sits up in the dark of me now, turning its head, listening.

And in the morning, when I went out to feed the goats, the crow was waiting on the fence post, and he had brought friends. Seven of them, silent, watching the house.

"The paper man," said the old crow, "left something under the piano bench."

I stood there with the feed bucket in my hand, steam rising off the mash, and every sensible bone in my body said *go inside, bake something, mind your own valley.*

I set the bucket down.
