## 4. The Book in the Piano Bench

I had told Coral I was an archivist.

It was, by the standards of my profession, almost true. I did keep archives. I did handle rare documents with white gloves and a certain reverent dread. What I had neglected to mention was that the archive I kept belonged to an order that most people would call a hunting lodge if they were being generous, and something considerably worse if they weren't, and that the document I'd come to Hollowmere to verify had a name written on it in ink that hadn't dried yet — a name belonging to the person who had spent the whole evening laughing at my terrible jokes as though they weren't rehearsed, which they were, because I'd read them first.

But that was tomorrow's guilt. Tonight's guilt belonged to the chapel.

The chapel sat at the crook of the valley like a thumb pressed into dough, whitewashed and squat, with a bell tower that had never once rung in living memory — or so the townsfolk said, cheerfully, the way people say things they've been told so often they've mistaken them for observations. Everyone in Hollowmere said it. *That old bell's not rung since before my grandmother's time.* Everyone. The same words, the same shrug, the same pleasant blankness behind their eyes when they said it, like a drawer sliding shut.

I noticed things like that. It was the job.

Getting into the chapel at eleven o'clock at night required no skill whatsoever, which should have been my first clue that I wasn't meant to be stopped. The side door was latched with a hook older than my grandmother. The floorboards announced me to the entire building, but nothing else did. No dogs — there were no dogs in Hollowmere, another cheerful consensus nobody could source — and the caretaker, if there was one, slept like the rest of the town seemed to sleep: deeply, uniformly, at exactly the hours the valley preferred.

I stood in the nave and let the dark settle around me, and I did what I always did in new places. I listened.

Chapels have a sound. Most of them hum with the residue of use — candle smoke, hymnals swollen with damp, the particular silence of a thousand bored congregations. This chapel didn't hum. It *waited*. The silence here had weight, like the pause an orchestra holds before a downbeat, and every hair on my arms stood up and informed me, formally, that we were somewhere we shouldn't be.

"Noted," I whispered. "Thank you for your input."

My breath made a small ghost in the moonlight from the east window. The glass depicted a shepherd, which struck me as optimistic for a valley whose residents couldn't name a single flock beyond their own kitchen gardens.

I started where I always start: the obvious places, done obviously, so that if anyone caught me I'd look like exactly what I claimed to be — a curious tourist with an interest in local history. I flipped through the guest ledger (last entry: four years ago, a wedding; the groom's handwriting deteriorated halfway through his own name, as though he'd forgotten how to finish it). I examined the pulpit Bibles. I ran my fingers along the shelf of hymnals and found them all identical, all pristine, all printed by a press called Meridian & Sons, London, 1899 — every single copy, including three that looked brand new.

Hymnals don't get reprinted identically for a hundred and twenty years. Hymnals rot, tear, lose pages, acquire marginalia in the handwriting of teenagers in love. These had been *maintained*. Or replaced. Or — and here the cold feeling in my stomach began its slow climb — these had never been used at all, merely displayed, the way you'd display props on a stage set.

"Hello," I murmured to the chapel generally. "What are you?"

The chapel declined to answer. Chapels are better at this than goats. Even the goats back at the Dunmore farmstead had gone quiet when I arrived — not afraid-quiet, which I know how to read, but attentive-quiet, like an audience settling before a performance they'd been promised long ago. Coral hadn't noticed. Coral had been too busy being nervous and lovely and pretending the date wasn't arranged by an aunt who'd practically shoved us both through the gate.

Coral. I let myself think the name properly now, alone, where thinking it couldn't be weaponized against either of us. *Coral.* A good name. A name with edges.

Stop it, Joubert. Work first. Feelings after verification. That was the order's rule and I had recited it to myself across nine counties, and it had held, right up until a person with hay in their hair and beast-speech hidden under their tongue like a stolen coin had asked me whether the roads outside really went anywhere, with such naked longing that I'd nearly told them everything on the spot, over the cheese course, in a restaurant where the menu hadn't changed since — I checked my notes — 1899.

*Work.*

There had to be a record. There was always a record. The order's founding premise, the thing old Master Corvane liked to say with his tea going cold in front of him, was that *the world writes itself down, and our task is simply to read faster than fate does.* Every prophecy-book ever catalogued had a home: parish chest, crypt niche, the locked cabinet under some bishop's bed. And every prophecy-book we'd ever recovered about erased places shared one feature — they were kept close to the center of the erasure, because paper remembers what people are made to forget.

So: the chapel. The center. The bell that never rang.

I worked through the vestry. Robes, moth-eaten, smelling of lavender and dust. A donation box containing two coins, both dated 1899, both in suspiciously fine condition. Behind the vestry door, a stair down to a crypt that smelled of wet stone and turned back on itself after twenty feet, ending in a wall that my lantern showed to be seamless but my knuckles reported as hollow. I marked it mentally — *later, maybe, if tonight came up empty* — and climbed back up into the waiting silence.

Which is when I saw the piano.

It stood against the north wall, an upright, walnut-cased, absurdly domestic object for a chapel, like finding a kitchen chair at an altar. Its keys were yellowed ivory. Its bench was the kind with a hinged lid, the kind every piano bench in the world uses to store sheet music nobody plays anymore.

I had walked past it twice without seeing it. That bothered me more than anything else so far. I'm not a man who walks past things. Something about that bench had been quietly, competently refusing my attention, the way the town refused questions about roads, the way Coral's aunt Verity had refused to meet my eye for longer than politeness allowed at dinner — not hostile, just *managing* me, the way you manage a fire you haven't decided whether to put out.

I sat down on the bench. The wood creaked its ordinary creak. I lifted the lid.

Sheet music, top layer. "Hymns for Small Congregations," water-stained, harmless. Beneath it, a folded christening gown wrapped in tissue. Beneath *that* —

A book.

Not a grand book. That's the thing nobody tells you about prophecy-books: the ones that matter are never grand. The grimoires with iron clasps and chained pages, those are mostly theater, sold to men who want their magic visible. Real prophecy comes dressed as accounting. This was a ledger-sized volume bound in gray-green cloth gone soft at the corners, spine cracked in three places, with a title hand-lettered on the cover in faded sepia:

*Records of Hollowmere: Being a True Account of Things to Come. Begun in the Year of Our Lord 1899.*

Begun. Not completed. *Begun*, as though whoever wrote it had understood that a book like this is never finished, only interrupted.

I put on my gloves. My hands were shaking, and the gloves were less about protecting the book than about giving the trembling something respectable to hide inside.

The paper inside was heavy stock, cream-colored, ruled faintly, and the hand that filled it was small and upright and utterly calm. The opening pages were administrative. Dedications. A preface explaining, in the tone of a woman writing minutes for a committee, that the author had been granted sight of "the thread" following a fever in the winter of '98, and had resolved to set down what she saw "for the benefit of those who come after, should any after arrive."

*Should any after arrive.* She'd known. Whatever she was — sibyl, accident, fever-dreamer — she'd known that the future she was recording might have no readers, because the place she lived might have no *after*. She wrote it anyway. I have a great deal of respect for that. It's the whole job, really: writing letters to people who may never exist.

Then the entries began. Dates. Ordinary dates, marching down the years in that calm upright hand, each entry a paragraph or three, describing — verbatim, precisely, with dialogue rendered as faithfully as a court transcript — things that had not yet happened when she wrote them.

And then, near the end of the volume, in a different ink, a slightly larger, slightly less patient hand — the original author's, aged perhaps, or a successor trained to imitate her — a date I recognized.

Tonight's date.

I found it with my finger the way you'd find a wound. I read it once at speed, the way you rip off a bandage. Then I made myself go back and read it slowly, because the order pays me to be thorough and thoroughness was the only thing standing between me and running out of this chapel, out of this valley, out of this whole comfortable lie, and never looking back.

*The archivist arrives,* the entry began.

My mouth went dry.

*He comes by the eastern road wearing borrowed charm and calls himself an archivist, which is true in the way that a knife is true. He dines at the Lamb and Lantern and tells two jokes he learned from a man in Ashford, and she laughs at the second one even though it isn't funny, because she wants him to feel clever, and because she has decided, already, that she likes him — which is the cruelest thing the thread has shown me in years, because he knew her name before he ever saw her face.*

The entry continued. It gave the walk afterward along the mill lane. It gave the moment a moth came to the lantern and Coral cupped it in both hands and carried it outside rather than let it batter itself to death — I hadn't told anyone about the moth. I hadn't told anyone about the moth. It had seemed too small a tenderness to survive being spoken aloud, and here it was, preserved in century-old ink like a fly in amber, *and she will carry the moth out into the dark rather than harm it, and he will understand then that he is lost.*

*I am sorry,* the unknown writer added, in a smaller line beneath, as though apologizing directly to me across a hundred and twenty years. *You seem kind. But the thread shows what it shows, and the thread shows you lying to her still.*

I closed the book. I sat in the dark chapel with my palms flat on the cover and breathed the way they teach you — four counts in, seven counts out — and I did not succeed in feeling calm.

Because here is the thing. Here is the thing I have not yet confessed, the thing I'll confess eventually, probably to her, probably at the worst possible moment, because that's how confessions work: I *had* read her name before I came. Three weeks ago, in a dispatch from the Order of the Pale Thread, sealed with wax I broke in an inn outside Ashford while rain came down like judgment. *A beast-speaker survives in the erased valley of Hollowmere. Confirm identity, confirm capability, confirm willingness. Extraction follows confirmation.* And beneath the official language, in Corvane's private cipher, the part that made the dispatch feel heavier than paper: *If the subject cannot be persuaded, note the guardians' names and withdraw. We do not need her cooperation. We need only her voice, and voices can be taken.*

They'd sent me because I was good at this. Because I could sit across from a stranger over cheese and make them laugh and mean none of it, and file the report, and sleep. Nine counties of practice. And then Coral had laughed at my unfunny joke — kindly, deliberately, a gift of laughter — and cupped a moth in two hands like it was something holy, and I had lain awake in my room at the Lamb and Lantern understanding that the order had made a catastrophic miscalculation.

They thought I was a knife. Knives don't rust from the inside.

I opened the book again. I needed to see the rest. There were entries beyond tonight's — I could feel them, the way you can feel extra weight in a letter — and I turned the pages with fingers that had steadied themselves by force.

The next entries described things that hadn't happened yet. A crow that would speak to her — crows, I noted, *paper men*, whatever that meant. Papers burned in a kitchen. A book found. An argument between her and the aunt who raised her, transcribed with terrible fidelity, both their voices cracking on lines that hadn't been spoken. And further on, in entries growing shorter, sparser, as though the thread itself were thinning toward its end: a door. A price. A memory given up like a coin across a counter. A figure walking out of a valley into a country so wide it frightened the page.

And beside that final entry, crammed into the margin in a third hand — hurried, slanting, urgent, pressed so hard into the paper it had nearly torn through:

*She forgets him. Write to her. Leave her something. Trust must survive the forgetting — leave proof —*

The sentence stopped. Whoever had written it had run out of room, or time, or nerve.

I sat there a long while. Somewhere above me, the bell that had never rung hung in its tower, silent as a held breath. Outside, the valley lay under stars it couldn't name, snug inside its erasure, dreaming the dream someone had paid for it. And in my hands lay the account of everything that was coming — the crow, the burning, the rupture, the door, the price — laid out in calm century-old ink, ending in a margin-scrawled plea from someone who had evidently loved her enough to try to cheat fate with half a sentence.

The order wanted confirmation. Well. Consider me confirmed. I knew who she was. I knew what she could do. I knew, now, what it would cost her, because the book told me plainly, and the book had been right about the moth.

Which left the question the book couldn't answer, the question that had followed me up nine counties and down into this crypt-smelling dark: when the moment came, whose side would I be standing on? The order's, which owned my oath? Hers, which owned — apparently, inconveniently, irreversibly — everything else?

Or the margin-writer's, whoever they'd been, who had looked at the thread of fate and decided, with the last of their ink, that love deserved a fighting chance?

I wrapped the book in the christening gown's tissue paper, tucked it inside my coat, and stood. Tomorrow I would have breakfast at the Lamb and Lantern. Tomorrow I would see her again, and lie to her again, and watch for the crow.

And tomorrow night, I decided, watching my breath ghost up toward the bell that never rang — tomorrow night, I would burn my report unsent, and start writing letters instead. To her. In the margins.

Just in case the book was right about how this ends.

* * *

I was three steps from the side door when the piano began to play.

One note. Middle C, soft, unmistakable, though no hand touched the keys and no bench creaked under any weight but mine. Then a second note, a fourth above it. Patient. Deliberate. Like something tuning itself after a very long silence.

Like something that had just realized it had been read.

I stood in the dark with a stolen century under my coat and did not move, and the chapel waited with me, and far above, in the tower, the bell that had never rung shivered — once — without making a sound.

Someone, or something, knew I had the book.
