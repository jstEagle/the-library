## 5. Written Before We Met

### Coral

Here is what I knew about Jude by the end of our second evening together: he liked his tea too sweet, he flinched at the word *road* the way other people flinched at the word *dead*, and he had a way of looking at me like I was a sentence he was trying to parse before it finished.

I should also mention that I was falling for him. Rapidly. Embarrassingly. The kind of falling where you catch yourself composing jokes for him in advance, rehearsing your own charm like a girl with a crush instead of a woman of twenty-six who owns goats and a healthy distrust of surprises.

Aunt Verity had engineered this date with all the subtlety of a landslide. "Jude's staying through Sunday," she'd said, buttering toast like a woman who had already won. "You might show him the orchard. Or don't. Do what you like." She then spent forty minutes arranging flowers in a room that contained no flowers yesterday.

So there we were, Sunday-adjacent, walking back from the orchard with cider on our breath and my hand dangerously close to his, when I remembered I'd left the chapel gate open. Small thing. Goats get everywhere; you learn to check gates the way other people check locks.

"Wait here," I said.

"No," said Jude, immediately. Then, softer: "I mean—I'll come."

I thought nothing of it. That's the part that still stings. I thought nothing of it at all.

The chapel sat at the valley's center like an old tooth everyone was fond of but no one used. Hollowmere wasn't much for worship—we weren't much for anything requiring an audience beyond ourselves—but we kept it swept, and Aunt Verity kept its piano tuned, and once a month the choir made sounds in it that we politely called singing.

The gate was open. Fine. But the chapel door was ajar too, and through the gap came light and the sound of pages turning. Fast. Frantic.

I pushed the door wide.

Jude stood at the front pew with a book open across both hands, and the look on his face was not the face of a man reading. It was the face of a man drowning in something he'd read before.

"That's not yours," I said.

He didn't jump. That was the first wrong thing. Anyone else in Hollowmere would have leapt out of their skin. Jude just closed the book slowly, like shutting a door on weather.

"It might be," he said. "It's been here since 1899, by the flyleaf."

"The piano bench is Verity's. Everything in this chapel is accounted for."

"Coral." He said my name carefully, the way you set down something breakable. "I need you to sit down."

"I'm fine standing."

"You're going to want to be sitting."

Reader, I did not sit. I marched down that aisle and took the book out of his hands, because I am, at my core, a person who checks gates.

The cover was leather gone soft as ear skin, stamped with a title worn to ghosts: *Annals of Hollowmere*. The date inside the front cover was 1899. And someone—many someones, over many years—had written in it in a dozen different hands, cramped and looping, filling page after page with dates and names and dialogue.

Dialogue. In quotation marks.

I turned to the newest entry, near the end, in handwriting so fresh the ink still smelled faintly of the well.

And I stopped being fine standing.

### Jude

She read it in silence, which was worse than shouting. I watched her lips move over her own words—the ones she'd spoken to me two nights ago at the gate, verbatim, down to the joke about my coat looking like a drowned crow—and I watched the color leave her face in stages, like dusk.

"How," she said.

"I don't know how."

"When."

"The ink smells new. Days old, maybe. But the date at the top—" I didn't finish. She could see the date herself. It predated us by decades.

"This is a joke." Her voice had gone flat and bright, the voice of someone holding very still on ice. "Verity put you up to this. Some test. Some—" She laughed, one hard note. "No. No, she wouldn't. She can barely arrange a blind date without burning the biscuits."

"Read the last lines," I said. "Out loud. Please. Because if you say them—if they come out of your mouth in that order—then it isn't a trick, and we both have to deal with what that means."

She looked at me for a long moment. I have faced interrogation by people professionally trained to unmake me, and none of them frightened me the way she did standing there with that book, deciding whether to trust the evidence or the man holding it.

She read:

"'Jude will ask what happens if she refuses. And Coral will say—'" Her breath caught. "'Coral will say: *Then the book is wrong about me.*'"

Silence. The chapel held it the way chapels do, letting it swell.

"Have you asked me that yet?" I asked quietly.

"No."

"Do you want to?"

Her jaw worked. "What happens if I don't? If I refuse to say it? Does the ink—" She stopped. Started again. "Is this thing writing us, Jude, or are we writing it?"

"I've been asking myself that since before I met you." Which was true, and more true than I wanted it to be, and I watched her hear the wrongness in it—the little hitch of a sentence carrying freight it shouldn't.

"Before," she repeated. "Before you met me."

"Coral—"

"You asked odd questions at dinner. About roads. You asked Tam Wicker how far it was to the crossroads town and he said three days and you *laughed*, Jude. You laughed like he'd told a joke. Nobody laughs at that. Everyone knows it's three days."

"Does everyone know why it's always three days?" I asked. "Has anyone ever walked it?"

That landed. I saw it land. In Hollowmere, apparently, nobody left—not because leaving was forbidden, exactly, but because nobody wanted to, which is a far more effective wall than any lock. She'd told me that herself, on our walk, with a restlessness under her words she probably thought was hidden.

"Sit down," she said, turning my own medicine on me. "Start at the beginning. Whatever your beginning is."

So I sat in the pew where the book said I would sit, and I began to lie to her—or rather, I began to tell her a truth shaped like a smaller truth, because the whole truth was a knife I hadn't earned the right to hand her yet.

"I work for an archive," I said. "Old records. Prophecies, mostly—books that write themselves. There aren't many. This one shouldn't exist outside its own country."

"Hollowmere isn't a country."

"Isn't it?" I let that hang. "I was sent to find it. The book led me here—entries about a beast-speaker named Coral Dunmore. Except when I arrived, the entries had changed. They weren't about a stranger anymore. They were about—" I gestured helplessly at the last week of my life, transcribed in another century's ink. "They were about this. Us. Every word. I checked the night I got here. Before dinner. I knew what you'd order. I knew you'd say my coat looked like a drowned crow."

Her eyes went bright and terrible. "You let me say it anyway."

"What was I supposed to do—interrupt fate?"

"Yes!" The word cracked out of her and echoed off the rafters, and somewhere up in the bell tower something shifted—a pigeon, maybe, or maybe the building itself listening. "Yes, you interrupt it, you warn me, you—you don't sit there smiling while I perform my life like a play I haven't auditioned for!"

"I'm sorry."

"Sorry doesn't—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eye. "Do you understand what this means? If every word is written, then nothing I feel is mine. Not this—" she gestured between us, furious and mortified at her own honesty, "—not whatever this is. It's just the book doing what books do."

"Or," I said, "the book only writes down what's already true."

"That's worse."

"I know."

"Do you?" She stepped closer, and up close her anger had grief underneath it, the way most anger does. "Because here's what I keep snagging on, Jude. Look at the margins."

I looked. In the margin beside tonight's unwritten-yet lines, in handwriting I recognized now—loopy, impatient, slanting uphill—was a note:

*He hesitates before answering. He's hiding something. Ask him again.*

Dated 1903.

"That's your handwriting," I said stupidly.

"That's my handwriting," she agreed, "and I have never seen this book before tonight, and it knows things about you that I noticed but never said." She tapped the margin. "It says you hesitate. You do hesitate. So either this book is recording us from some impossible elsewhere, or—" she swallowed, "—or at some point, I wrote in it. A me who isn't me yet. Or was. Jude, is there time in this thing? Does it run both ways?"

I thought of my orders. I thought of the sealed instructions, the warnings, the reason beast-speakers were hunted. I thought: *tell her everything*. I thought: *if you tell her everything, the mission dies, and possibly you die with it.*

"I don't know," I said, which was cowardice wearing honesty's coat, and from the look on her face she knew it too.

But then she did something I didn't expect. She sat down next to me, hip to hip on the hard pew, and closed the book, and put both hands flat on its cover like a woman pinning something down in a storm.

"New rule," she said. "Whatever the book says we say next—we don't say it. Tonight, you and I are going to have a conversation that isn't in there. Off the page. Can we do that? Is there any part of us left that's off the page?"

"There's always parts off the page," I said. "The book records words. It can't record—" I almost said *what I feel*. The book, of course, had recorded what I felt, in the margin, dated tomorrow.

"What?" she asked.

"Nothing. Ask me what you were going to ask me."

She studied my face for a long time. Outside, the light was failing; the chapel windows went from gold to ash. Somewhere distant, faintly, someone was humming—one of those tunes everyone in Hollowmere seemed to know and nobody remembered learning.

"If I refuse," Coral said softly. "If I refuse to be what's written. What happens then?"

And there it was. The line. The exact line, waiting in the book under her name, and I felt the strangest vertigo—as though we were two actors who'd found the script mid-performance and couldn't stop saying our parts.

"Then the book is wrong about you," I said, before I could stop myself, and heard the words leave my mouth in the ordained order, and saw her hear them land in hers.

We sat there, both of us trembling slightly, in the wreckage of free will.

"That counts," she whispered. "That counts as saying it, doesn't it. Even though I asked you to prompt me. Even though I chose—" She laughed, shaky. "Or did I choose? Did the book choose the choosing?"

"Coral."

"Don't. Don't say my name like that, like it's a comfort, when for all we know it wrote 'don't say my name like that' right there on the next—"

I kissed her.

I want it noted, for the record—which, God help us, there is a record—that I checked first. I flipped to the following pages, frantic, scanning ahead, and the transcription simply *stopped*. Mid-sentence. After her line about the book being wrong about her, the careful antique handwriting trailed off into nothing, into blank paper, as though whatever force held the pen had reached the edge of its knowledge.

As though we had just walked off the map.

So I kissed her, off the page, unwritten, unaccounted for, and she kissed me back like a woman proving a point, and when we broke apart we were both laughing and crying a little, which is what people do when they discover they're real.

"It stopped," she breathed. "Jude, it stopped. We're past it. We're past the writing."

"For now."

"Don't ruin it."

"I'm not ruining it, I'm—look." I turned the book around and showed her the blank pages, dozens of them, empty as snowfall. "Everything else is filled. Years and years. But from tonight onward—nothing. Whatever erased this valley, whatever keeps it hidden, it only wrote as far as it could see. And we just moved past where it's looking."

She ran her fingers over the empty paper like it was water. Then her expression changed—shifted from wonder to something colder, sharper.

"Wait." She was flipping backward, fast, past decades. "If the writing stops tonight... then whoever's been keeping this book updated stopped recently. Someone's been adding entries. Someone alive." She looked up at me, and I watched her arrive at the same door I'd arrived at days ago. "Who has access to this chapel, Jude? Who plays the piano? Who 'keeps it tuned'?"

I said nothing. She said nothing. The humming outside drifted closer, sweet and tuneless, and passed away down the lane.

"Aunt Verity," said Coral.

### Jude

I want to state clearly, for my own conscience, which at this point had the structural integrity of wet paper: I did not want to involve Verity. I wanted to take the book, complete my verification, and send my report, and never think about the way Coral's mouth felt, because men in my line of work who think about mouths end up in shallow graves.

But Coral was already moving—book clutched to her chest, chin set, marching toward the door with the stride of a woman whose goats had escaped one time too many.

"She burned papers," Coral said over her shoulder. "Two nights ago. In the yard. I saw the ash and thought—old letters, receipts, whatever. Papers burn. People burn papers." She stopped with her hand on the chapel door. "People who aren't hiding something don't burn them at midnight, though, do they."

"Coral, wait. Think. If she's kept this secret for decades—"

"Decades." She turned. In the dying light her face was pale and absolutely certain. "You said decades. How do you know it's been decades? I never told you how long the entries went back."

The trap I'd built for myself, sprung neatly, by my own mouth. I am, professionally speaking, an excellent liar. I had simply gotten out of practice, these past few days, of wanting to be.

"Because I read the whole book," I said. "Last night. All of it. And Coral—there's something in there you need to see before you go home and have this fight. Something from 1971. Something about a fire, and a man who tried to leave, and—"

The color drained out of her again, faster this time.

"My uncle," she whispered. "My mother's brother. Nobody talks about him. Nobody even—there's no grave, no story, nothing. He's just a shape where a person used to be." Her grip tightened on the book until her knuckles went white. "What does the book say happened to him?"

"It says he reached the valley's edge," I said. "And it says what was waiting there. And Coral—it says the valley itself stopped him. The ground. The stones. Like the whole place was..." I searched for the word and hated it as soon as I found it. "Awake."

Outside, full dark had come down. And beneath our feet—so faint I might have invented it, so faint Coral clearly didn't hear it at all—I felt the chapel floor give a long, slow shudder, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

The stone under Hollowmere had heard us talking about it.

"Come on," Coral said, and pushed open the door into the dark. "We're getting answers tonight. From her, from the book, from whatever's listening under the floorboards." She glanced back at me, and despite everything—despite the terror and the betrayal and the centuries of ink—she almost smiled. "Off the page, Jude. Let's go be off the page."

Behind us, on the pew where we'd left it, the Annals of Hollowmere lay closed and silent.

And in the margin of the last written page, in ink that had not been there an hour before, three small words dried slowly in the dark:

*She knows now.*
