## 6. Hollowmere, Population Forgotten

I did not sleep.

I want to say that dramatically, the way the ballads would have it — *she lay wakeful till the cock crowed three* — but the truth is less poetic. I lay in bed doing arithmetic. The book was in Jude's room at my aunt's house, because Aunt Verity had taken one look at us on the doorstep, said "Ah," in the tone of a woman whose prophecy has come home to roost, and put him in the blue room without being asked. And I lay there calculating how many hours until dawn, and whether it would be rude to read a stranger's found book at two in the morning, and whether anything about my life could still be called rude or polite when apparently none of it had ever happened the way I thought it had.

At some point a goat started complaining in the yard, and I got up because that's what I do instead of thinking. I stood at the window with the cold coming through the gaps in the frame and watched Bramble yell at the fence post for no reason anyone but me could hear.

"They're asleep," I told her. "Everything's asleep."

*I'm not,* said Bramble, which is goat for *neither are you.*

"No," I agreed. "Neither am I."

Here is what I knew by then, and let me lay it out plainly because it took me the whole night to make it sit still: there is a book, bound in cracked green leather, dated 1899, that contains my evening of last Tuesday word for word. Not similar. Word for word. Including the joke I made about the cheese, which I have always considered spontaneous, and including a line Jude says near the end of tonight — whenever tonight ends up being — that I have not heard yet and now cannot stop imagining.

And the book is not the only one. Jude said — carefully, the way you set down something breakable — that the chapel keeps shelves of them. One for every year Hollowmere has existed. Which means every conversation my aunt ever had with the butcher, every quarrel between the Hollis boys, every prayer old Mother Anselm mumbled into her soup, all of it written down before any of them opened their mouths.

Which raises a question I kept circling like a moth around a lamp: if everything we say is already written, who is holding the pen?

The goats didn't know. Goats never do. That's their charm and their limitation both.

* * *

Morning came up grey and apologetic, the way it does when it knows you've been waiting for it. I was in the kitchen before Verity, which almost never happens, and I had the fire going and the kettle on and I'd arranged my face into something neutral. She came down in her dressing gown with her hair in its night braid and stopped dead in the doorway when she saw me, and I watched her decide, visibly, whether to pretend.

She decided against it. I'll give her that forever.

"You read some of it," she said.

"Jude showed me the entry." I poured her tea because my hands wanted a job. "Last Tuesday. Me and him. Every word."

"Yes."

"You knew."

"Coral—"

"You *knew.*" I set the pot down harder than tea deserves. "You've known since before I was born, presumably. You burned papers in your yard two nights ago while telling me the smoke was from the prunings. You sent me on a blind date you already knew would go exactly as it went. What I want to understand, and I want you to hear this as an actual question and not just me shouting, is what part of my life has been mine."

Verity sat down slowly, like a woman much older than she was, and wrapped both hands around her cup.

"That," she said, "is not a question I can answer honestly before breakfast. And it's not the question you should be asking first anyway."

"What should I be asking first?"

"Ask what the book says about where we are." She looked at me over the rim of the cup, and her eyes were red-rimmed, and I realized she hadn't slept either. "Not what we said. Where we *are.* Because that's the part I only half-understood myself, until last night, when that young man turned pages like he was looking for his own name in a casualty list. Ask about Aldric. Ask about the spell. And then, Coral — then ask yourself why nobody in this valley ever once wondered why there's no road out."

I opened my mouth to say there was a road out, obviously, the north road past the Hollis farm —

and stopped, because I tried to picture it, actually picture it, the road going out past the last field, and my mind slid off the attempt like a boot off wet clay. There were fields. Then there was fog. Then there was more valley, somehow, folded around, the way a dream folds and you don't question it because questioning dreams isn't done.

"There's no road," I said. My voice sounded strange in my own kitchen. "There's never been a road."

"No," Verity said. "There never has. Eat something first."

"I can't eat something. My entire country doesn't exist."

"It'll hold another twenty minutes. It's held a hundred years."

* * *

We ate toast. I want that recorded somewhere outside the prophecy books: the morning my reality came apart, my aunt and I ate toast and marmalade across from each other in silence, because grief is a thing the body insists on scheduling around meals regardless.

Then we went to the blue room, where Jude was awake — had been awake, by the look of him, in the same way I'd been awake, all night, doing arithmetic — and he had the book open on his knees and he stood when we came in like a man rising in court.

"I marked the passage," he said. "I wasn't sure if you'd want me reading ahead."

"Were you?" Verity asked dryly. "Sure?"

"No," Jude admitted. "I read ahead. I'm sorry. I read all of it."

"All of it being—" I began.

"The 1899 volume. Front to back." He held it out to me, open to a page near the middle where someone had pressed a flower so long ago it had gone the color of dust and memory. "It stops being a record about halfway through. Or rather — it starts being a record of something else. Read it aloud. I think it needs to be heard."

So I read it aloud. In my kitchen, because Verity refused to discuss the end of the world anywhere without a teapot in reach. The hand changed partway through the book — I noticed that first, the way you notice a stranger walking in a familiar field. The early entries were round, patient script. Then, dated the ninth of October, 1899, the writing went thin and hurried, as though the pen were being dragged by a dying hand.

Because it was.

*"The king is dying in the chapel,"* I read, *"and the door-guardians will not leave him, and he has asked for ink. He says a country must not simply die; a country must be closed properly, the way a grave is closed, with words over the stone. His men are gone — fled or dead, I cannot tell which, and neither can he, and he laughs at this, which frightens me more than the armies did. He has asked me to write as long as I am able, and I will, because someone must remember that there was a here here."*

I stopped. "Who wrote this?"

"A chronicler," Jude said quietly. "One of the king's people. Keep reading."

*"He tells me what the spell will do while I write it down, as though dictating a recipe. The valley will fold inward. The roads will unbraid themselves. The maps beyond will forget our shape the way water forgets a stone lifted out of it. And the people — he says the kindest thing, the cruelest thing, is that they will not grieve. They will not know anything was taken. He calls it mercy. I asked him, mercy for whom, and he looked at me with eyes already going glassy and said, for them, because grief needs a before, and they will have no before."*

My voice caught on that. Grief needs a before.

I had spent the whole night grieving, and I hadn't been able to say why, because there was nothing specific — no face, no name, no loss I could point to. Just a hollow ache like a tooth pulled in a dream. And now I knew why. I was grieving a theft I couldn't see because the thief had also stolen the receipt.

*"He says the beasts will keep the borders,"* I went on, and my mouth had gone dry, and Verity refilled my cup without being asked. *"The guardians — the great stone ones under the hills, and the small ones too, the crows and foxes and goats who carry a spark of the same work — they will remember, because beasts are not people and the spell does not reach into them so deep. But they will not be able to say. A guardian cannot speak of the door to a soul inside the fold. That is the binding's first law. Only a beast-speaker could even ask the question. And the king laughed again and said, and there are none left in the valley, none born since the closing, I have seen to the arithmetic of it, and I wrote that down and hated my hand for writing it."*

The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere outside, Bramble was saying something to the other goats, ordinary goat gossip, and it sounded suddenly like a language carrying secrets it wasn't allowed to deliver.

"None born since the closing," Verity repeated softly. "That's what the old texts say too. No beast-speech in Hollowmere. Not in a hundred years."

"And yet," I said.

"And yet." She reached across the table and took my wrist, not gently — urgently, the way you grab someone standing at the edge of something. "You talked to animals before you could talk properly, girl. Full sentences. Your mother thought it was a trick you'd grow out of. I told her to hide it, and thank God she listened to me, because I'd read enough of these books by then to know what the valley does with things that don't fit the pattern. It smooths them over. It forgets them on purpose."

"Is that why you burned papers?"

"That's why I burned papers." Her grip tightened. "Every few years the valley produces something that doesn't fit. A song nobody taught. A dream of a road. A child who talks to crows. And the valley — the hymn, the choir, whatever engine Aldric built into this place — notices, and the noticing feels like everyone in town looking at you sideways for a season, and then either the oddness fades or the person does. Your mother's cousin Tam. Remember Tam? Neither does anyone else. That's my point. That's exactly my point."

I pulled my wrist free, not because I minded the grip but because I needed my hands to press flat on the table, to feel something solid that wasn't words.

"So I'm a rounding error," I said. "In a country that doesn't exist. Written down a hundred years ago by a dying scribe, guarded by goats, maintained by hymns, and the only reason I'm still here talking about it is that the spell's arithmetic missed."

"Not missed," Jude said. He'd been quiet through all of it, hands folded, watching me with an expression I was beginning to recognize as guilt wearing patience as a coat. "Missed implies accident. The king's arithmetic counted on a zero. You're not an error, Coral. You're the exception the whole design forgot to forbid."

"That's supposed to comfort me?"

"No," he said. "It's supposed to inform you. Comfort comes later, maybe. If we earn it."

* * *

I walked out after that. I needed sky.

The valley was doing its morning act, and God forgive me, it was a good act. Mist lifting off the low fields. Smoke from chimneys standing straight up in the windless air. Mrs. Calloway beating rugs across her fence rail with the rhythm of a woman who has beaten rugs at that rail every Monday of her life and will beat them every Monday forever, amen. Down the lane, the Hollis boys were arguing about a cart wheel, and their voices carried exactly the way voices have always carried here, warm and close and going nowhere.

A country within a country. Folded inward. Unbraided from every road.

I stood in my own yard and looked at the hills that ringed us, the ones I had climbed a hundred times, the ones I had *never once* climbed all the way over, and I understood at last why. Not because I was lazy. Not because the paths gave out. Because the paths gave out in exactly the right places, gently, the way a lullaby gives out into sleep, and I had always, always turned back feeling satisfied instead of cheated. The spell didn't build walls. It built contentment. It built a valley full of people who felt complete at its edges, and called the completeness peace.

Even the crows. Even the foxes and the goats, carrying their little sparks of guardianship, sworn silent by the first law of the binding. All those years I'd chattered with Bramble and the hens and the half-wild dog on the ridge, and not one of them — not once — had ever said *there is a door, there is an out, there was a before.* They couldn't. They'd have told me. I know animals, and I know that particular straining silence of a creature holding a secret bigger than its mouth, and I had heard it my whole life and mistaken it for animal privacy.

Grief needs a before, the scribe wrote. Well, I had my before now, handed to me secondhand off a dead man's page, and it was worse than no before at all. Because now I knew there was a version of me — a real one, unhidden, with roads attached — that had been erased before I ever drew breath, and I hadn't lost her, exactly. I'd been *kept* from ever having her. Stolen goods don't grieve like losses. They grieve like debts.

Mrs. Calloway paused her rug-beating and waved. "Lovely morning, Coral!"

"Lovely morning," I called back, and meant: *do you know what you are? Do you know you're a note in a dead king's score?* And knew she didn't, and knew that knowing wouldn't free her, and felt the strangest tenderness for the whole lying valley rise up in me like floodwater.

Behind me, the gate creaked. Jude came down the path with the green book under his arm, moving careful, like a man approaching a spooked horse.

"I'm not spooked," I said, before he could say it.

"You're a little spooked."

"I'm allowed." I nodded at the hills. "How far does it go? The fold. Does anyone out there miss us? Is there a Coral-shaped hole in some map somewhere?"

"I don't know," he said. "That's the truth. Outside, Hollowmere isn't hidden — it's *absent.* There aren't rumors of a hidden valley, no legends. There's nothing. The absence is perfect. That's what makes finding it nearly impossible, and what makes the door worth—" He stopped himself.

"Worth what?"

He looked at the book under his arm, and then at me, and I watched him choose a smaller true thing over a larger one, and I filed that away, the way you file away a splinter to deal with later.

"Worth understanding," he said. "Before anyone acts on it."

From the ridge above the orchard, a crow dropped down onto the fence post and fixed me with one flat black eye. It didn't speak. It couldn't. But it looked at me the way Bramble looks at a gap in the fence — meaningfully, patiently, waiting for me to be the one who finally asks the right question.

And I realized, standing there with my stolen history in my chest and my stolen country in my eyes, that I had been asking the wrong question all along. Not *what am I.* Not *where is the road.*

The crow tilted its head, and the question assembled itself in me whole:

*If the valley maintains itself through forgetting — what happens to a place like this when somebody remembers?*

Jude followed my gaze to the bird, and his face went pale as paper.

"We need to talk about the choir," he said.
