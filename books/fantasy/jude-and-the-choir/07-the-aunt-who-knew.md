## 7. The Aunt Who Knew

My aunt Verity has exactly two volumes in her emotional library: *Everything Is Fine* and *We Do Not Discuss That*. I have lived twenty-six years inside those covers. So when I came down the hill from the chapel at a dead run, prophecy book clutched to my chest like a stolen infant, and found her standing in the kitchen doorway with her arms already crossed—arms crossed before she even knew what was coming—I understood that some part of her had been waiting for this night for a very long time.

"You've been in the piano bench," she said.

Not: *What's wrong?* Not: *Why are you out of breath?* She said it the way you'd say *You've tracked mud on the floor.* Flat. Resigned. Already sweeping.

I held the book up between us like evidence at a trial. "There's a book under the hymnals that knows everything about everyone in this valley, Verity. It knows what I said to Jude tonight. It knows what I'm going to say tomorrow. It knows—" My voice did something humiliating in the middle, cracked like a fourteen-year-old's. "It says we don't exist."

She didn't gasp. She didn't go pale. She looked at the book the way you look at an old dog that's finally knocked over the good vase after years of threatening to.

"Tea," she said. "You'll want tea for this. I know I will."

"I don't want *tea*, I want—"

"It's chamomile or nothing, Coral. Even apocalypses wait for the kettle in this house."

And that, more than anything, is how I knew she'd known. You can't be calm about the end of the world unless you've rehearsed.

* * *

The kitchen of Dunmore cottage is the warmest room in Hollowmere, which I have always taken as a point of pride and now understood as a strategy. Copper pots hung from hooks my grandfather hammered in before I was born. A loaf cooling on the sill because Verity bakes when she's nervous, and she'd apparently been nervous all day—the whole counter was bread. The clock ticked with the particular smugness of clocks in rooms where someone is about to confess something.

Verity poured two cups and sat down across from me, folded her hands, and became, visibly, a different woman. Not the aunt who pinched my cheek at choir and told me my beast-noises would get me talked about. Someone older. Someone who had carried a sack of stones up a very long hill and had just been given permission to set it down.

"Ask," she said. "You'll ask anyway. Better you ask in order."

I put the book on the table between us. Its cover was cracked leather gone soft as cloth, its pages the color of weak tea. "How long have you known about this?"

"Since I was nineteen." No hesitation. Nineteen. That was forty-one years of *Everything Is Fine*. Forty-one years of *We Do Not Discuss That.*

"Forty-one years." I heard my own voice go thin and strange. "Aunt Verity. You've known since before you raised me. Since before Mother died. You've known every single day of my entire life that there's a book in the chapel that says Hollowmere was *erased*—"

"Lower your voice." Not sharp. Weary. The weariness of someone who has lowered her own voice for four decades.

"Why? Who's going to hear? The neighbors who are also characters in the book? The goats?" I laughed, and it came out ugly. "That's the thing, isn't it. That's why the animals never talk back properly. That's why nobody ever leaves. We're not a town. We're a—what does it call us—a *pocket*. A pocket sewn shut by a dying king, and you found the seam and you *sat on it*."

"Coral—"

"Nineteen!" I stood up, because sitting felt like agreeing. "You were nineteen. What did you see? What made you decide that lying to every person you loved was the kind thing?"

She looked at me then with an expression I'd seen maybe three times in my life and never understood until that moment. It wasn't guilt. It was grief so old it had fossilized into patience.

"I saw the price," she said.

* * *

Here is what she told me, in the flat voice of a woman reciting a lesson learned too well, while the kettle ticked itself cool and neither of us touched our tea:

She had found the book the way I found it—by accident, chasing a sound. In her case it was the piano. The chapel piano used to play a single note at odd hours, one low A, like a heart clearing its throat, and nineteen-year-old Verity, who was curious the way I am curious, went looking. Found the bench. Found the book. Found her own name in it.

"Your grandmother's funeral," she said. "Written out. Every word the priest said. Every word *I* said. And below it, in the margin, in ink that hadn't dried yet—" her hands, for the first time, were not steady— "*Verity will look again. Verity will always look again.*"

"And you did."

"And I did. I read the whole thing. Took me a week, hiding in the chapel like a thief in my own town. And do you know what the last chapter said?" She smiled, and it was the worst smile I have ever seen on her face, because it was proud of itself and ashamed at the same time. "It said *the girl who reads to the end will tell no one, for she will understand what forgetting costs.*"

"But you didn't understand. You couldn't have. You were nineteen, you hadn't lost anything yet—"

"I had a husband," Verity said.

The kitchen went very quiet. The clock ticked. Outside, a nightbird started up and then, I noticed, thought better of it.

"His name was Aldous," she said. "You won't remember him because there's nothing to remember. He was a beekeeper. He had a laugh like a cart going over a bridge, he sang bass in the choir off-key on purpose to make me laugh during the hymns, and he wanted to see the ocean." Her thumb moved along the rim of her teacup, around and around. "Everyone here wants to see the ocean eventually. It's the one wanting the valley doesn't grind down. So one autumn morning he kissed my head, said he'd be back by supper, and walked up the north road."

"The north road doesn't go anywhere," I said. "It just... stops. Past the Hendersons' orchard it's all bramble and stone."

"That's what it does *now*. That's not what it did then. He got as far as the old gatepost—you know it, the one with the iron ring—and the valley let him take ten steps past it. Ten steps into the real country. Then the covenant took its toll." She said it evenly, the way you'd report weather from decades ago. "He didn't die. Death would have left something. A grave. A coat. A story. The covenant took him *whole*—took him out of the world and out of the book, Coral. I went to look him up that winter, in the prophecies, because I'd read them all by then and I knew where names lived. And his page was blank. Not torn. Blank, like it had never been written, like he had never—" Her voice held, but only the way a dam holds. "Twenty-six years I've raised you and you never once asked why I bake bread on the anniversary of nothing."

I sat down. My legs made the decision without consulting me.

"He tried to leave," I said slowly, "and the valley erased *him*."

"The valley keeps itself. That's what the book taught me. It isn't a spell that was cast once and left running. It's a spell that *feeds*. It eats the leaving. And when it can't eat the leaving, it eats the memory of the leaver, and then it eats the memory of the eater, and round and round until nobody remembers there was ever a door at all." She finally looked up at me. "Except the book remembers. The book is the one thing King Aldric's dying magic couldn't digest, because the book was written *outside*. It sits in our chapel like a splinter. Like a witness."

"And you read all that, and you chose silence."

"I chose *bread*," she said fiercely, and now the dam was leaking, and I hated myself a little for being glad to see it. "I chose teaching you to keep your mouth shut around the horses. I chose telling you your gift was a *flaw* to hide instead of the key to everything. Because the alternative was telling a child she was a loaded weapon living inside a creature that eats people for trying to use the door, and watching her spend her whole life either terrified or tempted. I chose the comfortable lie because I watched the truth take Aldous, and Coral—" she reached across the table and gripped my wrist, and her hand was shaking now, properly shaking— "the book says the door opens for *beast-speech*. It says the girl with the gift can command the guardians. It has said that since before you were born. Do you know what that means? It means this book has been waiting for you. Sitting in that bench for a hundred years like a trap with your name on the trigger."

"So you hid it under hymnals."

"Hymnals nobody opens. The choir sings from memory; that's part of the design, I think. The valley doesn't want us reading instructions." She laughed, wet and brief. "Do you know how many nights I lay awake deciding whether to burn it? Forty years of matches on the nightstand. And I never could. Because burning the witness felt like helping the murderer."

* * *

We sat there a long time. The loaf on the sill went from hot to warm to room temperature, marking the hours like a sundial of disappointment. When I spoke again, my voice had come back down out of the rafters, but it had brought friends.

"You let me grow up thinking I was broken."

"You weren't broken."

"You told me the goat-speech was a nervous condition. You told me if the miller's boy heard me talking to the oxen I'd be sent away. You built my whole childhood around hiding the one thing that makes me *me*, and you knew—knew—it was the door key. You knew what I was *for*."

"I knew what they'd say you were for," she corrected quietly. "Outside. There are people beyond the brambles who hunt your kind, Coral. The book mentions them. Margins, mostly, in handwriting that isn't Aldric's. Beast-speakers don't get welcomed out there. They get *collected*." Her grip on my wrist tightened. "Every lie I told you had a wall behind it. You're angry at the wallpaper. Fine. Be angry at the wallpaper. But I built the walls."

"And Jude?" I asked. "Was the stranger at the gate also wallpaper? Because the book has him in it, Verity. Whole chapters. His words, my words, words we haven't even said yet. If the book is a splinter from outside, then he's—"

"A splinter too." Something moved through her face that I couldn't name yet, though I'd learn its name soon enough. "Or a knife. I don't know which, and that is precisely the problem." She released my wrist and sat back, and the aunt I knew reassembled herself piece by piece, like armor going on. "Which is why you're going to stop seeing him."

"No."

"Coral—"

"*No.*" And here's the thing about rupture: it doesn't announce itself. You're having an argument and then somewhere in the middle of it you realize you're not arguing anymore, you're just two people on opposite sides of a table with a burned bridge running between you, and neither of you knows exactly which sentence did it. Maybe it was mine. Maybe hers. Maybe it was the moment I understood that she had loved me *and* lied to me *and* those were the same act, and that I could not hold both truths in one pair of hands, and neither could she.

"I spent twenty-six years being content," I said. "Content like a jarred peach. Sweet, sealed, shelf-stable. And you did that to me with love, I believe that, I will *always* believe that—but I can't un-know what I know, Verity. The valley erased a man you married. It will erase me the same way the day I try the gate. And you want me to stop seeing the one person who makes me feel like there might be a country where I fit?"

"I want you alive and whole and *remembered*."

"By whom?" I stood, and took the book off the table, and hugged it to my chest again. "By a valley that can't even remember your husband? By neighbors who sing hymn-instructions in their sleep?" At the door I stopped, because some small cowardly remnant of the girl she raised wanted to soften it, wanted to take it back, wanted chamomile and bread and *Everything Is Fine* forever.

I didn't take it back.

"I'm going to read the rest of it," I said. "All of it. Including the parts about the margins and the hunters and the door. And then I'm going to decide. Not you. Me."

Her face did something terrible and brave—she let me see her be afraid. "Coral. Whatever the book tells you the price is—it's worse. It's always worse than the page admits."

"Then I'll pay attention," I said, and heard, even as I said it, how much I sounded like my aunt, and hated it, and kept walking.

Behind me, in the kitchen that had raised me, the clock ticked on, counting down something neither of us could name. And out in the dark, at the edge of the garden where the hens slept, every single animal had gone silent at once—all except one crow on the fence post, who looked at me with one bright eye and said, in the flat grammar of beasts, in a voice like a hinge:

*The paper man watches the window. The paper man counts your breaths.*

I looked back toward the cottage. Upstairs, behind the lace curtain of the guest room, a lamp was lit, and a silhouette stood motionless against it, holding something rectangular and old.

Jude was reading my family's copy of the book.

And I had never mentioned that we had one.
