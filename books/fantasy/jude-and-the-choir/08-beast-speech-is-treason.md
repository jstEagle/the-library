## 8. Beast-Speech Is Treason

The order came to me in a room with no windows, which is how the Order of the Quiet Key says *we trust you*—by denying you any view of the sky you might be tempted to sell.

"Read it again," said Brother Cassian. He has a voice like a door hinge that's been oiled out of spite.

I read it again. I'd already read it four times, but there is a particular kind of man who believes comprehension is a discipline and not a gift, and Cassian is three of them stacked in a cassock.

*"Verify the beast-speaker of Hollowmere,"* I recited. *"Confirm the gift. Do not reveal your purpose. Do not open the door. Report by crow."*

"And?"

"And nothing. That's all it says."

"That's all it needs to say." He folded his hands on the table between us, fingers interlaced like he was praying to himself. "Beast-speech is treason, Jude. Say it back to me."

"Beast-speech is treason."

"Do you believe it?"

Here is the trouble with being raised by an Order: they teach you answers before they teach you questions, and by the time you're old enough to wonder why, the wondering itself feels like heresy. So I said, "Yes, Brother," in the flat voice we were all issued at age seven along with our first black coat, and I believed it the way you believe the floor will hold—without ever once thinking about what holds the floor.

Cassian smiled. It did not improve his face. "The last verified speaker was forty years ago. A girl who could call wolves out of their own skins. She opened three doors before we caught her."

"What happened to her?"

"She was corrected."

He said *corrected* the way other men say *buried*, and I understood both meanings perfectly, and I filed both away in the part of my head where the Order keeps its things—the part that doesn't ask what holds the floor.

"The one in Hollowmere," Cassian said. "The records say the valley swallowed a whole village in King Aldric's time. Erased from every map, every prophecy-book, every memory that crossed its border. Except—" and here he leaned forward, savoring it, a man about to share gossip with God as his witness, "—the books remember what people forget. Paper has no mercy. Somewhere in that valley there is a text naming the speaker. A child born with the old blood, grown now, untrained, unsuspecting. You will find them. You will confirm them. And then, Jude—"

"I know. Report by crow."

"*Then wait.*" His knuckles whitened on the table. "You do not touch them. You do not warn them. You do not, under any circumstances, become fond of them. Verification is not friendship. The moment you start thinking of them as a person instead of a threat is the moment you become useless—and useful brothers are the only kind we keep."

I want to tell you I flinched at that. I want to tell you some buried part of me heard the warning and rebelled against it on principle, some romantic ember refusing to be lectured into obedience.

What actually happened is I nodded, thanked him for his time, and spent the walk back to my cell composing clever remarks I never delivered. That's the kind of man I was: armed with wit, unarmed with anything else.

* * *

Getting into Hollowmere should have been impossible. That was the first thing I noticed, standing on a hillside in the real country looking down at a valley that was not on any map I'd ever stolen, memorized, or burned.

There was a road. There shouldn't have been—a road implies destinations, and destinations imply witnesses—but there it was, winding down through birch trees like it had always been there, like it had simply been waiting for someone with the right kind of wrongness in their pockets. The Order had given me a token: a coin stamped with a key on one side and nothing on the other. Hold it, walk downhill, and don't think too loudly.

Halfway down, a fox stepped onto the road ahead of me and stared.

Now, I've been around animals my whole life. The Order keeps ravens—we'll get to that; the ravens are a whole sermon unto themselves—and I know how creatures look at a man. With appetite, with wariness, with the profound indifference of something that has better things to do.

This fox looked at me the way a customs officer looks at a suspicious passport.

"You're not from here," it said—or rather, the meaning arrived in my head wearing the shape of words, which is how beast-speech works when you have the ear for it. Most people hear nothing but barking and birdsong. I heard accusation.

"No," I agreed, because lying to a fox seemed unwise.

"It's been a long time since anyone walked down this road." The fox sat, wrapping its tail over its feet like punctuation. "They don't come down. They only go up, and even then only in dreams."

"Dreams?"

But the fox had lost interest, or decided I wasn't worth the conversation, and trotted off into the bracken with the air of a bureaucrat ending a meeting. I stood there holding a coin with nothing on one side and thought: *that valley talks to its animals, and the animals talk back, and somewhere down there is a human being who can hold up their half.*

Verification, I told myself. Just verification. Find the speaker, confirm the gift, send the crow, go home. In and out like a pickpocket. Nothing in the order said anything about staying for supper.

Which is how I learned my second lesson about orders, the one they don't print in the rulebooks: the shortest word in any instruction is the one that hides the biggest lie. *Do not become fond.* Four syllables of prophylactic, as if fondness were a disease you could decline vaccination against.

I walked down into Hollowmere carrying my coin and my immunity, and I caught my death within the week.

* * *

Let me describe the date, because I have replayed it so many times it's worn smooth as a river stone, and because the honest version requires me to admit that everything—the awkwardness, the goat incident, the way Coral laughed with their whole ribcage—was, from my side, surveillance.

That's the word. Surveillance. I sat across a table from the most alive person I had ever met and took mental notes.

Subject displays the gift constantly but unconsciously—commands the goats without noticing, soothes a spooked horse with two words, argues fluently with a hen who, I swear on every relic the Order pretends to venerate, argued back and won. Subject does not appear to know what they are. Or knows and hides it well. (It was neither, as it turned out. It was worse. It was someone taught to be ashamed of the truest thing about themselves.)

Animals fall silent around me—I'd noticed it since childhood, the ravens excepted; even the ravens only speak to me when spoken to, like colleagues forced to share an office. But around Coral, the silence broke. Birds sang at them. Dogs leaned on them like furniture with opinions. And I sat there across the table cataloguing evidence while my chest did something the training manuals had no section for.

They asked me odd questions, Coral did. Or I asked them—honestly, the evening was such a tangle of mutual missteps that the record needs a footnote. They asked about roads. Where I'd come from. Which road, specifically, because Coral named three roads that do not exist, and watched me with those eyes that missed nothing, waiting to see if I'd flinch at the trap.

I flinched at the trap. I'm telling you this because I promised myself this account would be honest, and honesty means admitting I am not actually very good at espionage. My cover story was a disaster held together with charm. I claimed to be from "up north," which in Hollowmere apparently meant a place with a lake, and I confirmed the lake, and Coral's aunt later told everyone the lake had dried up thirty years ago, and I spent a sleepless night constructing elaborate theories about dried-up lakes before concluding I had simply lied badly.

And still. Still. When Coral laughed—at me, at my terrible lies, at the goat that had eaten my sleeve—they laughed like someone opening a window in a house sealed for winter, and I sat there with my little coin in my pocket and my little order in my head, taking notes, taking notes, taking notes, and underneath the note-taking a quieter voice kept saying: *you are going to be the reason this person stops laughing.*

I drowned that voice. I was good at drowning voices; the Order teaches swimming lessons in exactly one direction.

* * *

Report the first, sent by raven on the third night:

*Speaker located. Gift confirmed beyond doubt. The beasts obey them without command—instinctive, constant. Recommend full classification. Will continue observation. — J.*

I wrote *observation* and thought about the way Coral had tucked a stray dog's ear back behind its head, murmuring something too low for me to catch, and how the dog had sighed like a man setting down a heavy load, and how I had felt, watching it, a homesickness for a home I'd never had.

Report the second, sent a week later:

*Subject suspects nothing. Valley docile. No anomalies to report. — J.*

This was a lie. The valley was full of anomalies. The choir sang hymns nobody had taught them. My landlady gave me directions using landmarks that weren't there and didn't notice. The chapel held a book older than the town that remembered more than anyone in it. I had found the book myself by then—I'll confess that much here, since confession is apparently the price of admission to my own memory. I'd gone looking for proof and found tonight's date written down a century before either of us was born, including a joke Coral hadn't made yet.

I reported none of it. *No anomalies to report.* I told myself I was protecting the mission's integrity, that loose information compromised operational security, that a good brother reports only verified facts.

What I was doing was buying time. Time with Coral. Time in a valley where the bread was warm and the strangers got waved at and nobody had ever once asked me to prove I was useful enough to keep.

Report the third, I never sent. It sits in my coat pocket still, crow-stamped and unsent, and it reads:

*Brother Cassian. Request guidance. The subject is—*

And then nothing. Three words and a dash, like a sentence walking off a cliff. Because every time I sat down to finish it, I discovered that the next word after *is* was one I couldn't put in an official report. *Kind.* *Funny.* *Lonely in a way I recognize.* *The only person who has ever made me feel like less of a forgery.*

The Order has a word for what I was becoming. They use it at correction ceremonies, quietly, the way you name a disease. *Compromised.*

I prefer the word *awake,* but nobody asks the compromised for terminology.

* * *

Here is what they never told us in the windowless rooms, or maybe what they told us so early we forgot learning it: beast-speech is treason not because it's dangerous in general, but because it's dangerous *specifically*. The door-guardians—the old ones, the stone-and-starlight ones that stand at every threshold the king's spell ever touched—were made to answer one voice. The king's voice. Aldric's voice, and the blood that carried it.

A beast-speaker can command a guardian to open a door.

One door is a scandal. Every door is a war.

That was the catechism. That was what I'd been raised on: somewhere out there, speakers are being born, and every one of them is a key that fits every lock, and keys that fit every lock cannot be allowed to know they are keys. Better they live and die thinking themselves odd, unlucky, strange. Better a lifetime of small shame than an empire of open doors.

And I believed it. God help me, sitting in that valley eating Coral Dunmore's aunt's apple cake, I still mostly believed it. Belief isn't a coat you take off when it gets uncomfortable; it's skin, and peeling it off bleeds.

But belief bends. Mine bent the afternoon I watched Coral crouched in a pasture arguing with a goat named something absurd—Barnaby? Bartholomew?—and I saw the goat *listen*. Not the dumb listening of an animal awaiting food. Real listening. The goat tilted its head, considered, and then did precisely what Coral asked, and Coral patted its nose and called it a scoundrel, and the goat made a sound that was unmistakably laughter.

Nobody had trained them. Nobody had even told them what they were. They spoke to the world the way I breathed, effortlessly, and the world answered, and if the Order's catechism was right, this person was the most dangerous creature in existence.

And if the catechism was wrong—if the doors were locked not to protect the world from speakers but to protect a dying king's lie from being undone—then Coral wasn't dangerous at all. Coral was a hand on a handle that everyone else had forgotten existed.

I didn't know yet which truth it was. I knew only that I was supposed to determine it and send a bird, and that every day I delayed, the bird got heavier.

* * *

The raven came for me on a Tuesday. They always know; I've never learned how. It landed on my windowsill in Aunt Verity's guest room, feathers slick with rain, and fixed me with the flat golden stare of a collections agent.

"Well?" it said.

"Not yet."

"'Not yet' was last week's word. And the week before. Brother Cassian grows creative in his impatience. He asked me to remind you that useful brothers are the only kind kept."

"Tell him I'm verifying thoroughly."

"Tell him yourself, someday, on your knees." The raven preened, unbothered. Then, softer, with that horrible avian perceptiveness: "The speaker. Is it as they say?"

I looked out the window. Down the lane, Coral was walking home from the chapel in the last of the light, and a crow flew alongside them—not following, accompanying—and I could see their mouth moving, and the crow's head tilting, two friends arguing about something in the easy old way of long acquaintance.

"It's as they say," I told the raven. "And more."

"Mm." The bird hopped closer, rain dripping off the sill. "Then you should know the Order's crows have started circling the valley, Jude. Not mine. Theirs. Blacker than me and twice as hungry. Whatever you are waiting for—wait faster."

It left without another word, as ravens do, and I stood at the window watching Coral until the dark took them, and I understood that my time had run out in some way I couldn't yet measure.

Because here is the thing I finally admitted to myself that night, alone, with the unsent letter digging into my ribs through my coat: I had stopped being able to imagine the endgame. Every path from where I stood led through Coral—verification, extraction, correction, whatever word the Order dressed it in this century—and every one of those paths ended with that laugh going silent, and I found that I could plan the steps and could not, for the life of me, plan to take them.

Compromised. Awake. Call it what you like.

The next morning, Coral knocked on Verity's door with a book under their arm and terror and fury fighting for space on their face, and said six words that ended my career as a spy forever:

"We need to talk about the book."
