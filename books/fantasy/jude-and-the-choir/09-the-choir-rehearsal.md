## 9. The Choir Rehearsal

There are two kinds of silence in Hollowmere, and I'd spent twenty-six years learning to tell them apart.

The first kind is the good silence, the one the valley does on purpose: the hush that settles over the fields at dusk like a quilt being tucked in, the quiet of a town that has nowhere better to be. It smells like woodsmoke and bread and the particular green smell of the mill pond at evening. That silence says *you're safe, you're home, stop asking questions.*

The second kind is the one the goats go into when something's wrong.

Thursday was choir night, which meant Aunt Verity spent forty minutes telling me I looked underdressed for an event where everyone wore the same brown smocks, and I spent those forty minutes pretending my collar didn't itch. The choir had been meeting every Thursday since before I was born—same chapel, same pews, same eleven people who could not carry a tune in a bucket but carried it anyway, out of some stubborn civic devotion. Mrs. Pell. Old Tam and his brother Older Tam. The Wicker twins, who harmonized only by accident. My aunt, soprano, front row, pitch-perfect in a way that always struck me as slightly show-offy for a woman who claimed not to believe in showing off.

I went because Coral Dunmore goes to choir. That was the whole reason. That's most of the reasons in Hollowmere, if you're honest: because so-and-so goes, because we've always gone, because going is what people here do instead of leaving.

I hadn't wanted to go since the book. Since Jude sat across from me in the chapel kitchen with a prophecy text older than the chapel itself and read me my own sentences back in someone else's handwriting. Since the words *King Aldric* and *erased* and *the door beneath* had rearranged everything I thought a valley was. Since I learned that the cozy silence I'd been wrapped in all my life was less a blanket than a lid.

But you can't skip choir without people noticing, and lately I'd developed a theory about noticing: in Hollowmere, noticing was the first symptom of a disease the valley treated very seriously.

So I went.

The walk over was wrong in small ways I catalogued without meaning to. The mill pond had no ducks on it. There were always ducks—greedy, rude ducks that used to mob me for bread and got nothing, because I don't feed animals I can't talk to, which is all of them now, thanks to a promise I made myself after the crow. But Thursday there were no ducks at all, just water lying flat as poured glass, holding a sky with no clouds in it. And the hedges along Chapel Lane were blooming out of season, little white flowers opening as I passed them and closing again behind me, like eyes deciding whether I was worth staying awake for.

"You're imagining it," I told myself, in the tone Verity uses on me, which means *stop it.* It's remarkable how much comfort you can take from your own voice when you're scared. It's also remarkable how little.

The chapel smelled the way it always smelled—candle wax and old hymnals and the ghost of a thousand casseroles—but underneath that, tonight, there was something else. Something like the air after lightning. Ozone with a sweetness in it, faint, almost pleasant, the way rot can be sweet if you catch it early enough.

Mrs. Pell met me at the door with her usual crushing warmth. "Coral! We saved you a seat next to Verity." Her smile was wide and fixed, like a painting of a smile rather than the thing itself. "We're doing a new piece tonight."

"A new piece?" In thirty years of Thursdays, the choir had sung maybe six songs, rotating like crop fields. New pieces were not a thing that happened in Hollowmere. New pieces were practically emigration. "Who wrote it?"

"Oh," said Mrs. Pell, and her smile did something terrible, which was nothing at all. "It's been in the book."

She turned and bustled inside, and I stood on the step a moment longer, because my body had apparently decided that crossing that threshold required a vote, and the vote was not coming back unanimous.

*Coral,* said a voice near my ear, dry as paper. *Little speaker.*

I looked up. On the chapel roof ridge, folded into the shape of a gargoyle that gargoyles would have found flattering, sat the crow—the crow, the one who'd warned me about the paper man, looking pleased with itself in the way only crows and aunts can manage.

*You again,* I thought at it, careful not to move my lips. Talking to animals in public was bad enough when nobody knew. Talking to them while they talked back, in front of a chapel full of neighbors, was a whole different category of trouble.

*You shouldn't sing tonight.*

*It's choir,* I thought back. *Singing is the entire point.*

*The song isn't for you. The song is for the valley. You'll hear it anyway. Don't let it in through your mouth.* The crow shuffled its feathers, and one black eye caught the last of the light. *Your mother sang it once, you know. Before you were born. She stopped coming after that.*

My mother. Who had left when I was three, in the way people leave in Hollowmere, which is to say completely and without explanation, and whom Verity never discussed except to say *she had her reasons* in a voice that closed doors. My chest did something complicated.

*What happened when she sang it?*

*Ask the stone man,* said the crow, which was its way of saying *ask the guardian*, which was its way of saying *you know exactly what that will cost you,* and then it flew off toward the bell tower like punctuation leaving a sentence.

Inside, the pews were full. Eleven singers plus me made twelve, and Verity patted the spot beside her with a look that said both *sit down* and *we will be discussing the crow later,* which meant she'd seen it too, which meant she'd been watching the roof, which meant she knew more about crows than she'd ever admitted. I sat. The hymnals were already open on the racks—not to any of the six familiar songs, but to a page I'd never seen, cream-colored, unnumbered, the ink still somehow wet-looking though the paper was yellowed.

The page had no title. Just a verse, set to a tune printed above it in notation that hurt to look at directly, the way bright snow hurts.

"From the top," said Mr. Aldous from the organ bench, though no one had appointed him to lead, and no one questioned it.

And eleven people who could not sing opened their mouths and sang.

Here is the thing about the Hollowmere choir: they are bad. They are gloriously, reliably bad. Mrs. Pell goes sharp whenever she gets excited, which is always. Older Tam sings a half-beat behind Younger Tam like an echo with opinions. The Wicker twins have never once landed the same note at the same time. Their singing sounds like a bag of cats falling downstairs, and everyone loves them for it, and it is one of the truest things about the valley—that we do things badly together and call it belonging.

Tonight, twelve voices came out of those twelve throats in perfect unison.

Perfect. Every note. Every entrance. The harmony stacked itself up clean and cold as ice on a fence rail. Even my own voice—I felt it happen, felt my mouth open and my breath support and the sound come out rounded and true, a voice I have never had, a voice trained by no one—and I could not stop it. My jaw moved like it belonged to the song. I dug my nails into my palm and kept singing.

And the words, once I heard them properly, once I stopped drowning in the wrongness of how good we sounded—

The words were instructions.

*Keep the gate shut, keep the name dim,*
*what was given, hold on a hymn;*
*let no road run past the well's dark rim,*
*let no stranger count our kin.*
*Sow forgetting, reap the same,*
*sing it soft, and stay unseen—*
*the king sleeps deep beneath the lane,*
*and the door stays shut between.*

That was the first verse. There were four.

Verse two told us to turn travelers around gently, kindly, with offers of tea and wrong directions. Verse three instructed us to love each other loudly in public and quietly keep watch—"a warm face is the best fence," it said, and something in my stomach turned over, because that was Hollowmere exactly, that was the whole valley distilled into one line, kindness deployed as architecture. And verse four—

Verse four was addressed to whoever sang it. Not to the congregation. To the singer. *If one wakes and asks the why,* it went, *sing louder till they drown;* *what the valley loves, it keeps;* *what the valley keeps, it keeps down.*

*I* was the one who woke. I was sitting in the second row watching my own mouth explain to me, in four-part harmony, precisely how I would be handled.

I stopped singing. Or tried to. The song kept coming out of me anyway, smooth and obedient, and the horror of that—of my own breath conscripted—made sweat start cold at my hairline. Beside me, Verity sang on with her eyes fixed straight ahead, her face composed into serene nothing, and I saw then what I should have seen years ago: she wasn't enjoying choir. She wasn't even participating. She was *enduring* it, the way you endure a medical procedure, her jaw tight behind the melody, her knuckles white on the hymnal.

She knew. She'd known for decades. Every Thursday of my childhood, she'd come here and sung this thing and come home and kissed my forehead and never said a word.

Around me the faces glowed. That was the worst part, worse than the lyrics, worse than the stolen perfection: everyone looked *happy*. Mrs. Pell's painted smile had become a real one, beatific, eyes half-closed. The Tams swayed together in time for perhaps the first time in their lives. Whatever the song was doing, it was feeding them something—warmth, belonging, the feeling of the valley loving them back—and they drank it down gratefully, and the valley drank down their voices, and somewhere beneath the lane something old slept on, fed and guarded by a roomful of people who thought they were at practice.

The song ended. Perfectly, of course, on a chord so resolved it felt like a lock clicking.

Silence—then the ordinary rustle of hymnals closing, the ordinary chatter: "Lovely, isn't it?" "Been in the book ages, wonder why we never—" "Same time Thursday!" Faces flushed and pleasant. Mrs. Pell squeezed my arm. "You've such a lovely voice, Coral. Never noticed before."

Nobody remembered sounding different. Nobody noticed they'd sounded perfect. Nobody had noticed the lyrics at all, or if they had, the noticing slid off them like rain off wax. Already the song was smoothing itself out of their memories, I could see it happening, the way a pond closes over a thrown stone.

Except Verity. And except me. And, I realized with a chill, except the empty twelfth chair in the back row—the one reserved, every week, for a member who never came, whose name was penciled on the roll in faded ink: *J.Visitor.* A joke, we'd always assumed. A place set for no one, the way you set a plate for Elijah.

A place set for a visitor. For a paper man. For Jude, whose name was in a century-old book, whose arrival had been written down before either of us was born. The choir had been saving him a seat.

"Coral." Verity's hand was on my sleeve, low and urgent, her voice pitched under the shuffle of departing neighbors. "Not here. Walk with me. Take the long way past the mill."

"The song," I whispered. "Aunt Verity, the *song*—"

"I know what the song is." For the first time in my life I watched fear move across her face without permission, raw and quick as a fish under ice. "I've sung it every Thursday for forty years so you'd never have to hear it. Tonight you heard it, which means the valley knows you're waking, which means we have very little time." She glanced up at the rafters, at the bell tower, at whatever listened. "The guardian under the chapel has started humming along, Coral. Do you understand? It hasn't done that since the year your mother left."

* * *

Verity took the long way like she'd said, past the mill, where the wheel wasn't turning though the sluice was open, water pouring through a wheel that had simply decided to wait. The ducks still weren't anywhere. The white flowers on the hedges tracked us the whole way, opening and shutting, opening and shutting, a hedge-full of slow applause or slow warning.

"Forty years," I said finally. "You've known forty years and you let me sit in that pew every week."

"I let you sit in that pew because a Dunmore who stops attending choir in Hollowmere gets *visited*." She said it flatly, without drama, which made it worse. "People who opt out get reminded. Gently. Then less gently. Your mother opted out, Coral. Ask yourself what happened to her."

"She left."

"Did she? Is that the story? Because I was there, and I remember a woman who walked to the chapel one Thursday and came home with her eyes wrong, and within a month she couldn't remember why she'd ever wanted anything, and then one morning she was walking toward the far gate and none of us could say her name, our tongues wouldn't make it, and by afternoon nobody could remember she'd existed except me, because I'd bitten my tongue bloody during the hymn to keep from singing it." Verity's breath came short. "The valley doesn't kill dissenters. It *forgets* them. It sings them out of existence. And I have spent forty years making sure it never had a reason to sing you."

The mill wheel groaned once, as if turning over in its sleep, and did not turn.

"So the choir maintains it," I said. My voice sounded far away to me. "The spell. Aldric's spell. It runs on—on Thursdays and brown smocks and Mrs. Pell going sharp."

"It runs on consent that doesn't know it's consenting." Verity stopped at the footbridge and gripped the rail, looking down at the black water. "That was the genius of it, I think. A dying king couldn't guard a door forever. So he taught the door to guard itself, using the people, using their love for each other, using the thing they'd least suspect. Every hymn re-ties the knot. Every friendly neighbor is a thread in it. Every potluck is a ward." She laughed, one hard syllable with no humor in it whatsoever. "I used to think the worst thing about this valley was that nothing ever changed. Now I know the truth. Nothing changes because change is being *sung against*. Continuously. Forever. By people who think they're just keeping a tradition alive."

Below the bridge, in the water, I could see it if I didn't look directly—a shadow under the surface, long and patient, keeping pace with us. The guardian. Listening. Maybe humming.

"And Jude?" I asked. "Where does he fit? The twelfth chair—"

"—has been waiting for him since 1899," Verity said. "Yes. I know. I've read the book, Coral, all of it, more times than you've read your own name. The prophecy doesn't end where you stopped reading. There's more. About the door. About what walks out of it." She turned to me then, and her face in the dusk was older than I'd ever let myself see. "And about the singer who opens it, and the price, and the choice she makes after. But not here. It hears everything sung and half of what's spoken. Come home. Bolt the door. And Coral—" she hesitated, and for a moment she was not my formidable aunt but just a tired woman who'd been holding a valley's secret alone since before I was born, "—whatever you do, do not hum."

We walked home under a sky with no stars in it, because the valley had never needed stars, and behind us the chapel bell rang nine o'clock though no one had pulled the rope, and the sound it made was almost, almost the first line of the hymn.

And from the roof of my aunt's house, when we reached it, the crow watched us come with its head tilted, and spoke—one word aloud, clear as a dropped coin:

"*Thursday.*"

As if the whole valley were a song, and we had just been given our cue.
