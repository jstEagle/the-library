## 10. First Memory Gone

The thing about a secret you can't stop using is that it stops being a secret and starts being a habit.

I'd been talking to the goats my whole life, obviously, but since the night I found the book in the piano bench—since the whole rotten architecture of Hollowmere came tumbling down into the space between me and Jude—I'd started talking to *everything*. The fence posts. The rain barrel. The old stone man at the crossroads who everyone called the Sentinel and everyone also pretended not to see, which is a very particular kind of rural madness: acknowledging a nine-foot statue enough to name it, but never enough to wonder why its eyes follow you.

I wondered now. Wondering was practically my occupation.

"Good morning," I said to the Sentinel on a Tuesday that smelled like cut hay and coming rain. "Don't suppose you're listening."

The morning did what mornings do, which is nothing.

But here's what nobody tells you about beast-speech: it isn't a language so much as a permission. You don't say *hello* to a goat in goat. You say something older, something that means *I see you as you are*, and the goat decides whether that deserves an answer. And the Sentinel, standing there at the crossroads with his lichen-streaked face and his hands folded like a man eternally waiting for a bus that stopped running in 1899—

The Sentinel answered.

Not with words. With a *turning*. A sensation like a great door swinging on hinges the size of seasons, somewhere behind my ribs. The air around the stone went taut, the way it does before lightning, and every hair on my arms stood up and paid attention.

*I hear,* said something vast and patient and terribly tired. *Little speaker. What do you want.*

I should have run. Instead—and this is the part where you can really admire my judgment—I sat down on the wet grass at his feet like a child at a grandfather's knee and asked him how the door worked.

* * *

I want to be fair to myself. I hadn't slept. Jude had left before dawn—he said he needed to check something at the chapel, and the way he said *check something* made me certain he was going to do something brave and stupid without me, which is apparently his love language. Aunt Verity had spent breakfast pretending her tea was fascinating. The prophecy book lay under my floorboard with its 1899 date and its transcription of an evening that hadn't finished happening yet, and every time I thought about the lines neither of us had spoken, my chest did a thing that wasn't quite fear and wasn't quite hope and was exhausting either way.

So yes. Sleep-deprived, lovelorn, freshly informed that my entire hometown was a hole in the world's memory. Naturally I went to interrogate a statue.

"The door," I repeated. "Under the chapel. How does it open?"

*It opens for the voice it was locked against,* the Sentinel said. Rain began to fall, soft and unhurried, and did not touch me. I noticed that the way you notice your own heartbeat: distantly, then all at once. *King Aldric made the lock of forgetting, so he made the key of remembering-speech. The beasts remember everything. That is why they may command what men have forgotten.*

"You're a guardian. Of the door."

*I am a guardian of many things.* Something moved through his voice like weather. *Ask what you came to ask, Coral Dunmore. You are wearing your question like a wet coat.*

I picked at the grass. "Jude thinks—no. We both think there's a price. For speaking the door open. The book says every attempt costs a memory."

Silence. The kind that has texture.

"How much?" I asked. "For one attempt. Just one. To test it."

*One memory,* said the Sentinel. *Chosen by the door, not by you. It takes what is loosest. What sits nearest the surface. What you would least think to miss until it is gone.*

"And after? Does it come back?"

*Nothing given to the valley comes back.* A pause, and then, gentler than stone has any right to be: *You may walk away, little speaker. This is still true. You may walk away and remain whole and small and safe inside your aunt's kitchen until the day you are buried in the ground of a place that does not exist.*

"That's not safety," I said. "That's just a smaller cage."

*Yes,* he agreed, horribly. *I know. I built it.*

And I swear on every goat I've ever owned, the stone man sounded like he meant *we*. Like he'd been part of it. Like he'd been carrying that sentence for a hundred and twenty-odd years and had finally found someone he could put it down in front of.

But grief makes people selfish, and I was drowning in mine, and what I heard most clearly in that vast tired voice was not the confession. It was the mechanics.

One memory. Chosen by the door. Loosest first.

I thought: *I have hundreds of memories. Whole decades. What's one?*

Reader, write that down somewhere. Put it on a card. *What's one?* — the four most expensive words in the English language.

* * *

I didn't tell Jude. This requires some unpacking, because we had recently exchanged confessions the way other couples exchange colds—thoroughly, painfully, with a great deal of lying down afterward. He'd told me about his Order, the hunt for beast-speakers, the fact that walking me out the door might mean delivering me straight into their hands. I'd told him about the choir hymn, about the neighbors' faces going smooth and watchful, about the way my own reflection sometimes felt like a stranger doing an impression of me. We were, I believed, past secrets.

But here is what I've learned about myself since: I hoard the dangerous things. I always have. When I was eight I found a dead sparrow and buried it under the lilac and told no one, and went out every day for a month to sit with the little mound, guarding my private grief like treasure. Some part of me decided, sitting in the wet grass at the Sentinel's feet, that testing the price was my sparrow. My mound of turned earth. Mine to dig alone.

Besides—if it worked, if the door groaned open even a crack, imagine Jude's face. Imagine handing him that. You don't hand someone a gift you haven't checked for teeth.

That evening I told Verity I was going to the chapel to pray, which was funny in a way she couldn't appreciate, because the chapel is the least holy building in Christendom—it's a lid. She looked at me over her mending for a long moment, needle paused mid-stitch, and I watched her decide, visibly, to trust me one more time. Her face did something complicated. I was too preoccupied to read it properly. I wish I hadn't been. There was information in that face I would have given a lot for later.

"I'll leave the lamp lit," she said.

"Aunt Verity—"

"Mm?"

"Nothing." I kissed the top of her head, which smelled of lavender and woodsmoke and twenty-six years of unasked questions, and walked out into the blue dark toward the crossroads.

* * *

The Sentinel stood under a sky rinsed clean of cloud, stars scattered thick as spilled salt. Hollowmere's nights were always beautiful in that suffocating postcard way—the whole valley prettied up for an audience that could never come.

"Alright," I said. My breath fogged. "Let's say I want to try. Tonight. Just a crack. Just to know it's real."

*It is real,* he said. *It has always been real. That is the tragedy of it—the lie is only ever painted over truth. It never replaces it.*

"Will it hurt?"

*No.* And then, because stone cannot lie but stone can be precise: *That is the trap. It will feel like nothing. You will pay and not feel the coin leave your pocket. You will simply be lighter, and call the lightness relief.*

I laughed. It came out wrong, thin and high. "You're very cheerful for a doorstop."

*I am very old,* he corrected. *Cheerfulness is a young country's disease.*

He told me how. Or rather, he didn't tell me—he showed me, the way you teach a bird to fly by falling off the roof beside it. There were words underneath his words, the same permission-language I used with goats, but deeper, older, folded through with names I felt rather than heard. Names of things that held up the world when it was younger. He set them in my mouth like stones in a nest, and told me to stand at the crossroads, and speak.

I stood. I spoke.

I won't transcribe it. Not because it's forbidden—I suspect nothing about it is forbidden anymore, that ship sailed, burned, and its ashes were erased from prophecy—but because it wasn't language in any way that survives being written down. It was more like crying out loud in a grammar. It was the sound of asking a locked thing to be honest.

The valley *moved*.

Not visibly. Underneath. I felt the hills shift their weight like sleeping cattle. I felt the chapel above the town exhale something it had held for a century. Down the road, every dog began barking at once and then, in perfect unison, stopped. The air split along a seam I couldn't see—a seam running from the crossroads to the chapel door, glowing faintly, the color of the light through closed eyelids.

And through the seam, for three heartbeats, I saw out.

Grass. Ordinary grass, going on forever, silvered by moonlight, rising and falling in a wind that had never once touched Hollowmere. A road. An actual road, rutted and honest and leading away. Somewhere far off, a light that might have been a farmhouse or might have been a star come down to earth.

Three heartbeats. Then the seam drew shut with a sound like a book closing, and the valley settled back onto itself, and the night resumed its postcard smile.

"It worked," I whispered. I was laughing, or crying, or both; my face was wet and my chest was full of helium. "Sentinel. It *worked*. Did you see—there's a road, there's actually a—"

*I saw,* he said quietly.

And then I reached for my mother's face and it wasn't there.

* * *

I need to describe this carefully, because there is no good way to describe it and I refuse to do it badly.

I know I had a mother. That knowledge sat intact, solid, load-bearing. I knew her name—Maren. I knew she died when I was four. I knew Verity raised me after, and that the house had two of everything for a while because Verity couldn't bear to pack away a dead sister's things, and that eventually she did, one box per Sunday, and cried in the kitchen where she thought I couldn't hear.

All of that remained. The scaffolding of her stood complete.

Only the face was gone. Not blurred. *Gone.* I reached for it the way you reach for a word that's on the tip of your tongue, and there was no tip, no tongue, no word—just a clean absence shaped exactly like a face. I tried to rebuild it from parts. Brown hair—was it brown? Eyes—I knew they'd been a color. I knew she'd smiled at me. I could not, for the life of me, tell you what her smile had done to her face. I stood in the moonlit crossroads trying to summon my own mother and getting, instead, a description. A file. A census entry where a woman used to live.

She'd sung to me. I remembered that—or I remembered knowing it. Had I remembered the tune? An hour ago, maybe. Now the tune was gone too, and I only knew there'd been a tune the way you know there'd been a chair in a room after the room's been emptied.

"No," I said. Out loud. Stupidly, to the empty air, as though the air could give it back. "No, no—that's not—that's not *fair*, I barely used that one, I wasn't even—"

*Loosest first,* said the Sentinel, and there was no triumph in it, no satisfaction, only that terrible patience. *I told you, little speaker. It takes what sits nearest the surface. What you would least think to miss.*

"She's my *mother*."

*She died twenty-two years ago,* he said gently. *Her face has had a long time to grow loose. The door knows its business. Grief that has healed leaves shallow roots.*

I sat down in the road. Actually sat, in the dirt, in my coat, and the night went on being beautiful around me, indifferent as a painting. Somewhere a nightjar started up its churring call, and I realized with a lurch that I could understand it—*small death, small death, the moon is a mouth*—and that understanding birds had cost me nothing tonight except everything.

Here is the arithmetic I did, kneeling in the road, and I want you to check my work, because I got it wrong:

One memory bought a crack in the door. Three heartbeats of the real country. Which meant the whole door—wide enough for a person, wide enough for two—might cost ten memories. Twenty. Might cost, memory by memory, the entire person doing the buying. Not death. Worse than death, maybe, because death at least takes all of you at once, honestly, whereas this would take you in installments and leave a functioning stranger standing in your coat saying *who was Maren again?* and meaning it.

Erosion. That's the word. Water doesn't hate the stone. Water isn't even thinking about the stone. But given enough time, water gets exactly what it wants, and the stone becomes sand, and the sand becomes a riverbed, and the riverbed doesn't remember it was ever anything else.

Hollowmere had been eroding us for a hundred and twenty years. I'd simply found the tap.

"Coral."

I hadn't heard footsteps, but Jude was there, coming down the crossroads road at a half-run, coat flying, lantern in hand, face white as the moonlight. He must have seen the seam. Must have felt the valley shudder from the chapel steps. He dropped to his knees in front of me and took my face in his cold hands and said my name again, twice, like checking a coin for counterfeit.

"What did you do? Coral. *What did you do?*"

I opened my mouth to answer, breezy, wry, fine—my default settings—and what came out instead, in a voice I didn't recognize as mine, was:

"Jude. What did my mother look like?"

His hands stilled on my cheeks. I watched him understand, watch it land on him the way the rain hadn't landed on me at the crossroads—all at once, everywhere, soaking through. His thumb brushed my cheekbone and his eyes went glassy and he said, very softly:

"Oh, Coral. Oh, no. What did it take?"

And I opened my mouth to tell him, and discovered the second loss, the free one, the one the door throws in: I couldn't remember her face well enough to mourn it out loud. All I could say was, "Something loose. Something I wouldn't miss." And then, because the night was enormous and I was suddenly, newly alone inside my own skull in a way I had never been, I pressed my forehead against his shoulder and let him hold me up.

Above us, the Sentinel watched with his lichen eyes, and said nothing, and kept my secret the way he keeps everything.

It was only later—much later, when the lantern was guttering and Jude had walked me home through streets gone strangely, watchfully quiet—that I noticed the third thing.

My mother's face was gone. That I knew.

But standing at Verity's gate, looking up at the lit window, at her silhouette bent over her mending—at a face I had seen every single day of my twenty-six years—I felt something flicker at the edges of it too. Faint. Frayed. Like a photograph left too long in the sun.

The door had taken the loosest memory.

And it seemed the door had opinions about what else counted as loose.
