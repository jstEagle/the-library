## 11. Jude's Confession

There are a hundred ways to lie to someone, and I had tried most of them on Coral Dunmore. I'd lied by omission, which was the valley's own favorite recipe. I'd lied by charm, which came easily and cost me sleep. I'd even lied by telling the truth in fragments small enough to pass for pebbles rather than the landslide they belonged to.

Tonight there would be no more pebbles.

I found them at the goat pen at dusk, because of course I did. Coral spent more time with animals than with people, and I was beginning to understand that this wasn't shyness. It was fluency. Watching them talk to those goats—low, easy syllables that weren't quite words—was like watching someone speak their mother tongue while everyone around them pretended it was gibberish.

The goats went quiet when I reached the fence. They always did. Coral didn't turn around.

"I know you're there," they said. "You walk like an apology."

"That's the kindest thing anyone's said about my walking."

"Wasn't kind." But they turned, and there was something in their face that stopped my mouth before it started—the flat, waiting look of someone who has already read the ending. Because they had. We both had. The book in the piano bench had seen to that.

"Coral," I said. "We need to talk."

"We've been talking for two weeks." They latched the gate with a small precise click. "That's what scares me. Every word we've said is sitting in that book, typed out in 1899 by some dead person's steady hand, and I keep wondering what else is in there that we haven't gotten to yet."

I had wondered the same thing, every night, lying awake in Verity's spare room with the ceiling beams close enough to count. The difference was that I knew what else was in there. Not the words—I hadn't dared read ahead past our evening—but the shape of them. The order's archives had taught me the shape.

"Not here," I said. "Walk with me?"

* * *

We walked up the shepherd's path behind the chapel, past the stone marker that said HOLLOWMERE as if a single word could hold a whole stolen country. The sun went down behind hills that weren't on any map outside the valley, because outside the valley there were no maps of here at all. That was the first thing I had to make them understand. Everything else followed from it like water following a crack.

"Do you remember," I said, "when you asked me where I was from, and I said 'up north,' and changed the subject?"

"You're doing it again right now."

"Yes." I stopped walking. "Because everything I'm about to say sounds insane, and I'd like to get through all of it before you decide to walk home."

They crossed their arms. Waiting. Coral waited the way stone waited—patiently, with the implication of enormous weight.

"My name is Jude Marren. I told you that. What I didn't tell you is what I do. There's an order—no real name, they'd laugh if one of us tried to give it one—we call it the Registry, because bureaucracy is the last disguise evil ever wears. It keeps the prophecy-books. All of them. Every soul recorded since the first scribe put ink to vellum in the year whatever-it-was. Births, deaths, marriages, betrayals. The whole shape of every life, written before it's lived."

I watched their face for the flinch. It came, right on schedule, at *betrayals*.

"And your book—" Coral started.

"Is missing from the archive. Has been for ninety years. A book goes missing, the Registry sends someone. Usually someone boring, who files a report and goes home." I looked out at the darkening valley, at the lights coming on in windows that shouldn't exist anywhere. "They sent me because the missing book wasn't just missing. It was *wrong*. It described people the Registry had no record of. Souls that had never been written. And there are only three explanations for souls that aren't in the books, and none of them are good: they're dead, they're hidden, or they were erased so thoroughly that even the ink forgot them."

"Hollowmere."

"Hollowmere." Saying it felt like saying a password to something. "A king named Aldric was dying about a hundred years ago, and he was afraid—not of death, kings can buy their way past that fear, but of being *forgotten*. So he did the opposite of what you'd expect. Instead of building himself a monument, he built himself a tomb the size of a valley, and he filled it with people, and he made the whole thing invisible to fate itself. No prophecies reach in. No records reach out. A blind spot in the shape of home."

Coral was very still. Stillness was their armor; I'd learned that much. When the world got too big, they went quiet and let it break against them.

"And the Registry sent you to find us."

"To find one specific soul." Here it came. The part I'd rehearsed on this same path at midnight, alone, hating myself in several efficient new ways. "The book describes you, Coral. Not just your evenings with a stranger. Your beast-speech. The Registry has a term for people like you—*vocal*, they say, which is the most bloodless word imaginable for what you can do. You talk to animals. More than talk. You *command*."

"I don't command anyone."

"The goats would disagree."

"The goats have opinions."

"So did the wolf." I hadn't meant to say that yet. It fell out anyway, the way true things do when you've been carrying them too long. "Two years ago, in the northern ranges, a Registry team cornered a vocal—a girl of nineteen who could call beasts out of the trees. She called a wolf pack down on the agents sent to take her. Eleven men died. They burned her after, and wrote it up as a weather event."

Coral's hand had gone to their throat. "That's not— I talk to *goats*. I ask them to come in from the rain. I've never—"

"You've never needed to. Yet." I made myself hold their eyes. "Here's the thing about the door, Coral. Aldric's spell didn't just hide the valley. It locked it. And the lock isn't a mechanism—it's a *guardian*. Old magic, older than the king, the kind of thing that answers to voice. Beast-speech and door-speech are cousins. Maybe siblings. A vocal doesn't just open doors. A vocal can command the things that guard them."

The color had gone out of their face entirely now, and the first stars were coming up over Hollowmere like witnesses.

"So the Registry doesn't want the book back."

"The Registry couldn't care less about the book." I laughed, and it came out wrong, hollow as a struck bell with a crack in it. "The book was the excuse. The mission—the actual mission, the one I was given in a room with no windows—is verification and extraction. Confirm the vocal exists. Assess the guardian. And bring the vocal out through the door, into the hands of the people who have been hunting your gift for two hundred years."

Silence. Even the goats, far below, had gone quiet, and I thought maybe the whole valley was listening, holding its breath around its one secret.

Coral spoke finally, and their voice was almost steady, which was worse than screaming. "So this whole time. The date. Aunt Verity arranging it, the awkward dinner, the walk by the mill—"

"The date was real." I stepped closer and they stepped back, and I deserved that, I deserved every inch of it. "Verity arranged it because she's been trying to marry you off to anyone with a pulse for years, and I showed up on her doorstep looking respectable. But I chose to stay. After the first night, I could have filed my report. *Vocal confirmed. Guardian dormant. Requesting extraction team.* Two sentences and I'd be home by winter. Instead I kept taking you to dinners and asking you questions I already knew the answers to, because—" My voice did something humiliating. I pushed through it. "Because somewhere between the goat pen and the choir practice I stopped being your handler and started being—"

"Don't." Coral's voice cracked clean through the middle of the word. "Don't you dare say it while telling me you were sent to deliver me to butchers."

"I'm not delivering you anywhere. That's the confession. That's the whole ugly architecture of it. Yes, I was sent to use you. Yes, everything I am is built for it—trained, filed, obedient, a paper man in a paper coat. And yes, I still haven't reported in. Three weeks overdue. Which means the Registry knows I've gone soft or gone native or gone dead, and either way, they'll send someone else. Someone without my inconvenient conscience." I dragged a hand through my hair. "I came up this path tonight to tell you the truth and offer you a choice, and I'm going to finish offering it even if you never forgive me, because you deserve to choose with your eyes open. That's the one thing they never gave you. Nobody in this valley got to *choose*."

"Choose what?"

"Whether to leave." The words came out quiet, and once they were out, they couldn't be taken back, and I found I didn't want to take them back. "The door can be opened. You're the key—I believe that now, I believed it the moment I heard you talk to that crow like it was an old friend with a grudge. But opening it means walking out into a country where the Registry is waiting, and I don't know how to protect you from them. I genuinely do not know. I've been trying to build a plan for three weeks and every version ends with you in a cell or me in a grave or both. So no, I'm not handing you over. I'm standing here telling you exactly what I am and exactly what's out there, and asking you to trust me anyway, which may be the most selfish thing anyone's ever asked of you."

Coral looked at me for a long time. The dusk had gone full dark now, and the valley lights glittered below like a jewelry box someone had buried alive.

"You could have just left," they said at last. "Filed your report. Gone home."

"Yes."

"Why didn't you?"

And there it was—the question I'd been avoiding since the night I caught myself memorizing the way they laughed, hoarding it against the day the Registry took it from me. Every honest answer sounded like a lie, because I'd earned that suspicion honestly.

"Because the Registry writes everyone down," I said. "Every soul, every fate, every betrayal, inked before it happens. Do you know what that does to a person? Growing up inside your own transcript? I read my own page when I was sixteen. It listed everything. Who I'd marry. How I'd die. I've spent my whole life being a quotation." I stepped forward, slowly, giving them every chance to step away. They didn't. "And then I walked into this valley where the books can't see, and met someone the ink missed, and for the first time in my life I was in a room with a person whose next word I didn't already know. You are the only unwritten thing I have ever loved, Coral Dunmore. I was sent to file you. Instead I'm asking to be unwritten with you."

Their breath went ragged. For a long moment neither of us moved, and the guardian beneath the chapel seemed to shift in its sleep, a groan of stone so deep I felt it in my teeth.

Then Coral said, in a voice like a door deciding:

"There's something under the chapel. I heard it tonight, the first time, when you said *door*. It answered you."

"It answered *you*," I corrected softly. "I'm just standing next to the key."

Below us, in the town, the church bell began to ring—not the hour, not a service. Nine strokes, ten, eleven, and it did not stop, and every light in Hollowmere began, one by one, to go out.
