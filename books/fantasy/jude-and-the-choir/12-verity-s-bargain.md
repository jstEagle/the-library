## 12. Verity's Bargain

I found the note tucked under my door at dawn, written in my aunt's spidery hand on the back of a seed catalog: *Gone to settle a debt. Don't follow me. V.*

Which, of course, is the single most effective way in the history of aunts to make someone follow you.

The goats were fed, mostly. I'd left Bramble's gate unlatched as an apology for the rushed milking, and he'd accepted it with his usual grace by headbutting the water trough until it tipped over. I didn't stop to fix it. I took the prophecy book from beneath the floorboard—Jude had insisted I keep it after that night in the chapel, said it was safer with me than with him—and I followed the one road out of Hollowmere that never actually went anywhere.

Everyone knows the Millward Road. It runs west past the tannery and the old well, climbs through birch scrub, and ends—there's no other word for it—*ends*, like a sentence somebody lost interest in finishing. Children dare each other to stand where the grass stops growing. Adults don't talk about it at all. The road just stops, and beyond it there's a hillside that looks exactly like the hillside behind you, and if you walk long enough toward it you find yourself coming home without ever having turned around.

That's where I found my aunt. Standing at the end of the world in her good coat, the one she wore to funerals and harvest festivals and apparently to whatever this was.

"I told you not to follow me," she said, without turning around. She has eyes in the back of her head. I've suspected since I was six.

"You told me not to follow you," I said. "You didn't say what would happen if I did. There's a difference between an instruction and a threat, Aunt Verity, and you've always been too precise a woman to confuse them."

Something moved in her jaw. Almost a smile. Almost grief. With her they live in the same house and share a wardrobe.

"Go home, Coral."

"The guardian," I said. "You're going to talk to it."

Now she turned. And I saw that she wasn't surprised—which frightened me more than anything else could have, because Verity Dunmore being unsurprised meant she had already accounted for me, already folded me into whatever plan she'd built, the way she folds extra plates into a table setting when company might come.

"So you know," she said quietly.

"I've been practicing." I held up my hands like a failed magician revealing empty palms. "Talking to stone. It works. It costs. You knew that too, didn't you? All those years of telling me to hush when I spoke to the animals. It was never about the neighbors thinking me odd."

"No," she said. "It was never about that."

* * *

Here is what I understood, standing there in the thin morning light with the dead road stretching away behind us: my aunt had been managing a catastrophe for longer than I'd been alive, and she'd done it with casseroles and curfews and a thousand small lies, and she had done it alone.

The guardian came when she called it.

I don't know how else to describe it. One moment the hillside was a hillside; the next, the hillside was paying attention. The stones near the end of the road shifted—not moving, exactly, but arranging themselves, the way a dog arranges itself when its name is spoken. A shape assembled out of granite and shadow and the particular gray of an overcast sky, taller than the birches, featureless except for a slow seam of pale light where a face might have been.

My skin crawled. My hindbrain, the part that still believed Hollowmere was safe, screamed at me to run home and bake something.

Verity didn't flinch. She walked right up to it, close enough that I could have counted her steps, and stood with her hands clasped in front of her like a woman waiting for a bus.

"I've come to make a bargain," she said to it.

The seam of light brightened. Not words—I felt pressure instead, a weight against the inside of my skull, like the sensation of remembering something painful. And then, faintly, a voice that was less sound than geology:

***THE DEBT IS PAID.***

"Not all of it." Her voice stayed level. I marveled at it. I would have been weeping into the moss. "You know it isn't. You've been patient, and I'm grateful, but the girl is asking questions now, and there's a man here who shouldn't be, and the valley is waking up whether any of us want it or not. So I'm offering. Take me instead."

***YOU HAVE NOTHING LEFT TO GIVE.***

"That's a cruel way to speak to a lady."

***IT IS AN ACCOUNTING, NOT A COURTESY.***

And that's when I stepped forward, because I am my aunt's niece down to the bone, and neither of us knows how to leave well enough alone.

"What did she pay?" I asked the guardian. "Thirty years ago. What did she give?"

The light-seam turned toward me. I felt it the way you feel a spotlight, though it had no eyes. The pressure in my skull deepened, and I understood suddenly why beast-speech was treason outside the valley—because things like this *answered*, and answering things are dangerous, and dangerous things attract worse things, and so on down the chain of trouble until you arrive at kings and dying spells and whole countries erased from the record books.

***SHE OFFERED A MEMORY OF HER HUSBAND,*** it said. ***AND WHEN THAT PROVED INSUFFICIENT TO OPEN THE DOOR, SHE OFFERED THE MAN HIMSELF.***

The hillside went very quiet. Even the wind seemed to hold its breath.

"That's not—" I started.

"It is," Verity said. Her voice cracked on the second word, the first crack I'd ever heard in thirty years of unshakeable Verity Dunmore. She kept facing the guardian, but she was talking to me now. "It is exactly that. So before you go feeling sorry for yourself, niece, before you sit up nights grieving your mother's face or whatever little coin you've already spent—you should know what I paid to keep you from ever standing at this road's end."

* * *

She told me right there. In front of the thing made of stone. I think she needed the audience, or maybe she needed the witness to be something older than human, something that had watched it happen and could confirm she wasn't lying to herself. People do that. They need their worst stories corroborated.

His name was Aldous. My uncle, technically—the man whose photograph sat on the mantel in a frame Verity dusted weekly but never looked at directly. I'd grown up believing he'd died of fever before I was born. Everyone in Hollowmere believed something similar about everyone, because that was the local industry: plausible deaths, tidy explanations, a cemetery full of stones marking graves nobody had actually dug.

"He found the book first," Verity said. "Not me. 1963. He was repairing the chapel roof and found the loose slate, and beneath it, this chamber, this shelf, this—" she gestured vaguely at everything, at the valley, at the lie of it all. "Aldous was a curious man. Curious men don't do well in places built on forgetting. He read the whole thing in a fortnight. And then he came to me, white as whey, and said, 'Vera, love, none of this happened. None of us happened. We're a king's dying thought.'"

The guardian stood silent as only stone can be silent.

"We tried to leave together. We got as far as this road." She laughed, and it was the ugliest sound I've ever heard her make. "We didn't even know it was a road's end. We just walked west, the way the book said the door once opened, and we walked, and we walked, and the birches kept repeating themselves, and by evening we were passing our own footprints going the other direction. Do you understand? Our own footprints. Coming toward us."

"But the guardian—" I said. "Someone spoke to it. Someone opened the price."

"Aldous did. He'd taught himself, from the marginalia, from the lists of names. He was cleverer than me about such things. He put his hand on the stone and asked it to open, and it asked its price, and he gave it the memory of our wedding day." Her hands had gone white where they clasped each other. "And the door didn't open. Because one memory wasn't enough. The covenant is old, Coral, and it's *hungry*, and it takes what it takes and gives nothing on credit. So Aldous—" 

She stopped.

The guardian finished for her, in its voice like settling gravel: ***HE GAVE THE REMAINDER. ALL OF IT. EVERY MEMORY IN WHICH SHE APPEARED. AND THEN HE GAVE HIMSELF AS COLLATERAL AGAINST THE COVENANT'S CONTINUANCE. HE WALKS THE VALLEY STILL. HE DOES NOT KNOW WHY HIS FEET FOLLOW CERTAIN ROOFTOPS. HE DOES NOT KNOW YOUR FACE.***

I looked at my aunt. My aunt looked at the middle distance.

"The tanner," I said stupidly. "Old Aldous who works the tannery. Who tips his hat to everyone and can't remember names."

"He remembers hats," Verity said. "He's very good with hats."

I sat down. I don't remember deciding to; my legs simply resigned. The moss was cold and wet through my skirt and I didn't care. Somewhere behind us a crow made a remark, and even the crow sounded embarrassed.

"You let him stay," I whispered. "You could have—"

"Could I?" She finally turned to face me fully, and her face was terrible—not angry, not sad, but *used*, worn smooth like a step everyone walks over. "Could I what, Coral? Remind him? Every time I tried, in the first years, he'd look at me with those polite blank eyes and apologize for intruding. Once he asked me if we were acquainted, and I said yes, and he asked how, and I said 'we were married,' and he said—"

Her voice went out like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

"He said, 'Then I'm sorry I don't recall it, ma'am. You must have married a better man than the one you got.' And he tipped his hat. And he walked off toward the tannery. And I understood that I hadn't saved him at all. I'd just become a stranger with a claim on him, which is worse than a widow, because widows get to grieve."

* * *

We sat there a while, the two of us, in the shadow of the thing that had eaten her husband. The light-seam dimmed to a patient ember. It was in no hurry. Stone never is.

"So when I was born," I said eventually, "and the beasts started talking to me—"

"I panicked," she said simply. "Aldous had spoken to stone. You spoke to everything—goats, crows, the river once, God help me, you told me the river missed the sea. I knew what the book said about beast-speakers. I knew what they could do. I knew that the moment anyone outside these hills learned what you were, every hunter and order and ambitious fool in the real country would come running, and they wouldn't ask nicely. So I did what I do. I managed it. I told you to hush. I burned pages. I arranged a nice quiet life for you with a nice quiet man someday, and I prayed the covenant would hold another generation, because holding was all I knew how to do anymore."

"And Jude?"

Her mouth twisted. "Jude is the crack in the dam, child. That boy walks through Hollowmere like the rules don't apply, and the animals go quiet around him because they can't decide what he is. I've watched him for three days. He looks at you the way Aldous used to look at me. And that terrifies me more than the hunters do, because I know what it costs when someone loves you enough to open doors."

She got up then, brushed off her skirt, and turned back to the guardian. Straightened her spine. Verity Dunmore, sixty-eight years old, facing down a piece of the machinery of erasure with nothing but her good funeral coat and whatever was left after thirty years of payments.

"Now," she said. "About that bargain. I've had time to think since the last time I stood here, and I've worked out where I went wrong. Last time I offered pieces. Memories. Bits and bobs. This time I'll offer the whole account." She tapped her own chest. "Everything. Every year I've hoarded, every memory of raising her, every Sunday dinner and scraped knee and lullaby. Take the lot, open the door, and let the girl walk out owing nothing."

***THAT IS NOT HOW THE PRICE WORKS,*** the guardian rumbled.

"Then explain it to me," she snapped, with the tone she used on tax assessors. "Because I've been paying installments for three decades and I'd like, just once, to see a complete invoice."

The light-seam flickered. And I swear on every goat I've ever milked, the stone thing hesitated.

***THE PRICE IS MEASURED BY WHAT THE SPEAKER CANNOT BEAR TO LOSE,*** it said at last. ***NOT BY WHAT THEY OFFER. YOU CANNOT CHOOSE YOUR PAYMENT, WOMAN. THE DOOR CHOOSES FOR YOU.***

"Then what will it take for her?"

***ASK HER WHAT SHE CANNOT BEAR TO LOSE.***

And both of them—the ancient granite intelligence and my iron-willed aunt—turned to look at me.

I thought of Jude's laugh, wrong-footed and startled, like a man surprised to find he owned one. I thought of the book in my hands with our evening written out in a dead century's ink. I thought of my mother's face, already gone, a smooth place in my memory where something dear had been quarried away.

I thought: *oh. Oh, no. I know exactly what I cannot bear to lose. I've known since the night we read our own futures aloud.*

"Aunt Verity," I said slowly, "when did you send the note?"

"Dawn. Why?"

"Because Jude read the marginalia last night. The parts about the door beneath the chapel. And this morning he wasn't at the boarding house." I stood up, my legs steadier than they had any right to be. "He's gone ahead. He's gone to open it himself, or die trying, and he doesn't even know the choir sings the valley shut every Thursday night, and Thursday is tonight."

The guardian's ember-light pulsed once, almost like laughter.

***THEN YOU HAD BEST HURRY,*** it said. ***THE DOOR CHOOSES ITS MOMENT. AND SO, APPARENTLY, DOES HE.***
