## 13. The Road That Was Never Built

### Coral

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in Hollowmere at dawn, and I have spent twenty-six years mistaking it for peace. It is not peace. It is held breath. It is the sound of a valley listening to itself to make sure it's still there.

I used to find it soothing. Now it sounds like a choir holding a note.

Jude arrived at my door before the goats did, which was its own small miracle—the goats are punctual in the way of creatures who have never once questioned their purpose—and they were carrying two loaves of bread and the prophecy book wrapped in my aunt's good tablecloth like contraband, which, I suppose, it was.

"You slept," I said, taking the bread because it seemed rude not to.

"Barely." They looked like it, too. There was a bruised quality under their eyes, the look of someone who has spent a night arguing with a book. "Did you?"

"I lay down," I said. "Lying down and sleeping turned out to be different activities."

We stood in my doorway for a moment, neither of us moving, both of us aware that the kitchen table inside had become, overnight, something between a war council and an altar. Three days ago this person was a blind date my aunt engineered. Now they were the only other person alive who knew what I knew, and there is no intimacy quite so sudden as shared heresy.

"Verity's still asleep?" Jude asked.

"Aunt Verity sleeps like a stone with opinions. We have until she wakes."

"And then?"

"Then we show her what we found, and she either helps us or turns us in to a town that sings itself into forgetting." I stepped aside to let them in. "So let's be quick, or clever, or ideally both."

* * *

The prophecy book had been Jude's idea first—spreading it open on my scrubbed pine table, weighting the pages with the salt cellar and the honey jar, treating a century-old book like a map, which was exactly the insult it deserved and exactly the use we needed from it. Because here is the thing about the marginalia: whoever wrote it hadn't just recorded the future. They'd annotated it. In the margins, in a cramped hand that faded and strengthened like breath, someone had left directions.

"Look at this one again," Jude said, tapping a page near the middle where the transcription of some long-dead Tuesday gave way to a scrawl: *the road runs under the singing place. Aldric built his door where the voices were loudest, so the song would keep it shut.*

"The singing place," I said. "That's helpful. Hollowmere has three churches, a schoolhouse, and a barn where Marisol Kettle holds dances when her husband's away."

"It's not any of those."

"How do you know?"

Jude hesitated. This is a thing I've learned about them over these strange days: they hesitate before every truth, like each one costs a coin. "Because the handwriting isn't a stranger's. Look at the ink changes. The early entries are one hand, but the margins—some of the later ones—" They turned the book toward me. "These were added over decades. Someone kept writing in this book for most of a century. Someone who lived here. Who found it, read it, and instead of burning it, started answering it."

I looked at the margin notes properly then. Not directions, exactly. Corrections. Beside a prophecy from 1931 predicting a flood that never happened, someone had written: *did not flood. the valley will not allow harm to itself. it protects the forgetting.* Beside an entry from 1954: *asked the guardian what lies beyond. it said: everything, and it said it sadly.*

And beside an entry dated forty-one years ago—a date that made my stomach drop, because I know what happened around that time, or rather I don't, because there is a hole where something should be—someone had written: *tried the door. lost Thomas. do not try alone.*

I put my hand flat on the page. The paper was cool, which felt wrong; books should warm to you.

"That's Verity's handwriting," I said. My voice came out very level, which is how I sound when I am not level at all. "*Thomas.*"

"Your aunt never mentioned a Thomas?"

"There's no Thomas. There's never been a Thomas." And then, because the valley has taught me to doubt my own memory as a matter of course: "Or there was, and there isn't anymore, and the not-anymore feels exactly like there-never-was."

Jude didn't say anything. They just reached across the table and put their hand over mine on the page, and we sat there like two people warming ourselves at a fire made out of grief.

It was me who broke first. "The singing place. Say it's the chapel. Say it plainly, because if you say it plainly then I have to do something about it, and I've been enjoying doing nothing about it. Paralysis is very restful once you get used to it."

"It's the chapel, Coral."

"Why the chapel? Why would Aldric build his door under the one building where everyone gathers weekly to make noise?"

Jude leaned back, and I watched them assemble the answer, watched the shape of their thought change their whole face. "Because noise is cover. Because a song is a spell with a tune, and a congregation is a chorus of casters who don't know they're casting. You don't hide a door by burying it. You hide it by making everyone who stands above it sing it shut, once a week, forever, gladly." They laughed, but without any humor in it. "My order hunts beast-speakers and door-guardians and all manner of things that go bump in the covenant, and even we didn't know about Hollowmere, because the hiding here isn't a wall. It's a hymn."

Outside, a rooster crowed the hour, and I flinched, which was new. Roosters used to just be roosters. Everything was evidence now. The bread smelled like surveillance. The sunlight through my kitchen window looked staged.

"So we dig up the chapel floor," I said.

"We do nothing of the sort yet. We map first. Every margin note, every reference, cross-checked against the town. If the door's under the chapel, the book will tell us how it opens, and more importantly—" Jude tapped the page where Verity had written *do not try alone*, "—it will tell us what it costs. Because your aunt already paid once, Coral. I'd like to know the price tag before we get to the till."

I looked at them across my grandmother's table, this stranger with the wrong-footed charm and the too-many questions, and I thought: *you knew before you came. The book told me that much. You walked into my valley already knowing my evening word for word.*

And I thought, also, with considerably less sense: *but you're here. You could have verified me and reported back. Instead you're sitting in my kitchen at dawn, plotting treason against a town that would forgive you anything if you'd just sing along.*

"All right," I said. "Conspirators, then. But I have conditions."

"Name them."

"One: we tell Verity before we act. Whatever she did with Thomas, she did it trying to protect someone, and I want her on our side, not against it." Jude opened their mouth and I held up a finger. "Two: no more hesitation before truths. You pay whatever internal toll you're paying, but I get the truth whole. Three—" and here I paused, because condition three had arrived in my mouth uninvited, "—when this is over, whichever way it goes, you tell me what you were before you walked through my gate. All of it. Even the parts that make you the villain of the story."

Jude looked at me for a long moment. The light came through the window and caught the dust motes between us, and even the dust seemed to be waiting.

"Agreed," they said. "All three. Though I warn you, you may prefer me hesitant."

"Everyone does," I said. "It's my worst quality as a conversationalist."

We got to work.

* * *

### Jude

Here is what nobody tells you about conspiracy: it is mostly clerical work.

By midmorning the Dunmore kitchen had become an archive. Coral had produced paper, ink, and a ruler, and had organized the prophecy book's chaos with the brisk competence of someone who has spent years managing goats and aunts—that is to say, someone who understands that large stubborn entities respond best to calm, repeated pressure. I copied margin notes onto one sheet. Coral built a map of the town on another, drawing the streets freehand from memory, and it was watching them do this that I understood something important.

They drew roads that aren't there.

"Wait," I said. "Go back. What's that line, there—the one running behind the tannery toward the north ridge?"

Coral frowned at their own pen. "That's... Millwright's Road. It goes over the ridge to the low country. Takes half a day to walk." They stopped. Their frown deepened. "Except I've never walked it. Nobody walks it. When you get to the ridge the road just—" They made a vague gesture, a gesture of dissolution. "You turn around. Everyone turns around. You don't even notice yourself deciding to."

"But you remember it existing."

"I remember my mother talking about it. Taking it to market, before I was born." Coral set down the pen very carefully, the way you'd set down something that might bite. "Jude. The road is in my head but not in the world. Which means either I'm mad, or the valley deleted the road and forgot to delete the remembering."

"Or it couldn't delete the remembering," I said slowly, "because the road isn't part of the spell. It's part of what the spell cut. Aldric erased the connections—the ways out—but he couldn't reach inside every skull and pull out the knowledge of what used to connect. So the town kept the ghost of the road. Memory without object." I pulled the book toward me and flipped to the earliest entries, 1899, the dying king's testament transcribed in a hand that shook. *I cannot save my kingdom,* Aldric had written, *so I will fold it away, safe from my enemies, until a hand worthy of the guardians comes to unfold it.*

"'A hand worthy of the guardians,'" Coral quoted flatly. "Meaning me. Meaning beast-speech. Meaning the thing I've spent my whole life hiding in the goat pasture is apparently the royal skeleton key."

"Meaning you," I agreed. I did not add: *and meaning that outside this valley, there are people who kill for that key, and I am one of those people, and every honest hour with you is a debt accruing interest.* There would be time for that confession. There had to be. But not yet—not while we were building something together, not while Coral was drawing ghost-roads with ink-stained fingers and looking, for the first time since I'd met them, less afraid than angry.

Anger is fuel. Fear is just weight. I needed Coral fueled.

"Here," I said, laying my copied notes beside their map. "Cross-reference. The margins mention landmarks—'under the singing place,' 'where the millstream bends twice,' 'beneath the third stone of the old step.' If we mark them all..."

We worked. The morning sun crossed the table. Somewhere outside, Hollowmere went about its morning—chickens, cart wheels, Mrs. Pettibone greeting the postman in a voice pitched for the whole street to hear—and every ordinary sound now read to me like a sentry's report. I kept catching myself listening past the sounds to the space underneath them, listening for the hum Coral had described, the note the valley held beneath everything like water under ice.

It was Coral who saw the pattern first. Of course it was.

"Jude." Their voice had gone strange. "Come look at this."

Every marked point on their map—the singing place, the millstream bend, the old step, six more references we'd decoded from the margins—every single one sat within fifty paces of the chapel. Not scattered across town the way you'd expect if Aldric had hidden his door well. Clustered. Concentric. Like ripples around a dropped stone, except the ripples were buildings and the stone was buried.

"They're not hiding places," Coral whispered. "They're anchors. He anchored the door from every direction. The whole town center is a lid." They looked up at me, and their eyes were wet and blazing at once, which is a combination I was rapidly learning to fear and admire in equal measure. "We sit on it, Jude. Every Sunday. We sit on the door to our own prison and we *sing*."

* * *

### Coral

We told Aunt Verity at noon.

Not because we'd planned to—because she caught us. She appeared in the kitchen doorway with her arms crossed and her mouth in a line I recognized from childhood, the expression she wore when I'd tracked mud across clean floors, except now the mud was a century-old prophecy spread across her table and the floors were the foundations of everything.

She looked at the map. She looked at the book. She looked at Jude, then at me, and something moved behind her face like weather.

"The chapel," she said. It wasn't a question.

"You knew," I said.

"I knew the shape of it." She came to the table and put one finger on the cluster of marks, on the lid we'd uncovered, and her finger trembled, just slightly, just enough. "I found this book when I was younger than you. I read it the way you two have been reading it—greedy, at night, by candle. And I got as far as you've gotten, and then I got further, because I had something you don't yet." She looked up. "I had Thomas."

The name again. The hole in the world wearing a man's name.

"Verity," Jude began, gently, and she raised a hand.

"Not yet. Not standing up, with the sun high and the neighbors awake. Some truths need the dark to breathe." She straightened, and pressed her palms flat against the table, and when she spoke again her voice had gone hard and practical, the voice of a woman who has survived something and organized the survival. "But hear me now, both of you. Tonight, after full dark, come to my house. Bring the book. Bring everything you've mapped. And Coral—" her grip found my wrist, quick as a hawk, "—you will hear what the door cost me before you decide what it will cost you. Do you understand? Whatever fire you two think you've found, I have stood closer to it than you can imagine, and I am still standing, and I will tell you how. Tonight."

She released my wrist. She gathered the bread knife and the teapot and did the ordinary things of an ordinary kitchen, and the extraordinary hung in the air between us like smoke.

At the door, on their way out, Jude paused. "Mrs.—Verity. The margin note. *Do not try alone.* Did you write it for yourself, or for whoever came after?"

Aunt Verity smiled, and it was the saddest thing I had seen all week, which was saying something.

"For whoever came after," she said. "It took me thirty years to understand that the warning wasn't *don't*. It was *together*." 

She shut the door behind them, and turned to me, and began, calmly, terribly, to make tea.

"Now," she said, "tell me exactly what my niece's book says happens next."
