## 14. Neighbors With Knives

The trouble started with eggs.

I want to be clear about that, because in the days that followed, people would remember it differently—remember torches and shouting and a mob at the gate—but it began the way most terrible things in Hollowmere begin: quietly, with someone doing something kind.

Marta Pell came by on Tuesday morning with a basket of eggs. She'd been bringing me eggs since I was eleven years old, since the week after my mother's face went out of the world and Aunt Verity burned toast for a month straight because her hands wouldn't stop shaking. Marta's eggs were a fixture. They were geography. You could have navigated to my house blindfolded by the smell of her brown paper bags.

"Thought you might want some," she said, setting them down without coming inside. That was the first wrong thing. In twenty-six years, Marta Pell had never once failed to come inside, complain about my aunt's curtains, drink tea she didn't like, and leave forty minutes later.

"Thank you," I said. "Is everything—"

"You're looking well," she said, in the tone you'd use on a sick person, and she looked past my shoulder into the house like she was counting the rooms. "Jude still staying with you?"

"He's been helping with the goats."

"Mm." She smiled, and the smile didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. It stayed down around her mouth like something drowned. "Well. Give him our regards."

*Our.* As though there were an *our* now. As though Hollowmere had always spoken with one throat.

She left the basket on the step instead of handing it to me. I stood in the doorway and watched her walk down the lane, and I noticed—I noticed this with the cold, clear attention I usually saved for animals in pain—that she didn't turn around once. Marta Pell always turned around. She was constitutionally incapable of leaving a conversation; she had to get the last word or at least the last wave.

The lane took her away from me like water closing over a stone.

* * *

By Wednesday, everyone knew something, the way everyone in Hollowmere knows everything, which is to say instantly and through channels no one will admit to. The valley has no newspaper and no gossip column, but it has Mrs. Aldous's bakery, which functions as both.

I went in for bread because I am a fool who believes in routine, and the whole room did the thing. You know the thing. The pause. Twelve conversations continuing at full volume, and then, when the bell over my head rang, a silence so complete I could hear the oven ticking. Then everyone resumed talking at once, slightly too loud, about nothing—about the weather, about Tam Wicker's new fence, about anything except the fact that every eye in the room had touched me and slid away.

"Coral," said Mrs. Aldous from behind the counter. Her forearms were dusted white to the elbow. She'd taught me to knead dough when I was nine. She used to let me shape the little rolls we called mice, and she'd laugh and say I had a way with creatures, which is what everyone said back then, before I learned to hear what lived underneath the saying. "Loaf for you? On the house."

Nothing in Hollowmere is ever on the house. There is no such thing as generosity here, only exchange, only the long slow ledger of favors that keeps a small place knitted together. A free loaf is a debt. A free loaf is a leash.

"I'll pay," I said.

"No need." She wrapped it herself, slowly, and set it down between us on the counter, and kept her hand on it a moment longer than wrapping required. "Coral. You know we all love you here."

Here it comes, I thought.

"And your aunt. We love Verity. Whatever she's gotten into her head lately—" She stopped. Recalibrated. Smiled with her whole face, which was somehow worse than Marta's half smile. "Well. Old women get notions."

"What notion would that be?"

"Oh, none. None at all." She pushed the loaf across. "You take care now."

I walked home through a town that was watching me. I don't mean that figuratively. Curtains twitched in a rhythm too coordinated for coincidence. Tam Wicker paused in his field and tracked me across it, pitchfork resting in the dirt like a spear he was deciding about. Old Mr. Fenn sat on his bench outside the chapel—he practically lives on that bench—and he watched me pass, and he nodded, one slow nod, like a man confirming a count.

And the animals. God, the animals. The goats wouldn't come near the fence. The crows had gone somewhere else entirely—not silent, not warning me, just *gone*, the way birds go before weather, before fire. Only the guardian under the chapel still spoke to me, low and constant, a sound like a mountain clearing its throat: *soon, soon, soon,* which I couldn't decide was comfort or countdown.

I got home and told Jude, and he listened the way he listens, which is completely, with his whole body turned toward yours, and then he said, "We should move faster."

"We can't. The door needs the right night. The book says—"

"The book says a lot of things." He was pacing my kitchen, and I realized I'd never seen him pace before. Jude was stillness personified; stillness was how he'd been trained. "Coral, an order like mine doesn't send one person and wait politely. If they think I've gone soft—if they think you're close—they'll send someone else. And the town—" He stopped pacing. Looked at me with something raw in his face. "The town isn't going to let you go. You understand that, don't you? Not because they're evil. Because you're the thread. Pull you out and the whole garment unravels, and they can feel it pulling even if they can't name it."

"They love me," I said, and heard how young I sounded.

"Yeah," Jude said gently. "That's the problem."

* * *

Thursday, Aunt Verity didn't come to dinner.

In my entire life, she has missed perhaps four dinners, and each time there was a body involved. I walked up to her house in the dark and found every lamp burning and her sitting at the kitchen table with the prophecy book open in front of her and a look on her face I'd only seen once before—the night I was twelve and asked her what happened to my mother, and she'd said, *the valley keeps what it loves,* and refused to say another word.

"They came to see me today," she said, without looking up. "A delegation. Marta, Mrs. Aldous, Tam Wicker, Mr. Fenn. Sat right where you're sitting, the lot of them, polite as a funeral."

"What did they want?"

"To know what I'd been reading." She finally looked at me, and her eyes were red-rimmed and furious. "They didn't say it plain. They never say anything plain in this valley, that's the curse of the place, everything arrives sideways. They brought a pie."

"A pie."

"A gooseberry pie, Coral. Brought it in with their own hands, set it on my table, and Tam Wicker says, 'Shame, isn't it, how a person can get strange ideas from old books,' and Mrs. Aldous says, 'We'd hate to see anyone leave Hollowmere, we'd hate it so much,' and then they ate a slice each and thanked me for my hospitality and left the rest." She pushed the pie across the table toward me. Two slices gone. "It's still warm. They made it this morning. They wanted me to know they know, and they wanted me to know they're being *nice* about it."

I sat down. The pie smelled wonderful. That's the horror of it; I want you to understand that the pie smelled wonderful, buttery and sharp with fruit, made by hands I've known all my life.

"What do we do?" I asked.

Verity laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as a struck match. "What we've always done. We stay pleasant. We keep our voices down. And we work faster than they do." She closed the book. Underneath it, I saw, she'd been writing—a list, dates and names and times, her handwriting cramped and urgent. "The choir sings tomorrow night. Full rehearsal for the Founding Feast. Every soul in this valley in one building, singing themselves forgotten." Her jaw set. "And beneath them, dear girl, is our door."

* * *

Friday morning, someone killed one of my goats.

I'm going to tell this plainly because I have tried, in the nights since, to make it softer, and it won't soften. I went out at dawn and Nettle was in the far pasture with her throat opened, laid out neat, deliberately, in the center of the grass where I couldn't miss her. No tracks. No blood trail leading away. Just her, arranged, the way you arrange a message.

I knelt in the wet grass and put my hands on her and she was cold, hours cold, and the other goats stood at the far fence line and would not come to me, and the silence where the crows should have been pressed down on the valley like a lid.

I have spoken to beasts my whole life. I have never once heard a dead one speak, and I'm grateful, because I don't know what I'd have done.

Jude found me there. He didn't touch me right away; he stood at the edge of the pasture like a man at the edge of deep water, and then he crossed it fast and held me while I shook, and over my shoulder he looked at the tree line, at the lane, at the houses with their patient smoke, and his arms around me went very still.

"This wasn't a warning about leaving," he said quietly. "This was a warning about *you*. About what you are."

"The valley doesn't kill things."

"No. People do. The valley just makes people forget they're the kind of people who would." He pulled back and took my face in his hands, and his thumbs were wet from my tears, which embarrassed us both, and neither of us mentioned it. "Listen to me. Tonight, the whole town will be in that chapel singing. It's the best chance we'll ever get and the worst place we could possibly stand. Verity wants to go under during the hymn. I think she's right. I also think that once you speak the guardians, once the door starts moving, every person in that building is going to feel it in their teeth, and they're going to come down those stairs, and they're not going to look like neighbors anymore."

"They'll look like what?"

He hesitated. Jude, who had confessed to me the order that hunted beast-speakers across the real country, who had handed me his treachery like a bouquet, hesitated.

"Like the people who killed your goat," he said finally. "Loving you the whole time."

* * *

We spent Friday afternoon preparing, and the strangest part of it was how ordinary the town remained around our preparations, the way a lake stays smooth over a drowning. Verity baked bread—for real, for provisions, wrapping loaves in cloth while I memorized the guardian's true name from the book's margins. Jude sharpened a knife I didn't ask about and cleaned a lantern I'd never seen before, and I chose not to wonder where a man on a blind date keeps a knife.

At four o'clock, Marta Pell came back.

She didn't bring eggs this time. She stood at my gate with her hands folded, and behind her, casually, at intervals along the lane, stood other people. Tam Wicker. The Aldous boy, grown now, broad as a door. The Widow Crane. Not a crowd. A *spacing.*

"Coral," Marta said. "We wondered if you and your young man would be coming to choir tonight. Founding Feast rehearsal. Everyone's expected."

"Everyone's expected," I repeated.

"It matters, dear. It matters more than you know." Her voice was gentle, gentle as the pie, gentle as the free loaf, and her eyes went past me to the window where Jude stood, and something moved behind her face—grief, actual grief, the grief of a woman watching a child walk into floodwater. "The song holds us together. When someone's missing, we all feel the hole. You've felt it, haven't you? Lately? Like something's coming loose?"

I had felt it. God help me, I had felt it—a fraying at the edges of things, a sense that the valley's hem was unraveling, and I'd thought it was the spell weakening near the door. But standing there, I understood it was simpler and worse. The valley was fraying because I was fraying it. My wanting had been a wound all along, and they could all feel it bleeding, and they had come to press a hand against it.

"We'll be there," I said.

Marta's shoulders dropped a fraction, relief or disappointment, impossible to say. "Good girl," she said, and patted my hand through the gate bars, and her fingers were cold as the pasture grass at dawn.

When she'd gone, Jude came out and stood beside me, watching the lane empty of its casual sentries.

"You realize," he said, "that if we walk into that chapel tonight, we're walking into the mouth."

"I know."

"And if we run instead, they'll know for certain, and they'll come for us in the dark, and there are a hundred of them and two of us."

"I know that too."

"So why are you smiling?"

Because, I wanted to tell him, because the fear had finally burned off everything vague, and what was left underneath was clean and simple and mine: a dead goat, a warm pie, a woman's cold fingers, a lifetime of being loved exactly as long as I stayed inside the lines. Because I had spent twenty-six years mistaking a beautiful cage for the whole world, and today the cage had shown its teeth, and there is a horrible freedom in that.

"Because they've shown us the shape of it," I said. "Tonight, when the hymn starts, Verity goes to the front and prays—out loud, longer than usual. That's our signal. We slip down behind the vestry to the cellar stairs. You hold the lantern. I talk to the stones."

"And if the town comes down before we're through?"

I looked at the lane, at the chapel rising gray and patient above the rooftops, at the bench where old Mr. Fenn was sitting even now, keeping his count.

"Then they'll learn," I said, "what beast-speech is actually for."

Above the valley, the first star came out, and beneath the chapel, faint as a heartbeat, the guardian said *soon.*
