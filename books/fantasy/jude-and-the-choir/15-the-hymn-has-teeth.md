## 15. The Hymn Has Teeth

I want it on the record—whichever record survives this, prophecy or police blotter—that I did not get trapped in the chapel through stupidity. I got trapped through a very specific and well-documented flaw of mine, which is that when Coral Dunmore says *wait here*, I wait here. It's a character defect. My superiors would call it insubordination waiting to happen. Coral calls it being trainable. Both are correct.

The plan had been simple, in the way plans are simple right up until they aren't. Tomorrow night we open the door beneath the chapel. Tonight, Coral needed an hour alone with Aunt Verity—the kitchen-table kind of hour, the kind where old women decide whether to hand over the last piece of the truth—and so I was to occupy myself elsewhere. *Wait by the chapel,* she'd said, kissing my cheek like it was nothing, like her mouth hadn't gone pale doing it. *Don't go inside. The choir rehearses on Thursdays.*

Naturally I went inside. Not far inside. Just into the vestibule, just to look at the door at the back of the nave, the one that didn't match the others—newer hinges, older wood, the grain running wrong, like the planks remembered being something else before they were a door. The marginalia in the prophecy book had pointed here three times. Beneath the singing, it said. Beneath the hymn no one taught them.

And then the doors at the front opened, and the choir came in, and I understood with the particular clarity of a man watching his own funeral procession that I had made a tactical error.

* * *

There were maybe thirty of them. Mr. Aldous from the bakery with flour still ghosting his forearms. The twins who ran the ferry—both ferries, both directions, which should have been my first clue about Hollowmere's relationship with geography. Old Mrs. Pell, whose hands shook so badly she couldn't thread a needle but who held her hymnal perfectly steady. They filed into the pews without speaking to one another, without looking at one another, arranging themselves the way water arranges itself in a glass. No chatter. Choirs chat. Every choir in every country I've ever infiltrated chats, complains about the organist, gossips about the soprano section. This one moved like a single organism putting on the costume of thirty people.

I pressed myself into the shadow behind the vestry curtain, which is exactly as effective as hiding places in stories always are, which is to say not at all if anyone actually looks.

Mr. Tallow mounted the little podium, a thin man with a face like folded paper. He didn't raise his hands for attention. He simply opened his mouth, and the choir opened theirs, and the hymn began.

I know hymns. Part of my training—part of the Order's whole grim curriculum—is liturgy, because faith is where power pools, and beast-speakers historically hide in congregations of the old religions, the ones that kept the animal names. I can sing the Vesperal in four dialects. I have opinions about verse structure. So believe me when I say: this was not a hymn anyone had written. Or rather, someone had written it, once, and then the song had eaten the writer and kept going.

The words were plain enough on the surface. *Keep us, keep us, fold us close / under hill and under rose.* Lullaby stuff. Valley stuff. But underneath the plainness there was a second text, the way you can see two faces in an inkblot if you let your eyes go soft. And because I'd spent three weeks reading Aldric's spellwork in the margins of a book that predates every building in this valley, I could read the inkblot.

It wasn't a prayer. It was maintenance.

*Let no road remember our feet,* they sang, sweet as cream. *Let no ledger hold our name. Let the maps stay blank where we sleep, and the books stay blind to our flame.* Each line was an instruction, addressed not to any god but to the valley itself—to the covenant, the great sleeping machinery King Aldric had bolted together with his dying breath. The choir wasn't singing to feel holy. The choir was a wind-up mechanism, and Thursday evenings were when somebody—or something—turned the key.

And the key, I realized with a cold spreading feeling in my chest, required singers. Required believers. Required people who didn't know what they were saying.

That's when the hymn turned its attention to me.

* * *

I don't know how else to describe it. A song shouldn't be able to notice things. But halfway through the second verse, the melody bent—not the notes themselves, those stayed obediently on the page, but the meaning underneath them shifted, the way a river bends around a stone. Around me. I felt it find the edges of my mind the way you feel a draft find the gap under a door.

*Let no stranger carry his purpose home,* sang Mrs. Pell, in a voice like a bell wrapped in wool. *Let his orders dissolve like foam.*

My orders.

Now, I should explain what my orders were, while I still have them, in case this chapter ends badly. They were written on paper I burned three weeks ago, but burning paper doesn't burn memory, though I suspect this valley would disagree. They read, in the dry officialese of the Order of the Unwritten: *Subject identified as beast-speaker, class three or higher. Confirm capacity to command threshold guardians. If confirmed, extract subject to the nearest Order waypoint for assessment. Subject is not to be harmed. Subject's cooperation is not required.*

That last sentence is the Order in miniature. Fourteen years I've served them, hunting the last beast-speakers out of the world's hiding places, and I used to tell myself the work was mercy—better the Order finds them than the kings' hunters, better a gilded cage than a shallow grave. Then I met Coral feeding goats in a valley that doesn't exist, calling each of them by names older than the Order itself, and I stopped telling myself anything. You can't lie fluently in a language you've only just learned to speak.

Anyway. Those were my orders. And the hymn wanted them.

I felt it working like fingers working a knot. Not painful—worse than painful, *pleasant*, which is how you know a thing means to take something from you. It started with the small details. The color of the wax seal on my orders—I reached for the memory and found the seal grey, then blank, then gone. The name of the officer who briefed me—Wren? Wray? A face made of weather. The city where I'd received them—I knew there had been a city. There was definitely a city. It had streets. Probably.

Panic does strange things to memory. For one horrible moment I couldn't tell which gaps were the hymn's making and which were just fourteen years of hard living. That's the genius of it, I thought, huddled behind the curtain with my heart going like a drum corps. It doesn't steal cleanly. It steals messily, so that afterward you can't even mourn properly—you can't prove what was taken. The perfect crime, committed weekly, by thirty people who think they're praising the light.

On the podium, Mr. Tallow's face had begun to run slightly at the edges. Or perhaps my eyes were. The third verse began.

*What he came for, let him lose. What he loves, let him refuse to remember loving. Let him stand at morning like a man born new, with no debt upon his hand.*

No. Absolutely not. There are exactly two things in this world I am unwilling to forget, and one of them is why I'm here, and the other is standing in Verity's kitchen right now learning the truth about a husband lost to a stone guardian, and I will be damned by every god including the ones in the margins before I let a lullaby take either.

But wanting isn't a defense. Wanting is exactly what the song eats. I needed a weapon.

I ran through my options with the part of my mind that wasn't busy hemorrhaging cities. Counter-spell: I knew maybe six, all requiring components currently located nowhere near my person. Physical escape: the chapel doors had closed themselves after the choir entered, quietly and completely, the way doors do when a building has opinions. Noise, violence, breaking a window: against thirty witnesses in a town that had already started looking at me with the polite flat eyes of neighbors turning watchful.

Then I remembered what Coral told me, weeks ago, laughing, in the goat field, in the first hour of a blind date neither of us had chosen: *Everything here answers to speech. Even the forgetting. Especially the forgetting. Words built it, so words can argue with it.*

Aldric's covenant was a spell. Spells are sentences. And every sentence, however old, however mighty, can be interrupted.

You don't fight a song with silence. Silence is just a rest between its bars. You fight a song with another song—with a word that won't fit its meter, a note outside its key, a stone in the river that makes the whole current split.

I picked my stone carefully. It had to be true. It had to be load-bearing. It had to be a word that meant more to me than the entire weight of the hymn's forgetting.

* * *

"Coral," I said.

Not loudly. Whispered into the dark behind the vestry curtain, half-swallowed by felt and dust. But the effect was immediate and disproportionate, like dropping a lit match into a hay barn. The hymn stuttered. One note—a whole beat of silence from thirty throats—and in that silence I felt the stolen things shift, like furniture sliding back across a floor. The wax seal flickered red again. Wren. His name was Wren. He had a scar through his left eyebrow and he'd shaken my hand and said, *bring her in gentle, Voss, they spook.*

"Coral Dunmore." Louder now, and the choir faltered worse; Mrs. Pell's hymnal dipped, and I saw her face for the first time as a *face*, confused, blinking, a woman waking mid-sentence. "Coral, who talks to goats. Coral, who called a crow a liar to its face. Coral, who—" and here the memories came flooding back not because I summoned them but because naming their source defended them, the way a man's house is defended by the man standing in the doorway. "Coral, who looked at me across a table of terrible stew and asked me what the roads outside Hollowmere were called, and I lied, and she knew I lied, and she laughed anyway."

The hymn gathered itself. I felt it regroup the way weather regroups—Mr. Tallow's arms rising, the melody climbing into a fourth verse I was certain hadn't been on any page, a verse with teeth fully bared now:

*Say his name and lose it, then. Say her name and lose the man. Every word he spends in love becomes a coin the dark can plan—*

Oh, that was nasty. That was genuinely nasty. It was offering me a trade dressed as a threat: speak her name, and the name costs you. Speak of love, and love gets invoiced. The valley had learned, somewhere in a hundred years of Thursdays, that the surest way to stop a man fighting is to make his weapons cost him.

For a moment—one long, cowardly moment—I considered shutting up. Sitting down in the dark and letting the song wash through me until I woke tomorrow a pleasant stranger with no orders and no mission and no guilt, a tourist in a pretty valley, free. There's a version of that life. I could learn the ferry schedule. I could take up bread.

But I thought about Coral rehearsing, alone, in the dark, letting go of me. Chapter sixteen, if the prophecy book has its way. She'd decided already to pay the price the door demanded, and she hadn't told me because she knew I'd try to stop her, and she was right, I'd have tied her to a chair. She was walking toward losing me on purpose, with her eyes open, to buy everyone in this valley—including these thirty singers who were currently trying to lobotomize me—a way out.

And she thought I might not come out the other side of her remembering. Fine. But I'll be damned if I walk into that forgetting unarmed. If she's going to meet a stranger on the far side of that door, the stranger will at least arrive carrying everything, holding all of it, so that when she's ready—if she's ever ready—someone can hand her back to herself.

"Coral Dunmore," I said, and stood up out of the shadows, and walked down the aisle of the chapel with the hymn screaming its fourth verse at full throat, and every word of her name I spoke knocked a brick out of it. "Coral, who hides her gift like a thief hides silver. Coral, whose aunt burns papers and calls it tidying. Coral, who is going to open a door that's been shut for a hundred years, and I am going to be standing beside her when she does, and your song can eat every memory I own except that one, because that one isn't a memory anymore—it's a *plan*."

The choir broke.

All at once, like a wave collapsing. Thirty faces turned toward me, empty of malice, empty of intention, full of the mild bewildered horror of people surfacing from deep water. Mrs. Pell was crying without knowing why. The twins gripped each other's hands. Mr. Tallow stood at the podium with his mouth still open around a word that had dissolved before it landed, and I watched his expression move through confusion into something older and worse: recognition. He looked at the hymnal in his hands as if he'd never seen it. Maybe he never had. Maybe none of them had ever seen the pages; maybe the book simply appeared in their laps every Thursday the way dreams appear, and vanished the same way.

The chapel doors unlocked themselves with a click like a bone setting.

I walked out into the evening air and stood in the churchyard shaking, doing inventory. City: yes, still there, grey and enormous, I could almost smell it. Orders: intact, word for word, *cooperation not required*. Coral: every piece, from the goat-field laugh to the kiss this evening, catalogued and counted.

Mostly.

One thing was missing. I turned it over and over, hunting the gap, and found only smooth walls where something used to be. Small thing. A detail. Something about the day I arrived—the gate, the first sight of her. I knew I'd arrived; I knew she'd been there. But the image wouldn't come. The hymn had taken a hostage after all, one small one, tucked away in its pocket: the first time I ever saw Coral Dunmore's face.

I was still searching for it when I heard footsteps on the lane—two sets, fast, one of them Verity's uneven tread—and Coral came around the hedge at a dead run, white-faced, with the prophecy book clutched to her chest like a wounded bird.

"Jude," she gasped. "Jude, the book just changed. There's a new page."

She shoved it open to show me, and there, under tonight's date, in ink that was still drying, was written an account of a man standing alone in a chapel reciting a woman's name against a singing congregation.

And beneath it, in fresh marginal handwriting that was not mine, not hers, not Aldric's—

*He kept it. He kept all of it. Now comes the part nobody wrote yet.*
