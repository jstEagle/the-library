## 16. What Coral Chose to Forget

The guardian's words had followed me home like a stray I'd fed once and couldn't shake.

*One more. The last one must be what you love best.*

I'd stood there in the dark under the chapel with stone dust on my hands and the taste of my mother's face already gone—gone, and I hadn't even noticed it leaving, which was the cruelest trick of the whole arrangement. You don't feel a memory leave. There's no tug at the sleeve, no door closing somewhere in your chest. It's just that one morning you reach for a thing you've carried all your life and your hand closes on nothing, and you have to decide whether to grieve or pretend you never reached at all.

I'd grieved for three days. Then I'd gotten practical about it, because that's the Dunmore way: cry into the dishwater, then wring out the cloth and get on with supper.

So now it was Thursday, and Thursday was rehearsal day—not choir rehearsal, God forbid, not after what we'd heard in those hymns—but my own private, deeply stupid rehearsal, conducted alone in the goat pasture, where the only witnesses were goats, who are terrible confidants but excellent at keeping secrets, mostly because they don't care.

"Right," I said to Marigold, my oldest doe, who was regarding me with the flat amber patience of an animal who has heard every one of my problems and been impressed by none of them. "Let's say I have to forget Jude."

Marigold chewed.

"And let's say I want to know what that looks like before it happens, so it doesn't ambush me." I sat down in the wet grass, because standing felt too much like giving a speech. "Because here's the thing nobody tells you about losing something on purpose: you get to watch yourself do it. That's not mercy. That's cruelty with a viewing gallery."

The crow—the same crow, I was nearly sure, the one who'd started this whole catastrophe by announcing the paper man—landed on the fence post and tilted its head at me like it was taking notes.

"Don't look at me like that," I told it. "You're the reason I'm in this mess."

*Caw,* said the crow, which in crow could have meant *you're welcome* or *the door opens at moonrise* or simply *I see a worm.* Beast-speech is a gift of precision undermined entirely by beasts.

But I wasn't going to speak to it properly. Not anymore. Every word I spent was a word off some ledger I couldn't see, and I'd learned to count.

Here is what I knew, gathered from the guardian's riddles and Verity's reluctant fragments and the margins of a book written before either Jude or I was born: the door beneath the chapel needed three keys of speech. The first had cost me my mother's face—a memory so old it had gone soft at the edges anyway, worn smooth like a river stone, so that losing it felt less like amputation than like discovering I'd already been robbed years ago and only just noticed the empty drawer. The second key, spoken two nights ago, had taken the smell of rain on Hollowmere dust. I still remembered that there had been such a smell. I remembered knowing it. But when I tried to summon it now, I got a description instead of a sensation, the way you know a country exists without having stood in it.

That was the pattern, then. Each payment took something and left behind its receipt.

And the third payment—the last one, the one that would swing the door wide enough for two people to walk through and a town's worth of fury to stay shut behind them—had to be what I loved best.

I didn't need a prophecy book to tell me what that was. I'd known since the night at the gate, since the awkward, electric disaster of our first date, since he'd asked about roads that don't exist and I'd lied about them beautifully and he'd seen straight through the lie and liked me better for telling it.

* * *

Jude came by that afternoon the way he always did now, sidling up the lane with his collar up and his eyes doing that constant anxious inventory of windows, as if Hollowmere itself were watching him, which, increasingly, it was.

"You're in the pasture," he said, arriving at the gate. "Talking to goats again."

"I'm consulting with goats. There's a difference. Goats are very wise."

"They're eating a bucket."

"They're meditating on the bucket."

He smiled—that crooked, apologetic smile that had undone me from the start, the smile of a man perpetually braced for rejection and astonished whenever it didn't come—and let himself through the gate, and I did what I'd done a hundred times now: I looked at him and tried to memorize him on purpose.

This is a strange exercise, deliberate memorization. You'd think love would do it automatically, but love is lazy; love assumes forever. So I made lists while he talked about the marginalia he'd found that morning—lists I wrote down later and hid in the lining of my coat, because paper survives where memory doesn't, and if I was going to be robbed I intended to leave notes for the burglar's successor, whoever she turned out to be.

*He holds his cup with both hands like it might bolt.*

*He hums when he thinks no one can hear, always the same three notes, always slightly flat.*

*He says "right" before he lies, every time, and thinks I haven't noticed.*

*When he laughs hard he presses the back of his hand to his mouth, like laughter is something he was taught to contain.*

*He looked at me today the way people look at weather they've been promised.*

I wrote them all down. I was building, coat-lining stitch by stitch, a Jude made of paper, in case the flesh-and-blood version got confiscated.

"Coral?" He'd stopped talking. He was looking at me with that expression—the one where his guilt showed through like bones through thin skin. "You went somewhere."

"Just counting goats."

"There are four goats and you have five fingers."

"Advanced technique."

He laughed, then pressed his hand to his mouth, and I filed that away too, and hated myself a little for how good I'd gotten at filing.

* * *

That night I took the prophecy book out from under my floorboard—I'd reclaimed it from the piano bench weeks ago; leaving it in the chapel felt like leaving your diary in a church—and read our evening again. Our first evening, transcribed in 1899 by some long-dead hand, word for word:

*"You'll ask me if the valley has an inn," Coral will say, "and I'll lie, and you'll know I'm lying, and neither of us will mind."*

We had said exactly that. Word for word. And in the margin, in faded ink that I had come to believe—with mounting vertigo—was my own handwriting, someone had written: *Check the ledger under the east stone. Count what you pay. Leave receipts.*

Past-me had known. Past-me, decades ago somehow, or across whatever broken time Hollowmere ran on, had sat with this book and planned her own robbery and left herself instructions. I found myself oddly furious with her. She'd had all the information and none of the dread. She'd written "leave receipts" like it was a chore list.

But she'd also written, further along, in a hand pressed so hard the pen had torn the page: *THE PRICE IS JUDE. THE PRICE IS ALWAYS WHAT YOU LOVE BEST. PLAN ACCORDINGLY.*

Plan accordingly. As though the heart were a line item.

I closed the book and sat in the dark kitchen and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding. The door needed my dearest memory. My dearest memory was no longer a single moment—it had become, over these weeks of conspiracy and terror and stolen afternoons, an entire architecture built out of him. If the guardian took my mother's face and the rain-smell as installments, the final payment wouldn't be one scene. It would be the whole cathedral.

And here was the question that kept me up past the hour when even Hollowmere's lamps go out, all together, on schedule, like the valley breathing out: if I rehearsed losing him—if I practiced the forgetting until it fit comfortably in my mouth like a prayer—was I protecting myself, or was I practicing a smaller kind of surrender? Was preparation wisdom, or was it just grief arriving early to get a good seat?

Verity would say it was wisdom. Verity, who lost a husband to that door and rebuilt herself around the hole the way a tree grows around a fence wire. Verity, who loved me in the shape of silence for twenty-six years because silence was the only shape she trusted.

But Verity also shouted the truth in the chapel square last week, and held back a mob of neighbors with nothing but her voice and their own buried shame, so maybe Verity's definitions had loosened lately too.

* * *

Friday morning I began the rehearsals in earnest.

Not with Jude present—I couldn't do it with him present, it felt too much like practicing a eulogy at the birthday party. Alone, then, in the pasture, in the fog that Hollowmere lays down every morning like a sheet over furniture in a house nobody visits.

Exercise one: describe him without feeling.

"He's tall-ish. Brown hair, does what it wants at the left temple. Eyes the color of strong tea. Hands careful with animals. Laughs behind his hand." I recited it flat, inventory-style, the way you'd describe a chair. It worked. For about nine seconds I felt nothing but the mild satisfaction of accuracy.

Then he appeared at the gate—he had a way of arriving exactly when I was being monstrous in private, as if the universe kept a schedule—and said, "Are you describing me to the goats?"

"No," I said. "I'm describing a chair."

"It sounds like a handsome chair."

"It has one crooked temple."

He climbed the gate instead of using the latch, because latches were for people with patience, and sat beside me in the wet grass, shoulder almost touching mine, and exercise one collapsed like a card table.

Exercise two, attempted that evening: imagine the morning after.

I lay in bed and made myself build it. Wake up. Feed the goats. Notice a man in the kitchen who is a stranger. Feel—what? Nothing. Polite wariness. The small animal alarm you feel toward any unknown person in your space. And he would be standing there holding his cup with both hands, and I would not know why that detail snagged at me, only that it snagged, like a nail head working loose through wallpaper.

Would he tell me? Of course he would tell me. He'd tell me immediately, honestly, guiltily, saying "right" first, and I'd look at this stranger crying in my aunt's kitchen claiming we'd loved each other, and I'd have to take it on faith. Faith in a stranger's testimony about my own heart. It was obscene. It was like waking up with someone else's name stitched into your coat.

Unless I left receipts.

I got up, lit the lamp, and added to the coat-lining list:

*If you wake up and don't know him: he is not dangerous. He is the opposite of dangerous. He is the man who stayed.*

*You laugh at his jokes before you finish hearing them. Trust the reflex even if you can't source it.*

*Ask him about roads. He'll understand why you're asking.*

I stared at that last line a long time. I didn't remember writing it, which meant past-me had written it, sometime, in some fold of time I couldn't see—which meant past-me had survived this exact decision, or at least imagined surviving it well enough to leave advice.

Or the book was lying to me. Everything else in Hollowmere lied. The hymns lied. The comfortable covenant lied. Even the valley's kindness had teeth. Why should my own handwriting be exempt?

But I sewed the page in anyway.

* * *

Sunday, Jude found me on the hill above the chapel—the chapel where the door slept beneath the floorboards where the choir stood, the chapel whose hymn had instructed generations of us to stay small, stay sweet, stay forgotten. The whole valley spread below us, lamplit and tidy, so pretty it made you sick once you knew what the prettiness was for.

"Soon," he said. Not a question. We'd stopped making questions of things we both already knew.

"Soon."

"The order will have noticed I'm overdue. When the door opens, they may already be—" He stopped himself, jaw working. He'd confessed everything two weeks ago: the order, the hunt, the fact that beast-speakers were hunted precisely because we could command guardians, the fact that walking me out of paradise might mean delivering me into a cage. "I keep thinking I should talk you out of it."

"You've thought about it for two weeks and haven't managed it yet."

"Because it's a terrible argument. 'Stay in the prison, Coral, the outside has worse prisons.'" He shook his head. "It's just—every road out of here goes through people who would use you. And I led them to you."

"You led them to a person," I said, "who turns out to be able to command the things they fear most. Jude. I'm not the lamb in this story. I never was. The valley just dressed me like one."

Below us, faintly, the evening bell rang, and the lights came on all at once, punctual as ever, and I watched my whole world light itself up for the night and felt the strangest tenderness toward it—toward the suffocating, lying, beloved place. That was the trap within the trap: the covenant hadn't just hidden Hollowmere from the world. It had made the hiding feel like warmth. I could walk away from a prison. Walking away from warmth was another matter entirely.

"Coral." Jude's voice had gone careful, the way voices go when they're carrying something breakable. "What's the third payment?"

I'd told him about the mother's face. About the rain. I'd kept the rest vague, the way you keep the diagnosis vague in front of the patient.

"You already know," I said.

"I want to hear you say it isn't—"

"It is." I kept my eyes on the lights below. "It's you. It's all of you, every bit of you, the whole ridiculous archive. That's the price of the door swinging all the way open. The guardian takes what I love best, and apparently my heart has gotten embarrassingly single-minded these past months."

He was silent for a long moment. When I finally looked at him, he had his hand pressed over his mouth—not laughing this time.

"You could choose something else," he said. "Love something else more. The goats. Marigold. I'd raise a statue to Marigold."

"I've considered it. She's unmoved. Also I'm not going to arrange my heart around a vacancy just to make the bill cheaper. That's not saving love, that's downsizing it."

"That's insane logic."

"It's Dunmore logic. We cry into the dishwater and wring out the cloth."

He laughed then, wetly, horribly, hand still at his mouth, and I memorized that too—the sound of him breaking and joking in the same breath—and added it to the list later that night:

*When he cries he still tries to make it funny. Don't let future-you find this annoying. Find it dear. It's the dearest thing about him.*

* * *

Tuesday night I did the final rehearsal, the one I'd been circling for days like a bird over water.

I sat on my bedroom floor with the prophecy book open and I spoke aloud, to the room, to the guardian's listening stones, to past-me in the margins: "I know what it costs. I've counted it. I've measured it in cups held with both hands and three flat notes hummed and a crooked temple, and I'm telling you now, in advance, while I still know his name: I pay it. Open the door. Let us out. Take the receipt."

My voice didn't shake, which surprised me. Grief, it turns out, can be steady when it's given a job.

Then I opened the book to the last clean page, and I wrote—not in code, not in the cramped defensive hand of the coat-lining notes, but large and plain, letters big enough for a stranger's frightened eyes to find:

*His name is Jude. He loves you. He stayed. Read this every morning until it stops being words and starts being true.*

I underlined *stayed* twice.

And that was when the floorboards downstairs creaked in the specific rhythm of someone trying very hard not to be heard, and the lamp guttered though there was no draft, and from the direction of the chapel—half a mile off, through fog and dark—came a sound I had heard only once before, the night I first spoke to the guardian:

Stone, grinding against stone. Waking up.

Except this time, I hadn't called it.
