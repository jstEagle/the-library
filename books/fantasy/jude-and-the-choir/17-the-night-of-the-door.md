## 17. The Night of the Door

### Coral

The chapel basement smelled like candle wax and a hundred years of nobody's business, which in Hollowmere was the same as smelling like every family's business at once. Jude held the lantern low so the light pooled on the floorboards instead of climbing the walls. Smart. Light climbing walls was how you got noticed.

"Say it again," Jude whispered. "The command. I want to hear it one more time before—"

"You want to hear it because you think repetition is comfort." I crouched beside the flagstone where the marginalia said the door slept. The stone was ordinary. It had been ordinary my whole life. That was the trick of this valley: everything that mattered was dressed as furniture. "It isn't. It's just repetition."

"Coral."

"I know what you're doing. You're memorizing me. In case there's less of me after." I didn't look up. Looking up meant seeing his face do the thing it did when he thought about what I'd chosen to give away, and I could not afford that face right now. Not tonight. Tonight I needed to be a key, not a person.

Above us, faint through the floor, the choir was gathering. I could hear Mrs. Pell's cough, Deacon Harrow tuning nothing, the shuffle of hymnals that no one remembered printing. They always rehearsed Thursday nights. Tonight they would sing the forgetting-hymn with particular enthusiasm, because the valley had begun to itch. Neighbors with knives, remember. A town can smell its own ending even when it can't remember its own beginning.

"The guardians answer beast-speech," Jude said. It was almost a catechism between us by now. "You speak, they obey. Every word costs a memory. The door needs—" 

"—three commands. Open, hold, and let-pass. Three memories." I pressed my palm flat against the cold flagstone. "I've already spent mother's face on the practice run. I budgeted for tonight weeks ago. Stop auditing me."

He sat down next to me, shoulder warm against mine, and said nothing, which was the bravest thing anyone had done in that basement all evening.

Here is what I hadn't told him: I'd already decided which two memories went with the remaining commands. The first was easy, or seemed easy—the day Aunt Verity found me talking to the goats when I was six and instead of scolding me she sat down in the mud and asked them their opinions on weather. That memory was a door itself. Losing it felt like losing permission.

The second was Jude. All of him. The wrong-footed stranger at the gate asking after roads that didn't exist. The crows screaming *paper man* while I laughed at them. His face over the prophecy book, guilt and wonder fighting for space. Every single thing, filed under a name I wouldn't recognize.

I had rehearsed letting go of him in the dark for eleven nights. I'd gotten very good at saying his name like it belonged to someone else.

Above us, the choir began to sing.

* * *

### Jude

I have stood before the Archivists of my order and recited the Litany of Names without my voice shaking. I have watched men die for lesser truths than the ones in my satchel. But listening to Coral breathe slowly and deliberately—counting breaths, the way I count pages—while three hundred feet of choir sang the valley's lullaby overhead, my hands would not stay still.

The plan was simple, which meant it was doomed, which meant we'd accounted for doom, which meant maybe we'd live an hour longer than expected. Simple plans: Verity holds the choir. The truth, shouted aloud, breaks the hymn's hold long enough for us to work beneath it. Coral speaks the guardians open. We walk out into a country neither of us has ever truly seen.

And then the order finds the open door, and everything I confessed to Coral in chapter eleven of our disaster becomes relevant again. Betrayal offered honestly. Love offered anyway. I'd given them both, and Coral had taken both, and now we were here, in a cellar, waiting for the price of freedom to be collected from the only currency either of us had left.

The hymn changed.

I felt it before I understood it—a softening behind my eyes, like warm water poured over ink. The words drifting down through the floorboards stopped being words and started being instructions. *Stay. Sleep. You were never promised anything. You came here for no reason. There is no Coral. There never was a Coral.* The song reached into me with the confidence of something that had been rewriting minds since 1899 and had gotten quite good at it.

*Coral,* I said inside my own skull. *Coral Dunmore. Beast-speaker. Goat-herder. Terrible at lying, worse at accepting kindness. Coral.*

The ink stayed put. Barely. My hands found the lantern handle and gripped it like a railing over a long drop.

"It's trying to take you too," Coral said quietly. They'd seen it happen—seen my eyes go somewhere else. "Fight it out loud. Whisper your orders back at it. It hates competition."

So I whispered my orders. The hunt for beast-speakers. King Aldric's dying spell. The door beneath the chapel. And then, because the song kept pressing, I whispered things that weren't orders at all: the way Coral talks to crows like disgruntled colleagues. The exact pitch of their laugh when they're pretending not to be delighted.

The singing above us stopped.

Then started again—but wrong. Off-key, stumbling, voices tripping over each other. Somewhere up there, an old woman was shouting over three hundred years of comfortable lies.

Verity.

* * *

### Coral

You have never heard anything until you have heard your aunt—the woman who raised you on stewed apples and gentle evasions—standing in a choir loft, screaming the truth at a town built to reject it.

I heard her through the floorboards like a hammer through a coffin lid:

*"WE ARE NOT FROM HERE! THERE IS NO 'HERE'! THIS VALLEY IS A GRAVE THAT KING ALDRIC DRESSED UP AS A PARLOR!"*

The hymn faltered. Voices cracked. Someone dropped a hymnal—I heard it hit the boards like a shot—and in the gap, other voices rose, confused, angry, some crying, one woman (Mrs. Pell, I'd swear it) laughing in the unhinged way of someone whose whole life has just been revealed as scenery.

"She's buying us minutes," Jude breathed. "Maybe seconds."

"Then stop narrating and help me move the stone."

We got the flagstone up together—it came away easier than any stone should, as if it had only ever been pretending to be heavy—and beneath it was not dirt. Beneath it was a stairway going down into dark that moved. Not wind-dark. Thinking-dark. The guardian-light, pale as milk, rising toward us like something inhaling.

And there they were: the door-guardians. Shapes like the valley's hills if hills had joints and patience. Eyes like wells. I had spoken to the stone guardian once before, at terrible cost, and it had told me, in its voice like gravel settling, that it remembered every beast-speaker who ever lived, and that it had waited a hundred years for one worth obeying.

I put my hand on the cold edge of the opening. Behind me, Jude made a small sound—not a word. He knew better than to say goodbye. Goodbyes were for people who might be remembered.

"Guardians of the threshold," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved. "By the old covenant of horn and hoof and feather, I command you: OPEN."

The first memory went.

It went the way water goes down a drain—all at once and then spiraling, the mud, the six-year-old knees, Verity sitting down beside me in the goat pen asking a nanny named Bess what she thought about rain. Gone. I felt the shape of the loss but not the loss itself, which is the cruelest engineering imaginable. I knew I had lost something. I couldn't tell you what. I knew I had loved something. I couldn't tell you what.

Below me, the dark groaned. The stairs uncoiled downward, deep, deep, toward a seam of light that was not candlelight and not moonlight—real light, country light, the rude enormous light of a world that did not know our name.

"One more," I whispered.

"Coral—" Jude's voice broke on it. "Coral, look at me. Before. Look at me once, so I know you saw me."

"I see you," I said, and it was true, and it was the last time it would ever be true, and we both knew it, and he kissed me anyway, quickly, like a man signing a document he hated, and then he stepped back and became a stranger-in-waiting.

Overhead, Verity's voice was fraying. *"YOUR HUSBANDS AND WIVES NEVER EXISTED! YOUR CHILDHOODS ARE FURNITURE! I STAYED SO YOU COULD KEEP YOUR LIES AND I AM DONE PAYING FOR THEM—"* Something crashed. Shouting. Boots on stairs—many boots, coming down toward the basement, the town converging like blood to a wound.

"Guardians!" I shouted, louder than fear, louder than the boots. "HOLD THE THRESHOLD!"

The second memory went, and this time I felt it leave—felt something enormous detach from me like a limb, and I heard myself say, in a voice gone suddenly strange and distant, "Whoever that was, they must have loved me very much."

There was no time to grieve. The basement door burst open and the choir came down—Deacon Harrow first, face gray, hymnal clutched like a weapon; Mrs. Pell behind him; half the town, faces I'd known all my life looking at me like I was a fire in a dry field.

"Don't let her speak!" Harrow roared. "Stop her mouth, the song says—stop her mouth—"

Jude stepped between us, arms spread, a paper man making himself a wall. "She's opening the way OUT," he shouted. "All of you—you can come too. Nobody has to stay forgotten!"

And Verity—Verity came down the stairs last, hair loose, sixty years old and furious as scripture, and planted herself between the townsfolk and me.

"Touch my niece," she said, "and I will spend what's left of my voice telling you every truth I swallowed to keep you comfortable, starting with the year 1899, and I promise you none of you will sleep again." She raised both hands like a conductor. "Now SING SOMETHING ELSE."

And God help me, they did—some of them, a handful, voices cracking into a different hymn entirely, one nobody taught them, one that sounded like morning—and the forgetting-song lost its grip, stuttered, unraveled—

I turned to the dark. I turned to the light at the bottom of the world.

"Guardians of the threshold," I said, and my voice was already someone else's, calm and clear and grieving without knowing why. "LET US PASS."

* * *

### Jude

The third memory took her mid-step, and I watched it happen from behind—watched her shoulders drop a fraction, watched her head tilt, watched her pause at the lip of the light like a woman who'd come downstairs and forgotten why.

She didn't look back.

That was the thing. She walked into the real country—vast, loud, unhidden, the sky enormous above her like a held breath finally released—and she did not turn around to find me. Because there was no me to find. The price had been paid in full, and I was the coin.

The guardians closed ranks behind her—not against her, but around the threshold, patient as hills, holding the line against the crowd still shouting in the ruined basement. Verity stood among them, voiceless at last, tears cutting through the dust on her face, and she looked at me and mouthed one word: *Go.*

I went.

I climbed out of the earth into a night bigger than any night Hollowmere had ever permitted, stars stacked on stars, wind carrying smells the valley had never owned—pine, distance, rain on open land. Coral stood ten paces ahead in all that enormity, breathing hard, staring at the horizon like a person reading their first sentence.

They turned around.

They looked directly at me—at the stranger standing at the mouth of the buried chapel, holding a lantern and a century-old book—and their face was polite. Curious. Completely empty of me.

"Hello," Coral said pleasantly. "Did you follow me out? Are you from here?"

The door groaned shut behind me, stone grinding against stone, sealing the valley's scream into silence.

I opened my mouth to answer, and discovered that a man can prepare for every battle except the one where the person he loves asks him who he is.
