## 18. Out

The door did not open so much as *give way*, the way a held breath gives way, the way grief gives way on the third day when your body decides it is tired of carrying itself like a stone.

I had expected light. Everyone expects light at moments like this; it's in all the songs. What came instead was wind — real wind, valley wind with all its soft edges filed off, wind that smelled of pine resin and far-off rain and something burnt and something green and something I had no name for because no one in Hollowmere had ever needed a name for it. It pushed past me into the dark of the under-chapel like it had been waiting twenty-six years for someone to leave the window open.

Behind me, Jude's hand was on my shoulder. I remember that. I want to be very clear that I remember that, because it is one of the last things I will ever remember about him, and I have decided to hold onto it the way you hold water in cupped hands — knowing full well what happens next, doing it anyway.

"Say it," he said. His voice was wrecked from the hymn-fight, from shouting my name against a choir that wanted to eat his reasons for coming. "Coral. Say the words."

"I know the words." My beast-speech sat in my chest like a second heartbeat, the gift I'd buried as a child and dug up again over one impossible autumn. Above us the choir was singing, and Aunt Verity was shouting over them — I could hear her, faint and furious, telling three hundred neighbors the truth they'd paid their whole lives not to know: *You were stolen. You were hidden. You were never safe, only comfortable.* Somewhere in that din was Mrs. Pell's famous walnut cake voice and Mr. Ostler's laugh and every child I'd ever taught to whistle to the goats. All of it dissolving into noise. All of it deciding, right now, whether we were family or prey.

"Coral." Jude again. Closer. "Whatever it costs — whatever you lose — it will be worth it. I promise you that."

And here is the thing I cannot explain without sounding like a fool: some part of me already knew what the cost would be. Not the shape of it. The guardian doesn't itemize its bill in advance; it just holds out its hand. But I knew there would be a price, and I knew Jude would be standing near me when it came due, and I knew — with that terrible animal certainty that has nothing to do with prophecy books — that I should look at him now. Really look. Burn him in while the burning still worked.

So I turned around.

He was pale, singed at the edges, his coat torn where the door-guardian's shadow had raked him. He looked like a man who had run through a storm to reach a house that was already on fire. His eyes were gray-green, sea-glass, and they were fixed on me with an expression I didn't have a word for then and have spent every day since trying to find one for.

"What?" he said, because I was staring.

"Nothing," I said. "Just memorizing."

"Memorizing what?"

But the guardian was waiting, and Verity's voice above us cracked on a word, and there wasn't time. There is never time. That's the great joke of it — the prophecy book gave us a century of warnings and not one extra minute.

I put my hand against the stone of the threshold, felt the old king's spell humming under it like a hive, and spoke.

I won't write the words here. Some of them were mine and some of them belonged to beasts older than the valley, and all of them together are the most treasonous sentence ever spoken in Hollowmere. But the stone heard me. The stone *obeyed* me. And the moment it did, something reached into me — gently, horribly gently, the way Verity used to pull splinters out when I was small, all apology and efficiency — and took.

I felt it go. I felt the exact weight and shape of what it took, the way you feel a tooth missing with your tongue before the pain even starts. Something dear. The dearest thing. The guardian hadn't lied about that; the price was calibrated like everything else in that cursed, cozy valley, measured to the last drop.

Then the door was open, and the world was on the other side of it, and I walked through.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being erased your whole life: the un-erasing is violent.

The real country hit me like cold water hits a sleeper. First the size of it — sky that didn't stop at the ridge line but kept going, going, insolent and enormous, with clouds moving in it like ships. Then the sound: birds I'd never catalogued, insects droning in layers, a river somewhere below talking to itself in a language with more consonants than Hollowmere's tame little creek ever used. And underneath it all, so constant I almost missed it, a hum — the hum of roads somewhere distant, of engines, of people whose names were written in books that existed, whose fates were tracked and tallied and known.

We had been invisible for a hundred and twenty-seven years. I stood on the hillside outside my own front door — except it wasn't my front door, it was just a door, set into a hillside that showed no seam from the outside, nothing, a blank slope of grass and gorse — and I wept at the sheer *volume* of existing.

It was the goats that brought me back. Of course it was. A dozen of them had followed the exodus out — the beasts always liked me best, and the guardians let them pass, because beasts were never fooled by the old king's spell in the first place. They crowded around me, bleating their plain, honest complaints, and their voices were the same as they'd always been, and I answered them without thinking, in the old tongue, the treasonous one:

*Small ones. Grass here. Water there. Be easy.*

They settled. And I stood there among them, feeling lighter than I had in years, feeling *free*, thinking: it worked. We're out. Whatever the price was, I paid it, and I'm still me, I'm still Coral Dunmore, I remember the chapel and the choir and the kitchen with the blue crockery and—

I turned to tell Jude.

* * *

There was a man standing ten feet away, holding a battered book against his chest like a shield.

He was tall-ish, rumpled, singed, mud to the knees. He had the look of someone who had recently been through a war and lost track of which side he'd been on. He was watching me with an expression I want to describe carefully, because I've had weeks now to study it and I still can't improve on the first version: he looked at me the way people look at a house they grew up in, long after it's been sold to strangers.

"Hello," I said. Wary. Polite. The voice you use for strangers on the road.

His face did something complicated. It started hopeful and ended somewhere else entirely, and the distance between those two points was maybe a quarter of a second, and I watched him travel it.

"You don't know me," he said.

"Should I?"

"No." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry and cracked. "No, you shouldn't. You absolutely shouldn't. That's — yes. That's how it works."

I glanced behind him. The hillside was empty except for gorse and one indignant crow — our crow, actually, the one who'd tried to warn me about "the paper man," riding the wind with an air of profound vindication. No aunt. No choir. The door in the hillside had already forgotten it was a door; the grass had grown over the seam, or had perhaps always grown over the seam, and only the goats remembered, milling around my legs and complaining.

"Where's Jude?" I asked. "My aunt? Verity? Did they come through?"

Something crossed the stranger's face then that I genuinely could not read. Grief, maybe. Or guilt wearing grief's coat.

"There was a choice made," he said carefully, "about who came out and who stayed. Your aunt stayed. By her own decision. She's — she chose to keep the threshold. Someone has to hold it from the inside."

"And Jude?"

The stranger hugged the book tighter. "Jude came through."

"Well, where—" I gestured at the empty, enormous, indifferent landscape. "Where is he?"

The silence went on long enough that I counted my own heartbeats. Four of them. Five. The crow landed on a gorse bush and said, conversationally, in the old tongue: *Tell him. Coward.*

"Look at me," the stranger said quietly. "Really look."

I looked. Sea-glass eyes. Mud to the knees. A coat torn at the shoulder in a long rake-mark, as if something with claws —

— as if something with claws had caught him, sometime, somewhere, during some fight in some dark place under a chapel, and why did my chest go tight at that, why did the image arrive pre-loaded with dread and tenderness, why did I know — *know*, bone-deep, goat-simple — that I had seen this exact man singed and muddy once before, in a doorway, saying *whatever it costs*—

"I don't understand," I whispered.

"The price," he said. "For opening the door. The guardian takes the dearest memory. Yours was—" His voice failed and restarted. "Yours was me."

* * *

I want to record my reaction honestly, because future-me deserves the truth and not a flattering version.

I did not cry. I did not scream. I stood very still among the goats, in the vast loud world, and I thought, with perfect clarity: *that explains the hole.*

Because that's what it was. Not absence exactly — you can't miss what you can't remember — but a hole with a shape to it. All morning I'd been aware of it without naming it: the way my hand kept wanting to reach sideways for someone who wasn't there; the way certain words (*doorway*, *memorizing*, *worth it*) arrived in my head with emotional freight and no cargo; the way the sea-glass color of this stranger's eyes made my ribs ache like an old injury in wet weather.

"I loved you," I said. Not a question. The hole confirmed it. Holes always do.

"Yes," he said.

"How much?"

"That depends which of us you ask." He smiled, wretchedly. "I'm told you were quite dramatic about it. There are marginalia."

"The book." I held out my hand. "Give me the book."

He hesitated — and I understood the hesitation even then, even hollow: inside that book was a hundred-year-old prophecy containing every word of an evening two strangers had spent falling in love, plus, presumably, a margin full of notes in handwriting I didn't recognize and yet somehow did, the way you recognize a house by its creaks. Handing it over meant handing me evidence. Evidence is a dangerous thing to give someone who might, upon reading it, decide the whole thing was a trick — a paper man's con, a prophecy fulfilled by coercion.

He gave it to me anyway. That mattered. I noted that it mattered, in the hole where feelings about him should have been.

The cover was warm from his chest. I opened it to a ribbon-marker near the end, and there it was: tonight's date, transcribed in 1899, verbatim, including — God help me — including the line *"Just memorizing."*

Below it, crammed into the margin in cramped, slanting, urgent script, a note in ink that was newer than the page:

*"If you are reading this and you do not know who wrote it: you wrote it. You are Coral. Trust the sad man with the book. You loved him enough to pay the door's price rather than lose anything else. Don't waste it being proud. — C."*

I read it twice. Three times. The goats pressed against my legs, sensing weather.

"He waited," I said slowly. "All this. He knew, and he waited for me to read it myself, instead of just telling me and hoping I'd believe him."

"I'm standing right here," the stranger said, "and this is deeply strange for me too."

"I know." I closed the book. Held it against my sternum, where the hole was. "I know you are. That's the worst part. You feel like — " I stopped, because the sentence had no honest ending. *Home* was too much. *Almost* was too cruel. "You feel familiar," I finished. "Like a song I know all the words to but can't remember learning."

He made a sound that was half laugh, half wound.

"So here's my situation," I said, and my wry narrator's voice came back the way it always does when I'm frightened — armor first, feelings later. "I am standing in a country I've never seen, that I technically saved myself to enter, having forgotten the only person from my old life who came with me. My aunt is on the other side of a door that no longer exists. My neighbors probably think I'm dead, or worse, a lesson. And the one piece of hard evidence I possess that any of my life was real is a book that says I should trust a sad man with mud on his knees." I looked at him. "Who are you, by the way? Your actual name. Since apparently we never got properly introduced, or since I un-got introduced, or whatever the tense is."

"Jude," he said. "My name is Jude."

"Jude." I tried it out. It fit in my mouth like a key fits a lock it hasn't opened in years — smoothly, with a small resistance at the end that might have been rust and might have been recognition.

Above us, the crow screamed once — triumph or warning, hard to say — and wheeled east, toward the horizon, toward the hum of roads and engines and books that recorded souls.

And it was then, following that bird's flight with my eyes, that I saw it: far off across the valley floor, a line of riders where no road was. Dark shapes, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who have never once been lost. People who knew exactly where this blank hillside was, because a door had just been flung open in it, and doors that open can be found.

Jude saw them a half-second after I did. I knew that much about him — I could read the stiffening of his shoulders even if I couldn't read his history.

"Friends of yours?" I asked.

His face went gray. "No," he said. "But they're looking for me. And for you."
