## 19. The Stranger Who Cries

Here is a thing they don't put in the training manuals of my order: the hardest part of any mission is not the lying. Lying is easy. Lying is just telling someone a story they want to be true and then standing very still while they believe it.

The hard part is standing in the rain outside a door that shouldn't exist, watching a woman look at you like you're weather—present, mildly inconvenient, nothing personal—and knowing that three days ago she said your name in her sleep.

Coral had stepped through the door into a country that roared. That's how I'd describe it, anyway, from where I stood behind her: Hollowmere was a held breath, and the world outside had never once needed to hold its breath about anything. Wind with actual teeth in it. Sky so wide it looked like an error. Somewhere downslope, a river arguing with its own rocks, loud as a congregation.

And Coral stood in the middle of all that noise and turned around to ask me—who I was.

The price. Of course. The guardian had told her what it would cost, and she'd paid it anyway, and the coin it took was *me*.

I want to say I was noble about it. I want to tell you I stood there thinking only of her freedom, radiant and selfless, a good soldier of love. What I actually thought, in the first raw second, was: *she's forgotten me, which means part of me is now dead, which means I'm talking to her as a ghost.* And the second thing I thought was even less flattering: *good. Maybe this is cleaner.*

It wasn't cleaner. Nothing is ever cleaner. But let me start at the beginning, because beginnings are the one thing I've never been able to lie about well.

* * *

The door stayed open exactly long enough for two people to leave and not one second more.

I heard it before I saw it happen—a groan from under the hill, the sound of stone reconsidering itself—and then the chapel behind us folded shut like a book being closed by someone who'd found a better one. Where the entrance had been there was now just a wall, old and ordinary and mossy, with no opinion about anything. Verity's voice was still ringing somewhere above us, down in the dark, shouting truth at a choir until the hymn choked on it. I'll hear that sound on my deathbed, whatever my deathbed turns out to be. A woman in an apron yelling *you were stolen, all of you were stolen* at people who sang forgetting like it was a lullaby.

Then quiet. Real quiet. The kind Hollowmere had never once allowed, because real quiet lets you hear yourself think, and thinking is how forgetting fails.

Coral stood in the wet grass with goats' hay still clinging to her sleeve—I noticed that, God help me, I noticed the *hay*—and looked at me with the flat polite attention you give a man who has asked you for directions.

"Sorry," she said. "Do I know you? You came out right behind me, so I'm assuming—" She stopped. Frowned. Her hand went to her temple the way it always did when something hurt in there. "I came out of a hill. Why did I come out of a hill?"

And here is where I stood at the crossroads I'd been walking toward since chapter one of my own damn story, though nobody handed me the map.

Option one: walk away. My order would have preferred it, actually. The mission parameters had been *verify the beast-speaker, extract if possible, do not become emotionally compromised*, and I had failed that last clause so comprehensively it should have been taught as a case study. If I walked away, Coral Dunmore would wander into the real country with no past she could prove and no enemies she could name, and the Order of the Unwritten would eventually find her anyway, but it wouldn't be on my conscience specifically. It would just be on my conscience generally, which is much more comfortable. Generally-conscience is a big soft pillow. Specifically-conscience is a nail.

Option two: tell her everything. That I was sent. That the order hunts beast-speakers because we're the only ones who can command the guardians—the same guardians she'd just spent memories commanding for her own sake, not mine. That every kindness I'd shown her was technically operational. That when she'd fed me stew in her aunt's kitchen and laughed at my terrible joke about the goat named Duchess, I'd been filing reports in my head, and the reports had gotten shorter and shorter until they were just sentences like *she hums when she cooks* and *I didn't write anything today*.

She was waiting for an answer. The rain was starting properly now, fat cold drops that the valley had probably never permitted either, because rain washes things and Hollowmere hated clean surfaces.

"You followed me out," she said slowly. "Out of a hill. With no door."

"I did."

"Why?"

There it was. One word, and every version of my future hanging off it like coats on hooks.

* * *

Let me tell you what I know about crying men, since apparently I am one now.

Men in my line of work cry exactly twice: once when the torture works, and once when it doesn't. Everything else is discipline. So I stood in the rain—which gave excellent cover, credit where due—and I felt my face doing something unsanctioned, and I thought, with genuine professional horror, *you are about to cry in front of a woman who does not remember giving you a reason to.*

"Coral," I said.

Her eyes flickered. Not recognition—there was none left to flicker—but something older than memory, some groove worn deep enough that even erased, the record still wanted to spin. She frowned harder.

"That's my name."

"Yes."

"And you are?"

"Nobody," I said. "That's the problem. I was sent to find you and I found you and somewhere along the way I stopped being what I was sent to be, and now I'm nobody standing in the rain, and you have every right to call the constable." I took a breath. "My name is Jude."

"Jude." She said it carefully, tasting it. Then she went pale—not dramatically, not in the way of stories, just a slow draining, like milk poured into tea. "Why do I know that word?"

"You don't. Not anymore."

"*Jude and the Choir,*" she whispered. "That's—that was written in the book. In my handwriting. I wrote that in the margins before—" Her hand flew to her pocket, her bag, searching. "Where's the book? I had the book, I took the book—"

"You left it," I said gently. "You left a lot of things. It's okay. I brought mine."

Because here's the thing I'd decided, standing at those coat-hooks of possible futures, while the rain came down like the sky finally admitting what it had been holding back: I couldn't give her back her memory. Nobody could. The price was paid; the guardian doesn't do refunds; the whole economy of magic runs on loss and always has. But I had something almost as good and infinitely more humiliating.

Evidence.

I pulled the prophecy book out from inside my coat—the 1899 edition, water-spotted, spine cracked from a century of waiting—and held it out to her like a man surrendering a weapon. Which, in fairness, I was. It had been a weapon when I arrived. It was the reason I'd come, the commission, the proof-of-claim my order required. And I was handing it to the person it was written about, on a hillside, in the rain, with no claim attached at all.

"What's this?" she said.

"My confession," I said. "Read the entry for tonight."

* * *

She read standing up, hunched over it, hair plastered to her forehead, and I watched her face go through weather systems. Confusion. Recognition without context—the worst kind, like hearing a song from a house you lived in before you could talk. Then fear, sharp and quick, when she reached the parts about King Aldric and the erasure and the covenant, because grief for a stolen self apparently survives memory-wiping. It lives deeper down than memory does. Memory is furniture. Grief is load-bearing.

Then she got to the dialogue.

I watched her lips move over the transcribed words of our first date. Every awkward pause. The bit where she'd knocked over the cider. The exact sentence where she'd said, *You ask questions like a man reading off a list, Jude, and I'd like to see the list.* She looked up at me.

"This says I said this. Word for word."

"Yes."

"And this—" her finger trembled on a line lower down the page, a line from later that night, from after the piano bench, from the dark kitchen where we'd sat shoulder to shoulder terrified and electrified in equal measure. "This is... this is a conversation we haven't had yet. This says I say—" She stopped. Read it again. Her ears went red. "Oh."

"Yeah," I said, ears going red in solidarity across the gulf of her forgetting. "That one happened too. Last night. Before the door. Before you..."

"Before I forgot you." She closed the book. Held it against her chest like a shield or a wound, possibly both. "I paid for the door with you. Didn't I. Whatever you and I were—I paid for it."

"You chose it," I said. "There's a difference, and I need you to know I know the difference. You didn't lose me. You *spent* me. On the door. Deliberately. Because you rehearsed it—" and here my voice did something embarrassing, cracked right down the middle like cheap crockery, "—because you rehearsed letting me go for three nights, sitting up with that book, practicing. I watched you practice losing me and I never said a word because I knew you'd only do it if you loved me, and I couldn't take that away from you, the loving. Even though it meant—"

I stopped. Rain. River noise. A crow somewhere overhead making commentary, because crows are constitutionally incapable of silence during other people's worst moments.

"So," I said, when I'd reassembled myself into something approximately human. "Here's the whole truth, since truth is apparently the family business now. I work—I worked—for an organization called the Order of the Unwritten. We keep records of everyone the prophecy-books miss. People outside fate. And for a long time, that meant we hunted beast-speakers, because beast-speakers can command the guardians, and guardians guard doors, and doors are power, and power is—" I made a gesture. "You know how organizations are."

"They sent you to fetch me."

"They sent me to verify you. Whether extraction was viable." I swallowed. "They also sent me to assess whether you'd be an asset or a threat. Beast-speakers don't get to be neutral, Coral. Out here, you're a key that walks around. Someone will try to turn you. I'd rather it was me, honestly, except I quit, effective immediately, as of the moment I watched you pay a memory for your own life. I'm done being their hands. I'd like to be yours instead, except—"

"Except I don't remember wanting that."

"No."

"And you're telling me anyway. Without the wanting. Just... presenting the evidence and hoping."

"Basically," I said, "I am a sad man with a book."

Something moved across her face then. Not memory. Something adjacent to memory—the shape memory leaves in the carpet after the furniture's gone. She stared at me for a long time, and I let her, because staring is free and I had already lost everything chargeable.

"You're crying," she observed.

"It's the rain."

"It's absolutely not the rain." And then, horribly, wonderfully, she smiled—the crooked half-smile, the one from page forty-seven of the transcription, *she smiles like she's keeping half of it for later*—and caught herself doing it, and looked almost frightened by her own face. "I don't know why I did that."

"Muscle memory," I said. "Yours, not mine."

* * *

We walked down out of the hills together because it was getting dark and because, she said, she wasn't going to make decisions based on a stranger's sad eyes, but also she wasn't going to leave a stranger with sad eyes alone in the wilderness, because apparently decency survived memory-wipes too. Good. Excellent. The foundation holds even when the house burns.

We talked about nothing important—goats, roads, whether the moon outside Hollowmere was bigger or if she'd just gotten smaller—and every mile she glanced at me sidelong like a woman doing arithmetic she couldn't show her work for.

At the edge of a fallow field she finally stopped and said, "Show me the margins."

"The what?"

"In the book. I wrote notes. Past-me left instructions—it's the only thing I trust right now, honestly, her. She seemed to have her head on straight." She held out her hand, imperious, and I surrendered the book again, and she turned pages by feel in the dying light until she found them: my marginalia, her handwriting, dense little annotations climbing the borders of the printed prophecy like ivy.

Her finger stopped on one.

She read it. She read it twice. And then she looked up at me with an expression I will be unpacking in therapy for the rest of my natural life, and read it aloud:

"'Trust the sad man with the book.'"

Silence. The river went on arguing. The crow had opinions.

"He cries," past-Coral had added underneath, in smaller letters, almost fondly. "It's fine. It's actually—God, it's embarrassing how fine it is."

"Well," I said, into the longest silence of my career, "that's deeply unfair to both of us."

But Coral wasn't laughing. She was looking past me, down the valley, back the way we'd come—and her face had gone still in a way I recognized from the last chapters of the book, from the entries about the choir, about neighbors with knives, about a town that defended its own forgetting.

Down in the dark behind us, where a hidden valley had been, lights were moving. Many of them. Organized. And they weren't coming from Hollowmere.

They were coming from the road. From outside. From my order, whose scouts had watched that door open like hawks watching a rabbit hole, and who were now crossing the fields toward two small figures on a hillside—one of whom was the most dangerous unregistered asset in a generation, and the other of whom had just committed treason in the rain.

"Coral," I said quietly. "We need to run. And I should warn you—everything I know about running comes from the people chasing us."

She slung the book into her bag, took my hand like it was the most obvious thing in the world, and said, "Then lead, sad man."

So I led.
