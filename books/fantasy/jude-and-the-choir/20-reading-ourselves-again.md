## 20. Reading Ourselves Again

The inn at Millbrook had two kinds of rooms: the ones that smelled like lavender because someone had tried, and the one we could afford, which smelled like lavender losing an argument to damp wool. I lay on the bed with my boots off—Jude's insistence; he'd said something about my feet having earned better—and stared at the ceiling where a water stain had spread into a shape like a hand reaching for the door.

I did not know the man sitting across the room from me.

That was the fact of my life now, as fixed and unlovely as the stain. There was a stranger in my room. He was sad in a way that seemed structural, like a sagging beam, like something the whole building depended on. He had a book. And according to the handwriting in the margins of that book—which was, impossibly, my handwriting—the appropriate response to him was trust.

I'd read the note four times since we'd left the door behind us. *Trust the sad man with the book.* It sat there on page 212 of a prophecy text dated 1899, wedged between a verse about King Aldric's dying breath and a list of names I didn't recognize, written in ink the brown of old tea, in loops I knew better than my own face.

Because I did know my own handwriting. What I didn't know was writing it.

"You're staring at it again," Jude said. He didn't look up from the window. He'd taken the chair by the glass the way a man takes a post—like watching the road was a job he'd been assigned by someone with authority over him. Maybe he had. Maybe the authority was guilt.

"I'm allowed," I said. "It's my handwriting."

"It is," he said, and there was something in his voice I couldn't name—not agreement, exactly. More like relief wearing agreement's coat.

"How do you know it's mine? You said you knew me. Before." The words came out flat. I'd been practicing saying them without either hope or accusation, and mostly failing at both. "You knew me before, and I walked out of that valley not knowing you existed, and now you tell me the book proves everything, but how do I know the book isn't part of it? Whatever *it* is. Your order. Your mission. The hunt."

He turned then. In the lamplight his face did the thing it apparently always did when I pressed on that particular bruise—it went careful. Composed. Sad the way a locked church is sad.

"Because I watched you write some of them," he said. "In the valley. Nights you couldn't sleep. You'd take the book off the piano bench and sit in the kitchen with your aunt's sewing scissors as a paperweight and you'd write in the margins like the margins were talking back to you." His mouth did something almost like a smile, and failed at the last inch. "You called it correspondence."

"Correspondence with what?"

"With yourself. With whoever you'd be after." He looked away again. "You were very thorough about her. About you. About—after."

* * *

Here is what I remembered of my life, in full inventory:

A valley called Hollowmere. Goats—seven of them, with names I still carried around like coins in a pocket: Bramble, Sorrel, the twins (Hatch and Brood), old Magpie who butted knees, young Fern, and the billy, Grudge, named honestly. An aunt named Verity who raised me and lied to me and stayed behind at a door under a chapel holding back a choir with nothing but the truth shouted out loud. A gift of beast-speech I'd spent twenty-six years hiding and three weeks using to command stone guardians, at the cost of every memory that mattered.

And a hole where a man should be. Not a vague hole—a shaped one. The way a keyhole is shaped. I kept catching myself turning toward him with things half-said, offering observations to a person I trusted on credit, laughing a beat before he finished jokes I hadn't heard yet. My body knew him. My mouth knew him. My grief knew him so specifically that it frightened me, the way you'd be frightened to find a dent in your pillow exactly the shape of a head you've never seen.

Everything else—the real country outside, the roads, Millbrook with its actual sky and its terrifying abundance of strangers—all of it was loud and new and slightly too much, like being handed a whole orchestra when you'd only ever heard one instrument through a wall.

Except the book. The book was familiar in a way that made no sense, and my handwriting in its margins was the most familiar thing in the world, and that was the thread I had decided to pull.

"Read them to me again," I said. "All of them. Start to finish. I want to hear my own voice telling me who I am, even if it's secondhand."

He brought the book over and sat on the edge of the bed, careful, leaving a gap between us like a man respecting a fence he himself had built. Then he opened to the beginning of the marginalia and began.

* * *

The notes went back further than I expected. The earliest ones weren't addressed to future-me at all—they were arguments with the text itself, the kind of thing you scrawl when you think nobody will ever read it:

*If the covenant keeps us safe, why does safety feel like a held breath?*

*Aldric didn't erase Hollowmere to protect it. He erased it to hide it. Ask the difference.*

*The choir sings the forgetting. Who taught the choir? Nobody. That's the point.*

Then, somewhere past the middle, the handwriting changed. Tighter. Deliberate. The voice turned from a girl arguing with a book to a woman leaving instructions, and the dates—when Jude checked them against the prophecy entries—stopped making sense. Notes dated days ahead. Notes dated months ahead. Notes, once, dated eleven years in the future, which I chose not to think about too hard while lying down.

*The guardian takes memories in order. Dearest first or nearest first? Test this. Record the answer. —C.D., who may not remember writing this*

*I tested it. Nearest first. Mother's face went third. I don't remember her face. If you are reading this and you also don't remember her face: I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm sorry.*

That one had a stain on it. Water, or something else. Jude read it steadily, but I saw his thumb press white against the page.

"Keep going," I said, because if I let him stop I would have to say something about that stain and I wasn't ready to know whose grief it was.

*To whoever comes out the door:* the next note began, and my skin prickled, because this was the voice of a person planning her own amputation and doing it neatly, the way Verity hemmed trousers. *You will not know Jude. This is not an accident. You chose it. Here is why: the door needs a memory dearest of all, and if you keep anything, you'll keep him, and if you keep him you'll hesitate, and if you hesitate the town will reach you and the valley will close over your head like water. So you gave him up in advance. You rehearsed it. You cried about it in advance, which felt ridiculous and was not.*

*Rules for After:*
*1. Do not go back inside for any reason. The door opens outward once.*
*2. Trust the sad man with the book.*
*3. He will offer to leave. Do not let him. He is better than his orders, but he doesn't believe it yet, and he'll test whether you believe it by offering to go.*
*4. Read the last page before you decide anything.*

Jude stopped reading. "That's—" He cleared his throat. "Rule three. You wrote rule three before any of it happened."

"And here you are," I said, "offering to leave."

"I offered this morning. At breakfast."

"I know. I said yes. I started packing."

"You did," he said quietly. "And then you found the note."

"And then I found the note." I looked at him. He looked like a man who had just been seen through a wall by someone who shouldn't have been able to see him. "You were going to go, weren't you. You thought I'd be better off without a handler from the order trailing her."

"I thought you'd be safer deciding freely."

"I decided freely," I said, "eleven years ago, apparently. Sit down. Keep reading."

* * *

There were more rules. Rule five concerned the goats—*they'll follow the scent of Hollowmere's soil on your boots for a while; don't take it personally; they're grieving too*—and rule six was crossed out so hard the pen had gone through the paper. I held the page up to the lamp and read the ghost underneath anyway:

*6. Ask him about the night at the gate. If he tells the truth about what he was sent to do, forgive h——*

The rest was destroyed. Jude watched me read it and went very still.

"The night at the gate," I said. "When you arrived. For the blind date my aunt arranged."

"I was sent to verify you were the one. The beast-speaker the prophecies pointed to." His hands were flat on his knees, knuckles pale. "I arrived knowing what you were. Knowing what I was supposed to do with that knowledge. And you came out to meet me with hay in your hair and you apologized to a goat for keeping her waiting, and she answered you, and you pretended she hadn't." He exhaled. "I stood at your gate thinking: they sent me to appraise a person, and she's apologizing to a goat. Something in me broke rank right there and never reported back in."

"That's the truth?"

"That's all of it. The uglier parts included."

I sat with that. Past-me, it seemed, had anticipated that I would need to sit with it, because the next margin note said exactly that:

*Sit with it. Don't rush forgiveness and don't perform it. Evidence first, feeling after. You can rebuild a house on evidence. Feeling alone is building on weather.*

"She was wise," Jude said softly. "Past-you."

"She was bossy," I said. "I like her."

And then, because the room was small and the lamp was warm and the man was sad and the note had told me not to perform anything, I said, "Tell me the rest. Not the mission. The nights. What were the nights?"

He hesitated. "Are you sure? It's cruel, hearing about a self you don't have."

"My whole life is a self I don't have. One more evening won't drown me." I pulled the blanket over my legs. "Start anywhere."

So he started at the piano bench. Told me about finding the book before I ever caught him with it, and how he'd read our date transcribed word for word and sat in the chapel feeling like a thief who'd broken in and found his own name on the inventory. He told me about choir rehearsals where I'd hum harmony wrong on purpose to make him laugh, and how I'd once fallen asleep mid-argument on his shoulder in Verity's kitchen and he hadn't moved for two hours because his leg went numb and he decided numbness was a fair price. He told me I sang badly and loudly and that he'd lied to me about it once, early, and that I'd known immediately and never let him forget it.

"Do I forgive easily?" I asked.

"No," he said. "You forgive thoroughly. It takes longer but it holds."

"Hm." I filed that away next to the shape of the keyhole in my chest. "Go on."

He talked until the lamp burned low. And the strange arithmetic of it settled over me gradually, the way dusk does: I was not learning a stranger. I was being reintroduced to a house I owned but had never entered, going room to room with a man who knew where all the furniture was, taking inventory, checking each item against the ache it left in me. Most of them matched. That was the terrible comfort of it. The memories were gone but their outlines remained, pressed into me like flowers in a closed book, and his stories fit those outlines the way a key fits the lock it was cut for.

Evidence first. Feeling after. She was right, past-me. The feeling came anyway—but it came with receipts.

* * *

Near midnight I took the book from him and turned to the last page, because rule four said to, and I had decided that the version of me who survived a dying king's spell and an amnesiac door and a town full of knives probably deserved compliance on at least one instruction.

The last page was not prophecy. It was a letter. Full sentences, written slowly, in the steadiest hand in the whole book—as though the writer had been afraid of trembling and had gripped the pen hard enough to hurt.

*Coral. If you're reading this, you're her, and I'm gone, and I'm glad, because being gone was the whole plan. Listen:*

*You lost the memory of him. You will feel like the love was never real, because how can you love someone you can't remember? Here is the answer, and I need you to actually hear it: the love isn't stored in the memory. The memory was just where you kept it. The love is in what you do next.*

*He waited for you. He fought a hymn with your name. He followed you out of a country that tried to eat his mind rather than lose you. Those facts are in the book. Facts are better than feelings. Build on them.*

*And when you're ready—and only when you're ready, don't let anyone rush you, including him, including me—choose him again. Not because you owe the past-self who paid for it. Because you'll have looked at all the evidence and found him worth choosing. That's the only love that counts. The kind you'd pick twice.*

*P.S. Check the spine.*

I frowned. "Check the spine?"

Jude leaned over. Together we tilted the book, running fingers along the cracked leather binding near the bottom—and felt it. A ridge. A seam that wasn't stitching.

I got the sewing scissors—Verity's, which I'd carried out of the valley in my coat pocket along with seven goat hairs and a pebble from the doorstep, my whole archaeology of a stolen life—and worked the blade into the seam. The leather parted with a sound like a held breath released. Inside the spine's hollow, rolled tight as a wick, was a strip of paper.

I unrolled it on the bed. It was dense with my handwriting, smaller than the margins, packed edge to edge—dates, names, directions, and across the top, underlined three times:

*THE ORDER IS COMING THROUGH THE DOOR. YOU HAVE LESS TIME THAN YOU THINK.*

Below that, in a different, hurried script—not mine—I recognized the jagged letters I'd seen once before, scratched into the threshold stone beneath the chapel. Verity's hand. Her last message, passed forward like a relay baton:

*I'm staying. Someone has to hold the threshold. The guardians will listen to you from any distance—that's the nature of the covenant, it runs in blood. Call them when they come. Call them LOUD.*

And beneath that, newest of all, in ink still faintly shiny, dated three days ago—the day we walked out:

*They followed you out. Two riders, grey cloaks, asking after a beast-speaker and her keeper. They reached the door at dusk. Verity turned them back once.*

*She cannot turn them back twice.*

The lamp guttered. Outside, far down Millbrook's one street, a dog began to bark—not the ordinary complaint of dogs, but the flat, rhythmic alarm I'd learned in the valley meant *stranger, armed, approaching.*

Jude was already on his feet, snuffing the lamp, moving to the window with the book clutched to his chest like a shield. Down the dark road, two points of light swung side to side. Lanterns. Grey cloaks, if grey cloaks could be seen at all in the dark, which I suspected was rather their point.

"Coral," he said quietly. "They're here."

I put Verity's letter in my pocket, next to the pebble and the goat hairs and the scissors. Past-me had built me a house out of evidence, and I intended to live in it—and, if it came to it, to defend it.

I opened my mouth, and reached for the oldest language I knew.
