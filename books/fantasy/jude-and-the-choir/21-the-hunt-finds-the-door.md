## 21. The Hunt Finds the Door

### Coral

The door stood open for exactly as long as it took me to notice that freedom smells like rain and diesel and something green I had no name for.

Then the hillside started screaming.

Not people. Stone. The guardians—the ones who'd spent a century pretending to be boulders and hedgerows and one deeply unconvincing scarecrow—woke all at once, and their waking sounded like a quarry being asked a question it didn't want to answer. I felt them through my feet before I heard them with my ears, the same way I'd always felt the goats, the way I'd felt everything in this valley since I was small enough to think every child could hear the fence posts gossiping.

"They're not screaming at us," Jude said beside me. He had the book clutched to his chest like a hymnal. His voice did something complicated on the word *us*. "They're screaming at what's coming."

I turned back toward Hollowmere. From the threshold of the chapel's undercroft, looking out through the groaning gap King Aldric had sealed and I had unsealed with three words and one mother's face, the valley looked the same as it always had. Golden light. Chimney smoke. Aunt Verity standing above us on the chapel steps, holding the choir off with nothing but her voice and the truth, which apparently cut deeper than any knife when you shouted it loud enough.

*We were taken. We were stolen. There is a country outside and it never knew our names.*

Half the choir had stopped singing. Half of them were crying. And half—this was the part that made my stomach drop—had kept singing, faces smooth as eggs, because some lies hold on harder than others when they're all you have.

"Coral." Jude's hand found mine. His palm was sweating. Three days ago I would have known why. Three days ago I'd known everything about him: how he took his coffee, which knee he favored, the way he said my name like he was checking whether it still worked. Now he was a sad stranger with a book who cried at gates, and I was trusting him anyway, because past-me had left me a note in her own handwriting saying I should.

*Trust the sad man with the book.*

Fine. But I was keeping my hand near the stones.

"What's coming?" I asked.

He looked at me with an expression I couldn't read anymore, and that—that was the worst of it. Not the missing memories themselves but the way they'd taken my fluency in him with them. He used to be a language I spoke without thinking. Now he was a foreign film with the subtitles burned out.

"The Order," he said. "The ones who sent me. They've been waiting a hundred years for this door to open. Beast-speakers are the only thing that can command guardians, and guardians are the only thing that can hold a border like this one. If they get inside—" He swallowed. "They don't want to free these people, Coral. They want the valley. A whole country nobody remembers is a very convenient place to put things you never want found."

"Things like beast-speakers?"

"Things like beast-speakers."

And then, over the ridge where no road had ever been built, because roads require a country that admits you exist—I saw them coming.

* * *

There were nine of them. Later I would learn there had been more at the start, but nine was what arrived, riding horses that moved wrong—not lame, not limping, just *wrong*, the way animals move when someone has told them to be brave and they haven't decided whether to believe it.

I could hear the horses.

That sounds like nothing. It wasn't nothing. Every animal in my life had always been audible to me, a low constant murmur under the world—the goats grumbling about the weather, the crows running their little corruption schemes, the worms in the garden complaining about drainage. But those horses came up over the ridge broadcasting something I'd never heard from anything wearing hooves.

*Don't let her see us flinch. Don't let him see us flinch.*

They weren't afraid of me. They were afraid of the men on their backs, and they were hiding it, the way children hide bruises, the way Verity had hidden a century-old book in a piano bench and called it dust.

Something in my chest went very cold and very clear.

"Coral." Jude stepped in front of me, which was sweet and also completely useless, because I could feel the guardians rising around us like a tide answering a moon, and the moon was me. "Whatever you're about to do—"

"You mean the treason?"

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny. You literally wrote it down." I nodded at the book. "Chapter eight. *Beast-speech is treason.* Your handwriting, sad man."

His face did the thing where grief and love and guilt all tried to use the same muscles at once. I was starting to learn that face again, one expression at a time, like relearning a town after a flood. "I wrote it down because it's true. If you do this—if you turn the guardians on the Order—there's no walking it back. They'll hunt you until one of you is finished. Not arrest. Finish."

"Jude." I squeezed his hand once, then let go, because what I needed my hands for next wasn't holding. "I spent twenty-six years in a valley that stole my whole life and called it safety. My aunt gave forty years to a lie because she thought the lie was kinder than the truth. I paid a memory I can't even grieve properly because I don't know what it was worth." I looked up at the ridge, at the nine riders and their frightened, loyal, lying horses. "I'm done paying other people's prices quietly."

The ground answered me before I finished the sentence.

* * *

### Jude

Here is what they don't tell you about serving the Order of the Quiet Border, and I include the recruitment pamphlet, which I now understood had been written by someone with a gift for fiction that rivaled anything in Coral's library: they don't tell you what the work looks like up close.

I had verified eleven beast-speakers before Coral Dunmore. That was the arithmetic I carried into the light spilling out of the opened door. Eleven names in a ledger. Eleven times I'd watched from a distance while others did the taking, because verification was my talent and I was careful never to be present for what verification made possible. I'd told myself the distance was decency. Standing in the undercroft, watching the woman I loved—had loved, still loved, would go on loving whether she remembered or not—turn toward nine riders with the full attention of a hundred stone giants, I finally admitted the truth: the distance hadn't been decency. It had been cowardice wearing a clerk's coat.

The first guardian rose out of the hillside like a decision being regretted.

I'd seen guardians before, in old engravings and once, badly, through fog. Nothing prepared me for the reality. The thing that had spent a century disguised as a hay barn unfolded to its full height and it was less a creature than an argument the land was making—shoulders of fieldstone, eyes like wells, moss hanging off it in ropes. And it wasn't alone. Along the ridge line, all around the bowl of Hollowmere, the landscape stood up. Fences straightened into limbs. Boulders shrugged off their boulder-ness. Even the scarecrow, that eternal embarrassment, peeled itself off its post and cracked its neck with a sound like a wagon axle.

Coral stood in front of all of it with dirt on her sleeves and said, in the voice she used for goats:

"No further."

Two words. I have heard generals address armies with less effect and more words.

The lead rider stopped his horse so hard the animal sat back on its haunches. He was older than I expected, gray at the temples, with the particular neatness of a man who has never once been surprised on purpose. Magister Corvane. I'd delivered reports to him for six years. He'd signed my orders. He'd shaken my hand and said, "Bring me confirmation of the last one, Jude, and you'll never have to do fieldwork again."

I had brought him Coral instead.

"Step aside," Corvane said, and his voice carried the flat authority of a man reading from a document. "This matter does not concern outsiders."

"I'm not stepping anywhere," Coral said. "This is my valley. Well—it isn't, actually, it's stolen, we've established that, the paperwork's right here." She jerked her chin at the book under my arm. "But it's *my* stolen valley, and those—" she gestured at the guardians, who had arranged themselves along the threshold like a jury, "—are mine too. They answer to me. Ask them why, if you like. They talk. Well, they rumble. It counts."

Corvane's gaze slid from her to me, and what I saw in it made my mouth go dry. Not anger. Recalculation. I was, in that moment, ceasing to be an asset and becoming an entry.

"Brother Jude," he said mildly. "You were sent to verify a threat. You appear to have married one."

"I haven't married anyone."

"A distinction without a difference, given time." He turned back to Coral, and here was the thing I needed Coral to understand, the thing I'd tried to tell her in chapter after chapter of confession, and which the open door was making urgent: "You cannot hold this border forever, girl. Guardians tire. Speakers erode. Every word you spend is a memory gone. I can wait. I am very good at waiting; it is most of the job."

And Coral—who could not remember her mother's face, who had rehearsed letting go of the person she loved most in a chapel pew by candlelight while I pretended not to watch—smiled at him.

It was not a nice smile.

"You're right," she said. "Every word costs. So I won't waste any." She raised one hand, and every guardian on that ridge leaned forward like grass under wind. "I'm not going to fight you, Magister. Fighting costs. I'm going to *introduce* you."

She turned her head slightly, toward the horses.

"Girls. Boys. You've been carrying men who frighten you for a long time. I can hear it. I want you to know two things." Her voice dropped into that register I'd only heard once before, beneath the chapel, the one that made bedrock sit up. "First: no one will ever punish you for what you do in the next minute. Second: the stable is warm, and it's that way."

What happened next took four seconds and I will be honest about how it looked from where I stood, which was: like nothing. Like nine riders simply found themselves on the ground, gently deposited, weapons pinned under hooves that were suddenly extremely interested in grazing. Horses are honest creatures. Give them permission and a direction, and they will choose warmth and grass every single time. Corvane hit the turf with the dignity of a dropped briefcase and lay there staring at the sky while his own mount ate daisies two feet from his head.

The choir, watching from the chapel steps, burst into applause. Then caught itself. Then applauded again, louder, deciding it meant it.

* * *

### Coral

Here's what nobody tells you about power, either: it doesn't feel like triumph. It feels like withdrawal.

Because while I stood there, flush with the obedience of a hundred tons of animated geography, the cost arrived on schedule, punctual as rent. Something slid out of me and I felt it go—a warm ordinary thing, the shape of an afternoon. I grabbed for it and got smoke. Gone. Some afternoon I'd loved, spent on four seconds of horse diplomacy, and I would never know which one. Maybe the day I learned to whistle. Maybe the day Verity taught me to make jam and swore me to secrecy about how much sugar was actually in it. The ledger doesn't itemize. That's the cruelty of it. You don't lose a memory; you lose a memory *and the knowledge of what you lost*, so you can't even mourn correctly. Grief with the label torn off.

I staggered. Jude caught my elbow. His face was doing the new-language thing again—worry, awe, fear, all at once—and I realized he'd seen it happen. Seen the price. Seen me pay it.

"How many left?" he asked quietly.

"I don't know. That's the point. You don't get a balance statement."

"Coral—"

"Don't say we should stop. Look at them." The riders were being marched—not dragged, the guardians were polite about it—toward the chapel steps, where the half-remembering choir stood blinking in daylight like people emerging from a long nap. Corvane walked with his hands bound in what appeared to be ivy, radiating the fury of a man composing a report in real time. "If I stop, the door closes, and everyone in that crowd goes back to sleep, and Verity's forty years become a waste, and your order wins by default. I'm not stopping."

"Then we need to talk about what happens when they send more than nine."

"We'll need more than a hundred tons of rock."

"And," he said, and here his voice changed, went careful, the way voices go around wounds, "you should know what I saw in Corvane's face when he looked at you. He wasn't planning to take you prisoner, Coral. Prisoners come home. He was planning to end the bloodline. All of it. Every speaker, everywhere, permanently. That's the standing order. It's older than Aldric's spell. The door opening just made it *urgent*."

The cold came back into my chest, and this time it stayed.

"So this isn't a rescue," I said. "It's a siege."

"This is a siege. And sieges have keepers."

Which was when I noticed that Aunt Verity wasn't with the choir anymore.

* * *

She was standing at the threshold itself, in the doorway King Aldric had made and I had opened, one hand resting on the doorpost as if she'd been holding it her whole life and had merely been waiting for the rest of us to catch up. The morning light came through the gap behind her and made her look, for a moment, younger than I'd ever seen her—or maybe it was the opposite, maybe it showed me all her ages at once, stacked like rings in wood.

"Coral," she said. "Come here. There's something I should have shown you years ago, and there's a conversation your mother's ghost and I are going to have about my cowardice, but first things first."

I went. Of course I went. Some things don't change with memory loss; my feet still knew their way to her.

She pressed her hand flat against the doorframe, and the stone warmed under it—warmed and brightened, faintly gold, like a coal remembering fire—and the guardians along the ridge all bowed their great heads at once. Not to me. To her.

"You—" I stared. "They answer to *you?*"

"They answer to the keeper." She said it simply, the way you'd say *the kettle's on*. "Someone has to stay, love. Someone has to hold the door from this side—decide what comes in, decide what goes out. It can't be you. You've spent enough. Look at your hands." I looked. They were trembling, fine as plucked strings. "And it can't be the boy, because your order knows his face and the door needs a face the enemy can't predict." Her eyes crinkled. "Besides. I've had forty years to practice saying no to powerful men. I'm rather good at it."

"Verity." My voice cracked down the middle. "You'd stay? Alone? In the lie?"

"Not in the lie." She reached up and tucked a strand of hair behind my ear, the gesture so familiar it bypassed every missing memory and landed somewhere pre-verbal. "In the truth, dear. That's the whole difference. I stayed forty years in the lie because I thought I had no choice. Now I stay in the truth because I'm choosing to. Turns out that's the entire difference between a prison and a posting." She smiled, and behind the smile I saw it—the husband-shaped absence I'd only just learned about, the man the valley had charged her decades ago. "Besides, I have a great deal of unfinished business with a piano bench, a choir that needs reteaching its own names, and nine gentlemen in the ivy who require feeding and questioning, in that order."

Behind her, the sun cleared the ridge and lit the road that had never been built—the one running away west, out of the valley, into the enormous loud unhidden real country where my life was supposedly going to happen.

Jude came and stood beside me. Close, not touching. Leaving me room.

"There's a village three days west," he said. "People who help speakers. People who'll teach you what your voice can do that doesn't cost memories—there are ways, Coral, there have to be, the Order suppressed the research but it existed—" He stopped himself. Took a breath. Did the humble thing, the thing past-me must have loved him for, the offering-without-claiming. "Or there's nowhere. Or somewhere else entirely. I'll walk whichever direction you pick. I just—wanted you to know the first option exists."

I looked at the open door. At my aunt, keeper of the threshold, already turning to bark instructions at the choir with the crisp authority of a woman who had waited four decades for a job worth having. At the strangers in the ivy. At the vast green smell of the world outside, patient as bread rising.

My hands had stopped shaking.

"West," I said. "Three days. And Jude—" I looked up at the sad man with the book, this stranger I kept choosing, "—tell me one story about us. Just one. Pick a good one."

His whole face opened like a window.

"Oh," he said, "do I have one."
