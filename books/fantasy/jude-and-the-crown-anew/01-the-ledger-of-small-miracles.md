## 1. The Ledger of Small Miracles

The winter ledgers smelled of tallow and old paper, which was how Petra Dunmore knew the year was ending. The Guild of Impossible Things kept its books the way other houses kept their fires — constantly, carefully, and with an unspoken agreement that everyone would pretend not to notice the cost.

She worked alone in the counting room on the third floor of the guildhall, where the windows were tall enough to show the rooftops of Candlemere going grey with evening frost. Three lamps burned at her elbow because the guild's rules required light for every audit: one lamp for the entries as written, one for the entries as intended, and one for the auditor herself, so that no number could be entered in darkness. It was a superstition dressed as procedure, which described most of the guild.

Petra had been the last oath-bound swordspeaker for fifteen years, and she had been the guild's keeper of ledgers for eleven of those, and in that time she had learned that miracles, like merchants, lied most often in the margins.

She turned the page with two fingers.

*Winter Quarter, First Month. Delivered: one answered prayer, minor (healing of a fevered child, age six), to the district of Lowmarsh. Broker fee: standard. Witnessed by courier J.*

She read it twice. Then she set down her pen, took off her spectacles, cleaned them on her sleeve, and put them back on, because this was the ritual she performed when she wanted the world to have changed while she wasn't looking.

It hadn't. The entry was still there. Neat, professional, entirely impossible.

Prayers were not supposed to be answerable. This was not a matter of policy but of physics, or whatever passed for physics in a world where the gods had gone quiet four hundred years ago. Petra had been born into that silence the way fish are born into water. In all her life, across all of Candlemere's nine districts and the eleven kingdoms that paid the guild its fees, a prayer was a letter sent to an address that had been demolished. You could compose it beautifully. You could seal it with wax and sincerity. Nothing came back. The church said the gods had ascended beyond hearing. The scholars said they had never existed. The guild, being practical, simply noted that petition-of-divine-intervention had been struck from its catalogue since before anyone currently employed had been born, and left it at that.

And yet here, in her ledger, in ink that was definitely not hers: *one answered prayer, minor.*

Petra did not gasp. She had been trained out of gasping at seventeen, when a swordspeaker who gasps gives away the shape of the blade. Instead she reached for the second lamp — the one for entries as intended — and held the page close, reading for the small dishonesties that always lived near large ones.

The entry was honest. That was the first wrong thing.

Fraud had a texture. She could feel it through paper the way a blind woman reads letters: the too-even hand, the fee rounded suspiciously clean, the witness column left blank or filled with a name that dissolved under inquiry. This entry had none of those flaws. The courier's mark — *courier J* — was real; Jude ran half the guild's delicate deliveries and had done so for the two years since arriving with no history and no yesterday. The broker fee matched schedule seven. Even the notation for the healing itself carried the correct technical phrasing: *minor miracle, somatic class, single beneficiary, no observable residue.* Whoever had entered it had been trained. Or had been telling the truth.

She turned the page forward, hunting.

There. Two pages on:

*Winter Quarter, First Month. Delivered: one answered prayer, minor (return of a drowned man's breath, three minutes past death), to the river-wardens of Lowmarsh. Broker fee: standard. Witnessed by courier J.*

And then, further along, in the same month, the same district, the same neat hand:

*Delivered: one answered prayer, minor (rain over the ash-fields, sufficient for planting). Fee waived — charitable act, per charter clause twelve.*

Three answered prayers. One city. One month. In a world where the gods had been silent since before the founding of the guild itself.

Petra sat back in her chair and listened to the building settle around her. Below, the guildhall went about its evening: brokers closing deals in the long gallery, the clink of the memory-vault's triple locks, someone laughing in the kitchens about a client who had tried to sell his own regret at a discount. A normal night at the trade house for miracles, curses, and memories. A normal night, except that somewhere beneath her hands, three impossibilities sat in her ledger like stones in bread.

She thought: *clerical error.* Someone had misfiled a mundane delivery under the old miracle taxonomy. Fever breaking was medicine, not divinity. Drowned men sometimes breathed again if you were fast and lucky. Rain came to ash-fields eventually, and calling it charity didn't make it weather-working.

She thought: *fraud.* Someone selling false miracles to desperate buyers, using the guild's name as collateral. That happened every few years, and every few years the guild ruined the fraudster thoroughly and quietly, because the entire value of the Guild of Impossible Things rested on a single principle: *we do not sell what we cannot deliver.* A lie about a curse got you fined. A lie about a god got you erased from the rolls and, in the older charters, from other things besides.

Both explanations were comfortable. Both were probably wrong. Petra had spent fifteen years learning the difference between the comfortable explanation and the true one, and the difference usually smelled like smoke.

Because here was the third thing she thought, the one she did not want to think: the entries were dated to Lowmarsh, and Lowmarsh had once been called something else. Before the Godswar, before the silence, it had been a holy city — the city of the Nine, the nine altars, the nine names carved into living stone. It was where the war had ended. It was where the gods had stopped answering, all at once, mid-prayer, like a choir cut off by a slammed door.

If prayers were being answered anywhere, they should not be being answered there.

Petra pulled the ledger toward her and began, methodically, to check everything. She verified courier J's delivery logs against the entries — they matched, though Jude's logs recorded only *parcel, sealed, delivered intact*, which was exactly what you'd write if you didn't know what you'd carried, or exactly what you'd write if you did. She checked the fee disbursements against the strong-room records. She cross-referenced the clients — a grandmother in Lowmarsh, a river-warden's office, an anonymous benefactor paying for rain — and found the accounts legitimate, funded, ordinary.

Everything was ordinary. That was the second wrong thing. Miracles, even small ones, should leave a wake. A fever healed by medicine left a doctor's bill. A fever healed by divinity ought to leave *something* — a residue, a shimmer in the aetheric ledgers, a candle burning wrong in the room where it happened. Her oath-trained senses, dulled as they were after fifteen years of disuse, should have felt the ripple from here, from Candlemere, sixty miles away.

She felt nothing. Either the miracles were fake, or they were being made so cleanly, so *quietly*, that even the world's own bookkeeping couldn't see them.

And there was only one craft she knew of that worked that cleanly.

Swordspeech. Speak a true name, bind a true thing. The oldest magic, the first magic, the one that had named the gods themselves into their crowns — and the one her order had died carrying, all but her. If someone was speaking names again, small names, gentle names, folding them small enough to pass through a courier's satchel —

Petra stopped that thought. She was getting ahead of the evidence, and auditors who got ahead of the evidence became prophets, and prophets, in her experience, made terrible bookkeepers.

She looked at the third entry again. *Rain over the ash-fields. Fee waived — charitable act, per charter clause twelve.*

Charter clause twelve. She knew the charters better than anyone alive; she had re-copied them herself during her first year, when the old clerk's handwriting had degraded past legibility. Clause twelve governed acts of charity performed in the guild's name. It was real. But it had a companion clause — clause thirteen, rarely cited, almost never needed — that required any charitable act touching *matters ecclesiastic* to be reported to the Master of Rolls within three days.

Master Vellam's initials appeared nowhere on these pages.

Petra sat very still for a long moment. Outside, the frost had finished its work and Candlemere glittered under a fat white moon, beautiful and indifferent. Somewhere below, Master Vellam was probably in his study, doing what he did every evening: drinking one glass of plum wine, reviewing tomorrow's contracts, asking after her health with genuine warmth whenever they crossed paths. He had taught her the ledgers. He had taught her that numbers were a form of honesty sterner than any oath.

He had also taught her, years ago, over those same plum wines, the story of the Godswar — and he told it strangely, she suddenly remembered. Not as tragedy. As *incompleteness.* "The war didn't end," he'd said once, swirling his glass. "Wars don't end, Petra. They wait for someone to find the paperwork."

She had laughed at the time. She was not laughing now.

Here is what Petra Dunmore did next, and she would think about it for many nights afterward, turning it over like a coin worn smooth: she tore the third page out of the ledger.

Not the first two. Those she left, folded down at the corner with a tiny pencil mark, the way she flagged anything needing follow-up — deniable, procedural, innocent. But the third page, the rain over the ash-fields, the charitable act, the clause-twelve entry that pointed a finger straight at the Master of Rolls — she tore it free, folded it once, twice, and held it over the flame of the first lamp.

The paper caught quickly. Old ledgers burn well; the guild used good stock. She watched the entry curl and blacken, the neat hand vanishing, the words *answered prayer* blooming orange and then gone, and she felt the specific cold satisfaction of an auditor destroying evidence of a crime —

— followed immediately, like a slap, by the understanding of what she'd actually done.

"Oh," she said aloud, to the empty room. "*Fool.*"

Because she had just done the one thing a keeper of ledgers must never do. She had made the books lie. Whatever those entries meant — fraud, smuggling, miracle, mistake — the truth of them had existed in exactly one place, in exactly one copy, and she had fed it to a lamp rather than let it be seen. If Vellam was guilty, she had protected him. If Vellam was innocent, she had framed him. And if the entries meant what some deep, oath-scarred part of her suspected they meant — if someone was carrying god-names through her guild like contraband, if the silence of four hundred years was developing cracks — then she had just burned the only receipt.

Her order had a saying for this. Her commander had said it a hundred times, standing over training yards full of young swordspeakers learning to hold truth in their mouths like a live coal: *What you destroy, you become responsible for.* Petra had always understood it as a rule about killing. Only now, watching the last corner of the page curl to ash between her fingers, did she understand it applied equally to paper.

She swept the ashes into the wastebin with the little brass shovel kept for exactly that purpose, because even her sins were tidy. Then she sat back down, opened the ledger to the gap where the page had been, and did the only thing left that was honest: she made an entry of her own, in her own hand, in the auditor's private log — the slim black book locked in the drawer beside her knee, the one no one else was permitted to open.

*First Month, twenty-third day,* she wrote. *One page removed and destroyed by auditor's hand. Reason: unknown. Contents reconstructed from memory as follows:* and then she wrote out the entry word for word, because fifteen years of oath-discipline had given her a memory like a vault door, and because some part of her already knew she would need it.

Below that, after a moment's hesitation, she added a second line:

*Query for tomorrow: locate courier J. Ask what was in the parcel.*

It was not a proper audit step. Proper audit steps involved warrants and countersignatures and informing the Master of Rolls, all of which now pointed back to a man whose initials were missing from a page she'd burned. Tomorrow she would figure out the rest. Tonight she closed the books, banked the lamps — one, two, three, in reverse order, the way she always did — and stood at the tall window looking out at Candlemere while the frost climbed the glass.

Sixty miles south, in a city that had been called the City of the Nine, someone had prayed for rain over an ash-field, and rain had come. Someone had prayed over a dead man, and he had breathed. Someone had prayed over a fevered child, and the fever had broken like a knuckle cracking.

Four hundred years of silence. And then, in a single month, three small voices answering back.

Petra pressed one fingertip against the frozen window and left a print in the frost, brief and human and soon gone. She was not a believer — the order had burned that out of her alongside everything else — but she found, to her considerable professional annoyance, that her hands were shaking.

"Get a grip," she told herself quietly. "You're a bookkeeper."

But bookkeepers know what certain entries mean. A fee waived is never just charity. A miracle logged without residue is never just a miracle. And a page torn out is never just paper — it is a door opened onto a dark stair, and the only question is whether you stand at the top listening, or whether you go down.

Petra banked the last lamp, locked the private log in the drawer, and went down to find out whether the kitchen was still serving supper — and whether a certain memoryless courier with humming cargo was taking deliveries these days.

She did not yet know that the satchel was already waiting for her, two floors below, and that it was singing.
