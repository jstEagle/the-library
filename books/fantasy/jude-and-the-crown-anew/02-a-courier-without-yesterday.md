## 2. A Courier Without Yesterday

Jude woke the way they always woke: like surfacing from deep water, hands first, reaching for something that wasn't there.

The room above the guild stables was cold. Frost ferned the inside of the window, and beyond it the city of Greyford lay grey and blue under a sky the color of unpolished pewter. Jude sat up, pressed their palms against their eyes until sparks bloomed, and did what they did every morning: they checked.

Two years. That was the length of them. Two years of memories, no more, no less — the first morning in this bed, the stable boy Tallow showing them where the chamber pot was, the guild badge warm in their hand and Master Vellam saying, *You came to us with nothing but good boots and a clean bill of silence, courier, which makes you either a blessing or a crime.* Everything before that was a wall of white. Not darkness — darkness had edges, darkness you could push against. This was nothing at all, smooth as fresh paper, and Jude had long ago stopped screaming at paper.

They dressed. They counted their things, because counting was a kind of memory you could carry in your pockets. One coat. One knife, good steel, worn grip shaped by a hand — their hand? They'd owned it since before the two years began; that was the joke of it, the only thing older than they were. One coin purse, one wax-sealed courier's chit, one pair of boots broken in exactly to their feet as if they'd walked a thousand miles in them already.

And the satchel.

It hung on its hook by the door, brown leather, brass buckles, utterly ordinary except for the humming. Not now. It only hummed when opened, a low note like a struck bell wrapped in wool, and every time it did it Jude felt the sound somewhere behind their sternum, as if the satchel were plucking a string they didn't know they had.

Tallow was mucking stalls when Jude came down. "Courier," he said, and then, because he was sixteen and couldn't help himself: "Dream anything?"

"I dreamed I was reading a letter," Jude said. "I don't remember what it said. Just that I was furious about it."

"Same dream three nights running?"

"Four."

Tallow grimaced sympathetically and went back to his shovel. Nobody at the guild made much of Jude's missing past anymore. The Guild of Impossible Things had a policy about such things, posted on the intake ledger in Master Vellam's careful hand: *We do not ask where our people come from. We ask only where they can go.* It was, Jude suspected, less a kindness than a convenience. But it meant that when they walked into the guildhall at dawn with frost in their hair, nobody looked twice. Couriers here came from everywhere and nowhere. Half of them probably had less past than Jude did, just better hidden.

The hall smelled of ink and tallow candles and last night's mulled wine gone sour in the cups. Brokers were already at their desks behind their brass grilles, weighing parcels on little scales, whispering offers. A woman in the corner was trying to sell a bottled thunderstorm to a farmer's agent; the agent kept shaking the bottle near his ear like a man testing an egg. On the great board above the exchange floor, chalk lines tracked the day's markets: *blessings, minor* down a half-penny; *curses, reversible* steady; *memories, common* flooded, as always, because everyone wanted to sell theirs and nobody wanted to buy.

Jude crossed to the duty desk, where the morning assignments were laid out in Petra Dunmore's handwriting.

Everyone in the guild knew Petra Dunmore's handwriting even if they'd never met her. It was famous the way certain swords were famous. Small, precise, upright, every letter standing at attention — the hand of a woman who audited miracles for a living and had never once been charmed out of a discrepancy. She was the last oath-bound swordspeaker, whatever that meant; the old soldiers who drank in the Lamb & Ledger said it meant she used to be able to kill gods with a sentence, back before the gods went silent four hundred years ago and took all the magic worth having with them. Now, mostly, it meant she ran the ledgers, and woe betide any broker whose numbers drifted.

Today there was a single assignment slip waiting for Jude, and pinned beneath it a note:

*Courier — report to my office at the eighth bell regarding parcel 4471-C. Do not open anything on the way. — P.D.*

Underneath, smaller, almost as an afterthought: *Breakfast is provided.*

Jude looked at the note for a long moment. There was something strange in it, though they couldn't have said what. Something in the way the small line sat apart from the rest, like a person standing too close to a door.

Parcel 4471-C. Jude remembered the delivery — one of the few jobs they could remember end to end, which was itself unusual. Three weeks ago, winter ledger season, a sealed crate from the temple district, addressed to the vaults, marked *fragile, do not inventory*. They'd carried it up from the docks through the snow, and the whole way the crate had felt wrong in a way they hadn't been able to name. Not heavy-wrong. *Listening*-wrong. And now it was missing, and the auditor wanted to talk about it over breakfast.

Jude went and got breakfast anyway. You didn't turn down free food in Greyford in winter, and besides, if you were going to be interrogated, it was best not to look hungry. Hungry people agreed to things.

* * *

Petra Dunmore's office was exactly what Jude had imagined and also nothing like it.

They'd imagined grandeur, or at least severity — the swordspeaker's office as a blade of a room. Instead it was small, cluttered, warm in a stubborn sort of way, a stove fighting the window drafts and losing gracefully. Shelves of ledgers rose on every wall, hundreds of them, spines labeled in that same upright hand: *WINTER LEDGER, YEAR OF THE QUIET SUN*, and so on back through decades. A sword hung above the mantelpiece, unsheathed, old, and so clean it might never have been drawn. On the desk: an abacus, a lamp, a teapot, and two cups.

Two cups. Jude noticed that before they noticed anything else, and hated themselves a little for noticing.

"Sit," said Petra Dunmore, without looking up. She was writing, fast, the pen moving in short decisive strokes. "You're early. Good. Early couriers lie less."

"Do late ones lie more, or just louder?"

That got her to look up. She had a narrow face, sharp at the cheekbones, dark hair pinned back with what appeared to be a pencil rather than a hairpin, and eyes the color of strong tea — the kind of eyes that had already read you, footnoted you, and flagged three discrepancies. But there was something else in them this morning, something Jude couldn't file. Tiredness, maybe. Or the particular loneliness of a person who spends all day being right.

"Louder," she said. "You're Jude. Best courier on the roster, according to Vellam, who exaggerates, but not by much. You've carried everything from love letters between feuding houses to a live curse in a hatbox, and your loss rate is zero." She turned a ledger around on the desk so it faced them. "Except once. Parcel 4471-C. Delivered to the vaults three weeks ago. Signed in. And then—" she tapped the page, "—gone. No signature on removal. No entry in the vault register. It has simply ceased, in a building where *nothing ceases*. I audit impossibilities for a living, courier, and this one is sitting in my books like a stone in my shoe."

"You think I took it."

"I think you're the last person known to have touched it, which in my trade is the same thing as being interesting." She poured tea into both cups and pushed one across. "Drink. You look like you slept in your boots."

"I did sleep in my boots."

"I know. I can tell. Drink anyway."

Jude drank. The tea was black and bitter and excellent, and it occurred to them, absurdly, that this was the most anyone had spoken to them in weeks. That was the shape of their life: deliveries. Hand a thing to a person, watch them take it, leave. They knew the city door by door and knew no one in any of them. Two years of doors.

"The crate," Petra said. "When you carried it. Tell me everything. Not what happened — everything. What it felt like. Smelled like. Whether the weight shifted."

"It hummed," Jude said.

Her pen stopped.

"Not the crate. My satchel." Jude hesitated. This was the part they never told anyone, the part that got you looked at the way brokers look at cursed stock. But her tea was bitter and her fire was warm and she had put out two cups, and some traitorous part of them wanted to see what she did with the truth. "Every time I open it. A low note. Like a bell wrapped in cloth. Only when it opens. Never when it's shut, never when anyone else carries it. I've tested. I gave it to Tallow for a week and he heard nothing. It only sings for me."

For a long moment Petra Dunmore didn't move. Then she set down her pen very carefully, aligning it parallel to the ledger's edge, and Jude watched her do it and understood that she was doing it so she wouldn't do something else.

"When did it start?" she asked.

"When I started. As far as I know. First day I woke up in the loft with two years of me and nothing else, and the satchel hanging on the hook like it had been waiting."

"And you never reported it."

"To whom? Master Vellam says we don't ask where our people come from." Jude turned the cup in their hands. "You want to know the funny part, auditor? Everyone here treats my missing past like it's polite not to mention it. Like it's a limp, or a bad debt. Nobody ever thought maybe I *wanted* it mentioned. Maybe I'd trade every miracle in your vaults for one hour of whoever I was before." They heard their own voice go flat and stopped talking.

Petra was quiet for a moment. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — the ledger-voice set aside, something plainer underneath it.

"I keep accounts," she said. "Every entry balanced, every debt chased, every miracle weighed and priced. Do you know why?"

"Because it's your job."

"Because fifteen years ago I lost an entire book." She looked at the shelves, at the hundreds of spines. "My order. The swordspeakers. Every one of them dead but me, and every one of them in my memory, and I am the only ledger left that records them. So I write everything down. Everything. Because writing is how I refuse to lose a second book." Her tea-colored eyes came back to Jude. "And you're telling me you're a book someone tore the pages out of, and everyone in this building has agreed not to notice."

The stove ticked. Somewhere below, the exchange floor murmured its morning arithmetic.

"Yes," Jude said. It came out smaller than they meant it to.

"Well," said Petra Dunmore, and picked up her pen, and was brisk again so quickly Jude wondered if they'd imagined it. "Then we have two problems, yours and mine, and I suspect they share a root. Here's what happens now. You're reassigned to me, effective this morning, at double rate. You'll carry nothing without my seal. If the satchel sings, you'll tell me the note. If you remember anything — anything at all, a smell, a word, a face — you'll bring it to me before you bring it to anyone else. Including Vellam." A pause. "Especially Vellam."

"Why especially him? He's kind to me. He found me."

"He found you," Petra repeated, softly, the way a broker repeats a suspicious number. "Yes. He told me that himself, more than once. More times than a fact needs telling." She wrote something, folded the page, and held it out — then didn't let go when Jude took it, so that for a moment they were connected by one sheet of paper and neither letting go nor pulling away. "One more question, courier, and answer it honestly, because I'll be checking your pulse while you do. When you opened the satchel this morning — did it sing?"

"I didn't open it this morning."

"Did you want to?"

Jude thought about the note behind their sternum. About the dream of the furious letter, four nights running. About the wall of white paper where a life should be.

"Yes," they said.

Her fingers released the page. On it, in that upright, unlying hand, was a single line: *Parcel 4471-C was not lost. It was collected. Find out by whom, and you find out what you are.*

"How do you know it was collected?" Jude asked.

"Because I burned one page of the winter ledger yesterday," Petra said, "and I've regretted it since, and the entry underneath it was written in a hand that isn't mine and isn't any broker's I know. Someone is filing miracles under my signature." She stood, buttoning her coat, all business, but at the door she paused with her hand on the frame and didn't quite look back. "Eat the rest of the toast. And Jude — the next time it sings, come find me. Whatever hour. I sleep badly anyway."

She left. Jude sat alone in the swordspeaker's warm, cluttered office with cooling tea and a page that called them a mystery, and realized they were smiling, and had been for some time, and could not remember — for once, for the first time in two blank years — minding what they couldn't remember.

Outside, far off across the frozen rooftops, a bell rang the ninth hour, and in the loft above the stables, unopened and unwatched, the satchel began — very softly — to hum.
