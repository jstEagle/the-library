## 3. What the Satchel Sang

The satchel sat on my desk for six hours before I touched it.

I want that on the record — mine, the one I keep in my head, the one no guild clerk can alter. Six hours. I catalogued it, weighed it, measured it, and wrote it up in triplicate like any other unclaimed parcel: *one courier's satchel, grey canvas, brass fittings, contents unknown, delivered to the Guild of Impossible Things by its own courier under circumstances the courier cannot recall.*

Jude couldn't recall. That was the trouble. The best courier this house has produced in a generation had walked through our doors at dusk with a delivery they'd opened somewhere on the road between Greyford and the city walls, and whatever they'd seen had taken two years of their life with it. Not knocked loose — *taken*. I've handled memory-loss before. It comes back ragged, in pieces, like a book left out in rain. Jude's past didn't come back at all. They remembered the guild. They remembered me, vaguely, as "the woman with the ledgers." Everything before two years ago was simply gone, cut clean, the way you'd trim a wick.

And the satchel hummed.

Not loudly. You had to put your ear near the flap and hold still, and then you heard it — a sound like a struck glass that never quite finished ringing. Brass doesn't do that. Canvas doesn't do that. Nothing in the material world does that, which is why it went into the vault overnight and came out to my desk at dawn, because Master Vellam said, with his usual terrible gentleness, *"You have the steadiest hands in the house, Petra. And the oldest oaths."*

I should have heard the warning in that. Vellam never says anything without three meanings folded inside it, like a letter passed twice through enemy lines.

* * *

Six hours of inventory first. This is how I keep myself sane: procedure. The Guild of Impossible Things runs on the fiction that miracles are just goods with unusual provenance, and I am the woman who makes the fiction true. Every impossible thing that passes through our vaults gets a ledger line, a valuation, a chain of custody. A bottled curse is an asset. A saint's fingerbone is collateral. A god's favor, if anyone were mad enough to trade one, would be entered under Livestock.

I know how that sounds. I wrote the classification system.

So: hour one, I checked the satchel against courier manifests. No entry. Hour two, I had it x-rayed by our resident bone-reader, old Mother Quill, who pressed it to her forehead and immediately developed a nosebleed and a headache and an expression I can only describe as *religious embarrassment*. She told me, wiping blood from her upper lip with tremendous dignity, that there was something inside the bag that was "larger than the bag."

"Physically?"

"No." She handed it back like it might bite. "In every other direction."

Hour three, I tried opening it. The buckle turned freely. The flap lifted an inch and stopped, not caught, not locked — *stopped*, the way a door stops when someone on the other side is holding it shut. I wrote that down too. My handwriting got smaller.

Hours four and five, I did what any sensible broker does when faced with an item that resists inventory: I made tea and read the file on the sender. There wasn't one. The parcel had entered our system the way prayers used to enter temples — unbidden, unsigned, addressed in a hand that shifted style halfway down the label as though two people had written it, or one person writing in two voices.

Addressed, incidentally, to me. Not to the guild. To *P. Dunmore, Keeper of Ledgers, who will know what to do.* Someone out there knew my name and thought flattery would move me. Flattery usually does.

Hour six, I ran out of procedure.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about oaths: they don't die when the thing they bound dies. They just go quiet. I took the swordspeaker oath at nineteen, kneeling in a hall full of burning candles while Commander Ashe laid the flat of her blade on my shoulder and spoke the litany — *speak a true name, bind a true thing; hear a true name, answer it* — and for fifteen years since, the order has been ash and the gods have been silent and the oath has been a scar I could recite but not feel.

Except.

There's a spot behind my sternum where the oath lives. Has lived, dormant, all these years, like a hook set in meat. And from the moment the satchel came through our doors, that hook had been pulling. Faintly. The way a fishing line pulls when something enormous circles far below the boat and hasn't yet decided about the bait.

Mother Quill felt nothing. The bone-readers felt nothing. The vault wards, which have caught demons, debts, and one genuinely catastrophic love potion, registered the satchel as *empty canvas, value nil*.

But I am the last oath-bound swordspeaker in a city that burned my order four centuries ago, and my oath heard the satchel the way a dog hears a whistle pitched above the human range. It heard, and it pulled, and after six hours I understood that procedure was finished with me and I was not finished with the satchel.

I unbuckled it.

* * *

The flap gave this time. No resistance, no drama — it opened like any bag opens, and I confess I felt foolish, sitting alone in my office with cold tea and a nosebleeded bone-reader's warnings, expecting the apocalypse from a courier's satchel.

Inside: packing straw. A oilcloth wrapping, tied with plain twine. And under the oilcloth, resting in the straw like an egg in a nest —

A name.

I know how that sentence sounds. Let me be precise, because precision is the only prayer I've ever trusted. It was a piece of paper. Folded — no, *compressed*, folded so many times and so finely that it had become a small dense square the size of a coin, smooth as river stone, warm to the touch the way a held hand is warm. Someone had folded a written name down past sense, past paper's right to complain, until the writing itself was squeezed into something you couldn't read but could *feel*, like a word shouted through a wall.

Nine folds. I counted them as I opened them. Nine. Remember that number; it matters later, or it mattered already, depending on how you keep time in this business.

The paper unfolded with a sound like a long-held breath released. And there was writing on it — not ink. The letters stood slightly off the surface, the way frost stands on glass, silver-white and faintly moving, and they were in no alphabet I knew and yet I could read them the way you can read shouting without hearing words.

One name. One god's name, folded small enough to fit in a palm.

The gods have been silent for four hundred years. Everyone knows this. The churches stand empty or repurposed — there's a very popular tavern in what used to be the Temple of the Tide, and I'm told the ale is excellent. Four hundred years of silence, four hundred years of philosophers explaining why the silence was always inevitable, four hundred years of the Guild of Impossible Things quietly making a living off the gap the gods left behind. We sell substitutes. Rain-charms instead of rain-gods. Memory-wine instead of ancestor-counsel. Curses with excellent customer service.

Nobody smuggles gods. You can't. They're not things; they're *weathers*. When they went, they went entirely, and the world adjusted its shipping routes accordingly.

I looked at the name on the paper, and my professional mind did what it always does: reached for a ledger line. *Unknown item. Organic? No. Mineral? No. Provenance:* and here the pen hovered over the page and refused to write, because provenance requires an origin and this thing's origin was a category we'd closed four centuries ago.

And because I am a fool who talks to herself over paperwork, I read it aloud.

I didn't mean to speak it properly. I meant to mouth it, sound it out, the way you'd test a foreign word. But the oath behind my sternum *pulled*, hard, all at once, like a hand closing around the hook, and my mouth shaped the syllables fully, truly, aloud — the first true name spoken in this building since before it was a guildhall, since it was a temple, since it was young.

I won't write the name here. Some entries don't belong in ledgers. But I'll tell you what it did.

Every candle in the guildhall leaned toward me.

We keep forty-one candles burning in the main hall — guild tradition, one for each founder and one for the founder nobody talks about. Wax candles, tallow candles, two expensive ones that burn underwater for reasons involving a mermaid debt. Forty-one flames, and every single one of them bent toward the doorway of my office at the same instant, like grass before wind, except there was no wind, and they didn't flicker. They *leaned*. Steady. Attentive. The way a room full of people leans when someone important has just walked in.

Then, one by one, they straightened. And stayed brighter than they should have been for the rest of the day.

I sat very still with a god's name in my open palm and listened to my own heart conduct its panicked audit. Because here's the thing, the thing that turned my dread into something worse and better: the candles weren't the miracle. Candles are stupid. Fire goes where fire's told. What scared me was that the flame *recognized* the name — responded to it the way iron responds to a lodestone — which means the name was real, which means the god it belonged to was real, or had been, or was being *reassembled*, which means the silence of four hundred years is not a law of nature but a condition, and conditions can change.

Someone is smuggling the gods home. One stolen name at a time. Through my guild. Into my ledgers.

* * *

I did three things in the next hour, and I've had a year to regret exactly none of them, so perhaps they were right.

First, I refolded the name. Nine folds, in reverse, and my hands shook so badly on the fourth fold that I had to press them flat on the desk and breathe like Commander Ashe taught me — *four counts in, four counts out, fear is just excitement that hasn't found its job* — before continuing. When the name was small again, I didn't put it back in the satchel. I put it in my bodice, against my skin, because some part of me — the oath part, the part I'd spent fifteen years pretending was a scar — wanted it close, and I've learned to trust the parts of myself that don't ask permission.

Second, I sealed the satchel in a report box marked *pending valuation* and locked it with my personal seal, which no clerk in the house can break without my blood on the lock. Chain of custody. If the smuggling was happening through my guild, then my guild's books were either compromised or complicit, and either way, from this moment forward, I was the chain.

Third, I went down to the main hall and stood among the forty-one candles for a long time, watching them burn brighter than they should.

That's when I noticed the detail that finished the job. Candle thirty-seven — the one for the unnamed founder, the one that's been allowed to gutter half-dead for as long as anyone remembers, because the founders themselves decreed it — was burning tall. Brighter than the others. Leaning, even now, very slightly, toward the stairwell that leads down to the vaults.

Toward wherever the rest of the names were hidden.

I stood in the candlelight with a god warming itself against my ribs and understood, with the terrible clarity that only comes after the fear has finished its work, that I had a choice dressed up as a duty. Report the parcel to the masters, and let the institution decide what an institution decides — which is to say, whatever keeps the institution intact. Or follow it myself, off the books, out of the ledgers, into the dark below the stairs where candle thirty-seven was pointing.

Commander Ashe would have reported. Commander Ashe believed in the wall more than the people standing on it, and she died defending a wall for a god who never answered, and I have spent fifteen years being loyal to that mistake.

My oath stirred against my sternum. *Hear a true name,* it reminded me, patient as stone. *Answer it.*

I hadn't spoken a true name in fifteen years. I had just spoken one by accident, in a guildhall, and the whole building had turned to listen. Whatever was being assembled out there in the dark — name by folded name, altar by scraped-clean altar — my oath could hear it coming. Was built to hear it. Maybe, if I'm honest with myself in the way I'm honest nowhere else, was kept alive all these years *for* it.

Dread is just excitement that hasn't found its job.

I found mine.

I went back upstairs, took out a fresh ledger — not an official one, one with no heading, no house mark, a book that officially does not exist — and made my first entry in a private hand:

*Item: one god's name, folded nine times. Location: on my person. Status: awake. Action required: find the rest before someone finds them first.*

Below it, because the pen was already moving and because I am apparently the kind of fool who writes the truth when no one's checking:

*Note: the courier Jude remembers nothing, and the satchel was theirs. Coincidence is a word we use for patterns we haven't priced yet.*

I was still looking at that last line when someone knocked at my office door — two soft knocks, hesitant, the knock of a person who isn't sure they're allowed to exist.

Through the wood, a voice I'd heard only once, at the interview, asking lost questions about a missing parcel:

"I remembered something," Jude said. "About what was in the bag. And I think — I think you're going to want to sit down."
