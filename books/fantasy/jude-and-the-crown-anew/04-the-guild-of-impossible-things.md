## 4. The Guild of Impossible Things

**PETRA**

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its rules on a single brass plaque by the door, and the plaque was wrong in three places.

Petra had pointed this out to Master Vellam when she was nineteen. He had laughed and said the plaque was not a rulebook but a decoy — the real rules were taught by apprenticeship, by failure, by the particular way a broker's hand shook when they touched something that shouldn't be for sale. She had believed him then. She still mostly did.

*One: We sell what cannot be sold.*

*Two: We buy what cannot be bought.*

*Three: The guild takes no side in any war, divine or otherwise.*

That third one had been added after the Godswar, hammered into the doorframe like a nail through a coffin lid. Four hundred years of silence from the old gods, and the guild had built an empire on the echo.

Today she walked the floors with Jude at her elbow, ostensibly showing the new courier how the house worked, actually keeping them where she could see them. A courier without yesterday, carrying a satchel that sang when opened. The audit notes in her coat pocket felt heavier than paper should.

"The ground floor is trade," she said. "Watch your hands, your tongue, and your eyes, in that order."

Jude watched their hands. Good instinct. They walked with the flat-footed care of someone who had learned recently that the world could take things from you without warning.

The trading floor opened up beneath the dome like the inside of a bell. Brokers sat at long scarred tables under lamps of green glass, and on every table lay something impossible: a bottled Tuesday, still damp; a debt owed by a mountain; a love letter written by a woman to her own younger self, sealed with wax and regret. Buyers moved between the tables with the hushed shuffle of people in churches or hospitals, which Petra supposed were the same thing.

"What's that one?" Jude asked, nodding toward a table where an old broker named Cassiline presided over a jar of fog that occasionally shaped itself into a face.

"Grief," Petra said. "Condensed. You can sell yours to her and she'll hold it for a year, or you can buy someone else's if you're the sentimental type. She does steady business in spring."

"And the guild takes a cut?"

"The guild takes a cut of everything. That's rule four." She paused. "Unwritten rule four."

They passed the memory exchange, where drawers within drawers held other people's childhoods in wax cylinders, labeled in a clerk's careful hand: *first snow, age six, slight melancholy, will not repeat*. They passed the curse desk, where a man argued over the return policy on a hex he'd purchased for his brother and now, with the brother dead, found surplus to requirements. They passed the prayer intake window — shuttered since before Petra's time, its brass slot green with verdigris — and she felt Jude's shoulder tense beside hers without either of them saying why.

"Upper floors are appraisal, binding, and storage," she said, steering them toward the stair. "Lower floors are the vaults. And the vaults have one rule above all others."

"Which is?"

"You don't go down alone. Not because of what's down there." She met their eyes. "Because of what you might decide, alone, that you deserve."

Jude's mouth twitched. "You rehearse these speeches."

"I've given them forty times to forty couriers who all thought they were the exception."

"Was I the exception?"

"No," Petra said. "You were worse. You didn't even ask permission first."

But there was no heat in it, and Jude knew it, and the walk down the spiral stair had the strange companionable silence of two people who had already stopped pretending they weren't curious about each other.

* * *

**JUDE**

The vaults smelled of cold stone and old decisions.

Jude followed Petra down past levels marked in chalk — *SEASONAL, RESTRICTED, HERESY (PENDING REVIEW)* — and tried to ignore the way the air thickened as they descended, like wading into water that remembered being deep. Their satchel hung quiet against their hip today. He had checked it three times before leaving his room. Quiet was better than humming. Humming meant wanting things.

At the fourth level down, Petra stopped at a gate of black iron and pressed her palm to it. Somewhere inside the metal, something tasted her blood through the skin and decided, grudgingly, to allow it.

"Storage for items of contested divinity," she said. "Or what the founders' charter calls 'unresolved theological inventory.' There's a whole wing of prayers nobody answered, back from the silent years. People paid us to keep them safe from disappointment."

"Do they ever get answered late?"

"They get catalogued." Petra's voice was level, but her jaw did something small and hard. "Come on. I want you to see the ledger room."

The ledger room was the true heart of the guild, more than any vault. It was a round chamber lined floor to ceiling with books — thousands of them, spines cracked, corners soft with handling — each one a year, each page a transaction. Petra ran her fingers along a shelf the way another person might touch a lover's sleeve.

"Everything we've ever done is written here," she said. "Every miracle brokered, every curse resold, every memory pawned and redeemed. When I die, they'll bury me somewhere near the S shelf, if I'm lucky."

"You love it here."

It wasn't a question, and it caught her off guard — Jude saw the flicker of it, a woman surprised at being seen. "I trust it here," she corrected. "Love implies it loves you back. Ledgers don't love you back. They just don't lie to you, which after fifteen years starts to feel like the same thing."

Jude understood that more than they could say. Two years ago was a wall with nothing behind it. Before the wall: nothing, not even dark. But the ledgers of their own body still worked — hands that remembered knots, feet that remembered roads, a reflex in the wrist that flinched toward knives. Someone had written those pages. Jude just couldn't read the handwriting anymore.

"Can I ask you something improper?" Jude said.

"You're a courier. Everything you say is technically improper."

"When you interviewed me. About the missing parcel." They watched her face carefully. "You looked at me like you recognized something. What was it?"

Petra was quiet long enough that the ledger room's silence got loud. Then she said, "Loneliness keeps books too. I've learned to spot the format."

Before Jude could decide whether that was the kindest or cruelest thing anyone had said to them in two years, the iron gate groaned open above, and footsteps came down the spiral stair with the unhurried confidence of a man who owned the staircase and everything it had ever been attached to.

Master Vellam descended into view like weather.

He was seventy if he was a day, broad as a doorway, with a white beard trimmed to the exact length of institutional respectability and eyes the pale grey of coins worn smooth by passing through too many hands. He wore the guildmaster's chain of office loosely, the way he seemed to wear everything — authority included.

"There you are," Vellam boomed, warm as a hearth. "Dunmore giving you the grand tour, courier? Showing you where the bodies are filed?"

"Alphabetically," Petra said. "By cause."

Vellam laughed, big and easy, and clapped a hand on Jude's shoulder with a weight that was either affection or assessment. Jude couldn't tell which, and noticed that they minded.

"Now, don't let her bore you with the vaults," Vellam said. "Stone and locks. The real guild is upstairs, in the noise. Come find me later, courier — I'll teach you which brokers water their miracles and which ones pour honest stock. That knowledge is worth more than any satchel."

"I'll hold you to that," Jude said, and smiled, and hated how easily the smile came. Some skills survived amnesia. Flattery, apparently, was load-bearing masonry.

Vellam turned to Petra then, and his voice dropped half a register, casual as a man asking about lunch.

"Oh — before I forget. You finished the winter audit? Found anything... irregular?"

There it was. Jude heard it — a hairline crack in the warmth, thin as a wire. The question asked too lightly. The pause on *irregular*, half a beat too long, like a coin spun to see which face landed.

Petra's face did nothing at all, which was itself an answer only Jude was positioned to read. "Three entries out of eleven thousand," she said. "Clerical errors. I've flagged them."

"Clerical errors." Vellam nodded slowly, and his eyes — those smooth worn-coin eyes — rested on her a moment longer than the sentence required. "In this house? Imagine that." Another laugh, softer now. "Well. Flag them, flag them. Paper forgives, that's what it's for. Unlike people."

He climbed back up the stairs, whistling something old and tuneless, and the gate closed behind him with a sound like a full stop.

* * *

**PETRA**

She waited until the footsteps faded entirely before she exhaled.

"That," she said quietly, "was the most dangerous thing that has happened in this building all week."

"He seems kind," Jude said.

"He is kind. That's the problem." Petra pulled her audit notes from her coat and looked at them without really seeing them. "Vellam taught me everything. How to read a contract for the lie hidden in the punctuation. How to price a miracle so the buyer feels robbed and grateful in the same breath. How to sit in a room with a god's leftover shadow and not blink." She tucked the notes away. "And in twenty years I have never once known him to ask about my work. He trusts me absolutely. So when he asks — casually, on a stairwell, in front of a witness—"

"He's checking," Jude said.

"He's checking whether I'll lie to him." She turned to look at the shelves of ledgers, all those years of honest ink. "And the worst part, courier, the part that's going to keep me up tonight — I told him clerical errors. Which means I lied. Which means whatever game this is, I just made my first move without meaning to."

Jude leaned against a shelf, arms crossed. "Maybe he's protecting you. Maybe whoever's smuggling those names would hurt you if you looked too close, and he's standing between you and it."

"Then he can tell me that plainly, and I'll believe him, because I always believe him, and that—" Her voice snagged on something. "That is exactly why I can't afford to."

The lamp above them guttered once. In the vaults below, faintly, through four floors of stone, something shifted in its sleep — or turned over, listening. Jude's satchel stayed mercifully silent, but Jude's hand went to it anyway, the way a person touches a wound they've decided not to look at yet.

Petra straightened her coat. Professional calm slid back over her like a visor coming down.

"Tour's over," she said. "Go home, courier. Sleep. Don't open the satchel, don't talk to brokers, and if anyone asks you what's in your bag again—"

"It won't be casually," Jude said.

"No," Petra agreed grimly. "Next time, it won't be casual at all."

She was halfway up the stairs when the thought arrived, cold and complete, the way the worst thoughts do: *Vellam asked me what I found. He never asked what I burned.*

Only three people alive could have known a page had gone missing from the winter ledger at all.

And she had lied to one of them on a staircase, in front of the second, while the third stood somewhere in this building, holding nine stolen names and a head start.
