## 5. Nine Names Missing

The debt was three hundred crowns and one apology, which in the guild's books counted as a single line item. I read the ledger slip twice on the way out of Petra's office because her handwriting had a way of making even apologies sound like arithmetic.

*Collect from the Temple of the Ninefold Dawn, Greyford Reach. Principal: three hundred crowns. Interest: one apology, delivered aloud. Note: the temple has been a ruin for two centuries. Collect anyway.*

"Collect from a ruin," I said.

"The ruin still owes," Petra said without looking up. She had ink on her left knuckle and didn't seem to know it. "Impossible Things means impossible debts too. The founders were very clear."

"And if nobody's home?"

"Then you'll have a nice walk and I'll have an entry that balances." She did look up then, briefly, and something in her face made me think she wasn't as bored by this errand as she sounded. "Jude. If anything out there feels wrong — not dangerous, just *wrong* — you turn around. That's not a request, it's a clause. Clause nine."

"I don't remember agreeing to clauses."

"You agreed to wages. Wages come with clauses."

I took the slip and went, because she was right about the wages, and because there was a part of me — the two-years-old part, the only part I owned outright — that wanted to see what a god's house looked like after everyone stopped believing in it.

* * *

Greyford Reach was a day's ride north, most of it through country where the hills had given up pretending to be gentle. The temple sat on a shelf of rock above a dry riverbed, and even at half a mile away I could tell something was off about it.

Ruins lean. That's what ruins do; they sag into themselves like old men in chairs. This one stood straight. Every wall plumb, every arch true, the whole structure holding its shape with the stubbornness of a building that refuses to accept it's dead. It should have been comforting. It was the opposite. A ruin standing straight is a ruin that's being maintained, or a ruin that won't let go.

I tied my horse at the base of the steps. The satchel hummed against my hip.

It had been doing that more lately — the low note, almost below hearing, like a struck bell heard through a wall. I'd learned to ignore it the way you ignore your own heartbeat, but here, with the temple filling my view, the hum sharpened into something with intent. Not a warning exactly. More like a dog turning its head toward a door before anyone knocks.

The doors were stone and open, and had been for a long time, because the hinge-stones were worn into smooth cups by feet that hadn't passed this way in living memory.

Inside: a nave, long and cold, lit by holes in the roof that dropped coins of afternoon light onto a floor of white tile. And along both walls, evenly spaced, nine altars.

I walked the first one slowly. It was granite, chest-high, carved with a sunburst whose rays had been chiseled away. Not broken — *cut*. Someone had taken a tool to each ray and removed them cleanly, leaving smooth channels where the carving used to be. At the altar's heart was a recess the size of an open book, and inside the recess, nothing. Just a shadow shaped like absence.

The second altar was worse. Its god had apparently been a river-mother or a rain-giver, something with flowing lines, and whoever had cut it out had worked fast and sloppy, gouging deep. But they had gotten all of it. There wasn't a curve left, not a suggestion of the figure that must have been there. It was like reading a page where every word had been razored out but the paper kept.

Altars three through eight told the same story nine ways. Cut, cut, cut, cut. Whoever had done this had been thorough, and patient, and — this was the part that put a cold finger on the back of my neck — *careful*. You don't scrape a name off an altar carefully unless the name matters. You don't do it at all unless you're afraid of what happens when the name is spoken.

I reached the ninth altar and stopped.

This one hadn't been scraped. This one had been *opened*. The recess at its heart was wider than the others, wide enough for a body to lie in — and someone had, once, because the stone floor beneath it was stained dark in a human outline, arms spread, head tilted. Around the outline, cut into the tile in a circle, ran a single line of writing in no alphabet I knew, though my eyes followed it easily, the way they follow any text.

*We gave him up so he could be given again.*

I stood there a while. The light moved. My hands were steady, which seemed like the wrong response and I noted it the way I note everything strange about myself — filed under the growing category of *things Jude does not remember learning*.

Because here's what I know about myself: I woke up two years ago on the guild's doorstep with a satchel, a knife, and no yesterday. I know how to read eleven scripts and can't remember being taught. I know how to swear oaths in three dead languages. And I know, in the specific unhelpful way of the body rather than the mind, exactly where every blade in a room is pointing at any given moment.

Which is why, when the air behind me changed — when the temperature dropped four degrees and the dust on the tiles began, very quietly, to drift *upward* instead of down — I already had the knife out of my boot.

* * *

It came through the ninth altar's recess the way smoke comes back down a chimney: reluctantly, and all at once.

It wasn't a shape so much as a disagreement with the light. The coin of sun falling across the stained outline bent around something tall and thin, and the something turned toward me, and where its face should have been there was the same smooth absence I'd seen carved into the altars. Nine times over, multiplied. It had been emptied the way the altars had been emptied, and it had come here because this was the last place it remembered being full.

Every instinct I couldn't account for said: *don't run.* Running turns you into prey. Prey is a story with an ending.

So I didn't run. I sidestepped, slow, putting a fallen pillar between us, and I said — in a language I had no right to know, a language with gravel in its vowels:

*"You are owed. I am not the debtor."*

The thing stopped.

For a moment the nave was so quiet I could hear my own blood. Then it made a sound — not a voice, more like a page tearing very slowly — and drifted forward, and I understood with terrible clarity that whatever words I had were a stay of execution, not a pardon. It was hungry in the way only an emptied thing is hungry. It wanted a name. Any name. Mine would do.

It came around the pillar faster than smoke should move, and my body did something my mind had no part in.

I don't remember deciding to fight. I remember the space between decisions: the knife leaving my hand at the exact moment its edge would matter, my weight dropping onto my back foot, my free hand finding a loose tile and sending it spinning toward where the thing's knees would be if it had knees — not to hit, but to make it flinch, because everything flinches, and a flinch is a door. Then I was moving through the door, past it, close enough to smell it (cold iron and old rain), and my knife-hand came back around with a grip I've never practiced and a cut I've never been taught, a short rising stroke that opened a line across the thing's midsection.

It shouldn't have worked. It was smoke and absence; knives don't argue with smoke.

But where the blade crossed it, the smoke *parted*, and underneath there was something solid, something with texture like dried skin stretched over a drum, and it screamed — the page-tear sound again, torn all the way through now — and recoiled into the ninth altar's recess and was gone. Not defeated. Withdrawn. There's a difference, and the difference was going to matter later, probably at a bad time.

I stood in the ruined nave with my heart slamming and my knife steady in a hand that belonged to someone I'd never met, and I laughed. Once, high, not entirely sane. Because I'd known the stance. I'd known the language. Somewhere in the two blank years before the guild's doorstep, someone had taught me how to talk things like that out of eating me, and then how to gut them when the talking failed, and that someone had also taught me never to tell anyone any of this.

Whoever trained me had trained me to survive people asking questions.

* * *

I collected the debt. That sounds absurd; let me explain.

In the ninth altar's recess, behind the stain, wedged into a crack in the stone, was a clay seal stamped with the guild's mark — the impossible knot, the one we press into every contract. Old wax, older than the guild's current charter. Somebody had left a token here long ago. Maybe a courier. Maybe me; I couldn't swear I hadn't stood on these tiles before, and the thought made my skin crawl pleasantly, the way an itch does when you can't reach it.

I took the seal. On the ride back I drafted the entry in my head: *Debt partially collected. One guild-seal recovered from ruin. Apology outstanding; recipient deceased approximately two hundred years. Recommend carrying balance forward indefinitely.*

Petra would hate it. Petra hated entries that didn't resolve. It was one of the things I liked best about her, the way she treated untidy numbers as personal insults. I found myself composing her reaction the whole ride home — the small frown, the tap of her pen, the way she'd say *balance requires a counterweight, Jude* — and caught myself smiling at a horse's ears, and decided not to examine that either.

The light was failing when Greyford's walls came into view, and that's when I noticed the second rider.

Far back on the road, at the exact distance where details stop, a figure on a grey horse. I checked it the way couriers check: glance, count to fifty, glance again. Still there. Same distance. When our road curved, it curved. When we forded the stream, it waited at the far bank, patient as a debt.

I tried the trick from the temple — the sideways watch, the counting — and got nothing new except a growing certainty shaped like cold water: it wasn't following me.

It was following the satchel.

At the city gate I paid my toll, rode through, and made myself walk the horse at a civilized pace to the guildhall, because running tells followers what you know. Inside the courtyard I handed off to the stable-boy, climbed the stairs two at a time, and knocked on Petra's door hard enough to rattle the ink bottles.

She opened it mid-sentence, some complaint already loaded, and stopped when she saw my face.

"Clause nine?" she said.

"Worse than clause nine." I came in, shut the door, and put the clay seal on her desk between the ledgers. "Nine altars in Greyford, scraped clean. Names cut out like pages. And something living in the empty ones — I cut it and it bled, Petra, smoke bled — and it followed me home. It's outside the walls right now, or it was an hour ago, sitting on the road like a bill collector." I pulled off the satchel and set it next to the seal. It hummed against the wood, louder than I'd ever heard it. "And I need you to tell me why a courier who woke up two years ago knowing nothing knows eleven dead languages and seven ways to kill a god-shaped thing in a temple."

Petra looked at the seal for a long moment. Then she looked at the satchel. Then she did something I hadn't seen her do before: she crossed to the window, checked the street below, and drew the shutter closed, and the room went dim and close as a held breath.

"Sit down," she said quietly. "Both of us need to sit down, because I burned a page out of the winter ledger last week, and until tonight I told myself I regretted it." Her eyes came up to mine, and there was dread in them, yes, but underneath the dread something worse and brighter, the look of a woman whose worst suspicion had just been confirmed and who was already, helplessly, making plans. "Jude. Tell me exactly what the thing looked like when you cut it. Start with what was underneath."

"Underneath?"

"When you opened it." She was already reaching for a fresh page. "What did it keep *inside*?"

And I realized I was going to have to say it out loud: the drum-tight skin, the shape beneath the smoke — the shape of a person, arms spread, lying in a recess built to hold a god.

"It looked like a vessel," I said. "It looked like somebody's insides had been taken out and the rest left walking."

Petra's pen stopped.

"That," she said, "is what I was afraid of," and outside the shuttered window, faint and patient, something hummed the same note as the satchel.
