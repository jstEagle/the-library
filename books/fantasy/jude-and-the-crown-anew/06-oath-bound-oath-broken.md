## 6. Oath-Bound, Oath-Broken

The road up to the Swordfield took two days on a mule the guild rented me under the category *livestock, sentimental purposes only*. Master Vellam had signed the requisition slip himself without asking where I was going, which should have comforted me and didn't. Vellam never asked questions he already knew the answers to. That was the trouble with mentors: eventually you learned their silences like handwriting.

I told myself I was traveling for the audit's sake. A swordspeaker's grave is an archive, after all. The dead keep records the living can't be trusted with.

That was true. It just wasn't the whole truth, and I have never been able to lie cleanly in my own head. One more reason they burned out my oath.

* * *

The Swordfield sits in a bowl of hills three days north of Corivale, where the last battle of the Godswar was fought and where the last order of swordspeakers was buried — not because we died there, but because we were born there, in the mud, in the hour when the gods stopped answering and someone had to speak for them.

Four hundred graves. I knew the number before I crested the hill, the way you know the number of stairs in your own house. Four hundred stones, each carved with a name and a single line of the Oath, so that even in death a speaker lay bound to something true.

Commander Ashka Verey's stone stood at the center, as it always had, weathered now to the color of old milk. I had not come in fifteen years. Fifteen years since I spoke my last true name, laid down my sword, and walked into the Guild of Impossible Things to do bookkeeping for miracles instead of performing them. Fifteen years since anyone called me by the title that used to sit behind my name like a drawn blade.

I tied the mule to the fence post — the fence was new; someone still tended this place, though I couldn't imagine who — and walked the rows until I reached her.

Then I sat down in the wet grass, the way I had sat down as a recruit twenty years ago, and said, "Commander. It's Dunmore. I've done something wrong."

* * *

You have to understand what the oath was, or nothing I did next will make sense to you.

People think swordspeakers were warriors. We were not, or not primarily. A sword cuts things apart. Our work was the opposite. In the last century of the Godswar, when the old gods were dying by attrition — forgotten name by forgotten name — the orders arose to keep them alive. Not with worship. With *binding*.

Speak a true name, bind a true thing. That was the whole of it, and it was everything.

A god's name, spoken truly, could not be unspoken. It anchored the god to the world the way a keel anchors a ship. While one speaker lived who carried a name, that god could not wholly die. We were not priests. Priests ask. We *held*. Each of us memorized names the way other soldiers memorized drill — dozens of them, hundreds, syllable-sequences that would boil the tongue of anyone unworthy, that lived in the memory like iron lives in blood.

And here is the part no ledger records, the part I confessed to no one when I left: the binding ran both ways. To hold a god's name was to give the god a hook in your own mind. They leaned on you. They remembered through you. Some nights I woke with snow falling inside my skull and knew it was Ullr-the-Quiet dreaming, and I had never met Ullr-the-Quiet, and he was dead four hundred years, and still he dreamed, and still I felt it.

Fifteen names, I held, at my peak. Fifteen gods alive in the back rooms of my head.

Then the Concord came, and the church, and the peace, and the slow consensus that the Godswar had been terrible enough that no god should ever again walk the world wearing power. The orders were dissolved. The speakers were given a choice: burn your names and live free, or keep them and be watched forever.

I burned mine. All fifteen, in a brazier, in front of witnesses, while Commander Verey stood beside me with her hand on my shoulder like she was holding down a lid.

She chose differently. She kept hers. She said someone should remember how it was done, in case the world ever needed doing again.

She died eleven years later, and whatever she kept died with her, and I told myself for a long time that this was mercy.

* * *

"Three entries," I said to the gravestone. My voice sounded strange in the open air, unused to carrying anything heavier than small talk. "Winter ledgers. Prayers answered in Corivale, paid out against the miracle account. Someone is moving god-names through my guild, Commander, and folding them small enough to fit in a satchel, and every candle in the hall leaned toward me when one slipped loose, which means my ears still work, which means the oath isn't gone from me, only sleeping."

The wind moved through the grass. Somewhere downslope the mule complained about the fence.

"I know what you'd ask first." I pulled a blade of grass and shredded it, an old nervous habit she'd once tried to beat out of me during drills. "You'd ask: reported or not. And the answer is no. I burned one page of the ledger, Commander. Page forty-one. Three lines. I fed it to the stove in the counting room and then I stood there feeling the ash settle and thought, *Petra, you are now either the guild's best auditor or its worst traitor, and you won't know which until you decide.*"

Because that was the arithmetic, and she had taught me the arithmetic. Report the smuggling, and the church learned the gods were returning, and the church had spent four centuries building institutions whose entire purpose was ensuring that never happened. There would be purges. There would be fires. Whether the smuggler meant well or ill, the discovery itself was the weapon.

Say nothing, and nine — I was nearly sure it was nine, from the scraped altars Jude described, and the number made my teeth ache in a way I refused to examine — nine names were already loose in the world, unbound, unhoused, drifting toward whoever gathered them. And someone *was* gathering them. Names don't move uphill on their own.

"Someone is rebuilding something," I said. "And I don't know yet if they're building a cradle or a cage."

* * *

Memory does what it likes at gravesides. Mine went back thirty years, to the training yard at the Swordfield, mud to the ankles, and Commander Verey walking the line of recruits with a wooden practice sword under her arm like a loaf of bread she was deciding whether to share.

"The oath," she said — I could hear it exactly, the gravel in it, the way she never raised her voice because she'd never once needed to. "Everyone thinks it's about power. Speak the true name and the god must obey. That's what the songs say. The songs are written by people who were never bound." She stopped in front of me, sixteen years old and soaked to the ribs. "Dunmore. What happens when you speak a true name?"

"You bind a true thing," I recited.

"Wrong. You bind yourself. The thing is already true. Truth doesn't need your help. The oath is a rope with two ends, girl, and you are holding both of them." She crouched to my eye level, and her eyes were gray and very patient. "So before you ever speak one, ask yourself: what am I willing to become, in order to hold what I'm about to hold? Because you will hold it for life. There is no setting down. There is only burning, and burning costs what it costs."

I asked her, that day, what she was willing to become.

She smiled, and it was the saddest smile I have ever seen on a living face. "Ask me at the end," she said.

At the end, she never gave me the chance to ask. She left me her sword, her ledger of bindings, and a letter I have read perhaps two hundred times, whose last line I will carry past every other memory I own:

*A name spoken in love binds gently. A name spoken in fear binds hard enough to break both ends of the rope. Choose which kind of speaker you are before the choosing chooses you.*

* * *

I sat with that letter in my coat pocket for a long time, not reading it, just weighing it.

The sun crawled west. The shadows of four hundred stones rotated around me like the spokes of some enormous patient wheel. I thought about Jude — I had been thinking about Jude more than was professionally comfortable, the way they'd flinched when I asked about yesterday, the way they'd said *I'm good at delivering things, apparently that's all I am,* with the flatness of someone reading their own obituary.

Two kinds of empty, I thought. Me, full of things I'd chosen to burn. Them, emptied by hands unknown. And somewhere between us, a folded name humming in a satchel like a bird in a box.

"Ashka," I said — using her given name, which I'd earned exactly once, the night before she died. "If you were sitting here, you'd tell me the oath never left me. You'd say the burning only took the names, not the ear. You'd say I heard that name in the candlelight because I am still, in whatever way matters, a speaker."

"And then," I went on, "you'd ask the question you always asked, which was the only question that ever mattered."

I asked it aloud, to the stone, to the hills, to whatever was left of her listening:

"*Who benefits?*

"Not the smuggler — smugglers profit in coin, and coin leaves traces, and whoever this is has left almost none. Not the church — they'd have burned the guildhall down already. Not the gods themselves; a god folded into a parcel is a god in chains, and I know what chained feels like from the inside.

"So: someone who wants the old gods *back* but not *free*. Someone rebuilding the machinery of the war years. Someone patient, someone inside, someone with access to my ledgers and my couriers and my—" 

I stopped.

My audit notes. My private working copies, locked in my desk, keyed to my own seal. Notes I had taken the night I found the entries, in shorthand only I could read.

I had left them in the guildhall. I had left them three days' ride behind me, in the building where the smuggler worked.

I stood up so fast my knees cracked. The mule looked over at me with the profound indifference of its kind.

"Commander," I said, backing away down the row of graves, "I'm going to need to report late."

* * *

But the road was long, and fear makes a poor traveling companion, so I made myself do what she trained me to do: think in bindings.

If someone was collecting names, they needed three things. A source — the scraped temples, the ruins where names lay sleeping in stone. A carrier — a courier who wouldn't ask questions, couldn't recognize what they held, and wouldn't remember deliveries even if they wanted to. And a buyer — because nobody gathers nine gods' names out of scholarship.

A source. A carrier. A buyer.

The carrier troubled me most, and not only because I liked them. Jude's missing two years weren't a tragedy, I realized somewhere past the second river crossing. They were a *modification*. Somebody had built themselves an ideal courier: skillful, loyal, and hollow. And either that somebody was the smuggler — convenient, careful, cruel in a tidy way —

— or the courier had been built for the *buyer*, and the smuggler had stolen the delivery system along with the goods.

Both possibilities turned my stomach. Both pointed back through my guild, through the winter ledgers, through the miracle account, to wherever the names changed hands.

Names move through middlemen. That was true of the miracle trade, and I'd have bet my sword-hand it was true of this. Nobody steals a god wholesale. You steal a god the way water moves — quietly, downhill, through channels cut long ago by people whose names the water has forgotten.

There was an old word for such channels. The trade still used it, in whispers, for the brokers who moved contraband miracles across borders: *tithes*. One-tenth of everything impossible, skimmed off the top, passed hand to hand in the dark.

I reached the crossroads at Greyford as the light failed, and there — nailed to the post where the salt road began, in ink still wet enough to shine — was a bill of sale.

WEATHER BY THE BARREL, it said. RAIN SOLD TO ORDER. INQUIRE WITHIN. THE BROKER OF RAIN NEVER SLEEPS.

Below it, in smaller letters, almost an afterthought:

*All tithes gratefully accepted.*

I untied the mule. My heart was doing something it hadn't done in fifteen years — not fear, exactly. Closer to hunger.

"Hold on, Commander," I said to the darkening road ahead. "I may have found the channel."
