## 7. The Broker of Rain

### Petra

The rain-broker's office smelled of wet slate and old money.

It sat at the end of Coil Street, where the city gave up pretending to be respectable and let the river do the talking. The sign above the door read *Harrow & Sons, Atmospheric Provisions*, and beneath it, in smaller letters: *Fair Weather Guaranteed or Your Coin Returned.* There were no sons. There had never been sons. I had checked the registry myself three years ago, back when auditing Harrow's weather contracts was just another line in my ledger instead of a thread pulled loose from the hem of the world.

Jude stood beside me on the step, hood up against a drizzle that was falling upward — a bad sign, professionally speaking. Rain that forgot its manners usually meant someone nearby was selling more than they could honestly deliver.

"Rules," I said quietly.

"You have rules."

"I have eleven rules. You'll need three." I held up fingers. "One: you don't speak unless he addresses you directly. Two: if he offers you tea, refuse. Brokers who drug their clients keep the good cups for themselves. Three—" I hesitated, because rule three was the one I'd invented this morning, standing in front of my mirror with a sword I hadn't drawn in fifteen years. "Three: whatever happens in that room, we leave together."

Jude looked at me. The drizzle beaded on their hood and slid off like it had been paid to.

"That last one doesn't sound like an audit rule," they said.

"It isn't."

They nodded once, and something passed between us that had no entry in any ledger I kept — no debit, no credit, no column to file it under. I turned to the door before it could become a thing with a name.

* * *

### Jude

The man behind the desk was small the way a locked box is small — compact, dense, full of things that would spill if you forced him open wrong. He had a rain-broker's complexion: pale as a mushroom, with the permanent damp look of someone whose livelihood depended on clouds forgiving him. His nameplate said OREN HARROW. His hands said he'd been counting other people's misery since before his beard came in.

He offered Petra tea. She refused. He looked almost disappointed, like she'd failed a test he'd been hoping she'd pass.

"Mistress Dunmore." He knew her name. That interested me. "Guild of Impossible Things. To what does Harrow & Sons owe the honor? We're current on our guild dues. I have receipts."

"This isn't about dues." Petra settled into the client's chair without being asked, which I was learning was how she did everything — took the seat, took the room, took the exact amount of space she required and not one inch more. "It's about your winter inventory."

"My inventory is weather."

"Your inventory is weather." Petra produced a folded paper from her coat. "Sixteen casks of stormfront, sold to the barley towns. Nine barrels of fog, contracted to the harbor authority. And one item listed only as *personal precipitation, bespoke, delivery refused twice*." She laid the paper flat on his desk. "You've never sold bespoke rain, Master Harrow. In eleven years of contracts, every product you've moved has been bulk commodity. Storms by the cask. Clouds by the cord. So I want to know what kind of rain gets delivered to a single address, refused twice, and then disappears from your books entirely."

I watched the broker the way I'd apparently been trained to watch people, though watching wasn't a thing anyone had ever taught me. His left hand went still. His right hand straightened a pen that didn't need straightening. And his eyes — his eyes went to the wall behind Petra, to a shelf of brass barometers, all showing fair weather, all of them lying.

"I don't discuss individual clients," Harrow said.

"This client paid in guild scrip."

"Then take it up with the guild."

"The guild's ledgers show the payment cleared through an account that was closed forty years ago." Petra leaned forward. Her voice stayed level, pleasant, the voice of a woman reading aloud in a quiet library. "Master Harrow, I audit impossibilities for a living. You sell them. Let's not pretend either of us is in an honest trade."

The silence stretched. Somewhere upstairs, water moved through pipes — his stock, I supposed, sloshing in its casks, waiting to be sold to towns that prayed differently now than they used to. Towns that prayed to nobody, because there was nobody left to hear.

Except somebody was hearing. Somebody was answering. And the answers were moving through places exactly like this room, packed in barrels next to the fog.

"I want immunity," Harrow said finally.

"No."

"A reduced—"

"Master Harrow." Petra didn't raise her voice. She lowered it, which was worse. "Two days ago I stood in a guildhall where every candle flame bent toward me when I read a certain word aloud. Whatever you've been handling, it is awake, and it is paying attention, and the people who sent it will come through this street eventually looking for everyone who touched it. Immunity from the Guild of Impossible Things will be worth precisely nothing when they arrive. What I'm offering you instead is simpler: tell me the truth, and when the storm comes, you'll be standing behind me."

I believed her. That startled me — not that she was convincing, but that some part of me, some part older than two years, older than my memory, sat up inside my chest and thought: *yes, that one, stand behind that one.*

Harrow must have felt something similar, because his shoulders dropped half an inch.

"There was a parcel," he said. "Small. Wrapped in oilcloth. Came to me with instructions — hold it through the dry season, then forward it inland. No return address. Payment in advance, in old coin." He swallowed. "It hummed."

"Hummed," Petra repeated.

"Like a struck bell that never finishes ringing. My clerks wouldn't go near the storeroom. One of them quit. The second time I tried to send it out, my own courier came back white as bone and wouldn't say why, just handed me the parcel and walked into the river." Harrow's hands were flat on the desk now, pressing down, as if the desk might float away. "So I opened it. Against every contract clause I've ever written, I opened it."

"What was inside?"

"A word." He said it carefully, the way you handle something with edges. "Not written. Not spoken. It was just — *there*, in the wrapping, the way cold is there. I read it without meaning to. And for about a minute, Mistress Dunmore, it rained inside my head. Rained for the first time in four hundred years on land that's been begging for it since the gods went silent." His voice cracked on the number. "Do you know what that's like? To feel something answer after a lifetime of nothing?"

I did, actually. I knew exactly what that was like. I woke up every morning as land like that — plowed, planted, and praying to a sky that had never once answered. But I kept my mouth shut, per rule one, and watched Petra instead.

"Yes," she said. "I think I do."

"The parcel's gone. Forwarded inland like instructed, six weeks ago. But I kept the shipping instructions." He fumbled open a drawer and laid a scrap of oilcloth-stiff paper on the desk. "There's no name. Just a place and a title. A waystation on the salt road, past Greyford. And a word for who receives there." He tapped the paper once. "*The Tithe.*"

Petra picked it up. I saw her read it twice, and I saw the exact moment the second reading changed her face — a small tightening around the eyes, grief putting on work clothes.

"Do you know what the Tithe is?" she asked.

"I know what tithe means," Harrow said bitterly. "A tenth part, owed. I've been paying tithes to the weather my whole life. But this one — the instructions said payment was accepted in advance, and the coin listed wasn't money." He looked at her, then at me, and I realized he was afraid for us, this little mushroom of a man, afraid in the specific way of someone who has glimpsed the invoice attached to a miracle. "The currency line said *memories, assessed at receipt*. Whoever runs this Tithe doesn't deal in gold, mistress. They deal in yesterdays."

* * *

### Petra

We walked out into a rain that had remembered its manners again, falling properly downward, and I made us go four blocks before I spoke, until the drum of it on awnings would cover our words.

"The Tithe takes memories," I said. "As payment."

"So it appears."

"Jude." I stopped under an awning and turned to them. "You have no memory past two years. None. And you carry a satchel that hums. Do you understand what I'm saying?"

They were quiet. Water ran off the awning edge between us, a thin silver curtain.

"You're saying maybe I already paid," Jude said. "That maybe I'm not broken. Maybe I'm *paid*."

"I'm saying it's a possibility I don't like."

"But it fits." They weren't upset, exactly — or they were, but in that flat, careful way I'd started recognizing, emotion pressed flat like flowers in a book so it couldn't stain anything. "Everything fits. The humming. The gaps. Vellam's face whenever he looks at me, that — pity, or guilt, I've never been able to tell which. Maybe I bought something once. Or sold something. Or was *sold*."

"Then we find the Tithe," I said, "and we ask for a refund."

Jude laughed — a short, surprised sound, quickly smothered. "You can't get a refund on memories."

"I've gotten refunds on curses, resurrections, and one haunted violin. I don't see why yesterdays should be exempt." I said it lightly, and meant none of it lightly. "But I need to know you'll come with me knowing what it might mean. If you paid the Tithe once, going back might cost again. The road past Greyford is salt and bandits and weather that hasn't decided which century it belongs to. This stopped being an audit two candles ago. This is now something else, and I won't pretend I can protect you from it."

Jude looked at me for a long moment. Rain-light moved across their face.

"You burned a page," they said.

"What?"

"The night you found the first ledger entry. You burned it in your office grate. I was delivering the evening dispatch; I saw the light through your window. You burned it, and then you stood there looking at the fire like you wished you could unburn it." They shrugged. "You didn't report it. You didn't bury it. You kept pulling the thread even though you knew where it led, because that's who you are. So don't ask me if I'll come with you, Petra. Ask me where we're sleeping tonight."

For a moment I couldn't answer. Four hundred years of silent sky, and here was a person with no yesterday telling me they trusted my tomorrow enough to sleep in it. I am a woman of precise entries and balanced columns, and I had no idea where to file that.

"The Salt Ram inn," I managed. "It has locks on the doors and terrible stew."

"Perfect," said Jude, and smiled, and the smile did something to the arithmetic of the day that I chose not to audit.

* * *

### Jude

We left at dawn, ahead of the weather and behind our better judgment.

Petra rode like she audited — efficiently, without wasted motion, checking our backtrail every quarter hour with a thoroughness that would have seemed paranoid if I hadn't seen what nine scraped altars do to a person's definition of paranoia. I rode beside her because riding behind her meant I couldn't see her face, and lately her face had become a thing I checked often. Like a barometer. All of hers lied less than Harrow's.

By midday we were clear of the river country and onto the salt road proper — white flats stretching to a horizon that glittered like spilled sugar, the way-stations few and far between. Nobody spoke much. The road had a way of making conversation feel like an expenditure.

It was Petra who broke first, that evening, at a fire she built with the competence of someone who'd once slept outside for years.

"Tell me something true," she said. We were sitting knee to knee over the flames because the wind demanded it, and the wind could have anything it wanted except distance between us. "Anything. I find I need true things today. After Harrow. After the word *Tithe*."

I thought about it. True things were complicated for me; my supply was limited and recently inspected.

"When I wake up," I said, "there's always a moment — half a heartbeat — where I reach for everything. My whole life, all of it, the way you reach for warmth in a cold bed. And for half a heartbeat I have it. Some shape of it. A kitchen. Someone laughing. Then it closes like a hand, and I'm just me again, the two-year version, the abridged edition." I fed a stick to the fire. "Every morning. Half a heartbeat of having a self, and then losing it again. You get good at not minding. You get very good at not minding, because the alternative is minding, and minding that much, daily, would kill a person."

Petra had gone very still. Firelight caught in her eyes and stayed there, refusing to fall.

"And today," I went on, "riding that road, I realized — if the Tithe took it, if I *paid* — then somewhere there's a shrine holding my mornings. Holding the kitchen and the laughing. And I hate it, and I also—" My voice did something embarrassing. I steadied it. "I also hope they kept the receipt. Because I want to know if the person I was would have liked the person I am now. Whether they'd have approved of this. Of—" I gestured at the fire, the road, her. "Of the company."

"Jude."

"Don't say something kind. I'll lose the thread."

"Then I'll say something true instead." She reached over and took my hand, deliberate as a signature. Her palm was warm and calloused in the places a sword leaves its handwriting. "Whoever you were — whoever the Tithe is holding — I think they'd look at you sitting at this fire and think: *good*. *Good, they made it out. Good, they're not alone.*" Her grip tightened. "And I think they'd be right on both counts."

The fire popped. Somewhere out on the flats, wind moved salt dust against the stars, hissing faintly, like the world whispering numbers.

I didn't kiss her. I wanted to; I want it noted somewhere, in some ledger, that I wanted to, and chose the morning instead — chose the version of us that had another day to be careful in. She squeezed my hand once more and let go, and we divided the watch hours without discussing it, the way we were starting to divide everything: silently, evenly, like people who intended to arrive at the same place.

I took first watch. The salt road ran pale toward Greyford, and beyond Greyford, toward the waystation where something called the Tithe kept its accounts — collecting yesterdays from desperate people, forwarding gods' names inland in oilcloth parcels, humming.

Around midnight, I heard the humming.

Faint. From the south. From my satchel, sleeping by the fire, singing softly to itself in a language of bells — and underneath it, far off, answering, something on the road sang back.
