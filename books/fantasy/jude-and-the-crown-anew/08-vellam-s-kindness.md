## 8. Vellam's Kindness

The kindness arrived on a Tuesday, which was the first suspicious thing about it.

Vellam did not do Tuesdays. Vellam did ledgers and long silences and the particular way he had of looking at you over his spectacles that made you re-count a column you'd already counted twice. In nineteen years at the Guild of Impossible Things, Petra Dunmore could count his spontaneous generosities on one hand, and two of those had been funerals.

So when he set a cup of tea on her desk — her desk, in the audit office where brokers came only to be flayed for arithmetic errors — she looked at the cup first and the man second.

"Chamomile," he said. "You've been burning the candle past midnight again. I can smell it on you."

"I can smell it on me too," Petra said. "It's cheaper than perfume."

He smiled, sat down uninvited, and folded his hands. Master Vellam was seventy if he was a day, built like a filing cabinet that had learned to walk, with a voice that belonged in a vault: dry, cool, and designed to keep valuable things from spoiling. He had taught her double-entry bookkeeping and single-entry mercy, and he had never once brought her tea.

"The winter audit," he said. "How goes it?"

"Slowly. The rain-broker entries are a mess."

"Mm." He turned his teacup a quarter-turn, though he hadn't drunk from it. "And the prayer entries?"

Petra kept her face doing what faces at the guild were trained to do, which was nothing. "Three of them. I flagged them for review."

"Flagged them," Vellam repeated, gently, as if tasting the phrase. "Petra. You know I have to ask this formally, so forgive the formality. Did you report them to the Registry?"

The Registry of Miraculous Transactions was the guild's internal watchdog, a committee of three elderly brokers who met monthly to decide whether anything on the books was too impossible to be possible. Reporting an answered prayer to them would mean archivists, inquiries, sealed columns, and — within the week — the church's auditors standing in the guildhall asking why a god had done business here without a license.

"No," Petra said.

"Why not?"

"Because I wanted to understand them before I handed them to three men who haven't left their chairs since the last coronation."

Vellam nodded slowly, and something moved behind his eyes that Petra would spend the rest of the day trying to name. Relief? She'd have expected disappointment. A mentor caught his student cutting corners; the correct expression was disappointment.

What she saw instead looked almost like gratitude.

"Well," he said, standing. "Between us, then: leave the prayer entries out of your final report. I'll handle them myself. Consider it handled."

"Handled how?"

"Handled," he said, "the way old things are best handled. Quietly." He patted her shoulder once, a gesture so unlike him that her skin crawled under her collar before her mind caught up. "Drink your tea while it's warm."

He left. The tea was chamomile, properly steeped, exactly the temperature she liked.

She poured it into the potted fern by the window, and the fern died within the week, which proved nothing except that Petra Dunmore no longer trusted gifts.

* * *

The trouble with being an auditor was that suspicion got into your blood like ink under fingernails. You couldn't scrub it out. You started reading everything twice.

For three days she did nothing. She filed her ordinary reports, ate her ordinary lunch, and let the guildhall believe she was the same woman who had been there yesterday and every day for nineteen years. Meanwhile she rebuilt the winter ledger from memory in a private notebook she kept sewn into her coat lining, entry by entry, date by date, the way you'd reconstruct a crime scene from a floor plan.

The prayer entries had been logged on the ninth, eleventh, and twenty-third of Frostmonth. Three prayers, answered, in a city where the gods had been silent for four hundred years. Each one entered in a neat clerk's hand — not hers, not any hand she recognized — and each one initialed in the margin with a small, precise *V*.

She had assumed the *V* was a clerk's mark. Clerks used initials all the time. There were four clerks whose names began with V.

But there was only one man in the guild whose initial was written like that: downstroke first, then the upstroke crossing it at the top like a blade going home into a sheath. Vellam had taught her letterforms in her first year. *"An initial is a signature wearing plain clothes,"* he'd said. *"Anyone can forge a name. Almost no one bothers to forge a habit."*

Almost no one.

On the fourth day she went to the archive room where the original winter folios were stored, and found the shelf empty. Not misplaced — empty, with a withdrawal slip in its slot dated three days ago, authorized by the Keeper of Records, countersigned:

*V.*

She stood in the cold of the archive for a long moment, listening to the building breathe around her. The Guild of Impossible Things occupied a converted temple, back from the years when temples had been safe to convert, and at night the old sanctum's acoustics still carried whispers from one end of the hall to the other. Brokers swore the building listened. Petra had always thought that was superstition.

Standing there, she thought: *if the walls are listening, they already know what I know.*

She took the notebook from her coat and read her own audit notes by the light of the archive's one permitted lamp. And that was when the cold went from her fingertips into her chest.

Her notes were altered.

Not rewritten — she would have caught rewriting the way a hound catches a changed scent. Altered. Subtly. Her own handwriting, her own abbreviations, her own cramped marginal style — but wrong. The entry for the ninth of Frostmonth, which she remembered writing as *prayer ans. — source unknown — FLAG*, now read *prayer ans. — source: licensed relic (see folio 12) — noted*. The word FLAG was gone. In its place, in her hand, in her ink, was a tidy little cross-reference pointing nowhere.

Someone had edited her memory of her own work, in her own voice, and put the notebook back in her coat while she slept, or while she bathed, or during some moment she could not identify because she had never known it was a moment at all.

That was the part that made her sit down on the archive steps. Not the forgery. The intimacy of it. Whoever had done this had studied her handwriting long enough to wear it like a glove. You didn't do that to a stranger. You did that to someone you'd watched for years.

You did that to a student.

* * *

She tested him. It was the only move she had that didn't show her cards.

On the fifth day she brought Vellam a fresh copy of the winter summary — clean, prayerless, exactly as he'd asked — and watched him read it. He read the way he always read, lips barely moving, one finger tracing the margin. When he reached the page where the prayer entries should have been, his finger paused for exactly as long as a finger pauses at a familiar landmark, and moved on.

"You removed them," he said. "Good girl."

The words *good girl* landed on her like a coin dropped down a well — she heard it fall for a long time before it hit bottom. Nineteen years of partnership, of apprenticeship, of him calling her *Dunmore* in front of brokers and *Petra* in private, and today, when she obeyed, he gave her the voice people use for children and dogs.

Or perhaps he had always used it, and she had only now grown ears to hear it.

"Where are the original folios?" she asked.

"In the strongroom, pending review."

"Whose review?"

"Mine." He smiled over his spectacles. "Petra. You look tired. Have you been sleeping?"

"No," she said honestly.

"You should. The mind does its best auditing asleep — sorts the true columns from the false ones." He tapped the summary against the desk edge, squared its corners with two precise blows, and handed it back. "You did well to bring this to me rather than the Registry. Some things the guild isn't ready to know. Some things the world isn't." His eyes held hers a beat too long, mild as milk. "You understand that, don't you?"

"Yes," Petra said, and hated how easily the lie came, and understood in the same instant that he was watching for exactly that ease — that her smooth obedience might be the thing that reassured him, or the thing that condemned her, depending on what he believed she knew.

"Then we're finished for today." At the door he stopped, half-turned, and said, almost fondly: "You know, when you were nineteen, you told me you became an auditor because you wanted a world where the numbers always told the truth. I've often wondered whether you'd still say that."

"It's still true."

"Hm," he said. "We'll see."

* * *

That night she did not sleep, which meant, per his advice, that her mind did no honest sorting. So she did it awake, at her kitchen table, with the notebook open and a candle burning down between her fingers.

Let it be laid out like a ledger, she told herself. Debits and credits. No sentiment.

Credit: Vellam had taken the folios before anyone else could find them. If the founders' cabal — if there was a cabal — was hunting evidence of smuggled god-names, Vellam had locked the door first.

Debit: he had altered her notes. Guardians do not falsify their wards' records. Forgers do.

Credit: he had warned her off the Registry, which, given the church's appetite for burning things, might genuinely have saved her life.

Debit: he had asked, twice, too casually, what she'd found in the winter ledgers. Once in the corridor outside the counting room, once over the tea. A man with nothing to hide asks once, or not at all.

Credit: the *V* initials predated her discovery. Whatever smuggling existed had been running through his initials for months. But — and here the column wobbled — a smuggler hiding names *from* the founders would also need to sign his work somewhere only he could read. An initial is a signature wearing plain clothes.

Debit: the fern was dead, and she had killed it herself out of distrust of a cup of tea, and she could not decide whether that made her prudent or paranoid, and the not-knowing was its own kind of wound.

The candle guttered. Petra pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes.

Here was the truth underneath all the columns, the entry no ledger had a line for: she loved him. Not romantically, not simply — but the way you love the person who built the room you live in. Vellam had found her at fifteen, orphaned and oath-sick, the last of an order the world wanted forgotten, and had given her a trade instead of a grave. Every straight column in her life ran back to his hand. If he was the smuggler, then either he was guilty of the strangest treason in four centuries, or he was guilty of protecting something from people worse than himself, and she could not tell which possibility frightened her more.

Because there was a third option, and it sat at the bottom of the page like sediment: that Vellam was exactly what he appeared — a kind old man covering for a reckless student — and the alterations in her notebook were someone else's work entirely, someone who wanted her to suspect her mentor, someone who had worn Vellam's initial the way the forger had worn hers.

*An initial is a signature wearing plain clothes.* And habits could be forged. He'd told her that himself. He'd armed her with the very blade now pointed at him.

She blew out the candle and sat in the dark.

* * *

In the morning she went to see Jude.

It wasn't a decision she'd made so much as one her feet had made overnight while the rest of her argued. The courier was in the delivery yard, checking the straps of that humming satchel with the wariness of a person checking on a sleeping animal that had bitten them once. Grey dawn, frost on the cobbles, breath hanging in the air.

"I need a courier I can trust," Petra said, without preamble, "for an errand that isn't on the books."

Jude looked up. They had that quality of attention that made you feel like the only lit window in a dark street. "Trust," they repeated. "I'm told I have no history. People usually mean that as a warning."

"People usually mean a lot of things. Your satchel hums. My mentor signs his lies with his own initial. Between the two of us, I think we're the only honest bookkeeping in this building."

Something moved across Jude's face — quick, unguarded, gone. Recognition, maybe. Two creatures raised in different cages, pressing palms to the same bars.

"Where's the errand?" Jude asked.

"A temple ruin outside the walls. Old St. Verren's. There's a debt on the books owed to it — nine names' worth, if my arithmetic is right, and my arithmetic is the one thing in my life that hasn't lied to me yet." She hesitated, then added, because honesty was a currency and she was suddenly poor: "And because if I go alone and something happens, there'll be no witness. I've recently learned what it is to have your own record edited. I'd like mine kept by someone else for a while."

Jude slung the satchel across their shoulder. The humming deepened for a moment, like a struck bell remembering itself, and settled.

"For the record," Jude said, falling into step beside her, "I don't remember learning to fight. But yesterday a cutpurse in the Lantern Market found that out a half-second too late, and I've been wondering ever since whose hands these are." They flexed their fingers, watching them as if they belonged to a stranger. "Maybe yours aren't the only records somebody's been editing."

The frost crackled under their boots. Behind them, high in the guildhall's converted belfry, a window opened — just a crack, just far enough — and closed again before either of them thought to look.

Vellam watched them go, and did not drink his tea.
