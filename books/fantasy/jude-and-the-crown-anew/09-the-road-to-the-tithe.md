## 9. The Road to the Tithe

The salt road began where the rain-broker's wagon ended, and for three days it gave us nothing but itself: white ruts through grey country, the horizon flat as a ledger line.

We were married now. On paper, at least — a guild-forged certificate that cost Petra forty crowns and one raised eyebrow from the notary in Harrow's Bend. *Jude and Petra Ash,* couriers of modest means, carrying salt contracts and nothing worth stealing. The names had been her idea. The surname she'd chosen herself, and I hadn't asked why, because I'd seen her face when she wrote it, and some things you don't audit.

"Married couples share a bed at waystations," she said on the first morning, not looking at me. "It's cheaper than two rooms."

"It's also a bed."

"I'm aware of how beds work, Jude."

That was Petra. She could recite the exchange rates of nine currencies from memory, could tell you what a miracle cost wholesale versus retail, and turned pink to the ears over the word *bed*.

I loved her by then. I want to be honest about the timeline, because honesty is the only inheritance I have. I don't know when I started loving her. That's the trouble with having no yesterday — every feeling arrives already old, like furniture left behind by a previous tenant. I couldn't tell you if I'd loved her for two years or two weeks. I only knew that somewhere between the satchel that sang and the candles leaning toward her like worshippers, my chest had made a decision my head was never consulted on.

Maybe that was the god in me. Maybe it was just me. I've spent a long time learning there isn't always a difference.

* * *

The salt road earned its name on the second day. We passed the flats where they cut it from the earth — vast white pans stretching to the mountains, men moving across them like punctuation marks. The air tasted of mineral and money. Every cart we passed carried the same cargo and the same bored guards, and none of them looked twice at a tired married couple with a mule named Duchess who hated everyone equally.

Petra talked business to keep her hands busy. "The Tithe sits at the crossing of the Vell and the old pilgrim road," she said, walking beside the mule because Duchess bit anyone who rode her. "Three days out, maybe four if the ford's up. It's not on any guild map. Officially it doesn't exist."

"And unofficially?"

"Unofficially, brokers whisper about it the way sailors talk about a reef. You pay to ask a question. It takes payment in kind." She paused. "Memory."

"You said you'd pay."

"I said I'd consider the price list."

"Petra."

"Don't use that tone with me. I invented that tone."

Duchess snorted, which I chose to interpret as agreement with me.

But here is what I remember most from those days on the road, and it isn't the salt flats or the ford or the cold. It's the small domestic machinery of pretending to be married, and how quickly pretending stopped requiring effort. Petra split the watch shifts without being asked. I learned she slept on her left side with her sword hand open, palm up, like she was still waiting for someone to put something in it. She learned I talked in my sleep — in no language she recognized, which frightened us both, and which we agreed silently never to discuss again.

At the waystation outside Corren's Wash, the innkeeper called us *the Ashes* and asked how long we'd been wed, and Petra answered, "Long enough," in a voice so warm I had to go check on the mule to keep my face from doing something embarrassing.

Long enough. Whatever that meant, when neither of us owned our whole selves.

* * *

The third night we camped in the lee of an old boundary stone, one of the pre-Godswar markers still standing after four centuries because even empires respect a good foundation. Petra built the fire with the efficiency of someone who'd done fieldwork before her ledgers, and I cooked — badly — while Duchess judged us both from the darkness.

The stars came out hard and clean. Out there, away from the city's candle-smoke, you could see them the way the old gods must have seen them: as a ceiling, or a door.

"Can I ask you something?" I said.

"You can ask. Whether I answer depends entirely on the question."

"When you were a swordspeaker. Before." I turned the stick in the fire. "Did you choose it? Or did it choose you?"

She was quiet long enough that I thought she wouldn't answer. Then: "My commander found me at fourteen, stealing bread off a guild cart. Told me I had quick hands and quicker eyes, and that both would get me hanged unless someone taught me a better trade." A log shifted; sparks climbed. "So yes. Chosen. But I stayed. Fifteen years, Jude. I spoke true names and bound true things, and I believed every word of the oath right up until the day the order died believing it too."

"The day the gods went silent."

"The day we learned they'd been silent longer than we knew." Her jaw did the thing it did when she was keeping her voice level through force of will alone. "Four hundred years, the church says the gods withdrew. My order's records say otherwise. They didn't withdraw. They were taken, or hidden, or folded up small enough to fit in a courier's satchel — and someone has been unwrapping them one name at a time."

"And you think Vellam."

"I think Vellam altered my notes with his own hand. I think he covered for me too easily, and kindness that convenient is a bill that comes due." She looked at me across the fire. "Why? What do you think?"

"I think," I said slowly, "that when I opened the satchel and the name sang, it wasn't singing at you. It was singing *through* you. Like it recognized something."

"In me?"

"In me." My hands had gone cold despite the fire. "Every god-name we've touched, Petra. Every altar, every parcel, every piece of this. It all bends toward me like water finding a low place. And I can't tell you why, because whoever I was before two years ago took the answer with him."

The fire cracked. Somewhere out on the flats, a night bird made a sound like a question.

"Say the rest," Petra said quietly. "You've been carrying it since Harrow's Bend. I do your books, Jude. I know when a number's missing."

So I said it. All of it, into the dark, because it was easier than her face.

"Waking up is the part people imagine wrong. They think it's like being born — blank, innocent. It's not. It's like arriving late to your own funeral. Everyone else remembers who I was. There are gaps everywhere I look. I don't know my mother's face. I don't know if I had a mother. I don't know what my laugh sounded like before I learned to make it smaller, or whether I've ever been someone's first thought in the morning, or last. There's a hole where my life should be and I walk around it every single day like a man avoiding a stair that isn't there anymore." My voice cracked on that last part, and I let it. "And the worst of it — the truly unspeakable part — is that I can't even grieve properly, because grief needs a *who*, and I don't know who I'm grieving *for*."

The silence afterward wasn't empty. Petra sat with it the way she sat with everything — fully present, taking inventory.

Then she got up, crossed the space between us, and sat down close enough that our shoulders touched.

"Here's what I know," she said. "I know you take the outside stairs because you like seeing the city wake up. You told me that in the vaults, and then looked surprised you'd said it, which means it came from somewhere real. I know you hum when you cook and deny humming when confronted. I know you sleep curled toward whatever's nearest — me, the mule, a wall — like you're guarding it, or it's guarding you. I know you gave half your pay to the widow on Candle Street and lied about it badly, and that you're the only person I've ever met who lies worse than he steals." She was counting on her fingers now, deadpan, precise, and it undid me completely. "I know that when the candles leaned toward you in the guildhall, you stepped in front of me before you even understood the danger. That's not a god's instinct, Jude. Gods don't protect people. That's yours. Whoever you were, whatever you were — that part survived."

"Petra—"

"I'm not finished. You want a past. Fine. But a past is just a record, and I am *extremely* good with records." She finally turned to look at me, and the firelight was doing things to her face that I would remember for the rest of my life, however long or short either of those proved to be. "So until you find yours, I'll be your witness. Everything you do from this day forward, I'll enter into evidence. First time you laugh at something stupid. First time you cry. First time you save someone, or fail to. I'll keep the ledger, and when you finally remember who you were, you can read it side by side and decide which one is true. That's the best I can offer. It's not a history. It's a—" She hesitated, and for once the great auditor of impossible things seemed to lose her grip on the exact word. "A promise."

"A promise," I repeated.

"Entered into the record. Witnessed." A pause. "By me."

I wanted to kiss her. I want to be honest about that too, since honesty is all I carry. I wanted it the way thirst wants. But duty sat between us like a third traveler — the Tithe ahead, Vellam behind, nine scraped altars and a crown being reassembled somewhere in the dark — and loving someone mid-investigation felt like spending coin you'd promised to a creditor. So instead I said:

"Noted for the record: on the salt road, under a boundary stone older than the gods' silence, Petra Dunmore made me a promise."

"That's Dunmore-*Ash*," she corrected. "Married couple. Keep your facts straight, courier."

And she smiled — actually smiled, unguarded, the rarest entry in any ledger — and I filed it away in the only archive I trusted, which was the one behind my ribs.

* * *

We broke camp before dawn on the fourth day, and the country changed around breakfast.

The salt flats ended at a ridge, and past the ridge the world went green and wrong. Grass grew too evenly. The Vell ran below in a channel cut too straight, its water moving without sound. And the road narrowed to a single track that curved, against all sense, back toward the mountain it should have been passing.

"Duchess doesn't like it," I said.

"Duchess doesn't like anything."

"She won't cross the ridge."

Petra studied the mule, then the valley, and I watched her do the arithmetic she did when a number refused to balance. "Shrines don't announce themselves," she said finally. "They *arrange*. Ground that leads you in. Water that keeps you quiet. If the Tithe is what the rain-broker said it is, then we're already inside its parish, and it knows we're coming."

"Should we turn back?"

"Do you want to turn back?"

I looked down at the valley, at the road curving home like an invitation written by something patient. Two years of holes. Nine emptied altars. A name folded small enough to fit in a palm, singing through me like I was made to be its bell.

"No," I said. "I want answers more than I want safety. I think that might be the one thing I brought with me from before."

Petra clicked her tongue and Duchess, traitor to both of us, walked calmly down into the green.

We followed. And halfway down, as the sun cleared the ridge, every blade of grass in the valley bent at once toward the road ahead — toward *us* — the way candles lean toward a spoken name.

Petra's hand found mine. Neither of us let go.

Neither of us mentioned it, either. Some entries you keep off the books.

The shrine was waiting at the bottom, and it was hungry.
