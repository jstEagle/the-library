## 10. The Tithe

**Petra**

The rain-broker's map was drawn on the inside of a man's forearm, which told Petra two things before she'd finished reading it: the Tithe did not want to be found by anyone careless enough to lose a map, and the broker had been more frightened of the Tithe than of her knife.

She'd expected that. What she hadn't expected was the direction.

"North?" Jude said, squinting at the ink as it faded in the cold morning air. The broker's arm had stopped bleeding an hour ago; now the map was fading with it, lines thinning like breath on glass. "Everything north of the salt road is dead ground. No towns. No wells. Nothing to tithe."

"That's the point," Petra said. She rolled the man's sleeve down — he'd fainted twice during the reading and she wasn't going to make him watch his own geography disappear. "You don't build a shrine that eats memories where people can stumble into it. You build it where no one has any left to lose."

Jude was quiet for a while after that. They walked. The salt road gave way to a hunter's track, then to no track at all, just a seam of grey stone threading through hills the color of old ash. Three days out from the last farm, Jude started humming — a habit Petra had catalogued and never commented on, one of the forty-odd small mysteries she kept filed under *courier, memoryless, do not press*.

On the fourth day the humming stopped.

They crested a rise and looked down into a bowl of land where nothing grew, not even the stubborn grey lichen that claimed everything else this far north. At its center stood a shrine.

Petra had seen temples her whole life — guild business took her past them often enough. They were built to be looked at: white stone, gold leaf, doors wide enough for processions. This was the opposite. It was small, sunken half into the earth, built from field stones that might have been gathered by hand by someone in a hurry. It had no door. Just an opening, dark as a well mouth, and above it a lintel carved with a single word so worn she had to walk halfway down the slope to read it.

*TITHED.*

Below that, smaller, newer, cut by different hands over what must have been generations:

*One memory per transaction.*
*The shrine chooses which.*
*No refunds.*

"Well," Jude said. "That's honest, at least."

"It's a trap," Petra said. "It's honest the way a snare is honest."

"You want to turn back?"

She thought about Vellam's handwriting in her audit notes — her own words, copied in a hand almost but not quite hers, the corrections patient and loving and wrong. She thought about nine altars scraped clean. About a god's name folded small enough to fit in a palm, and how the candles had leaned toward her when she spoke it, hungry as dogs.

"No," she said. "But I want you to stay outside."

Jue looked at her. "Absolutely not."

"The shrine eats memories. You don't have any to spare. Whatever's in there takes a payment, it could take—" She gestured at all of them, the two years of person standing in the ash-colored grass with wind pulling at their coat. "There's less of you than there is of me. I can afford a loss. You can't."

"That's not how you decide who walks into a haunted building," Jude said. "That's how you decide who's worth more. And you've been doing it since the satchel sang, and I let it go because you were frightened, but I'm not doing it today." A pause. "Besides. If it eats a memory and gets nothing, maybe it'll tell us something just to make the trip worthwhile. That's courier logic. Dead weight pays double."

"That is not courier logic."

"It's *my* courier logic. I invented it. I have no past; I have to invent things."

She wanted to argue. She was, professionally, extremely good at arguing. But Jude had that set to their jaw that she'd learned meant the conversation had already ended somewhere inside them and she was only hearing the echo of it. So she did the next best thing: she took inventory.

"One transaction each," she said. "We ask our questions first, we pay second. We don't touch the walls. If it offers a bargain that sounds generous, we refuse — generosity from a thing like this is bait. And if anything happens in there—"

"Nothing will happen."

"—if anything happens, we leave separately and meet at the road. Not because I think you're worth less." She made herself say it plainly, the way she entered figures in a ledger: exact, unadorned. "Because I think you're worth exactly as much as me, and I don't get to spend you."

Jude blinked. For a moment something moved behind their face that they didn't have a history to name it with. Then they nodded once and walked down into the bowl, and Petra followed, and the grass got sparser and then stopped pretending altogether, and the silence got heavy the way water does at depth.

At the opening, the dark inside smelled — unexpectedly — of candle smoke and old paper. Like a study. Like home.

"After you," Jude said.

"No," said Petra, and stepped through.

* * *

**Jude**

Inside, the shrine was bigger than outside, which felt rude.

Jude had heard of space doing tricks like this — guild couriers traded stories about chapels that swallowed caravans and wardrobes that opened onto coastlines — but hearing about it and standing in it were different animals. The room stretched away in rows of shallow stone niches, thousands of them, floor to unseen ceiling, and in each niche sat a single object. A child's shoe. A wedding ring gone green with age. A lock of hair tied with ribbon. A wooden soldier with one arm. A key to a door that had probably burned down around it decades ago.

Memories, Jude thought. Or the shapes memories leave. Every niche a thing someone had carried until the carrying hurt too much, or until they needed the hurting's price paid for something else.

"They're not offerings," Petra said quietly. She'd gone still in the way she went still before drawing a blade. "Look at the dust. Nobody maintains this place. These aren't gifts. They're *receipts*."

Between the niches, candles burned. Thousands of them, none melting, none guttering, each flame perfectly still, like insects pinned to the air. Jude had the sudden, skin-crawling certainty that the flames were looking at them. All of them. Weighing.

A voice came from everywhere and nowhere, and it was not a voice so much as a sensation of being *read*. It arrived without sound, directly behind the eyes, patient as arithmetic.

*TWO WHO WOULD TRANSACT. STATE YOUR QUESTIONS. PAYMENT FOLLOWS ANSWER. THE SHRINE CHOOSES WHAT IT TAKES.*

Jude's mouth went dry. Beside them, Petra didn't flinch. Of course she didn't. Petra Dunmore faced down gods' names and mentors' betrayals with the same expression she used on late deliveries.

"We have three questions," Petra said. "We'll pay for three answers."

*THE SHRINE DOES NOT SELL BY NUMBER. THE SHRINE SELLS BY WEIGHT. ASK.*

"Who is buying the stolen god-names?"

The candles flared — every one of them, a single synchronized pulse, and Jude felt the whole room lean toward Petra like the guildhall's candles had leaned when she read the name aloud. Something in the walls exhaled.

*A QUESTION OF GREAT WEIGHT,* the presence said, and there was something new in it now, something almost like appetite. *ANSWER: THE BUYERS ARE MANY AND THEY ARE ONE. NINE FOUNDERS BUILT YOUR GUILD UPON A THEFT. THEY SEEK TO REASSEMBLE THE CROWN ANEW — THE INSTRUMENT THAT ONCE CHAINED EVERY NAME YOU NOW TRADE. FOUR SURVIVE. ONE SITS AT YOUR TABLE.*

Jude watched Petra absorb that. It hit her like weather hits a mountain — nothing visible moved, but you could feel the mass of it landing. One sits at your table. She'd already suspected Vellam. Suspecting and knowing were different currencies here, and Jude could see her converting one to the other, and the exchange rate was terrible.

*YOUR SECOND QUESTION.*

Jude opened their mouth to ask theirs — *who am I, what was taken from me* — and Petra's hand closed on their wrist. Not hard. Just present.

"Not here," she breathed. "Not as payment. Whatever it would take from you, you can't afford it. Ask something cheap. Ask about the road."

And that was — that was right, obviously it was right, and it was also the most frustrating thing Jude had ever been talked out of, standing in a room full of everyone's lost yesterdays with their own missing ones humming in the walls like a struck bell. But they swallowed it. "Fine. Second question. How do we stop the founders?"

*CANNOT BE ANSWERED FOR ANY PRICE YOU CARRY,* the shrine said, and Jude felt obscurely insulted. *ASK SOMETHING SMALLER.*

"How do we find the four surviving founders?"

*THAT IS A ROAD QUESTION. GOOD.* A pause, and the candlelight dimmed to something almost gentle. *FOLLOW THE TITHE. THE FOUNDERS STILL PAY THEIR TITHE HERE, EACH YEAR, ON THE LONGEST NIGHT. THEY PAY IN MEMORIES OF THE GODSWAR — MEMORIES THAT WOULD CONDEMN THEM IF SPOKEN ALOUD. THE SHRINE HOLDS THEM STILL. FOUR NICHE-LIGHTS BURN RED AT MIDWINTER. COME THEN, AND READ THE WALLS, AND KNOW THEM ALL.*

"And the third question?" Petra said.

*ASK.*

Petra was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had changed — lower, stripped of the ledger-clerk crispness, down to the raw wood underneath.

"Is there any way to give the gods back without a war? Without the Crown? Is there a third path, or is it only chains or slaughter?"

The silence went on so long Jude thought the shrine had refused the question entirely. Then, softly, in a tone Jude had not expected from a thing made of candles and niches — a tone almost like pity:

*THERE IS ALWAYS A THIRD PATH. IT COSTS MORE THAN EITHER OF THE OTHERS. THE SHRINE CANNOT SAY MORE. THE SHRINE ONLY KEEPS ACCOUNTS. IT DOES NOT WRITE THEM.*

Then: *PAYMENT.*

* * *

**Petra**

The candles rose off their pins of air, all together, and drifted.

That was the moment Petra understood the true architecture of the place — that the niches weren't storage, they were *selection*, and the drifting flames were the choosing, thousands of small lights sorting themselves toward the two of them like iron filings toward a lodestone. Most passed them by. Some circled Jude, hovering, considering, and Jude went rigid and pale, and Petra put her body between them and the light without deciding to. The lights withdrew from her shadow as though it stung.

Then one candle — one — turned from the far wall and came to her, unhurried, and stopped at the height of her heart.

*THIS ONE,* said the shrine. *YOU CARRY A MEMORY OF GREAT DENSITY. A FACE. A VOICE. A GRAVE YOU VISIT. A DEAD COMMANDER WHOSE ORDERS YOU STILL OBEY. THE SHRINE WILL TAKE THE FACE.*

"No," Petra said instantly.

*THE TRANSACTION IS NOT NEGOTIABLE. ONLY THE COIN IS. OFFER SOMETHING ELSE OF EQUAL WEIGHT, OR THE SHRINE TAKES ITS CHOICE.*

Her mind raced through her own inventory the way she audited a winter ledger — itemizing, weighing, hunting for a substitute. Her mother's kitchen. The oath itself, spoken at nineteen with snow in her boots. Fifteen years of mornings at her desk. None of it balanced. She knew, with a bookkeeper's cold clarity, exactly why: the commander's face was the heaviest thing she owned. It was the load-bearing wall of everything she'd built since the order fell. Remove it and the structure stood, technically, but nothing above it meant quite what it used to.

*YOU UNDERSTAND,* the shrine said, and it sounded almost sorry, *WHY THIS IS THE FAIR PRICE. YOU ASKED WHETHER THERE IS A THIRD PATH. THERE IS. IT IS MADE OF EXACTLY THESE PAYMENTS. SOMEONE MUST BE FIRST.*

"I know," Petra said.

"Petra." Jude's voice cracked on the second syllable. "Don't. Take my — take whatever it wants from me, I'll find a way to live with less, I already do—"

"You can't," she said. "That's the whole point of you." She smiled at them, and was surprised to find the smile real. "Jude. Listen to me. I visit a grave every year and talk to a woman whose face I'm about to lose. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I need someone to remember her *for* me. That's what witnesses are for. That's what you're for."

"I don't even remember myself—"

"Then we'll be even. Two people holding each other's pasts. It's a fair trade. I've checked the books."

The candle waited. It did not flicker. It had all the patience of an entry waiting to be posted.

Petra thought: *speak a true name, bind a true thing.* Her order's oath, drilled into her before she was old enough to grow a beard's first shadow. She had spent fifteen years speaking no true names at all, binding nothing, keeping her word the way misers keep coin. And now the first great binding of her second life would be this: handing over the face of the dead woman who'd taught her the oath, to buy information about whether the world could be saved gently.

There was a symmetry there. The commander would have appreciated it, or hated it, or both. Petra wished she'd have time to find out.

"Do it," she said.

The flame touched her forehead, and it did not burn.

What it did was *lift*. It reached into her the way a clerk reaches into a drawer of documents and removes one — cleanly, precisely, without disturbing the others — and Petra felt the commander's face go. Not the memory of her; the memory *of her*. The grave stayed. The orders stayed. The words *she taught me* stayed. But the face above those words became a smooth blank, like a coin worn featureless by centuries of thumbs, and Petra stood in the candlelight trying to summon her commander's eyes and finding, where they should have been, a soft and terrible nothing.

She made a sound. She would deny later that she made a sound.

*PAID IN FULL,* the shrine said. *THE LEDGER CLOSES.*

The candle returned to its niche. Around them the thousand flames settled back onto their pins of air, and the room began, very slowly, to contract — the far wall swimming up toward them, the niches folding away like a fan — until the shrine was small again, a stone hut sunk in dead ground, and Petra and Jude stumbled out into grey daylight that felt obscene in its ordinariness.

Jude caught her elbow. Steadied her. Looked at her face — really looked, the way you look at a document for alterations.

"It took her," Jude said. "I can see it. There's a hole where—"

"Don't describe the hole," Petra said. Her voice held. She was proud of that, in a distant, underwater way. "Describe her instead. While we walk. Everything you saw when I talked about her. Start with her hands."

Jude hesitated. Then they started talking — haltingly at first, then better, dredging up every detail Petra had ever let slip across a desk or a campfire: the commander's scarred knuckles, the way she'd cleaned her blade like it was a chore and a prayer at once, the joke about ledgers she'd told that Petra repeated every winter solstice without ever saying where it came from. Jude rebuilt her out of secondhand timber, plank by plank, walking backward down the slope so they could keep facing Petra, narrating a woman they'd never met into existence out of pure borrowed light.

And Petra listened, and grieved, and understood — with the finality of a figure posted to a ledger — that the founders had made this same trade hundreds of times, on the longest night, year after year, feeding their war-crimes into these walls so they could sit at banquets with clean consciences and empty places where the horror used to be.

Four red lights at midwinter, the shrine had said. Four founders, paying down their guilt one memory at a time, keeping it safely stored in a box in the middle of nowhere — not destroyed. Stored. Because men like that never destroy evidence of their greatness.

She stopped walking.

"What," Jude said.

"Midwinter," Petra said. "Jude. The founders pay their tithes at midwinter. Which means every memory of every crime they committed against the gods — the whole war, the whole theft, the whole reason the sky has been silent for four hundred years — is sitting in those niches, organized, indexed, and *readable*."

Jude turned slowly to look back at the little hut sunk in the hillside, its doorway dark and patient.

"That's not a shrine," they said. "That's an archive."

"Yes," Petra said. "And we've just learned how to check out a book."
