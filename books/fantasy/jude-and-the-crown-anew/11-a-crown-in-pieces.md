## 11. A Crown in Pieces

The Tithe's memory came back to me the way rain comes back to a drought town: all at once, and wrong.

I had paid for the buyer's name with my commander's face. I had walked out of that shrine knowing what I'd lost — a face I could no longer summon, only the fact of it, like a door painted over. What I hadn't expected was how the payment would keep arriving. The Tithe did not simply take. It traded. And somewhere in the ledger between us, a page turned over.

I was in my office on the third night after Greyford Road, cross-referencing the name I'd bought — *Corvane*, which meant nothing, which was the problem — when the missing piece surfaced.

Not Corvane's face. A room.

A banquet hall I had no business remembering, because I had never attended it. Long table of black saltwood. Nine chairs, one empty. Candlelight the color of old honey. And at the head of the table, laughing at some joke forty years dead, a young woman with ink-stained fingers and my own habit of sitting with her spine not touching the chair.

My grandmother. Sable Dunmore. Founder of the Guild of Impossible Things.

I sat very still. The candle beside me leaned toward my hand, the way candles do around unspoken names, and I let it lean because moving would mean admitting what was happening.

The Tithe hadn't given me a memory. It had given me a *witness*. Somewhere in its hoard of eaten recollections was a fragment that recognized me — recognized the shape of my attention, the way I read ledgers, the particular hunger of an auditor looking for the entry that doesn't balance. It had matched me to it the way a broker matches a curse to the customer who deserves it.

And the fragment showed me a table where nine people had divided something among themselves.

Nine chairs. Nine names missing from nine altars.

The arithmetic arrived before the horror did. That's always been my curse: numbers first, then the cold.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I pulled the guild's founding charter from the restricted shelf — I had the authority; I was the last swordspeaker on the rolls, and nobody had revoked that in fifteen years — and I read it the way you read a confession you already know is true.

Seven founders were listed. Not nine.

Two had been struck from the record. Their names scraped out of the vellum so cleanly that the parchment was thinner there, translucent as skin held up to a lamp. I knew that scraping. I had spent three days in a temple ruin watching Jude stare at altars cut open exactly the same way, god-names removed like pages torn from a book.

Someone had edited the founding of my own guild with the same blade that emptied those shrines.

And the Tithe's fragment gave me the ninth detail, the one that made my hands go numb against the desk: at that long table, in the center, under a glass cloche like a dish kept warm for a late guest, had sat a single sheet of paper. Folded small enough to fit in a palm.

I had seen a folded paper like that once before. In a satchel that hummed. On the desk of a courier who couldn't remember yesterday.

They weren't smuggling gods back into the world.

They were collecting them.

* * *

Jude found me at dawn, because Jude always found me at dawn now, standing in doorways with that expression of someone who has learned to knock by watching other people do it. Two years of memories, and most of their habits were borrowed. I found it endearing before. That morning I found it terrifying, for reasons I'll get to.

"You haven't slept," they said.

"I've been reading."

"You've been reading the way Vellam reads," Jude said, "which means whatever you found, you wish you hadn't." They set down two cups of tea without being asked. "Tell me anyway."

So I told them. About Corvane. About the witness-memory the Tithe had slipped me like a broker's bonus. About the two scraped names in the charter and the nine chairs and the folded paper under glass.

"Corvane isn't a buyer," I said. "Corvane is a *chair*. One of nine. And the thing they're buying isn't weather or curses or second chances. It's names. God-names. They're buying them back one at a time and folding each one small enough to hide, and they're hiding them—" I stopped, because saying it aloud made it real, and real things have weight.

"Hiding them where?" Jude asked quietly.

"In the Crown." My voice came out flat, professional, the voice I used for bad audits. "Or pieces of it. The Crown Anew wasn't one artifact, Jude. That's the lie we've all been told for four hundred years. It was a *binding* — a contract written across nine anchors, nine vessels, nine places where divinity could be caught and held and made obedient. The Godswar didn't end because someone won it. It ended because someone built a cage with nine locks, and then the builders broke the locks apart and scattered themselves so no one person could ever close the cage again."

"And now?"

"Now eight of the nine are apparently tired of being good."

Jude was quiet for a long moment. Steam rose off their untouched tea. When they finally spoke, their voice had the careful evenness of someone walking on river ice. "Petra. The Tithe took your memory of your commander's face. What else did it give you? Because brokers don't hand out bonuses. Brokers make change."

"I know," I said. "That's what frightens me. It gave me exactly enough to find the next page. Which means either it wants me to keep reading—"

"—or someone paid it to steer you," Jude finished.

We looked at each other across the desk, two people who had spent weeks learning each other's silences, and both of us arrived at the same name without saying it. Vellam. Who had covered for me too generously. Whose handwriting I'd found altering my own audit notes — corrections, always gentle ones, always steering. Who had taught me everything I knew about finding the entry that doesn't balance.

Except maybe he hadn't been correcting my notes. Maybe he'd been annotating them. Leaving breadcrumbs in a margin only I would think to check, because only I had been trained to distrust a balanced book.

"The empty chair," I said. "At the table in the memory. Nine chairs, eight occupied."

"Vellam," Jude said.

"Or whoever Vellam is protecting." I rubbed my eyes. "Gods, Jude, do you understand what this means? If the church learns the Crown still exists — if any remnant of the old gods learns their names are being gathered into it again — the war doesn't reignite. It *completes*. Four hundred years of quiet, of gods gone silent and mortals learning to live without miracles, all of it ends because eight frightened immortals decided they'd rather be masters than mourned."

"That's assuming they're frightened," Jude said. "People who scrape altars clean aren't usually frightened. They're thorough."

* * *

I spent the middle of the day doing what I do best: accounting.

If you want to hide a miracle trade, you don't hide it in the impossible entries. You hide it in the boring ones. So I went back through forty years of guild ledgers — the winter accounts, the drought-year surpluses, the pension rolls of retired brokers — looking for payments that made no sense. Tithes to a shrine that shouldn't exist. Courier fees for routes that delivered nothing. Insurance premiums against a catastrophe no one had named.

I found eleven of them, spread across four decades, each one small enough to pass any single year's audit. Each one signed off by a different department head. And every department head whose signature appeared — I checked the portraits in the founders' gallery afterward, because I am thorough and because by then I needed to see it — every one of them was dead.

All except the signatures that weren't signatures at all. They were marks. Nine different marks, and when I laid them side by side on my desk, I saw what connected them: each was half of a broken seal. Eight halves. If you assembled them, they would form a single sigil I had seen exactly once before, carved into the lintel of the temple ruin where Jude had collected a debt — the ruin with the nine scraped altars.

The ruin where something had followed them home.

I sat back. Outside my window, Greyford city went about its afternoon: cart wheels, gull cries, the smell of bread from the street below. An ordinary city that had forgotten its gods and called the forgetting peace. And beneath it, patient as interest accruing, eight people who remembered everything were rebuilding the instrument of the world's worst war one stolen name at a time, paying for it out of petty cash.

The scale of it didn't horrify me immediately. That came later, in the dark. First came the auditor's vertigo — the realization that I was looking at a fraud so old it had become architecture. This wasn't embezzlement. This was the foundation.

* * *

Jude came back at dusk with news that made the vertigo worse.

"I went back to the Tithe," they said. They stood in my doorway with wet shoulders and an expression I hadn't seen on them before — something older than two years, something that fit badly, like a coat inherited from a taller person. "I paid another memory. Don't look at me like that. I paid a cheap one. I don't remember my twelfth birthday anymore, and honestly it sounded dull."

"Jude—"

"It showed me the vault." They crossed the room and put their hands flat on my desk, leaning in, and up close I could see they were shaking. "Under the founders' gallery. There's a door behind the portrait of the woman with the ink-stained fingers—"

"Sable Dunmore," I said.

"You know her?"

"She's my grandmother."

Jude blinked. Something moved behind their eyes — recognition reaching for a shelf that had been emptied — and then they shook it off. "Behind her portrait. The vault isn't storage, Petra. It's a *body*. Nine chambers in a ring, and eight of them are full. I saw through the keyhole. There's light in them. Not lamplight. The kind of light that leans toward you when it notices you're looking."

"What's in the ninth chamber?"

"Nothing yet." Jude's voice dropped. "But the door's newer than the others. And Petra — it's sized for a person."

The candle between us guttered and straightened, leaning toward Jude like a plant toward sun. I watched it happen and felt the floor tilt under my certainties. Nine chambers. Eight filled with stolen god-names. And a ninth, newly built, human-sized, waiting.

Every founder at that table had taken a lock of the Crown into themselves — that was the price of breaking it apart, I understood now, the reason they'd scattered and hidden and edited themselves out of their own charter. They weren't reassembling the Crown as an object. They were becoming its anchors again. Eight old immortals, hollowing themselves out chamber by chamber, each swallowed name a lock turning.

Which left the ninth anchor. The keystone. The vessel.

"Not Vellam," I whispered. "He's not building the cage. He's the one founder who refused his chair. That's why the seat was empty. That's why he's been smuggling names *out* — hiding them in satchels and couriers and prayer-deliveries, scattering them before the other eight can collect them. He's not the thief, Jude. He's the sabotage."

"Then who's the vessel?" Jude asked. "Who fits a door built to human size?"

And there it was. The question I had been circling for days, the entry that wouldn't balance, the number that only worked one way. A courier with no memory beyond two years. A satchel that hummed. Reflexes with a blade they'd never been taught. A presence that candles leaned toward. Someone emptied so thoroughly that even they believed the emptiness was natural — someone prepared, patiently, over years, to be filled.

I looked at Jude, and Jude looked back at me, and I saw the exact moment they did the same arithmetic.

"No," they said.

"Jude—"

"*No.*" They stepped back from the desk. Their voice cracked down the middle. "I'm a courier. I'm — I deliver things. That's all I am. That's all I have. You don't get to take the only two years I own and tell me they were a waiting room."

"I'm not telling you anything. I'm telling you what the evidence says."

"The evidence says I'm a *door*." They were breathing fast now. "It says everything I am was carved out of me so that something else could be poured in. That my whole self is a chamber with the furniture removed. Petra, I can't — if that's true, then what I feel about—" They stopped. The sentence hung there, unfinished, shaped unmistakably.

I stood up slowly. "What you feel about me?"

"Nothing," Jude said, which was the least convincing word ever spoken in that office. "Forget it."

"Jude." I came around the desk. I didn't touch them; I wanted to, and I suspected that wanting was visible, and I let it be visible, because if their feelings might not be theirs, then mine at least should be legible. "Listen to me. Whoever emptied you did it to make a vessel. But a vessel is just clay until something fills it. Everything you've chosen since you woke up — every delivery, every kindness, every terrible joke, every time you found me at dawn — none of that was poured into you. That was you. Whatever they took, what stayed is more than what they planned for."

"You can't know that."

"No," I admitted. "But I can audit it. It's what I do. And my professional judgment is that you are the least hollow person I have ever met, and I include the eight immortals currently swallowing gods to avoid dying."

That startled a laugh out of them — wet, ragged, but real. They wiped their face. "You're going to confront them. The founders. The eight."

"I'm going to do worse. I'm going to attend their table." I turned to the window. Below, the city lights were coming on one by one, small mortal fires against the dark. "There's a banquet. Founders' Day. Three weeks from tonight. Every surviving founder attends — it's tradition, it's public, it's the one night they can't refuse a guest with the right credentials. And I have very good credentials."

"They'll know why you're coming."

"Yes. That's the point. A confrontation in private is a disappearance waiting to happen. A confrontation at their own table, in front of the guild, in front of witnesses — that's chess." I looked back at Jude. "Which is why I need someone downstairs in the vault while I'm upstairs keeping eight monsters talking. Someone small enough to move quietly and stubborn enough not to run."

"You're asking me to break into the body of a god-cage."

"I'm asking you to steal the ninth chamber's key. Or the keystone page. Whatever's in there that makes the ring close. We take it, the Crown stays in pieces, and the Godswar stays a story we tell children to scare them about the price of obedience."

Jude was quiet for a moment. Then: "And if the vault recognizes me? If the ninth chamber knows its vessel when it sees one?"

I had no answer for that. I said so.

"Good," Jude said. "Honest books. That's why I trust you." They picked up their tea at last, cold as it was, and drank it like a vow. "Three weeks. Teach me everything you know about the founders' gallery. And Petra—"

"Yes?"

"When you sit at that table, and you look at them, and you realize what they are — don't let them see you count. You count when you're afraid. Your eyes move left to right. It's very cute and it will get us killed."

I stared at them. "How long have you known that?"

"Since the winter ledgers," Jude said, and for the first time in days, they smiled. "Some things, apparently, survived the emptying. Apparently they're all about you."

Outside, far off toward the harbor, every bell in Greyford rang once — unscheduled, unexplained, and perfectly together — and stopped. Neither of us mentioned it. We were already too busy planning a theft from the body of a god.

But I counted the bells. Old habit. Nine rings.

I pretended I hadn't noticed.
