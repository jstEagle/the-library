## 12. What Jude Forgot

The payment echoed.

That was the only word Jude had for it, and they hated the word, because words were supposed to be things they owned, and this one felt borrowed. They sat in the back room of the Tithe's shrine-house while Petra slept sitting up, her back against the wall, her sword across her knees even in exhaustion — a woman who had just handed over a memory of her commander's face and hadn't cried about it, which was its own kind of frightening — and Jude tried to be quiet and failed, because inside their skull something was ringing like a struck bell that wouldn't stop.

The Tithe had taken from Petra. That was the transaction. One memory, paid, done. Clean as coin.

But the shrine had touched Jude too. Barely. A brush of that cold attention as they'd crossed the threshold, the way you might brush a hand along a fence rail — and the bell had started then and hadn't stopped since. Not pain. Not quite. More like a door in a house they'd never been in, rattling in wind that wasn't blowing.

"Stop staring at me," Petra murmured, without opening her eyes.

"You're not sleeping."

"I'm resting my eyes and pretending you're not about to say something that will ruin my night."

Jude almost smiled. Almost. The bell rang again, and the almost died. "Petra. When it took your memory. Did you feel it leave?"

Her eyes opened. In the shrine-house's guttering light they were the gray of a ledger's margins. "Yes."

"What did it feel like?"

She considered the question the way she considered everything — like an entry to be audited. "Like a word on a page being blotted. The shape of it stayed. The ink went." She paused. "Why?"

"Because I think it took something from me too. And I don't know what it was. That's the problem." Jude laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, thin. "I don't know what anything was. That's always the problem."

Petra sat up straighter. She didn't reach for them — she never reached first, Petra, who could speak a true name and bind a true thing but couldn't cross two feet of floor to touch another person without permission — but she turned, fully turned, and gave them her whole attention, which was more than most people ever gave anything.

"Tell me."

* * *

It had started as a hum. The satchel hummed — that was old news, that was chapter one of Jude's strange life, the bag that sang when opened — but this was a different sound, underneath it, or maybe not a sound at all. A pressure behind the eyes. A taste like rain on hot slate.

They'd walked out of the Tithe's shrine-house into the gray morning and the world had been wrong in a way they couldn't name. Not the light. Not the cold. The *order* of it. Streets they'd walked a hundred times looked like streets someone had described to them. The cobbles looked like a rumor of cobbles.

And then, at the corner of Tanner's Row, they had stopped dead, because there was a rain-barrel there, half-full, and Jude had looked into it and seen their own reflection and thought, with total calm and total horror:

*That's not my face. That's the face they gave me when they emptied the other one.*

They didn't know what it meant. They didn't know who *they* was. The thought had arrived whole, the way all Jude's worst thoughts arrived — from nowhere, from the locked room at the back of their mind, from the two years of nothing before the guild found them on the salt road with a satchel and no yesterday.

"Jude." Petra's voice, in the back room, now. "You've gone somewhere. Come back."

"I'm trying to tell you where I went." They pressed their palms against their eyes. "When the shrine touched me. It was like — you know how a tuning fork makes another fork ring? It rang something in me. And now things are falling out."

"Things."

"Memories. Or — no. Not memories." Jude lowered their hands. "Instructions."

* * *

The first fragment came back that afternoon, in the safehouse above the dye-shop, while Petra slept and Jude pretended to.

They were standing in a white room.

No — they were *remembering* standing in a white room, and the remembering was so violent it felt like standing in it. White stone. White light that came from nowhere. And a table, and on the table a page, and on the page a name written in letters that hurt to look at, and a voice — an old voice, dry as a ledger closing — saying:

*"The vessel must be empty. That is the whole art of it. A god poured into a full person spills. Poured into an empty one, it sets."*

And a younger voice, arguing, and Jude knew the younger voice, knew it the way you know a voice you've argued with your whole life, and it said: *"Empty isn't nothing. You're not making a vessel. You're making a wound and calling it a vessel."*

The old voice laughed. Jude's stomach turned, because they knew that laugh. They had heard it — when? where? — across a table, over wine, in a room full of founders, and they had been *there*, they had been *in the room*, they had —

The fragment snapped shut like a book.

Jude came back to themselves kneeling on the safehouse floor, hands flat on the boards, breathing hard. Sweat. Actual sweat. Petra was crouched in front of them, having crossed the two feet without permission this time, because some things outranked permission.

"What did you see?" she asked.

"A white room. A page. Two voices." Jude's mouth was dry. "One of them was arguing for me. One of them was laughing."

"Vellam," Petra said quietly. "The one arguing. It was Vellam."

"I don't know. Maybe. Petra — the word *vessel*. They said *vessel*."

She went very still. Jude watched her arrive at the same place they'd arrived at the rain-barrel, and hated watching it, because Petra's face when she understood things was a terrible thing to see — all the warmth draining out of it and the auditor moving in.

"The Crown Anew," she said. "The founders are reassembling it. We know that. The Crown needs a bearer. The old Crown had a bearer — a slave, the histories say, someone the founders hollowed out and filled with the weight of every bound god." Her eyes came up. "Jude. The histories say the vessel was never found. The vessel *escaped*, the night before the coronation, four hundred years ago —"

"I'm not four hundred years old."

"No," Petra said. "But the Crown's makers were patient people. And the vessel doesn't have to be old. It has to be *empty*." She reached out and, carefully, like handling a page that might tear, took Jude's hand. "When were you going to tell me you remember a white room?"

"About thirty seconds before you asked."

* * *

The fragments kept coming, and they came in order of cruelty.

That was the thing Jude noticed first, lying awake that night while Petra finally slept properly — the fragments weren't random. They were sequenced. As if the shrine's touch hadn't broken a lock but *turned a key*, and something on the other side of the key was choosing what to hand through, and whatever it was, it had an editor's instincts. It gave Jude the white room. Then it gave them the corridor.

The corridor was long and white and lined with doors, and behind each door a name was being read aloud, and with every name Jude felt a weight added to their chest, a weight like a stone, like a coin, like a crown — and they understood, in the way you understand things in dreams, that each name was a god and each god was being poured into them and each pour was *cold*, so cold, colder than the Tithe, colder than the salt road in winter, a cold that didn't hurt so much as *erase*, and they were being filled the way a ledger is filled, line by line, entry by entry, and somewhere far away a voice was saying *hold still, hold still, almost done, almost crowned* —

And then a door. An unremarkable door at the end of the corridor, and hands on their shoulders — Vellam's hands, Vellam's voice, shaking, actually shaking — saying:

*"I'm sorry. This is the only mercy I have left to give you. Forget the corridor. Forget the room. Forget your name — your real one, the one they'll look for. Be no one. No one is safe. Someone is a vessel."*

And Jude had said — and this was the part that broke them, this was the part they sat up in the dark and mouthed along with because they could hear their own voice saying it — *"Will I be happy?"*

And Vellam had said: *"I don't know. I hope so. I hope you get to choose something, at least. Even if it's small. Even if it's just who to walk beside."*

* * *

Morning came gray through the dye-shop shutters. Petra was already up, already armed, already auditing — she'd laid out the Tithe's answer on the table, the name of the buyer they'd paid so dearly for, the cabal of founders, the Crown in pieces. There was work to do. There was a war to prevent. There was, in short, an entire plot that did not care about Jude's crisis, and Jude loved the plot a little for that, for giving them somewhere to put their hands.

But they couldn't pick up the satchel.

They stood over it — the humming bag, the bag that had started everything, the bag that sang — and they could not make themselves touch it, because they had remembered, in the smallest and worst fragment of all, delivered sometime in the dead hours: *they had packed it themselves.* Before the corridor. Before the emptying. The satchel was the escape kit. Vellam had packed into it everything the vessel would need to survive as no one — coin, papers, a blade, and the god's name folded small enough to fit in a palm, the first stolen name, the seed of the whole smuggling route, hidden in the one place the founders would never think to look.

Inside the courier.

Inside the empty thing.

"You're not carrying it today," Petra said from the doorway. She'd read the whole crisis off their back, the way she read everything. "I'll take it."

"It's mine." Jude heard their own voice come out flat and strange. "That's the joke, Petra. It's actually mine. I packed it. I packed it and then I made myself forget I'd packed it, because —" They stopped. Started again. "Because a hiding place you don't know about is the only hiding place that works. Even from yourself. *Especially* from yourself."

Petra crossed the room. She didn't touch the satchel. She touched Jude — took their face in both hands, which she had never done, which she did now with the deliberateness of a woman signing her name to something — and said:

"Listen to me. Whatever they made you, whatever they poured into you, whatever you were before — I've walked two hundred miles beside you. I've watched you give your bread to beggars and your blade to strangers and your last coin to a rain-broker's orphaned clerk. The Crown didn't teach you that. The white room didn't teach you that. I don't care what they emptied you *of*. What got filled back in is *you*."

"You don't know that."

"I audit things for a living," Petra said. "I know a forged entry when I see one. You're not one."

And Jude wanted — with a want that ached all the way down into the locked room — to believe her.

But the bell was still ringing. And the last fragment of the night was still sitting in their chest like a swallowed stone, the one they hadn't told her, the one they were going to have to tell her, because Petra deserved better than a courier with a hidden ledger, and because the fragment was this:

*The night before they'd run, standing in the white room with Vellam's hands on their shoulders, the vessel had looked at the empty satchel and felt — nothing. No fear. No hope. No love. The vessel had been designed to feel nothing. And then Vellam had said, "When you're no one, when you're safe, when you're walking some road with no name — you'll meet someone. And you'll feel things. And you'll wonder if they're yours."*

*"How will I know?" the vessel had asked.*

*"You won't," Vellam had said. "That's the price. That's the whole price. You'll never know which feelings were planted and which grew wild. You'll have to choose to trust them anyway."*

Jude looked at Petra's hands on their face. Petra's gray, honest, ledger-margin eyes. The love they felt for her rose up in them, enormous and warm and terrifying.

And for the first time, Jude could not tell whether it was a feeling that had grown wild — or one that had been poured in, cold, line by line, by people who needed a vessel that would walk willingly to its crowning.

They stepped back out of her hands.

"We need to move," Jude said, and their voice was steady, and the steadiness was the most frightening thing about it. "The founders will know the Tithe talked. And Petra — there's something else. The last thing I remembered, before the emptying. The last thing they let me keep."

"What?"

"They let me keep a countdown." Jude picked up the satchel at last — it was theirs, whatever else was true — and slung it across their body, and felt it hum against their heart like a second pulse. "The Crown isn't being reassembled in some vault, in some slow cabal of old founders, on some schedule we can interrupt. It's being reassembled around a *date*. And the date —"

The bell in their skull rang one final time, and a number surfaced, clean as ink:

"— is in nine days. And I know, Petra, because I was the one who was supposed to be wearing it."
