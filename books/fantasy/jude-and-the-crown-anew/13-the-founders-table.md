## 13. The Founders' Table

The invitation arrived on gilded cardstock, which was the first wrong thing. The Guild of Impossible Things did not deal in gilding; gilding implied you had something to hide behind the shine.

*Master Petra Dunmore is requested at table with the Founding Circle, on the eve of the Long Audit.*

Petra read it three times by candlelight, then held it over the flame until the corner curled. She didn't burn it. She just wanted to see if it would flinch.

It didn't. Gilded things never flinched.

"They know," Jude said from the doorway, arms crossed, satchel-less for once — the keystone page already sewn into the lining of their coat, a hard little rectangle over their heart like a second oath. "If they're inviting you to dinner, they know."

"Then we give them a dinner worth remembering." Petra straightened her collar in the smudged mirror. Fifteen years of guild service and she had never once been asked upstairs to the Founders' Table. It sat above the vaults, above the ledgers, above everything she'd spent her life keeping honest — a long black table where six chairs waited for nine founders, four of them dead and never replaced, because you don't replace the people who built the cage; you just leave their chairs empty so everyone remembers who's still inside it.

"You don't have to go down there alone," Jude said.

"I won't be alone." She met their eyes in the glass. "I'll be with you. That's different."

* * *

The vault beneath the guildhall had seven locks, and Jude had picked five of them before the soup course was served.

They worked by feel in the dark, coat off, fingers steady, listening through two hundred feet of stone to nothing at all. That was the trick of it, Vellam had told them once — the best thefts happen while someone else is being interesting. And no one alive was more interesting than Petra Dunmore with a knife hidden in her sleeve and fifteen years of grievance laid out like silverware.

Lock six fought back. Old work, pre-Guild, the kind of mechanism that remembered hands that weren't its maker's. Jude breathed slow and let their fingers do what they apparently had always known how to do, some muscle memory older than their own name, and hated it a little less tonight than usual.

Below them, muffled through stone, the banquet began.

* * *

There were five of them at the table: Founder Casserly, who owned half the river trade and all of the rain contracts; the twins Ondrey and Maren Skell, whose faces were so alike they seemed like one person disagreeing with itself; old Hessa Vane, ninety if she was a day, knitting something that was either a scarf or a net; and at the head, the chair that should have been empty but wasn't —

Vellam.

Petra stopped at the threshold. "You sit there now?"

"I've sat there eleven years," Vellam said mildly. "You never looked up."

That was true, and it landed harder than any accusation could have. She took the seat left for her at the foot of the table, as far from him as the room allowed, and let the first course come.

Soup, naturally. The guild's famous bone-broth, made from the marrow of a thing nobody named twice. Around her the candles burned perfectly upright. She counted them out of habit. Forty. All leaning, very slightly, toward her.

"You found the winter ledgers troubling," Casserly said. Not a question. He was a broad man gone soft at the edges, like a book left too long in the rain.

"I found them *incomplete,*" Petra said. "Three prayers answered in a silent city. Nine names missing from the temple ruin at Greyford. A middleman called the Tithe. Shall I continue, or would you rather I summarize for the minutes?"

Maren Skell laughed — or Ondrey; Petra genuinely couldn't tell. "She audits us. After all these years, she actually audits us."

"That is my function."

"No," Hessa Vane said without looking up from her knitting. "Your function was to be the last swordspeaker and never speak. We built you a desk so you'd have somewhere quiet to forget your order died." Needles clicked. "Someone has been careless."

"The someone," Petra said, "is sitting at this table."

Silence, except the needles.

Vellam poured wine. Four glasses. He set one at her place and drank none himself. "Tell us what you think you know, Petra."

So she did. Not everything — she kept the Tithe's price, kept the memory she'd paid, kept the commander's face that lived now only in the shrine's cold ledger — but enough. The smuggling route. The names folded small. The Crown Anew reassembling piece by piece in the deep vault, and the buyer's identity written in the founders' own accounting hand.

"We aren't rebuilding it," Casserly said when she finished. "We're *finishing* it. There's a difference, and the difference is war. You remember the Godswar, girl? Of course you don't. Nobody does — we bought the forgetting ourselves, paid the Tithe a generation's memories to make the world safe from its own history. And now the gods are leaking back in through cracks our own courier keeps opening—"

"Careful," Petra said softly.

"—and we have perhaps a season before the church notices, the remnants notice, and every army on the continent learns there's an object under this city that can put a leash on heaven. We finish the Crown, we control the terms. Or we burn it, and pray no one else rebuilds it. Those are the only two endings, Mistress Dunmore. Choose one."

"There's a third ending," Petra said. "It's called asking the gods what *they* want."

Hessa Vane finally set down her knitting. Her eyes were milky and ancient and entirely awake. "Child," she said, "we were in the room when the gods were asked that question last time. The answer was fire."

* * *

Sixth lock. Jude's fingertips were bleeding freely now, slick on brass, and the mechanism had begun — faintly, horribly — to hum the way the satchel used to. A god-name in the works somewhere, ground into the gears as grease. Of course. The Crown's locks would be sealed with stolen divinity. You couldn't pick what was praying at you.

So Jude stopped picking and started *listening*, the way Petra had taught them to listen to ledgers — for the lie in the numbers, the entry that didn't balance.

The lock wanted a name. Any true one would do.

Jude thought about it. Thought about the two years of self they owned, the salt road, the fire, Petra saying *I'll be your witness.* They gave the lock the only name they were sure belonged to them.

It opened like a held breath released.

* * *

"—the point," Petra said, rising, "is that I'm done choosing between your endings. Consider this my resignation from the audit. Consider it whatever you like." She set her untouched wine down. "I came to tell you the Crown will not be finished. By tomorrow it won't exist."

Casserly stood too, chair shrieking. "Guards—"

"Sit down, Casserly." Vellam hadn't raised his voice. He didn't need to. "Let her go."

"You—"

"*Let her go.*" And something passed across the old man's face then, quick as a fish under ice, and Petra — who had spent twenty years reading his silences the way other people read letters — finally saw it whole.

He wasn't covering for himself. He never had been. The altered audit notes, the generous lies, the casual questions — he'd been building her a trail because he couldn't build one himself. Eleven years at the head of the Founders' Table, close enough to strangle the project, trusted enough to be ignored. He had smuggled nine god-names out from under these people using *her guild*, her ledgers, her stubborn incorruptible bookkeeping, as his road. And when she'd gotten close enough to be dangerous, he'd invited her to dinner to spend his last authority buying her one hour.

One hour. The soup course. The wine he didn't drink. The twins' laughter stretched thin.

He wasn't letting her go. He was spending himself.

"Vellam," she said quietly, at the door.

"Don't," he said. "Whatever it is — say it after. Come back and say it after, if there is an after." He turned back to the table, to Casserly's fury, to Hessa's needles beginning again, patient as a countdown. "Now run, child. You have a vault to rob and I have a diversion to finish being."

* * *

Jude had the keystone page free of its case and halfway up the stairs when the seventh lock — the one below, the great one, the door itself — began to scream.

Not metal. A voice. Folded names, crying out all at once, and every candle in the guildhall above leaned toward the sound, and somewhere overhead the dinner ended early.

Jude ran.

Petra met them at the crossing corridor, skirts still banquet-neat, knife now openly in hand, and they ran together through halls that had never once felt like a trap until tonight, past portcullises dropping in sequence behind them — dropped *behind*, she noticed, never ahead. Someone was closing doors at their backs and only their backs.

At the final gate, the guild's front doors, standing open onto the winter night, Vellam's voice reached them one last time through the speaking-tube in the wall, tinny and calm:

"The east postern is unwatched. Tell Petra—" a pause, the length of a man deciding what can fit in a dying moment, "—tell her the broth was always terrible, and I'm sorry about her commander's face. Some prices shouldn't be paid twice. Go."

Then the tube went dead, and the screaming from the vault changed key — from alarm to grief — and Petra understood exactly how much time they had bought, and exactly what it had cost.

She gripped Jude's hand and ran into the snow.

Behind them, the Guild of Impossible Things began, very quietly, to come apart.
