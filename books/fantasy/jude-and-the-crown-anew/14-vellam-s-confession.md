## 14. Vellam's Confession

*To be opened only by Petra Dunmore, and only after my death — or after she finds the ninth ledger, whichever comes first.*

Petra.

If you are reading this, then either I am dead or you have already decided I am guilty, and in this trade those two conditions are closer than either of us would like to admit. I taught you that. I taught you most of what you know about ledgers, which means I also taught you how to read one backwards, which means you have probably already worked out half of what I'm about to confess. Let me have the other half anyway. A man should be allowed to say his own sins aloud once, even if only to paper.

I emptied Jude.

There it is. The sentence I have rewritten four hundred times in four hundred different hands, and every version of it is true, and none of them is the whole truth, so let me start at the beginning like an honest broker and work forward.

You remember the founders' table as it is now: six old names on old chairs, drinking old wine, harmless as furniture. You were a child when you first saw them. You thought they were dignified. So did I, once. What you don't know — what no one under fifty knows — is what that table was *for*. The Guild of Impossible Things did not begin as a trade house, whatever our charter says. It began as a treasury. Specifically, it began as the treasury of the Godswar's last faction, the men and women who built the Crown Anew: the artifact that did not kill the gods but *bound* them, name by name, until divinity itself could be carried like coin and spent like coin. When the war ended and the Crown was broken into pages and scattered, the founders did not disband. They went into trade. Miracles, curses, memories. All of it inventory. All of it, eventually, meant to be reassembled.

For forty years I sat at that table and poured their wine and told myself I was keeping the peace from inside. That is the lie I want you to hear clearly, because everything else follows from it. I did not stay to keep the peace. I stayed because leaving would have meant the keystone page passing to someone with fewer scruples than mine, and for forty years my scruples were the only lock on the vault.

Then Jude arrived.

* * *

You will not remember the day they came through our doors because I made sure of it. Winter, sixteen years ago. A parcel with no sender, no destination, and no contents except a child — perhaps fourteen years old, perhaps younger; it is difficult to age someone who has no face in their own memory. The parcel tag read, in a hand I recognized immediately and have never been able to forget:

*Vessel, one (1). Empty if possible. Do not let the Founders complete the set.*

The child had eyes like a struck bell. They didn't cry. They stood in my office and asked me, politely, terribly, where they were supposed to be going, because they couldn't remember being anywhere at all. Not a name. Not a mother. Not the color of their own front door. And beneath the nothing — I felt it the moment I took their hand to steady them — something vast and patient, folded small, waiting to be filled.

Do you understand yet? The Crown needed a vessel. A mortal shape capacious enough to hold the assembled names without burning. The founders had searched for two generations. And here was the perfect candidate, delivered to the one man in the city who might still choose otherwise: a child already hollowed, already trained by whoever made them — trained so thoroughly that even empty, the reflexes remained. You've seen Jude fight. Of course you have. Blades remember what couriers cannot. That skill isn't talent, Petra. It's the residue of purpose. Someone built that body for a crown.

I could have delivered them. One signature. One entry in one ledger: *one vessel, received, condition good.* The founders would have rewarded me beyond anything the guild ever paid. And instead I did the only thing I have ever done in my life that I believe was wholly right: I falsified my own books.

I wrote the child in as an orphan courier intake. Standard guild practice; we took in dozens over the years. I burned the parcel tag. I invented a history — a village, a fire, the usual merciful fiction — and I gave it to the healers to seed, so that even the memory of having no memory would feel explained rather than ominous. And then, because a hollowed vessel does not stay hollow on its own, I performed the second emptiness myself. There is a rite for it. There is a rite for almost everything, if you know where the rites are kept. I took what remained in Jude — the god-shard coiled at the bottom of them, the thing that made their satchel hum and their shadow lean wrong at noon — and I folded it down beneath two years of blankness. Not gone. Sealed. A name too large for a person, pressed flat enough to fit inside one.

That is why Jude wakes with no yesterday. That is why the past stops at a clean edge like a cut page. I drew that edge. Every morning they wake without a history is a morning the Crown goes unworn. I have never regretted it. I have regretted nearly everything else.

* * *

Now the harder confession, the one that will make you angry, and rightly: the smuggling.

You found three prayers answered in the winter ledgers. There were ninety. Nine names missing from nine altars — you'll have heard by now, Jude walked into that ruin and saw the scrapings — and every one of those names passed through my hands before it vanished. I am the thief, Petra. I have been the thief for eleven years.

Here is what I discovered, sitting at the founders' table, pouring wine: the Crown Anew cannot be rebuilt from pages alone. Pages are instructions. To bind a god you must hold its *name*, whole and willing or whole and chained, and the founders' original cache was looted during the war's last winter by persons unknown. For decades the table believed the names destroyed. Then, eleven years ago, one of them — I will not write which; burn this page if you must but do not make me name a brother — found the first one. In a drought-town rain-broker's cellar, of all places, folded inside a weather contract. A god's name, alive, small enough to fit in a palm.

The table rejoiced. I understood, before any of them said it aloud, what rejoicing meant. A recovered name is not a rescued god. It is a part. And parts get assembled.

So I became the middleman. The Tithe — yes, the shrine on the salt road, the one that eats a memory per transaction — is mine, or was mine originally; the shrine came first, older than all of us, but I bent it to my use. Every name the founders' agents recovered passed through the Tithe on its way to the vault, and at the Tithe, in transactions the founders never saw itemized, I diverted them. Folded small. Smuggled out. Scattered across the country into hiding places only I know: inside rain contracts and marriage debts, inside the ledgers of honest merchants who don't know what they're carrying, inside the humming satchel of a courier whose emptiness makes them invisible to every divination the founders possess. No one searches a hollowed vessel. Why would they? The vessel was supposed to arrive full.

Jude has been carrying stolen gods for eleven years and doesn't know it. Neither did you. I chose you as auditor eight years ago not despite your oath but *because* of it — a swordspeaker can hear a name even folded flat, and I needed someone who might, someday, hear the truth in my books when I could no longer protect it by silence. If that sounds like I used you, I did. Add it to the account. I have used everyone. It is the only talent the founders ever actually taught me.

* * *

Why not destroy the names outright? You will ask. I asked myself for eleven years. Here is the answer, and it is the answer I least wanted: because a god's name cannot be destroyed, only hidden or held. Burn the page and the name simply falls back into the world, unclaimed, and the founders hunt it again — except now it screams, and screaming names draw the kind of attention that ends cities. And because — this part shames me more — some small coward in me could not bring himself to murder gods twice. They died once already, Petra. Four hundred years of silence. I told myself I was not killing them; I was keeping them safe until the world grew gentler. I may have been lying to myself. Ledgermen are good at that. We balance what we cannot bear by moving it to another column.

But understand what the alternative was. If I had reported the first name honestly — to you, to the church, to anyone — the founders would have learned their cache was found, accelerated the assembly, and there would be a crowned vessel within five years. A vessel, Petra. And I knew exactly which vessel. If I had exposed them publicly, the church would have marched, the remnants of the old gods would have answered, and the Godswar would have restarted with better bookkeeping. Either road led through a field of the dead. So I chose the third road, the narrow one, the crooked one: steal from thieves, hide from hunters, and buy time. Eleven years of time. It was almost enough.

It is no longer enough. By the time you read this, the founders will have noticed the shortfall in the vault. I have been covering the gaps with fakes — folded blanks, names I wrote myself out of grief and grammar — but a fake name doesn't hum, and someone at that table finally listened closely. They know a theft occurred. They do not yet know the thief. I have days, perhaps. You may have fewer.

* * *

What I ask of you is nothing. That is not humility; it is strategy, and I want to be honest about even my honesty. If you expose me — to the guild, to the church, to anyone — the names surface, the founders move, and everything I emptied Jude to prevent happens anyway with worse actors holding the pieces. If you protect the secret, you become what I have been: a keeper of stolen gods, growing old in the dark, balancing accounts no one may ever audit. I would not wish it on you. I did it so you would never have to. But I am old, and my hands shake when I pour now, and the table has begun to notice that too.

Jude must never learn what they are. Not from me, not from the founders, not from the Crown. You will be tempted — the truth sits in you like a stone, I know, I have carried its twin for sixteen years — but consider what knowing does. The moment Jude understands they are the vessel, the seal weakens. Emptiness holds only while it is unquestioned. Their love for you, their fear, their stubborn decency at two in the morning over bad tea — all of it real, all of it theirs, and all of it resting on ground I dug out from under them. If they ever look down, Petra, the ground gives way. Keep them looking forward. That is the cruelest thing I have ever asked of anyone, and I am asking it of the most honest person I have ever taught, and I am sorry. Put the apology in the ledger. Charge it to my account.

One more thing, and then I will let you hate me in peace. The keystone page — the instruction-sheet that tells the Crown how to be a crown — is no longer in the founders' vault. I moved it the night I emptied Jude. Its location is written nowhere, which means it exists now in exactly one place: the memory of a man who eats memories for a living, parked in trust at a shrine on the salt road, retrievable only by paying the Tithe's price. I know what that price is. I know what you already paid to learn the buyer's name, because I designed the transaction you walked into. I am sorry about your commander's face. I have carried mine for forty years and it has not gotten lighter.

If the founders come for the Tithe before you do, the keystone dies with the shrine, and the Crown becomes a very expensive paperweight. That was always the final lock. Not steel. Oblivion, contracted and notarized.

* * *

I have written this letter eleven times and each time it has ended differently, so let me end it plainly, the way I should have ended everything.

I am not a good man. I am a careful one, which in this trade passes for goodness the way counterfeit coin passes in candlelight. I stole gods to save them and saved a child by taking their life away, and I have signed my name to both acts in a hand you would recognize, because that hand taught yours. Whatever you decide — expose me, forgive me, burn this letter and pretend you never read it — decide quickly. The omens are turning. You'll have seen them yourself by now: rivers running the wrong way, soldiers dreaming in tongues dead since the Godswar, candles leaning toward people who haven't spoken a name in fifteen years. The hidden names know they are hidden. They are beginning to call to one another. And things that call to one another, Petra, can be answered.

The founders are answering.

Last night I sat at the table and watched Six pour wine with his left hand — his *left* hand, the hand he lost at Greyford Bridge, grown back wrong and gleaming — and he looked at me across the candlelight and said, mildly, as if discussing freight rates: "Vellam. Where do you keep the winter names?"

And I smiled the smile you are thinking of right now, and lied to him in the voice I taught you to trust, and understood, as the candle between us leaned toward him instead of me, that I have run out of ledger.

Whatever comes next, check the ninth ledger. And Petra — when the war weather gathers, and it is gathering — remember that I loved this guild the way a father loves a child he has done unforgivable things to protect. Badly, and completely, and far too late to matter.

— M.V.

*P.S. The satchel hums because I tuned it that way. If it ever goes silent, do not open it. Come find me instead.*
