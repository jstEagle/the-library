## 15. War Weather

The river outside Greyford ran backward for three days before anyone official noticed.

Petra noticed on the first morning, because she was awake at the hour when rivers are honest — that grey sliver of dawn when the mist lifts and the water shows you what it's doing without ornament. She stood on the guildhall's back step with her tea going cold in one hand and watched the Ash carry its leaves upstream, patient as a clerk counting coins into the wrong drawer.

She had audited enough impossible things to know the difference between a miracle and a symptom. A miracle had a broker, a price, a signature. A symptom had nothing but itself, repeating.

The Ash running backward had no signature. Which meant it wasn't being done to anyone. It was being *felt* by something, the way a floorboard creaks under a weight no one can see.

"Third time this week," said Sefa, the night porter, coming out with her own cup. She was a broad, unhurried woman who had worked the guildhall doors through two floods and one small apocalypse of bees, and she did not startle easily. "Fisherfolk down at Tallow Bend say the eels are leaving. Whole river of them, swimming the wrong way like they've got an appointment."

"Where do eels go," Petra asked, "when they know something?"

Sefa considered this with the seriousness the question deserved. "Somewhere older," she decided. "That's my guess."

Petra drank her cold tea. In her breast pocket, wrapped in oilcloth and folded smaller than grief, the keystone page from the Crown Anew sat against her heart like a second pulse. She had carried it for eleven days now. It did not hum. It did not whisper. It simply *was*, with the terrible completeness of a thing that has waited four hundred years to be whole again, and knows exactly how long it is willing to wait.

*Hold fast,* she told herself, which was what her commander used to say, which was the only prayer the swordspeakers had ever been permitted, because everything else was already taken by gods.

Then she went inside and opened the ledgers, because the world could end after the books were balanced or during, but never before.

* * *

By noon she had three columns of omens, and the columns were growing faster than she could rule lines under them.

Item: the Ash, reversed, three days running. Item: eels departing Tallow Bend in numbers the fisherfolk described as "biblical," which was a strange word for them to reach for, since the church of the Nine had spent four centuries teaching this country that there was nothing left in the world worth biblical language.

Item: at Fort Marren, forty miles east where the garrison watched the old border, soldiers were dreaming in dead tongues. This came to her not as rumor but as paper — a requisition slip from the garrison quartermaster requesting, of all things, *forty copies of a dream-dictionary, any language, priority*. The guild filled stranger orders weekly, but Petra pulled the slip and held it a long time. Soldiers didn't buy dream-dictionaries. Sergeants bought them for soldiers who woke the barracks screaming words no living mouth should remember.

She knew some of those words herself. Her oath knew more of them than she did — that was the nature of swordspeech, the names lived in you like swallowed coals, and lately, in the hour before waking, Petra could feel them stirring, turning over in their sleep, as if something vast and buried had rolled closer and they'd felt the warmth of its breath through the ground.

Item: the candles. Every candle in the guildhall leaned toward the vault stair, all day, all night, regardless of draft. The chandlers' apprentices had started propping the tallow sticks upright with wax puddles and giving her looks. Nobody said anything. The Guild of Impossible Things had a genius for not saying things, and right now the silence had a texture, a pressure, like the air before hail.

Item, and this was the one that made her stop ruling lines: the prayers had changed.

For months, ever since Vellam's smuggling ring had first crossed her desk, the prayers landing on guild paper had been the usual mortal fare — rain for crops, a son home from the road, a tumor gone by morning. But in the last week, the prayer-ledger's intake had shifted. The requests had gotten older. Stranger. One, written in a child's careful hand, asked simply: *please tell the lady of the river we haven't forgotten her name, even if you have.* Another was not words at all but a pressed flower, a white crocus that bloomed out of season, with no note — and when Petra touched it, every candle on the main floor leaned toward the vault at once, hard enough that two guttered out.

The old gods weren't answering prayers anymore. They were *sending* them. Petitions, not pleas. And petitions meant someone expected to be in a position to grant them.

Or expected to have to negotiate.

She thought of the freed names that had arrived at the guildhall weeks ago, not as conquerors but as supplicants, asking what mortals intended for them. She thought of the long night of small-hours diplomacy, the bargain struck — mortality shared among willing bearers, no crown, no war. She thought it had held. She had written it into the new ledger herself; she had believed ink was still stronger than iron in at least one room of the world.

But the founders were not all dead. The founders had votes, and vaults, and patience measured in generations. And somewhere below her feet, behind three locks and a warding Vellam had died to keep shut, the Crown Anew sat in pieces, missing only what she carried against her heart.

The river ran backward. The candles leaned. Somewhere, something enormous was turning over in its sleep, and everyone in the world who had ever learned to read omens was reading the same page.

* * *

The church came calling at dusk, which was unlike them. The Nine's clergy kept banker's hours and had, in Petra's experience, the institutional courage of a filing cabinet.

There were two of them. The elder was a canon named Ossory whom Petra had dealt with twice over contract disputes — once over a rain-brokerage the church considered blasphemous until the drought towns started tithing, and once over a memory-brokerage the church considered *very* blasphemous right up until a bishop's widow bought back her wedding day. He was a dry, precise man with the face of a man who has read every book in his library and liked none of them.

He did not come into the guildhall. That was the first wrong thing. He stood on the steps under the lintel, hat in hand, and his junior — a young deacon with ink-stained fingers and frightened eyes — stood one step below him like a man positioned to run.

"Dunmore." Ossory's voice was level, but he pitched it low, and that was the second wrong thing. Churchmen performed their volume. "May I speak with you plainly? Not as contract parties."

"You may speak however you like on public steps," Petra said. "Plainly is extra."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth — respect, or its funeral. "We have a problem," he said, "that I believe predates our problem. My superiors disagree. They believe your house caused it. I have spent thirty years reading your ledgers, Dunmore, professionally and otherwise, and I know the difference between a fire and an arsonist, and what is moving in this country just now—" he stopped himself, jaw tightening, "—is weather. Not a hand. Weather."

"Go on."

"The garrison at Marren sent us a soldier last month who dreamed in Old Vessic. Fluent Old Vessic. Conjugating verbs that died with the Godswar." Ossory's hands, she noticed, were not quite steady around his hat brim. "We questioned him. Gently. He told us the dreams had a direction. Do you understand me? Dreams don't have directions. This one did. He said they came from *underneath*, and that they were getting louder, and that whatever was speaking was counting down."

The keystone page lay warm against Petra's sternum. Or perhaps that was her own heart, working harder than it liked.

"What's it counting down to?" she asked.

"If I knew that, I would be standing somewhere else." Ossory put his hat back on, and the gesture had finality in it, a man closing a ledger he wished he'd never opened. "My superiors are convening a Synod of Inquiry. They will come to this guildhall within the fortnight, formally, with writs. If they find what I suspect is in your vaults, there will be no negotiation, no small-hours bargains, none of your house's elegant arithmetic. There will be a purge, and it will not stop at your door, because fear never does." He looked at her then, straight on, and for one moment the dry little canon was gone and something much older looked out — a man who had spent his life serving dead gods' empty houses and knew exactly how hungry living ones could be. "I am telling you this because a war between your founders and my church would kill everything either of us pretends to protect. You have days, Dunmore. Not weeks. Whatever you intend to do with what you're carrying — do it fast."

He descended the steps. At the bottom he paused, without turning.

"The soldier," he said. "Before we released him, he asked us one question. He asked why, if the old gods were dead, someone was teaching their names to the dark underneath." A beat. "I didn't have an answer. I hoped you might."

They walked off into the blue evening, and Petra stood on her own doorstep feeling the city breathe around her — smoke and river and lamplight, eight thousand small lives, none of whom knew the ground under them had started keeping time.

* * *

She wrote to Jude that night, though Jude was two days south on the salt road closing out the last of the old miracle contracts — the guild was winding down the impossible trade contract by contract, and every closure required a courier's signature in person. The letter was short, because letters could be read by anyone, and shortness was a kind of armor:

*Omens converging. Church synod within a fortnight, hostile writs. Something beneath Marren counting down. Come home. Not for the guild. For the conversation we promised ourselves we'd finish.*

She read it back, struck her name, added *— P,* then sat with the pen hovering, wanting to add the third thing, the true thing, the thing about how the candle in her room had leaned toward the door all evening as if even fire understood waiting for someone.

She sealed it instead. Some sentences kept better spoken than written, and she intended to be there when this one landed.

Then she went down to the vault.

Three locks. Vellam's warding, which still smelled faintly of him — pipe smoke and chalk and stubbornness — though he was eleven days in the ground and the door remembered him better than most of the living did. Inside, on a plain table under a lamp that burned without leaning (she had warded it personally; a woman needed one honest flame), the Crown Anew lay in its pieces, and beside it, in a shallow dish of salt, the nine scraped god-names recovered from the temple ruin, each folded into its own small envelope like seeds.

She had counted them every night. Tonight she counted them twice.

Nine envelopes. Nine names. Safe.

But the Crown's fragments had shifted. She was sure of it — a hair's width, a jeweler's difference, the way you can tell a locked room has been breathed in. The broken circlet lay a finger's breadth closer to the table's edge than it had yesterday, pointed, all its broken points aimed outward, toward the stairs, toward the street, toward the fort at Marren and whatever slept beneath it, counting.

Petra stood very still in the lamplight and listened with the only instrument she had left — the oath, the swallowed coals of it, the names sleeping in her blood.

They were not asleep anymore. They were standing at attention.

And far away, so deep beneath the earth that she felt it in her teeth rather than her ears, something answered them — slow, vast, patient, keeping perfect time.

Like a clock. Like a countdown. Like a heart.

She blew out the lamp, took the keystone page from her breast, and held it in her fist in the dark, where no one — not the church, not the founders, not the dark underneath — could see what she was deciding.

Two days for Jude to come home. A fortnight before the synod arrived.

And underneath everything, getting louder, the count went on.
