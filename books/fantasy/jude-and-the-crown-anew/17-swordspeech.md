## 17. Swordspeech

The wind at Greyford Bridge smelled of iron and old snow, and it carried a sound Petra had not heard in fifteen years: the ring of drawn steel that was not hers.

She stood at the center of the span with the keystone page pressed flat against her sternum, inside her coat, over her heart, where it had been since Jude's voice cracked through their bond-signal an hour before dawn — *they're coming, Petra, all of them, I can't hold the far end alone* — and she had run, because there had never been any question of running the other way. The river below was high with meltwater, brown and furious, slamming itself against the bridge's stone legs as if it too wanted this crossing ended. Mist rose off it in ropes. The morning sun came through sideways, thin and gold, lighting nothing warmly.

Jude stood twenty paces off, at the bridge's northern throat, breathing hard. There was blood on them — not much of theirs. Their sword hung low and easy in a grip Petra now understood they'd been carrying in their bones for longer than either of them had known. When Jude remembered everything on that terrible night two weeks past, they had also remembered what they were: the vessel the founders had hollowed out to hold the Crown Anew, the empty cup shaped for a god. They had remembered, and chosen Petra anyway, and every day since had been a small argument with that choice, won quietly, over breakfast, over ledgers, over the touch of hands.

Now the argument was coming up the salt road with banners.

"Twelve," Jude said. "Maybe fourteen. The two outriders are enforcers — guild-trained, like us. The rest are founders' men." They turned, and in the flat gray light their face was very calm, which Petra knew was how Jude looked when they were most afraid. "Vellam isn't with them."

"I know," Petra said.

"You hoped."

"I did." She let herself have one breath of grief, no more. Vellam was dead or dying or worse behind them in the vaults of the guildhall, holding a door against men he had once called brothers, and Petra could not afford to feel the whole weight of that yet. Grief was a debt; she would pay it later, when there was later. "It doesn't change what we do."

"And what we do is—" Jude gestured with their chin at the approaching column, at the mist, at the impossible arithmetic of two against twelve. "Hold a bridge."

"Hold a bridge," Petra agreed.

The keystone page pulsed faintly against her chest, warm as a sleeping animal. It was only paper — a single leaf of vellum torn from the Crown Anew's binding, the page that held the artifact's *grammar*, the instruction by which all its stolen god-names could be read and ruled. Without it the Crown was jewelry. With it, the Crown was the end of free gods and the beginning of a war that had already killed more people than anyone alive could count. The founders' cabal wanted it back. The old gods' remnants, stirring in the rivers and the dreams, would want it destroyed. And Petra Dunmore, last oath-bound swordspeaker of an order everyone else believed extinct, intended to make sure that neither side ever held it again.

There was exactly one way she knew to do that.

She had sworn she never would again.

* * *

The order of Swordspeakers had not fought with swords, whatever the ballads said. They had fought with words, and the sword was only the instrument that made the words true. *Speak a true name,* Commander Ashe had drilled into her, walking the training yard with his hands clasped behind his back while ten-year-old Petra sweated through forms in the summer dust, *and bind a true thing.* Not a name you were told. A name you had earned — earned by knowing a thing so completely, loving it or hating it so honestly, that to speak it aloud was to lay your own truth down beside it like a wager. The oath took the wager seriously. Speak falsely, and the oath took your voice. Speak truly, and the oath took payment anyway: a memory, a season, a piece of the speaker's life, ground fine and spent like coin. Every true name spoken was a small death paid in installments.

Petra had spoken three true names in her life. She could still remember the first two. She could not remember what the third had cost her, only that afterward there was a hole in the shape of something, and she had stopped trying to find what fit it.

Then the Godswar's last embers died, and the church burned the order's charter, and her brothers and sisters were scattered or worse, and Commander Ashe went into the ground at Greyford — of all places, this place, this bridge town — and Petra Dunmore had folded her oath away like winter clothes and become instead an auditor, a keeper of ledgers, a woman who counted other people's miracles and touched none of them.

Fifteen years. She had told herself the silence was peace. It had been, mostly. But she had felt the oath wake in her the night a god's name sang from Jude's satchel and every candle in the guildhall leaned toward her like flowers toward sun — felt it stir under fifteen years of ash and open one eye — and she had known even then, dread sharpening into purpose, that if it ever came to this bridge, this day, she would pay anything the oath asked.

Because she had found a true name. She had been carrying it for weeks without letting herself look at it directly.

The name was not the Crown's. Names of artifacts were just labels; the oath didn't deal in labels. The name was the keystone's *truth* — what it truly was, beneath the vellum and the founders' ambitions and four hundred years of fear. Petra had read every surviving account of the Godswar. She knew what the Crown Anew had been built to do: to take the names of gods and hold them, fold them small enough to fit in a palm, and rule them. Everyone had called it a crown because crowns are worn by kings. But that wasn't its true shape. Its true shape —

Its true shape was a ledger.

An account book. A record of debts owed by the divine to mortal ambition, kept so that the debt could be enforced forever. The founders thought they were building a throne. They had actually built a book of accounts, and Petra Dunmore, who had spent her whole adult life keeping accounts honest, knew a thing about ledgers that kings and founders and gods alike always forgot:

A ledger binds both parties who sign it.

If she spoke the keystone's true name and bound herself to it — bound herself as co-signatory, as the second party to the account — then the keystone could not be carried by anyone else. Not by force, not by theft, not by the founders' rituals or the gods' wrath. An account with two names on it requires both signatures to be altered. As long as Petra Dunmore lived and remembered herself, the keystone was hers, and only hers, and dead weight in everyone else's hands.

And the price? The oath would collect continuously, for as long as she held the bond. Memory by memory. Season by season. Everything she was, spent slowly, like a candle burning from both ends, until either the bond released or Petra Dunmore was someone else entirely — a woman with a stranger's face and a hole where her whole life used to fit.

Triumph laced with doom. That was the taste of it. That had always been the taste of swordspeech.

"Petra." Jude had come close. Their hand found hers around the keystone's warmth through the wool of her coat. "I can hear you deciding something. You get a line right here—" they touched the space between her brows— "when you're doing sums you hate."

"I'm going to speak," Petra said. "A true name. First time in fifteen years."

Jude went very still. Of all people, they understood what that cost; they had watched the Tithe eat Petra's memory of her commander's face, had held her afterward while she grieved a man she could no longer picture. "What will it take?"

"Everything, eventually. While I hold the bond, it takes as it likes." She made herself smile. "You'll have to keep my books."

"Petra—"

"It's the only way the keystone stays out of every hand but mine. And mine—" she pressed the page harder against her heart— "mine is the only hand that knows what to do with it. Which is nothing. Hold it, and hold it, and hold it, until we find a better answer. Until the gods and the mortals talk instead of burn. I can be the lock, Jude. Someone has to be the lock."

"The founders will kill you to break the bond."

"They'll try." She looked down the bridge at the column, close enough now to pick out faces. In front walked a broad man in gray with a founder's chain half-hidden under his collar — Corvane, third chair, the one with the soft voice and the accountant's patience. He had come personally. That told Petra how badly they wanted the page. "But here's what they don't understand about oaths. You can't kill your way out of one. Kill me and the bond doesn't break — it *settles*. My estate transfers. To my named heir." She met Jude's eyes. "Which means we need to finish a form before they arrive."

Understanding moved across Jude's face like weather. "Petra, no. If you die, it lands on *me*—"

"If I die, someone has to hold the door until a better answer comes. You've already proven you can carry unbearable things and stay kind." Her voice roughened. "That's the whole job, as far as I can tell."

"That's not fair."

"No," Petra agreed. "Almost nothing true is."

* * *

Corvane stopped his men forty paces off, at the foot of the bridge, and raised an open hand, and smiled his patient smile. "Dunmore. Courier." He nodded at each of them in turn, courteous as a broker at closing. "You've cost us a great deal of money this month. I'd admire it if it weren't so expensive."

"You can afford it," Petra said. "Founders always can. That's the trouble with founding things."

"The keystone page, please. Walk away, both of you, rich and forgotten, and this ends without unpleasantness. The Crown isn't for you to decide about. It's bigger than you. Bigger than the guild."

"It's a ledger," Petra said. "Nothing is smaller."

Something flickered in Corvane's composure — the first hairline crack. Behind him, the enforcers shifted, hands drifting toward hilts, reading the geometry of the bridge: narrow span, two defenders, high water, no retreat worth taking.

"Last offer," Corvane said.

"Actually," Petra said, "here's mine."

She drew her sword.

Not for fighting. She drew it and drove it point-down into a seam between the bridge stones, and gripped the pommel with both hands the way the order had gripped it for a thousand years, and she let the oath rise.

Fifteen years of silence broke like ice on a spring river.

The power didn't roar; that was the ballads' mistake. It *opened*, quiet and vast, like a window in a wall she'd forgotten was a wall. The world sharpened. She could hear the river's individual stones turning over. She could hear Corvane's heartbeat quicken. She could hear, underneath everything, faint and patient, the sound the oath made when it opened its books and waited to see what she would wager.

She thought of Commander Ashe and could not remember his face, and grieved him in advance.

She thought of the guildhall candles leaning toward her like flowers.

She thought of Jude, and deliberately did not think of losing Jude, because the oath heard thoughts too, and she would not put that on the table.

Then she spoke.

"*Ledger of the Divided Crown, whose every entry is a debt and every debt a chain: I know you. You were built to bind, and binding is your nature, and a binding cuts both ways. I am the second signature. I am the counterweight. While I live and remember myself, no hand but mine carries you, no ritual opens you, no crown closes upon a head through you. So bound. So witnessed. So written.*"

The word *witnessed* rang out over the water, and the river answered — a long shudder through the current, the old gods' remnants feeling the shape of it, and somewhere downstream something vast turned over in its sleep and listened.

The keystone page flared against her chest like a struck match. Heat, then cold, then a sensation like a quill writing directly onto her ribs. The oath reached into her and began, gently, horribly, to collect.

The first thing it took, she felt go: the smell of the guildhall's winter kitchen, bread and tallow, gone as cleanly as if it had never existed. Then the name of her first fencing instructor. Then the color of her childhood bedroom wall. Small things first. It was polite that way. It was the politeness of a landlord.

Her knees buckled. Jude caught her before the stone did.

Across the bridge, Corvane had gone pale. His men had gone paler. They had grown up on stories of swordspeakers the way children grow up on wolves, and none of them had ever actually seen one stand and *speak*, and the speaking had changed the light on the water.

"Kill her," Corvane said, and his voice cracked on it, and the enforcers charged.

What followed lasted ninety seconds and Petra experienced most of it from her knees, gripping her sword, feeding the bond memories she could feel leaving like birds from an open hand — the layout of a market in a city she'd loved, her mother's laugh, the year of her own birth — while Jude did something at the bridge's throat that she would remember, in the parts of it she got to keep, for the rest of however long she had.

They fought like the vessel they'd been made to be. Fourteen trained blades against one courier, on a span wide enough for three abreast, and Jude moved through them the way Petra's oath moved through her memory: inevitably, spending themselves without hesitation, sword flashing in the thin gold light. Two enforcers went into the river. Three disarmed themselves against Jude's guard and sat down on the stones, hands up, done. Corvane himself drew at the end, and Jude broke his blade and put their own point at his throat and held it there, trembling — not with fear, Petra saw. With the effort of *not*.

"It's done," Jude called to him, breath ragged. "The bond is set. You felt it — you all felt it. The page is hers. Kill her and it settles to me, kill us both and it settles to whoever we named, and there is no number of murders that unwrites it. Go home. Tell the table what you saw here."

Corvane stared at Petra, kneeling, white-faced, holding a bridge with a word. Then at the river, where the water had begun, very slightly, to flow the wrong way.

"Four hundred years," he said quietly. "We waited four hundred years." He sheathed the stump of his sword. "You've started something, Dunmore. The gods will come to you now. Do you understand? You've made yourself the address."

"I'm an auditor," Petra managed. "I keep office hours."

He laughed — one short, unhappy bark — and gathered his men, and the founders' gray column receded up the salt road like weather moving off.

* * *

Afterward, the bridge was very quiet. The mist burned away. The sun climbed enough to mean it.

Petra knelt at her sword a long time after she needed to, taking inventory. The losses so far were small and strange: she could no longer recall her grandmother's recipe for barley soup, or the exact words of the guild's founding charter, or what rain had felt like on her face the day she'd taken her vows. She tested the big things. Her mother's face — still there. The guildhall's corridors — there. Jude—

Jude was there, kneeling in front of her now, wiping blood from their cheekbone with the heel of their hand, looking at her with an expression she recognized and suddenly, urgently, wanted to keep forever, which was exactly the wrong instinct to have while bonded to an oath that collected whatever it liked.

"How much?" Jude asked softly.

"So far? Soups and charter clauses." She tried to smile. "It starts cheap. It won't stay cheap."

"Then we write it all down." Jude said it fast, urgent, already pulling the little courier's notebook from their coat, the one they'd carried since before they could remember carrying it. "Everything. Tonight, tomorrow, every night — you dictate, I record. Dates, faces, smells, stupid jokes, the way you take your tea. If the oath takes it from you—" their voice caught— "then it has to take it from both of us. Because I'll still have the pages. And I'll give them back to you. Every morning if I have to. I'll read you yourself until you believe me."

Petra looked at this person — memoryless once, emptied once, escaped from being a god's cup, choosing her anyway, twice, three times, in every version of the story — and felt the bond tighten around her heart like a second ribcage, and found that she did not regret it. That was the doom and the triumph braided together: she had bought the future with herself, and she would do it again.

"There's one thing to write first," she said.

Jude uncapped the pen, waiting.

"*First entry,*" Petra dictated, watching their face, memorizing it while she still could. "*Petra Dunmore holds the bridge. Whatever she forgets, she chose. Whoever she becomes, she chose that too — she chose it standing up.*"

Jude wrote it in their careful courier's hand, and closed the notebook, and helped her pull her sword from the stone.

Far downstream, where the river ran backward, the water had gone still as glass — and on that glass, faint as frost forming, shapes were gathering. Petitioners, arriving early. The gods had heard the second signature, and they were coming to the address.

Petra stood at the center of Greyford Bridge, lighter by a soup recipe and a charter clause and the color of a childhood wall, heavier by a kingdom's worth of consequence, and watched them come.
