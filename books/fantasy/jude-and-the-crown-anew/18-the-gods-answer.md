## 18. The Gods Answer

**PETRA**

The candles went first.

Petra had been awake for thirty hours, drafting the terms of a surrender that was also a coronation, when every flame in the guildhall stood up straight at once — not flickering, *leaning*, the way wheat leans toward a passing storm. She had seen candles lean once before, in this same hall, over a god's name folded small enough to fit in a palm. That had been one name. This was different. This was the whole ledger of them.

"Jude," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "They're here."

Jude came down the stairs two at a time with the keystone page still strapped against their chest under their coat, where it had lived since Greyford Bridge, where it hummed like a second heart. They stopped on the last step and looked at the windows.

Outside, the winter dark was full of lights.

Not lanterns. Not the city's lamps spilling up from Candle Row. These were points of pale gold hanging in the air above the guildhall's courtyard, arranged in no pattern Petra recognized and then, slowly, horribly, in all the patterns at once — a ring, then a wheel, then a constellation she'd only ever seen inked into the margins of pre-war maps, the one the cartographers called *the Old Court*.

"They're not coming in," Jude said.

"No."

"They're waiting to be *invited*."

"Yes." Petra closed the draft she'd been writing. Her hand left a damp print on the page. Four hundred years ago, gods did not wait to be invited anywhere. Four hundred years ago, they arrived the way weather arrives, and mortals built doors that could be broken. "That's what frightens me."

She crossed the hall and unbarred the great door herself. The wood was old guild-oak, carved with the Guild of Impossible Things' motto — *WE CARRY WHAT CANNOT BE CARRIED* — and her fingers found the grooves of the letters the way they found the hilt of a sword she hadn't worn in fifteen years.

Then she opened the door, and the night walked in.

It didn't walk, exactly. It *arrived*, the way dawn arrives: gradually and then completely. The courtyard lights became figures without ever quite being figures — tall shapes made of candlelight and river-smell and the particular silence of high places. Nine of them. The nine names cut from the temple altars. Behind them, fainter, a crowd of others: names smuggled in satchels and prayer-books and one memorable case inside a wheel of cheese, all the stolen divinity of four centuries gathered now in her doorway like travelers at an inn.

One of them stepped forward. It had taken the shape of an old woman carrying a bundle of wheat, because — Petra would understand this later — it had read her ledger, or the part of herself that was still a ledger, and knew she trusted things that looked like work.

"You are the keeper," the old-woman-shape said. Its voice was every voice that had ever prayed aloud. "We have come to ask a question."

Petra's oath stirred in her chest, fifteen years asleep, and whispered: *speak a true name, bind a true thing.* Her hand ached for a sword that was ash in a grave in the eastern hills.

"Ask it," said Petra Dunmore, last swordspeaker, keeper of ledgers, alone in a doorway between the mortal world and everything it had buried.

And the god asked:

"What do you intend for us?"

* * *

**JUDE**

Jude had died once, at Greyford Bridge, or something adjacent to dying — they remembered now, that was the cruelty of remembering, they remembered *everything* — and even that had been quieter than this.

They stood at the foot of the stairs with the keystone warm against their sternum and watched Petra hold the door the way she held ledgers: open, unflinching, ready to record whatever entered. The nine shapes filled the hall without displacing anything. Candles bent toward them like courtiers. Dust motes hung motionless in the air, as if even the small physics of the room were listening.

Jude should have been terrified. Part of them was. But another part — the part that had been emptied, the part Vellam had hollowed out to keep it from being used — looked at those nine waiting shapes and felt something worse than fear.

Recognition.

Because Jude knew what it was to stand in a doorway with no self, waiting to be told what you were for.

"We don't know yet," Petra was saying. She hadn't flinched at the question. Of course she hadn't; she'd spent three days drafting the answer and had thrown away every version that pretended certainty. "We don't intend anything for you. That's rather the point."

The wheat-bearer tilted its head, and the light of it shifted through colors Jude had no names for.

"For four hundred years," it said, "we were *intended*. Intended by the Crown, which wore us like rings. Intended by the founders who are reassembling it. Intended by priests who starved themselves to hear us and kings who burned cities to own us. We have been carried in your guild's satchels like contraband. And now we stand in your hall, and the woman at the door says she intends nothing." A pause, vast as a season turning. "We find we do not know how to be unintended."

"Then learn," said Jude.

Every shape turned toward them. The temperature of the room changed — not colder, not warmer, just *more*, the way a room is more when everyone looks at you at once. The keystone pulsed against Jude's chest, and beneath their ribs something answered it, something old and patient that had ridden in their blood since before Vellam scraped them clean.

"You carry our crown," said the wheat-bearer.

"I carry a page," Jude said. "A keystone. Words. I've been words before. It didn't go well." Their voice shook, and they let it. "I know what it is to be a vessel. I know what it is to be *intended*. Someone intended me to be the Crown's hands, its throat, its slave wearing a courier's face. Someone else loved me enough to empty me instead. So believe me when I tell you—" and here their eyes found Petra's across the candlelit hall, and held there, anchored— "being intended is not the same as being alive. You're asking the wrong question."

The silence stretched. Somewhere upstairs, the guild's night-bell rang two o'clock, absurdly ordinary.

"Then what question," said the wheat-bearer, very quietly, "should we ask?"

"That's yours to find," Jude said. "That's what mortality is. Nobody hands it to you finished."

* * *

**PETRA**

Later — much later, in the small hours when the negotiation had moved from the doorway to the long table in the counting room, gods seated among brokers' chairs like giants borrowing furniture — Petra understood what had happened at the door.

Jude had answered a god the way you answer a frightened animal: honestly, gently, without reaching. And the god had believed them, because gods, it turned out, were excellent judges of truth. They'd had four hundred years of nothing but prayers to practice on.

Now the counting room smelled of tallow and cold tea. Master Quill, the guild's oldest broker, had come down in his nightshirt and refused to leave, on the grounds that he had negotiated with a storm once and saw no reason to be impressed by nine deities. He sat with his ledger open, recording everything, because someone had to and because it calmed him. Petra sat at the head of the table with the draft she'd burned three times and rewritten four. Jude sat beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, and the keystone lay between them on the table like a hostage neither side would name.

"So summarize it again," said the wheat-bearer, whose name, offered shyly, was Harrow-of-the-Fields, and who had once been worshipped by an entire river valley and now kept glancing at Quill's ledger with something like anxiety. "You propose that we... disperse."

"Distribute," Petra corrected. "Among willing bearers. Mortals who choose to carry a god the way my couriers carry parcels — knowingly, contractually, revocably." She tapped the draft. "No crown. No vessel. No single throat for all of you to pour down. Instead, hundreds of small throats. A farmer carries Harrow-of-the-Fields and knows when to plant. A midwife carries the name of easy births. A ferryman carries the river-god, if the river-god consents to be so modestly housed."

"And if a bearer dies?"

"The god moves on. Or rests. Or ends, if they choose." Petra met the ancient light of the thing's eyes. "Mortality. Shared out. It's not servitude and it isn't glory. It's *tenancy*. You'd feel weather again. Hunger. Grief. Death, eventually, unless you keep finding new hands."

The nine shapes were silent. In the silence Petra heard the sound she would remember for the rest of her life, which was the sound of gods considering their own deaths, and finding the idea not horrifying but — strange as it seemed — *clean*.

"The Crown made us eternal," said a second shape, narrow and bright, whose name was Lantern-in-Fog. "Eternity was the punishment. We only understood that afterward." It turned its light toward the keystone. "And that? What happens to that?"

"Nothing good happens to it," Jude said flatly. "It goes in a fire, or a vault deeper than the founders can reach, or I swallow it and dare someone to dig."

"You would not survive—"

"I've survived worse. I've survived *remembering*."

Petra put her hand over Jude's on the table. The gods watched the gesture with an attention that made her skin prickle — the attention of beings who had once demanded worship and were now, tentatively, studying affection like a language they'd forgotten.

"It will be dealt with after the terms are agreed," she said. "One crisis at a time. That's guild policy."

Harrow-of-the-Fields made a sound that might have been laughter, if avalanches could laugh softly. "Guild policy. We ruled harvests across a continent, and our fate comes down to guild policy and a burned draft." The light of it dimmed, gentled. "Very well, keeper. Show us your terms. All of them. And know that if there is a clause in which we end up owned — by your guild, by your founders, by any crown however small — we have waited four hundred years, and we can wait four hundred more, and the war you fear will come anyway."

Petra slid the draft down the table.

Quill dipped his pen.

And that was when the doors of the counting room opened without being opened, and Master Vellam stood in the frame — old, gray-robed, leaning on his cane, looking at nine gods in his guildhall with an expression Petra had never seen on him before.

Not guilt. Not triumph.

*Relief.*

"You started without me," Vellam said hoarsely. "Good. I've been preparing my testimony for forty years." He limped forward into the candlelight. "But before anyone signs anything, there's something about the Crown — something about what the founders really built it *from* — that you need to hear. Because it changes the terms. And because it involves the courier."

The keystone, on the table, went cold as grave-dirt.

Jude's hand found Petra's under the table and gripped hard.

Vellam pulled out a chair, sat among the gods like a man sitting down to a long-overdue confession, and began: "Forty years ago, the founders needed a vessel, and the vessel they chose was not born. It was *made*—"
