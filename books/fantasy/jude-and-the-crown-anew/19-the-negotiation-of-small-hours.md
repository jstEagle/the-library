## 19. The Negotiation of Small Hours

The candles never guttered. That was the first thing Petra noticed, in the way she noticed everything now — as a broker notices, weighing cost against coin. A candle should die. These had been burning since the god-names arrived at dusk, and they had not lost a finger's width of wax. They leaned toward the long table in the guildhall's counting room like a congregation leaning toward a pulpit, and they would lean there until someone gave them permission to stop.

Petra sat at one end of the table with a ledger open before her and a pot of cold tea at her elbow, because if this was going to be a negotiation, then by every god living and dead, it would be conducted properly. Terms in writing. Consideration exchanged. Witnesses noted.

The witnesses were the problem.

There were forty-one names in the room. She could see three of them: Jude, hollow-eyed and half-remembered, sitting close enough that their knee touched hers under the table and did not move away. And Vellam's empty chair, which had stopped being a chair an hour ago and become something more like an absence with opinions.

The other thirty-eight were the gods.

She could not see them either, not exactly. What she saw instead was what they chose to be seen as — impressions, suggestions, weather given posture. The one who sat nearest her had taken the shape of a woman made of river-smell and the memory of floods. Another occupied the corner by the window and was, as far as Petra could tell, a season. Not autumn specifically; the idea that things end. The oldest of them had not bothered with a shape at all and simply made the far wall feel like standing at the edge of a very deep well.

They had come at dusk, as promised, and they had not come as conquerors. That was the miracle no ballad would ever bother with. They had come through the guildhall doors like petitioners, like farmers with a grievance about fencing rights, and they had arranged themselves around a counting table and waited to hear what mortals intended for them.

Four hundred years ago their last gathering had ended with the Crown Anew riveted shut over heaven's brow and a war that had salted half a continent. Petra kept reminding herself of that. Every time one of them spoke and the floorboards groaned, she reminded herself: they are here to talk. So talk.

"State your terms again," said the river-woman, whose name — the small folded scrap of it, recovered from a smuggler's satchel two winters past — was written on a card before Petra in careful guildhand: *Ossa, Who Remembers Water.* "Slowly. You keep hiding the arithmetic."

"The arithmetic is simple." Petra turned to a fresh page. Her hand was steady. She was privately astonished by how steady it was; fifteen years of bookkeeping had apparently prepared her for anything, including negotiating with the drowned dead of heaven. "You want to exist. We cannot afford you existing the old way. The old way — temples, tithes, chosen vessels, a crown to keep you ordered — ends in one of two places. Either you serve us and hate it, or we serve you and become what we were before the Godswar. Neither survives another four centuries."

"And your alternative?" This from the wall-that-was-a-well. Its voice arrived inside her chest rather than her ears.

"Mortality," Petra said. "Distributed."

Silence. Even the candles seemed to listen harder.

"You carry a name," she went on, tapping Ossa's card. "Each of you carries a name, and each name needs somewhere to live or it frays — that's why the smuggling worked at all, why Jude could hold what they held without burning up. A name can live in a mortal. We've proven it. What I'm proposing is that it doesn't have to be *one* mortal. No vessel. No vessel means no crown, because the crown only mattered when divinity pooled in one place deep enough to drown a world."

"Spread thin," said the season in the corner.

"Shared wide. A god's name divided among willing bearers — hundreds of them, thousands. Each carrying a fragment. A farmer who carries a sliver of Ossa remembers where wells were dug before anyone alive was born. A midwife carries a piece of whoever governed birth. Nothing anyone carries is strong enough to command them. It's only enough to remember you by." Petra looked down at her ledger. "You wouldn't be worshipped. You'd be *kept*. The way a family keeps a grandmother's recipe. Nobody worships the recipe. But nobody lets it burn either."

* * *

They broke her first draft twice before midnight.

The first objection came from a war-god whose name had been cut from an altar in chapter and whose presence made the tea in Petra's cup tremble in rings. He — it felt like a he, though Petra suspected gender was a garment they wore for her benefit — objected that mortality was a punishment, that the founders' Crown had taught his kind exactly what it was to be diminished, and that he would rather go out as a shout than fade as a murmur.

Petra heard him out. Then she opened the ledger to a page she had prepared and read him the casualty figures of the Godswar. Not the battles. The numbers after. The fields that hadn't grown for sixty years. The bloodlines that simply stopped.

"You call mortality a punishment," she said. "I call it the only thing that ever let anyone put a sword down. Immortality has never once ended a war. It just guarantees the same people are holding the grudge forever."

The war-god said nothing for a long time. When he finally spoke, it was quieter, and quieter, from him, sounded like surrender: "And if your mortals refuse us? If they will not carry?"

"Then you fray, and we grieve you honestly, and no one burns for it." She met the direction of his attention as best she could. "That's the whole offer. Willingness on both sides. It's the only currency I've got."

The second break came near the smallest hours, and it nearly broke the night instead.

A name she had no card for — one of the nine missing, recovered off Jude's own satchel strap, something old and agricultural and patient — asked the question Petra had been dreading since dusk. It asked it gently, which was worse.

"If we accept thinning," it said, "what stops your founders' heirs from building another Crown? What stops any clever mortal, in any century after yours, from deciding pooled divinity is too useful to leave scattered? You are asking us to trust not your generation but all of them, forever."

And there it was. The hole in the whole architecture. Petra had known it was there for three days, had turned the ledger page past it every time, and now it stood up in the middle of her negotiation and demanded an answer.

She looked at Jude.

Jude had barely spoken all night. They sat with their hands flat on the table — a courier's habit, Petra had learned, keeping the delivery always in view — and their face in the candlelight was the face of someone doing arithmetic of their own, the kind with no clean answer. They knew what Petra was about to say. They'd known since Greyford Bridge, since the keystone, since the oath had started eating Petra's memories one ledger-line at a time.

"The guarantee is me," Petra said.

The word came out steadier than anything deserved. Under the table, Jude's hand found hers.

"My order bound itself to true names. Speak a true name, bind a true thing. Last week I bound myself to the Crown's keystone so that no founder, no heir, no clever mortal in any century can carry it — because carrying it requires unbinding it from me, and unbinding requires my oath-memories, which the binding consumes as long as I hold it." She turned her palm up on the table so the gods could see, though there was nothing visible to see. Only a faint scarring along the lifeline, old swordspeaker ink gone pale. "Every year I hold it, I lose more of myself. The commander who trained me is already a blank line in my ledger. In twenty years there'll be nothing left to ransom the keystone with. The Crown dies with my memories. That is the collateral. That is the price, and I am paying it, and I am telling you plainly because you deserve plain dealing after four hundred years of being inventory."

The silence afterward was the longest of the night. The candles leaned. Somewhere in the walls the guildhouse itself creaked like a ship.

It was the well-wall that spoke last. "You understand," it said, "that we could simply wait. Refuse. Fray slowly, in the dark, and let your kind forget us cleanly. It would cost us less than trusting you."

"Yes," Petra said. "You could. And some of you should, and I won't pretend otherwise. But fading clean isn't the same as being remembered, and you didn't fight your way back out of four centuries of silence just to be *tidy*." She closed the ledger. "You're traders' gods. You came to a trade house. Take the deal or don't. But take it or leave it tonight, because tomorrow the church wakes up, and the day after that, somebody's cousin finds the founders' vault, and then everyone chooses for everyone."

* * *

They signed at dawn.

Not with ink. With the candles — every flame in the hall bending low at once and rising again, and Petra understanding, in the exhausted marrow of her, that she had just watched forty-one signatures written in fire. The terms went into the ledger in her own hand, the most important entry the Guild of Impossible Things would ever record: *mortality, distributed among willing bearers; worship discontinued; remembrance guaranteed; crown dissolved.*

The gods thinned even as they agreed — not dying, Petra thought, but *pouring*, going out into the world the way light goes out from a struck match, into whoever would carry them. The river-woman paused beside Petra on her way to everywhere, and something that was almost a hand rested a moment on Petra's shoulder, and the smell of clean water filled the room.

"He chose love," Ossa said, nodding toward Jude, who had fallen asleep sitting up sometime in the fifth hour, chin on chest, hands still flat on the table. "Your courier. He was offered the pool. All of it. He asked instead to be a man with a bad memory who is loved by a stubborn woman. We found this interesting. We found it *instructive*." The presence withdrew toward the door, leaving only the words behind: "Tell him the vessel is retired. Tell him whatever he becomes next is his own authorship."

Then the hall was just a hall, and the candles guttered — all at once, together, like an exhale — and morning came gray and ordinary through the windows, and it was the most beautiful thing Petra had seen in her life.

She sat there a while. She let herself sit, which was not a thing she did. Then she reached across and touched Jude's wrist until they surfaced, blinking, disoriented, reaching instinctively for a satchel that wasn't there.

"Terms accepted," Petra said. Her voice cracked on the second word and she let it. "Signed at dawn. Forty-one names, distributed. You're officially unemployed, courier. We both are."

Jude stared at her. Then at the ledger, open to the entry. Then back at her, and Petra watched something move through their face that she recognized from the mirror — the look of a person realizing the worst day of their life has somehow become the best one without announcing it.

"I spent all night," Jude said slowly, "trying to think of what I'd say to you if we survived. And now we've survived, and I can't remember a single sentence, and I think—" they laughed, ragged, wiped their eyes with the heel of one hand, "—I think that might be the first thing that's ever happened to me that I don't need a past to understand."

"Say it anyway."

"Petra." Just the name. They said it the way she'd once heard a priest say an opening prayer, back when prayers still landed somewhere. "I love you. Not as a vessel. Not as a debt. As a man with a bad memory who is—" their voice caught— "who is loved by a stubborn woman, if she'll have me. I heard what the river said. I choose it. All of it, in writing, witnessed."

Petra looked at the ledger between them. At the entry above the great one — the small line she'd written without thinking, somewhere in the fourth hour, when the night felt endless and safe: *witnessed: J., who stayed.*

"Witnessed," she said, and closed the book, and leaned across the counting table and kissed him while the candles smoked and the gray morning came on.

It lasted exactly as long as it took for the guildhall doors to bang open and Master Corrin Hale, junior factor, third-highest ranking survivor of the founders' purge, to stand in the doorway white-faced with a letter in his fist.

"Petra," he said. "It's Vellam. There's been a breach at the vault. The founders' enforcers — they're not retreating. They're regrouping. And the letter's sealed with his seal, and he's—" Corrin's voice failed, gathered itself. "He wrote it to be opened only if he didn't survive the night."

Petra rose, and her knees held, and the ledger went under her arm.

"Then we open it," she said.
