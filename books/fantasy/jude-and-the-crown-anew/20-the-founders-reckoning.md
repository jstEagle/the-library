## 20. The Founders' Reckoning

The candles were still burning when the doors came down.

Not broken — *opened*, which was worse. The guildhall's great doors had been sealed since the negotiation began, sealed by Petra's own swordspeech, and the founders had simply walked up to them and spoken words older than the hinges, and the doors had remembered who built them.

I had a candle in my hand. That was the whole of my armament at that moment: a candle, guttering, and the small crown of mortal names in my pocket, warm as a sleeping bird.

There were six of them. The surviving founders of the Guild of Impossible Things, come up from wherever founders go when they are not being charming to couriers. I recognized two from the banquet — the woman with the silver thread through her ear, whose name I still didn't know because she'd never offered it; and the fat man with kind eyes whose kindness I now understood as inventory, something he counted like everything else.

They looked at us across the hall — at Petra swaying on her feet with her oath half-spent, at me, at the keystone page bound against my chest under my coat — and the silver-eared woman said, "You've had your night. It was a lovely night. Now give it back."

"You're too late," I said, and my voice did something I didn't expect. It didn't shake. Two years ago — no, one year ago, before Greyford Bridge, before I chose to remember — my voice would have shaken. Now there was a god-shaped steadiness in it, or maybe just a Jude-shaped one, and honestly I couldn't have told you the difference, and I think that was the point.

"Too late," the fat man repeated, tasting it. He smiled at Petra. "Child, do you know what we spent building this guild? Four hundred years ago the gods went silent, and everyone said the world would end. We made a business out of the ending. We kept the lights on. And you propose to hand the divine back to — " he gestured at the hall, at the petitioning names still folded in their satchels, at all of us — "to *whoever wants some*?"

"Yes," Petra said.

Just that. She was grey-faced and her hands wouldn't close properly anymore — I could see the oath taking its price out of her in installments — but she said yes the way she wrote ledger entries: final, legible, dated.

The silver-eared woman sighed like a schoolteacher. Then she snapped her fingers, and the enforcers came in.

* * *

I should describe the enforcers, because I remember them now, which means I remember what they are.

When I recovered my memories at Greyford Bridge, I got all of them — including the ones from before I was Jude. I got the vessel-years. I got the cold room where they kept me between uses, and I got the men and women who guarded the door of that room. They wore grey then too. They were called wardens then, and they fed me on schedule and spoke to me gently, the way you speak to a well you intend to drink from.

These were their descendants. Same grey coats, same unhurried walk, same faces arranged into expressions of professional regret. The Crown Anew needed vessels and vaults and quiet violence, and the guild had always provided, the way a good trade house provides anything.

Six enforcers, moving to flank us. Petra couldn't raise a blade. I could — I had, at the bridge, done things with steel that I still dreamed about, none of them pleasantly — but every time I reached for that skill now, I felt the god in me lean forward like a dog hearing its name, and I had promised myself, an hour ago, kneeling among the freed names, that I would not open that door again unless the alternative was worse than the door.

So I did the only thing left to a courier.

I ran for the stairs.

"Jude—" Petra started, and then she saw where I was going, and she stopped talking, because Petra understands logistics better than anyone alive, and logistics said: the keystone page is the crown's heart, and if the heart leaves the building, the fight has to follow it.

I took the servants' stair three at a time. Behind me, boots on stone. Above me, Vellam's study, and beyond the study, the old counting room, and beyond that —

Vellam was standing in the corridor outside his study, holding a lantern, dressed as though he'd been awake all night, which he had. He looked at me, and then past me, at the sound coming up the stairs, and I watched him do arithmetic.

"No," I said. Because I already knew. You spend enough time around bookkeepers and you learn to read a balance sheet forming behind someone's eyes.

"Boy," he said — not unkindly; Vellam was never unkind, that was the whole horror of him — "how fast can you run?"

"Faster than six grey coats."

"Then run." He set the lantern down on the floor, carefully, the way he did everything, and turned to face the staircase. From inside his coat he drew something I had only ever heard described in the oldest ledgers: a broker's bond, written on skin, signed in a god's dried blood. One miracle, redeemable once. He must have been carrying it for decades, waiting for a debt worth calling in.

"Vellam," I said. "Whatever you're about to—"

"The guild taught me compound interest," he said. "This is me collecting."

The first warden came around the stairwell and Vellam spoke the bond aloud.

* * *

I didn't see it. I want to be honest about that, because this chapter of the ledger deserves honest entries and not heroics. I was already running when the bond discharged, so what I know is what I heard: a sound like a word being unsaid, backwards, enormous. What I felt: heat on the back of my neck, and the whole building shudder like a struck bell. What I understood, later, when the ash was counted: the bond had unwritten the staircase. All of it. Stairs, walls, six wardens, and the man standing at the top of them.

Vellam died holding the door. That is the sentence history will get, and it's true, and it isn't. There was no door. There was a man who spent his last miracle making sure there wasn't a doorway, because a doorway is how they would have followed me, and a man who knew exactly what it would cost, because Vellam always knew prices better than anyone. He had emptied me once to save me from the Crown. He spent himself, in the end, the same way — quietly, in advance, without telling anyone the fee.

Petra told me later that she found his lantern still burning at the top of nothing, and picked it up, and carried it down herself. She's the one who enters things in ledgers. Someone had to enter him.

* * *

By the time I came back down — out the loading dock, through the rain, around the block, because I am a courier and couriers do not leave packages unattended, and Petra was my package that morning, or I was hers, we had stopped keeping track — the fight in the great hall was over.

Not won. Over. There's a difference, and the difference was the guild itself.

While the founders' attention was on the stairs, on the thunder, on me — the staff of the Guild of Impossible Things had done what staff does when the owners aren't watching. The brokers had locked their cabinets. The clerks had pulled the master ledger off its chain and put it on the central table, open, under lamplight. Old Sefa, who has kept the guild's accounts since before I had a name, stood beside it with the voting stones in a bowl, the ones used for quorum, the ones nobody had touched in living memory.

"A trade house," Sefa said to the founders, in the voice she uses on clients who are trying to renegotiate signed contracts, "is governed by its charter. The charter says the guild trades in impossible things." She turned the bowl toward the hall. "It also says the guild exists by vote of its members, and that no founder holds more weight than any clerk. Article One. You wrote it yourselves, four hundred years ago, when you needed the members to fund you. Did you think we couldn't read our own founding documents?"

"We wrote it when we needed—" the silver-eared woman began.

"You wrote it," Sefa said. "Signatures don't expire. Vote's called. All in favor of the guild ceasing trade in miracles, curses, gods, and names — permanently — say it with a stone."

And here is the thing I will carry longer than my memories, longer maybe than my name: the stones came down like rain.

Clerks. Brokers. The kitchen staff. The gate-wardens who weren't dead or grey-coated. Stone after stone after stone into the bowl, until it overflowed, until Sefa had to hold it with both hands, until even some of the founders' own retainers — people paid in guild coin, insured by guild policy, buried by guild custom — added theirs.

Against: six stones. The founders themselves, and not one soul besides.

The fat man looked at the bowl for a long time. Then he laughed, short and terrible, the laugh of a man reading his own audit. "Four hundred years," he said, "and the membership votes us out like a bad investment."

"That's exactly what happened," Sefa said.

* * *

What remained was messier than the vote.

Founders do not dissolve gracefully. Two of the six accepted it with the numbness of ruined merchants. The silver-eared woman tried to leave with the guild seal in her pocket and was stopped at the door by two apprentices who weighed perhaps a tenth of her combined, but who stood there anyway, arms crossed, until she gave it up. The other three — the fat man among them — simply vanished between one hour and the next, and I will not pretend that doesn't sit in my chest like a splinter. Men like that do not vanish. They regroup somewhere with soft chairs and long memories, and someday someone may have to write another chapter about them.

But that is future business. This was present business, and present business was counted honestly, as Petra insisted, line by line, while dawn came up grey over the city:

Six wardens, unwritten. One mentor, the same. One staircase gone, to be rebuilt by masons who would ask questions and receive answers we'd have to invent. Nine god-names freed and sheltered, pending terms. One Crown, broken, its keystone page surrendered to my keeping and Petra's binding both. One guild, voted out of the impossible, effective immediately, with a transition period for outstanding contracts that Sefa insisted on and Petra granted, because even revolutions honor their invoices.

And one courier, standing in the corner with a candle that had finally burned down to his fingers, watching Petra write all of it in her sharp, unforgiving hand.

She looked up at me when she finished. Her eyes were doing something complicated — grief and exhaustion and the specific terror of a person who has just lost the memory-shape of someone she loves and is checking, constantly, whether the love survived the loss.

"Vellam's entry," she said. "I can't get it right. Everything I write sounds either too small or too large."

"Read me what you have."

She read: "*Master Vellam Corrin, broker, mentor, keeper of doors. Paid in full.*"

I thought about the empty room at the top of the missing stairs. About a man who spent fifty years being suspected so that a frightened vessel could grow up into a person. About compound interest, collected.

"That's right," I said. "That's exactly right. Don't touch it."

* * *

We buried what we could bury at sunset — the wardens too, because Petra said the ledger doesn't distinguish, and because she was right, and because some of those grey coats had been children taken the way I was taken, and no one had ever asked them either. The guildhall bell rang nine times, one per name we'd freed, and the ninth ring hadn't finished fading when the candles did it again.

Every flame in the hall leaned. Not toward me, this time. Toward the center of the room, where the air had begun, very gently, to gather — the way weather gathers, the way a crowd gathers before someone speaks.

"They're early," Petra murmured. "The terms aren't finalized. We haven't—"

"They were never waiting on terms," I said, and found that my hand had found hers without asking either of us permission. "They're waiting on an answer. We promised them one by morning."

Outside, the bell tower stood dark against a sky that had started, faintly and impossibly, to fill with wings.

The negotiation of small hours had begun.
