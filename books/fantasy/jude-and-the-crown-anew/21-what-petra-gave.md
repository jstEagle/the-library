## 21. What Petra Gave

The oath did not take her memories all at once. That was the first cruelty of it.

She had imagined a door closing, a page torn out, some clean and merciful amputation. Instead it came like frost coming across a window in winter — slow, creeping from the edges inward, so that she could watch it happen. She sat in the guildhall's east reading room with a candle burning low and a fresh ledger open before her, and she watched the frost come.

It began with small things. The name of the road that led to Greyford Bridge went first, thinning out of her mind like ink left in rain. She had walked that road a hundred times. She could feel the shape of the walking — the ache of her boots, the smell of wet stone — without any sense of where she had been going. Then the faces of her sword-sisters softened, not vanishing but blurring, as if she were remembering them through smoked glass. She gripped the table's edge and held on.

*Speak a true name, bind a true thing.* She had spoken the keystone's name at Greyford Bridge with the Crown's enforcers closing like a fist around them, and the binding had held — no hand but hers could carry the keystone now, and no hand would ever want to, because carrying it cost everything she was made of.

The oath was patient. It had waited fifteen years for her voice; it could wait out the rest of her life.

"Petra."

Jude stood in the doorway, still wearing the dust of the bridge. There was a cut above their eyebrow that they refused to let anyone else dress. They had chosen to remember at Greyford — all of it, the vessel's whole terrible inheritance — and they wore the memory now the way other people wore armor: carefully, and only because they had to.

"It's happening," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "You should write things down."

"I know." Jude crossed the room and set down a leather-bound book, thick as a brick, its spine cracked from use. "I already started. Last night, when you told me about your commander — you told me his name three times and each time you said it slower."

She looked at the book. *For Petra,* the cover said, in Jude's blunt courier's hand. Below it, smaller: *So you don't have to be your own witness.*

"I don't remember telling you about him," she said. And then, horribly, she checked — reached back for the commander's face and found the frost already there, white lace over features she knew she must have loved. "No. I do. His name is—" She stopped. Reached again. The lace thickened. "His name is—"

"Corvane," Jude said quietly. "Commander Corvane Ashfall. You called him Old Crow. He taught you swordspeech by making you memorize the names of rivers before he'd let you hold a blade. He died at Vellam's side during the last purge, and you buried him yourself, and you've visited his grave every year on the first frost."

Petra stared at them. The words landed somewhere below memory — she felt them settle like stones into deep water, ripples spreading over a surface she could no longer see into. She believed every word. She simply couldn't reach them anymore.

"You're keeping me," she said.

"I'm keeping what you gave away." Jude sat across from her and opened the book. "There's a difference. I intend to be insufferable about the difference."

* * *

They built a ritual out of it, because rituals were how the guild handled anything too large to hold bare-handed.

Every evening, an hour after the candles were lit, Jude read to her from the book. Not the way you read to someone losing their mind — there was none of that pity in it, none of the careful loudness people used on the dying. Jude read to her the way couriers read manifest lists: briskly, precisely, with an occasional dry commentary that made her laugh even when the entries broke her heart.

*Entry the ninth. Petra Dunmore, age nineteen, failed her first swordspeaking trial because she laughed during the binding. She says the god whose name she spoke had a funny voice. She has never told anyone this. I am writing it down because she told me today and then dared me to forget.*

"That one's true," Petra said, though she had no memory of the laughing, or the trial, or the dare. But she recognized herself in it — recognized the shape of her own mouth doing the thing her mouth did. "Was I really nineteen?"

"Nineteen and furious about it." Jude turned the page. "Here. This is from two days ago. Your handwriting's already getting worse."

The entry was in her own hand, dated, precise: *The bridge. I held the keystone while the water ran backward and I sang the true name until my throat bled. Jude fought beside me and I have never seen anything like it and I never will again if the frost gets there first. If I forget the bridge, make me go look at it. Don't tell me what happened there. Let me see it myself. Some things should be weather, not ledger.*

Below it, in Jude's hand: *Noted. Stubborn to the last.*

"The oath could have taken it cleanly," Petra said. She had said this before, she suspected, and would say it again. "One clean payment. Everything at once. Instead it makes me watch."

"Maybe that's the price," Jude said. "Not the memories. The watching."

She thought about that for a long time, candle burning between them, frost creeping along the shelves of her mind. Somewhere past the reading room windows, the freed gods were settling into their strange new tenancy — mortality parceled out among willing bearers, a crown worn by ten thousand heads instead of one. She could feel them faintly, the way you feel neighbors through a wall. One of them hummed when it rained. Another had taken up residence in the guild's kitchen girl and made the bread rise twice as fast. They were small now. They were learning to be small.

Her oath-memories were the last currency of the old arrangement, and she was paying them out like a broker closing a house.

"What's left?" she asked.

Jude consulted the book the way they once consulted delivery routes — that same focused squint, that same finger tracing lines. "The order. Most of it. The bridge. Me." A pause, controlled, professional. "Nearly all of me. You kept yesterday. You keep most yesterdays. It's the older layers going first, and the ones tied to the binding. The oath eats outward from the true names." Their voice stayed level. Their knuckles didn't. "You asked me to be honest in the ledger, so: it's getting close."

"How close?"

"Last night you looked at me across the breakfast table," Jude said, "and you asked me how long I'd been working at the guild."

The candle guttered. Neither of them moved to fix it.

"And what did you say?"

"I said two years." Jude closed the book. "Because that's the truth of the record, even if it isn't the truth of us. And you said, *you're very good at your job,* and you meant it kindly, and it was the worst thing anyone has ever said to me, and I wrote it down so I'd never have to hear it fresh twice."

Petra reached across the table. Her hand found theirs — she knew the gesture even if the history behind it was frosting over, knew that her hand belonged in theirs the way a key knows its lock. "Then we change the record," she said. "Starting tonight. New entry. Today's date."

Jude didn't open the book. "Dictate it."

"Petra Dunmore," she said slowly, choosing each word like a coin counted out, "does not remember falling in love with Jude. Remembers almost nothing of it — a warmth here, a certainty there, the feeling of a door she knows she walked through even though the room behind it is gone." Her voice caught; she let it. "But she looks at them across a table and her chest does a thing, and she has decided to trust the thing, and to build on top of it, floor by floor, day by day, until whatever they build is bigger than what the frost took."

Silence. The candle steadied.

"That's not a ledger entry," Jude said, and their voice had gone rough. "That's a vow."

"We're a trade house," Petra said. "We deal in impossible things. Write it down anyway."

* * *

The frost reached the order on the fourth night.

She woke knowing something was wrong the way sailors know weather — pressure behind the eyes, a hollowness in the chest where weight used to sit. She lay in her narrow bed above the ledgers and cast about inside herself and found the place where her sisters had been swept clean. Fifteen years of them: drills and feasts, funerals and promotions, the whole bright noisy country of the Swordspeakers' Order, gone smooth and white like snow over a field. She remembered that the field existed. She could not find a single footprint in it.

She got up and went down to the reading room, because the reading room was where she went when she was breaking, and found Jude already there, waiting up, the book open, two candles lit.

"It came," she said.

"I know. You called out in your sleep. You said a name — Sera? Sera something."

"Sera Voss." It surfaced as she said it, one last bubble rising through ice. "My shield-sister. Left-handed. Sang off-key. Died—" The ice closed. "She died," Petra finished, flat. "I know that much. I'll never know the rest, will I."

"You'll know what's in the book." Jude turned pages. "Sera Voss. Here — you told me about her on the salt road, the second night. You said she once bet you a month's wages that you couldn't speak a curse politely. You won. The farmer who hired you got his curse lifted and apologized to the god for the inconvenience, and the god was so charmed it left a blessing by mistake, and Sera paid up and never gambled against you again."

Petra laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, grief and delight tangled together the way they always are at the bottom of loss. "That sounds like me."

"It is you," Jude said. "That's the whole point. Listen to me, Petra." They leaned forward, and the candlelight caught the scar above their eyebrow, and their eyes held hers with a ferocity she had seen once before — on a bridge, over backward-running water, when they chose to remember rather than survive comfortable. "The oath took the rooms. It can't take the architecture. Every one of those memories passed through you on its way out, and it shaped you, and the shape stays. You are still the woman who laughed during her first binding. You are still the person who buried her commander and came back every year. The frost can cover the field. It cannot unmake what grew there."

"You can't know that."

"I'm the expert," Jude said. "On being emptied. On what survives it." Their voice dropped. "Everything that matters survived in me too. It just needed someone to hand it back."

And that was the thing she had been circling for four nights without letting herself land on. She looked at Jude across the candles — at the keeper of her stolen self, the courier who had once been a vessel, the person who had chosen love over divinity on a bridge and carried the choice home like contraband — and she said the hard thing.

"I'm going to lose you. Not all at once. But the entry you showed me — the breakfast table — that keeps happening. One morning I'm going to wake up and you'll be a stranger who knows too much about me, and it will frighten me, and I might say something cruel, because frightened people are cruel, and I need you to promise me you won't take it personally. Promise me you'll just read me the book."

"No."

The refusal landed like a slap. "Jude—"

"No," they said again, gentler. "Not because I won't do the work. I'll read you the book every morning for the rest of our lives, I'll be your ledger and your witness and your courier of one. But I won't promise to stand in the cold and be mistaken for a stranger and call it fine. So here's my counteroffer." They slid the book across the table to her. "When the bad morning comes — and it will — don't take my word for anything. Read it yourself. Start at the first page. It takes about four hours, and by the end of it you won't love me yet, but you'll believe that you could. And then you decide. Every morning, you decide. Not memory deciding for you. You. Choosing. Which is more than most people ever get, if you think about it. Most people just inherit their love like furniture. Ours, you'll have to earn daily."

Petra looked down at the book in her hands. Heavy as a brick. Light as a held breath.

"That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me," she said, "and also the most exhausting."

"Welcome to trading in impossible things." Jude smiled, and under the smile was fear, honestly worn, which was the only kind of courage she trusted anymore. "We put the terms in writing."

* * *

The bad morning came eleven days later.

She knew it before she opened her eyes — the quality of the silence in her own head, the absence of the warm gravity that had pulled toward the room next to hers. She dressed like a woman going to a funeral, which in a sense she was, and came downstairs, and Jude was at the long table with bread and tea and the book, and looked up at her with a face carefully arranged into neutrality, and said, "Good morning. I'm Jude. I work with you."

"Yes," Petra said. Polite. Distant. Her chest did nothing at all. "I know."

She sat. She ate. She drank tea with a kind stranger who watched her with their whole heart hidden behind their teeth, and when the meal was done she picked up the brick-heavy book and began, as agreed, at the first page.

She read for four hours.

She read about a satchel that sang, and candles leaning. She read about nine scraped altars and a blade wielded by hands that shouldn't have known how. She read about a salt road and a fire and the terror of having no past, and a woman saying *let me be your witness instead.* She read about a shrine that ate a commander's face. She read about a bridge, and a choice, and a name sung until the singer's throat bled. She read the entry she had dictated herself, eleven days and a lifetime ago, about trusting the thing her chest did.

And at the end of it she looked up, and Jude was still there, still pretending to sort delivery manifests, still giving her exactly the space she had asked for, and Petra understood suddenly what the oath had actually bought with all that frost. Not safety. Not peace. It had bought her this: the chance to fall in love on purpose. To stand where the field had been snowed over and choose to plant it again, seed by seed, entry by entry, with full knowledge of the cost.

Most people inherited their love like furniture.

Hers, she earned daily.

"Jude," she said.

They looked up. Hope and terror sharing one face.

"Tell me about the bridge," she said. "Not from the book. From you. Take all day if you need it."

Jude closed the manifests. And began.

* * *

Winter deepened. The frost in her kept its patient pace, and the book kept pace with it, and the guild learned its new shape around them — no more miracles, only possible things: letters delivered, debts collected, weather negotiated fairly, gods housed gently in mortal neighbors who had volunteered and been trained. The keystone sat in Petra's keeping, bound to her by the oath, lightening month by month as she did, until some mornings it weighed no more than a folded page.

On the first frost of the following year, Jude walked with her to the grave of Commander Corvane Ashfall, because her own handwriting in the ledger insisted she still come, and stood quietly by while she laid winter roses on stone she could not connect to any face.

"He taught me rivers before blades," she said. The words came from the book, but her mouth knew how to mean them.

"So the ledger says."

"I'd like to have known him."

Jude took her hand. "You did," they said. "That's why the roses."

They stood a while in the cold, two people keeping faith with a dead man neither of them fully remembered, and then turned back down the hill toward the guildhall, where the lights were lit and the kettle was on and a new ledger waited on Petra's desk with its first page still blank — a blank she had been saving all autumn, because some entries deserved to be written in good light, with steady hands, at the start of things.

She knew what it would say. She had known since the bridge. But knowing a thing and committing it to the permanent record were, in her trade, very different acts — and tomorrow, with Jude beside her, she intended to commit it.
