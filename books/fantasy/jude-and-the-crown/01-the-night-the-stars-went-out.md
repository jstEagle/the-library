## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The night Ivo Blackwood decided to run away from home, the stars went out.

She had been counting them, in the way she counted everything when sleep refused her — the knots in the cottage roof beams, the cracks in the plaster shaped like rivers, the forty-one jars of Maren's tinctures lined on the shelf like a choir of amber ghosts. Stars were harder to count because Hollowmere's sky only ever offered a handful, dim and watery behind the valley's perpetual gloom, as if the heavens themselves had grown tired of looking down at this place and drawn a curtain halfway across its face.

She was at the window with her pack already strapped beneath her coat, watching three pale stars hang over the ridge called the Old Man's Teeth, and one by one they went out.

Not set. Not clouded. *Out* — snuffed like candles pinched between wet fingers, each one dying with a small silent absence that Ivo felt in her teeth before her eyes confirmed it. The third star held on longest, trembling at the edge of the Teeth as if it knew what was coming for it, and then it, too, was gone, and the sky above Hollowmere became a thing she had never seen before: whole. Black. Empty.

Ivo did not scream. She had been raised by Maren Thistlewood to believe that screaming was for people who had no better tools, and so instead she stood very still at the window with her heart slamming against her ribs like something trying to get out, and she listened.

The valley listened back.

Hollowmere had always made noise at night — the sigh of peat bogs settling, the mutter of wind through the black pines on the crater rim, the far-off complaint of sheep whose dreams were troubled by whatever lived in the fens. But this was different. This was a sound underneath the sounds, a groan so deep and slow that Ivo felt it through the floorboards, up through the soles of her feet, into the roots of her teeth. It was the sound of something old being asked to hold more than it could bear.

*The wards,* she thought, though she didn't know yet how she knew. *Something's wrong with the wards.*

Everyone in the valley knew about the wards, the way everyone knows about the weather or the color of the sky — not as knowledge but as fact of existence. The wards sealed the valley mouth where the river cut through the Old Man's Teeth, a shimmer you could see on certain evenings if you squinted, like heat-haze in winter, like the memory of light. No one went past them. No one spoke of going past them. When Ivo was seven she had asked Maren what lay beyond, and Maren had looked at her with an expression Ivo had spent years trying to forget — not anger, but something worse, something like grief worn smooth by repetition — and said, *Nothing that wants us, and nothing we want.* Then Maren had made her copy out the hedge-chants for warding a doorway eleven times, which was how all of Ivo's questions were answered in that house.

But tonight there were no questions left to ask. Tonight the wards were dying, and Ivo could hear it.

She lit no lamp. Her hands found her pack by touch, checked the bread, the flint, the folded map she'd copied from Maren's locked drawer over three stolen evenings — a map of the valley with the border marked in faded ink and, beyond it, nothing but blank paper and two words in Maren's cramped hand: *the world.*

Then the sound came again, closer now, higher, and this time it wasn't a groan but a ringing — thin and crystalline, the note a wineglass makes just before it shatters — and it seemed to come from everywhere at once, from the stones of the road outside and the bones of the hills and the marrow of Ivo herself.

The wards broke.

She felt it happen. There was no other way to describe it. Somewhere down the valley, four miles of darkness away, something that had stood for longer than memory gave way all at once, and the sound that rolled through Hollowmere was like every bell in the world cracking in unison — and then silence, real silence, a silence with edges, and the air itself changed.

It came into the room like weather. Like a tide.

Ivo would spend the rest of her life failing to describe what happened next, because there were no words for it in any language Maren had taught her, and Maren had taught her three. Magic flooded into her. Not the thin gruel of hedge-magic she had known all her life — the little charms that made milk keep an extra day, the chants that eased a calving ewe, the small warm tricks that were all Hollowmere allowed and all, Maren had always insisted, that Ivo would ever manage (*You have your mother's talent for almost, Ivo, and your father's patience for nothing*) — but magic entire, magic undiluted, pouring into her like the valley floor drinking a flood after twenty years of drought.

Her hands glowed.

She stared at them in the dark, at the soft silver light rising off her skin like steam off summer stone, and she thought, with the strange calm of total shock: *So this is what I am.*

Every charm she had ever chanted half-alive roared into fullness. The dried lavender hanging from the beam burst into bloom. The forty-one jars on the shelf began to sing, faintly, harmonics rising off their glass lids like breath on a bottle's mouth. Outside, she heard the garden answer — a rushing, rustling sound, green things growing in the dark at a pace green things should not grow — and through the window she saw moonflowers opening along the fence in chains, white trumpets blooming out of season, out of turn, out of their minds with joy.

And under all of it, under the flood, she felt the other thing. The emptiness where the stars had been. A vast cold ache in the sky, like a tooth pulled from the world's jaw, and the magic pouring into her felt suddenly less like a gift than like water rushing toward a drain — toward her, through her, as if she were standing in the path of something enormous returning to somewhere it belonged.

*I'm a door,* she thought. *I'm standing in a doorway and something is coming home.*

Footsteps overhead. Maren, awake, moving fast for a woman of sixty. Ivo had perhaps ten seconds to decide whether tonight was still the night she ran.

It should have been simple. She had planned it for months — the pack under the loose plank by the goat shed, the route down the fen-path to the wards, the long walk out of the valley to wherever the world started. Fourteen years in Maren's cottage had taught her everything except how to stay: the cold lessons, the endless copying, the way Maren never once said *well done*, never once said *I love you* in words instead of soup and mended coats and bitter corrections. The valley itself had taught her to leave — its gloom like a lid on a pot, its people walking the same circles their grandparents walked, its sky rationed to a handful of tired stars. She had resented it the way you resent a wall: completely, without ever quite seeing it anymore.

Now the wall was broken. Now the sky was open and empty as a stripped bed. And Ivo stood in her glowing skin between the life she had fled in her heart a hundred times and the dark unknown beyond the Teeth, and discovered that terror and exhilaration are not opposites but twins, born together, holding hands, indistinguishable in the dark.

Maren came down the ladder-stairs without lighting a lamp either. In the glow of Ivo's own hands, Ivo watched her mistress's face do something she had never seen it do.

Maren Thistlewood was afraid.

Not stern-afraid, not disappointed-in-you-afraid, but truly afraid, her sharp face gone gray, her eyes fixed first on Ivo's shining hands and then flying past her to the window, to the starless sky, with an expression like a woman reading a letter she has dreaded for fifty years and finding it worse than she imagined.

"You're packed," Maren said. Her voice was steady. Her hands, gripping the back of a chair, were not.

"I was leaving tonight." Ivo's voice sounded strange to her own ears, layered, as if the magic had given her a second voice underneath the first. "Before any of — whatever this is."

"Whatever this is," Maren repeated softly. She crossed the room — Ivo stepped back, an old reflex — and pulled shut the curtain over the window, as if fabric could hold back what was coming. "Sit down, girl."

"I'm not —"

"*Sit.*" And there it was, the old iron, the voice that had ruled Ivo's childhood — but underneath it now was something else, and Ivo, flooded with new senses she didn't know how to close, tasted it plain: fear, grief, and guilt, guilt thick as tar.

She sat. The stool creaked. Her hands kept glowing, and Maren looked at them a long moment, and when she spoke again her voice had changed — quieter, older, as if she were speaking from a great distance down a tunnel of years.

"I hoped I'd die before telling you this," she said. "I hoped the wards would outlast me and the lie with them. But the stars are out, and the wards are broken, and you are *glowing*, child, which means the covenant has come home to roost in the last place I wanted it." She closed her eyes. Opened them. "Your blood is not what I told you it was."

Outside, far down the valley, a dog began to howl. Then another. Then the whole valley's dogs, a ragged chorus rising toward the broken border, answering something none of them could name.

And beneath the howling, faint but unmistakable, carried on air that tasted of ozone and crushed herbs and the cold clean smell of deep winter — hoofbeats. Many horses. Coming up the valley road from the direction of the shattered wards, moving fast.

Maren's head snapped toward the sound. When she turned back to Ivo, all the guilt had burned off her face and left something harder behind, something Ivo had glimpsed only twice before and both times had run from.

"The Crown's wardens will be here by morning," Maren said. "Get your pack. We have until dawn for me to teach you fourteen years of lessons in one night — and Ivo—" her voice cracked, just once, on her name, "—the first lesson is that I was cruel to you on purpose, and you may hate me for it later, when hating me is safe. *Move.*"

Ivo moved. And in the space between one heartbeat and the next, the valley she had spent her whole life resenting became, in a single terrible instant, the only thing in the world she could not bear to lose.
