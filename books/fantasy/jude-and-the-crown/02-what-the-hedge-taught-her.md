## 2. What the Hedge Taught Her

The valley had never known true dark before that night. Hollowmere lived under a permanent dusk — the stars above it long since gone out, the wards at the valley mouth drinking what little light the sun offered and returning it as gloom. But this was different. This was a darkness with weight, a darkness like water closing over her head.

Ivo ran through it with Maren's silver thimble in one fist and nothing else.

Behind her, the mistress's cottage was already a smudge of darker black against the hillside. She did not look back. Looking back was how you tripped; looking back was how you died on the roots of your own hesitation. Maren had taught her that much, at least, though she'd taught it with a switch across the knuckles rather than kindness, the way she'd taught everything.

*Run north,* the old woman had said, shoving her out the door into the first moments of the starless dark. *Don't use anything. Don't light anything. And if the magic finds you — and it will find you, girl, tonight of all nights — don't fight it.*

She hadn't understood until half a mile down the drover's path, when the flood came.

It began in her palms. A warmth like holding a bird too tightly, then a burning, then something that was neither warm nor hot but *vast*, pouring up through the soles of her boots from the ground itself, as if the whole valley were a cup finally tipped toward her lips after years of thirst. Ivo stumbled to her knees in the nettles and gasped. Every hedge-charm she had ever failed to cast — every knot that wouldn't hold, every sigil that stayed stubbornly ink, every whispered word that fell dead from her mouth inside the wards — all of it rose in her at once like water finding its old channel. The nettles around her knees bloomed. In the dead of a starless night, in soil that should have been asleep, white flowers opened in a rough circle where her hands pressed the earth.

She stared at them until her eyes stung, and then she laughed — a wild, cracked sound she didn't recognize as her own — and then the terror arrived, right on schedule, the way terror always followed exhilaration like a hound follows a cart.

Because the magic had come *now*. After six years of failure. After six years of Maren's disappointment and the village children calling her hedge-witch's whelp behind their hands. It had come the same night the stars went out, the same night the wards at the valley mouth shattered — she had felt that too, a soundless breaking deep in her sternum, like a bone cracking in someone else's body three rooms away.

Magic did not arrive by accident. Magic arrived because something had opened.

And whatever had opened wanted something.

Ivo got up. She wiped her hands on her skirt, leaving green smears and crushed petals, and she ran north along the drover's path, and the flowers bloomed behind her footsteps like a trail of small white witnesses she could not silence.

* * *

Lowmere was dying when she reached it.

She smelled the village before she saw it — not smoke, though there was smoke enough, but sickness: the sour-sweet reek of animals failing in their pens. Lowmere sat in the crook of the river where the valley widened, forty households under thatch, and Ivo had passed through it twice a year on Maren's errands, carrying charm-bundles for the byres and tinctures for the midwife. The villagers had always looked at her with that particular mixture of pity and suspicion the valley reserved for apprentices. Now they looked at nothing at all.

Lamplight moved in the windows — ordinary tallow lamps, guttering, doing what lamplight does. But the charms over the doorways hung dead. Ivo saw it as she passed the first cottage: a woven rowan cross, the kind every household in Hollowmere had hung since anyone could remember, meant to glimmer faintly with the valley's ambient warding, meant to be the last small warmth against the cold beyond the walls. It was gray. Not weathered-gray but *dead*-gray, the way a fire is gray an hour after it goes out, and when Ivo reached toward it instinctively she felt nothing answering — no thread, no hum, no whisper of the covenant her blood supposedly carried.

The wards were broken, and everything the wards had fed was starving.

A cow lowed in the nearest byre, a sound wrong in pitch, wrong in length, the complaint of an animal in pain it couldn't name. Through the open door of the stable Ivo saw the farmer's boy, Tam Ostell, maybe twelve years old, kneeling in straw with his arms around the beast's neck. He was crying without noise, which was somehow worse than wailing. On the wall beside him hung a milking-charm braided from rushes, and even in the dimness Ivo could see it had gone brittle, crumbling at the edges like something centuries old instead of seasons.

"Tam." Her voice came out strange, too loud in the sick quiet.

He turned. His face was wet and blank. "Ivo Blackwood." He said it like an accusation, or a question he already dreaded the answer to. "Is it true? About the stars?"

"There are no stars," Ivo said. "There haven't been stars over the valley in a hundred years. What happened tonight is worse."

"The cows—" He swallowed. "They've been screaming since moonset. The charms won't take. Pa tried three times, fresh-braided, proper words, and they just—" He held up his hands. They were shaking. Rush-fibers clung to them. "Everything we make just dies."

Ivo knelt beside him without deciding to. Her hand found the cow's neck — hot, trembling, the pulse beneath her palm running fast and shallow — and the new power in her rose eagerly, greedily, a tide pushing against the inside of her skin. *Here,* it seemed to say. *Here is something broken. Let me.*

She let it.

Warmth flowed out of her and into the animal, and the cow sighed, and its trembling eased, and Tam made a sound like a prayer cut short. For one breath Ivo felt magnificent. For the next breath she felt the cost — a dragging emptiness behind her ribs, a chill climbing her spine, the unmistakable sensation of having paid for something with coins she hadn't counted. Outside the valley, Sef would later tell her, everything costs something. Inside the valley, apparently, so did miracles.

"It's only easing," Ivo said, standing, her legs unsteady. "I can't fix it. Whatever broke the wards broke everything downstream of the wards. Tell your father to slaughter the sickest ones now, while the meat's still good. Don't wait for them to waste."

"You're just going to leave?" Tam's voice cracked on it. "Everyone's saying the border's fallen. Everyone's saying—"

"I know what everyone's saying." She looked at him, this boy who had once thrown a clod of mud at her for being the witch's apprentice, and found that she couldn't hate him, not tonight, not when he was twelve and crying over a dying cow. "Get everyone into the kirk cellar. Stone walls. If anything comes out of the dark tonight, stone walls."

"Where are you going?"

North. Toward the thing she'd spent six years trying to escape. The irony of it would have made her laugh again if laughing wouldn't have broken something loose that might never stop.

"To my mistress," she said, and was surprised to hear herself call Maren that — not *the old woman*, not *her*, but *my mistress*, possessive, belonging. "If anyone knows what's happening, it's her."

She left him there in the straw with his dying cow, and the flowers bloomed behind her all the way out of Lowmere.

* * *

Two more villages between her and the head of the valley, and both were worse.

Netherby had simply gone out, like a lamp pinched between fingers — doors standing open, supper still on tables, no people, no livestock, not even bodies. Just absence shaped like a village. Ivo stood in its lane for a long moment, listening to her own heartbeat, and the power in her skin crawled like ants, reading something in the air she couldn't parse: residue, perhaps, or invitation. She did not stay to learn which. In Harrowgate the villagers had fled toward the valley's heart, a stream of carts and torches passing her in the opposite direction, and an old man had grabbed her sleeve and shouted, "The scouts are coming! Crown men, at the breach! They're coming *through*!" before being dragged onward by his family.

Crown men. Wardens. Here, inside Hollowmere, where no crown had reached in living memory.

Ivo ran the last miles with a stitch in her side and her mind full of the old stories — the ones told in kitchens, the ones Maren had dismissed with a sneer but never corrected. How the valley had been sealed a hundred years ago by the last of the old kings, sealed *for its protection*, the priests said, warded against the court beyond the sky whose stars had gone out the same night. Sealed so tight that even the valley's own small magics were muffled, hedged, kept in ledgers and licensed charms. Sealed so well that a child born inside it with keeper's blood — if such blood existed anywhere anymore — would grow up feeling like a locked room.

Maren's cottage crouched on its hill above the last bend of the drover's path, black-windowed, chimney cold.

Ivo stood at the foot of the garden path and could not make her feet move. Six years of that house rose up in her: the cold mornings hauling water, the endless copying of charm-formulae that never worked for her, the switch, the silences, the flat gray eyes that watched her fail and said nothing, said *nothing*, year after year, until Ivo had packed her bundle and sworn she would walk out of that valley or die at the wards trying.

She had run away from this door. Now the world had broken, and her feet had carried her back to it without asking permission.

She climbed the path.

Before her knuckles touched the wood, the door swung open. Maren stood in the gap, small and straight-backed, wrapped in a shawl the color of ash, and her eyes — those flat gray eyes Ivo had catalogued as cruelty for six years — were wide and wet and afraid.

"You took your time," Maren said, and pulled her inside, and threw the bolt.

* * *

The single room was lit by one candle stub, and even that flame burned strangely, leaning toward Ivo as if it recognized her. Herb bundles hung dead and brown from the beams — every drying charm failed, every preserving sigil gray. Maren's worktable had been swept clear except for three objects laid out in a row with terrible deliberateness: a knife, a bowl of water, and a book bound in leather gone soft with age.

"Sit," Maren said.

"Mistress—"

"*Sit.* We have less time than I hoped and more than I feared. Sit, and listen, and do not interrupt me, for once in your life."

Ivo sat. Up close she could see what she had refused to see for years: that Maren had aged decades in a single night. The lines around her mouth were canyons. Her hands, folded on the table, trembled like leaves — not with fear, Ivo realized, but with effort, with the strain of holding something back, the way a person holds a door against wind.

"You felt it," Maren said. "When the wards broke. Don't lie to me."

"Yes."

"And the magic came into you like a river into dry ground, and you couldn't have stopped it if you'd tried, and part of you didn't want to try." Ivo said nothing, which was answer enough, and Maren nodded slowly. "That's how it was for me, the night my mother died and it passed to me. That's how it has been for every keeper of our line since the binding. We don't choose it. We inherit it like debt." Her voice dropped. "You're not my apprentice, Ivo. You never were. Apprenticeship was the only cover I could give you. You're my heir."

The candle flame leaned. Somewhere far off, down the valley, a dog began to bark and did not stop.

"The covenant," Ivo said. The word came up out of the old stories, out of kitchen-tales, and sounded different in her own mouth — heavier, realer. "The one with the star-court. The one that ended when the stars went out."

"It didn't end." Maren's laugh was a dry, terrible thing. "It was *eaten*. Do you understand me, girl? A hundred years ago the last king of Vaelport looked at the Starwell — the heart of the covenant, the anchor that held both realms open to each other — and he saw not a sacred trust but a treasury. He built the crown-iron chains. He drained the star's light into the royal line, generation upon generation, and when the star dimmed and the court beyond began to die, he sealed this valley to hide the evidence. To hide *us*. The keepers. The bloodline sworn to hold the covenant in trust." Her trembling hands unfolded and flattened on the table. "Every king since has drunk from what remains. Every warden's charter, every licensed charm, every scrap of power the Crown dispenses like charity — it all comes from a stolen star. And tonight the theft finally failed. The chains are breaking. And the power that was hoarded for a century is spilling back into the world — into the soil, into the wards' ruins, into *you*."

"Why me?" Ivo's voice came out very small. "Why not you? You're the keeper. You've had it for thirty years."

"Because a covenant held by one hand is a treasure, and a treasure gets robbed." Maren reached across the table and took Ivo's wrist, and her grip was iron despite the trembling. "Our line learned that too late. So we thinned ourselves deliberately, generation by generation, hiding the blood, scattering it, letting it sleep — waiting for a night when the wards would break and the inheritance would wake in someone young enough and stubborn enough and *unloved enough by power* to refuse what kings could not. I was hard on you because tenderness makes people pliable, and pliable people kneel." Her gray eyes held Ivo's, and Ivo saw, with a lurch, that everything she had catalogued as cruelty had been architecture — walls built around a girl the world would come hunting. "Tonight they will come for you. Not the Crown's common soldiers — wardens, scouts, the ones with the iron badges and the quiet voices. They felt the wards fall the same moment you did. They know a keeper woke. And they need a keeper's blood to reforge what their masters stole."

Outside, the dog stopped barking.

The silence afterward was worse.

Maren released Ivo's wrist and stood, and her movements had a terrible economy now, a woman spending her final minutes like a miser counting out her last coins. She pushed the book across the table — the soft-leather book, older than either of them, its pages exhaling a smell of dust and something faintly sweet, like flowers in a closed room. "Everything is in here. The road to the Starwell. The words of the original binding. Read it on the road, not here. There's a letter sewn into the spine — read that when you're ready, and not before." She took up the knife, weighing it. "Back door. Down into the mill-race. Follow the water north."

"Mistress, come with me—"

"They'll want proof the line is ended." Maren smiled, and it was the first genuine smile Ivo had ever seen on her face, and it broke Ivo's heart precisely because it came too late. "I intend to give them proof. Expensively."

The knock came then — not on the door but on the air itself, a pressure, a wrongness, the way the valley's silence bent around a presence. Through the window, at the foot of the garden path, three shapes resolved out of the dark. Tall. Unhurried. The dull gleam of iron badges catching the dead light.

Maren blew out the candle.

In the blackness her hands found Ivo's shoulders, turned her, pushed her — gently, gently, the gentlest touch in six years — toward the back door. "The covenant was never meant for one pair of hands," she whispered. "Remember that above all else, when they offer you crowns and when they offer you comfort both. It was always meant for many. Go."

Ivo went, with the book against her chest and tears cutting hot tracks she didn't dare wipe. Behind her she heard the front bolts draw back, heard Maren's voice ring out clear and contemptuous across the garden — "*Come up the path properly, gentlemen, or shall I fetch you a ladder*" — and then light flared, and shouting, and a sound like a struck bell.

She was in the mill-race, thigh-deep in freezing water, before she let herself stop. Above the bank, orange flickered against the underside of the clouds — firelight, or witch-light, or both — and she heard the crackle of a hedge-ward igniting, Maren's last working, the old woman spending herself like a bonfire. Then a sound Ivo had no name for, a sound like a held note snapping, and the light went out, and the night came down complete.

Ivo pressed the book to her chest and stood in the cold water and shook, and the white flowers bloomed around her in the dark, opening and opening, marking her grief in a language she hadn't asked to speak.

Then she turned north, toward the shattered border and the starless world beyond it, and walked out of the valley that had made her — carrying, whether she wanted it or not, everything it had buried.
