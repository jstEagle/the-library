## 3. The Cartographer's Crime

The cell had no window, but Jude Ashgrave had learned to read the hour by other means — by the drip of condensation from the ceiling stone onto the back of his neck, by the rhythm of the boots above him on the guard-walk, by the way the dark changed its weight between the changing of the lamps. Three years in the Saltcells of Vaelport and he had become a cartographer still, mapping a country with no roads: the geography of his own confinement.

He was mapping it again in his head when the door came apart.

Not opened — *came apart*, hinges sheared, oak split along its grain as though something enormous had leaned on it out of boredom. Lamplight poured in like water into a drowned hold, and behind the light stood four wardens in the grey-and-gold of the Crown's Long Watch, and behind them, filling the corridor with his shadow, the Lord Warden himself.

Jude did not stand. He had a rule about standing for people who broke doors.

"Cartographer." The Lord Warden's voice was quiet in the way that deep water is quiet. "You're wanted."

"I'm serving eleven years," Jude said. His voice came out rusted; he hadn't spoken aloud in weeks. He cleared his throat and tried again. "For sedition, forgery of royal instruments, and the theft of Crown secrets. I remember the sentence. I helped write the confession."

"You did. It was admirably thorough." The Lord Warden stepped into the cell, which was not built for two men and became smaller by half. Up close he smelled of cold iron and lamp-oil and something faintly vegetal, green, wrong — like a garden grown underground. "The stars have gone out."

Jude blinked. Of all the openings he might have imagined — and he had imagined many, in three years of imagining — this was not one.

"The stars," the Lord Warden said again, patiently, as though to a slow student, "have gone out. Every one of them. Last night, at the turn of the tide. And the wards at Hollowmere fell with them, all at once, all together, like a throat opening." He crouched then, bringing his face level with Jude's, and his eyes were pale as skimmed milk. "You drew that valley once, Ashgrave. You drew what was under it. And now I find myself needing a man who can read his own hand."

* * *

They gave him a bath first, which frightened him more than the door had.

Three years of grime soaked off him in grey ribbons, and beneath it his skin was the white of things kept from the light, soft as paper. A barber shaved him while two wardens watched with drawn steel, and Jude studied his own face in the cloudy mirror and found a stranger: cheekbones too sharp now, hair going iron-grey at thirty-one, eyes that had learned not to move quickly. Prisoners' eyes. He looked away from the stranger before the looking became unbearable.

They dressed him in borrowed clothes cut fine enough to insult him — good wool, real linen, a coat that fit almost exactly, because of course they knew his measurements. They fed him lamb and new bread and small sweet pears, and he ate slowly and refused to enjoy it, and enjoyed it anyway, his treacherous body singing over every mouthful.

Then they brought him up into the Warden's Tower, into a round room walled entirely in maps.

That was the cruelty of it. That was deliberate; he understood deliberate when he saw it. Every wall of the Lord Warden's study was hung with charts of the realm — coastlines inked in blue-black, mountain ranges hatched like sleeping animals, the great processional road running north through the heart of the map toward a blank space where the old kings had ordered the parchment left empty. Jude knew that blank space intimately. He had been the last man licensed to fill it. Filling it had cost him everything.

"Sit," said the Lord Warden, and this time Jude sat.

A fire burned in a hearth shaped like a crown. On the table between them lay a single sheet under glass, and even before the Lord Warden lifted the glass Jude knew what it was, because he would have known that handwriting anywhere. It was his own.

The Starwell chart. Or a copy of it — the original had been sealed in the royal vaults, or burned, or so he'd told himself for three years to make the loss bearable. The lines were young lines, confident, drawn by a man who still believed maps were instruments of mercy. The crater rendered in careful concentric rings. The chains of crown-iron radiating outward like the spokes of a wheel. In the margin, his own younger hand had written: *Here lies the anchor. Here lies the wound.*

"Do you know," the Lord Warden said, "how many men have seen this chart?"

"No."

"One. Me. The king never looked at it. Kings don't look; they are *shown*." He laid one long finger on the crater's rim. "I have spent nine years assembling what you mapped in eight months. I have bought dying hedge-witches for their fragments. I have sent expeditions into the thorn country and buried the reports of the ones who returned. Nine years, Ashgrave, and you did it in eight months because you were permitted to walk where others were forbidden — and because you believed, didn't you, that you were saving lives."

Jude's hands were flat on the table. He made sure they stayed flat. "There was famine. The southern provinces were eating seed-corn. The court said the separation of the realms was starving us — that the old covenant's breaking had cut off something the land needed. They said the Starwell could be reopened. Managed. I thought—" He stopped.

"You thought you were drawing a well," the Lord Warden finished softly. "You were drawing a cage. Yes. I've read your confession. You wrote very movingly about it. 'I mistook the instrument of my ambition for an act of charity.'" He smiled without warmth. "Do you know why the Crown sealed your chart and imprisoned you, rather than using it?"

"They feared it."

"They *used* it." The Lord Warden let that sit in the room like smoke. "Every expedition I mentioned? Yours. Every fragment purchased from every dying witch? Purchased along routes you plotted. You have been working for the Crown these three years, Ashgrave, whether you knew it or not. Your maps have been in my hands since the week after your arrest. All that has changed is that tonight, for the first time, I am telling you."

The fire cracked. Somewhere below, the bells of Vaelport rang the hour — seven strokes, muffled by fog, because Vaelport was always fogged now, the sea-weather strange since the night the sky went blind.

"What do you want from me?" Jude asked.

"Your eyes," said the Lord Warden, "and your feet, and your silence. One journey."

* * *

He laid it out the way a good officer lays out any operation, and that was the most chilling thing of all — how ordinary it sounded. A party leaving within the fortnight, north along the processional road. A guide who knew the old ways. Himself, the Lord Warden, traveling incognito as far as the Gate of Thorns.

And bait. The word was never used, but Jude heard it anyway, humming under every clean sentence like a fly in a jar.

"The fall of the wards will have been felt," the Lord Warden said. "Whatever slept at the valley's edge is awake, and whatever the covenant bound will seek its keeper. There is a bloodline. There has always been a bloodline — the keepers of Hollowmere, the hedge-touched, the ones whose small magics were never theirs but *held in trust*. When the wards fell, that trust reverted. Somewhere in that valley or on the roads beyond it, a person who has never mattered in her whole life is suddenly carrying the last living thread of an ancient covenant." His milk-pale eyes fixed on Jude. "She will run. The frightened always run, and running leaves tracks. And when she runs, she will need help — shelter, passage, knowledge. She will need someone trustworthy."

"And you want me to be trustworthy."

"I want you to *be found*," the Lord Warden corrected. "By her, or by those who find her. You are, after all, famous in a narrow way — the disgraced cartographer, the man who sinned for the realm's sake. Sympathetic figures attract strays. And should she reach the Starwell before we do—" he turned the chart slightly, aligning its north with some private compass, "—she cannot open what she cannot find. The thorns have swallowed the approaches. Only a man who has walked them in memory knows the way. Only your hand can lead her there."

"To the place you intend to drain."

"To the place I intend to *reforge*," the Lord Warden said, with the mild patience of a man correcting a child's arithmetic. "The covenant was a leash poorly fitted. It bound both realms to each other through a handful of peasant bloodlines — trust, Ashgrave, as the binding agent. Trust! No wonder it failed. What I propose is order. The covenant reforged upon the throne itself, power flowing downward as power ought, the star-court held fast in service rather than left to rot in the dark and drag our harvests down with it." He rose, and his shadow went up the map-wall and lay across the northern blank space like a lid closing. "The king agrees. The king agrees to anything, these days. The sky went out over his palace and he sat up all night asking God's pardon for sins he couldn't name."

"And if I refuse?"

The Lord Warden considered the question honestly, which Jude respected and hated. "Eleven years becomes twenty-two. Your cell moves two floors down, where the dripping starts in autumn. And the expedition goes anyway, with lesser guides, losing more months — and the keeper dies badly somewhere on the road, unhelped, unmapped, and the covenant falls into hands rougher than mine." A pause. "I am not the worst thing that wants her, Ashgrave. I am merely the best organized."

It was, Jude thought, the truest sentence spoken in the room.

* * *

They put him in a guest chamber that night instead of returning him to the cells — locked, of course, but a gilded lock — and Jude stood a long time at the window looking out over Vaelport, which glittered below him like spilled coins, beautiful and blind.

No stars. He kept searching for them anyway, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth. The sky was a smooth black nothing from horizon to horizon, and the city's ten thousand lamps seemed suddenly obscene, a civilization pretending at candlelight while the roof of the world stood open and hollow. Out in the harbor the ships rode at anchor with no lights to steer home by. Somewhere north, past the moors and the ash-country and the thorn country, a valley called Hollowmere sat under the same blind sky, and inside it, a girl he had never met was learning that her whole life had been a lie told in small magic.

He thought about Maren's cottage — no. Not Maren. He thought about the hedge-witches he'd interviewed, years ago, for the survey. Old women in doorways who had given him charms against river-fever and asked nothing, who had trusted the king's cartographer because the king's colors meant protection, and whom he had later sold piece by piece to men who paid in secrets. He remembered one particularly: a bent-backed woman at the valley mouth who had gripped his wrist with surprising strength and said, *Boy, whatever they've sent you to draw — draw it twice. Once for them, and once for the truth.*

He had laughed at her. He had drawn it once.

Now the Lord Warden wanted him to draw it a third time — wanted his shame worn as a lure, his ruined face set out on the road like a lantern to bring the keeper in gentle as a moth. And the terrible thing, the thing Jude sat with through the small hours while the fog pressed its face against the glass, was how easy it would be. How natural. He was good at being trusted. It was the one craft the Crown had ever truly taught him.

He thought of the girl — whoever she was — walking out of a dead valley into a world that ate the trusting. He thought of her finding him: the famous penitent, kind-eyed, useful, safe. He thought of leading her north by his own remembered roads, straight into milk-white eyes and a cage reforged on a throne.

And he thought, with equal clarity, of twenty-two years in the wet dark, and of the fact that refusal would not save her either. The Lord Warden had been honest about that. The machine moved with or without him; his choice was only ever where to stand when it passed.

Toward dawn, the fog thinned for a moment — a trick of the wind off the sea — and for three or four breaths the eastern sky showed clean and black and bottomless, and Jude had the sudden vertiginous certainty that he was looking not at night but at absence, at a hole in the world where something used to burn. Then the fog closed again, and the moment was gone, and he made his decision the way he had once made all his worst decisions: quickly, practically, and telling himself it was mercy.

In the morning he signed. Ink, wax, his name in the old confident hand — and beneath it, in the clause he insisted on adding himself, in full view of two clerks and the Lord Warden's own eyes:

*The undersigned requests sole authority over route-finding, per royal warrant of 8 months prior, including all approach-maps to the site designated Starwell, copies of same to be withheld from all persons save the Lord Warden until arrival.*

Control of the maps. It looked like pride. It looked like a broken man grasping at the last scrap of his old importance, and the Lord Warden smiled at it indulgently and initialled the clause, because what danger could there be in letting the traitor keep his pens?

Jude bowed, received his warrant, and walked out of the tower into the fog of Vaelport with the chart folded against his chest like a second heartbeat — knowing already which copy he would draw twice, and for whom, and knowing only that the road north began at the pilgrim ford below the moors, where anyone fleeing a fallen valley must come, sooner or later, hungry and hunted and hoping for a kind face.

He went to buy a horse, and a plain coat, and the look of a man worth trusting.

The hunt was on. He intended to win it — he simply had not yet decided, and would not decide for some miles yet, which side he was hunting for.
