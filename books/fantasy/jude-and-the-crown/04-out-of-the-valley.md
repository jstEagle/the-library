## 4. Out of the Valley

The wards had not merely broken. They had *opened*.

Ivo understood that only when she stood before what remained of them, at the valley mouth where the road narrowed to a throat of standing stones. All her life she had been told — by Maren, by every hedge-wife and charm-seller in Hollowmere — that the border was a wall, a sealing, a shutting-away. But the shattered wards hung in the air like the aftermath of a struck bell, and what she felt radiating from their ruin was not a broken lock.

It was an open hand.

The stones themselves were older than any kingdom's memory, taller than the watchtower at Millhollow, veined with silver that pulsed faintly even now, even dead. Frost clung to their southern faces in the shape of letters no one in the valley could read anymore. Between two of them, the air shimmered like heat over summer wheat — except it was deep autumn, and the shimmer was cold, and beyond it the world simply *stopped*, the way a page stops at its margin.

Ivo pressed her palm against the space between the stones.

It parted around her hand like water. Not resistance — invitation. The magic that flooded up her arm was so much larger than her small charms, her weather-senses, her little knacks for coaxing beans out of stubborn soil, that she laughed aloud, alone in the dark, a wild unlovely sound that scared a crow off one of the standing stones. The crow flew into the shimmer and vanished, and she felt it go, felt the tiny spark of its life pass through the threshold as easily as breath.

*Everything on the other side of this is starless,* she reminded herself. *Everything on the other side of this wants something.*

She went through anyway.

* * *

Crossing felt like being born again, if birth were remembered. For one long heartbeat she was nowhere — no valley, no world, just a warmth like a held note — and then the night opened around her and she was stumbling onto foreign grass, gasping, her heart slamming against her ribs.

And the magic *roared*.

Inside Hollowmere, her gift had been a candle in a cellar: real, but smothered, good for nothing grander than knowing when rain was coming three hours early. Here, outside, it was as though someone had opened a door in her chest and let the whole sky in. She could feel the land beneath her feet breathing. She could feel the roots of the grass tangling with the roots of the trees, a conversation going on all around her, patient and green and utterly indifferent to human grief.

Flowers bloomed at her footsteps.

She didn't command it. She didn't even understand it. She took a step toward the tree line and crocuses — crocuses, in autumn — split the dark soil and opened their pale throats, and where her second step fell they came up white as milk, and by her third she was walking in a trail of impossible blossom, and she was laughing again, weeping again, both at once, because this was what she had been *for*. This was what Maren's harsh lessons and bitter silences had been grinding against all those years: not a hedge-witch's trickle, but a river with the dam pulled out.

"Look at you," she whispered to her own hands. Light gathered in her palms like fireflies come home to roost. When she flexed her fingers, sparks leapt off her skin and died in the grass with tiny sounds like distant applause. "Look at what I—"

The word *am* never got said, because somewhere behind her, in the direction of the broken stones, a whistle sounded. Two notes, flat and deliberate. Then a third.

Wardens' signals. Every child in Hollowmere knew them from stories: the Crown's grey riders, the leash-holders, the ones who came for hedge-folk who wandered too near the border. Stories said they hadn't crossed into the valley in a hundred years.

Stories had also said the stars would last forever.

Ivo ran.

* * *

She ran until the blossoms stopped following her — which took longer than she'd have guessed, half a mile of flowering wake marking her path like a wound across the dark hills — and then she ran some more, because terror is its own fuel and does not ask permission. The land here was wrong to her senses: too open, too tame, hedged and fenced and road-cut, a country worn smooth by generations of use. Hollowmere had always felt like a held breath. This felt like an exhale that had gone on so long everyone had forgotten what air was for.

Above her, where stars should have been, there was nothing. Not cloud-cover — she knew cloud-cover, she'd grown up under a sky like dirty wool. This was a clean, hard, absolute blackness, a ceiling rather than a sky, and looking at it too long made her eyes water and her stomach turn, the way staring down a well does.

She finally stopped in the lee of a drystone wall, lungs burning, and took stock. Satchel: yes. Maren's knife, the good one with the bone handle: yes. Bread, hard cheese, a twist of salt, the flint: yes. The little bundle of Maren's charms — iron nail, red thread, a dried sprig of rowan — pressed against her hip like a sleeping animal.

And under everything, humming through her blood, the new roar of herself.

She should keep moving. She knew she should. The whistles had come from the valley mouth, and wardens rode faster than frightened girls walked. But her legs had opinions of their own, and they had chosen the wall, and so she sat down in the shadow of it and ate half the bread and tried very hard not to think about Maren's door, or the splintered frame, or the way her mistress had looked at her in the last moment — not with fear, Ivo realized now, not with fear at all, but with the fierce particular satisfaction of a woman whose whole life had been aimed at something and had finally, finally hit it.

*Go,* Maren had mouthed, holding the door with her whole slight body. *Go and be worth it.*

Ivo was still chewing the grief down along with the bread when the girl dropped out of the tree above her.

There was no warning. One moment the branch overhead was empty; the next a shape resolved out of the darkness the way a fish resolves out of muddy water, and then hands — quick, grimy, shockingly strong hands — had the satchel strap over Ivo's head and off her shoulders, and a knee was pressing her back into the wall, and a blade was resting against her throat with the calm familiarity of an old friend.

"Don't," said the girl.

She couldn't have been more than fifteen or sixteen, all elbows and angles, wrapped in layers of stolen clothing that might once have belonged to four different people. Her hair was cropped raggedly short. Her eyes, in the starless dark, caught what little light there was and gave none of it back.

"That's mine," Ivo managed.

"It's everyone's." The girl's voice was flat as slate. "That's how it works out here. You had it; now I have it. Keep your chin down and your hands open and you'll live to be robbed again."

"You don't understand—" Magic surged up in Ivo without her asking it to, the way a dog surges at the end of a rope. Light spilled off her skin, bright enough to make the girl flinch back a step, bright enough to show Ivo her own attacker properly: thin face, chapped lips, a brand on the side of her neck, half-hidden under the collar of her coat, that might have been a letter or might have been a cage drawn in lines. "I can—"

"I know what you can." The girl didn't sound impressed. She sounded tired in a way that went all the way down to the marrow. "You're glowing like a festival lamp, and every warden within five miles can see it, and in about forty seconds they're going to come over that hill and take you apart to find out what you're made of. So thank you kindly for the light. It's made my evening much easier."

She slung the satchel across her own back.

"Give it back." Ivo got her feet under her. The magic was still roaring, and it wanted things — it wanted to lash out, to bloom, to burn — and she reined it the way you rein a horse that has never once been ridden, barely, badly, with sweat running down her spine. "Please. There's food in there. There's—"

"There's food everywhere, if you're not picky." The girl was backing away now, melting toward the treeline, and there was something almost ritualistic about her retreat, something rehearsed, and it took Ivo a moment too long to understand why.

Because it wasn't a retreat.

The whistles came again — closer, three parties now, triangulating — and the hillside behind the drystone wall lit up with lantern-light, and grey shapes crested the ridge: riders, six of them, in the long coats and high collars of the Crown's wardens, and the lead rider's voice carried down the slope, bored and terrible:

"There. The glowing one. Take her living."

They came down at a trot, and Ivo did the arithmetic of a girl with a knife, a satchel-less hip, and a gift she had owned for exactly one day, and the answer was: dead. The answer was: dead, and worse than dead, because the lead warden was dismounting now and pulling something from his saddlebag that was coiled and glinting and looked horribly like chain.

Then the treeline exploded.

Not the trees — the girl. She came out of the dark the way she'd come out of it the first time, from nowhere, but this time she came *low and fast*, and she wasn't carrying her little belt-knife anymore but something longer, something that moved in the dark with a sound like tearing cloth, and the first warden went down with a scream, and then it was all noise and lantern-shadows and Ivo pressed against the wall watching a sixteen-year-old fight six trained men and understanding, with a lurch, that the girl was not trying to win.

She was trying to make a hole.

"MOVE!" she screamed at Ivo, and Ivo moved — stumbled, actually, straight into the gap the girl had torn between two wardens — and then they were both running, uphill, downhill, Ivo honestly couldn't have said, through gorse that raked her arms, while behind them a man shouted orders and someone else shouted about the brand, *check the brand, it'll be the kennel-girl, it's the kennel-girl—*

The girl flinched at those words so visibly that even running blind in the dark, Ivo saw it.

* * *

They stopped, eventually, because they had to — in a hollow under a fallen beech, where the roots made a roof and the leaf-rot made a bed neither of them deserved. The girl sat with her back against the trunk, breathing hard, a dark stain spreading slowly down her left sleeve.

"You're hurt," Ivo said.

"You're observant."

"Let me—" And before she had decided to, her hands were already moving, already reaching, and the magic came to meet them like water finding its level. The girl jerked away.

"Don't touch me."

"It heals. That's what it does, I think, it's what it did for the— for someone, it mends things—"

"I said don't." But she was swaying now, and the stain was spreading, and after a long moment she swore a word Ivo didn't know and thrust her arm out like a queen granting an audience. "Fast. Before I change my mind."

Ivo peeled back the sleeve. A slice across the forearm, deep but clean, still bleeding. She laid both palms over it the way Maren had taught her to lay hands on sick lambs — *gently, girl, gently, healing's mostly listening* — and listened.

What she heard nearly made her pull away. Under the girl's skin, threaded through her like wire through wax, was a working. Old, ugly, deliberately cruel: a tether-work, a tracking-spell, sunk so deep into flesh that removing it would kill. It pulsed in time with the girl's heartbeat, and each pulse sent a thin thread of information outward, into the dark, toward whoever held the other end.

Toward the wardens.

"They know where you are," Ivo whispered. "Right now. Always."

The girl looked at her with those lightless eyes, and something in her face — the first thing all night that wasn't armor — cracked and showed through.

"Aye," she said. "So do you, now. Congratulations. Hope it was worth the look."

"I can't fix that. I'm sorry. I can fix the cut." And she did, or the magic did — warmth pouring through her palms, the wound knitting with a sensation like the memory of itching — and when it was done the girl flexed her arm, stared at the unbroken skin as if it had personally betrayed her, and then, without a word, took Ivo's satchel off her shoulder and threw it back into her lap.

Ivo blinked at it. "You're giving it back?"

"I'm lending it back. There's a difference, and the difference is I know where you sleep." The girl dug in the satchel, found the bread, tore it in half with great ceremony, and kept the larger piece. "Sef," she said, around a mouthful, which Ivo eventually understood to be a name.

"Ivo."

"Aye, well." Sef swallowed. "Ivo. Lesson one of the outside world, since you're clearly fresh off the turnip cart: nobody saves anybody for free. I spent a knife-blade and a pint of blood pulling you out of that, and I did it because a glowing girl who doesn't know how to stop glowing is worth more alive and grateful than dead in a ditch. Understand? Everything costs. Out here, everything *always* costs."

The words landed like stones. But underneath them, Ivo heard what Sef probably thought she'd buried: that the girl had spent blood at all. That she could have taken the satchel and melted into the dark and left a stranger to the chains, and instead had turned back into six swords for a person whose name she didn't know.

"Thank you," Ivo said.

"Don't do that either." Sef lay back against the roots and closed her eyes, though Ivo noticed her knife-hand stayed loose and ready even in apparent sleep. "Gratitude's just debt wearing a nice dress."

They sat in the dark under the dead beech, in a starless country, and Ivo ate her smaller half of the bread and thought about Maren's door and the open hand of the broken wards and the brand pulsing under Sef's skin like a second heartbeat, and she understood that the wonder she'd felt crossing the border — the crocuses, the roaring river of herself — had already curdled into something harder and more useful.

Wonder told her she was powerful. Wariness told her she was prey. Both were true, and only one of them would keep her alive.

"Sef," she said quietly. "The wardens are coming for me. But they track *you*."

"I know how tracking works, turnip-girl."

"So we can't stay anywhere. We can't stop moving. Which means—" Ivo took a breath. "Which means I need you. Not your knife. Your knowledge. Everything out here costs, you said. So name your price, and I'll pay it, but I need you to get me south. To Vaelport."

Sef opened one eye.

"Now why," she said slowly, "would a girl who glows like a festival lamp want to walk into the Crown's own city?" And she sat up, and the ruined starless dark seemed to lean in close around them both, listening for the answer.
