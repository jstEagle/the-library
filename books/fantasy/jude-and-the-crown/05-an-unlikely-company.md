## 5. An Unlikely Company

Jude Ashgrave had spent eleven days learning to walk like a pilgrim.

It was harder than it sounded. A man who had spent nine years bent over drafting tables and four months in a Crown cell walked a particular way — shoulders drawn in, eyes down, hands folded into himself like a letter awaiting the seal. Pilgrims, by contrast, walked like the road owed them nothing and might yet give them everything. They swung their arms. They sang badly. They stopped to argue with shrines.

So Jude practiced. He loosened his shoulders on the road out of Vaelport until they ached. He bought a pilgrim's staff and a hat with a brim gone soft as old bread. He learned the pilgrim's greeting — *the road rise to meet you* — and the answer, and the little knot of fingers over the breastbone that meant *I carry nothing worth stealing*, which was, in his case, the truest prayer he had ever made.

The lie he carried instead was sewn into his boot heel: a pardon signed by the Lord Warden himself, conditional on one delivery. And in the false bottom of his ink-case, a sheaf of coded letters waiting to be sent from waystation drop-boxes, each one a small betrayal in a neat cartographer's hand.

*Two travelers, southbound on the pilgrim road. One girl, dark-haired, perhaps seventeen, moves like she's never been hungry. One feral child, quick hands, watches the treeline. The girl's magic is visible from a distance — flowers where she walks. Impossible to miss. Impossible to miss.*

He had written that last line three times before he understood he wasn't describing her for the wardens. He was warning himself.

He found them on the fourth day, at the shrine of Saint Aldhelm-the-Forgetful, where the road forked around a mossy stone figure whose face had been worn to a suggestion. They had made camp in the shrine's lee, and the girl — Ivo, the wardens' description had said, Ivo Blackwood, runaway apprentice, keeper's blood — was asleep with her cheek on her pack like it might bolt without her.

The other one was not asleep. The other one was crouched on a boulder above him with a knife, and had been, he would later learn, since the moment he came around the bend.

"Evening," Jude said, because his heart was hammering and the only way he knew to survive fear was to talk through it. "The road rise to meet you."

"Depends," said the feral child, "who's asking it to."

"A traveler. Same as you." He spread his empty hands. "Jude Ashgrave. Late of Vaelport. I'm for the northern shrines before solstice."

The child's eyes were the color of river ice and just as warm. She looked at him the way a crow looks at a scarecrow — checking, with professional interest, whether anything was stuffed inside the coat. "You walk like a clerk."

"I *was* a clerk."

"Uh-huh." She didn't lower the knife. But she raised her voice, just slightly, toward the sleeping shape. "Ivo. There's a clerk."

The girl woke the way animals wake — all at once, no groggy borders. She sat up, saw him, and Jude watched something move across her face that he would spend the rest of the road trying to name. It wasn't fear. It was recognition of a kind, the look of someone seeing a coin from a country they'd only ever heard described.

"You're from the city," she said.

"Vaelport."

"Then you've seen it. The sky." Her voice was careful, level, and underneath the levelness something was burning. "The stars. Have they come back anywhere?"

Jude had prepared for this question on the road. He had prepared a lie — *no, nothing yet, the astronomers are baffled* — because the truth was that in Vaelport the sky was not merely starless but *thick*, a low grey ceiling like the inside of a pot, and that the Crown had posted criers explaining it was an atmospheric phenomenon, a temporary inversion, nothing to do with the wards at Hollowmere, nothing to do with anything, please return to your labors.

He looked at her burned-bright eyes and heard himself say: "No. Nowhere. And it's not weather."

She exhaled, long and shaking, and something in her shoulders came down half an inch — the half inch of a person who had been carrying a suspicion alone and had just found one witness. The feral child, watching this exchange, lowered her knife with the air of a merchant closing a deal she didn't like.

"Sef," she said, by way of nothing. "That's Ivo. You can sleep on the far side of the fire. If you come near our packs in the night I'll take your fingers."

"Sef," Jude repeated gravely, "I have never in my life been so reassured."

* * *

He sent his first letter from the drop-box at the Millbrook waystation, a hollowed saint's hand set into the shrine wall, and hated every minute of the writing and every minute of the mailing and mailed it anyway.

That was the arrangement. That was the *deal*. The Lord Warden Corvane had come down to the cells personally — that was the thing people didn't understand about the Lord Warden, that he came down *personally*, that he liked the smell of the cells, that he had sat on the stool across from Jude's bars with his hands folded and his voice gentle as a man discussing weather with a neighbor.

"You mapped the Starwell," Corvane had said. "You are the only living soul who knows the northern approaches. The wards are down. The keeper's blood will surface — it always surfaces, it's like water, it finds its level. And when it does, it will walk north." He had smiled then, and Jude had understood that he was not looking at a man who wanted a pardon granted. He was looking at a man who wanted a debt incurred. "Bring me to her, Ashgrave. Or bring her to me. Either way, you walk out of Vaelport a free man with a full purse. Fail, and you walk back into a cell that no longer has a door."

So: the letters. The coded drops, three days apart, describing their pace and their route and the girl's growing command of her power, each report a stone laid carefully on the path that led, Jude told himself, to a cell with a door again. He was not a monster. He was a pragmatist. The Crown would take her either way — better he guide the taking than be ground under it. He had made his peace with the Crown years ago, when he'd inked the Starwell's coordinates onto vellum and told himself the famine was real and the realms' separation was its cause and a map was only a map, not a knife.

A map was only a map. He had repeated it like a rosary for nine years. It had never once worked.

* * *

The farm came on the sixth day, in a fold of hills where the pilgrim road dipped through country too poor for the Crown to bother taxing and too stubborn to empty out. The house was stone and turf, low against the wind, and the family that lived in it was dying of something the locals called *grey-lung* — a sickness that had come, the neighbors said, when the stars went out, as though the dark itself had gotten into the water.

Jude saw the cross of rag on the doorpost and would have walked past. Pilgrims walked past sick houses; that was doctrine, that was sense, that was what a man protecting a cover story did. Sef walked past without slowing. But Ivo stopped at the gate, and stood there, and Jude watched her hand rise to the latch and hover, and watched her face do a complicated arithmetic — *I am hunted, I am carrying the last covenant in my blood, every hour I delay is an hour the wardens gain* — and watched her open the gate anyway.

"We can't," Sef said flatly.

"I know," Ivo said, and went in.

Inside, the house smelled of smoke and sour milk and the sweet-rot smell Jude knew from the plague years, the smell of lungs giving up. There were five of them: a grandmother in a chair by the fire, breathing in whistles; a man and woman in the loft, too weak to climb down; two children, maybe six and nine, curled together on a straw pallet, their lips the grey-blue of dusk. The mother tried to rise when Ivo entered and managed only to turn her head, and her eyes said what her voice could not: *whoever you are, whatever you are, please.*

"I can't fix this," Ivo said quietly. Not to the family — to herself, an honest ledger. "I'm not — I only know hedge-charms, small things, my mistress always said—" She stopped. Her hands had already started moving.

Jude had seen hedge-magic before, in the valley villages on his survey years: charms against chilblain, charms to keep milk sweet, little knotted threads of power that cost the hedgewife a headache and bought a week of luck. It was small magic. It was *supposed* to be small magic.

What Ivo Blackwood did in that turf-roofed house was not small.

She knelt first by the children, and Jude saw her lay her palms flat on the small grey chests, and he saw — there was no other word for it — *light* go out of her. Not light like a lamp. Light like water finding a channel, running down her arms and into the two small bodies, and where it ran the grey receded like frost from a warming pane. The younger child coughed — a real cough, a living cough, wet and furious — and began to cry, which was the best sound in the house.

Then the grandmother. Then she climbed the ladder to the loft, though her legs shook, and did the parents, and by the end she was grey-faced herself and Sef had to catch her elbow at the top of the ladder, and the whole time she was working she was talking to them in a low voice, telling them how to boil the water, where to cut the willow bark, what to feed a lung that was learning to work again — knowledge, plain and practical, the unglamorous half of healing that cost nothing and was worth everything.

The father got down the ladder on his own feet, weeping without noise, and tried to press a coin into Ivo's hand. A copper, green with age. It was, Jude could see, the last coin in the house; he'd watched the woman hide it under a hearthstone that morning with the furtive motion of the truly poor.

"I can't," Ivo said.

"You'll take it," the man said, "or I'll have sinned twice, once in despair and once in pride."

Ivo looked at the coin. Then she closed the man's fingers back over it and said, "Give it to the shrine at Millbrook. Tell them a keeper sent it. They'll know what to do with it by solstice." And she smiled — tired, crooked, real — and went out the door, and the family stood in their own doorway like people waking from a long bad winter.

Outside, Sef was furious. "You gave away *power*. Free. Do you know what power costs out here? Do you know what people *pay*? You could have—"

"I know what it costs," Ivo said. "I paid it. That's the whole point of paying it yourself." She swayed; Sef caught her; and the two of them stood there in the road, the thief holding up the saint, arguing in fierce whispers while the farm's chimney began, for the first time in a week, to send up smoke that smelled of supper instead of sickness.

Jude stood apart, at the gate, and found that he could not move.

* * *

That night he took out the second letter and could not write it.

The drop was due at the Harrow waystation in two days. The format was simple; Corvane's cryptographers had designed it for tired men: route, pace, condition, threat. *Southbound pilgrim road, making poor time. The girl spent herself healing a farm family at the grey-lung crossing — no payment taken. She will be weak for a day, perhaps two. This is your window. Send riders to the Harrow fork.*

He had the words. He'd had them since the farmhouse. All he had to do was put them down in the little grid cipher, fold the paper into the saint's-hand box, and walk away, and in three days riders would come down the road with warden steel, and the girl who had given away her last strength to strangers' children would be taken — gently, Corvane had promised, *gently, we need her alive* — and hauled north to be a key in a lock, and Jude Ashgrave would walk back into Vaelport with a full purse and a door on his cell that only locked from the outside.

He sat by the fire with the blank paper on his knee and looked across the flames at her.

She was asleep again, worn down to the wick, and Sef sat watch, sharpening her knife, watching Jude with those ice-water eyes. And Jude sat with his coded paper and his cartographer's discipline and thought about maps. About what a map was. He had told himself for nine years that a map was only a description, that the Crown's use of it was the Crown's sin and not his. But he had stood in that farmhouse doorway and watched a girl heal five dying people for the price of a copper she refused, and he had understood, with the clean terrible clarity of a surveyor seeing the true contour of land he'd been flattening on paper for years, that the Lord Warden had not asked him for a map this time.

He had asked him for *her*. And a person was not terrain. A person was not a coordinate. A person was a grey-lung child crying because her chest hurt and her mother was alive, and no grid of squares and numbers would ever make that a thing a man could hand over with clean hands.

*She will be weak for a day, perhaps two. This is your window.*

He looked at the words in his head and heard them in Corvane's gentle voice, and for the first time he heard what they were: not a report. A *price*. He had been pricing her since Vaelport, line by line, the way the Crown priced everything — the Starwell in chains of crown-iron, the star-court's light hoarded in vaults, the famine answered not with roads opened but with a deeper dark. That was the Crown's whole grammar. *What is it worth. Who pays. Who is spent.*

Ivo Blackwood had stood in a dying house and spoken a different language entirely. *I paid it. That's the whole point of paying it yourself.*

Jude looked down at the blank paper for a long time. The fire popped. Somewhere in the dark a nightjar called its one dry note, over and over, a sound like a stitch being pulled through cloth.

Then he stood, walked to the edge of the firelight, and fed the paper to the flames. He watched it curl, blacken, and go up in a ribbon of orange, and he felt — not relief, nothing so kind as relief — but a *shifting*, deep in the foundations, the sound a house makes when something has finally given way. He had crossed lines before, for the Crown, in ink and vellum and silence. This was the first line he had crossed *away* from them, and he understood that there was no surveying back. The map did not fold along old creases anymore. He had torn it.

"You're smiling," Sef said, without looking up from her knife. "I don't trust it."

"You shouldn't," Jude said. His voice came out strange to him, lighter, like a man setting down a weight he'd mistaken for his spine. "I've just done the stupidest thing of my life."

"Good," said Sef. "We were worried you were boring."

He laughed — actually laughed, a rusty sound with no practice in it — and sat back down by the fire, and did not tell them what he'd burned, or what would come looking when the drops went silent. He did not tell them that the Lord Warden did not forgive silence, that Corvane had built his career on reading absence the way other men read letters, and that somewhere north a very patient man was already noting that Ashgrave's reports had stopped.

He did not tell them that the road ahead now led through the Harrow fork, where the riders would come whether he called them or not — and that when they came, he would be standing on the wrong side of them.

The nightjar called. Ivo slept on, her hands still faintly luminous, like a lamp banked but not out. And Jude Ashgrave, disgraced cartographer, betrayer by trade, pulled his soft-brimmed hat over his eyes and began, for the first time in nine years, to think about what he would do instead of what he could survive.
