## 6. The Road of Hollow Kings

The road began where the pilgrim road ended—at a gate that was no longer a gate.

Ivo stopped at the threshold of it and felt her breath go strange in her chest. Two pillars of black stone rose from the earth, taller than any tree she had ever seen, and between them the old processional road unrolled northward like a ribbon pulled taut by some enormous patient hand. The paving stones were fitted so precisely she could not have slid a knife-blade between them, and they were warm under her boots. Not sun-warm. It was an overcast morning, gray pressing down on the hills like a lid, and yet the stones held heat the way a sleeping animal holds it.

"They called this the King's Way," Jude said behind her. He had his map out even though there was nothing on it worth consulting for the next fifty miles—a single line drawn in faded red ink, running north-northwest through blank country marked only *the old processional*. "Kings walked it to be crowned. Or so the histories say."

"And you don't believe the histories."

"I believe maps." He folded the map away with the crisp economy of a man who had done it ten thousand times. "The histories say crowned. The road says something else. Look at the wear."

Ivo looked. She saw it then, and wished she hadn't: the ruts worn into the stone weren't wheel-ruts. They were footprints—or rather, the ghost of footprints—two shallow channels ground into the rock by centuries of feet, and every single one of them, as far as the road vanished into the haze, pointed *south*. Coming home. Nobody's feet had ever worn the road walking away.

Sef crouched at the base of the left pillar, running her thumb along a groove cut deep into the black stone. "There was writing here," she said. "Scraped off."

"Everything on this road has been scraped off," Jude said quietly. "Keep your hands off it anyway."

But Ivo had already touched the pillar. She couldn't say why—only that the magic in her, which since the night the stars went out had been less like a talent and more like a second heartbeat, leaned toward the stone the way a hungry child leans toward a lit window. Her palm met the surface and the cold went through her arm and into her ribs, and for half a breath she heard—not words, not quite—a vast slow *attention* turning toward her, the way a fish turns in dark water when a fly lands on the pond.

She snatched her hand back. The place where she'd touched was darker than the stone around it, wet-looking, as if the pillar had drunk something out of her skin.

"Jude," she said. Her voice came out flat and small. "What does this road eat?"

He looked at the darkened patch of stone, and then at her, and for once he didn't answer like a man managing what other people were allowed to know. He answered like a man who was afraid.

"Let's walk fast," he said.

* * *

They made eleven miles before dusk, and all eleven miles were lined with kings.

They stood along both verges at intervals of perhaps a quarter mile—statues, or what had been statues, carved from the same black stone as the pillars, each figure twenty feet tall or more, robed and crowned and holding some emblem of office: a sword, a scepter, a set of scales, a book. Every face was gone. Not broken—chiseled away, deliberately, smoothly, leaving a blank oval of rougher stone where features should have been, and the blankness was worse than any defacement. A smashed face is violence. This was *removal*, careful and complete, as though someone had gone down the whole line of them with infinite patience and taken away everything that made a king a person.

The statues had fallen. That was the strangest part at first—that they lay toppled like felled trees, some snapped at the knee, some tipped whole out of their plinths, sunk to the shoulders in the turf of the roadside. A road built for triumph, lined with its own dead kings lying face-down (or face-*blank*) in the grass, and nobody in a hundred years had bothered to clear them away.

"Why would anyone carve a king and then take his face off?" Sef asked, walking backward so she could stare up at the nearest standing figure. She did not touch it. Even she had learned that much by now.

"Because of what he did with it," Jude said.

Ivo stopped walking.

It wasn't a thought so much as a pressure—the magic in her reacting before her mind caught up, the way her hand jerked back from a stove. She turned slowly, looking down the line of toppled figures receding southward into the murk, and let herself feel what she had been refusing to feel all day: that the pressure was coming *from* them. From all of them. Each blank face was faintly, terribly *warm*, the way the road-stones were warm, and the warmth had direction. All of it flowed one way. South. Behind them. Toward Hollowmere.

Toward the valley where no stars shone, where the wards had held the light in and everything else out, for longer than anyone could remember.

"No," she said aloud.

"Ivo?" Sef had her knife half-drawn, scanning the road behind them for whatever Ivo had seen.

"It's not defacement." Ivo's mouth had gone dry. She knelt beside the nearest fallen king, close enough to see the chisel-marks in the ruined face, and forced herself to look at them properly. They were wrong. Chisel-marks bite; they leave bright fresh scars in old stone. These marks were *smooth*, worn soft as river pebbles, and the stone around the empty face was not rougher than the rest of the statue at all. It was polished. Glassy. Worn the way the hollow of a stair is worn by ten thousand feet.

Or the way a cup is worn by ten thousand drinkings.

"They're drinking," she whispered. "Jude. The faces aren't scraped off. They're being *eaten*. Slowly. For years. Centuries." She looked up at him and the whole day rearranged itself around the understanding. "The light comes out of Hollowmere because the stars went out—and it goes somewhere. Everything flows somewhere. It flows down this road. Into them."

Jude had gone very still, the way he did when a map showed him something he'd refused to see. "The Crown took the faces off," he said slowly, "because the faces started going first. And they didn't want anyone asking why."

"No." Ivo stood. Her legs felt far away. "The Crown took the faces off because they wanted people to think *they* did it. To think it was punishment, politics, some old war. But look—" she pointed south, down the endless line of fallen kings, "—it doesn't flow toward Vaelport. There's no road to Vaelport here. It all drains *south*, into the valley, into the ground, down toward—"

"The Starwell," Jude said.

And beneath their feet, so faint that later Ivo would wonder whether she had imagined it, the warm road gave a long, slow pulse—like the swallow of something very large, very deep underground, that had just been reminded it was still hungry.

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of a fallen king, because there was nowhere else with any shelter from the wind that came moaning down the empty miles, and because Sef argued that anything which ate light probably didn't like fire, and a fire might be the safest thing they could carry. Ivo wasn't sure she believed that anymore. But she built the fire anyway, small and careful, and the three of them sat inside its little ring of orange and pretended it was enough.

Sef slept first, curled with her back to the flames and her knife under her hand, the way she always slept—like a door left locked in an empty house. Jude sat awake, mending a strap on his satchel, though the strap needed no mending. Ivo knew he was keeping watch without saying so. She was learning to read him: the way his stillness changed quality when he was guarding something instead of hiding it.

Sleep took her like water takes a dropped coin.

She dreamed of the road—but the road in the dream ran *downhill*, endlessly downhill, though the waking country was rolling hills, and the fallen kings stood upright along it, thousands of them, ranks upon ranks receding into a darkness above and below, and every blank face was turned toward her. She could hear them breathing. Stone shouldn't breathe. It was a sound like wind through a cavern, in and out, in and out, slow as seasons.

Then the dream opened beneath her, the way dreams do, without transition or consent, and she was falling down through the roots of the world, past strata of dark and older dark, and the breathing grew louder until she understood it was not breathing at all. It was singing. A song with almost nothing left in it—threadbare, frayed, sung by a voice that had been singing so long it had forgotten any other way to make sound.

*Ivo.*

Her name, in that voice. It knew her name. It said it the way Maren used to say it when Ivo had done something foolish and brave at once—with exhaustion, and something underneath the exhaustion that might have been hope.

*Ivo Blackwood. Little hedge-witch. You are walking my funeral road, and you are carrying the only torch left.*

"Who are you?" she asked, or tried to ask. In dreams the question came out of her without her lips moving.

A silence, long enough that she felt the shape of it: something gathering itself to speak, the way an old woman gathers herself to rise from a chair she has sat in for years.

*I am what the well holds. I am what your kings drank. Come north, keeper's daughter. Come and see what was buried with me—and bring your hands. You will need more hands than you have.*

The voice began to fade, or to sink—it was hard to tell the difference, and the difference mattered, though she could not yet have said why—and Ivo clawed after it through the collapsing dark, shouting questions she could no longer hear herself ask—

—and woke with her fingers dug into cold dirt and her whole body shaking, the fire burned down to a red eye, and both Sef and Jude crouched over her, awake, alarmed, Sef's knife bare in her fist.

"You were talking in your sleep," Sef said. "You don't talk in your sleep. I've watched."

"What did I say?"

Neither of them answered. Which told her everything.

Jude fed a stick to the fire, and the light jumped, and she saw his face properly for the first time since she'd woken: gray, drawn, older than it had been at sunset. He was looking south down the road, back the way they'd come, and his jaw worked once before he trusted himself to speak.

"There's something I mapped," he said. "Years ago. Something I never put on paper, because putting it on paper would have meant admitting I'd seen it." He turned and looked at her, and behind the bitterness and the shame in his eyes there was something new, something that looked almost like fear offered honestly, for the first time, to another person. "The survey teams that went into the Starwell before the Crown sealed it. Eleven men. Ten came back. The one who didn't—we found his notes. The last page said the same thing, over and over, forty times, in a handwriting that got worse on every line."

"What did it say?"

"*It knows my name.*"

The fire cracked. Somewhere far down the dark road, faint as a memory of a memory, Ivo heard the stone kings breathing—in and out, in and out—and beneath the breathing, threadbare and patient, the song went on singing her name.

To the north, where the sky should have shown the first gray hint of dawn, the darkness had begun, very slightly, to glow.
