## 7. Sef's Price

The brand woke before she did.

Sef had learned to sleep the way a hare sleeps — one ear up, heart running even at rest — so when the heat bloomed under her collarbone she was already moving, already rolling off her bedroll and into the dark with her knife out, before she understood there was nothing to fight. Just the mark on her skin, burning like a coal pressed to her chest, pulsing slow and patient.

*North,* it said. *Northeast. Come home, little hound.*

She crouched at the edge of their camp — three bedrolls now, hers, Ivo's, the cartographer's — and breathed through her teeth until the flare guttered down to an ache. The stars were still gone. The dark was total, the kind of dark that made a person feel like a held breath. In it, the brand's glow was invisible beneath her shirt, but she could feel it all the same: a thread of warmth running south behind her, back down the pilgrim road, back toward Vaelport and the kennels and the man who owned the other end of the leash.

Three years since she'd cut that thread once already. Three years, and it had grown back.

Behind her, Ivo stirred. "Sef?"

"Go back to sleep."

"You're bleeding."

"I'm not." She was — her palms, from clenching the knife. She wiped them on her trousers and sat down again, deliberately, where Ivo could see her doing something ordinary. "Bad dream. Sleep."

Ivo didn't sleep. Sef heard her breathing change, heard the small sounds of a girl lying awake and deciding whether to push. That was the trouble with Ivo Blackwood: she noticed things. Hedge-witch eyes, Maren-trained or not, missed nothing — the way Sef counted exits first, ate last, never slept with her back to the road. A week ago those habits had been survival craft. Now they were evidence.

Sef stared north, where the Starwell waited somewhere past the ash-line, and told herself what she always told herself.

*They'll leave you anyway. Better they leave you angry than dead.*

* * *

The kennels smelled of tallow and wet dog and the particular sourness of children who have stopped crying.

That was the first thing Sef remembered, always, when memory came for her: not the cold, not the hunger, but the smell. She'd been six when the sedan chair came to the court nursery — too young to understand why her mother turned her face away, too old to believe it was a game. Her father was a minor lord with major debts; the Crown's wardens paid well for court-blooded children, because a child raised among nobility knew passwords, knew faces, knew which doors mattered. The wardens called them *ferrets.* Small, quick, sent down holes.

The kennel-master was a grey woman named Brack with hands like tree roots, and the first lesson she taught was written on the skin. Each ferret got a brand at seven — a small sigil over the collarbone, crown-iron, no anesthetic worth the name. "It finds you," Brack said, while Sef screamed into a leather strap. "It calls you. And if you run far enough, it burns you. Some children die of it. Think of that as a kindness the Crown allows."

There were eleven of them in her year. By ten, there were four.

Sef survived because she was good at being small and quick. She memorized guard rotations the way other children memorized songs. She learned to open windows with a hairpin, to walk like a servant, to cry on command and mean none of it. At twelve she was sent into her first noble household as a scullery maid with ears, and she came back with the names of three conspirators, and Brack gave her an apple, and Sef ate it in the yard and hated herself with a purity that felt almost holy.

At fifteen, she was sent after a warden defector. She found him. She also found his daughter, who was eight, who shared her bread with a stranger because that's what country children did, and something in Sef went off like a trapdoor opening over a pit.

She didn't report the defector. She reported false intelligence instead — careful, plausible, bought her three weeks. Then she ran, the night before the truth came out, through the drains of Vaelport with the brand screaming against her ribs, and she kept running until the burn nearly killed her on the moors outside Hollowmere, and a hedge-witch named Maren found her half-dead in a turnip field and asked no questions at all.

Maren hadn't asked questions. Maren had fed her, splinted her, and then put a hoe in her hand and worked her like a hired mule, and Sef had stayed two winters because it was the only place she'd ever been where nobody wanted anything out of her.

Then Ivo had come. Or come back — the apprentice who'd fled, the girl Maren watched the road for when she thought no one saw. And Sef had seen the way Maren looked at her, all that buried love worn raw as an old coin, and something in Sef's chest had twisted and refused to untwist, and she'd known she couldn't stay. Not watching that. Not wanting it.

So she'd run again. And then the stars had gone out, and the wards had broken, and the world had ended, and somehow — by accident, by gravity, by some flaw in her own nature — she'd run straight back to both of them.

Now there were three of them on a dark road going north, and Sef carried a lit fuse under her shirt, and every mile closed the distance between her and everything she'd escaped.

* * *

The checkpoint was a shrine, once.

You could see it in the bones of the place: a stone arch straddling the pilgrim road, carved with suns and wheat-sheaves and the old blessing — *all who pass are passed in peace* — weathered soft as sea glass. Now there was a palisade built through the arch, sharpened stakes and warden-grey canvas, lanterns hung in a row like a butcher's display. Six wardens. Two on the barricade, two at a table checking papers, two walking the queue with the loose-hipped swagger of men bored enough to be dangerous.

Sef counted them twice, then a third time, from the gorse slope above the road while the others waited below. Counting was the first skill and the last one; counting was how ferrets stayed alive.

"Six," she said, sliding back down. "Two look green. The big one at the table is the handler — see the badge? Third rank, he'll want to do the talking. There's a dog."

"There's a *dog?*" Ivo whispered.

"Tracking hound. Chained by the fire, but chained isn't blind." Sef pulled her hood lower. "Pilgrims still travel, even now. We go through like everyone else. Heads down, coins ready, let me talk."

Jude shifted beside her, and she caught the look he gave her — that narrow, measuring look he probably thought was subtle. He'd been studying her for days, she knew. Cartographer's habit, reading terrain. She wondered what he thought he'd charted.

"They'll ask your business," Jude said quietly. "Where you're bound, why now, in this." He tapped the black sky above them without looking up. People didn't look up much anymore. Looking up cost hope.

"We're a family," Sef said. "Going north to my aunt's after our father died. Grief makes people stupid about roads. They won't like it, but they'll wave us through, because grief is boring and they want their supper."

"And if they don't?" Ivo asked.

Sef looked at her — at the witch-girl with the bruised, stubborn face and magic rolling off her skin like heat off a summer stone. Outside the valley, Ivo couldn't hide it. Flowers opened in her footprints. Warden wards sparked blue when she brushed them. Getting her through a checkpoint was like smuggling a lit lantern through a powder store.

"If they don't," Sef said, "you do exactly what I say, exactly when I say it, and you don't be a hero."

Ivo's jaw set in that way it did. Sef had learned to read the set of it like weather.

*I'll be a hero,* the jaw said. *Watch me.*

* * *

The queue moved like molasses. Sef stood between them — Jude on her left, tall and stooped and playing the weary clerk he'd once been so convincingly that she sometimes forgot it was play; Ivo on her right, hooded, gloved, hands jammed in her pockets like a sulking boy. Behind them, a tinker with a handcart, a widow with a goat, two brothers hauling bundles of salt fish. Ordinary sorrow, ordinary noise. Sef folded herself into it, became a tired girl with chapped lips and a dead father, and let her eyes do their work.

Handler at the table. Green one by the brazier, warming his hands. Dog asleep — no. Not asleep. Nose up. Testing the air.

Her blood went thin.

Dogs weren't the danger. Dogs smelled fear and rats and whatever the tinker had under his floorboards. But the brand — crown-iron soaked in covenant-work, forged in the same fires as the valley wards — the brand had a scent on the other side of its leash, and hounds bred in the kennels were trained to it. Trained to point at their own.

Twenty feet from the table. Fifteen.

"Names," said the handler, without looking up.

"Aldous," Sef said, pitching her voice dull. "These are my brother and sister. Our da died at harvest. We're going to our aunt's past Thistleford."

The handler looked up. He had the flat, unhurried face of a man who enjoyed queues. "Harvest was four months gone, girl."

"Folk don't stop grieving on a schedule."

Something flickered behind his eyes — irritation, maybe respect. He beckoned, and the green warden came around the table with a slate and began writing names. Sef gave them. False ones, smooth as river stones, and watched the dog.

The dog was standing now. Head level. Nostrils working. Staring directly at her chest.

Ten feet. The green warden handed the slate back. The handler reached for his stamp —

— and the brand woke.

It wasn't pain, at first. It was *attention,* like a finger laid along her spine. The sigil under her shirt flushed hot, hotter, white-hot, and Sef knew — with the sick certainty of a body remembering its training — that somewhere behind them, miles down the road or leagues away in Vaelport, an iron plate on a kennel wall had just begun to glow, tracing her position in real time for anyone who knew how to read it.

And the handler knew how to read it. She saw it happen: the stamp pausing mid-air, his gaze drifting from the slate to her, to the shape of her shoulders, to the way she stood — weight balanced, knife-hand free. Ferret posture. His nostrils flared slightly, like the dog's.

"Sef," Ivo breathed beside her, barely sound at all. "Your shirt."

A thread of light showed through the linen. Faint. Pulsing. Like a heartbeat under her collarbone that wasn't hers.

Time did the thing time does in the last moment before violence: it slowed and went clear. Sef took inventory the way Brack had taught her, the way that had kept her alive through nine households and one escape. Handler reaching for the whistle at his collar — two seconds. Green one nearest, hand on his baton, uncertain — one second. Brazier warden turning at the dog's low growl. Two walkers thirty feet down the queue, backs turned, busy shaking down the salt-fish brothers. Barricade pair beyond the palisade, out of the first exchange.

The math was bad. The math was very bad. Every instinct she had, every scarred-in rule, said the same thing: *run alone, run now, survive.*

Instead Sef heard herself say, low and fast: "When I move — get behind the table. Both of you. Stay down."

"Sef—" Ivo started.

"*Move.*"

She threw the widow's goat-water into the brazier.

The hiss and steam-bang swallowed the camp for three heartbeats, and in those heartbeats Sef was already inside the green warden's reach, heel-stamping his knee sideways, taking his baton as he folded. The handler got his whistle to his lips — she put the baton across his wrist and heard the small wet crunch, and then the table was going over between her and the brazier-pair and Jude was *not* staying down, of course he wasn't, the fool had grabbed the handler's stamp-arm and was hauling him back from the whistle, shouting something about *go, girls, go—*

The second walker came around the palisade fast, sword clearing its sheath, and Sef met him low, drove the baton into his thigh, felt him buckle — and behind her she heard Ivo scream a word she didn't know, and the world went gold. Light burst off the witch-girl like a struck bell; every ward-charm on the checkpoint flared blue and died; the dog howled and bolted its chain clean off its stake; the remaining wardens staggered, blinded, clawing at eyes full of starless radiance.

It should have been enough. It would have been enough.

But the handler, one arm ruined, did the thing Sef would remember for the rest of her life: he didn't reach for his sword. He reached for his belt pouch, and drew the reader-plate, and slammed it down on the overturned table, and shouted "*Point!*" at the brand on her chest like a man calling a hawk to fist.

The answering flare lifted her off the ground.

Later, she would piece it together from fragments: the light tearing out of her, white and total. The smell of her own skin burning. Ivo screaming her name. And Jude — Jude Ashgrave, disgraced liar, coward, traitor, whatever he was — stepping *into* the beam, between her and the plate, arms spread, as if a soft-bodied clerk could shield anyone from covenant-fire.

He couldn't. The flare took him across the shoulder and side, and threw him ten feet into the palisade stakes, and the crack of his body hitting wood was the loudest sound in the world.

After that, it was simple and awful and fast. Ivo, weeping with fury, pulled power out of the air like pulling a thread that unraveled the night itself — Sef felt it pass over her, felt the brand *shriek* and go dark, burnt out or broken or merely cowering, she didn't know which. The last standing warden chose that moment to decide he was elsewhere. Sef dragged Jude upright, got his arm over her shoulders — he weighed nothing, he was all bird-bones and shame — and ran.

They ran north, into the dark, three of them, leaving the burning checkpoint behind like a dropped torch.

* * *

They made four miles before Jude's legs quit.

She found a hollow under a fallen rowan, off the road, screened by bracken, and laid him down while Ivo hovered with her hands full of useless glowing light. Blood soaked his left side where a stake-point had opened him, and his shoulder was wrong, sat wrong, the whole arm hanging like something packed badly for travel.

"Don't," Jude said, when Ivo reached for the wound. His voice was papery. "Don't waste it on me. You'll need—"

"Shut *up,*" said Ivo, and pressed her palms to him, and Sef watched hedge-magic pour out of the girl like water finding a hole — golden, clumsy, enormous. Too much. Ivo swayed. Sef caught her by the shoulder and pushed her down onto her haunches.

"Less," Sef said. "Slow. You'll kill yourself and then he's still dead."

Ivo glared at her with wet furious eyes — but she listened. Slowed. The bleeding eased to a seep. Jude's breathing steadied from ragged to merely shallow, and his eyes found Sef's across the dying firelight of Ivo's hands.

"So," he whispered. "Kennels."

Sef went still.

"Court-child. Wardens' ferret." His mouth twitched, half a smile, mostly pain. "The way you count. The way you stand. The brand." He tipped his chin at her chest. "I mapped things for them too, girl. We've both got ink on our skin."

The old reflex rose in her like floodwater: deny, deflect, run. Give them nothing, keep the knife-hand free, survive alone the way she'd always survived. Her body was already cataloguing exits — the streambed south, the bracken, the dark.

But Ivo was looking at her too. Not with suspicion. With something worse. With recognition — the look of a girl who had also spent her whole life being someone's tool, someone's asset, someone's price. And her hand, still faintly shining, lay open on her knee. Not grabbing. Open.

"The plate," Sef said. Her voice came out rusty. "It burned out. Maybe. Or it's reporting right now that we're here, and by morning this hollow is full of wardens." She wrapped her arms around her knees, making herself small, making herself honest. "I should have told you. Back at the river. I should have—" The words snagged. She forced them through. "I've never told anyone. Telling people gets them killed. Or it gets me given back."

Silence, except wind in the rowan and Jude's careful breathing.

Then Ivo moved — crawled across the leaf-litter, awkward, and put her open hand on top of Sef's clenched fists, and left it there, warm and light as a bird's foot.

"You could have run," Ivo said. "In the queue. Alone, before any of it. Your math was bad and you stayed anyway."

"Your math is terrible," Jude agreed faintly, from the ground. "Both of you. I'd have walked away."

"You *didn't* walk away," Sef said. "You stepped in front of covenant-fire with your arms out like a scarecrow. That's the stupidest thing I've ever seen anyone do."

"Mm." His eyes were closing. "Runs in the company, apparently."

His breathing lengthened toward fever-sleep, the wounded kind, the dangerous kind. Ivo kept her hand pressed over the wound, feeding him strength in sips, her face grey with effort, and Sef sat in the dark with a burnt-out brand under her shirt and did the hardest arithmetic of her life.

Three years alone had kept her alive. Three nights with these two had gotten her branded, hunted, and — she tested the word in her head, carefully, the way you test ice — *known.*

Known, and still here. Still chosen. Ivo's hand hadn't moved.

Far off to the south, down the black ribbon of the road, Sef thought she saw a light that wasn't a star — there were no stars — a lantern-glow, steady and organized, moving. More than one.

"Hounds," she said softly, and touched Ivo's wrist. "We have to move him. Now. And Ivo — when we stop next, there's something I have to show you how to hide. Because if the brand isn't dead, then whoever's holding the leash knows exactly where we're going."

She looked north, into the dark where the Starwell waited.

"And I think," she said, "it's the same place we are."
