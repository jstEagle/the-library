## 8. The Warden's Mercy

Fever has a cartographer's precision, Jude thought. It mapped him inch by inch, territory by territory, and everywhere it went it redrew the borders.

He lay in the loft of a shepherd's abandoned bothy two days north of the checkpoint, and the fever drew him faithfully: the archive at Vaelport with its lamps of green glass; the Lord Warden's gloved hand unrolling his atlas of the deep wilds; the Starwell itself, which he had never seen but had drawn anyway, from surveyor's fragments and a dying man's ravings, drawing it so well that the Crown had needed no one else.

Someone was wringing a cloth into a basin. Water, then — real water, cold against his brow, though in the fever it tasted of ink.

"Ivo," he said, or thought he said. The name came out as something small and ruined.

"Hush." Her hand on his forehead was steady. "You're burning. Drink."

He drank. He had been drinking for what felt like days — willow-bark tea gone bitter, broth Sef poached from somewhere he didn't ask about, rainwater caught in the bothy's split roof and strained through linen. Between drinks there were dreams, and the dreams were worse than the thirst, because in them the Lord Warden kept opening the atlas to the page Jude had drawn and saying, *Show me again where you buried us all.*

* * *

The truth was that Jude Ashgrave had once believed himself a good man doing necessary work, and the fever would not let him forget the arithmetic.

It had been the Famine Years. He had been twenty-four, brilliant and hungry, the youngest mapmaker ever given a desk in the Crown's Survey, and the granaries of Vaelport had been emptying while the valley of Hollowmere sat green behind its wards, fat and fed and sealed away from a world going grey. Everyone knew why, the scholars said. The old covenant had closed the border between realms, and everything the land needed to thrive — the light that ripened wheat, the rain that remembered how to fall — came from the starlit court beyond. Cut off from it for three hundred years, the human realm was slowly starving. That was the theory. Jude had believed it the way he believed in north on a compass: completely, without examination.

So when the Crown asked him to find the Starwell — the anchor-point, the hinge, the place where the covenant could be reached and remade — he had understood it as mercy. Open the border. Let the light back in. End the famine.

He had worked four years on those maps. He had interviewed mad hermits and bribed monks and walked the pilgrim road with a surveyor's chain until his feet cracked. He had found it, or enough of it — a crater in the far north beneath a hill called the Sleeping King, marked in his own hand in an atlas bound in calfskin and presented to the throne with a bow.

What he had not known — what he had told himself afterward, for years, that he could not have known — was what the Crown intended to do at the hinge. Not open the door. *Drink from it.* The expedition went north under a warden named Corvane, young then, hungry in a different way, and it came back with the calfskin atlas burned and Jude promoted and the famine easing within the decade — easing because something vast was being bled, slowly, drop by drop, into the Crown's coffers and granaries and engines of state. He learned the truth the way one learns a debt: gradually, then all at once, in a cellar full of confiscated documents he was ordered to catalog and did not destroy.

They had taken a living star and chained it. They had been milking it ever since. And when the wards finally shattered — when the girl came out of Hollowmere trailing covenant-light like smoke off a brand — Jude had understood exactly whose signature was on the instrument that made it possible.

His own.

* * *

On the third day the fever broke, and Ivo was asleep sitting up, her back against the loft wall, her chin dropped to her chest. Sef was gone — out scavenging, or watching the road, or both. Morning came through the bothy's broken shutter in long dusty blades, and Jude lay still and watched it move across the floorboards and felt, for the first time since the checkpoint, like a man occupying his own body rather than renting it out to fire.

Ivo woke the way she seemed to do everything: suddenly, without transition, already alert. She looked at him. He looked back.

"You nearly died," she said.

"I've been informed."

"Sef says wardens patrol this road every six days. We can't move you before tomorrow." She came closer, kneeling by the pallet, and he saw the exhaustion banked in her face like coals. Two days of nursing him, on top of everything else. On top of Maren. On top of the whole unraveling world. "So we have time. And I have questions."

"I imagine you do."

"Start with the easy one." Her voice stayed level, but her hands, he noticed, were knotted together in her lap. "Why does the Lord Warden want me badly enough to bleed men at a checkpoint? What am I to him?"

Jude looked up at the rafters, at a spider's web hung with dust like a banner of grey silk, and understood that the moment had arrived whether he wanted it or not. He could lie. He had lied beautifully for fifteen years; it was practically his trade, a map being nothing but a beautiful structured lie about the shape of the world. But Ivo Blackwood had pulled him half-dead from a river of his own blood without asking his price first, and lying to her now would be like forging a coastline he'd personally walked.

"You're the keeper," he said. "The last living thread of the covenant. You know that part."

"I know that part."

"Then here is the part nobody tells runaways." He pushed himself up against the wall, weak as a convalescent, which was precisely what he was, and made himself meet her eyes. "Thirty years ago the Crown sent an expedition north to the Starwell. Not to restore the covenant. To drain it. There's a dead star there, Ivo — a caged one — and the kings have been bleeding its light into the realm ever since. That's why the famine ended. That's why Vaelport glows all night. Every lamp in the capital is lit off a prisoner."

Her face did not change, and that frightened him more than any flinching would have.

"And you knew," she said quietly.

"I mapped it." The words came out of him like teeth being drawn, each one separately, each one with its own small agony. "Not the well itself — I never stood there. But I found it. Four years of work. Surveys, interviews, a dying pilgrim's testimony. I put the Starwell on a map and put that map in the Crown's hands, and I told myself it was mercy. The realms were cut off, the land was starving, and reopening the covenant would let the light back in. That was the story. That was the story I *needed*, so I never checked it." His laugh came out wrong, a dry rattle. "A cartographer who doesn't verify his ground. My master would have struck me off the rolls."

"The wards fell because of your map."

"The wards fell because the Crown used my map, thirty years ago, to build the engine that's been draining the covenant ever since. The wards didn't break, Ivo. They were *eaten*. Slowly. From the inside. And when they finally gave out—" He gestured vaguely at the world, the starless sky, the sickened villages, all of it. "That was just the last of the marrow coming out of the bone."

Silence. The dust-motes turned in their blades of light. Somewhere outside, a crow complained about the morning.

Ivo looked down at her knotted hands. When she spoke again, her voice was very careful, the way a person walks on ice.

"Maren died holding a door against men following your map's road."

"No." He shook his head, and even that cost him. "No — following *Corvane's* orders. Following the Crown's design. My map is paper, Ivo. Paper doesn't kill. But I won't pretend the ink isn't mine." He swallowed. "There's more. You should have all of it, because you're going to hear it eventually from someone who means you harm, and better it comes from someone who—"

"Who what?" she said. Not cruel. Just precise.

"From someone who owes you a debt he can never pay."

She waited. Outside, the crow flew off, offended by something.

"After the checkpoint, the wardens didn't chase us hard," Jude said. "Did you wonder? A wounded man, a girl, a thief, moving slow through open country — and the patrols thin out like they're being *pulled back*. I wondered too. Then last night, in the fever, I stopped wondering and started remembering. Corvane always did like to soften a target before he offered terms." He met her eyes. "He's close, Ivo. He's not chasing us because he doesn't need to chase. He's herding."

* * *

The Lord Warden came at dusk, and he came alone, which was somehow worse.

They heard the horse long before they saw it — a single set of hooves on the pilgrim road, unhurried, deliberate, the gait of a man who had never once needed to hurry. Sef materialized out of the bracken beside the bothy door with a knife already in her hand, and Ivo stepped into the doorway with her hedge-magic rising off her skin like heat-shimmer, and Jude dragged himself upright against the wall with the loft ladder biting into his palms and watched the rider dismount in the last grey light.

Corvane had not aged the way other men aged. The years had refined him rather than worn him, honed him down to essentials: tall, spare, silver at the temples like frost on dark iron, in a plain black coat with no insignia at all, because the Lord Warden's authority required no announcement. His eyes found the bothy door and then, immediately, unerringly, found Jude at the window, and he smiled the smile of a man greeting a colleague across a conference table.

"Ashgrave," he called, pleasantly. "You look terrible."

"You always did have an eye for detail, my lord."

"May I approach? I'm unarmed." He spread his arms, turning slowly, the theatrical gesture of a man demonstrating honesty who had never once required weapons to be dangerous. "And I mean the girl no harm today. Today I've come to make an offer, and offers are best made standing in the open, where everyone can see there's no ambush in the heather." A pause. "There is no ambush in the heather, Sef. I checked your people's patterns before I rode in. You're getting better. Orla would be proud — ah, but you don't know that name yet. Another day."

Sef's knife-hand trembled with pure fury, and she did not lower it.

Ivo stood in the doorway, and Jude watched her weigh him — watched her do the thing Maren must have taught her after all, despite their estrangement, despite everything: the hedge-witch's assessment, reading a stranger the way one reads weather. Whatever she saw, she lifted her chin slightly, and Corvane took it for the permission it was.

He stopped ten paces from the door, close enough to speak softly, far enough to be respectful of a keeper's power. Everything about him was calibrated. Jude had forgotten how exhausting it was to be in a room — or a road, or a world — with him.

"Ivo Blackwood," Corvane said. "Apprentice of Maren Ostrey, deceased. Last of the keepers' line. I'll be brief, because I know you're deciding whether to kill me, and I'd rather conclude my business first." He folded his hands. "Your valley's wards fell because the covenant that sustained them was consumed — not by accident, not by age, but deliberately, over decades, by instruments built from a certain cartographer's work. You may have gathered this. Jude is honest when the fever's done with him; it's one of his few virtues, and it took me years to appreciate."

"Don't," Jude said.

"You burned the letter," Corvane went on, as if he hadn't spoken. "My little bird in the archive reported the ash in your grate before you'd even left Vaelport. I confess I was disappointed — and then intrigued. A traitor to the Crown who becomes a traitor to the traitors. There may be hope for you yet, Ashgrave." He turned back to Ivo, and his voice changed — softened, warmed, became the voice of reason itself. "Here is my offer. Not to you, keeper. To him. Full restoration. Name cleared, debts forgiven, rank and pension and the archive itself placed in his keeping. All I require is the journey's end. Walk with him to the Starwell. Let him deliver you to the solstice binding. No harm will come to you — the covenant must be reforged with a keeper willing or it won't hold, and I have no interest in broken tools. You'll live, honored, in a realm where the lights stay lit and the winter ends. Or refuse—" and here the warmth left his voice entirely, like a lamp being shuttered, "—and I take you both at solstice anyway, along with the thief and anyone else foolish enough to stand near you, and I rebuild the covenant on whatever's left."

The silence after was enormous. A wind moved through the heather with a sound like the sea.

"Why?" Ivo said. "Why does it matter to you so much? The Crown's power, the binding — why does *Corvane* need it?"

For the first time, something flickered behind the Lord Warden's eyes — not guilt, Jude noted. Amusement. "Because thrones are temporary, keeper, and I am patient. But that conversation is for solstice." He mounted his horse in one fluid motion, gathering the reins. "Ashgrave. The offer stands until the moon rises tomorrow night. After that, I stop offering. You remember what I'm like when I stop offering."

"I remember everything, my lord," Jude said. "It's the curse of the profession."

Corvane smiled — a real smile, briefly, almost fond — and turned his horse, and rode north into the failing light, and did not look back, because he had never once in his life needed to see a thing twice.

* * *

They sat around the bothy's small fire after full dark, the three of them, and nobody spoke for a long time. Sef had finally put the knife away, but she sat closest to the door. Ivo fed the fire stick by stick, very carefully, as if each one were a decision.

"He'll come regardless," Sef said at last. "The offer's theater. He wants you to think there's a choice."

"Yes," Jude said. "But the theater matters. Because if I say yes aloud, even falsely, he'll have witnesses, and a witnessed bargain gives him leverage over me at the well. And if I refuse aloud—" he looked at the fire, "—then there's no pretense left to hide behind, for either of us. Refusal is just a slower version of the same death, but it's *our* death, chosen, instead of his script performed."

"So refuse," Sef said simply.

"It means both our deaths."

"It means both our deaths," Ivo said quietly, "instead of everyone else's. That's the arithmetic, isn't it? It's the only arithmetic you've ever gotten right." She looked up at him across the fire, and her eyes were steady and bright and terribly young. "Say it anyway."

Jude Ashgrave had spent thirty years learning every way a man can sell the world and call it necessity. He knew the price of loyalty to the ounce; he had weighed it professionally. But sitting in a ruin with a runaway witch and a feral child, with the Lord Warden's deadline ticking toward a moonrise, he discovered there was one transaction he'd never priced: the cost, exact and final, of meaning it.

He stood, swaying, gripping the mantel, and raised his voice so it carried past the broken shutter, past the heather, north along the dark road to wherever Corvane's ears might be.

"My lord Warden!" he called into the night. "Tell your masters the mapmaker refuses. Tell them Jude Ashgrave keeps his own accounts now!"

The wind took the words and scattered them. Nothing answered — no horn, no hoofbeat, no shadow moving in the dark. But Jude felt it settle over the bothy like frost: the knowledge that somewhere out there, a patient man had begun, at last, to stop being patient.

Ivo rose and stood beside him, shoulder to shoulder, and Sef came to stand at his other side, knife loose in her hand, facing north.

"Well," Sef said. "Now we run."
