## 10. What Power Wants

Orla had learned long ago that a warden's uniform never truly came off. Even stripped of the grey coat, even with the badge of rank buried in a Brackwater gutter and her hair cropped close as any smuggler's, she still stood like one — shoulders squared to doorways, eyes counting exits, hands resting near where a sword would hang if she still carried one. The body remembered its oaths. That was the cruelty of it. She had broken hers in every way that mattered, and still her spine held their shape.

She stood at the salt-crusted window of Tam's foundry loft now, watching the free-city breathe below. Brackwater at dusk was all bells and brine: gull-cry over the tidal flats, hammers ringing somewhere in the lower yards, lamplighters moving along the canal walks with their poles spitting small orange stars. A good city for hiding in. Too many noises, too many smells, too many people who asked no questions because they could not afford the answers.

Behind her, the loft smelled of hot bronze and tallow. Tam worked at his bench with his back to the room, sleeves rolled, forearms dusted white with casting sand, and beside him — this was the part Orla still could not look at without something tightening in her chest — sat Ivo Blackwood, holding a wax mold steady while the bell-founder tapped it true.

They spoke without speaking. Ivo would tilt the mold a fraction; Tam would nod or shake his head; once he laughed — silent, shoulder-shaking, eyes bright — at some mistake of hers, and she laughed back, unashamed, and Orla turned back to the window before either of them caught her watching.

*You are going to get them killed,* she thought. *Or worse. You are going to let them trust you, and then you are going to tell them.*

Because there were things she had not said. Things she'd kept behind her teeth since the night she'd walked out of the warden barracks at Saltcote with nothing but her sword-belt and eleven years of service burning behind her like a fired field. She had told them about the checkpoints, the codes, the way the Lord Warden moved his hunters through the free-cities like fish through water. She had not told them what she'd heard, three nights before her defection, standing outside a door no sergeant was meant to stand outside, listening to a voice she knew better than her father's.

*He doesn't want to serve the Crown,* she had wanted to say, a hundred times since. *The Crown is just the next thing he means to wear.*

* * *

It had been in the deep vaults beneath the Vaelport citadel, where the wardens kept the things too dangerous for evidence rooms. Orla had been summoned to escort an armory detail — ordinary work, crates and manifests, iron seals — and had arrived early, as she always did, because early soldiers saw things late arrivals never did.

She had seen him. Lord Warden Corvane, alone among the sealed shelves, holding a fragment of something that hurt to look at. Not bright — that was the wrong word entirely. It was dark the way a starless sky is dark, dark with appetite, and it drank the lantern light off the walls so that the whole vault seemed to lean toward his hands.

He was talking to it. Low, unhurried, almost tender. Orla had stood in the shadow of the stair and listened.

"They called it a covenant," Corvane said. "As though it were an agreement. As though power could be *shared*." He turned the fragment slowly, and where it passed, the shadows bent toward it like grass in wind. "The old kings understood. They took the first bite, and they built a kingdom on the taste. But they were cowards in the end. They hoarded and hid and called it duty, and when the well ran shallow they sealed the valley and told themselves it was protection."

He smiled then, and Orla had seen men smile before executions, and it was nothing like that. This was the smile of a man looking at a chair he intended to sit in.

"A crown is only metal," he said. "A throne only wood. What I will hold will be older than both. Let the boy-king keep his ribboned chair. When the covenant reforges at solstice, it will not bind to Vaelport. It will bind to me."

And the fragment — Orla swore this, later, to herself, in the dark, when she tried to convince herself she had imagined it — the fragment *purred*.

She had walked out of that vault and finished her escort duty and signed the manifests in a hand steadier than her own heartbeat, and three days later she had deserted, carrying the knowledge like a swallowed coal. And ever since, in Tam's loft, in safehouses, on the pilgrim road, she had watched Ivo Blackwood — nineteen years old, hedge-taught, brave in the specific way of someone who has never yet lost anything irreplaceable — and thought: *she doesn't know what's coming for her. Not really. None of them do. And every day I don't tell them, I am choosing his secrets over theirs.*

Tonight she would tell them. Tonight, because tonight the bell-key was finished.

Tam lifted it from the bench at last: a small thing, palm-sized, cast in bronze gone green-gold at the edges, shaped like a bell but mute — no clapper, no mouth, a bell that had decided against ringing. Its surface was worked all over with script so fine it looked like frost. Covenant-script, copied from rubbings Maren's letters had described, corrected twice by the dying song Ivo sometimes hummed without knowing she was humming it.

"It will open the under-chapel gate at the Starwell," Jude had explained, weeks ago, in the flat voice he used for facts he hated. "The Crown's iron chains have a lock. The lock answers to bells — the old processional bells, the ones they melted down for cannon. So we make one that remembers being a bell."

Ivo reached out and touched it, and Orla saw the bronze warm under her fingertips, saw the frost-script flicker faintly gold, like embers remembering fire.

"It's singing," Ivo whispered. "Quietly. Like it's shy."

Tam signed something quick and fluid. Sef, sprawled on the floor sharpening a knife against a whetstone, translated around the stem of a stolen plum: "He says shy bells ring loudest when it matters. He says also that you're holding it wrong and you'll drop it."

"I'm not—" Ivo adjusted her grip. Tam nodded, satisfied.

Jude sat apart, as he often did, at the table with his maps, but he wasn't working. He was watching the key with the expression of a man watching a door he'd hoped never to see again. Orla catalogued that look and filed it away, the old habit, the warden's habit, and hated herself a little for it.

"Well," Sef said, spitting the plum pit into her palm and pocketing it for reasons known only to Sef. "We've got the key, we've got the route, we've got—"

The bell above the canal landing rang.

Not Tam's mute bronze. The harbor bell — the alarm, three strokes, pause, three strokes. And beneath it, fainter, the sound Orla had spent her whole adult life learning to hear the way other people heard their own names: boots. Many boots. Moving in formation along the quay below, muffled by fog, wrapped in wool so they made no ring on the cobbles.

Warden boots.

"Off the light," she breathed, and was already moving. "*Now.*"

* * *

They killed the lamps and the loft went dark except for the banked forge-glow, which suddenly looked enormous. Orla pressed her eye to the gap between shutter-boards and counted silhouettes on the canal walk below. Twelve. No — fifteen, two peeling off toward the stairhead of the building opposite, one lingering at the mooring posts where a boat should have been tied.

Their boat. Their escape, already found.

"They didn't follow us here," she said quietly. "They knew. Someone gave them the address."

"That's not possible," Jude said. "No one outside this room knows—"

"The letter," Orla said, and watched his face go grey in the forge-light. "The coded letters you wrote on the road. You burned the last one. Did you burn *all* of them?"

"I—" His jaw worked. "There was one sent. Early. Before Brackwater. Before I—" He stopped. He did not finish saying *before I chose you*. He didn't have to. Everyone in the dark loft heard it anyway.

Below, fists hit the foundry's street door. Once, twice — polite, almost. Then the ram, less polite.

"Sewer grate," Tam signed sharply, already kicking open the floor hatch, pointing down the ladder into the dripping dark. "Tide tunnel. It lets out past the salt-works."

Sef grabbed the key from Ivo's hand and shoved it deep into her boot. "Move, move, map-man first, you're slowest and I hate carrying people."

But the street door went in before the second rung of the ladder, and the loft filled with grey coats and drawn steel, and the world became very simple and very fast.

Orla fought the way she'd been taught, which was to say without artistry and without mercy. A warden's halberd came over the hatch rim and she caught the haft, turned it, put its owner into the wall. Someone shouted "Sergeant Orla! Stand down, sergeant, you're under arrest for—" and she broke his nose with her elbow and did not hear the rest. Through the smoke and the swinging lanterns she saw Sef go up the roof-beams like a cat, Ivo below her with bronze light crawling up both arms — the girl's power flaring wild, sparks jumping off the wardens' mail, making them stagger — and Jude shoving the maps into the forge itself, feeding twenty years of his life's work into the coals rather than let them be taken.

Then a hand closed on Tam.

He had gone back for the mold-rubbings — the originals, the covenant-script, irreplaceable — and a warden lieutenant, young, precise, terrible, stepped out of the smoke and took his collar and his wrist together, and put a knife to the soft place under his jaw, and said, in the bored tone of a man reciting procedure: "The deaf founder comes with us. The rest of you can discuss it with the Lord Warden at your leisure."

"No—" Ivo's voice cracked across the loft like the bell she couldn't ring. Bronze light leapt up her arms, and Orla saw the lieutenant's mail begin to glow, saw the man flinch—

And Tam shook his head. Slowly. Deliberately. Looking straight at Ivo, he signed with his free hand — the hand the knife wasn't watching — a single sentence, clean and calm:

*If you burn him, they take all of us. Go. Ring my bell.*

Then the smoke rolled, and the hatch was under Orla's hands, and she was hauling Ivo down into the dark by the collar while the girl screamed a name into the wet stone of the tide tunnel, and the last thing Orla saw of the loft was Tam's face over the lieutenant's shoulder — not afraid. *Certain.* Looking at them the way a bell-founder looks at finished work.

*Go,* he'd said. *Ring my bell.*

* * *

The salt-works outlet stank of sulfur and dead tide. They came up out of it into reeds and grey drizzle, four of them where five should be, and no one spoke for a long time.

Sef paced. That was worse than crying; Orla had seen enough soldiers come back from their first engagement to know pacing when she saw it, the body trying to fight a battle that was already over. Jude sat on a fallen piling with his burned, empty hands hanging between his knees. Ivo knelt in the mud with the tide lapping her shins, and the bronze glow still crawled faintly over her skin, guttering, directionless, grieving.

"He went back for the rubbings," Ivo said finally. Her voice came out sanded flat. "For the *script*. Because we need it. Because the key needs it, because—" She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "Because everything costs something. That's the lesson, isn't it? Outside the valley, everything costs—"

"Stop." Orla crossed the mud and took the girl's wrists and pulled her hands down, and made her look. "Listen to me, keeper. I have carried something since before I met you, and I let you walk into tonight half-blind, and that is on me, and I will answer for it. But you will not drown in the shallows when the deep water's ahead. Do you hear me?"

Ivo looked at her. Waited.

"Corvane isn't serving the Crown." The words came out of Orla like teeth. "I heard him say it myself, in the vaults under Vaelport, three days before I deserted. He holds fragments of the old binding — stolen pieces, drunk-down pieces, the dregs the old kings left in the cup. And he means to reforge the covenant at solstice not for the boy-king, not for Vaelport, not for realm or throne. He means to bind it to himself. All of it. Every thread, every keeper's line, the star-court entire." She heard her own voice go quiet, which was how she knew it mattered. "He doesn't want to restore the Crown's claim, Ivo. He wants to end crowns. He wants to be the last authority in either world. A god-warden. And everything we've done since Brackwater — the key, the route, the names — everything he learns from Tam's mouth, he learns toward that end."

Silence, except the tide and the rain.

Jude laughed — one dry, awful syllable. "I mapped the Starwell for a man who wants to be God," he said. "Of course. Of course that's what it was."

"You knew?" Sef spun on her. "You *knew*, and you let us build all this—"

"I knew a piece," Orla said. "I didn't know he'd moved this fast. I didn't know Brackwater was blown until the boots hit the door." She looked at each of them, and made herself keep looking. "Which leaves us one conclusion, and none of us are going to like it. He took Tam. Tam knows the route, the contacts, the salt-works, and — if they've searched his boots, his forge, his sleeves — he may know the key exists. But he does not know what it opens, or how. And Corvane is a man who takes his time with knowledge. He'll want Tam alive and talking for days."

"So we go get him," Sef said instantly, knife already half-drawn.

"We go north," Orla said. "All of us. Tonight. Solstice is nine days out and the Starwell doesn't wait for grief." She saw it land on Ivo like a blow, and made herself say the rest, gently, because gentleness was also a discipline. "That's what he's counting on, do you understand? That love makes us stupid. That we'll turn south after one man instead of north toward the well. Corvane doesn't fear our swords. He fears our arithmetic."

"And if the arithmetic is wrong?" Ivo asked. Her eyes were red-rimmed and furious and absolutely clear. "If we reach the well and the gate won't open because we left the man who cast the key bleeding in a warden cell?"

Orla had no answer prepared. She gave her the truth instead, the whole ugly weight of it, because these people — this strange, impossible little family of a runaway, a thief, a traitor, and herself — had earned nothing less.

"Then we find another way to open it," she said. "Because that's what he will never understand, and it's the only weapon we have. He thinks power is a thing you hold. We know it's a thing you share. Now pick up your feet, keeper. The tide's turning, and so is everything else."

They went north out of the salt-works into the rain, four shadows on the drowned road, and behind them Brackwater's harbor bell tolled the alarm into the fog — three strokes, pause, three strokes — like a heart beating for the man who could not hear it anymore.

And in Ivo's pack, wrapped in oilcloth, the forged bell-key lay warm as a living thing, waiting for the gate at the end of the world.
