## 11. The Drowned Chapel

The voice had been calling her for three nights.

It did not use words, exactly. It used *places* — the smell of river-stone after rain, the green hush of a churchyard Ivo had never walked in and yet remembered, the weight of water held behind an old wall. Each night the dream grew closer, until on the third night she woke with wet hair and the taste of silt in her mouth, and Sef sitting bolt upright beside the fire, knife drawn, staring at her.

"You were humming," Sef said. "In your sleep. A song I've never heard."

"I don't know any songs."

"I know." Sef's eyes were wide and unblinking in the firelight. "That's what frightened me."

They found the chapel at dawn, or what passed for dawn now that the days were shortening into something wrong — a gray smear along the eastern hills that never quite committed to being morning. It stood at the bend of a drowned river valley, half-sunk, its bell tower leaning like a drunk against its own shadow. The floodwaters had taken the lower floor centuries ago; only the roofline and the arched windows of the upper nave rose above the brown-green water, their glass long gone, their stone frames furred with moss.

Jude spread his map on a flat rock and weighted the corners with pebbles. His hands were steady. His face was not.

"There," he said, tapping a mark so faint it might have been an ink stain, or a scar in the paper itself. "A chapel. No name given. Just a symbol." He looked up at Ivo, and there was something in his expression she had learned to dread: the cartographer's honesty, which was worse than lying. "The symbol for *memory*. The old kings marked places where something was kept alive on purpose. This is one of them."

"The voice is coming from under the water," Ivo said.

"Of course it is." Orla crouched at the water's edge, testing it with two fingers, then wiping them dry on her coat with the brisk disgust of a soldier who trusted nothing that could not be shot. "Of course the thing singing you across three counties is at the bottom of a flooded hole. Why would it be anywhere sensible?"

"It isn't singing to me," Ivo said. "It's singing to—" She stopped. She didn't have the word yet.

Sef did. "To whatever's left," she said quietly. "Like a lamp lit in a window when everyone's already dead inside."

No one argued with that. Sef had a gift for saying the unbearable things plainly, as if plainness could make them lighter.

* * *

The water was colder than winter had any right to make it. Ivo stood waist-deep on the chapel steps, which descended beneath the surface in worn stone curves, and felt her own magic rise up through her chest like a bird battering at a window. Outside the valley, her hedge-craft roared instead of whispered — flowers split the stones where she lingered, wards sparked blue off her knuckles if she brushed a boundary line — but here, over this drowned place, her power behaved strangely. It leaned toward the chapel. It *pulled*, the way a compass needle pulls, the way a homesick child pulls toward a lit door.

"It wants me to go down," she said.

"Then it can want," Orla said flatly. "You're not diving into black water in a ruined building on the strength of a feeling."

"She is," Sef said. "Look at her. She's already gone. She's been gone since last night." Sef waded out to stand beside her, thigh-deep, teeth chattering, arms crossed tight against the cold. "I'm not going to tell you not to do it. I just want you to know that if you drown, I'm going to be extremely annoyed with you for the rest of my life, which will be very long, because I intend to be difficult forever out of spite."

Ivo laughed — a small, cracked sound — and was surprised by how much it helped.

Jude came down the steps last. He carried no weapon. He carried the map, folded into a waterproof oilskin sleeve, and he pressed it into her hands.

"If there's something down there worth remembering," he said, "then remember it properly. That's all a map is. Someone else's survival, written down before they died." His jaw worked. "I mapped this country once for people who wanted to own it. Do this one for the rest of us."

She took the oilskin. It was warm from his hands.

"How deep?" she asked.

"Old chapels flood from the bottom up," Jude said. "The nave floor might be four fathoms down. The crypt deeper. There'll be air pockets — vaulted ceilings trap them." He paused. "Probably."

"*Probably,*" Orla repeated, to the sky, to no one.

"Take the rope," Tam signed from the bank — Tam had come with them as far as Brackwater's outskirts and refused to leave, his broad hands flashing in the gray light. *Tie it at your waist. Three tugs means come. Two means hurry.* He had cast her a bronze charm that morning, a small bell shape that sat cold against her sternum and would, he promised, ring underwater if she stopped breathing — a bell for her death, rung by her own body's silence. She tried not to think about how he'd had time to make such a thing. She tried not to think about why he'd thought of it at all.

She tied the rope. She filled her lungs.

And the voice — patient, ancient, tired beyond the reach of any living throat — finally spoke a word she could understand:

***Come and see.***

She dove.

* * *

The world below the water was green-black and slow. Light came down in slanted columns, thick with drifting sediment, like curtains drawn halfway across a room. The chapel's interior opened around her — pews furred white with water-mold, their wood soft as bread; a stone font still standing upright, holding a hundred years of dark water in its basin like a cup no one had cleared away. Fish scattered from her passage, quick silver question marks. Her bubbles rose past her face in trembling strings.

The rope paid out behind her. Three fathoms. Four. Her ears ached and equalized and ached again. The bronze charm lay cold against her breastbone, silent, and she made herself believe that meant *alive* rather than *not yet.*

At the foot of the nave, the floor gave way to stairs descending — carved with processions of figures, hand in hand, dozens of them, hundreds, spiraling down and down. She followed the banister of carved hands into the dark.

And there, at the deepest point, where the crypt opened into a round chamber that predated the chapel above it — the chapel had been built *over* this, she realized, a shell raised around a seed — there was light.

Not torchlight. Not reflected daylight. A soft, sourceless glow, the color of the sky just before stars appear, filling the chamber like water fills a cup. At its center stood a stone table, and on the stone table lay a skeleton.

Small. A woman, by the narrow hips and slight frame, though age was impossible to say. Her bones were arranged not as a body falls but as a body *lies down* — deliberately, with care, arms crossed over the hollow of her chest. And clasped in those bone fingers was a circle of some dark material, iron-dark but not iron, that drank the chamber's light and gave it back changed.

The memory-trap. Jude's symbol. *A place where something was kept alive on purpose.*

Ivo hung in the water above the table, lungs beginning to burn, and understood with terrible clarity that this was the moment the dream had been walking her toward for three nights. Whatever happened next would happen inside her, not outside. The trap — the *gift* — was not a mechanism. It was a choice.

She reached out and touched the circle in the skeleton's hands.

* * *

The chamber vanished. So did the water. So did her body, mostly — she became a point of attention adrift in someone else's remembering, and the remembering began.

She stood on a hillside under a sky so full of stars it hurt. Not the starless void she'd fled under — a sky *crowded*, blazing, every point of it alive, and between the stars moved shapes too vast and bright to look at directly: the star-court in its full glory, descending not as invaders but as guests, trailing light like banners.

And on the hillside stood people. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Farmers and smiths and weavers and children, kings among them but kings *unremarkable*, crownless or nearly so, standing in the crowd like anyone. Ivo knew — the way you know things in dreams — that she was seeing the first binding. The night the covenant was made.

A woman stepped forward from the crowd. Small, dark-haired, ordinary-faced. And Ivo's heart lurched, because she knew that face. She had seen it every day of her childhood, scolding and measuring and refusing to praise — Maren's face, or near enough to be her grandmother, her founder, her blood.

*The first keeper,* Ivo thought. *Maren's blood. My blood.*

The star-court's envoy — moth-winged, radiant, a shape Ivo recognized with a jolt as kin to the figure Vess she had been told waited at the Starwell — extended a single point of light and offered it to the woman. Not to the king. To the woman in the crowd.

And here the memory slowed, the way a teacher slows at the crucial lesson, and Ivo saw what she was meant to see:

The woman took the light — and passed it on.

She pressed it into the hands of the farmer beside her, who pressed it to the smith, who pressed it to the weaver, the miller, the child, the king, the beggar, around and around, a chain of hands each taking the light and giving it away, so that it belonged to no one because it belonged to *everyone*, and the covenant caught hold like flame catching along a fuse — no, like a song catching along a congregation, every voice carrying a part no single throat could hold alone.

The envoy spoke, and though the words were not words, Ivo heard their meaning settle into her like rain into dry ground:

*A burden held by one will break the one.*
*A burden held by many becomes a bond.*
*This is the whole of the law. There is no other.*

And then the memory turned, as memories do, toward its wound. Ivo watched the generations pass in a rush — the keeping of the light, hand to hand, season to season — and then she watched the kings begin to gather. Slowly. Respectfully at first. One king declared himself the covenant's *protector*, and the hands that passed the light began passing it through him. Another built the Starwell's first cage, claiming it was to keep the light *safe*. Another — and here the memory went cold and hard as crown-iron — simply took. Took and hoarded and drained, feeding the light into the foundations of his throne, and the star-court's brilliance dimmed year by year like a hearth left untended, and the last keepers scattered into hedge-witches and cunning-folk, hiding sparks of the old covenant in charms and blessings, waiting —

— and the memory ended where it must end: with the small dark-haired woman, the first keeper, lying down on a stone table in a crypt that would one day flood, folding her arms over a fragment of the covenant's light, and setting a trap that was not a trap at all but a *letter*, addressed across centuries, to whoever came looking with her blood and her questions.

The letter read, in meaning if not in words:

*We were never meant to carry it alone. Whoever you are: find the others. Find the hands. The light was always a circle, never a crown.*

* * *

Ivo surfaced gasping, hauled up by three sets of hands — Sef's grip on the rope, Orla's fist in her collar, Tam's arms hauling her over the stones — and lay on the chapel's flooded threshold coughing brown water and weeping without quite knowing why.

"She's breathing," Sef was saying, somewhere above her, high and fast. "She's breathing, she's fine, she's — Ivo, you absolute disaster, you were under there forever, the bell charm rang, Tam heard it—"

"I wasn't drowning," Ivo managed.

"No," Orla said grimly, wringing out Ivo's braid with two efficient twists. "Your heart stopped for four seconds. Tam's bell told us. That's what the bell was *for.*"

But Ivo was shaking her head, pushing herself up on her elbows, and the world had changed while she was under. She could feel it before she could name it. The gray dawn air was no longer empty. It was — *inhabited*. Faint, threadbare, dying, but present: a music underneath everything, vast and slow and sorrowful, a chord sustained so long it had become almost indistinguishable from silence.

Almost.

"The song," she whispered. "I can hear it."

"Hear what?" Sef crouched beside her, searching her face. "Ivo. Hear what?"

"The star-court." Ivo sat up. The chord swelled gently in her awareness, aching, beautiful, frayed to its last strands. "It's been singing the whole time — since the wards fell, maybe since before — a song to keep themselves anchored to this side, to keep from fading out entirely. And they're losing. They're so far away and so dim, and the Crown's been drinking their light for centuries, and they're *losing*, and nobody could hear them because nobody was listening the right way." She pressed her palm flat against her own sternum, where the bronze bell hung cold. "Now I am. Now I can't stop."

"What does it sound like?" Sef asked softly.

Ivo considered lying. She considered kindness. She had learned, on this road, that Sef wanted truth the way other people wanted blankets.

"Like a choir heard through a wall," she said. "Like the last verse of a hymn sung by voices that are falling asleep. Like — " her voice cracked — "like Maren, the last week. Still working. Still singing to herself over the kettle. Knowing."

Silence, except the lap of floodwater and the distant drip of the ruined eaves.

Jude had gone very pale. He stood at the edge of the group with the oilskin map still clutched in one hand, and Ivo watched him do the arithmetic she had already done — watched him arrive at the sum. If the star-court was audible to her now, then the solstice deadline wasn't a political convenience. It was a deathwatch. When the song ended, the court ended, and everything the covenant had ever joined — every ward, every blessing, every gentle thread between the two realms — would rot with it.

"We go north," Jude said quietly. "Tonight. Now, if we can."

Orla was already checking her blades. Tam was signing fast to Sef, who translated: *He says the bell-charms. He can cast more. If the covenant is a circle of hands — if that's really what you saw — then maybe more people can learn to listen. Maybe that's what keepers are.*

Maybe that's what keepers are. Ivo looked at her companions — the thief, the soldier, the founder, the traitor-turned-ally — and felt the first keeper's letter settle into her bones where it would stay for the rest of her life.

*Find the others. The light was always a circle, never a crown.*

She got to her feet on legs that shook, and turned her face north, toward the Starwell, toward the terrible clarity waiting for her there.

And beneath everything — beneath the wind and the water and the worry — the dying song sang on, and now that she had heard it, she understood the cruelest thing about it:

It was singing *her name.*
