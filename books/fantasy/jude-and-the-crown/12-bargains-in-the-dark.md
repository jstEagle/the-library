## 12. Bargains in the Dark

The road into Vaelport smelled of salt and old sin, the way it always had, and Jude Ashgrave walked it like a man walking back into a dream he'd spent three years trying to wake from.

He had forgotten how the city sounded at dusk. The bell-towers rang the hour in overlapping dissonance — nine parishes, nine bells, none of them agreeing on the time — and beneath that lay the groan of ships in the harbor, the cry of gull-flocks gone strange and silent since the stars went out. The lamplighters were lighting lamps that did nothing now. In a city where the sky had gone black as a shut eye, a candle was just a small surrender.

His chains were gone. That was the strangest part. Three years in a cell no wider than his outstretched arms, and now he strolled the Processional Way in a coat the Crown had returned to him, brushed clean, mended at the elbows, smelling faintly of the cedar chest they'd kept it in. They wanted him to look restored. They wanted him to look like theirs.

*Good,* he thought, turning his collar up against the harbor wind. *Let them look.*

In his breast pocket, folded twice, lay the letter that had cost him everything he hadn't yet lost: the route Ivo Blackwood would take north to the Starwell, the waystations, the fords, the days and hours. Every word of it true. That was the whole architecture of the lie — you built a betrayal out of truth, load-bearing truths, and then you hid one false wall inside it and prayed no one tapped the plaster.

The false wall was this: the route he'd given them was real, but it was three days stale. Orla had already moved the company to the salt-roads east, and by the time the Lord Warden's riders rode west to intercept a girl who wasn't there, Tam would be out of the Warden's cells or everyone involved would be dead.

Everyone involved included Jude. He was trying not to think about that part.

* * *

They received him in the Warden's Tower, in the map room — of course in the map room, because the Lord Warden understood theater the way other men understood breathing.

The room had changed since Jude had last stood in it, six years and a lifetime ago, unrolling his survey charts of the northern wastes while ministers leaned over his shoulder and murmured. Then it had been cluttered with instruments, brass and paper and the honest dust of work. Now the great table at its center held a single object under glass: a fragment of the old border wards, recovered from Hollowmere's shattered mouth, still faintly luminous even after all these months, glowing the color of a bruise.

The Lord Warden stood with his hands clasped behind him, studying it. He was not a large man. People expected him to be — they expected the architect of the realm's salvation to fill a doorway — but Corvus Hale was narrow and neat as a compass needle, gray at the temples, with the patient eyes of a man who had learned long ago that watching was cheaper than doing.

"Ashgrave." He didn't turn. "Do you know what this is?"

"The western anchor-shard," Jude said. "From the valley wards. Third of five."

"Second of five." The Warden's voice was mild. "We found another last week, buried in a peat bank forty miles south of where your maps said it should be. Which tells me something interesting about your maps, doesn't it?" Now he turned, and smiled the smile that had made three chancellors retire early. "It tells me your maps are honest about everything except the things that matter. A rare quality. Most men are the opposite."

Jude bowed. His knees felt like borrowed hinges. "I've brought what Your Grace asked for. The route. The girl's habits. Her companions' capabilities." He laid the packet on the table beside the glass case, and made himself let go of it. "And myself. Unconditionally."

"Unconditionally." The Warden picked up the packet but didn't open it. "You understand that word has a flavor here. It's the flavor we give things we intend to swallow." He circled the table slowly. "Tell me why. Three years ago you wouldn't give up a single coordinate without an appeal to the Archivist's Guild. Now you hand me a keeper's head on a plate. Men don't change that fast unless something broke."

*Something did,* Jude thought. *Something named Maren, dying in a doorway so a runaway could live. Something named a farm family healed for free by a girl who owed them nothing. Something named my own signature, at the bottom of the survey that taught you where to dig.*

"I starved in a cell for three years," he said aloud, and was almost proud of how flat his voice stayed. "I'd like to stop being useful only as an example."

"Mm." The Warden opened the packet at last. Jude watched his eyes move down the page — the route, the timings, the little cartographer's shorthand that meant *fordable at low water* and meant *ambush ground* if you knew how to read it. The Warden knew how to read it. That was the terror of him. He read everything.

"You're wondering," the Warden said pleasantly, not looking up, "whether I believe you."

The question landed exactly where it was aimed. Jude kept his face still. "I assumed you'd test it."

"Oh, I already have." He set the pages down. "Your girl healed a plague-house outside Millbrook two weeks past. Free of charge, no sigil, no license — hedge-work, raw as a wound. There were witnesses. One of them sells fish to my kitchens." He smiled again, thinner. "So yes. She exists. She moves. And your route says she'll cross the Stonebridge ford on the twenty-sixth, which is convenient, because I have four hundred riders who would very much like to meet her there."

*Four hundred.* Not fifty. Not a patrol. Four hundred — enough to take her, and enough to be nowhere near Vaelport when the cells were opened. Jude felt the arithmetic settle into place like stones into a wall, and hated how much of the wall was his own making.

"And Tam Vetch?" he asked. "The bell-founder. You're holding him."

"I'm holding a great many people." The Warden drifted toward the window, where the harbor lights were coming up one by one, pointless and brave. "A deaf man who casts charms in bronze. A sergeant who forgot her oath. A feral child with a brand on her shoulder that sings to me every time she crosses a checkpoint — did you know that? Little Sef. She thinks she cut it out of their reach. Brands aren't in the skin, Ashgrave. They're in the blood." He said it the way a man comments on weather. "But yes. The founder especially. Bronze remembers, you know. Melt it wrong and it holds the shape of everything it ever was. I find that useful."

"What do you want with him?"

"The same thing I want with you, eventually. The same thing I want with everything." The Warden turned from the window, and for a moment — just a moment — the lamplight caught his face wrong, and Jude saw something underneath the mildness that made his mouth go dry. Not cruelty. Cruelty was human. This was *appetite*, vast and patient and cold, wearing a bureaucrat's face. "To be needed. To be the hinge. Crowns come and go, Ashgrave; I've served four kings and buried three, and I promise you the crown is the least interesting thing in this room." He gestured at the bruise-glowing shard under glass. "That was a covenant. Made by frightened people, held together by trust — do you know how fragile that is? How *insulting*? Trust is a rope, and ropes fray. I intend to replace it with iron. Iron doesn't need holding. It holds you."

Jude heard himself say, quietly, "And the star-court?"

"Will kneel, or will be knelt." The Warden straightened his cuffs. "Take the eastern apartments. You'll dine with me tomorrow; we'll discuss your reinstatement. Full pardon, your name struck from the disgrace rolls, your old rank and stipend restored. And a commission." His eyes glinted. "Royal Cartographer to the Reunification. First of the new office. Your maps will redraw two realms, Ashgrave. Think of it. Everything you drew in secret, drawn in daylight, signed with your own name."

There it was. The thorn-hedge's gift, offered in marble and vellum, exactly the shape of the want Jude had confessed to a ring of living brambles in the dark. His name in stone. His work in the light.

"That's generous," he managed.

"I'm told I can be." The Warden smiled, and it reached his eyes this time, which was worse. "Sleep well. Traitors always sleep badly, and I'd hate for you to start now — it would make me doubt you."

* * *

They gave him a room with a lock on the *outside*, which told Jude everything about how much he was trusted, and a view of the harbor, which told him everything about how much he was watched. He sat on the bed until the watch-bells rang midnight, counting the sentries' circuits below — eleven minutes, twelve, eleven — and then he went to the window and did the thing he had actually come to do.

The drainpipe was old and the mortar was older. Brackwater's smithies made good climbing irons, and Tam Vetch had made him a bell-charm shaped like a beetle that, when its wings were pressed together, drank sound for a hundred heartbeats. Jude pressed the wings, dropped into silence and darkness, and went down the tower's blind eastern face like a rumor.

Orla was waiting in the coal-yard behind the chandler's shop, hood up, arms crossed, radiating the particular fury of a sergeant whose plan was working.

"You're late."

"I counted sentries."

"You counted sentries *and* stopped to admire the view." She shoved a dark cloak at him. "Cells are under the armory. Two guards on the door, rotation at the third bell, both of them on my old roster — Dree sleeps standing up and Hollis owes me his life and hates owing it. We go in through the coal chute, we walk out through the gate, and if anything goes wrong, you're a drunk cartographer looking for a tavern. Understood?"

"Understood."

"And Ashgrave." She caught his sleeve. Her voice dropped to something that wasn't anger at all, which from Orla was far more frightening. "If this is a trap — if any part of you wants your name back badly enough to sell us — I want you to know I'll put you down myself, quick, before they can take the rest of us. Not because I hate you. Because I promised the girl."

"I know," Jude said. "I counted on it. It's why the plan works."

She looked at him for a long moment in the coal-dark. Then she grunted — Orla's version of absolution — and led him into the night.

* * *

The coal chute was exactly as wide as a man who regretted his life choices, and twice as long.

Jue emerged coughing black grit into the armory cellar, where Sef was already waiting, crouched in the shadow of a rack of pikes with her brand-arm wrapped in wool. She looked terrible — hollow-eyed, jumping at drips of water — and she looked *furious*, which meant she was functional.

"He knows about the brand," she whispered, before Jude could ask. "He talked to me through a slot in the door yesterday. Knew my kennel-name. Called me *little six*. Said he'd been listening since the checkpoint." Her jaw worked. "I thought I cut it out of their reach."

"Brands aren't in the skin," Jude said gently. "I'm sorry. I should have warned you."

"You couldn't have known."

"No. But I should have guessed. That's the job. Guessing what powerful men know." He flexed his fingers, gritty and steady. "Which means I also know he's expecting trouble tonight, somewhere, and has decided it won't be here. Four hundred riders at Stonebridge, remember. We're a rounding error to him."

"Then let's stay a rounding error," Sef said, and moved for the door like smoke.

Dree was asleep standing up, exactly as advertised. Hollis took one look at Orla's face through the grate and unlocked the cell block without a word, hands shaking, not meeting anyone's eyes — a debt paid, a man released from his own guilt. And there in the second cell, chained to the wall with wardens'-iron that hummed faintly against his wrists, sat Tam Vetch, thin as a winter branch, lips cracked, eyes clear.

He saw them and did not smile. Instead he raised one chained hand and tapped a rhythm against the wall — slow, deliberate, three-and-two, three-and-two — the bell-founder's cadence for *wrong, wrong, too easy.*

Jude's stomach dropped. "Tam—"

The big man shook his head once and tapped faster, urgent now, then pressed his palm flat against the wall beside a hairline crack in the mortar. Behind it, faint as a heartbeat, something *hummed.* Listening-iron. The cell had been singing everything Tam heard to someone else for — how long? Days?

*He let us find that,* Jude thought, ice sliding down his spine. *No. He let us come.*

Orla had reached the same conclusion; he saw it hit her like a blow. But there was no time, and Tam was right in front of them, and whatever the Warden knew, they could not leave a friend in those chains to learn more. They broke the locks, wrapped Tam's wrists in Sef's wool, and went back up the coal chute with the bell-founder's breath rasping between them.

At the top of the yard, in the gap between sentry circuits, Orla paused at the wall and looked back at the tower. Its windows were dark. All of them. Even the Warden's.

"He's not asleep," she said flatly.

"No," Jude agreed.

"He's *watching us leave.*"

"Yes."

"Then why—" She stopped. Jude watched her arrive at it, watched her face change in the starless dark, and finished it for her, because someone had to say it aloud:

"Because we're the diversion. He thinks *we're* the diversion." Jude's mouth tasted of ash and coal. "The route I gave him. The ford. Four hundred riders. If he believed any of it, he'd have ridden out already. He hasn't. He gave me my rooms and my pardon and let me climb down a drainpipe, because he wanted to see who came for Tam — and now he knows. All of them. Every name, every face, every safehouse road out of Brackwater." He laughed, and it came out broken. "He doesn't need me to lead him to Ivo anymore. We just showed him the whole map ourselves."

Below them, the gate stood open, unguarded, courteous as a host showing guests to the door. Somewhere beyond it, horses waited that no one had bothered to steal, and the salt-road ran east toward friends who thought themselves safely hidden.

Sef tugged Tam's arm. "So we run."

"We run," Orla said grimly. "And we pray he's arrogant enough to have missed something."

Jude looked back once, at the black tower, at the one window where — he could have sworn, though he would never prove it — a lamp had just gone out, deliberately, like a closing eye.

The Warden had let them go. That was the triumph. That was also the sentence.

Somewhere south, four hundred riders were saddling in the dark, and they weren't riding to Stonebridge.
