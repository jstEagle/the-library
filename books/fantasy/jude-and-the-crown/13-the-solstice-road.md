## 13. The Solstice Road

The days began to die in the north.

Ivo noticed it first as a wrongness in the light — a thinness, the way milk thins when watered. They had left Brackwater with eleven hours of grey daylight between dawn and dusk, and by the fourth day on the solstice road they had eight, and then seven, and the sun itself seemed to be shrinking, pulling away from them like a hand withdrawn from a dying man's grasp.

"That's not natural," Orla said, standing at the roadside with her arms folded, watching the sun sink at what should have been midday. "Even in deep winter the light holds. This is—" She stopped. She was not a woman who liked to finish sentences that frightened her.

"The anchor's failing," Tam said. He could not hear the wind, but he felt it through his boots, and he read their faces the way other men read books. His hands moved as he spoke, bronze-stained fingers shaping the words with a precision his voice never quite managed. "The star-court holds the sky the way a keel holds a boat. Cut it loose and everything drifts."

"Cheerful," Sef muttered. She walked ahead as always, half a dozen paces out of conversational range, the brand on her collarbone hidden beneath a scarf she never removed anymore. Not since the checkpoint. Not since the blood.

Jude spread his map across a flat stone, weighting the corners with pebbles. The parchment showed the processional road unspooling northward like a grey thread, past the Waystation of Saint Aldhelm, past the Salt Fens, up into the high country where the Starwell waited at the bottom of its crater like an eye that had cried itself blind.

"At this rate we make the well two days before solstice," Jude said. "If the days keep shortening, we don't."

"Then we walk in the dark," Orla said.

"We've been walking in the dark since the stars went out," Sef said. "What's one more kind?"

But it wasn't one more kind. Ivo felt it in her teeth, in the roots of her hair, in the place behind her sternum where Maren's magic had lived and now something much larger lived instead. The dark outside the valley was not empty the way Hollowmere's gloom had been empty. It was hungry. It pressed against the little ring of their lantern-light like wolves against a fold, and every night the circle of safety seemed smaller, and every morning the sun rose later and weaker, and somewhere far above them the sky was coming apart at its seams because a court of starlit beings was suffocating in a cage of crown-iron, and no one else in all the world seemed to know or care except five ragged people walking north.

She kept her hands in her pockets. She had started doing that without noticing. Her hands scared her now.

* * *

The ash began to fall on the sixth day.

It came at first like snow that had lost its nerve — fine grey flakes drifting down through air gone utterly still, settling on their shoulders and the backs of the mule and the bracken by the roadside. Sef caught a flake on her palm and frowned at it. "Snow doesn't smell like anything," she said. "This smells like a burned book."

It was not snow. By noon the flakes were thick enough to obscure the road twenty paces ahead, and the light had gone the color of old tallow, and the silence was total — no birdsong, no wind, nothing but the soft hiss of ash landing on ash. Jude pulled his scarf over his mouth. "Skyfall," he said grimly. "There are records of this. Before the wards, when the covenant was whole, the sky anchored itself to the star-court. Without the anchor—" He gestured upward with one hand, a small helpless motion Ivo had never seen him make before. "Everything drifts. Even the sky burns, eventually. A candle flame needs something to hold it upright."

They wrapped their faces and walked on. The ash got into everything — gritted between teeth, crusted at the corners of eyes, sifting down collars to lie against skin like a cold gray shroud. The mule's breath came out in ragged white plumes. Twice they passed abandoned farmsteads, doors hanging open, tables still set for meals no one had come home to eat. In one, a child's wooden horse stood on a windowsill facing the road, as if it had been watching for them.

That night they sheltered in the lee of a fallen waystone, one of the ancient markers along the processional road, carved with kings whose faces had been eaten smooth. Ivo built the fire because no one else dared let her near it.

She saw how they did it now. Orla volunteered to gather wood and took Sef, and the two of them ranged far down the slope rather than near the camp, speaking low, and though Ivo could not hear what they said she knew the shape of it. She knew because she'd been having the same conversation with herself for three days.

Her power was growing. That had been the promise, hadn't it? The closer to the Starwell, the stronger the keeper. But strength was the wrong word. What filled her was less like strength than like pressure — a tide behind a bad door, a river pressing at ice. All day it had been leaking. When she'd reached to steady the mule's load, her fingertips had scorched black stripes through the leather straps. When she'd laughed at something Tam signed, sparks had jumped from her lips like a struck flint. When she'd knelt to pull a burr from her boot, the burr had curled and browned and died in her grip, and the grass around her knee had gone silver-brittle with frost-fire, which made no sense, heat and cold together, the magic not caring what it did so long as it did *something*, so long as it poured out of her like blood from a wound that wouldn't close.

"I can help with the warding," Tam offered, kneeling beside the fire. He had set out his little bronze charms in a crescent around their camp — bells without clappers, tuned to frequencies only the old magic heard. "We can bleed some of it off into the metal. Ground you, like lightning into earth."

"And ruin your charms." Ivo's voice came out rougher than she intended. "You cast those for weeks. One night of me and they'll be slag."

"They're bronze," Tam said, smiling his quiet smile. "Bronze remembers its shape. It's why I work it." He set the nearest bell in front of her, open side toward her heart. "Give it what you can spare."

She tried. She held her hands over the little bell and opened herself the way Maren had taught her a lifetime ago — *magic follows trust, Ivo, you cannot seize it, you can only leave the door unlatched* — and what came out was not a trickle but a torrent. The bell rang without a clapper, a high pure note that bent and warped, and then the bronze went soft as wax and slumped sideways into the ash, and the heat of her own power came back at her like a slap, and Tam had to drag her backward by the shoulders before the fire leapt the stone circle.

Silence. The others staring. The ruined bell lying in its puddle of cooling metal like a dead thing.

"I'm sorry," Ivo whispered.

"It's fine," Tam said, and signed it too, both hands, so she couldn't miss it: *fine, fine.* But he gathered up his remaining charms and moved them a full arm's length farther from where she sat, and she saw him do it, and he saw her see, and neither of them said anything at all.

* * *

She dreamed of the well that night. She always dreamed of the well now — the crater, the chains, the dead star caged at the bottom like a heart cut out of the world's chest. But tonight the dream changed. Tonight the star opened one eye.

*Little keeper,* it said, in the voice she'd been hearing since the drowned chapel, the voice like a choir heard through water. *Come quickly. Come carefully. And come knowing what you are becoming.*

"What am I becoming?" she asked inside the dream, and the star's eye turned slowly toward her, vast and patient and terribly sad, and—

She woke because Sef screamed.

Ivo came up out of her bedroll already burning. That was the horror of it — she woke and the power was already loose, pouring off her in visible waves, the air shimmering above her skin, and Sef was scrambling backward through the ash with her blanket smoking, her sleeve alight, her face a mask of terror lit orange from below.

Ivo didn't remember reaching for her. She didn't remember the decision. Later she would piece it together from the evidence: Sef's blanket scorched through in a perfect handprint, five fingers, as if Ivo had seized her in her sleep; the bedroll beneath Ivo burned to bare ground in a circle exactly her own size; Orla tackling Sef clear and rolling her in the ash to kill the flames; Jude standing frozen with his knife drawn — drawn, she understood dimly, not against any intruder, but against *her*, and she could not even blame him.

"Sef—" Her voice cracked apart. "Sef, I'm sorry, I didn't — I was asleep—"

Sef stared at her from Orla's grip. The sleeve of her shirt was burned away to the elbow, and beneath it the old kennel-brand on her collarbone had caught the firelight, and Ivo watched her realize it was showing and yank the ruined cloth closed over it, flinching, always flinching, and something in Ivo's chest tore straight down the middle.

"You were dreaming," Sef said. Her voice was flat. Controlled. The voice she used when she was deciding whether to run. "You were saying *come quickly* over and over. And then you grabbed me and you were so *hot*, Ivo, you were like a forge—"

"I would never hurt you. Not on purpose."

"No," Sef agreed softly. "Not on purpose." And that was worse than any accusation, because it left everything unsaid sitting there between them: *but on purpose isn't the only way things happen. Out here, everything costs something. You taught me that yourself, the day I stole your pack.*

Orla released her slowly. Tam signed something urgent — *she didn't mean it, she's sick with it, anyone can see* — and Sef nodded, and picked up her charred blanket, and moved her bedroll to the far edge of camp, past the stones, out of arm's reach, out of sleep's reach, and lay down with her back to all of them.

No one slept again that night. Ivo sat awake with her hands wrapped in wet strips of blanket, the way Maren used to bind a scalded hand, and watched the ash fall through the darkness like grey snow falling on the grave of the sky.

* * *

Dawn came late and wrong, a bruise-colored smear in the south that might once have been the sun. They broke camp without speaking. The mule's coat had gone grey with ash. The road north was buried under it, and their footprints filled in behind them within minutes, erasing themselves, as if the world had decided they had never passed this way at all.

Jude walked beside Ivo. He hadn't spoken to her since the night, and she expected reproach, or worse, pity. Instead he unfolded the map as they walked, holding it against his forearm against the falling grey.

"There's a waystation," he said. "Ruined, but roofed, maybe. Two days' walk — less, if we push. Old pilgrim shelter from before the wards. My map marks a spring nearby." He paused, and when he spoke again his voice was lower. "My grandmother's maps mark it, actually. The ones the Crown burned me for. There are things written in the margins about that place." A muscle worked in his jaw. "Things about keepers who couldn't hold what they were given. About what they left behind for the next one."

Ivo looked at him. Ash had settled in his hair, turning him grey at forty, and his eyes had the flat exhausted honesty of a man who has stopped calculating.

"What kind of things?"

"A letter," Jude said. "Maren's letter, I think. She stayed there once, years ago — I found her name in the guest-marks when I mapped it as a boy. If she knew you'd be the one to walk this road..." He didn't finish. He didn't need to. The thought had been living in Ivo's chest for days, feeding on her fear like the ash fed on the light.

They walked on through the falling sky, five figures and a mule, north toward a hole in the world where a murdered star waited to be either saved or spent. Behind them the footprints filled with grey and vanished. Ahead, the days grew shorter still.

And in Ivo's hands, wrapped in wet wool, something small and stubborn refused entirely to go out.
