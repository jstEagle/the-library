## 14. Maren's Last Lesson

The waystation had been a chapel once, or a granary, or a place where kings' processions paused to be blessed — the Road of Hollow Kings did not explain itself. Its roof had half fallen in, letting down a lattice of beams against the ash-grey sky like the ribs of something that had lain down and died mid-prayer. The wind came through the gaps in the walls carrying the smell of cinders, though there was no fire for a hundred miles. The sky itself was burning somewhere, slowly, invisibly, and everything below breathed its smoke.

Ivo set her pack down on a stone floor furred with grey dust and did not unpack it right away.

She knew what was inside it. She had known since Maren's cottage burned, since she'd pried loose the hearthstone with scorched fingers while warden boots rang on the road outside. A packet of oilcloth, flat and heavy as a hymnal, tied with the same waxed thread Maren used to bind her charms. She had carried it through the broken border, past dying villages, through Sef's ambush and Jude's lies and Orla's war, and she had not opened it.

Not because she feared what it said. Because some part of her — the part still twelve years old, standing in Maren's kitchen with blistered hands, waiting to be told she'd done well — knew that whatever was inside would not say *well done*. And Ivo Blackwood had run out of strength to be told she was wanting.

"You're doing the thing," Sef said from the doorway, "where you stand very still and look like you're about to be sick."

"I'm resting."

"You're holding your bag like it bites." Sef came in sideways, the way she came into everything, ready to leave. She dropped an armful of dead bracken by the cold hearth and began arranging a fire with the economy of someone who had slept rough since before she could count. "Jude says the storm will reach us by full dark. He says it like it's bad news. It's ash, not snow. Ash doesn't care if you freeze."

"Where is he?"

"Outside with Orla, arguing about whether the eastern wall will hold. He thinks it won't. He's wrong, but he enjoys being worried, so no one tells him."

Despite everything, Ivo smiled. Then the smile faded, and Sef saw it fade, and Sef — who noticed everything and acknowledged almost nothing — went quiet and busied herself too hard with the bracken.

Ivo sat down against a fallen beam, put the pack in her lap, and worked the waxed thread loose.

* * *

Inside the oilcloth there was a letter, and beneath the letter, wrapped in soft leather, a ring of iron keys worn smooth as river stones, and beneath those, a small square of star-metal — dull, pitted, unmistakably a fragment of the chains they had seen drawn in Jude's forbidden maps. Chains that caged a dead star at the bottom of the world.

Maren's handwriting was exactly as Ivo remembered it: small, slanted, merciless. No wasted strokes. She read aloud without meaning to, her voice low, and the ruined waystation seemed to lean in to listen.

*Ivo.*

*If you are reading this, then either I am dead or you have finally disobeyed me thoroughly enough to find my things, and honestly both outcomes please me.*

Ivo's breath caught. Her eyes blurred; she blinked the letter back into focus.

*You will hate this next part, so I will say it plainly, the way I never managed when you were a girl and I was afraid that if I spoke gently even once, the world would use it as a door.*

*I have known what you are since the night you arrived on my doorstep, blue-lipped and furious, clutching your grandmother's charm-bag. The wards sang when you crossed them. Do you understand? They had not sung in two hundred years. Old Tamrin — who taught me, whose teacher taught him, all the way back to the keepers themselves — old Tamrin wept over the sound. Then he told me what the singing meant: the covenant had chosen its last thread, and the last thread was a child, and children break.*

*So I decided you would not break.*

*Every harshness was a wall. Every withheld kindness was armor. You think I did not see you flinch? You think I did not lie awake cataloguing every wound I dealt you? I let you believe you were worthless so that when power finally came to you — and it will come, Ivo, it will come roaring — you would know in your bones that you were not made worthy by it. Worthless girls do not kneel to crowns. Worthless girls do not trade their names for thrones. Worthless girls, offered everything, ask first one question: why should I trust the hand that offers?*

*It was a cruel lesson. I am sorry. I am not sorry. Both are true, and you will learn that truth can hold contradictions the way a keeper holds light — many hands, many truths, none alone sufficient.*

Ivo stopped reading. Her hand was shaking. The ash-storm moaned against the eastern wall, and somewhere outside Jude raised his voice in vindication about masonry.

She thought of the kitchen. Of the ten thousand mornings of porridge eaten in silence. Of the charm-work corrected until midnight, praised never, dismissed always — *adequate, adequate, adequate* — while her hands bled from the nettle-thread and she hated the old woman with a hatred so clean and complete it had been almost holy. She had built her whole escape around that hatred. It had been the warmest thing she owned.

And now it lay in her lap in small slanted letters, transmuted, and she found she could not pick it up anymore. Grief came up through her like water through chalk, and she bent over the letter and wept — not prettily, not quietly, but the way she imagined Maren must have wept over Tamrin's grave: furiously, resentfully, grateful.

Sef said nothing. She simply moved closer along the fallen beam until their shoulders touched, which was, from Sef, a speech.

When Ivo could read again, she finished it.

*One more thing, and then I will stop lecturing, which you will note I never once refrained from in life.*

*The Starwell will offer you power. Not as a temptation is offered — not with horns and bargains — but worse. It will offer you the chance to fix everything. The famine, the darkened sky, the sick herds, the graves. All of it, yours to mend, if only you will hold it. If only you will be the one. The old kings took that offer. So did the Lord Warden, whoever he has become. Power does not corrupt the wicked, Ivo. It corrupts the kind, because the kind cannot bear to watch suffering when they could end it.*

*So when it comes — and it is coming — remember the hedge. Remember that a hedge is not a wall. A wall keeps the world out. A hedge is alive, and it has gaps, and things pass through it both ways, and it blooms. Bind nothing to yourself. Bind everything to each other.*

*You were never inadequate. You were never wanting. You were a seed, and I was winter, and I am sorry, and I love you, and grow.*

*M.*

Below the signature, in fresher ink, a single line added later, the letters shakier:

*P.S. — The keys open the undercroft at Hollowmere church. What the Crown buried there, they buried shallow. Dig.*

* * *

Ivo folded the letter along Maren's own creases, careful, precise, the way she'd been taught, and discovered that the teaching itself was now a comfort rather than a chain. She wrapped the star-metal fragment back in its leather and hung it around her neck. It was cold against her sternum and stayed cold, drinking warmth the way the faceless kings drank light.

Sef was watching her sidelong. "Good news?"

"She loved me." Ivo heard her own voice come out scraped raw. "In the worst possible dialect."

"That's the only kind anyone speaks," Sef said. She looked away toward the fire. "My mother sold me for six marks and a promise of visits. I've spent eleven years deciding that means she didn't." A pause. The wind filled it. "Maybe she loved me in a dialect I couldn't hear. Maybe that matters less than the selling. Maybe both." Sef shrugged, fierce and fragile at once. "Tam would say it better. Tam says everything better in bronze."

Ivo reached over and gripped Sef's wrist — the unbranded side. "Thank you for staying."

"I stole your supplies. We're even."

"We're not even," Ivo said. "We're family. It's different math."

Sef stared at her, and something behind her eyes — something that had been braced for abandonment since before memory — shifted a half-inch off its foundations. She didn't answer. She didn't need to.

The eastern wall groaned. Jude appeared in the doorway with ash already silvering his hair, Orla behind him, and announced, "The wall is going to hold, but only out of spite, so nobody lean on it."

* * *

They ate hardtack and dried apples around a fire fed on dead bracken, and the ash began to fall — fine, grey, unhurried, sifting down through the broken roof like the sky shedding skin. It settled on their shoulders and did not melt. Outside, the Road of Hollow Kings vanished under a grey tide, and the toppled statues drank their light and gave nothing back.

Ivo read them the letter.

She hadn't planned to. But Tam was gone, taken at Brackwater, and every hour he was in warden hands was an hour the bell-key in his captors' possession grew heavier, and tomorrow they would walk north again into the teeth of the solstice, and she wanted — needed — her people to know what Maren had known. That the temptation coming for them was not greed. It would wear the face of rescue.

She read it all, including the postscript, and when she finished, the fire cracked and the ash fell and no one spoke for a long time.

"The kind cannot bear to watch suffering," Orla repeated at last. She turned her sergeant's signet ring around her finger, the one she'd kept after defecting, the one she refused to throw away. "Thirteen years I watched good soldiers do monstrous things because the alternative was watching people suffer. That's how the Warden recruits. Not with ambition — with mercy, weaponized." She looked at Ivo. "Your mistress understood the enemy better than any briefing I ever sat through."

"She understood people," Ivo said. "That's rarer."

Jude had gone very still, the way he went still whenever the conversation approached the shape of his own confession. Finally he said, "There's a tradition. Older than the Crown. Older than the roads." He hesitated, and Ivo realized with a small shock that the man who had mapped realms for tyrants was nervous. "Keepers didn't swear oaths to kings. That was the whole point. They swore to each other, hand to hand, because a circle has no top. The Warden will bind the covenant to a throne if we fail. But tonight, here—" he gestured at the ruined roof, the falling ash, the small defiant fire, "—here we're not failing yet."

"What did the words say?" Ivo asked.

"No one knows the old words. They died with the valley's silence." Jude's mouth twisted. "Which means we make our own. Which means they'll be true, because invented oaths cost more than inherited ones. You actually have to mean them."

Orla laughed — short, surprised, real. "A cartographer proposing we draft our own covenant. The Crown would burn you twice."

"They can queue," Jude said.

They joined hands. It was awkward. Hands are awkward things — Sef's calloused and quick, Orla's scarred across the knuckles, Jude's ink-stained even now, Ivo's still tender from the hearthstone. They sat in a lopsided circle around a fire in a dead building under a dying sky, and it should have been nothing, four exhausted travelers playing at ceremony.

It was not nothing.

Ivo felt it begin in her palms — not the roaring flood that had knocked her to her knees the night the stars went out, but something smaller and steadier, a warmth passing hand to hand around the circle like a coal passed carefully, each person warming it and warming it and passing it on. Her hedge-magic, the useless little craft of charms and knots and blooming things, rose up and braided itself into the circle without being asked. Where their hands met, faint light gathered, gold-green, the color of new hedges.

"Oh," said Sef, very quietly, looking at their joined hands. "Oh, that's — don't stop."

"I wasn't going to," Ivo said. Her voice shook. "Say the words. Anyone. All of us."

They made them as they went, stumbling, overlapping, correcting each other, and the making was the oath:

*I hold nothing that I would not share.* — Jude, whose whole life had been hoarding knowledge, saying it first, hardest.

*I keep no secret that keeps my people in danger.* — Orla, thinking of thirteen years of sealed orders.

*I choose the circle over the escape.* — Sef, who had survived her entire life by running, and everyone heard what it cost her, and no one made her explain.

*I bind myself to these hands, and to the hands that join them after, and to no crown, and no throne, and no hunger that wears mercy's face.* — Ivo, and as she spoke the words she felt Maren's letter settle in her chest like a coal finding its bed, grief transmuted at last into purpose, winter acknowledging spring.

The light pulsed once around the circle — a heartbeat, a hedge in bloom — and sank into their skin and was gone, leaving only warmth and the smell of green things where there should have been only ash.

Nobody moved for a moment. Then Sef said, thickly, "If any of you ever tell anyone I cried, I will deny it and also steal everything you own."

"Noted," said Jude.

"It's in the covenant now," Orla said solemnly. "Binding and irrevocable."

And for one hour, in a ruined waystation under falling ash, they laughed — really laughed, the ragged kind, the kind that comes after funerals — and the Road of Hollow Kings outside drank its light and waited, and far to the north a dead star lay chained in the dark, and far to the south a stolen bell-founder sat in a cell, and the solstice came on like a tide.

But that was tomorrow's weight. Tonight they had made a family out of choice, and choice, Ivo thought, watching the fire, was the one magic the Crown had never learned to map.

Near dawn, Jude spread his maps by the embers and tapped the northern route with a charcoal stub. His face had gone grim in the way it did when the cartographer in him overrode everything else.

"There's a problem," he said. "The road forks at the Gate of Thorns. One path for each traveler — it's in the old survey notes, I thought it was decoration." He looked up, and the firelight caught the fear he wasn't bothering to hide anymore. "The notes also say the gate shows each walker the thing they want most in the world. And Ivo — the notes say the ones who see nothing are the ones it lets lead."

Ivo touched the star-metal at her throat, cold against her heart, and thought of Maren's voice: *worthless girls ask first one question.*

"Then we'd better find out what I want," she said, "before it does."
