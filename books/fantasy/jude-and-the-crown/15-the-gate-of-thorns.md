## 15. The Gate of Thorns

Sef smelled the thorns before she saw them.

It was not the smell of a hedge — no green sap, no crushed leaf. It was the smell of a fire burned down to its bones and then watered, ash and steam together, something old trying to remember how it had once been alive. She stopped at the crest of the ridge with her hand out flat behind her, the signal that meant *stop walking*, and the company stopped, because by now they had learned that Sef's nose knew things before eyes or ears did.

"What is it?" Ivo asked, coming up beside her.

"Wrong," Sef said, which was most of what she ever said about anything, and then, because Ivo had a way of waiting that made silence feel like a debt: "Old wrong."

Below them, filling the whole throat of the valley like a dam made of knives, stood the hedge.

It was not a wall so much as a wound given shape. Thorns the length of her forearm grew in whorls and spirals, black at the base where they emerged from soil the color of old blood, fading to a gray-white at their tips like bone left out in weather. There was no gap in it. There was no gate in it. And yet the road they had followed for eleven days — the Road of Hollow Kings, Jude called it, saying it the way other men said a curse — ran straight up to the thing and stopped, as if it had always been meant to end here, swallowed.

The wind came down off the northern crags and moved through the hedge, and the hedge did not rustle. It *breathed*. A slow expansion, a slow contraction, the whole mass of it swelling like the ribs of something enormous sleeping on its side. When it exhaled, the smell of ash-and-water rolled over them again, and beneath it, fainter, Sef caught something else: sweetness. Rot-sweetness, like fruit gone over, like the inside of the kennels' feed barrels in high summer.

Her stomach turned over. She kept her face still. She had gotten very good at keeping her face still; it had been the first lesson the kennels taught, before obedience, before fighting. *A face that shows nothing gets fed.*

"The map ends here," Jude said quietly. He had the old royal survey open across his forearm even though he'd stopped needing it days ago; he held it the way some men hold a rosary, less for reading than for touching. "The Starwell should be beyond this ridge line. Half a league, maybe less." He looked up at the hedge, and his jaw worked. "This wasn't on any of my maps."

"You mapped for kings," Orla said. "Kings don't come this far north. Kings send other people." Her voice was flat as slate, but Sef heard the edge under it, the sergeant's habit of sharpening grief into precision. Orla had been quiet since the statues. They all had been quiet since the statues, since Ivo woke screaming from dreams of a voice under the earth, since the ash began to fall instead of snow.

Ivo stepped forward to the edge of the road, close enough that the nearest thorn-curl hung a hand's breadth from her cheek. Sef watched the magic move in her — you could see it now, everyone could, a shimmer under the skin like light through ice, unstable, beautiful, frightening. Since the waystation and Maren's letter, Ivo had stopped trying to cage it. That was better, probably. Probably. Sef remembered what power felt like when it wasn't caged, and her memory of it was all teeth.

"It's grown from something," Ivo said. She didn't touch the thorn. "From the covenant's corpse. Like a tree growing where lightning struck." Her voice went strange, distant. "It's not guarding the Starwell. It's *made of* everything that died here."

"Then how do we get through?" Tam asked. His hands were wrapped around a scrap of bronze he hadn't stopped holding since Vaelport, rubbing it the way he used to rub his bell-molds, an anchor for a mind that thought in casting temperatures. He signed as he spoke, and Orla translated without being asked, the two of them moving like one creature now.

Nobody answered. The hedge breathed. Ash drifted down and settled on the thorns, and where it settled, the thorns drank it — Sef watched it happen, the gray flakes vanishing into the black wood like rain into thirsty ground.

Then the hedge opened.

There was no grinding of branches, no parting. One moment there was solid thorn-wall, and the next there was an archway, tall as a chapel door, framed in the longest spines Sef had ever seen, each one curved inward like a talon closing. Beyond it the road continued into fog, and the fog was not weather-fog. It glowed very faintly, the way the kennel lamps had glowed just before they were doused for the night.

"It wants us to walk in," Orla said.

"Of course it does," Jude said. "Everything on this road has wanted us to walk somewhere. The statues wanted us to look at them. The drowned chapel wanted Ivo underwater." He laughed, but it came out wrong, thin. "We're being invited to supper."

"It's a test," Ivo said. "Vess told me—" She stopped. She had been stopping herself from talking about Vess's words for two days, hoarding them, and now she let a little out through the crack. "The covenant's last keeper built defenses out of its own dying. Not to keep people out. To keep the *wrong* people out. People who would take rather than tend."

"And which are we?" Orla asked.

Ivo looked back at them — at Jude with his ruined hands and his maps, at Orla with the warden's brand scarred over on her own forearm where she'd cut it out, at Tam, at Sef. Looking at Sef last, and longest, and Sef hated how much she didn't hate it.

"We're going to find out," Ivo said, and walked through the archway.

* * *

Inside, the fog was warm.

That was the first betrayal of it. After eleven days of ash and killing cold, warmth should have been mercy, but Sef's skin crawled the way it crawled when a kindness came too easily, because in her experience kindness that arrived unasked was always a hook with bait on it. The fog pressed close, soft as wool, and the road underfoot turned to moss, silent, springy, and the thorns were gone — she looked back once and saw only white behind them, no archway, no hedge, no way out that her eyes could find.

They walked in single file because the fog allowed nothing wider. Sef took the rear position without discussion; rear guard was where she'd always been, the one watching backs, the one who saw trouble coming because trouble liked to come up behind people who trusted the person ahead of them.

She heard it start with Tam first.

A sound ahead, muffled by the wool-white: Tam's voice, but not talking to any of them. Talking to someone who answered. Sef edged forward along the line until she could see through a thinning of the mist, and what she saw made her blood go cold and slow.

Tam stood facing a forge.

Not a memory of one — a real one, or as real as anything in this fog, brick and iron and the orange bloom of a lit fire, and hanging above the bench, rows of bells in every stage of casting, raw clay molds and finished bronze, and standing at the bench, sleeves rolled, forearms scarred with the burn-spots of a working founder, a broad-shouldered woman with Tam's same heavy jaw. She turned and smiled at him and said something Sef couldn't hear, and Tam's face — Tam, who had crossed half a kingdom with a brand on him and never once wept where anyone could see — Tam's face broke open like a dropped pot.

His mother. Dead six years, if the stories were true. Standing in the fog offering him a hammer.

"No," Sef breathed, and reached for him, and the fog closed between them like a curtain drawn.

The road shifted. She felt it shift, actually felt the ground reorganize itself under her boots, patient and vast, and understood suddenly that they were no longer traveling together. Each of them had been handed their own thread of the road. The hedge had taken them apart the way a cook takes apart a bird.

"Sef."

The voice came from behind her. From the rear, where nothing was supposed to be, because she *was* the rear.

She turned.

The kennels stood in the fog.

Not the memory of them. The kennels themselves, or their perfect twin: the long low sheds with their turf roofs, the exercise yard ringed by the low stone wall the children were never allowed to climb, the bell above the gatehouse that rang changes and meals and punishments. Frost on the ground. Woodsmoke. The particular smell of tallow and dog and frightened child that lived so deep in her body she had never once managed to sweat it out, not in five years of freedom, not on any road, in any city, drunk on any stolen wine.

The yard was full of children.

They were doing morning drills, the ones she remembered, the line-walking and the stillness-standing, small figures in the gray coats with their hems weighted so they couldn't run well. And the warden at the fence — the warden at the fence was Kennel-master Voss, exactly as he'd been, big and slow-moving and gentle-voiced, the worst kind of cruel because his cruelty wore patience like a coat.

Except the yard was empty. Every shed door stood open. Every chain-hold on the wall hung loose and swinging. The frost lay unbroken across the whole exercise yard, no footprints anywhere, and the children — Sef made herself look, made herself count — the children were shadows wearing the shapes of children, hollow-eyed, going through the drills with no one making them, drilling for masters who were gone.

The kennels, empty.

The thing she had wanted every night for five years. The picture she built in her head to fall asleep to, the way other children built pictures of castles and cakes. Every shed empty. Every child run free or dead-free, either way past reaching. Voss swinging from the gatehouse beam, or better, Voss alive and kneeling, Voss learning what fear tasted like from the inside —

"There you are," said Kennel-master Voss, in the fog, kindly. "You're late for drill."

And there, breaking away from the line of shadow-children, running toward her across the frost with her arms out, a small girl in a weighted-hem coat with dark hair chopped short and a brand-new burn on the inside of her wrist — a girl Sef recognized the way you recognize your own hands.

"No," Sef said.

The little Sef ran closer, and the frost did not print her footsteps, and Sef understood what the hedge was showing her with terrible clarity: not a dream. An offer. Here was the want itself, pulled out of her chest and dressed in fog, and the hedge was asking the question underneath all questions:

*Take it. Walk into it. Stay. Be the child who never escaped, be the child whose world ended before it broke her — stay here, where the wanting lives, and go no further north.*

It would be easy. That was the horror of it, the thing Sef would never tell any of them: it would be so easy. She was tired. They were all so tired, and the Starwell ahead held a price that Vess had named and Ivo had refused to repeat aloud, and behind her — here — was a version of her life with all the wounds still open but none of them yet inflicted.

The little girl stopped three paces away, breathing fog. Waiting. All the shadow-children had stopped their drills to watch, a hundred hollow faces turned toward her, and Sef realized they weren't watching her refuse or accept. They were watching to see if she would do for them what no one had done for her.

Come back. Open the doors. Take them out.

"I can't," Sef said, and her voice cracked on it, and she made herself say the rest. "You're not real. And even if you were — even if I dug Voss's grave with my own hands — going back isn't the same as getting out." She knelt, slowly, in the frost, level with the child that was and wasn't her. "Getting out is *forward*. It's always been forward. Nobody ever came back for me. So I became the one who goes ahead." She reached out and touched the girl's brand-new burn, gently, the way nobody had touched hers. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry nobody came. But I can't live in the place where it hasn't happened yet."

The kennels sighed — the sheds, the walls, the whole gray world of it — and came apart like breath off a mirror. The last thing to fade was the hundred small faces, and they did not look angry, and somehow that was worse.

The fog thinned. The road was a road again. Ahead of her, forms were resolving out of the white: Ivo, standing very still with her arms wrapped around herself and tears freezing on her cheeks, looking at nothing. Orla on her knees in the moss, pressing her fist against her mouth. Jude leaning against a thorn-trunk with his eyes shut, whispering something that might have been a name.

They had all been alone in there. They had all been offered their own sweet poison.

Only Ivo — Sef watched her, watched the shimmering under-skin light gutter and calm — only Ivo stood in the middle of her own test like a woman standing in an empty room.

"Yours didn't come," Sef said, when she got close enough. "Whatever it showed you. Nothing came."

Ivo shook her head slowly. "There was fog. And then it was waiting for me to want something, and there was just—" she made a small, lost gesture at her own chest, "—nothing there. Just the road. I don't know what that says about me."

Sef looked at her — at the girl who had healed strangers for free, who had nearly burned her friends in her sleep and wept over it, who carried a dead woman's letter like a second heartbeat — and felt something turn over in her chest that she refused to name, because naming things gave them power over you, and Sef had spent a lifetime refusing.

"It says," she said, "that the hedge couldn't buy you. Come on. Everyone's still breathing."

* * *

Beyond the fog, the land changed.

The ash stopped falling — not gradually, but at a line, as if a door had shut above them, and beyond that line the sky was neither starless nor starred but something else entirely: deep violet, bruised, shot through with veins of dim gold like embers under a banked fire. The air smelled of cold metal and, impossibly, of summer. The moss underfoot gave way to pale grass, and the grass grew thicker with every step, silver-edged, luminous, the kind of grass that had never known a scythe.

They crested the final rise in a silence that none of them chose, and stopped, all four, in a row, like figures on a frieze.

Below them, the land fell away into a crater wide as a drowned valley, and at its heart lay the Starwell — Sef could see the dead star even from here, a vast dim sphere like a coal gone gray, lying in its bed of chains, crown-iron links thick as temple pillars winding over and around it, and every link humming, she could hear it now, a note below hearing, felt in the fillings of her teeth.

And on the crater's rim, between them and the descent, torches. Dozens of them. The black lacquer of warden shields catching the ember-light. Banners she knew too well, and at their center a figure on a white horse, still as a statue, waiting.

Beside Sef, Jude made a sound like a man swallowing broken glass.

"They're already here," Orla whispered. "How are they already here?"

Ivo stepped forward to the edge of the rise, and the ember-veined sky seemed to lean down toward her, and the dead star in its chains — Sef would swear this until she died — the dead star brightened, just slightly, just once.

Like something recognizing a knock at the door.

"It doesn't matter," Ivo said. Her voice carried strangely in the still air, clear as a bell Tam might have cast. "We didn't come this far to stop at the door." She started down the slope, toward the torches, toward the chains, toward whatever waited at the bottom of the world.

Sef put her knife where her hand could reach it, checked that Jude could walk and that Orla's axe was loose, and followed her down.
