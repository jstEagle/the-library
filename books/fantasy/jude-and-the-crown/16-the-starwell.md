## 16. The Starwell

They smelled it before they saw it.

Not the ash, which had been with them for three days now — a gray fall that hissed when it touched snow and left the taste of cinders on every breath. Not the cold, which had grown teeth. It was something older than either: a smell like hot iron and honey, like the moment just before a forge-strike, sweet and searing at once.

Sef stopped at the crest of the ridge and made a sound Ivo had never heard from her before. Small. Unfinished.

"Oh," Sef said. "Oh, that's *wrong*."

Ivo climbed the last few feet beside her and understood.

The land fell away into a crater so wide she could not see its far rim — a bowl of black glass where the ash-sky reflected in a perfect, terrible mirror. Rivers of frozen light ran down its inner walls like glacier-veins, silver-blue and unmoving, as though someone had caught a waterfall mid-fall and left it there for centuries. And at the center, sunk deep into the glass floor of the world—

A star.

It was dead the way a hearth is dead an hour after the fire goes out: still radiating warmth, still glowing at its core with a stubborn ember-light, but unmistakably, heartbreakingly dying. It hung above a shattered well-shaft, turning slowly, and around it — around *it*, around something that had once burned in heaven —

Chains.

Crown-iron links thick as oak trunks, each one stamped with the seal Ivo knew from Maren's ledgers and warden's badges and the coins she'd hoarded to buy her escape: the crowned tower, the unblinking tower-eye. They ran from the star's body outward to anchor-points driven into the crater's rim at eight intervals, and they were not rusted, not worn, not old-looking at all. They gleamed. They were fed.

"They've been drinking it," Orla said behind them. Her voice was flat in the way Ivo had learned meant the sergeant was closest to weeping. "All this time. Every lamp in Vaelport, every warden's ward, every blessed thing the Crown calls power — it's been bled out of *that*."

Tam touched one of the bronze charms strung at their belt — the forged bell-key, recovered at such cost, humming faintly against their hip — and signed rapidly. Jude translated without being asked, his voice hollow: "It's resonating. Tam says the key was cast to match this place. Says it's been singing since Brackwater."

Ivo could feel it too. The thread of hedge-magic in her blood, the small green gift that had roared to life the night the wards fell, was pulling toward the crater like a root toward water. But underneath the pull was something else — a sound only half-heard, a song played on an instrument with most of its strings broken. She had been hearing it in dreams for weeks. Here, standing on the rim of the Starwell, she could finally hear how few strings remained.

*Come,* said the song. Or maybe: *hurry.* Or maybe, worst of all: *too late, too late, too late.*

"We go down," Ivo said.

Nobody argued. That frightened her more than the chains did.

* * *

The descent took hours. The glass floor of the crater held them, but it flexed — a deep, tectonic shiver under each footstep, like walking on the skin of something sleeping. Frozen rivers of light crossed their path, and where they did, Ivo's magic sparked off her skin in visible motes, drawn out of her whether she willed it or not. By the third river she had learned to breathe through it. By the fifth, she noticed her companions keeping a careful distance from her, and pretended not to notice that either.

Jude walked beside her anyway. He always did now, since the fever, since the confession he'd made in delirium and repeated sober by firelight. He limped on the leg Sef's escape had nearly cost him and refused all mention of it.

"You're doing your thinking-out-loud face," he said.

"I don't have a thinking-out-loud face."

"You have several faces. I've catalogued them. It's what cartographers do." He gestured ahead with his walking staff, at the chains arcing overhead like the ribs of a buried cathedral. "Say it anyway."

Ivo watched the light run through the iron. It moved wrong — not flowing but *draining*, a slow tide pulled from the star toward the anchors, toward the rim, toward wherever the Crown's great siphons carried it. Generations of theft. Centuries of it. Her whole life inside Hollowmere's gloom, every gray noon and lightless midnight, had been downstream of this.

"The song," she said. "It's not calling me, exactly. It's calling anyone. Anyone who'll listen." She hugged her arms against the cold that wasn't entirely cold. "Maren's letter said the covenant was held by many hands. So why does this feel like a door with one keyhole?"

Jude was quiet for a long moment. In the ember-light his face looked carved, all the cynicism burned off it, leaving only the tired man underneath.

"Because you're the one who's going to ask," he said finally. "And whatever answer you get — I want to be standing next to you when you get it."

They came to the well-shaft as the ash-darkened day failed, which here meant nothing changed; the star's own dying glow was the only sun this place acknowledged.

The shaft was vast, ringed with terraces of worked stone — steps cut for processions, for hundreds of keepers descending hand in hand, if the drowned chapel's memory-truth had shown her rightly. Now the terraces stood empty except for the chains' roots, and frost-feathers of silver light, and bones. Old bones, honored bones, laid out in rows with care. Keepers who had come before. Keepers who had stayed.

Sef read the terraces the way she read everything — fast, wary, looking for exits — and found none, and said nothing about it, which was its own kind of courage.

At the shaft's lip, hovering above the dead star itself, was a figure.

Ivo had seen it in dreams without ever seeing it: a tall slight shape like a person assembled from dusk, with wings that were not bird-wings nor bat-wings but moth-wings — enormous, powdered with living starlight, trembling slowly open and shut. Where a face should be there was a soft oval of luminescence, featureless and yet somehow attentive, the way a lamp seems to watch you when you wake at night.

It descended as they approached, wings shedding motes of pale fire, and settled on the lowest terrace before them with the strange jointless grace of a creature that had never known gravity until recently.

"Vess," Ivo said. The name arrived in her mouth from somewhere deeper than memory. "You're the envoy."

The figure inclined its head. When it spoke, the voice was layered — many voices braided into one, some high, some low, all of them tired in a way that made Ivo's chest ache.

"I am what remains of the embassy. We were nine hundred, keeper-child, when the wells were young." A pause, and the wings drooped a fraction. "I am what remains."

Behind her, Orla went very still. Sef put her hand near — not touching, never presuming, just near — and after a moment Orla let her shoulder rest against Sef's, two soldiers holding each other upright.

"What happened?" Ivo asked, though the chains answered for themselves.

"The old kings built the first chain in famine years, or so they told themselves. To borrow. Just to borrow." Vess's light flickered. "But power is a door that only opens inward, keeper-child. Each king borrowed more and returned less. Your Crown did not break the covenant in a single night of greed. It ate it meal by meal across two hundred years, and called each bite necessity, and taught its children that the hunger was virtue." The oval face turned toward the chained star with something Ivo could only call tenderness. "That is my court's brother-sister up there. Dying slowly enough that no single theft seemed worth a war. Until the theft was everything, and there was no one left to fight."

The words landed in the silence like stones into a well. Ivo thought of Hollowmere's perpetual gloom, of villages where charms failed and livestock sickened, of Maren's hard hands and harder lessons — all of it downstream, all of it interest paid on a debt she'd never known existed.

"Then we restore it," Ivo said. "The covenant. That's why I'm here. Show me the binding."

Vess regarded her for a long moment. The moth-wings stilled.

"Yes," Vess said. "But first you must hear the price. All of it. No keeper has ever agreed to this with full knowledge, and I will not add your name to that dishonor."

The envoy raised one slender hand, and the star-song swelled — and Ivo realized Vess was not making sound at all, but *opening* her ears, the way the flooded chapel had opened her eyes.

"See," Vess said. "*Understand.*"

* * *

She stood at the center of the well, though her body had not moved.

Around her, hands. Hundreds of them, thousands — human and not-human, weathered and soft, scarred and ringed and clawed — joined palm to palm in an unbroken circle miles wide, ringing the Starwell like a crown of living fingers. Through the circle flowed the covenant: not power exactly, not anything so graspable. It was *attention*. It was ten thousand hearts agreeing, moment by moment, to hold a door open between two realms, forever, because a door held open by love lets light through and a door held open by force lets only hunger through.

She felt what it was to be one pair of hands among thousands. The burden distributed until it was almost weightless — a candle each, lighting one window each, the city bright because everyone kept theirs lit.

Then the vision shifted, and showed her the alternative.

One figure at the center. Alone. Holding the whole covenant in one body, one will, one pair of arms. And she *felt* it — oh, she felt it — the way absolute power feels from the inside: not like strength but like drowning in warm gold. Every prayer in both realms arriving in your skull. Every ward, every blessing, every spell a tug on your sinews. You would be worshipped. You would be needed. You would never again be a person, because a person can set down a load, and this load could never, ever be set down — not for sleep, not for grief, not for death. Death would simply mean the door slamming shut and both realms going dark.

Forever, or die trying. Those were the terms. There had never been other terms.

The vision released her.

Ivo came back to herself kneeling on the terrace stones, sick and shaking, Jude's hand gripping her arm, Sef's voice saying her name over and over in the tone of someone who has been saying it a long while.

"It showed me," Ivo managed. Her teeth chattered. "Both ways. The circle — the many hands — it works, it *works*, that's how it was meant—" She stopped. Because the other thing was still sitting in her chest like swallowed coal, and she was a hedge-witch from a gloomy valley who had spent her whole life wanting to matter, and she knew with terrible clarity exactly which version the hungry part of her had liked.

Vess knelt — folded, rather, joints bending in directions that should have been impossible — until the luminous face was level with hers.

"Now you understand," the envoy said gently. "The covenant can be reforged. The realms can reopen. The stars can climb home into your sky. But the binding must be *held*. By a circle of many hands, freely given — or by one soul at the center, forever, or until death ends the holding and unbinds everything again." A pause, delicate as wing-dust. "Your Crown means to take the second road at solstice. Their Lord Warden believes himself strong enough to carry it alone. He is mistaken, and the mistake will kill him and darken both worlds — unless someone stands at the well first, and chooses otherwise."

"And if we choose the circle?" Orla's voice, rough. "What then? Who holds it? All of us?"

"All who are willing," Vess said. "Willingness is the only coin the covenant accepts. That is why crowns cannot keep it, keeper-child. Crowns cannot make willingness. They can only counterfeit it, and counterfeits burn."

Ivo looked down at her own hands. Hedge-hands. Chapped, burn-scarred from a thousand small charms, hands that had learned magic from a bitter woman in a cottage at the edge of nowhere. Hands that had wanted, her whole resentful life, to be *important*.

The vision had shown her what importance cost. Forever, or die trying.

And beneath the fear, quiet as a banked coal, another feeling: the memory of the circle. Ten thousand candles. Nobody alone. Everyone held.

"There's a catch you're not saying," Ivo said, because Maren had taught her that much — that gifts arrive with invoices folded inside.

Vess's wings trembled.

"The Warden comes," the envoy said. "He has been following your light like a shark follows blood since the night the wards fell. He arrives with the royal host and with your friend the bell-founder in chains, and he arrives believing the choice is already made." The luminous face turned upward, toward the ash-choked sky where no moon could currently be found. "You have until solstice, keeper-child. One day. Decide what you will stand for — and know that whatever stands at the center of that well at moonrise tomorrow will hold it *tonight*, ready or not."

Far above, beyond the crater's rim, horns began to sound.

Iron horns. Crown-made, crown-fed, carrying on their notes the faint stolen shimmer of starlight.

Coming down the pilgrim road at double-march, with the whole horizon behind them moving like a sea of torches.

Jude swore softly in the language of maps and margins. Sef cracked her knuckles. Orla began counting soldiers by ear, lips moving, and Tam's forged key sang against their belt like something eager for a forge-fire.

Ivo rose to her feet on the terrace above the dying star, and felt the price of everything settle over her shoulders like a cloak cut exactly to her measure.

"Well," she said. "We'd better decide fast."
