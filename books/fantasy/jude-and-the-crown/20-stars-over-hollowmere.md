## 20. Stars Over Hollowmere

The wards rose at dusk.

Ivo stood on the southern ridge of the valley with the wind pulling at her hair and felt them come up through her boots like the first warmth of spring moving through frozen ground — slow, deep, unmistakable. Not the old wards. Not the ones that had sealed Hollowmere like a jar with its lid waxed shut, the ones she had spent nineteen years resenting without ever quite naming why. These were different in the way a door is different from a wall. They hummed where they met the earth, but they did not bite. They asked rather than forbade. When a nightbird crossed the valley mouth it passed through without so much as a ripple, and Ivo felt that too, the small parting of the magic around living things, courteous as a crowd stepping aside for a funeral.

Behind her, Sef whistled low. "It's like standing inside a held breath," she said. "But one that's going to let itself out."

"It's letting everything out." Ivo spread her fingers and watched the light gather in her palms — not the roaring, dangerous flood of the road, not the thing that had nearly burned Sef in her sleep on the solstice road, but something gentler, something with manners. The covenant ran through hundreds of hands now. Keepers in Brackwater, keepers among the defected wardens who had laid down their staffs on the crater rim, keepers in villages whose names Ivo would never learn. She was only one thread of it, and the relief of being only one thread was so vast she could have wept. "It doesn't need me to hold it anymore. It just — wants me to be here."

"That's called home," Orla said from behind them, and there was no irony in it at all.

They came down into the valley as the stars came out.

That was the other thing — the stars. For nineteen years Hollowmere had lived under a lid of gloom, the sky above the valley a bruised and starless ceiling even on clear nights, because the Crown had hoarded the light and the valley had paid for it in darkness. Now the first stars were rising over Torren Fell like sparks off an anvil, then more, then more than Ivo had ever seen anywhere in her life, because the sky here was washed clean by years of rain and the stars came down into it like seeds into turned soil. The Milky Way arched over the valley mouth, over the broken place where the old wards had shattered like glass on the night everything began, and the light of it lay on the fields so softly that the barley looked silvered, look touched with frost, though the night was warm.

Ivo stopped in the middle of the road and looked up until her neck ached.

"I used to think," she said slowly, "that if I ever got out of this valley I'd never look back."

"And?" Sef said.

"And now I can't stop looking." She laughed, a wet, unsteady sound. "It's the same valley, Sef. Same mud. Same smell of pig and peat smoke. It's the same place."

"No," Sef said, bumping her shoulder. "Look again."

So Ivo looked again. And it wasn't the same place. It was the same place the way a locked room is the same room after someone has opened the windows — the same walls, the same floor, but suddenly full of air and distance and the sound of things growing. Down in Millbrook the lamps were coming on, but half the houses didn't bother lighting them, because the starlight was enough, and people were standing in their doorways looking up the way she was looking up, and she could hear, faint across the fields, someone singing. An old harvest song, the kind Maren had hummed while grinding herbs, the kind nobody had sung in Hollowmere in Ivo's lifetime because singing to the dark seemed like mocking it.

The song rose up the valley and the valley took it and gave it back warmer.

* * *

Maren's cottage still stood. That surprised Ivo more than anything else on the walk down — she had expected ruin, expected the warden scouts to have burned what they couldn't take, expected ash and collapsed beams and her whole childhood erased. But the scouts had died at Maren's door before they'd done more than break a shutter, and the villagers, in those first terrible weeks when the charms failed and the livestock sickened, had apparently done what villagers do: they had patched the roof with borrowed thatch, propped the door on new hinges, and left the rest alone. Waiting. As if some part of them had known the apprentice would come back.

Inside, it smelled of dust and dried yarrow and, beneath that, faintly, impossibly, of Maren herself — camphor and bitterroot and the particular woodsmoke of the hearth she had banked exactly the way she liked and no other way. Ivo stood in the doorway a long time before she could make herself cross the threshold.

Sef waited outside. She didn't ask. That was one of the thousand things Ivo loved about her.

The grave was behind the garden, where Maren had buried every animal and every friend and, once, Ivo knew now, a lockbox of letters she'd meant Ivo to find. The village had marked the spot with a flat stone and no name, and Ivo knelt beside it in the starlight and put her palm flat against the cold ground and did not cry, or rather she cried the way rain falls, quietly, steadily, without drama, for a long time.

"You chose harshness," she said to the stone. "You chose it on purpose. You knew what I'd have to refuse, someday, and you made sure I'd be strong enough to refuse it." Her thumb traced the stone's edge. "I hated you for it. I want you to know that. I hated you thoroughly and for years, and you let me, because being loved gently wouldn't have saved anyone."

The ground gave nothing back. But the yarrow growing wild along the garden fence leaned toward her, all of it at once, the way flowers turn toward the sun, and Ivo huffed a laugh through her tears because Maren would have found that unbearably showy and said so.

She worked until dawn. She dug with a spade from the lean-to, and she lined the grave with sprigs of rowan and wormwood the way hedge-keepers were buried in the old days, before the Crown decided the old days hadn't happened, and she spoke the words Maren had taught her in her ninth year and made her repeat until they were worn smooth as river stones. *What was given returns given. What was kept is kept still.* And because the covenant lived in her hands now along with everyone else's, the words didn't fall on dead air. They settled into the earth like warmth settling into a bed, and when she finished, the grave-mound bloomed. Yarrow first, then rowan shoots, then a ring of white flowers Ivo had no name for, small and many-petaled and faintly luminous, opening in the dark as if they'd been waiting under the soil for nineteen years for permission.

By the time the sun came up, half of Millbrook was standing at the garden gate.

* * *

She had braced herself for the villagers the way you brace for a blow. These were the people she had grown up among and apart from — the children who'd called her witch-brat, the mothers who pulled their daughters close when she passed, the men who brought Maren their sick animals and never their thanks. In the old valley, strangeness had been a debt everyone collected on. She had fled expecting to owe them nothing, ever, and she had come back owing them everything, and she did not know how a person carried that.

Tam's bell answered the question before anyone spoke.

He had insisted on coming — had ridden two days out of his way with a bronze bell strapped to his pack mule, cast during the long nights in Brackwater, tuned by touch against his own wrist because he could not hear it and trusted his bones to do his hearing for him. Now he set it up on a frame of green branches at the edge of the burying-ground, and when the villagers gathered he struck it once with the heel of his hand.

The sound went through Ivo like the wards had gone through her on the ridge. It was a covenant-bell, one of the first Tam had cast since the reforging, pitched to the frequency of shared keeping, and when it rang, every keeper within earshot felt the ring pass through them and out of them and onward, hand to hand to hand, a reminder written in bronze: *you are not alone in this.* The villagers weren't keepers, most of them. But they heard it anyway. Ivo watched it move through them like wind through barley — shoulders dropping, faces turning, the miller's wife reaching without looking for the miller's hand and finding it.

An old woman stepped forward. Bramble Fenwick, who had once thrown a bucket of wash-water at Ivo for looking sideways at her hens. She was bent nearly double now, and her eyes when they found Ivo's were wet and unashamed.

"We thought you were dead," she said. "We thought you'd run off and got yourself killed, and Maren—" Her voice cracked and reassembled. "Maren sat on that step every evening for three weeks, watching the road. Never told us why. Never told us anything, that woman. Then the stars went out, and she stood at her door with a kitchen knife like a girl defending a fort, and we understood maybe one part in a hundred of what she was." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, as if annoyed at it. "You'll stay, then."

It was not quite a question. It was not quite anything. It hung in the morning air between them, and Ivo found, to her surprise, that the answer had already been in her mouth for miles — had been in her mouth since the crater rim, since the drowned chapel, maybe since the night the wards broke and magic flooded into her useless valley-hedge heart for the first time.

"Yes," she said. "I'm staying."

And the strange thing, the thing she would turn over in her mind for years afterward, was how ordinary it felt. No absolution, no ceremony. Old Bramble nodded once, sharply, satisfied, and turned to practical matters — whether the cottage roof needed redoing before autumn, whether Ivo would take payment in eggs or in mending — and the debt Ivo had feared dissolved not because it was forgiven but because it had been miscounted all along. They hadn't been strangers, her neighbors. They had been strangers the way family is strangers during a long illness in a closed house. The doors had simply never opened.

Now the doors were open, and the light came in.

* * *

Magic came back to the valley the way water comes back to a dried streambed — finding old channels, carving a few new ones, pooling where it pooled. Ivo spent the first weeks walking the villages with her sleeves rolled up, not performing miracles but teaching. A charm against blight in Duncarrow. A birthing-song in Lowfen that had survived thirty years of silence in one midwife's memory and came back whole the moment Ivo hummed the first line. The well at Stonereach, which had tasted of iron since before Ivo was born, cleared overnight when a boy of six — a keeper, the covenant had found him, small hands and huge eyes — pressed his palm to the stones and laughed.

Hundreds of keepers, the envoy Vess had promised, and the promise was proving true in ways none of them had imagined. The covenant did not pool in the strong. It distributed itself like seed on wind, landing where it landed — the blacksmith's daughter, the toll-keeper's widow, a drunk in Torren Fell who sobered up the week the light came into his hands and stayed sobered, saying the power felt wrong otherwise, saying it *checked* him, which was precisely the point of it, precisely what the old kings had never understood. Power held by one pair of hands curdles. Power held by hundreds stays clean, each hand checking the others, no single grip tight enough to strangle.

There was warden business still — Orla rode the roads with Sef, untangling the garrisons, sorting which wardens had loved the Crown and which had merely feared it, and the difference turned out to matter enormously — but that work happened elsewhere, out beyond the gates, and Ivo learned to let it happen without her. That was the hardest lesson, harder than any Maren had taught: that she was allowed to be one keeper among hundreds. That the covenant did not need her vigilance to survive. That she could plant beans.

She planted beans. They came up in four days, which embarrassed her, and she ate them anyway.

* * *

On the evening of the autumn equinox — the first equinox in nineteen years that the valley would see properly starred — a horse came down the pilgrim road at a canter, raising dust gold in the low light, and Jude Ashgrave swung off it in the yard of Maren's cottage looking thinner than when Ivo had last seen him and cleaner than he had any right to, given the state of the roads.

"You're supposed to be in Vaelport," Ivo said, wiping her hands on her apron and failing entirely to hide her grin.

"I was in Vaelport." He handed her a leather tube with a cartographer's seal — his own seal now, a compass rose with an open eye at its center, granted by a Crown that had learned, at terrible cost, to sign documents with humility. "Arguing about maps. Arguing about scale, specifically. There are men in the Survey Office who believe the borderlands should be drawn smaller, for tidiness. I told them tidiness is how we got the crown-iron chains in the first place." He looked past her at the cottage, the garden gone riotous, the luminous flowers ringing the grave-mound behind it, and something in his face went quiet. "Is that—"

"Maren. Yes. Buried proper, this summer. The whole valley came."

"Good," he said, and meant it, and let the silence hold it for a moment. Then he unrolled the tube's contents across the garden table, weighting the corners with stones and a teacup, and there it was: the new map. Two realms, human and starlit, drawn side by side for the first time in living memory, joined not by a sealed line but by open roads — real surveyed roads, dotted with waystations, with the Starwell marked at the center not as a fortress or a treasure but as a crossroads. And everywhere the surveyors' knowledge ran out, and it ran out often, gloriously often, Jude had left the parchment blank and inked in the same words across every empty stretch:

*Uncharted — go see.*

"The embassy leaves in spring," he said. "Sef and Orla are leading it. Vess says the moth-court has agreed to receive them, provided nobody kneels — they find kneeling confusing and slightly rude." He tapped the blank spaces one by one, the northern fens, the far shore, the whole unmapped spine of the starlit realm. "I've argued my way onto the expedition. Apparently a disgraced cartographer who betrayed a Lord Warden and lived makes excellent company in uncertain country."

"You're not disgraced anymore. You're pardoned. There's probably a statue."

"If there's a statue I will personally chisel the face off," Jude said, "as tradition requires," and Ivo laughed, and the laugh went out over the garden and up into the darkening sky where the first stars were arriving, punctual as ever, patient as ever, shining down on Hollowmere the way they had once shone before any crown existed to hoard them.

Later — much later, after bread and wine and arguments about scale that ended in mutual accusations of pedantry — Ivo walked out alone to the gate and stood where Maren had once sat watching the road, and watched the road.

Far down it, at the valley mouth, the new wards glimmered faintly where the starlight passed through them, unhindered, welcomed. Travelers were coming up the pilgrim road in the blue dark — she could see their lanterns, three of them, four, a little procession of the curious and the hopeful and the lost, drawn toward the open gates the way everything was drawn now toward whatever had been closed too long. Among the lanterns, nearer than the rest, moved a small figure on foot, a child by the stride of her, thin and quick and alone, and something about the way she flinched from the wards' soft glow — tested it, circled it, crept closer — snagged in Ivo's chest like a hook.

The girl stopped at the gate. Looked up at the stars. Looked, for a long moment, at the lit window of the cottage where Ivo stood.

Then she raised one small hand and knocked — on the air, on the threshold, on the invisible door where a wall used to be — and waited to be let in.

Ivo was already walking toward her, undoing the latch of a gate that needed no latching, when she understood that she had seen this before: a frightened valley kid at a doorway, deciding whether to knock. She smiled, and opened the gate wide, and the starlight came through it with the child like a tide coming home.
