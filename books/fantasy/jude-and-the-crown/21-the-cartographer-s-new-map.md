## 21. The Cartographer's New Map

The trouble with drawing a world that has just changed its mind, Jude Ashgrave discovered, was that the ink kept arguing with him.

He sat at the long table in the map-room of Hollowmere's new waystation — a room that had been a granary until the solstice, when Tam had knocked through the eastern wall to let in the light and declared it *good for drafting* — and stared down at the sheet before him with the particular despair of a craftsman whose subject refuses to hold still.

Two realms. That was the simple version. The human lands west of the old border, and beyond them the starlit country that had been sealed away for three hundred years, its meadows lit by no sun but by the slow silver breathing of its own sky. On his old royal maps, that second realm had been a blank: a wash of pale grey with a cartouche reading *TERRA INCOGNITA — BY ORDER OF THE CROWN*, which was the Crown's way of saying *we are afraid of this and have decided it is therefore not real.*

Jude had drawn that blank himself, once. He had been twenty-two years old and proud of the neatness of it.

Now he dipped his pen, touched it to the vellum where the border should run, and hesitated.

Because there was no border anymore. Not really. There was a road — the old processional road, cleared of ash, its toppled faceless kings righted or respectfully left lying as the villages along it had decided each statue deserved — and the road simply went on, from Hollowmere up over the shoulder of the fells and down into country where the grass grew faintly luminous at dusk and the rivers ran the color of dawn held too long in a cup. Where did one realm end? At the last human farm? At the first star-meadow? At the point where a traveler stopped being able to tell?

Ivo would say the question was stupid. Ivo would say the road was the border, and the border was people walking on it, and if he wanted to draw that he should draw feet.

Jude smiled at the vellum, alone in the lamplight, and drew the road instead of the line.

It was a good road. He gave it a double stroke, the way the old pre-Crown maps had given roads that were covenant-roads, open by treaty rather than by permission. He'd found one such map in Maren's cottage — tucked behind a loose hearthstone, along with her letter to Ivo, because that woman had hidden things the way other people breathed — and he had copied its conventions shamelessly. Roads as promises. Wells marked with a small radiating sun. And in every corner where the Crown's surveyors had once stamped their seal, the old keepers had written instead a single word in a looping, patient hand:

*Uncharted — go see.*

Jude wrote it now, in the great blank space east of the fells where the star-country opened out into regions no living mapmaker had walked. His pen hovered after, trembling slightly. Three hundred years of professional instinct told him that a blank was a failure. A blank was territory unclaimed, ungoverned, unknown — a wound in the paper.

But he had watched what happened when men could not tolerate blanks. He had mapped the Starwell for exactly that reason, filling in the one place the Crown most wanted known, and the filling-in had cost a dead star three centuries of captivity and Jude eleven months in a cell with damp in the walls and his own name struck from every register in Vaelport.

He wrote *uncharted — go see* again, further east. Then again, near the southern marshes where Orla swore there were drowned bell-towers ringing under the water. Then a fourth time, just because it felt like prayer.

Outside, the evening came on gently. A year since the stars had gone out; eight months since they'd come back, climbing out of the Starwell crater in a slow spiral of returning light while the Lord Warden's dust blew away on a wind that smelled, everyone swore afterward, of moth wings and rain. Eight months of the strangest peace Jude had ever lived inside — a peace with sawdust in it, and arguments about drainage ditches, and Sef teaching valley children how to pick pockets *for defensive purposes only,* she said, and Ivo's apprentice, Pip, following her mistress around like a small anxious shadow learning to be less afraid of shadows.

The lamp guttered. Jude trimmed the wick, and heard footsteps on the granary stairs — two sets, one light and quick, one heavy and deliberate — and then the door banged open without ceremony, because ceremony was another thing the year had killed.

"The scale's wrong," Ivo said.

"You haven't looked at it."

"I don't need to look at it." She came across the room with ink already on her fingers — she always had ink on her fingers now, or soil, or starlight, depending on the day — and planted both hands on his table, leaning over the map with her braid swinging. Behind her, Orla filled the doorway, arms crossed, wearing her defected-sergeant expression, which was the same as her regular expression but with more patience in it. "You've made the star-country smaller than ours."

"I have made it *accurately* smaller than ours—"

"You've made it smaller because you can't see all of it, so you left room for doubt. Doubt takes up space on a map, Jude. It makes everything past the edge of what you know look cramped and far away." She tapped the eastern blank. "That's not geography. That's fear with a ruler."

Jude sat back and regarded her. She was nineteen years old and she had held a dead god's power in her hands and let it go, and some days he still marveled that the same person had once stood in Maren's kitchen burning porridge and calling hedge-magic useless. "And what does the keeper of the covenant propose instead?"

"Scale it true. Big. Bigger than comfortable." Her finger traced east, off the vellum entirely, into the air. "Let the map run out of paper. Let people look at it and feel how much *more* there is. That's what your old masters never understood — a map isn't supposed to make the world feel finished. It's supposed to make them want to walk."

"That," said Orla from the doorway, "is the worst thing I've ever heard a cartographer say, and I once served under a man who thought rivers were decorative."

"They're not?" said Jude.

"Everything's decorative until you have to cross it at night in the rain." Orla pushed off the doorframe and came to stand at the table's corner, and Jude saw then that she hadn't come up to argue about scale at all. There was a folded paper in her fist, worn soft at the creases, and she set it down on the map's blank eastern sea with the care of someone laying down a card she'd been holding a long time.

"What's this?" Ivo asked.

"Our route." Orla's voice was even, but her thumb moved once across the fold, back and forth, the only tell she'd never trained out of herself. "For the embassy. First crossing since the reforging. Sef and I leave at the turn of the month."

The room went quiet in the way rooms did now, when the future arrived without knocking. Jude looked at the paper — a soldier's route-plan, all marching distances and water sources and fallback positions, annotated in two hands, Orla's tight block capitals and Sef's slanted scrawl full of marginal complaints (*this well is haunted, Orla says it isn't, we will see who is right*) — and felt something tighten and loosen in his chest at the same time.

"You're going into the star-court," he said. "With a girl who six months ago couldn't sleep indoors."

"With a girl who six months ago couldn't sleep *anywhere,*" Orla corrected, "and who now sleeps fine as long as she knows where everyone in the house is. Which is called progress, in my professional opinion." She straightened. "Vess sent word. The court wants to meet the keepers. Properly. They've waited three hundred years; they'd like to stop waiting."

"And they asked for you two?"

"They asked for 'the branded one and the one who counts.'" Orla's mouth twitched. "Sef is very pleased about the first part. Being seen whole, she calls it. Brand and all."

Ivo picked up the route-paper, read it top to bottom, and Jude watched her do the thing she did — the thing Maren must have done before her, the keeper's reflex — checking not the distances but the *people* in them. Whether the wells were safe. Whether the haunted one was. Whether Sef's margins were jokes or fears dressed as jokes.

"It's good," Ivo said finally. "But take the northern fork past the second ridge. The thorn-hedge is dying back there, but it dies slowly, and it still tests. You'll want to go through it together, holding hands, and you'll want to decide beforehand what you'll refuse."

Orla nodded slowly. "And what did you see? In the hedge. When we crossed."

"Nothing." Ivo said it easily now, though Jude remembered the night she'd first told them, the way her voice had shaken around the word. "That's why I could lead. If you see nothing — if you can stand in front of your deepest want and feel *no pull at all* — the hedge lets you pass, and everyone touching you comes with." She set the paper down. "So decide what you want before you get there. Name it to each other. Then it can't surprise you."

"Sensible," said Orla. "Horrible, but sensible."

"*Go see,*" murmured Jude, half to himself, looking at his blank spaces. "That's all any of it ever was."

* * *

They argued about scale until midnight, cheerfully, the way family argues — which is to say with total commitment and no stakes, because the argument was not actually about the map.

Ivo wanted the realms drawn equal. Jude maintained that equality was a moral position, not a cartographic one, and that the star-country's surveyed extent genuinely was smaller, and that honesty required him to render what was known. Ivo maintained that he had spent ten years rendering what the Crown permitted and ought to be cured of it by now. Jude said the cure was clearly incomplete. Ivo threw an apple core at him. He caught it, mostly.

In the end they compromised the way all real compromises happen: neither was satisfied and both were pleased. Jude redrew the star-country at true scale, vast and generous, its edges running clean off the vellum into the wooden frame itself — so that the map literally ended in wood, in the material of doors and tables and houses, in the stuff of the ordinary world stopping short of something larger. Tam, when shown it the next morning, laughed until she had to sit down, and then carved a small bronze moth to pin at the frame's edge, wings spread toward the outside.

"For whatever's next," she'd said, in her silent way, her hands moving fast. "Maps should end pointing somewhere."

Now, alone again in the lamplight with the household asleep, Jude worked on the details. The villages of Hollowmere valley, each marked with a tiny open gate instead of the closed ward-sigil the old maps bore. The Starwell crater, rendered not as a wound but as a fountain — concentric rings of light he built up in gold leaf, working with a badger-hair brush by lamp, until the dead-and-risen star seemed almost to pulse on the page. The processional road with its faceless kings, and beside each king a note in his smallest hand: *name lost — ask the locals; they remember better than stone.*

That was the sentence he was proudest of. He'd stolen it from Ivo, who'd said it standing among the statues on the solstice road, and it had gone through him like a splinter and never come out. Ask the locals. Ten years of royal cartography and no one had ever told him the truth of the craft fit in seven words.

He was adding the last of the gold leaf when the stairs creaked and Sef came up, barefoot, wrapped in a blanket, hair loose and wild. She didn't ask permission to enter; she never had. She dropped onto the bench beside him and studied the map upside-down for a long moment.

"It's wrong," she said.

"So I'm told."

"No — it's wrong *here.*" She leaned over and put one finger on the kennels. The warden kennels outside Vaelport, where the Crown had raised children as tools, where Sef had spent nine years learning that love was a leash. Jude had marked it with a small black square, the conventional symbol for a ruin, because the kennels had been burned in the spring — Orla's doing, with the Lord Warden's own garrison orders, a fire nobody investigated because everybody who might investigate had helped set it.

"What's wrong with it?" Jude asked. "It's accurate. It's a ruin now."

"It's a ruin, yeah. But you drew it the same size as everything else." Sef's voice was flat, careful, the voice she used when she was saying something that cost her. "It should be small. Tiny. Like—" she hunted for it, "—like a tick on a dog. Something you zoom past. Because that's what it is now. It used to be my whole world and now it's a speck, and your map should say that, because some kid's going to look at this someday, some other kennel kid, and they need to see how small it got."

Jude looked at her. Sixteen years old, blanket around her shoulders like armor she'd chosen herself, brand scarred into her wrist and embassy-bound in three weeks to a court of moth-winged immortals who had asked for her by name.

He took out his finest pen and shrank the kennels to a dot barely visible, a fleck of black like a fly-speck, and beside it he wrote, in letters smaller than any on the map: *was large once. it lies.*

Sef read it upside-down. She didn't smile — Sef rationed smiles like a besieged city rations bread — but something in her shoulders came down half an inch, which from her was applause.

"Good," she said. "Keep going. Fix everything." She stood, paused at the door, and added over her shoulder: "Also I ate your plums. The ones you were saving. I'm not sorry."

"I know," said Jude, and meant: *that's what family is.*

* * *

Dawn came up gray-gold over the fells, and with it came Ivo, carrying two cups of tea and the expression of a woman who had been awake since four thinking about contour lines.

"About the scale," she said.

"We settled the scale."

"We settled it wrong." She set the cups down, pulled her chair around to his side of the table — his side, an invasion she committed daily and he had stopped minding sometime around midwinter — and smoothed the vellum flat with both palms. "Look at it fresh. What do you see?"

Jude looked. Two realms, joined by a road. A risen star in gold leaf. Blank spaces seeded with *go see* like a gardener planting a field he'd never harvest. A speck where the kennels had been. A bronze moth at the frame's edge, wings toward the outside.

"I see," he said slowly, "a map that ends pointing somewhere."

"Mm. And what's missing?"

He frowned. Ran his eye along the road, the villages, the crater, the coast of Vaelport with its salt-stained bells. And then he saw it, and laughed — a short, startled laugh, the laugh of a man catching himself in an old habit.

"There's no legend," he said. "No key. Every map I've ever drawn had a key — crown symbols, ward symbols, danger symbols, *here be the things you should fear.*" He shook his head. "This one doesn't need one. Everything on it is just... named. Nothing needs decoding."

"Add one anyway," Ivo said. She turned his pen case around, opened it, selected the finest brush, and handed it to him handle-first, the way an apprentice offers tools to a master — except she wasn't his apprentice, and they both knew she was offering him something else entirely. "One entry. One symbol. For the thing that holds it all together."

Jude took the brush. Dipped it. And in the empty corner of the greatest map he would ever draw, beneath the words *UNCHARTED — GO SEE,* he painted a small circle of joined hands — many hands, overlapping, no wrist belonging to any single arm you could trace — and beside it, in his best hand, the only key either realm would ever need:

*Keepers. Found everywhere. Increasing.*

Ivo studied it for a long moment. Outside, the valley was waking — Pip's voice calling the goats, Tam's hammer starting its day-song, Sef and Orla arguing happily about boots somewhere near the woodpile, and above it all, faint and constant as breath, the sound of the wards humming their open-gate hum, the sound of a border that had become a door.

"Well," Ivo said, and drained her tea, and stood, and stretched like someone about to begin rather than finish. "That's the map done, then."

"It's never done," said Jude. "That's the entire point of it."

"Obviously." She was already heading for the stairs, braid swinging, ink on her fingers, pausing at the door with the morning light behind her. "Come on, then, cartographer. Vess sent word at dawn. The star-court's asking for the mapmaker too — they want to see how their country looks from the outside." A grin, quick and bright. "Bring the blanks. They'll want to argue about those."

Jude looked down at his unfinished masterpiece, at all its honest emptinesses waiting to be filled by feet rather than pens, and reached for his coat.
