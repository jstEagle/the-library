## 22. A Covenant Kept

The stars came back on the first day of the new year, and a year later they still had not learned to behave.

Ivo stood in the dooryard of Maren's cottage — her cottage now, though she still could not say the word *mine* without hearing her mistress snort at it — and watched the sky over Hollowmere do its evening work. The stars did not hang the way the old ballads claimed they once had, fixed and orderly as nails in a board. They drifted. They wandered in slow constellations that no cartographer had yet pinned down, and Jude swore — with the wounded delight of a man whose life's work had been made gloriously obsolete — that some of them were not stars at all but lanterns carried by the star-court on its long rounds through the upper dark.

Tonight they gathered thick above the valley mouth, where the old wards had shattered and risen again as gates. Where the walls of light had once stood shoulder-high and blind, there was now an archway open to the road, humming softly, like a held breath that had finally learned it could be released.

Tomorrow was solstice. Tomorrow the keepers would come.

Tonight there was bread to bake, charms to mend, and a girl hiding in the woodshed who thought Ivo didn't know she was there.

* * *

Her name was Wren — a coincidence Ivo had stopped finding amusing around the hundredth time someone made the joke — and she had been living in the woodshed for six days now, surviving on stolen apples and a hedge-charm so badly knotted it made the apples rot faster instead of slower. Ivo had watched her from the kitchen window each morning: a thin, sharp-elbowed child with ash-brown hair and the particular hunched stillness of someone waiting to be caught. Valley-born. Hollowmere-born. One of the children who had grown up under the gloom, who had never seen a star until last winter, and who flinched now whenever the sky did something beautiful, as if beauty were a trick that preceded a blow.

Ivo knew that flinch. She had worn it for nineteen years.

She let the girl steal for six days because Maren had let her steal for three years, and because trust, like bread, needed time to prove.

On the seventh morning she set two bowls of porridge on the woodshed step — one sweetened with honey, one plain — knocked twice on the door, and walked away without looking back.

When she returned at noon, both bowls were empty, and the girl was sitting on the step like something washed up by a tide she hadn't consented to, watching Ivo with the defiant terror of a cornered animal deciding whether to bite.

"You knot charms like you're strangling them," Ivo said.

"I don't—" The girl's chin came up. "It works."

"It rots apples." Ivo sat down on the step beside her, leaving a careful arm's length of space. "Magic follows trust, not command. You commanded those apples to keep. You never asked them."

Wren stared at her. "That's not how the wardens teach it."

"The wardens aren't here anymore." It was true; half of them had gone home after the solstice, when the Lord Warden's dust blew off the crater rim and the Crown's claim over both realms dissolved into so much parchment. Some had stayed. Orla drilled them now in the yard behind Tam's foundry, teaching them to kneel before doors instead of break them down, and every one of them flinched exactly the way Wren flinched, exactly the way Ivo had flinched, and Ivo had learned to be patient with flinching. "And anyway, they're wrong. Come inside. We'll start with the apples."

* * *

They started with the apples, and then the well water, and then the rheumatic hinges of Maren's garden gate, which squeaked until Wren coaxed it quiet and then looked at her own hands as though they belonged to a stranger. By late afternoon the girl sat at Maren's old worktable — Ivo had scrubbed it clean of three years of grief but not, somehow, of her presence; the grain of the wood still seemed to hold a disapproving patience — turning a sprig of rosemary between her fingers while Ivo showed her how to listen to it rather than bend it.

"Again," Wren said, when it wilted. Her voice cracked. "Again."

"That's enough for today."

"*Again.*"

Ivo heard Maren in it — heard herself, three years ago, red-eyed and furious over a failed mending-charm, screaming *again* at a woman who never once told her she was gifted, because gifted was a word that made people soft. She had hated Maren for it. She understood it now the way you understand a scar only after you've grown your own.

"Wren." She waited until the girl looked up. "Why are you here? Truly. Not the apples."

The silence stretched. Outside, the evening star-court drifted past the window in its slow procession, and the light through the glass went silver and faintly moth-winged.

"They said the wards fell because someone broke them," Wren said at last, very quietly. "Before. When I was little. That a keeper betrayed everyone and that's why we lived in the dark." Her fingers tightened on the rosemary until Ivo feared for it. "My da says it was the Crown that broke them. My ma says it doesn't matter, broken is broken. And I keep thinking — somebody has to know how to hold them up, if they fall again. Somebody has to be standing there. And nobody in my house knows how, and nobody in the village does, except—" She stopped. Swallowed. Looked at Ivo with an expression Ivo recognized from the far side of a mirror. "Except you. And you're going to die someday, probably, and then what?"

Ivo laughed before she could help it — a startled, unguarded sound — and saw the girl's face begin to crumple with shame, and reached across the table and took her wrist, gently, the way Maren had never done and Orla always did.

"You want to learn so the world can't be taken from you again."

"I want to learn so I'm not *useless* when—"

"Yes," Ivo said. "Both things are the same thing, at the start. They stop being the same thing later, if you're lucky." She turned the girl's hand over, palm up, and laid the rosemary back into it. "Maren taught me harshness. It kept me alive. But the first lesson — the real first lesson, the one she never got to finish teaching me — is this: magic follows trust, not command. Say it."

"Magic follows trust, not command."

"Say it like you mean it."

"I *don't* mean it yet."

"Good." Ivo smiled, and felt Maren smile somewhere behind her, dry and satisfied. "Neither did I. That's why it takes a year."

* * *

Solstice dawn came up gold for the second year running, and by midmorning the road down from the gate was a river of people.

They came from everywhere. Valley folk in their good coats, carrying loaves and lambs and jars of last summer's honey. Pilgrims from Brackwater wearing Tam's bronze charms at their throats, bells chiming small and clear with every step. A delegation of star-court envoys in their moth-winged cloaks, Vess at their head, walking with that unhurried gravity that made even the goats go quiet as they passed. Wardens in undyed gray — no more royal blue; Orla had burned the last bolt of it ceremonially, to Sef's loud approval — marching not as enforcers but as guests, hands empty, faces uncertain and hopeful in equal measure.

Sef arrived over the ridge at a dead run, as she arrived everywhere, with Orla strolling behind and pretending not to know her.

"You're late," Ivo called.

"I'm *dramatic*, there's a difference." Sef skidded to a halt in the dooryard, brand-side of her jaw catching the light — the scar had faded to a thin silver line since the kennels' records burned, since Tam cast the counter-charm that unhooked it from the warden's tracking-song, but Ivo knew she still touched it on cold mornings, checking. "Jude's coming up the road. He brought the map."

"He always brings the map."

"He's very proud of the map."

He was. Jude Ashgrave came up the valley road in his cartographer's coat, ink-stained to the elbow, carrying the great rolled map like a relic — which, in a way, it was. The new map of two joined realms, four years in the drafting, its borders drawn not as walls but as thresholds, its blank spaces marked in his own neat hand: *uncharted — go see*. He'd argued about the scale of the Starwell crater for an hour last month. He'd argue about it again next month. It was, Ivo had come to understand, how he said *I am staying*.

"Well?" he said, unrolling a corner against the fence post. "Look at the eastern passes. I've redrawn them from Vess's flight-paths. The mountains have moved, Ivo. *Moved.* Nobody tells me these things."

"Stars wander," Ivo said. "You wrote that yourself."

"Stars wandering is poetry. Mountains wandering is *cartographic malpractice*." But he was grinning, that crooked, guarded grin that had lost most of its guard over four years, and behind him the crowd was gathering at the field below the cottage — the field where, one year ago tonight and every year before that for a single generation, nothing had stood at all.

No throne. No altar. Just a circle of flat stones that Tam had cast in bronze and the valley had dressed in wildflowers, and people, hundreds of them, arriving to hold out their hands.

* * *

The renewal began at dusk, when the first stars woke.

There was no liturgy. There had never been a liturgy; that was the whole point, the truth Ivo had carried up from the drowned chapel and would carry to her grave: the covenant was always meant to be held by many hands, never one. So there was only a circle, and a passing, and a song.

Vess began it, as the envoy had begun it a thousand years ago at the well's rim. The moth-voice was not sound so much as light given language, and it moved around the circle from hand to hand — valley grandmother to Brackwater bell-founder to gray-coated warden to starlit courtier to Sef, who sang her part rough and off-key and absolutely without shame, because Sef had decided that anything worth doing was worth doing badly if the alternative was letting fear choose for you.

Ivo stood at the circle's heart, because someone had to stand at the center, and she had chosen forever, or die trying, and so far she was managing neither. The covenant passed through her like water through a channel — not hers, never hers, only trusted to her — and out again, into the joined hands all around, and up.

Above the valley, the stars leaned closer.

It never got old. That was the thing she'd never known how to tell anyone: that the exaltation did not fade into routine. Every solstice the sky came down a little way to meet the earth, and every solstice the earth reached up, and in the meeting — brief, warm, ordinary as a hearthfire — the two realms remembered each other. Wren stood at the circle's edge with her mouth open, seeing it for the first time, and Ivo watched her and saw herself at sixteen, feeling magic flood in for the first time, terror and exhilaration entwined, resenting a world she was about to lose and learning, slowly, that losing was not the only story.

The song ended. The circle broke into noise and bread and crying children and laughing grandmothers, and the stars settled overhead like a congregation taking its seats.

Orla found her by the cider barrel. "Warden recruits ask about you," she said. "The young ones. They want to know if the stories are true — that you refused the Crown's power when you could have taken it."

"What do you tell them?"

"That power shared is the only power that doesn't rot." Orla turned her cup in her scarred hands. "They never believe me. Then they watch you pass the bread around and take the smallest piece, and they start to believe me."

Ivo laughed. Across the field, Jude was showing the map to anyone who'd stand still, and Sef was teaching valley children a game involving stolen hats that bore an uncanny resemblance to her own biography, and Tam stood at the bronze circle ringing the renewal-bell with one soft strike for every keeper present, deaf to all of it and hearing, Tam said, everything that mattered through the soles of her feet.

One year. One year since the well. Since the Warden's dust. Since the dead star climbed home.

Justice, it turned out, was not a thing you won. It was a thing you *kept* — daily, hourly, in bread passed around and apples coaxed instead of commanded and gates left open on purpose.

* * *

She found Wren after full dark, sitting alone on the cottage step, looking up.

The girl didn't startle when Ivo sat down beside her. Progress. Six days ago she'd have bolted.

"Did you feel it?" Ivo asked.

"I felt—" Wren shook her head. "It went through my hands. Like the whole sky shook my hand. Like it *knew* me." She looked down at her palms, turned them over, turned them back. "Is it always like that?"

"No. Sometimes it's apples rotting. Most of magic is apples." Ivo pulled her cloak tighter against the night chill. Above them the star-court made its slow rounds, and somewhere among the drifting lights, she was nearly certain, a lantern flickered in greeting. "But yes. At the solstices. Always like that."

Wren was quiet a while. Then, in a voice carefully stripped of wanting — Ivo knew that voice, had used that voice, asking Maren for the same thing — she said, "You said a year. For the lesson. To mean it."

"I did."

"So if someone wanted — hypothetically — to start learning. Tonight. Or tomorrow. Whenever you—" She gave up, jaw tight, eyes bright and furious with hope. "Would you take an apprentice? Is that a thing you do?"

Ivo looked at her for a long moment. At the hunched shoulders and the defiant chin and the flinch barely healed and the hunger underneath it, so much like her own it ached.

"Maren never finished my first lesson," she said. "She died holding a door. So I'll finish it for both of us, and then you'll finish it for someone else, someday, and that's the covenant too. Bigger than any circle of stones." She stood, and held out her hand, palm up, the way you offer trust instead of command. "Come inside, apprentice. First lesson starts now. And bring the rosemary — we're going to sit with it all night until it tells us what it wants, and you're going to hate every minute."

Wren took her hand. Her grip was fierce, and frightened, and trusting.

Inside the cottage, on the worktable, the last of the solstice light lay pooled like honey — and in the middle of it, where no one had left anything, a single apple sat gleaming. Fresh. Unrotted. Impossible.

Wren stared at it. "Did you—"

"No," Ivo said slowly, and felt the night outside lean in, attentive, listening — the way the world sometimes did now, when something old and patient had decided to knock. "Did you?"

The girl's eyes went wide. "I wasn't even *touching* it."

Outside, far up past the gate, one star detached itself from the procession and began, unhurried and deliberate, to descend toward the valley floor.
