## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

There were three hundred and eleven ways through Thornfell, and Xanthe Underhill knew every one of them the way other people knew their own hands.

They did not count them aloud, of course. Counting them aloud was how guides got hanged. But on nights like this one—cold enough that the moss on the standing stones had gone stiff as old candle wax, dark enough that even the moon seemed to be minding its own business—Xanthe counted anyway, silently, the way a miser counts coins in a locked room. Three hundred and eleven roads. Nine years of debt. Four thousand, two hundred and six days since the Ember Temple had taken in a starving child with an empty stomach and a dead family's name, and put a ledger line beside it.

*Underhill, Xanthe. One life. Value to be determined.*

The caravan behind them was twelve wagons deep, carrying salt, iron nails, and—according to the manifest Xanthe was not supposed to have read—one very nervous man who claimed to be a wool merchant but whose boots were worth more than his entire fictional flock. Smugglers, all of them, though nobody used that word. They were *independent traders*. They were *logistics specialists*. Once, memorably, they had been described by a captured colleague as "agricultural improvisers," which had made even the magistrate smile before he fined him.

"Guide." The whisper came from the lead wagon, where Doss Halloway sat wrapped in three coats despite the fact that he generated heat like a small forge. "You certain about this cut? The Widow's Teeth don't look passable from here."

"The Teeth are passable," Xanthe said. "It's the road around them that isn't. The wardens moved their checkpoint two miles east last week. New protocol. Northern state's been rotating patrols at dusk because someone tipped them off about the salt runs."

Doss absorbed this with the expression of a man doing arithmetic he didn't like. "And how would you know that?"

"Because I'm also a scribe," Xanthe said. "At the Ember Temple. I file the warden reports."

A pause. Somewhere behind them, a wagon axle groaned.

"You *file* the reports of the men hunting us?"

"I file them neatly," Xanthe said. "In triplicate. With correct margins. Nobody suspects the person who cares about margins."

This was, Xanthe reflected, not entirely a joke, which was the problem with most of their jokes. By day they sat in the temple scriptorium with ink-stained fingers and an obedient posture, copying wardens' patrol logs into the archive because Vaelor's law required the temples to keep records of border enforcement, and because the Ember Temple required Xanthe to be useful, and usefulness was the currency in which debts were paid. By night they walked those same logs out the back gate in their head and sold the gaps between the patrols to people like Doss Halloway.

It was, technically, theft. It was also, technically, the only way Xanthe would ever clear four thousand days of debt before dying of old age at the copy desk. The temple paid a scribe's wage; the roads paid a guide's fee; and the difference went into the ledger, grain by grain, like sand filling an hourglass that had been built slightly too large as a joke by some long-dead abbot.

Three hundred and eleven ways through Thornfell. At current rate of repayment: seven more years.

Xanthe put the thought away where they kept it, in the same inner pocket where they kept their mother's voice and the smell of the house that no longer existed, and turned back to the work. "Single file past the second stone. No lanterns until the dip. And if anyone asks, we're pilgrims. Sad ones. Everyone believes sad pilgrims."

"Pilgrims carrying four tons of salt?"

"Pilgrims," Xanthe said, "who wept so much the salt got mixed in with our tears. It's very moving. Wardens hate crying pilgrims. They never know where to look."

Doss snorted, and the caravan moved, and for two hours there was only the work—the good part of the night, the part where Xanthe's mind went quiet and their feet did the thinking. Left at the leaning birch. Down through the dip where the stream ran loud enough to drown wheel-sound. Along the spine of rock the locals called the Old Man's Rib, where the drop on the right side was fatal and the view on the left side was almost worth it: Thornfell rolling away in folds of black pine, and beyond it, faint as breath on glass, the lights of the lowland farms.

Xanthe always stopped for exactly ten seconds at the Rib. Not for the view. For the arithmetic of it—a whole country asleep down there, and none of it knew Xanthe Underhill existed. Most people would have found that sad. Xanthe had decided, over nine years, to find it efficient. Invisibility was a skill. It kept you fed. It kept you uncaught.

It did not, however, keep you warm at two in the morning, and it did nothing whatsoever for the specific ache that arrived whenever the caravan crested the last hill and Xanthe saw, far off across the fields, the lights of the Ember Temple—seven lamps along the outer wall, each one tended by hand, each one burning the temple's own consecrated oil that smelled faintly of cedar and cost more per drop than Xanthe's monthly wage.

Seven lamps. Six lamplighters.

One Jude.

* * *

The temple gates took payment in coin or in silence, and Xanthe always paid in silence, slipping through the postern after the caravan dispersed, walking the long gallery with wet boots they'd hear about from Brother Cassian in the morning. The lamps came first. That was the rule Xanthe had set for themselves years ago and never broken: before sleep, before food, before the ledger, walk the wall and see the lamps.

Tonight the third lamp was guttering.

Xanthe found Jude already there, of course. Jude was always already there—it was one of the small mysteries of the temple, like how the kitchen cat opened doors, or how Brother Cassian could detect a misfiled document through two stone walls. Jude stood under the lamp bracket with a trimming knife and a cloth, and the light came down and caught him the way light always did, turning his ordinary face into something Xanthe had to look at sideways, the way you look at the sun.

"You're late," Jude said, without turning around.

"I'm never late. I'm temporally creative."

"You're soaked." Jude did turn then, and frowned at Xanthe's boots, and then—at Xanthe, briefly, in a way that lasted half a heartbeat longer than frowning at boots required. "Cassian will write you up."

"Cassian will glare at me with the fury of a thousand misaligned columns, and I will apologize, and he will forgive me because I am the only scribe alive who can read Prior Elowen's handwriting, and he knows it, and he hates that he knows it."

Jude laughed. He had a quiet laugh, more breath than sound, as if he'd agreed at some point not to take up too much space in the world—and Xanthe, who took up as little space as possible professionally, found this unbearable and wonderful in equal measure.

"Bad run?" Jude asked.

"Good run. Bad company. Doss Halloway asked if the Widow's Teeth were passable while standing in front of the Widow's Teeth." Xanthe leaned against the wall, close enough to feel the lamp's warmth, far enough to pretend otherwise. Below them the fields went on being asleep. Above them the sky was thick with cloud, no stars, just a bruised grey ceiling pressing down. "How was your week?"

"Quiet." Jude trimmed the wick with two precise strokes. The flame stood up straighter, taller, warmer. "Mostly I've been reading the founding covenant. The real one, from the archive, not the copy."

"You're allowed to read the founding covenant?"

"I'm allowed to dust the shelf it lives on," Jude said, with the ghost of a smile. "Reading is a side effect of proximity. You of all people should understand that."

Xanthe did understand that. It was practically their biography. "Anything interesting in it? Besides the usual 'and the land shall keep its people and the people shall keep the land' bit?"

For a moment Jude said nothing. His thumb moved along the handle of the trimming knife, back and forth, a habit Xanthe had catalogued long ago: Jude's tell, the one signpost in an otherwise unnavigable face.

"It says the land keeps the people," Jude said finally. "Not the crown. Not the borders. The land. I'd never noticed that before. Everyone quotes it wrong. Even the wardens' oaths get it wrong." He looked up at the lamp instead of at Xanthe, which made it easier for both of them. "Funny what you find when you actually read something. Instead of just filing it."

"That," said Xanthe, "is dangerously close to philosophy, and Brother Cassian will have you scrubbing the reliquary floor."

"He can join the queue." Jude reached up and cupped the flame with one hand—not smothering it, just steadying it, the way you'd steady a bird—and looked at Xanthe over the small golden roof of his palm. "Xanthe."

Nobody at the temple called them Xanthe. It was *Underhill*, or *scribe*, or, from Cassian, a disapproving silence shaped vaguely like their name. Hearing it now, warm and plain, did something unfair to Xanthe's chest.

"Yes?"

Jude opened his mouth. Closed it. The flame flickered in the draft off the fields, and whatever had been on his face folded itself away, neat as a letter returned to its envelope.

"Nothing," he said. "Safe roads tomorrow. Sleep well."

He gathered his tools and walked the wall toward the fourth lamp, and Xanthe stood there a moment longer than necessary, telling themselves the ache in their chest was just tiredness, filing it under *later*, under *things owed*, under the great ledger of small debts that never quite closed.

* * *

Sleep came late and left early. Xanthe was at the copy desk by dawn, quill moving, margins true, transcribing the northern state's new patrol rotations while outside the scriptorium window the sky did something it had never done before in nine years of mornings.

It stayed dark.

Not storm-dark. Not eclipse-dark. The sun rose on schedule—you could see it, a pale disc behind thin cloud—but the light it gave was wrong, thinned, as if the whole sky had been washed in ash water. And when Xanthe stepped out at midmorning to stretch their ink-stiff fingers, they heard the temple bells ringing wrong: not the hour, not the alarm, but the old sequence, the one from the founding days that nobody living had ever heard rung except once a year as a historical curiosity.

*Vaelor is fallen,* that sequence meant. *Vaelor is fallen. Gather.*

Brother Cassian appeared in the doorway with a face like spilled ink, holding a dispatch he'd evidently read four times hoping it would improve.

"Underhill," he said. "The wards on the border fell last night. All of them. Every star in the sky has gone out, and the capital—" He stopped, steadied himself. "The capital burned its own archives at dawn. Before anyone else could take them."

Xanthe stood very still. In their head, without permission, three hundred and eleven roads lit themselves up, every ford and checkpoint and blind cut through Thornfell—maps of a country that, as of this morning, officially did not exist.

Somewhere out on the wall, Xanthe thought absurdly, Jude would be lighting lamps that no longer marked any nation at all.

Then they remembered the founding covenant, quoted wrong by everyone, and the look on Jude's face over his cupped hand, the words swallowed, the *nothing* — and Xanthe began, for the first time in nine years, to run.
