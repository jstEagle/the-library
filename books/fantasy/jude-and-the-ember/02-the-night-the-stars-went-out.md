## 2. The Night the Stars Went Out

Xanthe Underhill had a rule about the sky: you checked it the way a merchant checks his scales—quickly, professionally, and without sentiment.

So when they stepped out of the Widow's Rest inn at half past midnight to meet the caravan, the first thing Xanthe did was look up. Habit of nine years. The stars were how you navigated Thornfell when the fog came down off the ridges like spilled milk; the stars were how you knew what hour it was when every clock in Vaelor was owned by someone who might report you; the stars were, in a way that Xanthe had never said aloud to anyone, the only part of the country that had never once asked them for money.

The sky was empty.

Not clouded. Not fogged. *Empty*—the particular, obscene emptiness of a purse turned inside out. Xanthe stood in the inn yard with their mouth open for a full ten seconds, which by their own accounting was eight seconds longer than they had ever wasted on anything, including the time Brannic Fell had tried to pay them in goats.

"Stars are out," said Doss, the caravan master, coming up behind them with a lantern he did not need, because there was nothing left in the heavens to need it against.

"The stars are *not* out," Xanthe said. "That's rather the point."

He squinted up. He was a big man, Doss, built like a grain sack with opinions, and it took him a while. "Well," he said at last, in the tone of a man deciding not to be troubled, "more room for cargo, I suppose."

"Doss." Xanthe turned. "Look at me. Look at my face. Do I look like a person who jokes about navigation?"

"You look like a person who hasn't slept since Larksfall," Doss admitted. "But that's most nights."

It was true, and Xanthe resented it. Nine years of walking smuggler roads by night and copying temple ledgers by day had given them a face like a well-used map: creased along reliable lines, faded in places, annotated with small ink stains of exhaustion under both eyes. They were twenty-six years old and felt like a document due for archiving.

And yet—the sky. Xanthe looked up again and felt something they had no ledger column for. The Thornfell wards ran along the whole northern reach of Vaelor, old as the founding covenant, humming so quietly that you stopped hearing them the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat. Every smuggler knew them the way fish know water: the wards bent light, muddled compasses, made the border roads loop back on themselves unless you knew the true names of the turns. Xanthe had spent nine years learning those turns because the Ember Temple held their family's debt-bond, and the Temple's price was simple: *map what cannot be mapped, and mind those who walk it.*

The wards were also, everyone assumed, the reason the stars over Thornfell always seemed slightly dimmer, slightly *elsewhere*, as if viewed through smoked glass.

Now the glass was gone. And so was everything behind it.

"We're not going out tonight," Xanthe said.

Doss blinked. "You've never once refused a run. Rain, snow, that business with the bear—"

"The *sky is wrong*, Doss. When the sky is wrong, the road is wrong, and when the road is wrong, I am not responsible for where your cargo ends up, which historically has been places cargo should not be." Xanthe was already doing arithmetic, though—arithmetic of a different kind. The checkpoints. The wardens' posts along the salt road. If the wards had fallen, then the border between Vaelor and the three hungry states beyond it was not merely open. It was *gone*. There was no line anymore. There was just geography, sitting there, undefended, like an unattended purse.

"I'll pay the rest-day fee myself," Xanthe added, and watched Doss's principles reorganize themselves instantly around this new information.

* * *

The trouble with being the person who knows all the roads is that when the world ends, everyone wants to know the roads.

By dawn, the Widow's Rest yard looked like a market day gone feral. Refugees from the valley farms, carts stacked with chickens and grandmothers. Smugglers arguing about whether the collapse meant prices went up or whether property itself had stopped meaning anything (a philosophical debate several of them settled by loading extra cargo). A boy of maybe ten tugging Xanthe's sleeve to ask if the stars would come back, and Xanthe, who lied fluently to wardens and debt collectors and occasionally to themselves, found they could not produce a single word for this child.

Because here was the thing Xanthe understood before almost anyone else in that yard, the thing that sat in their chest like a swallowed stone:

They carried Vaelor in their head. Every turn of Thornfell, every hidden ford, every warden post and its rotation schedule, every smuggler's shortcut and temple path—all of it, nine years of it, mapped in memory with a scribe's precision. It had been their burden and, in a strange way they'd never admit at lamp-lighting hour, their pride. *I am the person who knows this country.*

And now the country had been unmade. Word came down the valley with the morning riders: the capital had burned its own archives. All of them. The registry of deeds, the rolls of citizenship, the great census ledgers, the treaties—every record that proved Vaelor existed had been fed to fires by Vaelor's own clerks, on the crown's last order, so that the three neighboring powers could seize land but not *legitimacy*. You cannot claim a kingdom's borders if the kingdom has erased its own proof of them.

Which meant, Xanthe realized, standing very still in a chaotic inn yard while a chicken escaped somewhere behind them, that the maps in their head now described a fiction. A story. A place that officially, legally, treaty-ably *did not exist*. Their nine years of debt-work—the whole crushing weight of the Underhill obligation—had been paid in service to a country that had just deleted itself.

"That's either the saddest thing I've ever heard," muttered Xanthe aloud, "or the funniest, and I genuinely can't tell which."

A passing refugee gave them a wide berth.

They started for the temple at noon. On foot, because every horse in the valley had been bought, stolen, or eaten. The walk that usually took two days took one and a half, because Xanthe knew the ways that didn't have checkpoints—and then, because the world was ending, they walked straight through the ones that did, to see.

The first checkpoint, at Miller's Bend, was simply abandoned. The warden hut stood open, tea still warm in its pot, as if the entire concept of *northern border authority* had evaporated mid-sip. The second, at the Stone Gate, was worse: three different flags already planted in the mud, the black-and-gold of Norvane, the green sash of the Ostrin Compact, and something new and red that Xanthe didn't recognize, all within twenty feet of each other, the soldiers eyeing one another with the wary courtesy of dogs deciding whether today was a fighting day or a sharing day.

A Norvane officer stopped Xanthe on the road. "Papers."

"Papers for what?" Xanthe asked, honestly curious.

"For—" The officer faltered. It was a remarkable thing to watch, a professional confusion happening in real time. "For passage. Through. This territory."

"Whose territory?"

The officer's jaw worked. Behind him, a Compact soldier snorted. Somewhere, the unrecognized red flag fluttered with the smugness of a flag that had arrived early to a bankruptcy auction.

"That's what we're establishing," the officer said finally, with dignity, and waved Xanthe through, presumably because interrogating them would require admitting the question had no answer.

Xanthe walked on and felt the vertigo properly then—not in the stomach but somewhere deeper, in the ledger-part of the soul. *Every road I know is a road to nowhere now.* They thought of the debt-bond locked in the temple archive, listing the Underhill family's obligations in neat copperplate: debts owed to the Ember Temple of Vaelor. Owed to whom, exactly? To a temple in a country that had burned its own name? Could you default to a ghost? Was there a form for that? Knowing clerks, there was probably a form, and it was burning too.

They laughed, once, alone on the road, and it came out slightly unhinged, and they decided not to examine that.

* * *

They reached the Ember Temple at dusk—or what passed for dusk now, a colorless dimming, the sky like unwashed linen. The temple lamps were being lit anyway. That was the first thing Xanthe saw from the ridge: the long colonnade, and the small steady flames coming alive one by one down its length, stubborn as punctuation.

Jude lit them. Of course Jude lit them. Xanthe watched from the ridge for a moment longer than strictly necessary—watched the lamplighter move pole in hand from bracket to bracket, unhurried, as though the apocalypse were a scheduling matter and the lamps had been booked in advance.

There was a version of Xanthe that had rehearsed conversations with Jude for nine years. None of those rehearsals had covered "the stars have been stolen and our country has been un-invented, please advise." Xanthe descended the hill anyway, because the temple was the closest thing to an address they had ever had, and because—though they'd have chewed off their own arm before saying it—if the world was ending, they wanted to hear it standing next to Jude.

The colonnade smelled of lamp oil and cold stone. Jude saw them coming and paused, pole resting against their shoulder, and even in the bad light Xanthe could see that the lamplighter's face was doing something it almost never did: showing fear.

"You came back," Jude said. Quiet, as always. Like the words cost coin.

"It's my temple. I owe it—" Xanthe began, automatically, the reflex of nine years, and then stopped, because the sentence had collapsed mid-formation. *Owed to what?*

"You heard, then." Jude looked up at the empty sky. Up close, in lamplight, their eyes were doing the arithmetic too. "About the archives."

"Burned. All of them. The capital cooked its own memory." Xanthe leaned against a pillar, trying to be casual and knowing they'd missed casual by a wide margin. "I walked past three armies today arguing over the corpse. Very embarrassing for everyone involved. One of them has a flag I've never seen. Where do you even *get* a flag on short notice?"

"Xanthe."

And that—just the name, just their name said in that low careful voice—shut them up entirely. Jude set the lighting pole down. Reached into the cage of the nearest lamp. And lifted out the flame.

Not the wick. The *flame*. It came away in Jude's cupped hands like something tame, like a bird that had decided, after long consideration, to be held, and it was wrong in every way a fire could be wrong—it cast no heat Xanthe could feel, and its light fell upward, and it was the only warm-colored thing in the whole drained sky.

"Everyone's asking what happens now," Jude said. "The wardens, the states, the clerks. Everyone's asking who owns the ashes." The little fire turned in their palms. "Nobody's asking what Vaelor was *before* it was records. Before the ledgers. What it was when it was only a promise people made to each other, written down exactly once."

Xanthe's mouth had gone dry. "Written down where?"

Jude looked at them. Held the light between them like a confession.

"In here," Jude said. "And I need you to take me somewhere tonight, Xanthe. Because I don't know the roads. But you do."
