## 3. What the Lamplighter Carries

The temple had never been dark before.

Jude had lit its lamps for eleven years—every wick, every sconce, every guttering votive along the colonnade—and in all that time the Ember Temple had held a stubborn, holy glow, the kind of light that made even the high scribes look briefly kind. Now the great hearth was cold ash. The sanctuary lamps hung dead in their chains like hanged men. And Jude walked the corridors with an unlit lantern and the terrible knowledge that the only fire left in Vaelor was the one they were carrying against their ribs.

It weighed nothing. That was the horror of it. A fragment of the founding covenant—the spark that had drawn the first border of Vaelor in light when there had been nothing else but marsh and argument—and it weighed less than a coin. It could have lived in a thimble. It had chosen to live in Jude's lantern instead, coiled in the cold glass like a sleeping ember of ordinary coal, and it had chosen Jude, of all people, because Jude had once, as a child, cupped their hands around a dying votive during a storm and breathed on it until it caught.

The temple records said the covenant recognized tenderness. Jude had thought that was poetry. It turned out to be policy.

They found Xanthe in the map room, which was not really a map room anymore; it was a room where maps went to be mourned. Every chart of Thornfell, every survey of the salt roads, every careful inked line of a country that three neighboring states had declared legally nonexistent—all of it lay heaped on the floor, because the wardens had come through two days ago and burned the archive, and what couldn't be burned had been torn. Xanthe stood in the middle of the wreckage with a single scorched corner of parchment in their hand, turning it over as if it might confess to being something else.

"You're supposed to be resting," Jude said from the doorway. "You crossed half of Thornfell in one night."

"So did you," Xanthe said, without looking up. "I saw your lamp go out. I've never seen your lamp go out." They finally looked up, and their face did the thing it always did when they were frightened and refusing to say so: it became very competent. "The hearth's cold. That's not possible. The hearth has been burning since before the Underhills owed anyone anything."

"I know."

"Jude." Xanthe set down the scrap of map with exaggerated care, the way one sets down a thing that might otherwise be thrown. "Tell me the hearth is cold for a boring reason."

Jude came into the room and set the lantern on the table between them, and for a long moment neither of them looked at it. Outside, somewhere beyond the ruined checkpoints, a warden's drum was beating the slow count that the northern state used to mark a territory under new administration. One-two-three-four. As if counting would make it theirs.

"There's a story," Jude said, "that when Vaelor was founded, the first queen didn't draw the border. She asked the land to agree to her, and the land said yes, and the yes made a light. And the light was kept here." They touched the lantern's cold glass. "Everyone thinks the hearth is the keeping place. The hearth is just the chimney. This is the fire."

Xanthe stared at the lantern. Then at Jude. Then at the lantern again, with the expression of a person doing sums they don't want the answer to.

"That's a fragment of the covenant," Xanthe said. "That's the reason three armies are drawing lines through my head right now. Everyone who ever memorized a smuggler road is either evidence or a target, and you're telling me the actual—" they gestured, helplessly, at everything— "*yes* has been sitting in your lamp this whole time. While I mapped roads past it. For nine years."

"It liked the routes," Jude said, and heard their own voice crack into something almost like laughter, because terror does that. "When you'd come back from a crossing and stand under the colonnade lamps, it would—it brightened. I thought it was just glad you were home. I didn't know it was learning the country through you. Through everyone. It's made of everyone. That's what a covenant *is*, Xanthe, it isn't a treasure, it's an agreement, and the agreement needs—"

They stopped. Their hands were shaking. Xanthe noticed, because Xanthe noticed everything about Jude and had spent nine years pretending not to, and something in their competent face finally gave way.

"Sit down," Xanthe said.

"I can't. There's very little time."

"There's always very little time. Sit down anyway." And when Jude sat, Xanthe sat too, across the table, close enough that the dead lantern sat between them like a chaperone. "Now. From the beginning. Slowly. I'm going to be the guide, so I need the whole route."

* * *

So Jude told it: the moon would complete its thirteenth turn since the wards fell—or rather, since the night the stars went out, though the scholars argued about whether the sky or the country had failed first. At the fullness of that turn, the Door of Thirteen Turns, in the far western fells where no army bothered to look, would stand open for one night. Not open out. Nothing opened out of Vaelor anymore; the borders were sealed with other countries' law. But the door was old. Older than the three flags. It remembered the original agreement, and if the ember reached it, the ember could do what the ember had done at the beginning: ask the land to agree, and make a border—not around territory this time, but around whoever stood inside the light. A country made of people. Carried out through a door built for exactly this, by founders who had apparently assumed, with grim foresight, that someday someone might need to take Vaelor with them.

"And the wardens?" Xanthe asked quietly.

"All three want it. The north wants it because a covenant legitimizes conquest retroactively—they'd call themselves heirs instead of occupiers. The south wants it broken, because a living covenant makes their claim void. The east—" Jude hesitated. "The east wants it whole. They think it will choose them."

"Will it?"

"It doesn't choose kingdoms. It chose me because I breathed on a candle when I was seven." Jude laughed again, badly. "It chooses tenderness, apparently. None of the three flags have any."

Xanthe was silent for a while. Outside, the drum kept its patient count. When they spoke again, their voice had gone carefully flat, the voice they used for quoting toll schedules.

"You're asking me to guide you across Thornfell. In winter. Past three occupation forces, all hunting a light that weighs nothing and can't be hidden from anyone who knows what to look for. To a door at the far edge of the map, in thirteen turns of the moon." They looked up. "You're asking me to break my debt-bond. If the temple finds out I helped you, the bond transfers to whatever state holds the temple. I'd be—"

"I know what you'd be." Jude's throat closed. This was the part they had rehearsed for two days, walking dark corridors, and every rehearsal ended the same way: with the truth coming out sideways, disguised as logistics, because Jude had never in eleven years managed to say the plain thing to Xanthe's face. "You'd be forfeit. So I'm not asking. I'm telling you the route, and you can refuse, and I'll find another guide, and you'll be safe, and I want you to know—I need you to know—that I'm not asking because you owe the temple. I'm asking because—"

Because when you come home from a crossing, the first thing I do is check the colonnade to see if your silhouette is on the road yet. Because I have relit the same votive four hundred times and every time it was a prayer with your name folded inside it where the flame couldn't read it. Because the covenant chose me for one small kindness and I have been saving all the rest of mine for you for nine years—

"—because you're the only person alive who knows all three states' protocols *and* the salt passes *and* where the Door actually is," Jude finished. "And because I trust you more than I have ever trusted anything, including this." They touched the lantern. "There. That's the whole petition. Refuse, and I'll never mention it again."

The silence went on so long that Jude began composing apologies. Then Xanthe reached across the table and put their hand flat on the lantern's cold glass, the way one takes an oath, or checks a fever.

"Thirteen turns," Xanthe said. "First turn starts tomorrow night. We leave before dawn, ahead of it." A pause, and then, quieter, in the voice Jude had heard only once before—years ago, through a colonnade, overheard and hoarded ever since: "You should have asked me years ago, Jude. You could have asked me anything, years ago."

The lantern, between their hands, warmed.

Just slightly. Just enough that both of them felt it, and both of them pretended, for the sake of nine years of careful silence, that they hadn't.

Outside, the northern drum stopped mid-count. In the new quiet, faint and wrong, came the sound of boots in the temple courtyard—too many, moving in the crisp paired rhythm of a patrol that had stopped being a patrol and become a search.

Xanthe blew out their breath slowly. "Well," they said. "That's turn one starting early."
