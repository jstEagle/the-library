## 4. Three Flags on One Road

The trouble with being invisible for nine years, Xanthe reflected as the lantern-light swept toward them through the birch trees, was that you started to believe it.

They had been walking for two days since the temple. Two days of Jude's quiet footsteps beside them, two days of the ember riding in its lantern like a captive sunrise, two days of Xanthe reciting roads in their head the way other people said prayers. The old salt road out of Thornfell was the safest of the three routes Xanthe had considered — which, in the current state of the world, meant merely that nothing had gone wrong yet.

The lantern-light sweeping toward them through the birches was about to correct that assumption.

"Off the road," Xanthe breathed, and pulled Jude into the bracken.

But the light stopped moving. It hung there, patient as a debt collector, and then a voice called out — flat, official, and wearing a northern accent like a uniform:

"Travelers. You are observed. Remain where you are."

"They knew we were here," Jude whispered. "How did they—"

"The birds," Xanthe muttered. "Or the mud. Or my face, which apparently has *evidence written on it now*." They stood up, hands visible, heart hammering a rhythm they hoped didn't show. "Stay behind me. And whatever happens — the lantern stays dark."

Jude's hand closed over the lantern's shutter without argument. That was one mercy. Jude trusted them. It was the only thing keeping Xanthe upright, if they were honest, and they tried not to be honest with themselves very often. Honesty was expensive.

Three wardens stepped into the road. Northern issue: grey coats, silver cord at the shoulder, the stag-and-scales sigil of the Provisional Authority of Vaelor stamped on their satchels. The lead warden was young, maybe twenty-five, with the particular exhaustion of someone who had joined an occupation expecting glory and received paperwork instead.

"Name," he said.

Xanthe had spent nine years learning exactly how to answer that question, depending on who asked it. For northern patrols: give your name plainly, look bored, carry documentation of transit under Article Twelve of the Interim Administration Act. Northerners ran on paper the way temples ran on incense.

"Underhill," Xanthe said. "Road surveyor, formerly of the Ember Temple cartography office." They produced the travel writ they'd kept folded against their ribs since the night the stars went out — a small miracle of bureaucracy, since every archive in Vaelor had burned, but the checkpoint at Marrow Bridge had stamped it anyway, because checkpoints stamp things; it is their whole personality. "Transit pass, Article Twelve. I'm mapping road conditions for the resettlement census."

The warden took the paper. Squinted at it. And here Xanthe made their first mistake, which was relaxing.

Because the warden said: "There is no resettlement census."

*Ah,* thought Xanthe, with the distant clarity of a person watching a bridge collapse in slow motion. *Right. I invented that this morning.*

"There isn't?" Xanthe said. "Then I've been walking very far to deliver maps to no one, which would be embarrassing, so let's both agree I misremembered."

The second warden snorted. The lead warden did not.

"You're Underhill," he repeated. "The Underhill? The one who mapped Thornfell?"

And there it was. The thing Xanthe had dreaded since the sky went black: reputation. Nine years of careful anonymity, of being *the temple scribe, you know, the quiet one*, and it had all been quietly filed away somewhere by someone, waiting for the day when knowing every smuggler road in a vanished country became valuable instead of shameful.

"I've walked some roads," Xanthe said modestly.

"He drew half of them," Jude said helpfully, from behind.

Xanthe closed their eyes briefly. *Thank you, love. Truly. Your support sustains me.*

"The Provisional Authority requires your services," the lead warden said, and something in his tone shifted from *bureaucrat* to *salesman*, which was worse. "You will accompany us to Fort Halloway. There are documents requiring expert annotation. Roads, passes, border approaches. In return, your transit status will be regularized. Citizenship processing. Full standing under the Protectorate."

"Citizenship," Xanthe said carefully, "of what? Last I heard, Vaelor doesn't legally exist."

"Vaelor exists," the warden said, with the sudden fervor of a man quoting scripture he'd only just memorized. "It exists because we say it does. That is what sovereignty *is*. But a claim needs witnesses. Records. Maps drawn by hands that walked the ground before the Interregnum. Do you understand what I'm telling you, surveyor? Whoever holds the true map of Vaelor holds the proof of it. The south has their treaties. The east has their garrisons. We intend to have the *geography*."

Behind Xanthe, Jude went very still. Xanthe felt it rather than saw it — a change in the air, the way a room changes when someone stops breathing.

"And the ember," Xanthe said lightly, testing. "I hear the north is looking for one of those too."

Something flickered across the young warden's face — hunger, quickly buried under procedure, but not buried deep enough. "Rumors," he said. "Fragments of the founding covenant. If such a thing existed, it would be the difference between annexation and recognition. Every court in three kingdoms knows it. Why do you ask?"

"No reason," said Xanthe. "I collect rumors. Hobby of mine. Very cheap."

* * *

They talked their way past him. It took an hour, two forged initials, one heartfelt speech about the sanctity of Article Twelve (which Xanthe invented entirely, with great conviction), and a bribe of the good tea from their pack — northern wardens, it turned out, had been cut off from decent imports since the borders sealed, and patriotism wilts fast without caffeine. The lead warden let them go with a warning and a requisition form, because even the end of the world generates requisition forms.

But Xanthe didn't breathe properly again until the birches had swallowed the road behind them and the sun had dropped low enough to turn everything amber and forgiving.

"He knew my name," Xanthe said. They were crouched by a stream, refilling the waterskins, mostly so they had something to do with their shaking hands. "Not just my name. My *reputation*. The north keeps files on temple staff. Which means the south does too, and the east — the east probably has illustrations."

"You were magnificent," Jude said.

"I was a *liar* with excellent posture."

"Those have historically been the same thing." Jude sat down on a fallen log, set the lantern between their feet, and looked at Xanthe with that expression — the one that was equal parts worry and wonder, like watching weather approach a harvest. "He told you what the ember is worth. Did you hear it? Not a weapon. A *witness*. Proof that Vaelor was real, that its covenant was sworn and kept. Whoever carries it gets to decide what Vaelor becomes."

"Yes," Xanthe said. "Which is why three armies want it and why we should be moving faster than conversation speed."

"That's not what I mean." Jude turned the lantern slightly, and through the slats of the shutter the ember's glow lay across Xanthe's knees like spilled honey. "If it can prove Vaelor exists — really exists, in law, in memory — then it doesn't belong to any flag. It belongs to everyone who ever lit a lamp in it. Everyone who paid a debt to keep its doors open." A pause, delicate as a held breath. "Everyone like you."

Xanthe looked up sharply. Jude was smiling — small, crooked, infuriating.

"Don't," Xanthe said. "Don't go making me sound noble. I broke my back for that temple because I had to, not because—"

"You stayed nine years after the debt could've been bought down early," Jude said. "You never once left a traveler stranded on the high road, even the ones who couldn't pay. You memorized every refugee path through Thornfell 'for the work' when there wasn't any work in it. I mind the lamps, Xanthe. I see who comes home."

Xanthe found, to their considerable annoyance, that they had nothing to say to that. So they corked the waterskin with unnecessary force and stood up.

"We camp past the ridge," they said. "There's a shepherd's hollow — dry, hidden, one way in. And tomorrow we cross into the disputed strip, where the flags haven't decided whose dirt it is yet."

"Is that safer?"

"No. It's *confusing*, which is different, and sometimes better." Xanthe shouldered the pack. Then, quieter, because the evening made honesty cheap: "That warden offered me citizenship. Full standing. Papers. A country again, on paper. All I had to do was walk into his fort and start drawing."

"What did you feel?"

"Nothing," Xanthe said. "That's what frightened me. Nine years ago I'd have crawled to Fort Halloway on my knees. Tonight I just thought — *that's not mine to be given.*" They glanced back at Jude, at the lantern, at the small captive dawn sitting between the shepherd boy's boots. "Turns out I already gave myself away somewhere else. Must've missed the paperwork."

Jude's smile did something complicated and warm. "We'll file it later."

* * *

They heard the second patrol before they saw it — or rather, they heard what the second patrol had done to the first one.

At the ridge crest, in the last grey light, the northern warden's horse stood riderless by the roadside, cropping grass with the serene indifference of animals to human catastrophe. Thirty yards on, the lead warden himself sat against a milestone, alive but white-faced, holding a torn strip of grey cloth pressed to his arm. His two companions were nowhere. Their absence had a specific, organized quality to it.

Standing over him were four soldiers in eastern colors — oxblood coats, the triple-tower seal, bayonets fixed out of habit rather than need. And behind them, half-shadows among the trees, moved figures in southern grey-green with no insignia at all, because the southern states had never needed to announce themselves; they simply arrived, like damp.

All three flags. One road. One bleeding northerner between them.

"—told you," the northern warden was saying, voice cracking. "I *filed* the report. Surveyor named Underhill, southbound on the salt road, possible association with the covenant artifact, requesting reinforcement, and your lot sat on it for a *day*—"

"And now the artifact is a day further south," said the eastern officer pleasantly, "in territory we administer. Thank you for your diligence, sergeant. You may consider yourself relieved."

Xanthe drew Jude back off the ridgeline, slow, slow, into the bracken, and did not run until the darkness was thick enough to hide the shape of panic.

They ran anyway. Down the goat track, through the alder scrub, hearts and boots hammering, until the hollow opened before them — dry, walled with stone, one way in, exactly as promised. Xanthe barred it with a deadfall and stood there gasping, and Jude stood beside them, and for a long moment neither of them spoke.

"They're not chasing us," Xanthe said finally. "Did you notice? Nobody's chasing us. They don't have to. The north knows where we are, and now the east knows, and the south—" They laughed, a short ugly sound with no humor in it anywhere. "The south always knows. We're not running anymore, Jude. We're *being tracked*. Politely. At a distance. Because none of them wants to spill blood near the ember and crack it, or whatever it is these things do when you're careless with them."

"So what do we do?"

Xanthe slid down the wall to sit in the dark, and Jude sat beside them, and the lantern between them glowed faintly through its shutters like a secret trying to get out.

"For nine years I survived by being nobody," Xanthe said. "No name worth recording, no road worth guarding, no face worth remembering. That's over. I'm evidence now. I'm a *witness statement* with legs." They turned their head. In the ember's thin light, Jude's eyes were steady, and terrified, and trusting, and Xanthe thought: *well. If I must be evidence, let me testify well.* "So tomorrow we stop hiding from the patrols. Tomorrow we use them. Three flags on one road means three sets of orders, three chains of command, three ways to lie to each other — and I have spent nine years learning exactly how each of them lies."

"To the others," Jude said slowly.

"To everyone." Xanthe smiled, and it felt strange on their face, sharp and new. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow I teach you the eastern countersign, and you're going to be terrible at it, and I'm going to enjoy that enormously."

But sleep came late, and thin, and full of maps. And once, drifting at the edge of it, Xanthe heard Jude whisper to the lantern — soft, pleading, the way one speaks to something loved and frightened —

*"Hold on. We're almost there. Please hold on."*

And the ember, in the dark, pulsed once. Like a heartbeat answering. Like something counting down.
