## 5. The Debt Collector's Mercy

The Ember Temple had always smelled of tallow and old paper, but tonight it smelled of fear.

Jude noticed it as soon as they slipped in through the scullery door—the same door they had used for nine years of quiet lamplighting—and found the corridors lit not by their own careful hands but by every lamp in the building burning at once. The high scribe wanted to see everything. The high scribe always wanted to see everything; that was rather the point of High Scribe Verity Ombre, a woman built like a filing cabinet and about as easily moved.

Jude's lantern hung at their hip, heavier than any lantern had a right to be. They had taken to carrying it against their chest like a child who might wake crying.

*You are a lamplighter,* Jude told themselves, walking the corridor at the pace of a man going to trim wicks. *Lamplighters are furniture. Furniture is not arrested.*

The thought would have been more comforting if Xanthe hadn't been arrested-adjacent twice in the last three days, and if the whole country hadn't been legally dissolved like sugar in bad tea.

Verity's study was at the top of the scribes' stair, behind a door that had been re-hung three times because Verity believed doors should close with the sound of finality. Jude knocked. The sound that came back was "Enter," delivered in the tone of someone granting a small mercy and expecting gratitude.

Inside, Verity sat behind her desk with Xanthe's debt-bond laid out before her on a square of green felt, the way one might display a rare beetle. Nine years of Xanthe Underhill's life, reduced to vellum and wax and a column of figures so neat it looked embroidered.

"You've been difficult to find," Verity said.

"I light four hundred lamps a night," Jude said. "I'm exactly where I always am."

"And yet your lamp-room is empty, your bed is unslept-in, and Brother Cassian swears he saw you leaving the east postern with a satchel and a look of criminal purpose."

"Brother Cassian," Jude said, "has never once seen anything accurately. He thinks the compost heap is haunted."

"It may be." Verity folded her hands. "Sit down, Jude."

Jude sat. The chair was deliberately uncomfortable—Verity kept a set of them for interviews, upholstered in something that might have been horsehair or might have been regret.

"You know what has happened," Verity said. It was not a question. The stars had gone out; Vaelor had been erased from the map by men who owned the map; the temple stood now in a country that existed only in the memories of people standing inside it. "You know what the northern state is offering."

"I know they're offering legitimacy to whoever hands them something shiny," Jude said. "They're offering the same thing to everyone. It's very democratic of them."

Something flickered at the corner of Verity's mouth—not quite a smile, more the memory of one, filed away for later use. "The northern wardens came to me two days ago. They want the ember. They believe—I don't pretend to understand their theology—that a fragment of the founding covenant constitutes proof of succession. Whoever holds it inherits Vaelor's legal personhood. Borders, treaties, debts." Her eyes rested on Jude's lantern with the mild appetite of an accountant regarding an unexplained surplus. "Everything owed."

"Vaelor isn't an inheritance," Jude said. "It's people."

"People are portable. Documents are portable. Legitimacy travels beautifully." Verity drew a breath and, with visible effort, arrived at the point. "Here is my offer, and I will make it only once, so attend. Give me the ember. I will deliver it to the northern wardens myself, with all the ceremony and paperwork such a transfer deserves. In exchange—" she turned the debt-bond face-up, and her finger came down on the final line of figures, "—this is voided. Signed, sealed, witnessed. Xanthe Underhill walks out of this temple owing nothing to anyone. Free. Clean. A blank page."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere below, a lamp hissed.

Jude looked at the bond. Nine years. They had watched those years happen—watched Xanthe come in soaked from Thornfell rain, ink-stained, exhausted, counting what they owed in that flat voice that never complained because complaining wasn't efficient. Nine years of a person being spent like coin to cover a debt contracted before they could walk.

"They never asked you to save them," Verity said, and her voice did something almost gentle. "That's the cruelty of bonds, Jude. They don't require consent from anyone but the debtor. You could free them tonight. All it costs is a lantern."

*All it costs is a country*, Jude thought. Aloud, carefully: "And if I refuse?"

"Then the wardens search this temple themselves, and they are considerably less courteous than I am, and Xanthe's bond remains exactly as binding as it has ever been." Verity leaned back. "I am giving you mercy, child. Mine, and theirs. Mercy is rarely offered twice."

Jude thought about saying thank you. They thought about weeping, which would have been efficient, emotionally speaking, and useless in every other way. Mostly they thought about Xanthe's face when Jude had asked them—at three in the morning, in a doorway that smelled of rain—for rescue. Not demanded it. Asked. And Xanthe had said yes the way other people said good morning: immediately, without apparent consideration, as though it were obvious.

*I'm already evidence,* Xanthe had joked grimly. *Might as well be accomplices.*

"No," Jude said.

Verity sighed, the sigh of a woman whose filing system has encountered an unlabeled folder. "I confess I hoped you'd say that."

Jude blinked. "...You hoped I'd refuse?"

"I hoped you'd refuse *quickly*," Verity said, "because we haven't much time, and because I needed to know whether you were worth what I'm about to lose." She rose, crossed to the window, and checked the courtyard below with the brisk paranoia of long practice. Then she came back to the desk, and her voice dropped into a register Jude had never heard from her—one stripped of office, worn smooth by use.

"I was temple archivist when your grandmother carried that covenant fragment through the war, do you understand? Before you were born. I catalogued it. I shelved it. I wrote its accession number with my own hand, and then I watched men in better coats than mine decide it was a relic instead of a promise." She picked up the debt-bond between two fingers, distastefully, like something dead. "Now three empires want to make it a deed of ownership. And the only person I trust to carry it is a lamplighter who smells of lamp oil and bad decisions, accompanied by a smuggler-guide who owes this temple nine years of labor."

"The debt," Jude said slowly.

"Is the problem," Verity agreed. "Because while that bond exists, Xanthe Underhill belongs to the temple, and the temple's assets are subject to seizure by whichever state gets here first. The moment the northern wardens formalize their claim, they can demand you both. The bond isn't protection anymore. It's a leash, and they hold the other end."

"So void it."

"Oh, I intend to." Verity smiled, and it was the smile of a woman about to commit a crime with excellent penmanship. "Just not through the front entrance, so to speak. If I formally release Xanthe, it goes in the ledger, and the ledger gets audited, and auditors ask where the asset went. But if the bond were *lost*—misplaced in the chaos, perhaps destroyed when the archive wing caught fire last week, which it technically did, in a small and blameless way—then there is nothing to seize. Nothing to enforce. A debt no one can produce cannot be collected." She held it out. "Take it."

Jude didn't move. "You'd burn your own records. Your whole life is records."

"My whole life," Verity said, "is a temple dedicated to keeping a flame alive. Everything else is bookkeeping." She pressed the bond into Jude's hand and closed their fingers over it. "Go. Out the west cloister, past the bell rope, through the crypt—yes, the crypt, don't make that face, smugglers have used it for decades and Xanthe knows the route better than our own masons. There's a warden patrol on the north road within the hour. Be elsewhere."

"Why?" Jude asked. It came out smaller than intended. "Why help us? Why steal from yourself?"

Verity returned to her chair and settled into it, drawing a fresh sheet of paper toward herself, already composing whatever fiction would explain the next hour. Without looking up, she said:

"Because I have spent forty years writing down what people owe, Jude, and I have begun to suspect that no one ever wrote down what they're *worth*. Go and correct the imbalance."

* * *

The crypt smelled of stone and candle stubs and, faintly, of Xanthe—of rain and road dust and the particular cheap soap smugglers favored because it didn't smell like anything. Jude waited in the dark under the bell tower with the stolen bond inside their coat, over their heart, where it lay like a second lantern. Heavier. So much heavier.

Footsteps on the crypt stair. Jude's hand went to their knife before their brain reminded them that knives were for emergencies and this was merely the rest of their life arriving.

Xanthe emerged into the candlelight with a satchel, two waterskins, and the expression of someone who had just been given astonishing news by a talking cat and was deciding whether to mention it.

"Verity's clerk just told me the bond was destroyed in the archive fire," Xanthe said. "He was *crying*, Jude. He shook my hand. He called me 'a free soul of indeterminate residence.' I've never been described so kindly by an institution."

"He lied to you," Jude said. "She did. The fire was real, the destruction wasn't." They pulled the bond from their coat and held it out. "It's here. I stole it."

Xanthe stared. At the bond. At Jude. At the space between, as though the contradiction might resolve itself visually.

"You stole it."

"Yes."

"From Verity Ombre. The woman who once made a novice copy the entire tax code because he sneezed during inventory."

"Yes."

Xanthe took the bond with the reverence of someone handling either scripture or a live coal. Their thumb traced the final line of figures—the sum of nine years—and their face did something complicated that ended somewhere near tears before discipline hauled it back.

"You didn't have to become a thief for me," they said quietly. "I never asked you to—"

"No," Jude agreed. "You never asked. That's rather the whole point of you." They shouldered their satchel and nodded toward the dark mouth of the crypt passage, where the smuggler roads began, where three flags waited on one road, where the moon was already swelling toward its thirteenth turn. "Come on, Underhill. Let's go be uncollectable."
