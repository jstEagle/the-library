## 6. Salt Road, Ash Road

The first rule of the smuggler's road was: travel light, travel quiet, travel alone.

Xanthe had learned it at fifteen, from a woman named Brack who smelled of tallow and had a laugh like a cart going over gravel. *Alone*, she'd said, tapping Xanthe's chest with one cracked fingernail. *Every extra body is a weight you carry and a noise you make. The mountain doesn't care how many of you there are. It only cares how many it can take.*

Nine years of guiding caravans through Thornfell, and Xanthe had never once broken that rule.

They broke it on the third morning out of the temple, when a family of five fell into step behind them and refused to stop doing so.

It began with the grandmother. She appeared out of the treeline near the old salt cairn like a rumor, small and round and wrapped in a shawl the color of wet slate, carrying a bundle that was either a baby or a very ambitious loaf of bread. She planted herself in Xanthe's path with the confidence of someone who had been planting herself in paths for eighty years.

"You're going over the pass," she said. Not a question.

"I'm going to a place," Xanthe said carefully. "You wouldn't like it."

"Everyone's going to a place," the grandmother said. "That's what places are for." Behind her, as if summoned by her certainty, emerged the rest of them: a young man with a limp and a farmer's shoulders; two children, a boy and a girl, holding hands with the grim solidarity of siblings who had already survived something; and a woman — the mother, presumably — who looked at Xanthe the way people look at maps, which is to say, hungrily.

"We heard there's a guide," the mother said. "On the salt road. Someone who knows the way."

"There's no way," Xanthe said. "That's rather the problem with the whole country."

"The country," the grandmother said, "is a stubborn old mule, but it still knows where its legs are. Come on, then. Don't just stand there looking guilty. Guilt weighs more than luggage."

And that, apparently, was that. Xanthe opened their mouth to explain about traveling alone, about weights and noises, about Brack's cracked fingernail tapping against their sternum across nine years of memory — and Jude, walking behind them, made a small sound that might have been a cough or might have been laughter, and Xanthe found themselves turning up the trail with six extra heartbeats trailing after them like ducklings.

*Travel light,* said Brack, in the back of Xanthe's head.

*I'm carrying an ember that can resurrect a nation,* said the rest of Xanthe's head. *I think we've left "light" somewhere back down the mountain.*

* *

The pass earned its reputation by noon.

The salt road had been built, according to legend, by a king who wanted his armies to move faster than gossip, and it showed: long switchbacks cut into the mountainside, wide enough for carts once, now crumbling at the edges into scree and old snow. Above the tree line the wind came off the peaks with the flat, impersonal malice of weather that has never met a person it liked. And under everything, faint but constant, the smell of ash — because three days ago the capital had burned its own archives, and the smoke had climbed this high to watch the world end.

The boy — Tam, eight years old, with the solemnity of a magistrate — appointed himself Xanthe's shadow within the first hour. He walked exactly where Xanthe walked, stepped exactly where Xanthe stepped, and asked questions in the manner of someone who had decided questions were a form of currency and he intended to spend lavishly.

"Why do you walk on the inside of the trail?"

"So if the edge gives way, I fall and you don't."

"Why?"

"Because I know where the edge gives way and you don't."

He considered this with the gravity of a scholar reviewing a thesis. "That's not really an answer."

"It's the only kind smugglers give."

"What's a smuggler?"

"A person who knows where the edges give way," Jude said mildly, from behind, and Tam laughed like a bell being rung, and Xanthe felt something in their chest do a complicated maneuver they chose not to examine.

The girl — Wren, eleven, and clearly the actual magistrate of the family — said almost nothing, but watched everything, and by midday had memorized Xanthe's route well enough to correct her brother twice. Xanthe caught her doing it and, instead of being annoyed, felt an absurd flare of professional pride. Good instincts. Terrible circumstances. The story of everyone left in Vaelor.

The limping man was called Aldous, and he had been a tax collector before the borders dissolved, which meant he was possibly the most hated profession in a country that no longer legally existed, and he bore this fact with the sheepish endurance of a man who had heard every joke already. He kept count of the party's supplies without being asked, murmuring figures under his breath like prayers. When Xanthe caught him doing it, he flushed.

"Habit," he said. "Counting things. It's all I'm good for."

"Counting things keeps people alive on a mountain," Xanthe said. "Believe me. I've spent nine years being paid for it."

Aldous blinked at them as though a stone had spoken kindly to him, and thereafter counted louder.

The mother — Sera — asked no questions and offered no history, but she carried the bundle, which turned out to be neither baby nor bread but the family's entire remaining wealth: a copper kettle, a folded wedding cloth, and a jar of honey with a chip in its lid. She carried it the way other people carried infants. Xanthe understood immediately. You didn't flee a burning country with things. You fled it with proof that things had once been otherwise.

And the grandmother — Maren, she announced, unprompted, to everyone and no one — simply walked. Small steps, relentless, a metronome in a slate-colored shawl. Twice Xanthe tried to slow the pace for her sake. Twice she waved them off with a hand like a bird's wing.

"I buried a husband and two kings," she said. "This hill can try its luck."

* *

It was on the second night, camped in the lee of a ruined tollhouse, that the old code finally came due.

Snow had begun to fall around dusk — fine and dry, the kind that whispered rather than fell — and Xanthe lay awake doing arithmetic. Food for four days at current rationing, five if they were careful, six if nobody complained, which nobody would, because refugees were the politest company Xanthe had ever kept. The pass summit was two days ahead in good weather. In snow, three. Possibly four. The numbers did not close. Xanthe had spent nine years making numbers close, and these ones sat open on the page like a wound.

There was a solution. There was always a solution; that was the whole ugly craft of smuggling. You traveled light. You traveled alone. Six mouths were too many, and the ember was worth more than all of them combined, and the code said — Brack's cracked fingernail tapped, tapped, tapped — *you leave them at the tollhouse with half the food and a clear conscience, because a clear conscience is the only thing you get to keep.*

Xanthe got up quietly, meaning to check the weather, or the wards, or anything except their own thoughts, and found Jude awake, sitting against the tollhouse wall with the lantern unlit in their lap, watching the snow come down through the broken roof.

"You're counting," Jude said.

"I'm always counting."

"No. You're counting *them*. I've seen you do it to caravans. Weighing who slows you down." Jude's voice was gentle, which made it worse. "What's the number?"

Xanthe sat down beside them. The snow whispered. Somewhere below, a wolf sang one long note and got no answer.

"When I was fifteen," Xanthe said, "a woman taught me that every extra person on the road is a weight you carry and a noise you make. Nine years I lived by that. Nine years I got everyone I guided through alive *because* I never let anyone matter." They turned the ember-lantern's cold handle over in their hands. "And now there's no law left to hide from, and no ledger to settle at the end of it, and I keep catching myself doing things the code says are stupid. Slowing the pace. Teaching the boy the safe footing. Letting Aldous count our beans like they're treasure."

"They are treasure," Jude said. "Everything counts differently now. That's what it means, isn't it? For a country to vanish. All the old ledgers burn, and you find out what you were keeping score *for*."

Xanthe looked at them. Snow-light made Jude's face soft and strange, and the unlit lantern between them held its secret the way a cupped hand holds water.

"If we slow down for them," Xanthe said, "we might miss the door. If we leave them, we arrive faster and I arrive as the person Brack made, and I don't—" Their voice snagged. They pushed through. "I don't want to be her anymore. I just don't know what to be instead."

Jude was quiet a moment. Then: "My grandmother used to say belonging isn't something you're granted. It's something that follows you home and refuses to leave, and eventually you stop pretending you mind." A pause. "She also said it about a cat, so apply with caution."

Despite everything — the snow, the wolves, the burned country, the moon climbing toward its thirteenth turn — Xanthe laughed. It came out ragged, but it came.

"In the morning," Xanthe said, "I'm going to teach them all the safe footing. Every switchback. Where the edges give way. All of it."

"That's not the code."

"No," Xanthe said. "It's better than the code. It's the code with the selfishness taken out." They leaned their head back against the cold stone. "Brack would be furious."

"Good," said Jude. "Furious ghosts build character."

* *

They summited on the fourth day, through knee-deep drift and a wind that tried personally to murder each of them in alphabetical order. Maren went last, on Aldous's arm, singing something tuneless and defiant. Tam fell twice and got up twice without crying, which Xanthe privately rated as better performance than most hired caravan guards. At the crest, where the salt road tipped over into the long descent toward the valley of the Door, the whole party stopped and stood in the screaming wind and looked back the way they had come — at the white passes, the gray smoke smudging the southern sky, the whole vanished shape of Vaelor spread out below like a sleeping animal that didn't yet know it was dead.

Wren tugged Xanthe's sleeve.

"You walked on the inside of the trail the whole way," she said. "Every time."

"Somebody has to know where the edges give way," Xanthe said.

"Not somebody." She looked up at them with her magistrate's eyes. "*You.*"

And that was the moment Xanthe understood, with the vertigo of a map suddenly redrawn, that the thing they'd spent nine years avoiding had followed them up a mountain and refused to leave. Not a debt. Not a duty. Just seven people — six plus one lamplighter — moving together through the snow, bound by nothing but the footing they shared.

Then Jude touched their arm and pointed east, where the descent dropped into shadow.

At the bottom of the valley, small in the failing light, torches were moving. A column of them, organized, unhurried, fanning out across the road ahead like fingers closing around something.

Not one banner. Three.

"Well," said Maren, squinting into the wind. "Whose hill is this, then?"
