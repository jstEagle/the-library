## 7. The Warden Who Remembered

The trap was, Jude had to admit, a good one.

They had made it as far as the salt flats below the Verrow hills when the road simply stopped being a road. One moment there was the pale crust of the flats stretching south toward the border stones, and the next there were six riders arranged across it with the unhurried precision of people who had done this many times and never once had to chase anyone.

Jude reined in. Beside them, Xanthe's hand had already gone to the map-case at their belt—not for a weapon, for a route—and Jude saw the exact moment Xanthe understood what Jude understood: there was no route. The riders sat precisely where every path through the flats converged, which meant they knew the paths, which meant someone had sold them, or drawn them, or both.

"Temple lantern," said the rider in the center, in the flat southern accent of Vaelor's old garrison families. "Light it. Slowly."

Jude did. There seemed little point in anything else.

The woman who rode forward was tall and sun-browned, with the kind of face that had been handsome young and had decided not to stop. She wore no colors—none of the three states permitted their wardens to wear Vaelor's colors anymore—but her saddle blanket was stitched with a pattern Jude recognized instantly: the interlocking thirteens of the Salt Wardens' guild, disbanded nine years ago along with everything else that had belonged to the country that used to need wardens.

She looked at the lantern for a long moment. Then she looked at Jude.

"You're the lamplighter," she said. "From the Ember Temple. The one who taught the evening classes."

"I taught lamp-trimming," Jude said carefully. "It's less impressive than it sounds."

"Wick angle. Fuel ratios. How to read a flame the way you'd read weather." The woman swung down from her horse, and up close there was something terrible in her familiarity—the particular way she pushed her hood back with two fingers, the way she squinted against even a small light. "I sat in the third row. I asked too many questions. You told me a good warden reads flame before she reads faces, because faces lie and fire doesn't."

Jude's stomach performed a slow, complicated maneuver. "Sera Voss."

"Captain Voss now. The Meridian Protectorate is very fond of ranks." She said the word *Protectorate* the way one might say *mildew*. Her gaze slid past Jude to Xanthe, who sat very still in the manner of someone calculating six exits and disliking all of them. "And you'll be Underhill. The guide. Your reputation crossed the flats ahead of you, which is why we're standing here instead of chasing you."

"If you've heard my reputation," Xanthe said, "you know I'm extremely boring and worth nothing to anyone."

"I know you're evidence," Voss said, without malice. It landed worse than an accusation would have. "That's what the Protectorate calls people like you now. Living documentation." She turned back to the lantern, to the small gold steadiness of its flame. "We've been following you for six days. My orders are to take the ember and the carriers to Meridian City. Both of you. Alive. They want it presented properly, with witnesses, with ceremony. A founding covenant makes such lovely paperwork."

"And if we refuse?" Xanthe asked.

Voss smiled, and it was an old smile, a classroom smile, worn soft at the edges. "Then I arrest you, which takes longer, and my sergeant gets bored, and bored sergeants break things. Or—" and here her voice changed, dropped into something quieter, meant only for the three of them and the wind off the salt, "—we can stand here a few more minutes while my men check the far crossings, which they will find empty, because I sent them to the wrong ones. Old habit. I still read flame better than orders."

Xanthe made a sound that was almost a laugh. "You're letting us go."

"I'm considering it," Voss said. "But first, lamplighter, I want an honest answer, because I've ridden six days thinking about it, and I'd like to sleep sometime this decade." She stepped closer. Up close, Jude could see grey in her hair that hadn't been there in the classroom, and a scar along her jaw that hadn't been there either. "The Door of Thirteen Turns. What happens on the other side?"

"That's rather the question, isn't it," Jude said.

"It's mine. I asked it first."

Jude looked down at the lantern. The ember burned the way it always burned—small, patient, warm as a held hand—and lied about nothing, and promised nothing either. Nine years of trimming ordinary lamps had taught Jude every way a flame could behave, and this one behaved like none of them: it did not flicker in wind, it did not gutter when cupped, and it did not answer questions. It simply waited, the way a debt waits.

"I don't know," Jude said.

Voss's eyebrows rose. "You're carrying a piece of your country's soul across half a continent and you don't know what it does?"

"I know what it's *for*," Jude said. "It's the last unbroken clause of the founding covenant. The temple kept it lit for four hundred years so that Vaelor would always be able to... to say itself. To draw its own line. And now the records are ash and the borders are sealed and three empires are each claiming we never properly existed, and the ember is the only thing left that remembers otherwise." Jude's hands tightened around the lantern's handle. "What I don't know is whether opening the door saves us. Whether it carries us somewhere. Or whether it's just the covenant doing what covenants do at the end of a term—calling in the account."

* * *

They walked while the horses drank, because Voss refused to discuss matters standing still—it was, she said, a classroom habit; Jude had always paced during lessons, and some things apparently took root deeper than loyalty.

"The northern state has a doctrine," Voss said, walking with her hands clasped behind her back like a scholar on holiday. "They call it *succession of substance*. If they hold the covenant, Vaelor legally transfers to them. Clean annexation. The Kessarine Compact wants it as a trophy—proof their navy broke a nation. And my beloved Protectorate—" she spat neatly into the salt, "—wants it as a birth certificate. We were a Vaeloran protectorate once, did you know? Before the war. If they hold the ember, they claim they're not conquerors at all, merely *heirs*."

"Three thieves arguing over inheritance from a corpse they made," Xanthe said.

"Careful. That's sedition in two jurisdictions and treason in the third." But Voss said it lightly, and then not lightly at all: "Here's what keeps me up, lamplighter. Every report, every interrogation, every scrap the wardens have gathered agrees on one thing. The door doesn't open for armies. It doesn't open for treaties. It opens for someone who belongs wholly to Vaelor. Wholely. No debts outstanding."

Jude went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the wind coming off the flats.

"No debts," they repeated.

"That's the phrase in the old texts. The temple's own commentaries—I checked, before you look scandalized, I remember where the reading room kept them. Third shelf, behind the histories nobody wanted." Voss stopped walking and turned. "So ask yourself what that means for your guide here, whose entire existence for nine years has been a ledger entry. Ask yourself what the covenant considers a debt. Because I've read those commentaries, and they don't distinguish between money owed and lives owed and *selves* owed. To the old law, a person pledged against a temple account isn't free. And the door knows."

The silence that followed was very long, and very full of Xanthe not looking at Jude, and Jude not looking at Xanthe, and both of them looking with great intensity at the middle distance.

"There's another thing," Voss said, gentler now, and somehow that was worse. "My orders come with a date attached. The moon completes its thirteenth turn in eleven days. After that, if the door hasn't opened, the Protectorate declares Vaeloran persons *unowned property* and redistributes us. That's the actual language. Redistributes." She let the word sit in the salt air like a stone in a pond. "So everyone believes the door is rescue. The refugees believe it. Your smuggler believes it. Maybe you believe it. But I was your student, lamplighter, and you taught me better than to trust a flame I hadn't read to the root. Doors in the old covenants aren't exits. They're *assizes*. The thirteenth turn isn't a deadline. It's a court date."

"Judgment," Jude said quietly.

"Final accounting," Voss agreed. "Every covenant ends one of two ways—renewal or reckoning. And nobody, in four hundred years of temple record-keeping, ever wrote down what the reckoning looks like." She mounted her horse in one smooth motion, and just like that she was a warden again, hard-edged and official, except for her eyes. "I'm going to report that I found salt flats and bad luck. You have perhaps two days before someone less nostalgic picks up your trail."

"Why?" Xanthe asked bluntly. "Why risk it?"

Voss looked down at them both for a long moment. When she answered, she wasn't answering Xanthe.

"Because in eleven days, either your door opens or my whole world gets redistributed," she said. "And if it's going to be judgment anyway, I'd rather it came through a lamplighter who taught me to read flame honestly than through a committee in Meridian City." She gathered her reins. "Trim your wick, teacher. Whatever burns next—burn clean."

Then the six riders wheeled away toward the wrong crossings, and the salt flats were empty again, and the road lay open south under a sky that still, after all these weeks, had no stars in it.

Jude stood holding the lantern and felt the ember's warmth against their hip like a small, patient heartbeat, and for the first time since the night the stars went out, they were afraid of it.

*Eleven days.* Behind them, Xanthe was already checking the harness, already moving, already counting routes the way they counted everything—but when they glanced back, whatever they saw in Jude's face stopped them.

"What did she mean," Xanthe said slowly, "when she said the door won't open for anyone with debts outstanding?"

Jude looked at them—at nine years of quiet competence and unpaid freedom, at the person who had broken their bond on a mountainside with three armies watching, declaring themselves culpable so the refugees could pass—and felt the truth settle into place like a wick settling into wax.

"She meant," Jude said, "that we may not be walking out of Vaelor at all. We may be walking into its final accounting. And I think—" their voice caught, and they hated it, and they went on anyway, "—I think the covenant already knows exactly what we owe, and to whom, and it intends to collect."

Far to the south, past the edge of the flats, the moon was rising—fuller than it had any right to be, eleven days early in its heart if not its calendar, and burning white as a ledger page waiting for ink.
