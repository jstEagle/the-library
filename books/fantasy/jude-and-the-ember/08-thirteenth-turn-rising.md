## 8. Thirteenth Turn Rising

The moon did not rise so much as arrive, the way a debt collector arrives: inevitable, punctual, and unwelcome.

Xanthe stood on the last ridge above the Door of Thirteen Turns and watched it swell toward fullness, fat and pale as a coin someone had bitten to test. Thirteen nights of walking had worn them down to wire and stubbornness. Their boots were more prayer than leather. Somewhere below, in the bowl of the valley where the mountains folded shut like a book being closed, the Door waited—a seam of older-than-old stone that looked, in daylight, like nothing at all. In daylight it *was* nothing at all. That was rather the point.

"Stop counting," said Brammel, the grandmother who had appointed herself general of their ragged column somewhere around the ninth night, on the grounds that she had outlived two husbands and one government and therefore outranked everyone.

"I'm not counting."

"You're counting the warden fires."

Xanthe exhaled through their nose. "I'm *surveying* the warden fires."

"How many?"

"Forty-one." A pause. "Forty-two. They just lit another one on the eastern shelf, which is either very brave or very stupid, because that's salt-marsh ground and they'll be ankle-deep in bog water by midnight."

Brammel made a sound like a kettle deciding against boiling. "Three armies," she said. "For one door. For one—" she glanced sideways at Jude, who sat wrapped in his lamplighter's coat with the lantern between his knees like a sleeping bird, "—one small quiet person with a lamp."

"The ember isn't small," Xanthe said. "It just keeps its own counsel. Unlike some grandmothers I could name."

"I'm not a grandmother to you yet. You haven't earned the scolding rights."

"They come with the column, apparently. Free of charge."

That earned her laugh, which was the whole trick of Brammel—you fed her a straight line and she paid you back in laughter, and for a moment everyone within earshot forgot that forty-two fires ringed the valley below like a noose being assembled by committee.

Because that was what it was, Xanthe thought, looking down again. Three states that could agree on nothing—not borders, not tariffs, not even the spelling of Vaelor in their respective occupation documents—had agreed on this. The northern wardens flew grey banners stitched with their ledger-wheel. The southern flew black and gold, all tassels, like funeral drapery that had gotten ambitions. And the eastern—the eastern had arrived in river-steel and silence, camped without fires until tonight, when forty-two flames bloomed across the dark like the sky's missing stars had been conscripted into enemy service.

All three wanted the same thing: a fragment of the old covenant. Proof of succession. A candle to hold over their claim and say *see, legitimate*. And none of them, Xanthe would have wagered every map in their skull, understood that the ember was not a document. It was not a thing you could seize. It had opinions. It had, if the last thirteen nights were any indication, a sense of humor—Jude's lantern had flickered twice at wardens they'd slipped past, which Jude swore meant nothing but which Xanthe chose to read as the oldest institution in Vaelor being cheeky.

"They're moving," said Odell, one of the twins from the salt flats, scrambling up from the switchback trail. "Northern column's forming up. Grey line, maybe two hundred, coming up the pilgrim road. The southerners are taking the ridge path. Slow. They want the northerners to go first."

"Of course they do," Xanthe murmured. "Why break your own seal when someone else will break theirs."

Below, in the hollow where the refugees had gathered—two hundred souls now, maybe more, the column having grown the way snowballs grow, each person fetching three more—they were lighting their own fires. Little ones. Cooking fires, mostly, because people still needed to eat even on the night the country ended officially forever. Someone had started singing. It was an old threshing song, tuneless and repetitive, the kind of song that existed purely so many voices could hold it at once.

Jude rose and came to stand beside them at the ridge's edge. Up close, in the lantern-light, he looked calm in the way deep water is calm.

"You're doing the thing," he said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you count everything twice and tell no one what the numbers mean."

"That's called competence, lamplighter."

"It's called fear with good posture." He bumped his shoulder gently against theirs. "Tell me the real number."

Xanthe hesitated. Then, because it was Jude, they told the truth: "Six hundred soldiers, give or take. Three commands that hate each other more than they love their own treaties. And us."

"And two hundred people who followed you here."

"*Followed the road*, is what they did. I happened to be standing on it."

"Mm." Jude looked down at the little fires. "You know what I see? I see a border. Right there." He nodded at the scatter of cookfires, the song threading up through the cold air. "A boundary made of people. No empire has ever managed to draw one of those."

The song changed verses. More voices joined. Down in the dark, someone's baby started crying and was shushed by four different strangers, and Xanthe felt something in their chest go tight and strange, because nine years of mapping roads alone at night had never once prepared them for this: for belonging arriving uninvited, unasked-for, wearing a grandmother's disapproval and a pair of twins' terrible jokes.

"Brammel wants a war council," Odell reported breathlessly, returning. "She says, and I quote, 'Tell the guide if they think I'm letting six hundred armed men walk over my people while we stand here admiring the moon, they've been breathing thin air too long.'"

"She's not *your* people," Xanthe said. "She met you eleven days ago."

"And she's mine now. That's how she works. You don't get a vote."

* * *

The war council lasted four minutes, which Xanthe privately considered a record for a gathering that included two grandmothers, a disgraced toll-keeper, the salt-flat twins, and a man named Harrow who claimed to be a former diplomat and whom nobody believed.

"There are three approaches to the Door," Xanthe said, crouched over a scrap of hide where they'd scratched the valley from memory—though memory was hardly necessary; they could have drawn it blindfolded, drunk, mid-sneeze. "Pilgrim road from the north, ridge path from the south, and the dry wash from the east. All three converge in the last hundred paces. There's no way around it. Whoever designed this place wanted anyone arriving to arrive *together*. Which tells you something about the people who built it."

"It tells me they weren't smugglers," said the toll-keeper.

"It tells me they trusted people more than I currently trust six hundred soldiers. Yes. Agreed."

"So we can't hold it," Harrow said flatly. "Two hundred refugees, half of them children and elders, cannot hold a valley against three armies. I say we surrender the ember to whichever force arrives first and negotiate passage for the column."

"No," said Jude.

Everyone turned. He hadn't raised his voice. He almost never did. But the lantern at his feet brightened—just slightly, just enough that shadows leaned away from him—and every person in that circle felt it, the way you feel a change in pressure before weather.

"It doesn't work like that," Jude said quietly. "I've learned that much in thirteen turns. The ember isn't a key you hand over. It opens for—" he stopped, looked at Xanthe, and something passed between them that the whole circle politely pretended not to notice. "It opens for those who belong. Not to a crown. To the country. And there is only one country left here tonight, and it's made of us."

Silence. Then Brammel said, "Well. The quiet ones always turn out to be the dangerous ones," in the tone of a woman filing away useful information.

"We don't fight," Xanthe said. The plan had been assembling itself in their head since the second fire-count, ugly and clean. "We *shield*. We put the column between the armies and the Door. Two hundred civilians, singing. If the wardens want to reach the ember, they have to decide, in front of each other and in front of the moon, whether they'll cut through unarmed people to do it. Three armies watching each other. Nobody wants to be the first to bloody their hands. That hesitation is our wall."

"And if it fails?" Harrow asked.

"Then it fails loudly, historically, and on record," Xanthe said. "Every warden out there reports to a chancery that claims Vaelor never existed. Let them write home about what they did to its people."

* * *

They came down into the valley in the blue hour before moonrise, and Xanthe walked at the front, which felt wrong and right at once—nine years of making themselves invisible, of walking edges and shadow-lines, of being the person no checkpoint ever saw—and now here they were, planted in open ground like a flag.

The grey column reached the valley mouth first, two hundred strong, ledger-wheels catching moonlight. Behind them, the southern banner unfurled on the ridge like something molting. Eastward, river-steel glinted in silent ranks.

The lead northern warden rode forward, a broad woman with frost in her voice. "By authority of the Provisional Administration of the Northern Territories, this site is seized. Surrender any persons or objects bearing covenant-significance."

"Interesting phrasing," Xanthe called back, and heard Brammel snort behind them. "Very careful. Almost like you're not sure what you're seizing."

"Identify yourself."

And there it was. The moment. Nine years of debt, of small ledgers and smaller surrenders, of being property with good handwriting. Xanthe reached into their coat and drew out the debt-bond—Jude's stolen theft, carried these thirteen nights like a coal they were afraid to drop—and held it high where the moonlight could find it.

"My name is Xanthe Underhill," they said, loud enough to reach the ridgelines, the fires, the two hundred singers gone suddenly quiet. "Nine years bound to the Ember Temple of Vaelor in payment of my family's debt. This paper says I belong to someone else." They paused. "Here is what I have learned about papers lately: yours say my country never existed. Mine says I never existed. So let us be honest with one another, warden—tonight, on this ground, there are no legal persons at all. Only people."

They tore the bond in half. Then halves again, methodically, and let the pieces fall like moths.

"I am free," Xanthe said, and their voice cracked on it, gloriously, and they kept going anyway. "Which means no one owns my choices anymore—which means whatever happens next belongs to me entirely. Every step. Every word. Culpable, accountable, *present*." They spread their arms wide, the ruined bond drifting past their shoulders. "And I am standing between you and them."

Behind them, the column began to sing again.

The warden's horse shifted. On the ridge, the southern banner hesitated. From the east, the silent ranks took one step forward—

And stopped, all together, as the moon crested the peaks at last, full and enormous, and every lantern in the refugee camp flared gold at once without being touched—including, Xanthe realized with a jolt, the dead lamps of the old temple way-stones ringing the valley, lit for the first time in nine years, burning in a perfect unbroken line.

Jude stepped forward to stand beside them, ember-light climbing his face like dawn arriving early.

"Thirteen turns," he said softly, to Xanthe, to the valley, to whatever was listening beneath the stone. "It's time."

The ground under the Door of Thirteen Turns began, very quietly, to glow.
