## 1. The Charm Seller

The trouble with selling charms in a city that hangs charm-sellers is that you have to be very good at it, and being very good at anything draws attention, and attention, in Vellamarch, is the first symptom of a short life.

I had been careful for twenty years. Careful the way other people are religious — not out of conviction but because the alternative was unthinkable and besides, the habits had grown into me like roots into a wall. I rose before dawn. I opened my stall at the same hour as the fishmonger, whose trade smelled worse but was legal. I priced my wares honestly, which marked me immediately as either honest or stupid, and I cultivated stupidity the way some men cultivate mustaches: with dedication, and in full view.

"Charms!" I called when the market filled. "Charms against misfortune! Charms for luck in love! Perfectly ordinary trinkets, no better than they should be, cheaper than most!"

That last part was true, which helped. In Vellamarch, truth is the best disguise. Nobody suspects a man who undersells his own goods.

Let me describe what I actually sold, since the distinction matters to this story more than it did to my customers. On the left side of the table: pewter tokens stamped with a sun, sold as protection against bad weather. They did nothing whatsoever. A woman bought one every month for eleven years and complained of rain every month for eleven years, and kept buying, which tells you something about hope that I never had the heart to say aloud.

On the right side: knotted cords for safe travel, painted beads for fertility, small glass eyes on strings against the evil glance. All inert. All decorative. If you cut me open you would have found a merchant and nothing else, like a goose stuffed with receipts.

And then there was the third thing. The third thing wasn't on the table at all.

* * *

Here is how it worked, and I ask you to keep in mind — because someone will eventually read this account who deserves an explanation, and because I deserve to have written one — that every person involved came to me willingly. That word will matter later. *Willingly.* Write it on your hand if you have to.

A customer would buy a sun-token or a knot-cord, and while counting out coins, some of them would sigh. Not all. Maybe one in ten. It was a particular kind of sigh, the sigh of a person carrying too much, and after twenty years I could sort those sighs the way a baker sorts loaves by sound alone.

To those customers I would say, quietly, leaning over the coins: "You know, there's an old trick for troubles. You tell them to a stranger who's leaving town anyway. Costs nothing. Lightens the pack."

Most people laughed. Some looked embarrassed and hurried off. But some — the tired ones, the ones with the particular sigh — would look at me with something raw in their faces, and talk.

The cooper's wife told me her son hadn't written from the war in eight months. The ferryman told me he dreamed every night of the river taking him, and woke with wet hands. A girl of maybe sixteen bought a luck-bead and told me, in a voice so low the words barely cleared the table, that she was going to be married in spring to a man she'd met twice, and did I think that was enough times to meet a man before marrying him?

"It isn't," I said.

"I know it isn't." She laughed, wet-eyed. "But try telling my father."

And while she talked, while she poured that little weight out of herself into the air between us, I took it.

Not all of it. Never all of it. I am many things, but I am not a thief of whole lives; I take the way you might skim cream off milk. From her I took only the dread — the cold stone of it sitting in her chest — and left everything else: the father, the fiancé, even the fear itself, thinned to something she could carry without stooping. She paid for her bead, thanked me warmly, and walked away lighter than she'd come, wondering why the charm-seller's trinkets felt like they worked.

They didn't work. *I* worked. And I hated myself for it in a disciplined, scheduled sort of way — mornings, mostly, before the market opened — because the alternative was hating myself continuously, and nobody can run a business like that.

What did I do with what I took? I want to be precise here, because precision is all I have left. I didn't hoard it like a dragon. I couldn't have explained why not, back then. I think now it was because a memory held is a debt unpaid, and debts accrue interest, and I already owed enough. So I spent them. Small ones, carefully rationed, the way a poor man spends coins he found rather than earned. When the winter came and my hands cracked from cold, I could borrow warmth from the memory of a summer afternoon someone had given up to me unasked. When loneliness got its teeth into me — and it did, regularly, the way weather does — I could sit behind my shuttered stall at night and hold, for a minute at a time, the cooper's wife's love for her absent son, just to remember what having someone felt like.

I know how that sounds. I knew how it sounded then. There is no version of this where I come out clean, so let us proceed.

* * *

Vellamarch fears magic the way a scarred dog fears raised hands: totally, and without distinguishing between the blow and the caress. Two hundred years ago there had been a Gift War — capital letters in the histories, which is always a bad sign — and afterward the crown had built the Quiet Law, three sentences long and perfectly clear. To work magic is death. To conceal magic is death. To fail to report magic is death. Three sentences, and the third was the clever one, because it made every neighbor a potential executioner and every friendship a risk assessment.

There were burnings, occasionally. Public ones, in the square by the fountain, and the city turned out for them with the cheerful grimness of people watching a storm drain get cleared. I attended two in my early years, deliberately, forcing myself to watch, telling myself it was reconnaissance. It was partly reconnaissance. It was also penance of a strange backward kind — standing in the crowd, anonymous, sweating in the cold, thinking *that could be me* until the thought wore grooves deep enough to walk in.

The last burning I attended had been a woman accused of cursing a well. Whether she had cursed the well I never learned; the evidence was that the well water tasted of iron, which in that district it always had. What stayed with me was not the fire. It was the crowd's faces, which were not cruel. They were relieved. That was the thing I understood about Vellamarch, standing there with my collar up: the city didn't hate magic-users. It feared them, and fear relieved looks like hatred the way a mask looks like a face.

So I sold nothing that worked. I performed skepticism about my own merchandise — "mind, it's only pewter, but belief is cheap and I'm cheaper" — and the performance made people trust me, because a fraud is safe company. Fraudulence was my armor, and I polished it daily.

Twenty years. Do the arithmetic of that with me, because I did it every morning. Twenty years is roughly seven thousand market days. Seven thousand times I set out my inert trinkets and smiled my fraud's smile and listened for the sighs. Seven thousand nights I went home alone to two rooms above a tannery, smelling perpetually of someone else's trade, eating supper standing up because setting a table for one seemed obscene. Seven thousand mornings waking with the same first thought: *still undiscovered*, followed immediately by the second thought, quieter, the one I never said even to myself in full: *and still alone, and is the second the price of the first, and if so, is the exchange fair?*

It was fair. I never once believed it was fair.

* * *

Now I must tell you about the ledger, because the ledger is where the dread lived.

Every night I recorded my takings in a plain merchant's book — coins in, stock out, the dull arithmetic of survival. But in the back pages, in a cipher of my own devising, I kept another record. Not names — never names. Marks. A circle for the cooper's wife, a triangle for the ferryman, a small hook-shape for the girl with the marriage dread. Dates. Weights, if you can call them that — my own private scale for measuring how much of a person I had skimmed, though the units were nonsense, feelings-per-moon or somesuch, invented by a frightened young man who needed the theft to feel like bookkeeping.

Why did I keep it? Every sensible instinct said burn it. A written confession is a rope pre-tied. But I couldn't. Partly vanity — the cipher was elegant, I'll admit it, and vanity survives even in hiding, maybe especially there. Partly something worse: I needed the count. As long as I knew exactly what I owed, I could tell myself I intended to pay. The ledger was my promise to strangers who didn't know they'd been robbed, and I clung to that promise the way drowning men cling to driftwood that is also, technically, the reason they are drowning.

Some nights, after the candle was guttered, I would read the marks back to myself and try to feel the shape of the whole debt at once. I never could. The mind flinches from totals. It takes them one circle, one triangle at a time, and each one is bearable, and that is precisely the problem with small sins: they are sized for the conscience.

* * *

That autumn day — the day this story properly begins, though I didn't know it, none of us ever knows it — began like the six thousand nine hundred and some-odd before it. Fog off the canal. Fishmonger shouting about mackerel. First customer at half past seven, a bricklayer with a limp who bought a sun-token against rheumatism and sighed the wrong kind of sigh, the merely human kind, so I let him keep his troubles.

Midmorning brought the cooper's wife, my monthly circle, and I took the fresh edge off her worry and gave her, in exchange, a compliment about her hat that I genuinely meant, which is a form of payment too, however small. Then the lull. Then a cluster of pilgrims passing through toward the shrine at Ambron, who bought knots for their feet and told me nothing, being already emptied by their journey.

I was restocking the glass eyes — threading them, humming, entirely content in the shallow way of a man who has survived another morning — when the criers' bells started.

Not the hour bells. The other kind. The ones they rang, historically, for plague and war.

The market went quiet the way water goes quiet before it freezes. Heads turned toward the square. Somewhere a coin dropped and rolled and no one picked it up.

I stood there with a glass eye in my hand and an entire life's caution in my chest, and I did what I had trained myself to do for twenty years: nothing. I kept threading. My hands did not shake, because I had taught them not to, because shaking hands are evidence.

Across the aisle, the fishmonger caught my eye. He'd known me eleven years. He mouthed something — news, probably, prices, probably — and I nodded as if I'd heard, and threaded another bead, and felt the quiet dread beneath the routine shift its weight, the way a house shifts on its foundations before the crack appears.

Later, everyone would say they had known. Later, I would learn exactly how much the criers' bells meant, and who had been listening to whom, and which of the few people I trusted had been paid, all along, to stand close to me.

But that morning I knew only this: the bells were ringing, the fog was lifting, and somewhere in the crowd moving slowly toward the square was a customer I had never seen before — a young gardener in palace livery-green, walking through my market as if he had all the time in the world, looking at my little table of harmless lies as though he could see straight through them.

He was coming toward my stall.

I put down the glass eye and smiled my fraud's smile, and braced myself the way you brace for weather.
