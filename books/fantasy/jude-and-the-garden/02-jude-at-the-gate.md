## 2. Jude at the Gate

The trouble with selling luck is that people expect you to have some of your own.

I was restocking the shelf of small mercies — river-stones drilled and threaded on red cord, each one charmed to keep a fisherman's line from snagging, which is to say each one doing absolutely nothing except giving the hand something to hold — when the bell above my door rang in a way I didn't like. Bells ring all day in my shop. Most of them announce someone who wants a charm against a mother-in-law's tongue or a rival's envy, and those are easy: I sell them a knot of dried lavender and take, with their cheerful permission, a memory of some afternoon they'd never miss anyway. The bell I didn't like was the one that rang slow. One note at a time. The bell of somebody who had come on purpose.

He had to duck under the lintel. Tall, sun-browned, with dirt worked so deep into the creases of his knuckles that no amount of washing would ever get it out, and he wore it like other men wear medals. Palace livery, but the working kind — the coat of a gardener, not a footman, sleeves rolled to the elbow and grass stains at the cuff going green-gold in my shop light. He stood just inside the door a moment, taking stock of the shelves the way a man takes stock of a horse he's thinking of buying.

"Quentin Joubert?" he said.

"That depends," I said, "on who's asking, and whether the answer gets anyone hanged."

That was meant to be a joke. It came out exactly as true as it sounded, and he had the grace not to smile at it. Instead he crossed the floor — carefully, the way big men do around small shops, as if afraid his elbows might knock over twenty years of my livelihood — and set a folded paper on the counter between us. Unfolded it. Turned it to face me.

A palace seal. An actual, honest-to-the-Saints palace seal, pressed into grey wax, sitting on my counter like a spider on a wedding cake.

"An order," he said. "For the royal conservatory."

I looked at the order. Then at him. Then back at the order, because looking at him was doing something inconvenient to my concentration and I needed all of it for the wax spider.

Charm-sellers get three kinds of customers. There are the hopefuls, who buy luck and love and protection from creditors. There are the desperate, who come at night and pay in memories they can't afford to lose, and whom I cheat gently by taking less than they offer. And there are none from the palace, because the palace does not believe in charms, and the palace executes people who make them work. In twenty years behind this counter I had sold to fishermen and widows and one memorable smuggler, and precisely nobody whose wages were drawn from the crown.

"The royal conservatory," I said. "Which royal conservatory."

"There's only the one." He said it mildly, but his eyes stayed on mine while he said it, and there was a flicker in them — quick as a fish turning — that told me he knew exactly what a stupid question he'd let me ask, and was letting me hang myself with it to see what I did.

"And what," I said, "does the royal conservatory want with charms? Last I heard, the crown's position on charms was fire and rope."

"The head conservator wants the glasshouse kept free of blight. She read somewhere that iron nails blessed at a crossroads keep rot off the vines." He shrugged. "She reads a lot. Half of what she reads is nonsense. My job is to go find the nonsense anyway, because if I come back empty-handed she'll have me repotting orchids until midwinter, and I would rather be hanged, frankly. No offense."

"Some taken."

"Fair." He almost smiled. It was a good almost-smile, the kind that lives mostly in the eyes and suggests the mouth is saving itself for an occasion. "So. Do you stock anti-blight nonsense, or do I walk down to Ferrier's Lane and give my coin to old Maren, who at least pretends her charms are fake?"

I should mention here what I actually did, in that moment, because it's the sort of thing a man ought to confess. I reached across the counter and took his hand — ostensibly to examine the calluses, because gardeners' hands tell you about their work and I was stalling — and while I held it I let my gift brush the surface of him, light as a fly landing, tasting for fear. Fear has a flavor. I've drunk it often enough to know: copper and vinegar, a sourness at the back of the throat of a memory. Guilt tastes worse. Lies taste like nothing at all, which is how I know to look for them.

His surface was calm as a millpond. Underneath — nothing. Not emptiness; walls. A mind built like a walled garden, everything pruned, everything arranged, nothing growing where a visitor could see it. In twenty years I had never once met a mind so tidy. It was either the most honest man in Vairemont or the best liar, and both possibilities were standing in my shop holding a palace seal, and my heart was doing something unprofessional about the shape of his hands.

"Well?" he said.

"You have excellent walls," I said.

"I'm sorry?"

"Calluses. You have excellent calluses. Real gardener, then, whatever else you are." I let go of his hand before my gift got greedy, and turned to the shelf behind me, buying time with inventory. "Blessed iron I can do. Whether it keeps blight off anything is between the head conservator and her Saints. How many vines are we protecting?"

"Forty rows of table grapes, a wall of peaches, and something in the east house she swears is a rose and I swear is a briar that hates us personally."

"A briar with a grudge. That I can help with. Grudges respond to charm better than blight does." I pulled down a tray of nails — ordinary nails, honestly bought, blessed by me at genuine crossroads because if I'm going to commit fraud I do it thoroughly — and began wrapping them in brown paper while I thought. Here is what I thought: *trap*. Here is what I also thought: *he has a smear of moss on his jaw and hasn't noticed, and I could tell him, and telling him would be a kindness, and kindness is how they catch people like me.* The two thoughts sat side by side in my skull like cats that hadn't been introduced.

"Quentin Joubert," he said, reading my name off the sign above the counter, pronouncing it correctly on the first try, which almost nobody does. "Odd name for a charm-seller."

"It's an odd trade for a living. We suit each other."

"Where's it from?"

"Where's yours from?" I asked, because I've survived twenty years by answering questions with questions, and because I genuinely wanted to know.

"Gardeners don't get names. We get assignments." He said it lightly enough, but underneath the lightness there was that tidiness again, that walled-garden quality, a sentence with its roots cut short. Then, briskly, before I could dig: "Jude. Just Jude, since you're wondering and too polite to ask."

"Just Jude," I said, and found I liked the sound of it more than I wanted to. "Well, Just Jude, your conservator will have her nails. Twelve, wrapped against damp. Tell her to hang them at the row ends, one per post, and to replace them when the rain turns them orange. And tell her—" I hesitated.

"Tell her what?"

"Tell her the blessing wears off faster than the iron rusts. If the blight comes anyway, it isn't because the charm failed. It's because charms were never the point." I tied the string. "No charge for that part."

He took the parcel and weighed it in his palm, and something moved through his face too fast for me to name — there and gone, like the fish again, under the pond ice. "You always lecture your customers?"

"Only the ones I expect to see again."

"Expecting me, then."

"The palace pays its bills," I said. "That's reason enough."

It wasn't the reason. The reason was that I wanted him to come back, and every instinct I owned — and I own very good instincts; they're why I'm alive — was screaming that wanting him back was the most dangerous thing I'd done since I learned what I could do. He was palace. He was wrong for my shop the way a fox is wrong for a henhouse, and I stood there watching him tuck the parcel into his coat and thought: *run*. Twenty years of careful loneliness, twenty years of taking only what customers offered freely and never the same customer twice, twenty years of being nobody interesting — and one hour of this man and I was already composing reasons for him to return.

He stopped at the door, half-turned, backlit by the market noise outside. The moss was still on his jaw.

"You've got—" I gestured at my own chin. He scrubbed at the wrong side. "Other— no. Here." And before I could think better of it I leaned across the counter and brushed it away with my thumb, which was forward of me, and familiar beyond all sense, and his skin was warm from walking in the sun, and for one unguarded instant his whole tidy garden shook — something wild moving behind the walls, something that startled even him. I felt it like heat off a stone. Whatever Jude kept pruned down flat, it was alive under there, and large, and hungry for exactly this.

Neither of us said anything for a moment. Outside, a cart went by, and a woman argued cheerfully about eels, and the world continued being ordinary without our permission.

"Crossroads blessing," he said finally, voice slightly rougher than before. "Does it work on people, or just vines?"

"Don't know," I said. "Nobody's ever tested it on me."

He smiled then — the full version, occasion finally arrived — and it was worse than the almost-smile, considerably worse, and I understood that I was in a great deal of trouble. "Midweek, then. For the second dozen. Conservator says twelve won't cover the peach wall."

"Midweek," I agreed, and watched him go, and stood a long time in my empty shop holding my own thumb like it had betrayed me.

Here is the thing I couldn't stop turning over, after he left, while I pretended to price river-stones. When I'd touched his hand, I had tasted for lies and found walls instead. Fine. But there had been one crack in those walls, one hairline fracture where something had shown through before he caught it — and in that crack, just for a heartbeat, I had smelled salt water. Old salt water, and cold stone, and beneath it something green and growing that had no business being anywhere in a palace gardener's memory.

Vairemont is landlocked. I am landlocked. Neither of us has seen the sea in years, and neither of us should smell it buried that deep.

I told myself it was nothing. I told myself a lot of things that evening, locking up the shop, checking the bolts twice, three times, the way I do. I told myself the palace order was nonsense, that conservators collect superstitions the way magpies collect buttons, that a man with moss on his jaw and calluses like scripture was exactly what he appeared to be.

But I lit a candle I don't usually light — the stub of black wax I keep behind the ledger for occasions I hope never to have — and I set it in the window where the street can't see it, and I let it burn while I thought about salt water.

Because here is the last thing, the thing I haven't confessed yet. When I brushed the moss from his jaw and felt that wildness move behind his walls, my gift — quiet, disciplined, starved-of-company gift — had reached toward him on its own. Not to steal. To *recognize*. The way one flame leans toward another.

And somewhere far under the tidy garden of him, something had leaned back.
