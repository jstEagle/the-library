## 3. The Proclamation

The morning the world ended, I was haggling over a hairpin.

I want to be clear about that. Not a dramatic omen, not a raven on my awning or milk turning sour in its jug — just a woman named Odile Vasse who wanted six coppers off a silvered pin because it had, she claimed, a scratch on the clasp. The scratch was imaginary. The clasp was flawless. I knew this because I had polished it myself at midnight with a chamois cloth and the particular tenderness of a liar preparing his lies.

"Five," said Odile.

"It has a charm in it," I said, which was true in the way most of my best sentences were true — technically, and only where it mattered. "Keeps headaches away."

"That's the fourth headache charm you've sold me this year, Joubert."

"And have you had a headache since?"

She considered this. Odile was a fishmonger's widow with forearms like mooring rope and a memory I had skimmed exactly once, three years ago, with her cheerful permission: a sliver of her wedding day, bright as a struck match, traded for a love-token she'd wanted for her second husband. I kept it in a little glass vial on my shelf, one of forty-odd such vials, each stoppered and labeled in a cipher of my own invention. On bad nights I uncorked one and borrowed someone else's happiness for a quarter hour, the way a poor man might warm his hands at a richer man's fire.

"Six," said Odile, "and you throw in the ribbon."

"You're robbing me blind, Madame Vasse."

"I know," she said, pleased. "It's why I keep coming back."

And that, God help me, was the last ordinary transaction of my life. Because that was when the bells started.

Not the market bells. Market bells are cheerful, greedy things, rung to announce fresh eels or a cart of late plums. These came from the cathedral, and they came wrong — arrhythmic, urgent, the tolling they use for fires and royal deaths. Every head on Copperlane Row turned at once, a field of faces tilting like sunflowers toward a sun that had gone out.

Odile forgot about her ribbon. That should have told me something. Odile never forgot about a thing she could take for free.

* * *

The criers came through in threes, as they always do for proclamations of blood — one to shout, two to hold back the crowd and look official. This one was young, red-faced, with the raw throat of a man who had been shouting since dawn and would be hoarse by noon and rich by nightfall, because the crown paid criers double for death-notices.

"BY ORDER OF HIS MAJESTY THE KING—" and here he unrolled a scroll with a flourish that suggested he'd practiced in front of a mirror, "—THE GIFT HUNT IS DECLARED THROUGHOUT THE REALM AND ITS TERRITORIES. ALL PERSONS POSSESSING THE UNNATURAL GIFT, HOWSOEVER CALLED — WITCHERY, CRAFT, THE OLD BLOOD, THE QUIET ARTS — SHALL PRESENT THEMSELVES FOR REGISTRATION AT THEIR PARISH OFFICE WITHIN NINE DAYS."

He paused for breath. The crowd made no sound at all. In my experience crowds are like animals in that respect: they go quiet before the storm, not during it.

"THOSE WHO REGISTER SHALL BE MARKED, TAXED, AND PERMITTED TO LIVE UNDER CROWN SUPERVISION." A beat. He was enjoying this part; you could hear him enjoying it. "THOSE WHO DO NOT REGISTER SHALL BE HELD TO BE IN CONCEALMENT, AND CONCEALMENT OF THE GIFT IS TREASON AGAINST THE CROWN AND GOD. THE PUNISHMENT FOR TREASON—"

He didn't finish, because everyone knew. Everyone had always known. We didn't need the formula read aloud any more than we needed the alphabet explained. The punishment for treason was fire, and the punishment had been theoretical for so long that we had all gotten very good at pretending it was furniture — present in the room, but ignorable.

The crier moved on down the row. His colleagues stayed, hands folded over their batons, watching us with the mild professional interest of men counting sheep.

I stood behind my stall of harmless trinkets and did arithmetic.

Nine days. Registration meant marking, taxing, supervision — which meant examination, which meant a crown examiner laying his palm against your skin and asking polite questions until your gift leaked out of you like ink through wet paper. I had seen what happened to registered gift-bearers in the provinces, in the years when registration was merely local enthusiasm rather than royal policy. They ended up in the King's service, drained on schedule like dairy cows, or they ended up in the fire anyway, because a marked witch is only a witch awaiting paperwork.

Twenty years. Twenty years I had sold charms that worked by suggestion and sleight of hand, harvested memories only from people who laughed while they gave them, kept my ledgers in cipher, kept my face forgettable, kept my name — well. Kept a name. And all it took was nine days and a scroll to make the whole architecture look like what it had always been: a sandcastle built below the tideline, admired nightly by its architect.

"Copper for your thoughts, Joubert." Old Perrin from the lamp-oil stall, elbowing me, grinning with the nervous grin of a man who wants reassurance and is offering copper instead. "Good thing neither of us has anything to hide, eh?"

"No indeed," I said. "Nothing at all. My conscience is so clean it squeaks."

"Ha! Listen to him. Squeaking conscience." He laughed too loudly and went back to his oil, and I watched him laugh, and I thought: *Perrin, you dear fool, you've bought headache-charms from me for eleven years, and every single one of them was real.*

That was the trouble with twenty years of small kindnesses built on a secret. Each one had seemed safe alone. Stacked together, they were a confession.

* * *

By midday, Copperlane Row had emptied the way water leaves a sink.

It wasn't panic. Panic is loud. This was quieter and worse — the slow, deliberate withdrawal of people who have decided, without discussing it with anyone, that proximity is now a liability. Odile packed up her stall before noon and did not haggle. Perrin closed early, muttering about his wife's health. The pie-seller whose pies I had eaten twice a week for a decade suddenly had somewhere else to be, and took her cart, and did not say where.

I sat behind my own stall like an idiot, selling nothing, because packing up first is what a guilty man does, and I have survived twenty years by being the second-slowest gazelle. Let someone else bolt. Let the criers' assistants watch the ones who bolt.

A boy I didn't know lingered at my table, turning over a tin whistle with dirty fingers. Twelve, maybe. Sharp-eyed.

"You're the charm-man," he said. "They say some charm-men are real witches."

"They say a lot of things, mostly to hear themselves talk."

"My da says the ones who don't register get burned in the Granary Square." He set down the whistle. "Will you register?"

"Kid," I said, "the only magic I've got is making money disappear. Ask my landlord."

He grinned, gap-toothed, and ran off, and I stood there with my heart going like a drum in a parade, thinking: *that child will remember my face.* Everyone would remember my face now. That was the true mechanism of a hunt — not the soldiers, not the fire, but the ordinary neighbors, each one a lens focusing sunlight onto whoever they'd decided to notice. You didn't need to be caught. You needed to be remembered.

I closed the shop at dusk, earlier than I'd ever closed it, and hung the sign the way I always did. Then I stood in the empty street a long moment, breathing air that tasted of chimney smoke and fear, and went inside to burn twenty years of my life.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about destroying evidence: it takes ages.

My ledgers were not, admittedly, supposed to be readable — cipher of my own design, forty years of paranoia distilled into a script that looked like merchant shorthand if you squinted and like madness if you didn't. But paper burns whether it's legible or not, and there was so much of it. Twenty years of customers. Twenty years of trades: the wedding day for the love-token, the sailor's memory of his mother's kitchen for a luck-piece, the grieving woman's last afternoon with her son, given freely, gladly, because she wanted to stop carrying it and I was the only shop in the city with a shelf for that sort of thing.

People think a memory-thief steals. Most of what I actually did was disposal. I was less a thief than a rag-and-bone man of the soul, carting off what people couldn't bear to keep.

Some of it I still had. Forty-one vials, stoppered, labeled. Some of those belonged in the fire too — not because they were dangerous, but because they weren't mine and never had been, and a man about to run should travel light and owe nothing.

I fed the ledger pages into the stove one at a time, watching each curl and blacken, and I drank cheap brandy, and I conducted a thorough inventory of my sins, which is a grim way to spend an evening and worse sober.

The worst page wasn't a trade at all. It was a gap. Page after page in my oldest ledger — the one from before I learned to write neatly — covered in my own handwriting, in my own cipher, recording transactions I had no memory of making. Dates. Amounts. A recurring notation I'd never deciphered, a little hooked symbol repeated dozens of times, always followed by the same word rendered in cipher: *east*. I had found that ledger at nineteen, already filled, and assumed I'd been drunk, or mad, or both. I'd kept it out of superstition, the way you keep a key to a door you can't find.

Now the fire was eating it, and I felt a grief entirely disproportionate to losing a book I couldn't read. Somewhere under my own skull was a locked room, and I had just burned the only inventory of it.

Well. Locked rooms stay locked until someone pays for the key. That much I knew about myself, even if I didn't know how I knew it.

The vials were harder. I lined them up on the workbench — forty-one small green lights, because stored memory glows faintly, like foxfire, a fact I had spent twenty years making sure no customer ever noticed — and I told myself to smash them, pour them out, give them back. A man running for his life doesn't need cargo. A man running for his life especially doesn't need cargo that proves everything.

But I couldn't. Not yet. Call it sentiment. Call it the collector's disease that lives at the root of every thief. Call it the simple truth that these were pieces of other people's lives, and I had promised — silently, in the privacy of every trade — to keep them safe. If I smashed them, they'd be gone forever, and whatever else I was, I had never destroyed anything I carried.

So: I wrapped them in wool, wool in oilcloth, oilcloth in a false bottom of my traveling case, and hated myself efficiently while doing it.

The ledgers burned until nearly midnight. When the last page was ash, I swept the cold grate, scattered the ash in the rain barrel, scrubbed the stove, and sat down at my table with a blank sheet of paper to do the thing that terrified me more than soldiers or fire:

Plan.

* * *

Let me tell you how a coward plans an escape, since apparently I'm qualified.

First: money. I had coin sewn into three separate places on my person, plus a modest cache under the floorboard, plus — this is the embarrassing one — a hollowed-out copy of *Meditations on Humility* containing sixty crowns, because irony is free and money isn't. Enough to travel far if I traveled poor, and poorly if I traveled fast.

Second: route. East. I don't know why east. I want that on the record — I sat there with a map, and my finger went east before my brain had any opinion on the matter, toward the mountains, toward the passes, away from every port and border crossing a sensible fugitive would aim for. I stared at my own hand like it had betrayed me, then wrote *E?* in the margin, then crossed it out, then wrote it again.

Third — and here the plan stopped being a plan and became a wound — Jude.

Jude of the palace conservatory, who had appeared at my stall ten days ago with an impossible errand and a smile like a door left open in winter. Jude who had come back twice more without any errand at all. Jude who asked questions — charming, funny, sideways questions — and I had answered them, because I liked the sound of their laugh and the dirt permanently under their fingernails and the way they looked at my trinkets like a botanist looking at specimens.

An order for the royal conservatory. Charms for the king's own gardeners. Nobody sends a palace gardener to Copperlane Row for charms unless they want the charm-seller watched, and I had known that, and I had flirted with the trap anyway, because twenty years of caution is exhausting and Jude's smile was a vacation from it.

Stupid. Sentimental. Possibly fatal.

And yet. When the crier had shouted *nine days*, my mind had not gone to routes or caches. It had gone to a specific moment: Jude, last visit, turning a glass bead in the light, saying, *"If you ever had to leave this city, Crane — hypothetically — where would you go?"*

Hypothetically. Who asks a hypothetical like that? Who watches the answer with that particular stillness?

Either Jude was the trap, in which case going near them was suicide. Or Jude was warning me, in the only dialect available to someone standing inside a machine — in which case going near them was also suicide, but a warmer kind.

I put my head down on the table, on top of the map with its treacherous eastward smudge, and I laughed until it wasn't laughing anymore.

Nine days. Eight, now. The bells had stopped hours ago; the city outside my window was very quiet, the particular quiet of ten thousand people deciding whom to report by breakfast.

Tomorrow I would close the shop for repairs — a believable lie, landlords being universally credible villains. Tomorrow I would buy passage of a disreputable sort with disreputable coin. Tomorrow I would begin becoming someone else, again, for the third time in one lifetime.

Tonight, one more thing remained. I took out the oldest ledger's surviving scrap — the hooked symbol, the word *east*, the one page I had pulled from the fire half-charred because I couldn't make my fingers let go of it — and pinned it inside my coat, over my heart, where the stupidest decisions are stored.

And then, because the night demanded one more stupidity to round out the set, I blew out the candle and said aloud, to no one, in an empty room:

"If you're going to warn me, Jude, tonight would be excellent."

The city said nothing back. Cities are cowards too.

But I had barely gotten my boots off when someone knocked at my door — soft, twice, in a rhythm I knew, and my heart did the unforgivable thing and leapt before my fear could catch it.
