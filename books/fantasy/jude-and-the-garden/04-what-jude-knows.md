## 4. What Jude Knows

There is a particular way you learn to stand when you're about to lie to a man who can have your sister killed.

You don't slouch — slouching reads as fear. You don't stand too straight either, because that reads as rehearsal. You lean, slightly, against the doorframe of his office as though you've got somewhere better to be and he's wasting your time, and you let your eyes drift around the room like the room bores you. I'd been doing it for six years. I was very good at it. Some nights, lying in the gardeners' dormitory with my hands smelling of loam and other people's secrets, I thought it might be the only thing I'd ever been good at.

Handler Vess's office sat in the east wing of the palace, above the orchards, which was a joke someone in the crown's employ had surely appreciated: put the man who ran informants directly over the fruit trees, so that every autumn the smell of ripening apples drifted through his windows and sweetened whatever foulness he was conducting below. He kept the window open even now, in the cold, because Vess had a theory that fresh air kept his mind sharp. It also meant that anyone in the orchard could hear him if they stood on a ladder. Nobody ever did. Fear keeps better order than any wall.

"Sit," he said.

I didn't sit. That was part of the choreography too. "I'm due back at the conservatory by four," I said. "The head gardener wants the winter roses pruned before frost, and she counts the minutes I'm gone the way misers count coins."

"The roses will survive an hour." He poured two glasses of plum wine from the bottle he kept for occasions he wanted to feel like celebrations. I took mine because refusing it would be its own kind of message, and drank none of it. "Report."

So. Here is what I told him, and here is what I didn't, and if you want to understand anything about me — about Jude, palace gardener, loyal servant of the crown, six years in the crown's quiet employ — you'll want to pay attention to the difference between those two lists.

* * *

The charm shop sat on Tallow Lane, third from the corner where the lane bent around the old tannery, wedged between a candlemaker's and a shop that sold nothing but buttons, which even in a city full of strange commerce struck me as a gamble. The sign above the door read simply *CHARMS*, hand-painted, the letters going slightly crooked toward the end as though the painter had run out of patience or paint or both.

I described all this to Vess because Vess liked description. He liked to feel that his people saw things. He'd once spent forty minutes asking me about the grain of the wood on Quentin Joubert's counter, as though the answer might tell him something no amount of surveillance could. Maybe it had. Maybe that's how he thought. I never understood the man and I'd stopped trying, the way you stop trying to understand weather.

"Business is steady," I said. "Trinkets mostly. Charms against headache, charms for luck at cards, charms new mothers pin above cradles. Nothing that works — I've tested half of them myself. A bit of tin, a bit of glass, some dried herbs sewn into leather. The seller talks well enough that people convince themselves."

"And the seller?"

"Quentin Joubert." I let the name sit there a moment. Six years I'd been saying that name to this man, in this room, in this careful voice, and every time I said it something behind my ribs pulled tight, like a stitch in a garment made for a smaller body. "Mid-forties, maybe. Keeps regular hours. Closes early on market days. Lives above the shop alone. No family anyone speaks of. No close friends — customers, but not friends. Buys bread from the baker on the corner, fish on Fridays, doesn't drink much. Pays taxes promptly." I shrugged. "A gray sort of life, honestly. You'd die of boredom before you died of them."

Vess smiled. He had a smile like a crack in porcelain — fine, decorative, and indicating damage underneath. "And yet."

"And yet," I agreed, because we always arrived at *and yet*. It was practically the office motto. I could imagine it stitched on a cushion: AND YET. The crown's entire intelligence apparatus, summarized in embroidery.

Because here was the thing, the thing that had started all of this, six years ago, before I knew Quentin Joubert as anything more than a name on a list of persons of interest: the crown had received a report. Someone, somewhere, years back, had claimed to have sold their grief to the charm-seller on Tallow Lane. Had walked out of that shop lighter than they'd walked in, with their sorrow gone and no clear memory of the transaction itself, only the sense of having set down a heavy bag. The claimant had been drunk, then dying, then dead, and the crown could extract nothing more from them. But the report existed. And so a name went on a list, and the list went into a drawer, and eventually the drawer opened onto me.

Why me? Because I owed money. Because my sister was sick. Because a man named Vess had appeared at my sister's sickbed with an offer shaped like kindness, and I had been twenty-three years old and desperate enough to mistake the shape of a hook for the shape of a hand. But we're getting ahead of ourselves. Vess didn't care why I did it. He only cared that I did.

"You've seen nothing anomalous," Vess said. It wasn't quite a question. It was a question wearing a question's clothes, though, the way his statements always were.

"I've seen nothing," I said.

Which was true. Which was also, in the way that matters most, a lie. Let me explain the difference, because I lived inside that difference for six years and it fit me eventually like my own skin.

* * *

Here is what I had actually seen, across six years of buying charms I didn't need, of lingering over the counter while other customers came and went, of learning the rhythm of Quentin Joubert's days the way you learn a song without meaning to:

I had seen a woman leave the shop after her husband's funeral and walk differently than she'd walked in. Lighter. Not the lightness of distraction or drink. The specific lightness of a person who has set something down.

I had seen a young man come in three times in one month, each time selling a little more of something I couldn't see, each time coming out paler, until the fourth visit Quentin turned him away at the door and wouldn't take his coin, and the young man had wept in the street — not with grief, I thought, but with relief. Like a man refused another drink.

I had seen Quentin, twice, do nothing at all — sell a charm, take the coin, send the customer off — and watch them go with an expression I couldn't file under any category the crown had given me. Not greed. Not guilt exactly. Something older and wearier than guilt. The look of someone doing work they know is killing them a little at a time, and doing it anyway, and hating themselves in a way that has stopped generating heat and become merely structural, load-bearing self-hatred, the kind that holds a life up.

And once — once, and I have turned this memory over so many times it's worn smooth as a river stone — I had seen Quentin catch me watching. Just for a moment. Across the crowded shop, over the shoulder of a customer haggling over a luck-charm, our eyes met, and Quentin looked at me the way you look at a familiar piece of furniture that has suddenly moved. Recognition without place. And then the moment passed, and Quentin laughed at something the customer said, and I bought a charm against nightmares that I still have, still carry, still sometimes hold in the dark like a stone I can't stop turning in my hand.

Nothing anomalous. Right.

Here is what I did not tell Vess. I did not tell him that three weeks ago, a customer had left the shop, and Quentin had watched them go, and then Quentin had caught sight of me lingering by the button-shop window where I always lingered, pretending to compare buttons, and Quentin had smiled — not the merchant's smile, the other one, small and crooked and aimed at me alone, the one that said *you again* — and something in my chest had done a thing I am not going to describe to a man with a ledger full of ways to kill me.

I did not tell him that last week, when the frost came early and I'd come to the shop ostensibly to buy a charm for the conservatory's citrus trees, Quentin had pressed a cup of spiced wine into my hands and said, "You look half-frozen, gardener. Do they work you to death up there, or do you just enjoy looking miserable?" And I had said, "Both, usually," and Quentin had laughed, and the laugh had done something to the air in the room, warmed it, and I had stood there holding my cup thinking: *this person. This person is the person.* And then immediately thinking: *you absolute fool.*

I did not tell him that the night before, walking home past the tannery, I had rehearsed — out loud, in the dark, like a madman — the sentence I would say if I ever found the courage, some version of *there's a place outside the city I've wanted to see,* or *do you ever close early?* Rehearsed it the way you rehearse a confession. Which is what it was, though I hadn't let myself call it that.

And most of all — most crucially, in a way that would matter sooner than I knew — I did not tell him about the ledger.

Two weeks ago I'd stayed late at the shop, later than usual, later than my cover strictly required, helping Quentin haul a crate of stock up from the cellar because the delivery boy hadn't come. In the cellar, while Quentin wrestled with the crate, I'd seen the shelf. Ledgers. Twenty years of ledgers, their spines cracked and faded, arranged in neat chronological order. And Quentin had caught me looking and said — lightly, too lightly — "Accounts. Thrilling stuff. You gardeners would weep with envy at such orderly rows."

It had sounded like a joke. It had landed like a plea. *Don't ask. Don't look. Let me keep this one thing.*

And God help me, I had let it go. I had carried the crate upstairs and drunk my wine and bought my useless charm and walked home through streets going blue with dusk, and all the way home I'd thought: *those aren't accounts.* Accounts don't get stored like relics. Those were records of transactions, yes, but of what kind? If Quentin were truly just a fraud selling tin and optimism, the ledgers were harmless — inventory, income, the dull machinery of a small business. But if Quentin were what the crown believed—

Then those books were a confession. Twenty years of confessions, bound in cracked leather, sitting in a cellar on Tallow Lane, waiting for someone like me to mention them to someone like Vess.

I had mentioned nothing. I had omitted them entirely, and the omission had felt less like a lie and more like the only honest act available to me, which tells you everything about what my life had become.

"So," Vess said now, setting down his untasted wine. "Six years. Nothing. A fraud selling dried herbs to gulls." He leaned forward. "You understand, gardener, that reports continue to arrive."

My stomach dropped the way it does on stairs in the dark when you expect one more step and find floor. "What kind of reports?"

"A pilgrim, passing through last month. Claims the charm-seller returned something to her. Her mother's voice, she says. A memory she'd sold years ago, freely given back, no charge. She wept in the street about it, apparently. Made rather a scene." He examined his fingernails. "Memory doesn't come back, gardener. Everyone knows that. Unless it was never really gone."

"That's one drunk pilgrim's story."

"It is. And stories are how this whole affair began, if you recall." He rose and went to the window, standing with his back to me, looking down at the bare orchard. When he spoke again his voice had shifted into the register I dreaded, the reasonable one, the patient one, the voice of a man explaining arithmetic to a child. "The hunt begins soon, Jude. Perhaps within the month. There are proclamations being drafted. Registration rolls. Lists." He turned. "When it comes, the crown will move through this city like fire through dry grass. Every gift-bearer, every hedge-witch, every cunning man and charm-monger. Registered or destroyed. And I would prefer — purely for reasons of tidiness — that when we come to Tallow Lane, we already know what we'll find there. A fraud, and we cross the name off. Or something realer, and we act accordingly. Either way, I need certainty. Certainty is the whole of my profession." He smiled his porcelain smile. "Give me certainty."

"And if I can't?" I asked. My voice came out level. I was almost proud of it.

"Then I'll have to wonder why." He said it gently. Gently was worse. "Six years of wages. Your sister's care, paid in full, these many years. A debt forgiven that would have taken you thirty to discharge." He picked up his wine at last and drank, watching me over the rim. "You've been a good investment, Jude. I'd hate to think the returns had stopped."

There it was. The leash, run through its rings, pulled just enough to remind me it was there. Somewhere across the city, in a sanatorium that smelled of camphor and boiled linen, my sister was alive because Vess said she was, healed because Vess paid for it, and every year of her recovery was another year of my obedience, compounded. They never threatened her. Threatening would have given me something to fight. They just reminded me, softly, that she continued to exist at their pleasure. It was elegant. I hated how elegant it was.

"I'll need more time," I said. "If there's something to find, I'll find it properly. Not on a deadline. Rushing makes mistakes, and you don't want mistakes, you want certainty. Your word."

Something flickered behind Vess's eyes — annoyance, maybe, or respect, or the cold pleasure of a man watching a dog strain at a rope it doesn't yet know is tied off. "A fortnight," he said. "Then I act regardless. And Jude—" I paused at the door. "The cellar. Shops like that always have cellars. Look in cellars."

He'd known. Of course he'd known. He always knew; that was the whole horror of Vess, that you never actually withheld anything, you only delayed him, and delay was just interest accruing on a debt he'd collect eventually with the principal.

"Yes sir," I said, and went out, and walked down through the palace corridors with the plum wine untouched in my blood and my heart going like a hammer on a nail.

* * *

That evening I stood in the royal conservatory with pruning shears in my hand and did not prune anything.

The conservatory was my favorite place in the palace, which is a small thing to admit and cost me something to admit even privately. Glass roofs and iron ribs, warm wet air, plants from a dozen climates crowded together in tolerant proximity — ferns beside cacti, orchids drooping over rosemary, everything growing toward the same pale winter light. I liked the work. That was the trap within the trap: they'd given me a job I loved, so that leaving would cost, so that staying would feel like choice. Vess thought of everything. I suspected he thought of it in the bath, humming.

The citrus trees stood in their row near the south glass, the ones I'd bought Quentin's useless charm for. I'd hung it among the branches months ago — a twist of tin and lavender on a red string — and told myself it was cover, tradecraft, maintaining the fiction of a customer. But the tree it hung on had fruited this year, heavy and golden, when the neighboring trees had barely blossomed, and I had caught myself, more than once, touching the little charm like a man touching wood against bad luck. Knowing it was tin. Believing anyway. That was the whole trick of Quentin Joubert, I was beginning to understand. Not magic — or not only. Conviction, freely transferred. People walked out of that shop believing in something, and belief turned out to be nearly as good as the real thing, and maybe that was the real thing, and maybe I was not the right person to be making these determinations at seven in the evening among the orange trees.

I thought about the fortnight. Fourteen days. Then lists, and registration, and fire through dry grass, and soldiers on Tallow Lane, and Quentin —

I put the shears down.

Here is the truth I arrived at, standing among the oranges with my breath fogging the cooling glass, and I want to state it plainly because I spent six years refusing to state it plainly: I had known for a long time. Not the details — the crown never gave me details and I never asked, asking felt like signing something — but the shape. The shape of what I was. An informant. A leash-holder. The reason the hunt would someday knock on a certain crooked-lettered door, and the reason it would knock accurately.

And I had kept doing it because of Lira, because of the debt, because the alternative had teeth. Fine. Coercion is real. Fear is real. I will not pretend I was free when I wasn't; that's its own dishonesty.

But somewhere in the last year — I couldn't date it, couldn't point to the day, it had happened the way seasons happen — the balance had shifted. The fear had stayed the same size and the rest of me had grown around it, the way a tree grows around a fence wire, incorporating the constraint until the constraint is part of the grain. And now the wire was starting to cut. Now the tree was big enough to snap it, if it chose, and bleed doing it.

Fourteen days. In fourteen days, Vess moved with or without my report. Which meant that whatever I was going to do — warn, flee, confess, betray one master or the other or both — I had fourteen days to do it in, and every day I delayed was a day closer to soldiers on Tallow Lane.

Unless I sped things up. Unless the timeline wasn't Vess's to control entirely.

I looked at the little charm swinging in the citrus branches, tin and lavender and red string, worthless beyond measure.

I finished the pruning after all. Head gardeners notice unpruned roses the way Vess noticed omissions, and I had enough watchers in my life. Then I washed, ate, and lay in my narrow bed in the dormitory listening to the other gardeners breathe, and I made plans the way you make plans in the dark: roughly, desperately, holding each one up to the light of morning to see which survived.

One plan survived. It required me to go to Tallow Lane tomorrow, earlier than usual, with an errand plausible enough to pass and urgent enough to justify my face. It required me to look Quentin Joubert in the eye and buy something and say something ordinary, and to decide — finally, irrevocably, with my sister's breath hanging in the balance — whether I was a leash or a man.

And beneath the plan, beneath the fear and the arithmetic, one detail kept surfacing, the way a drowned thing surfaces, patient and inevitable: the cellar. The ledgers. Twenty years of a life written down in a room with one door.

Whatever happened next, I thought, staring at the ceiling, someone was going to burn those books. The only question left was whether it would be the crown, with the shop still standing and its keeper inside it —

or me, first, with a warning shouted ahead of the flames.

Tomorrow, I told the dark. Tomorrow I'd know which one I was.

The dark, being experienced in my promises, declined to answer.
