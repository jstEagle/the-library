## 5. The Knock at Midnight

I have always believed that the difference between a coward and a careful person is roughly forty years and a good night's sleep, and that I was firmly in the second category. Careful people do not get caught. Careful people count their coins twice, water their wine, and never — *never* — keep a ledger they cannot eat.

The problem with burning twenty years of ledgers is that ash does not burn twice. The problem with resolve is that it keeps you awake past midnight, staring at your ceiling like a woman waiting for a lover who is actually a headsman.

So when the knock came, I was not asleep. I want that on the record, wherever records go after cities fall. I was awake, dressed, sitting on my stool behind the counter with a cold cup of chicory and the particular clarity of mind that arrives only when you have already imagined your own death several hundred times and found it tedious.

Three knocks. Measured. Not the drunken fist of a constable, not the polite rap of a customer. The knock of men who are paid by the hour and know the door will open because doors always open for them eventually.

I looked at the door. The door, being a door, said nothing.

And then, before I could move — before I could decide whether to run out the back into the alley where, I would later learn, four soldiers stood smoking — someone knocked on the *window*. Softly. Twice. A rhythm I knew: the little pattern Jude used to tap on my counter when he wanted me to look up from my scales, two quick and one slow, like a bird deciding whether to land.

I crossed the shop without breathing and put my eye to the shutter crack.

Jude stood in the street in his palace greens, mud to the knee, holding a lantern with the shutter half-closed so its light made a coin of gold on the cobblestones between us. His face did something I had never seen it do. It was not afraid. It was worse than afraid. It was decided.

"Quentin," he whispered through the gap. "Back door. Now. There are six at the front, four in the alley, and a sergeant named Voss who has already written his report."

My body understood before I did. Twenty years of careful living had trained it well: hands moving, feet moving, the little drawer of charms swept into my apron pocket without my permission. My mind, meanwhile, stood in the middle of the room asking questions with the useless calm of a tax clerk.

*Why is he here? How does he know? How does a palace gardener know the name of a sergeant?*

"Jude," I whispered back. "What is this?"

"Later." His eyes were very bright in the lantern light. "Please. I counted the minutes wrong once already tonight. Don't make me be late twice."

There is a moment in every life, I think, where trust stops being a calculation and becomes a leap taken with your boots still unlaced. I had known this man three weeks. He brought me conservatory orders and complained about the crown's taste in topiary and once ate an entire meat pie standing in my doorway as though food might be outlawed next week. He laughed at my jokes a half-beat late, which I had privately diagnosed as either sincerity or excellent craft. And now here he was at midnight, calling soldiers by name, telling me to run.

Every sensible voice I owned said: *he is the trap. This is how it works — the kind face at the window, the escape route that leads exactly where they want you.*

But the sensible voices also said: *you have thirty seconds.*

"Through the cellar," I said. "Follow me and don't touch anything that hums."

"That's an odd instruction."

"You'll thank me. Everything down there hums."

* * *

The cellar of my shop had been, in a former century, a cistern-keeper's post. The city above was built on the bones of an older, wetter city, and the older city had left its plumbing everywhere, like a guest who forgets things in every room. I had bought the shop partly for the cellar and told the landlord it was for storage. In truth it was for the hatch under the flour sacks, and the stairs beneath the hatch, and the long black throat of the cistern tunnel that ran east toward the river gate.

I lit no lamp. I know those stairs the way I know my own teeth. Behind me, Jude descended in the dark with the uncomplaining competence of a man who has carried buckets in worse places, which — I would come to understand — he had.

At the bottom, the tunnel breathed. Cold air, wet stone, the smell of old rain. Somewhere ahead, water dripped in a rhythm too regular to be natural, and if you pressed your ear to the third arch from the stair you could hear the whole buried system ticking like a clock: the city's second heartbeat, the one nobody upstairs remembered owning.

"Where does it come out?" Jude asked quietly.

"The tannery sluice, near the river gate. Then we're outside the wall, if the flood-gates haven't been chained." I hesitated. "They chain them when there's a hunt."

"There's a hunt."

"Yes," I said. "I'd gathered."

We walked. The dark was total and then, gradually, wasn't — my eyes adjusting until the tunnel resolved into grey suggestion, arch after arch, our footsteps making small obscene echoes. I could hear him behind me, close enough that I felt his breath move the hair on my neck when he leaned forward to speak.

"Your hand is shaking," he said.

"My hand is *cold*. There's a difference, and I'll thank you not to narrate my physiology."

"It's shaking like a leaf, Quentin."

"It's a very cold leaf."

He didn't laugh. That frightened me more than the knocking had. Jude laughing was the world being ordinary; Jude silent was the world admitting what it was.

We reached the first junction, where the tunnels forked toward the merchant quarter and the river, and I stopped — partly to listen, mostly because my legs had filed a formal complaint. In the silence I heard it: boots. Above us, muffled by stone, many boots, and beneath them a sound I knew from the proclamation day, the sound of the market emptying itself around me — only now it was the *city* doing it, block by block, door by door.

"They're sweeping the streets," I whispered.

"Sweeping toward the river. Voss thinks you'll make for the docks. Everyone makes for the docks; it's in the manual." He paused. "That's why we shouldn't."

"*We*?" I turned to him, though I couldn't see him, which made the word easier and harder to say. "Since when is there a *we*, gardener?"

The silence went on a beat too long. Long enough for me to hear him swallow.

"Since about ten minutes ago," he said finally, "when I chose which side of a locked door to stand on. Ask me again in the morning. Or don't. But right now the sluice gate is two hundred paces that way, and it opens from the inside, and I am going to walk you to it even if you spend the entire walk interrogating me."

"I hadn't planned to interrogate. I planned to sulk."

"That I believe."

* * *

The sluice gate was a thing of beauty, which is to say it was ancient, rusted, and hated everyone equally. It took both of us to lift it — his shoulders against the crossbar, mine hauling the counterweight rope that shrieked like something murdered — and when the gap opened, black water gushed over our boots and out into the night beyond the wall, carrying the smell of the tanneries with it like a calling card.

I went through first. Of course I did. Cowardice and caution wear the same coat.

On the far bank, the city fell away behind us — the wall rising black against a sky bruised purple at the edges, watchfires crawling along its top like lice. Somewhere in the warren behind us, soldiers were breaking down the door of my shop, finding a cold cup of chicory, an emptied drawer, and twenty years of nothing. I had burned the ledgers, but I had left the stool. It seemed important, suddenly, that I would never sit on that stool again. You can lose a home in an evening. What they don't tell you is how specific the grief is. Not the bed. Not the roof. The *stool*.

Jude climbed through the sluice after me and stood dripping in the reeds, lantern dead, and for a while neither of us spoke. The river ran. The city watched itself in it, upside down, all its fires pointing at heaven like accusations.

"Voss wrote his report," I said at last. "You knew his name. You knew the count of men. You knew the sweep order." I kept my voice level, because a level voice is the last furniture a person owns when everything else burns. "Gardeners don't know sergeants, Jude."

"No," he agreed. "They don't."

"And yet."

"And yet."

The river filled the pause. I wrapped my arms around myself and discovered my hands had stopped shaking, which meant the fear had gone somewhere deeper, somewhere it intended to stay.

"I'm going to ask you one question tonight," I said. "Just one. You can lie if you like — I'll probably believe you, because I want to, which is my failing and not yours. But whatever answer you give me, I'll carry it into tomorrow, and tomorrows are expensive right now."

He looked at me across the dark. Even soaked, even mud-splattered, there was that steadiness in him, the same steadiness that had made me trust a stranger's conservatory order three weeks ago. Steadiness, I now understood, is also what a well-trained thing looks like.

"Ask," he said.

"Why did you warn me?"

And here is the thing I will remember until memory runs out of road: he didn't answer quickly. He didn't reach for a comfortable lie. He stood in the freezing reeds and visibly *chose*, the way a man chooses which weight to pick up knowing he must carry it a long way.

"Because when the order came," he said slowly, "they read me your description. Height, build, the scar on the left thumb, the habit of tapping scales twice before weighing." He held up his own hand. On the back of it, faintly, was an ink smudge — silverweed ink, the kind I used for price tags. "And I realized I've been touching that counter for three weeks, and I couldn't tell them where you sleep. So I told them where you *were* instead, and gave them a door that would be empty, and prayed to a god I don't believe in that you'd still be awake."

It was not a confession. It was not innocence. It was some third thing, raw-edged, offered with both hands. I had spent twenty years reading people's memories off their skin like weather, and I had never once needed the gift to know that this man was telling me almost none of what was true, and all of what mattered.

"Almost none of what's true," I said aloud.

His jaw tightened. "Yes."

"But all of what matters."

"I don't know," he said, and his voice cracked on it, just slightly, just enough. "I honestly don't know yet. That's the truth I have tonight. It's not much. It's what's in the purse."

I should have run from him then. Every careful bone in my body filed its motion, cited precedent, demanded a vote. Instead I heard myself say:

"There's a shrine two miles east, at the crossroads — pilgrims' shelter, no priest since the plague year. We walk through the marsh and sleep in the hay loft. At dawn you tell me the rest. All of it, or as much as you can stand, and I'll take whatever you can stand and hate you gently for the remainder."

"That's a terrible bargain."

"It's the only one on offer."

He almost smiled. It was a ruined smile, half-drowned, but it was his, and I found — to my considerable irritation — that seeing it loosened something in my chest that the soldiers' knocking had tied shut.

"All right," he said. "The marsh, the hay, the dawn. And Quentin—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Thank you. For the blind part. I know what it costs."

"You don't," I said. "But you will. Come on. The marsh waits for no one, and unlike me, it has no patience whatsoever."

* * *

The marsh took us in the way marshes do — ankle-deep, then knee-deep, then that sucking ambiguity where you stop being sure whether you're walking on ground or swimming badly. The reeds closed overhead and the city vanished, wall and fires and all, as though it had been a stage set and someone backstage had grown tired of us. Ahead, the crossroads shrine showed a single guttering votive light, orange as a coal, promising hay and rot and safety of a sort.

We walked in single file, Jude behind me, and I passed the time the way I always pass unbearable stretches: by cataloguing. One apron of charms, mostly worthless. One knife, small. One gift, thinning with every mile from the shop, the way a flame gutters in a draft — or perhaps that was only fear talking; gifts are superstitious that way. One companion of uncertain allegiance, proven courage, and mud to the thigh.

One future, previously furnished, now reduced to a hay loft and a dawn appointment with a truth I already dreaded.

Behind me, Jude stumbled, swore softly, and kept walking. And I thought: *whatever he is — informer, traitor, fool, saint — he is walking east into exile on the strength of ten minutes of decision.* People do not do that lightly. People do not do that at all unless something in them broke, or woke, or both.

At the edge of the reeds I stopped and looked back once. The city sat on its hill with its thousand lights, and somewhere among them a sergeant named Voss was writing his report, and in the report, I suspected, there was a line about a helpful witness from the palace gardens, and a door that had been empty.

"He'll hang for it," I said. "If they learn. They'll hang you for it."

"I know," Jude said, beside me now, looking back at the lights with an expression I couldn't read in the dark and wouldn't try to.

"And still you came."

"Still I came."

We turned east, away from the fires, toward the marsh and the hay and the dawn. The votive light at the shrine bobbed nearer, patient as a promise. And beneath my ribs, in the place where my careful heart lived, something shifted its weight and settled — not peace, nothing so clean as peace, but the strange, terrified steadiness of a person who has leapt and not yet looked down.

Trust extended blindly, I thought. May the gods of fools and gardeners forgive us both.

Behind us, faint across the water, a bell began to ring. Not the alarm bell. The other one. The one they rang, I would learn by morning, for lists — names read aloud to the city, one by one, beginning with the letter Q.
