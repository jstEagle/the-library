## 6. Salt and Ash Road

*Quentin*

The trouble with fleeing a city that wants you dead is that you have to do it at the speed of everyone else's business.

We joined the east road on the third morning out of the cisterns, when the stink of the flood-tunnels had finally stopped living in my hair, and we did it the way a thousand other people were doing it: slowly, publicly, in the company of strangers. The Gift Hunt had emptied half the eastern provinces. Farmers who'd read omens in their own wells. Weavers whose grandmothers had whispered to thread. Families carrying everything they owned in bundles that said *we are not running, we are traveling* in letters large enough for soldiers to read from a distance.

Jude bought us pilgrim gray from a peddler whose prices suggested he knew exactly what fear cost per yard. Two staffs. A tin badge of Saint Ferren of the Wells, patron of wanderers and, I suspect, of peddlers who profit off them.

"Pilgrims," Jude said, turning the badge over. "To where?"

"Does it matter? Nobody asks pilgrims where they're going. They only ask if they're blessed."

"And are we?"

"I've been blessed by worse," I said, and took the badge.

Here is what nobody tells you about running for your life: it is mostly waiting. Waiting for ferries. Waiting for bread. Waiting for the man ahead of you at the well to finish telling his entire family history to a stranger who never asked. Jude waited beside me through all of it with the patience of someone who has spent years being paid by the hour, and I found — this is the shameful part, the part I'd deny under oath — I found I didn't mind. The road was ash-gray and the checkpoints were frequent and every night I lay awake cataloguing all the ways we could be caught. But there was a strange rest in it too. Twenty years of careful, solitary, exhausting smallness, and now here I was, committing the largest crime of my life in broad daylight, walking beside a gardener who whistled off-key and shared their bread without being asked.

I should have known better. Happiness is how traps catch you. Nobody walks into a snare frowning.

* * *

*Jude*

There are three kinds of checkpoints.

The first kind is bored. A sergeant with a ledger and no ambition, who waves pilgrims through because pilgrims are somebody else's paperwork. You give them your name — a false one, chosen in advance, worn so long it fits like an old boot — and they write it down wrong, and you don't correct them, because correcting a bored sergeant is how you become interesting.

The second kind is hungry. Young soldiers, new to their livery, who want to be seen doing diligence. These you survive with humility and detail. Have a story ready that answers questions nobody asked. Jude the pilgrim, going to wash at the springs of Vell because their mother made a vow before she died. Give the dead mother a real-feeling name. Give her a real-feeling cough. Grief is the best disguise ever invented; nobody searches inside sorrow.

The third kind is clever, and you do not survive the third kind. You avoid the third kind. Which means knowing where they post them, which means knowing how the crown thinks, which means—

Which means I know things a pilgrim should not know.

That was the flaw in my own design, sitting there like a stone in my shoe for two hundred miles. Every trick I used to keep us alive was evidence. Every checkpoint I steered us around before it appeared, every patrol I heard coming over a hilltop before the hoofbeats carried, every time I chose which road not to take — Quentin watched me do it. Quentin missed nothing. That was the first thing I'd learned about them, back in the shop, watching those clever hands sort charms: nothing got past those eyes without being weighed and priced.

So I gave them explanations. Pilgrim lore, I called it. Springs and saints and the habits of the road. And mostly they smiled and accepted it, because Quentin wanted to trust me, and wanting something badly is its own kind of blindness.

I know, because I'd been counting on exactly that for six years.

* * *

*Quentin*

The fourth checkpoint was at a bridge over the Sorrel, and the sergeant there was of the second kind — young, thorough, in love with his own authority. He made everyone empty their bundles onto the planks.

Ours held, officially: a change of clothes, bread gone hard as roof tile, a clay saint, dried apples, a comb. Unofficially, in a hidden seam of my pack, three charm-stones I hadn't been able to make myself throw away. Not powerful ones. Little things. A stone that kept milk sweet another day. A stone that eased the ache of old scars. Souvenirs of my whole profession, and enough to hang me twice over.

I felt each one of them burning against my spine like a coal while the sergeant dug through our bread.

Beside me, Jude went still. Not frightened-still — I knew frightened-still by then, had seen it in refugees passing on the road, in my own mirror. This was different. This was the stillness of a body arranging itself, the way an actor finds their mark before the curtain. Their breathing changed. Their shoulders dropped half an inch. Their face did something complicated and brief, like a door opening onto a lit room and closing again before you can see inside.

Then they stepped forward and started crying.

Not sobbing. Nothing vulgar. Just tears standing in their eyes and a voice gone rough at the edges, saying, "Please, sir, my mother died in the spring, and the vow was hers, and if we're turned back her soul won't rest, and I—" They pressed the tin saint into the sergeant's hand. "She'd want him to have it."

The sergeant looked at the badge. Looked at Jude's wet eyes. Looked at me, standing there in honest confusion — and I'll admit the confusion required very little acting, because I had never once heard Jude mention a dead mother or a vow or any of it.

He waved us through. Kept the saint.

It wasn't until we were a mile past the bridge, until the road bent away from the river and the soldiers shrank to smudges behind us, that I let myself say it.

"You cried on command."

Jude glanced at me. The tears were long gone, rinsed away like they'd been turned off at a tap. "Pilgrim tricks. Everyone on this road learns a few."

"Do they." I walked a few steps. "You told that sergeant your mother made a vow at the springs of Vell. Very specific. Very rehearsed."

"It's a common story. Half the pilgrims on this road are carrying some dead parent's promise. It's practically the uniform."

"Mm," I said.

Because here was the thing, and I hated the thing, and I picked the thing up anyway and looked at it from every side like a merchant appraising goods: it wasn't just the story. It was the stillness before it. It was the way Jude's body had known what to do — had *rehearsed* knowing what to do — with the smoothness of long practice. I have spent twenty years reading faces across a market stall. I know the difference between fear and performance wearing fear as a coat.

I said none of this. Instead I asked, lightly, "Did your mother actually die in spring?"

A pause. Small. The length of a heartbeat deciding something. "Yes," Jude said. "Two years ago now."

That much, at least, rang true. And I thought: *there. You see? People carry true griefs and borrow shapes for them. It's harmless. It's survival.* I put the thing down. I picked it up again by nightfall. I put it down. By the third day I'd stopped pretending I'd ever put it down at all.

* * *

*Jude*

At the inn at Marrow's Ford, we shared a room because sharing a room is what poor pilgrims do, and one bed because the innkeeper charged by the bed, and neither of us mentioned the arrangement, which meant we both spent the evening thinking about it.

Quentin sat by the window mending a strap on their pack, and the last light came in sideways and turned them gold at the edges, and I thought — not for the first time — *you could stop. You could walk away from all of it. Find a village, plant something, grow old next to this sharp-eyed, stubborn, impossible person.*

And then I thought about the handler's voice saying *your sister eats better when you're useful,* and I picked the strap-mending silence apart instead, looking for what Quentin was really thinking.

They'd been quiet since the bridge. Three days of quiet, folded small, the way people fold a letter they intend to reread.

"The sergeant at the ford," Quentin said at last, not looking up. "Before he searched us. He checked a list against the arrivals. Did you see?"

"I saw."

"He was checking for registered gift-bearers. Names reported by informants." The needle paused. "Someone has already given names. Whole lists of them. People we may have stood beside in line at the ferry."

"Yes," I said. My mouth had gone dry. "That's how these hunts work."

"How would a pilgrim know how hunts work?"

"A pilgrim listens," I said. "Everyone talks about it now. It's all anyone talks about."

"Mm," Quentin said again, and that little sound was becoming the most frightening word in the language.

They set down the strap and looked at me across the room, and their expression was warm and open and utterly unreadable, which I recognized, with a sick lurch, as my own specialty. We sat there a moment, two liars admiring each other's craftsmanship, and then Quentin smiled and said, "Hand me the bread, would you?" and whatever had almost happened quietly un-happened.

I passed the bread. I ate my share. I lay awake listening to them breathe until deep in the night, doing arithmetic I couldn't balance: every lie keeping us alive was also, eventually, the thing that would end us. Because the crown had taught me its whole grammar, and Quentin was learning to read it off me sentence by sentence, and there were only so many sentences left before they reached the one that began *informant* and ended with soldiers at a garden gate.

* * *

*Quentin*

On the sixth day the road climbed into country where the wind came down off the hills smelling of salt and old fires — hence the name, Jude said, though they said it a beat too quickly, the way people do when they've decided an answer in advance. Salt and Ash Road. The fields on either side were gray-green and empty, and the checkpoints grew sparser, but somehow I felt more watched, not less.

We fell into step together on a long flat stretch, staffs clicking, and for a while neither of us spoke, and it was — I record this honestly, because honesty is the one luxury I've never learned to steal — it was good. Comfortable. Jude hummed their off-key whistle. I matched my stride to theirs without deciding to. Somewhere behind us was a burned life and ahead of us was nothing at all, and between the two was this stretch of road, and I wanted to live on it.

Which is why the doubt hurt the way it did. Doubt always arrives dressed as protection. *I'm only looking out for you,* it says, while it picks the lock on everything you love.

"Tell me about Vell," I said. "The springs. Since we're supposedly walking there."

Jude told me. Water hot enough to cook an egg, terraced pools, an inn that watered the wine. All plausible. All delivered with the easy fluency of a story told many times.

"Have you been?"

"No," Jude said. "Mother talked about it. She never got to go."

I nodded. I watched the road. And then, because I am apparently incapable of leaving a lock unpicked, even one I'm afraid of what's behind: "You steer us around patrols before we can hear them. You knew the ford sergeant would check lists. You cry like a professional. You know the crown's grammar, Jude, even if you've never spoken it."

Their stride didn't break. I'll grant them that. "You think I'm a hunter."

"I think," I said carefully, "that you're something. Everyone's something. I was a charm-seller, which was a lie. What were you, Jude? Before you were a pilgrim?"

The wind came down the hill and filled the silence for us. Jude looked east, at the empty gray fields, and for a moment their face was younger than I'd ever seen it, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with walking.

"I was a gardener," they said. "For six years. Before that—" A breath. "Before that isn't a story for the road."

"When will it be?"

"Soon," Jude said, and their voice was steady, and their eyes weren't.

That night we camped in the lee of a fallen wall, and I took the first watch, and I sat with my back against the cold stones and watched Jude sleep and did not know what I was guarding them from — the crown, or myself, or the truth. In my hidden pocket the three little charm-stones pressed against my hip like accusations. Somewhere east, past all these gray fields, there was a door I didn't yet know existed, waiting for debts I didn't yet know I owed.

And somewhere closer than that, sleeping an arm's length away, was a person who flinched at checkpoints like a soldier does, and lied like a friend does, and whose truths were stacked behind a door of their own.

I kept the watch. When dawn came I woke Jude, and we walked on, and I smiled at them, and they smiled at me, and both smiles were true, and both were lies, and the road went east.
