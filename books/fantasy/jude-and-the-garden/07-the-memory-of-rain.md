## 7. The Memory of Rain

The refugee camps along the Salt Road smell of wet wool and boiled turnips, which is to say they smell like every other place where hope has been stretched thin enough to see through. I had been three days pretending to be a pilgrim when I found him.

Pretending is a strong word. Jude had done most of the work — the rough-spun cloaks, the ash smudged on our foreheads in the pattern of the Wanderer's faith, the practiced stoop of people who have walked a long way to apologize to a god for existing. Jude even knew the prayers. I'd asked where a palace gardener learned pilgrim liturgy, and Jude had shrugged and said, "Everyone learns something in the gardens besides plants," which was either an answer or a very elegant way of not giving one. I let it go. That was becoming a habit with me and Jude: letting things go, the way you let a splinter work itself deeper because digging for it would hurt worse now than later.

But that was the road. Here in the camp it was raining, a thin gray drizzle that couldn't decide whether to fall or just hang in the air like a bad decision, and I was walking between the tents looking for nothing in particular when I saw the old man dying.

You can always tell. Not by the stillness — plenty of living people are perfectly still, especially refugees, who have learned that motion attracts soldiers. It's the breathing. The dying breathe the way tide goes out: each breath a little further from shore, and no promise of coming back.

He was lying under a cart, propped against a wheel, wrapped in a blanket that might once have been red. His eyes were open and fixed on the rain as if it were a play he'd paid good money to see.

"You're watching the weather," I said, because I am constitutionally incapable of walking past a stranger without saying something foolish.

"It's late," he said. His voice was dry as the road. "It should have come weeks ago. Back home it would have."

"Home is where?"

He smiled. It transformed his face entirely, the way a lamp transforms a room — suddenly there were furnishings in there, a life, windows. "That's the trouble," he said. "I keep almost remembering."

I should have walked on. I want that on the record, whoever keeps records these days, which is probably no one, since everyone who kept them has been arrested or burned. I should have walked on, because a dying man who has lost his home is a temptation wearing a welcome sign, and I had been hungry — properly hungry, gift-hungry — for eleven days, ever since the last checkpoint, since Jude flinched at the soldier's question and I pretended not to notice the flinch.

Eleven days. For an ordinary person that's an inconvenience. For me it's like holding your breath underwater while someone slowly increases the depth. My gift doesn't kill me without use, exactly, but it *aches*, the way a tooth aches, the way a door left unopened in a drafty house rattles on its hinges all night begging to be let through. Twenty years of rationing myself into small sips — a customer's first kiss here, a grandmother's kitchen there, never anything large, never anything that would leave a wound — and now, on this road, with checkpoints every thirty miles and my face on a list I'd never see but could feel like weather coming, I was down to fumes.

And here was a man with nothing left to lose and a whole life leaking out of him into the rain.

"What's your name?" I asked.

"Does it matter? In a day it won't be anyone's." He coughed, a small apologetic sound, as if even his lungs were trying not to trouble me. "Sit, if you like. Company's better than medicine at this stage. Cheaper, too."

I sat. The mud soaked through immediately. Pilgrim's luck.

"They say the rain here used to come in season," he said. "Used to come like a guest. Now it comes like a debt collector. You from the city?"

"Was."

"Ran?"

"Running."

He nodded with the deep unsurprise of the old. "Half the camp's running. Other half wishes they were. What did you do?"

"I sold charms."

"Harmless ones?"

"Completely harmless. Bits of string and glass. The magic," I said, and then stopped, because twenty years of habit sits in the throat like a fishbone. He watched me stop. Old men who are dying don't miss much; they've stopped bothering to pretend otherwise.

"The magic was extra," he finished for me. "Off the books."

Something in me went very still. "You're kind to keep my secret."

"I'm keeping nobody's secret. I'm eighty-one years old and I'm under a cart. Who would I tell?" He laughed, which turned into the cough again, longer this time. When it passed he lay looking at the rain some more, and I sat in the mud and felt my gift ache like a second heartbeat, and the two of us were quiet together in the way strangers sometimes manage better than friends.

Then he said, "Would you like to see it?"

"See what?"

"My home. Before." His eyes came off the rain and settled on me, and they were clear — clearer than they had any right to be, the clarity of a man who has already packed. "I can't hold it anymore. It slips. Every time I reach for it, there's less. This morning I couldn't remember my wife's voice. Last week it was her face. I've got the name still. Maren. I've got Maren. But it's getting lonely in there." He tapped his temple. "Everything else is leaving ahead of schedule, like rats on a sinking ship. So I thought — if someone could hold it, even a little while. Even wrong. Better wrong somewhere than gone altogether."

I knew what he was asking. I want to be precise about this, because everything that happened afterward, everything that led to the archive in the mountains and the woman with the mapmaker's hands and the word *erased* spoken like a curse over my own name — all of it began with the fact that he asked. He asked, and I said yes.

"You understand what I'd be doing," I said quietly. "It isn't kind, what I do. It takes something and leaves a gap."

"A gap's honest," he said. "A hole where the house stood tells you a house stood there. Nothing at all tells you nothing. Give me the gap. Give me the shape of the loss." He closed his eyes. "Take the rain one. Start with the rain."

* * *

Here is how it works, for those lucky enough never to have needed to know. Memory isn't stored like coins in a purse. It's more like water held in cupped hands — and I am very good at slipping my fingers in beneath yours and lifting out a single mouthful without spilling the rest. The trick is consent. Taken memory fights. Given memory pours. A willing mind opens like a hand unclenching, and what I take I take clean, with edges, so the absence it leaves behind is a shape rather than a wound.

I set my hand over his — his skin was paper over bird bones — and I did the thing I have done perhaps four thousand times, and I went in after the rain.

What I expected: a farmyard, mud, a roof drumming, a woman laughing under an eave. Ordinary rain, the kind that falls everywhere, the kind that falls in the city on market days and makes the awnings sag. I expected to lift it gently, wrap it in the careful folds I've developed over two decades of this work, and carry it away like a parcel.

What I found instead was green.

Not the green of fields. Fields are green the way a ledger is full of numbers — accurate, useful, dead. This was green the way a choir is loud. It came at me from every direction at once, and underneath it — under the green, under everything — was the rain itself, and the rain was *wrong*. Wrong in the way a song is wrong when you realize it's being sung in a language you thought you didn't speak and find you know every word. The rain in his memory fell upward. Not literally — I'm not claiming physics, I'm claiming recognition. It fell upward the way grief does, the way names do when you call them across water. It fell and the ground drank it and the drinking made light, actual light, rising out of the soil in slow green pulses, and there were trees whose roots showed above the earth like the fingers of sleeping giants, and the roots were warm, I could feel that they were warm, and —

— and there was a garden. A walled garden. Stone walls gone soft with moss, and inside them rows of something planted that wasn't quite any plant I know, and a child standing among the rows, and the child was crying, and I knew the crying. I knew it from the inside. I knew the taste of salt in that particular throat, the hitch in that particular chest, the exact weight of that particular small body shaking with sobs in a garden that —

I pulled back.

I pulled back hard, too fast, the way you jerk your hand off a stove, and I sat there in the mud with the old man's rain-memory wrapped tight in my keeping and my heart going like a drummer boy's and the drizzle falling on my face and Jude's voice somewhere behind me saying my false pilgrim-name — "Sera? Sera, there you are" — and I could not answer. For a moment I could not make my mouth produce words, because my mouth remembered being smaller, and crying, and I did not remember why, and the not-remembering had teeth.

"Sera." Jude crouched beside me, and I felt the question before it was spoken: *what did you touch?*

"He asked me to," I said. Which answered the wrong question, and Jude, to their credit, understood that it answered the wrong question, and looked at the old man instead.

"Maren," the old man said, to no one, testing whether the name still held. It seemed to. He smiled up at the rain. "Told you it was late. She always said the rains would fail someday and I told her she worried like a miller's daughter." He patted my hand where it still rested on his, feather-light, as if comforting me. "Thank you, charm-seller. Whatever you took — mind how you carry it. Heavy things, memories. Heavier the happier they were."

"We'll send someone," Jude said. "Someone will sit with you."

"Don't bother. I've got the rain till morning, maybe. Good night for it." He turned his face up. "Good night for anything."

Jude drew me away, arm through mine, firm as a shepherd. We walked past tents and cookfires and children chasing each other through the puddles, and Jude waited until we were behind our own lean-to with its little pilgrim shrine of river stones before speaking.

"What did you take?"

"A rainstorm."

"That's not an answer, Quentin."

"It's the only one I have." I sat on our bedroll and put my head in my hands. "It was rain. It was supposed to be rain on a farm, and instead it was —" Words failed me, which for me is roughly equivalent to the sun failing to rise. "There was a garden. Walled. There were roots above the ground and the soil drank light and there was a child crying and Jude, I *knew* the crying. Not recognized. Knew. The way you know your own sneeze is coming."

Jude was quiet. Too quiet. I've spent twenty years reading silences the way other people read faces, and Jude's silence had a texture I hadn't felt before — dense, deliberate, a silence being *held* rather than merely occurring. But I was too shaken to press it, and pressing it would have meant admitting I'd noticed, and noticing meant questions, and questions meant answers I wasn't sure I wanted yet. Letting things go. The splinter goes deeper.

"There are stories," Jude said at last, carefully, each word placed like a foot on ice. "Old ones. About a country east of the mountains that isn't on maps. My grandmother sang a lullaby about it. She said it had no name because it gave its name away."

"Gave it away to whom?"

"To everyone. To anyone who asked. That was the story — that the country was so generous it gave away even itself, and when the last person forgot the last syllable of its name, the country folded up like a letter and posted itself nowhere." Jude shrugged, and the shrug was a shade too casual. "Nursery nonsense."

"And you believed it enough to learn the song."

"I believe songs. Songs cost less to believe than governments." Jude knelt and started fussing with the fire we weren't supposed to have, feeding it twigs, hiding their face behind the smoke. "Why? What did you see?"

"Walls. Moss. Roots like sleeping giants. Soil that glowed when it drank." I heard my own voice go strange. "And a walled garden, Jude. Rows of planted things. Someone tending them." I looked at the fire. "I sell charms in a market city two hundred miles west of here and I dream about gardens I've never visited. Or I did. I thought I'd never visited."

The fire popped. Somewhere across the camp a baby cried and was hushed.

"Maybe," Jude said, to the flames, "you should stop taking memories from dying men."

"Maybe." But we both knew I wouldn't. The ache was already easing, the gift fed, the hunger banked — and underneath the relief, coiling like a root finding water, was the wanting. Not hunger this time. Homesickness. Homesickness for a place I could prove I'd never been.

* * *

He died in the night. Of course he did; he'd been waiting for permission, and I'd given it. The camp buried him at dawn in a row of mounds at the treeline, each marked with a stick and a strip of cloth, and Jude stood at the edge of the little procession with their hood up and their lips moving — the pilgrim prayers again, fluent, automatic — while I hung back like a thief at a wedding, which is essentially what I was.

Maren, I thought. Her name was Maren and his was — and his was —

His name was gone. I'd taken his rain and left his Maren, per his request, and everything else had already fled the ship ahead of schedule, and so somewhere between his last breath and this muddy graveside, his name had slipped out of the world entirely, and I stood there holding a stolen rainstorm and realized I couldn't even carve the right letters on his stick. Jude carved it anyway — asked the tent-neighbors, pieced it together from what the dying man had let drop over the weeks. Aldric. They carved *ALDRIC* neat and deep, and put the stick at the head of the mound, and brushed the dirt from their hands with the same efficient tenderness they used on seedlings.

"You're good at that," I said.

"At what?"

"Caring for things properly. Even dead ones. Even strangers."

Jude looked at me a moment too long. "Somebody has to be," they said. "The world's mostly good at the opposite." And then, lighter, in the tone people use to close doors: "Come on. Checkpoint at midday. We should be through it before the line gets long."

We walked east out of the camp, two pilgrims with ash on our foreheads and lies in our mouths, and behind us the mounds at the treline darkened with rain, real rain this time, ordinary rain, the kind that falls anywhere and remembers nothing.

But I carried the other rain. I carried it wrapped in the careful folds, heavy as Aldric promised, heavier the happier it was — and all that day, as the checkpoint soldiers peered under my hood and waved us through, as Jude flinched at the right moments with that practiced ease I was pretending not to catalog, the memory sat against my ribs like a coal that wouldn't cool. And every few miles, when the road bent and the mountains opened ahead of us, I caught myself leaning toward the east. Toward the green. Toward a walled garden with soft walls and a crying child I had no business knowing, whose sobs fit some hollow in me the way a key fits a lock it was cut for.

That night, Jude slept and I did not. I sat up with the ember of our forbidden fire and unwrapped the memory a crack — just a crack, just to look — and in the green light of a country that appears on no map, the child in the garden turned around.

And wore my face.

I did not sleep at all after that. And when dawn came gray over the passes, Jude woke to find me already packed, already standing, already facing east, and whatever was in my face made them ask no questions — only reach over and straighten my pilgrim's cloak with those gardener's hands, gentle, careful, and lie to me with their eyes the way they had been lying for years.

I didn't know that yet, then. All I knew was that the road east was calling something home in me, and that the person walking beside me knew more about it than they would say.
