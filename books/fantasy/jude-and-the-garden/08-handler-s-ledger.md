## 8. Handler's Ledger

There is an arithmetic to shame. I've done the sums often enough that I could teach them.

One sister, sick in the lungs since she was nine. One father, dead of the same lungs two winters before her. One debt owed to the Guild of Apothecaries for medicines that cost more per ounce than silver, because the good physicians had all moved to districts where people could pay. One gardener's wage from the palace conservatory, which is a fine wage as wages go and a joke as medicine bills go.

And one man in grey who appeared at my sister's bedside on the worst night of the worst month, holding a small glass vial of something clear and expensive, like he'd brought holy water and knew it.

His name was Corvel. I have hated the sound of it ever since, which is inconvenient, because I say it out loud every week when I kneel in his office and report what I've seen.

* * *

But let me do this properly, because if I'm going to be honest — and lately honesty has been sitting on my chest at night like a cat that wants feeding — then let me be honest in order.

The winter Wren was fourteen, the coughing turned to blood. I remember the exact shade of red on the handkerchief because I'd spent my whole life around flowers and I had never once confused the two until then. The conservatory kept me late that season; the crown wanted winter-blooming stock for some ambassadorial nonsense, camellias in January so that foreign ministers could pretend our city wasn't made of ice and fear. So I was coming home past midnight through streets where the lamps burned low, because lamp-oil was another thing the crown economized on, and I found Corvel already inside our rooms, sitting in our one good chair, warming his hands at our thin fire as though he owned the lease.

Wren was asleep. He'd given her something, he said later. A kindness. That was the word he used. *Kindness.*

"You're Jude," he said. "Palace gardens, six years' service, no debts except one enormous one, no vices except loyalty." He smiled the way a ledger smiles. "I admire loyalty. It's rarer than talent."

I told him to get out of my house.

He told me the apothecary's bill would be settled by morning, and that Wren would have the true physician from the Spire district — the one with the silver instruments and the reputation — twice a month for as long as she needed, and that in exchange I would keep my eyes open and my mouth shut, and that neither of those things would cost me anything I'd miss.

That's the lie they tell you first: *it won't cost you anything you'll miss.*

I want to be very clear about what happened next, because there are versions of this story where a man is dragged into treason by the collar, kicking, and none of them are true. What happened next was that I sat down across from Corvel, because my legs stopped working, and I said: what do you want me to look at?

He wanted magic. Specifically, he wanted people who had it and hid it. The crown had a name for them even then — *gift-bearers*, as though it were a christening — and a policy, which was not yet written down but lived comfortably in the way policies do before they're laws: register quietly, or disappear quietly. My job was not to catch anyone. Catching required soldiers, warrants, mess. My job was to *notice*. To walk the markets and the workshops and the conservatory rows where I already went every day anyway, and to notice the small wrongnesses that a certain kind of person leaves behind them like footprints in flour.

A lock that opens too easily for its owner. A customer who never haggles because the seller's charms always feel lucky. A neighbor whose grief dries up overnight, clean as a washed plate.

"Memory-workers," Corvel said, "are the most dangerous and the most common, and they are almost never what people imagine. No thunderbolts. No demons. Just small thefts, kindly done, which is how all the worst things get done." He looked at me over his folded hands. "You'll know them when you see them. Gardeners know what doesn't grow naturally."

I didn't sleep that night. Not because of guilt — guilt came later, guilt took its time, guilt is a slow crop — but because I lay awake doing the arithmetic again, and for the first time the sums came out even. Medicine for Wren against watching. Watching against telling. Telling against — what? Against whatever happened to the people I pointed at, which Corvel assured me was nothing dramatic. Interviews. Registrations. *Kindness.*

The physician came on the first of Frostmonth. She had hands like cold water and she listened to Wren's chest for a long time and then prescribed a tincture that cost more than my father had earned in a year. Wren slept through the whole visit. When she woke she asked why the room smelled of the Spire district, and I told her the palace had sent medicine for its gardeners, because the crown was generous.

That was the second lie. The crown is not generous. The crown is a creditor like any other, and I had signed.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about informing: it fits into your life the way water fits into a wall. You don't rearrange anything. You just start noticing that everything is already full of it.

My reports went into a ledger Corvel kept — an actual book, leather-bound, with a clasp, which struck me even then as theatrical — and my entries were written in my own handwriting, which meant that my hand learned the shape of betrayal the way a hand learns any craft. Repetition. Neat margins. Dates.

*Third of Frostmonth. The charm-seller on Cooper's Lane sells love-lures that work too well; recommend observation.*

*Ninth of Frostmonth. The midwife Vessa delivered the Ostler child after the physicians gave it up; mother recalls nothing of the birth now, seems contented. Recommend observation.*

*Sixteenth. Nothing.* Even the nothings felt like sins by then. Even silence had become evidence of something — of sloppiness, maybe, or of mercy, and I couldn't always tell which.

In four years I gave Corvel eleven names. I want that number on the page where I can see it. Eleven. Some of them I still defend to myself at three in the morning — the love-lure seller was genuinely selling compulsion bottled, and I don't care what century you live in, that deserves watching. The midwife may simply have been a gifted midwife; plenty of women are, without a drop of magic, only hands and sense. But some of the eleven I cannot dress up in any coat.

There was a lamplighter named Odo who had a trick — I never learned if it was a Gift or just a trick — of humming a note that made drunk men go home instead of brawling. Every quarter-night, the whole street, calm as a chapel. I wrote him down because his street's incident reports were anomalous and anomalies were what Corvel paid for. They interviewed Odo for three days. Afterward he still lit his lamps, but he'd stopped humming, and the quarter-nights got loud again, and once a boy had his arm broken over a dice debt that Odo's note would have soothed away. I walked past that boy being splinted and thought: *that's mine. That broken arm is in my ledger.* Corvel would say the arm was the brawlers' doing, and technically he'd be right, the way a man who sells knives is technically innocent.

And there was a woman — I never learned her name — a flower-seller at the Gate market who sold forgetfulness. Not the flower itself; the feeling. Brides bought sprigs from her to take the sting out of old sweethearts. Mourners bought them to survive the first year. I watched a woman buy one after her son's funeral, pay a copper, smile a small real smile for the first time in a week, and walk home lighter, and I stood there in the crowd understanding with total clarity that I was going to write this down, that I was already composing the entry, that some part of me had become a clerk in Corvel's office wearing my body like a borrowed coat.

Did I write her down?

Yes.

She was never interviewed, as far as I know. Maybe Corvel filed it under *too small to bother*. Maybe somewhere in a grey office there's a drawer with her name in it and my handwriting underneath, neat margins, dated. I think about that drawer more than I think about God.

Here's the thing about the eleven names, though. Here's the thing I've been circling for years and can only say plainly now, lying in a ditch outside a checkpoint town while Quentin sleeps ten feet away with their back against a cartwheel:

Every one of those eleven was a stranger. That was the rule I set myself, the private law, the little altar I built inside the temple of my shame: *never someone whose bread I've eaten.* Informing is a trade like any other and trades need ethics or they rot the trader. Strangers were currency. Friends were not legal tender. I held that line for four years, and I was proud of it, God help me — proud of it the way a thief is proud of leaving the poorbox alone.

Then Corvel gave me the conservatory errand.

* * *

"You know the charm shop off Coppermarket," he said. It wasn't a question; he'd known the answer before he asked it, the way he always did. "Small place. Trinkets, mostly. Luck-charms, traveler's blessings, the usual pious fraud. But the owner buys materials I can't account for, and there was an incident last spring — a customer, a moneylender's clerk, who left the shop weeping and couldn't say why, and then couldn't remember a debt he'd been chasing for a month." Corvel turned the ledger toward me. "Your handwriting, Jude. Two years ago. You flagged the shop yourself."

I hadn't remembered. That's the truth, the ugly ordinary truth: I had flagged Quentin Joubert two years ago and forgotten it, the way you forget a cough, and now here it was again, grown.

"So observe," I said. "Interview. Whatever the procedure—"

"No." He closed the ledger. "This one's different. This one you'll cultivate."

"Cultivate."

"A friendship, if you can manage it. You're personable. You like plants; they like plants, apparently — there's a conservatory order pending, which is convenient, and which I arranged, also conveniently." He smiled his ledger smile. "Gift-bearers hide from officials. They don't hide from friends. Get close. Learn whether the gift is real, how strong, how used. And Jude—" he paused, and I understood even then that this pause was rehearsed, that Corvel rehearsed his cruelties the way I prune roses, for effect — "this one matters above your usual work. There's a hunt coming. Within the year. The crown has decided the age of quiet arrangements is over. When it comes, I will need more than a name. I will need a door opened from the inside."

I said yes.

Of course I said yes. Wren's physician visits were monthly now and the tincture had changed to a costlier one; the debt had grown new branches while I slept. And besides — and I record this because it's true and because it damns me in a different way than the money does — besides, some part of me was curious. Some part of me, the part that had watched the flower-seller's customers walk away lighter, wanted to sit across a counter from a memory-thief and see what kindness looked like on the far side of the trade. We were both of us in the business of taking small things from strangers. I told myself we'd recognize each other.

I told myself a great many things that first morning I walked into the shop with an impossible order for the royal conservatory and watched Quentin squint at it and say, "Whoever wrote this list has never met a plant in their life," and laugh — that dry, guarded laugh, one syllable, quickly closed like a shop shutter — and felt something in my chest do exactly what Corvel had planned for it to do.

Open.

* * *

Two years. Let me inventory what two years bought.

Wren is alive. Her lungs are scarred but quiet; last summer she walked to the river and back without stopping, and when she got home she was so proud of herself she cried, and I cried in the privy where she couldn't see. The crown owns her breath. Every easy inhale she takes is drawn on credit against my soul, and the interest compounds nightly.

Quentin trusts me. I know the exact day it started — not the banter days, not the charm-and-countercharm days, but the evening the frost took the window-boxes and I stayed to help carry them in and Quentin handed me their knife to cut the twine without a flicker of hesitation, and I stood there holding a blade I had, in every meaningful sense, been sent to sharpen against their throat.

And I know — I have known for months, with the certainty of a man reading his own ledger — that the hunt is coming. Criers within the season. Registration lists. Gallows, eventually, because quiet arrangements end the way Corvel said they would, and the people in his drawer stop being interviews and become examples. Quentin's name is already in the file. Mine is in the margin beside it, in my own neat handwriting: *asset, cultivated, reliable.*

Reliable.

Last week Corvel asked me for a final confirmation. Details. The layout of the shop, the hours, the cellar, whether there was a back way out through the cisterns — he asked about the cisterns, and I heard myself say I didn't know, and heard the lie come out smooth as poured cream, and understood that I had just crossed a line I'd pretended for two years was still ahead of me.

Because here is the entry I have not yet written. Here is the omission sitting in my chest like a swallowed coal.

Corvel asked: does the gift-bearer suspect anything? Any unusual wariness, any preparations to flee?

And I said: no. Nothing. Same as always.

Which is nearly true. Which is true enough to pass any inspection of my face. But there was a moment — last market-day, when the criers' bells rang wrong, half a beat early, and Quentin's head came up like a deer's and their eyes went to the alley behind the shop before they'd consciously noticed the bells at all — there was a moment when I saw that Quentin has been afraid much longer than I've been watching, and that the fear is organized, and that a person that afraid for that long has a plan, and that the plan probably has my shop-visit hours memorized alongside everything else.

They may already know what I am. Or half-know. Or know nothing and simply be wise, which in this city amounts to the same survival skill.

Either way, I stood in Corvel's office with my heart going like a hammer on a nail and lied to the crown for the first time not by silence but by sentence. *Nothing. Same as always.* Four words. I've killed men with less — no, that's not true, and I should stop reaching for grandeur; I haven't killed anyone, I've only fed eleven strangers into a machine that grinds at its own pace, which is worse in the way slow things are worse.

Tonight I'm to give my regular report. Tomorrow or the day after, Corvel says, the raid comes — midnight, quiet, soldiers out of livery, the cistern route watched in case the bird flies. He told me the hour the way you'd tell a man the time of his own wedding, cheerfully, assuming he wants to be there.

So tonight I will kneel in that grey office and recite my inventory of harmless nothings — the midwife's deliveries, the weather, the price of lamp-oil — and I will leave out the one fact that matters most, the fact I have no right to withhold and am going to withhold anyway:

Quentin Joubert keeps a packed bag behind the seed cabinet. Has kept one for years. And if the knock comes at midnight, the bird will fly long before the watchers reach the cistern door — unless someone warns them first.

Unless I run ahead of the knock through streets I've walked ten thousand times, and betray in one hour every month of four years' service, and spend Wren's breath like a stolen coin, and become finally, irreversibly, exactly what the ledger always said I was.

I've been writing reports for Corvel since Frostmonth, four years ago. Tonight's entry will be short.

*Tenth of Greenmonth. Nothing to report.*

It will be the first honest thing I've ever put in his book.

The bells ring the hour. Time to go and lie to save a liar, and see which of us the truth ruins first.
