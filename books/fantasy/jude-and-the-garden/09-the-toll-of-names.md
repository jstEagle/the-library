## 9. The Toll of Names

The town was called Ferrow's Rest, which was optimistic of it. It sat at the knee of the border road like a beggar at a rich man's gate, all of it leaning eastward as if straining to hear what lay beyond: a river the color of dishwater, a bridge with customs officers on it, and past that, hills that belonged to nobody in particular because nobody had bothered to claim them recently.

We had been posing as pilgrims for six days, and I had learned three things about piety. One: pilgrims walk slowly, so we walked slowly, though every nerve in my body wanted to run. Two: pilgrims sing, so Jude sang, badly, hymns about a saint whose name changed depending on who was listening, which struck me as theologically honest. Three: pilgrims are ignored, which was the whole point, and mostly true, except by children, dogs, and one customs sergeant with a memory like a ledger.

"Your hands," he'd said at the last checkpoint, taking mine without asking and turning them over like a man inspecting a horse's mouth.

"Chapped," I said. "Prayer is hard work."

He'd grunted. He'd looked at my palms, where twenty years of charm-work leaves no mark at all, which is precisely why they check palms — looking for marks that aren't there is a game the crown plays with itself — and then he'd waved us through with the bored contempt of a man who has already decided the world contains nothing worth noticing.

I should have been relieved. Instead I spent the next mile composing increasingly elaborate lies in case he asked again, which is a habit I have never managed to break. A thief of memories, you understand, must be a hoarder of inventions. You can never have too many.

Jude noticed my silence, because Jude noticed everything, which I was beginning to find less charming than exhausting.

"You're doing the thing," Jude said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where your face goes still and your eyes keep working. Like a shop window after closing."

"I'm praying," I said.

"We're supposed to be doing that anyway."

"Then I'm doing it very well."

They laughed, and I hated how much I loved the sound of it, and I said nothing else, because ahead of us the road spilled down into Ferrow's Rest and the town square was full of people standing very quietly, the way people stand when standing still is the safest available motion.

* * *

There is a particular quality to a crowd gathered for an official purpose. I had seen it twice before in my life — once when I was small, before I learned what I was, and once from behind the counter of my own shop, watching through glass as a crier announced something in another district. It is a silence with weight. It presses on the ears. Birds leave. Dogs are taken home. The whole square arranges itself around a cleared space the way water arranges itself around a stone, and everyone pretends, with tremendous discipline, not to look at the stone.

In Ferrow's Rest the stone was a scaffold. Not even a proper one — a cart bed, two posts, a crossbeam, thrown up in a night by men who wanted it done before anyone thought better. Beneath it stood a woman I knew.

Her name was Anselma Voss. She sold candles in the third row of the Marchmarket, two stalls down from mine, close enough that for eleven years I had smelled her beeswax every morning and complained about her singing every afternoon. She sang while she worked, hymns mostly, off-key, and she gave me the ends of her candle-stubs for my mother's grave-lamp, though I had told her no such grave existed and she had decided not to believe me. She had bought a luck-charm from me once, a little braided thing with nothing in it but thread, and paid full price, and winked, because Anselma understood perfectly well that half the trade in charms is the buying of hope at retail.

She stood under the crossbeam with her wrists bound and her chin up, and she was not singing, and that was the worst part. Anselma Voss, silent.

"They've been reading names since dawn," said the innkeep's boy, who had materialized beside us the way boys do, drawn to catastrophe like gulls to a gutting table. "A herald reads them off the list. If your name's read, you come forward and register, or—" He shrugged with the terrible fluency of the young. "Or they do this. She wouldn't come forward. Said she'd done nothing to register *for*."

"And had she?" Jude asked quietly.

The boy looked at them with pity for our ignorance. "Everyone's got something. That's the point of a list."

* * *

We found a place against the wall of the wool-factor's house, where a low roof threw a shadow across us both, and I watched the square through the gap between two barrels, which is a position I have held more often in my life than I would like to admit.

The herald was a narrow man with a voice built for courtyards and a scroll built for cruelty. He unrolled it with ceremony and began to read.

Not charges. Just names. That was the design of it — I understood the design immediately, with the sick clarity of someone recognizing a trick they might have invented themselves. No crimes were named because no crimes needed naming. The list was simply names, and each name was a question, and the question was: *what are you?* And the only answers permitted were *nothing*, spoken loudly enough for neighbors to hear, or silence, which was answered by the cart-bed and the crossbeam.

"Hetta Brunn, cooper's widow."

A woman stepped forward from the crowd, white-faced, and knelt at the registration table, and a clerk wrote her down, and she was allowed to rise having confessed to being, officially, nothing. I watched her walk away and saw how she kept her eyes on the cobbles the entire way, as if meeting anyone's gaze might cost extra.

"Piet Salterman, ferryman." Another kneeling. Another confession of emptiness.

The crowd did this all morning. Name, kneel, write, rise. Name, kneel, write, rise. It had the rhythm of a liturgy, which I suppose it was — the crown's oldest religion, the worship of its own fear. And through it all Anselma stood beneath the beam and waited, because her name had already been read, hours ago, and she had already given her answer.

I felt Jude's shoulder against mine. They had gone very still in the way that I now recognized — the shop-window stillness, the face closed and the eyes working — and I remember thinking, distantly, that we were making the same expression, and wondering idly what it was that Jude was afraid of, since Jude was, so far as I knew, exactly what they appeared to be: a gardener with a talent for plants and a talent, lately, for keeping me alive.

I did not yet know how good a question that was. Hold onto that. There will be a moment, later in this account, when I would give a great deal to un-know it.

"—Anselma Voss, chandler," the herald read again, from the bottom of his list, the recalcitrant names repeated like debts called a second time. "Come forward and be registered."

Anselma lifted her head. Even at forty yards I could see her look out over the crowd, unhurried, the way she used to look down her stall at customers trying to haggle her below the honest price of wax. Her eyes passed over the crowd the way a lighthouse passes over water. Looking for someone. Saying goodbye to everyone at once, perhaps, in case the someone wasn't there.

And then, God forgive me, I nearly moved. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger keeps such things: I nearly stepped out of the shadow. Eleven years of beeswax and bad singing and candle-stubs for a grave that didn't exist. What stopped me was not courage's absence but its arithmetic — that stepping out would buy her nothing and spend everything, that the dead cannot be joined by appointment, that the whole machinery of the hunt ran precisely on love walking into open squares. I stayed in the shadow and I will hear that decision for the rest of my life, however long the crown permits it to be.

The sergeant gave the order. It was very quick, because it is designed to be very quick; slowness is where doubt breeds. And the square made a sound I had heard once before, long ago, and hoped never to hear again — a sound like a great intake of breath that no one lets back out.

Then the crowd did the thing crowds always do, the thing that makes them forgivable: they dispersed too fast, faces turned away, everyone suddenly devoted to errands, and in their haste they permitted themselves, collectively, to forget. By evening Ferrow's Rest would tell itself it had witnessed weather.

I did not disperse. I stood against the wool-factor's wall and I looked at the cart-bed and the crossbeam and the rope, and I let myself see it, because somebody in that square owed Anselma Voss the dignity of witness, and it was clearly not going to be the herald.

That was when I noticed the paper.

It was nailed to the registration table's post, a fresh broadsheet, ink hardly dry, copies of it curling on the wet cobbles where the wind had lost its grip on a few. I crossed the emptying square — grief does strange things to caution; I have since learned to distrust myself whenever I feel brave — and picked one up.

It was not a list of names. It was a description.

*A person of middle years, trading under various signs, last known keeping a charm-stall in the Marchmarket of Corvane. Sells small goods, harmless-seeming. May travel alone or in company. Approach with care. Do not engage. Report.*

Below it, a reward figure with a great many zeroes, and below *that*, in smaller type, the detail that took the ground out from under my feet:

*Named by a reliable source. Long-standing.*

Long-standing.

Not a neighbor who had glimpsed my hands at a checkpoint. Not a customer grown frightened. Someone who had known about me for *years*. Someone the crown trusted enough to print those two words and expect the whole border country to believe them. Twenty years I had kept my secret the way you keep a fire in a storm — cupped, sheltered, fed in the dark — and there had been exactly one person, in all that time, positioned to hand it over drop by drop, season by season, *long-standing*—

I stopped.

Because the thought arrived fully formed, wearing its own verdict, and it wore Jude's face.

No. No, that was fear talking, fear dressed up in logic. Jude had pulled me out of the cisterns at midnight with soldiers at our backs. Jude had sung bad hymns for six days and flinched at checkpoints and slept sitting upright between me and the door of every room we'd rented. Whatever the crown's broadsheet meant by *reliable source*, it could not mean—

But it could. Of course it could. That was the elegance of it, and I have never underestimated elegance since: the best informant is the one who saves you first. The one who carries you out of danger is carrying you somewhere. The leash feels like a hand in yours right up until you notice the collar.

I stood in the square of Ferrow's Rest with a dead woman's name drying in the air above me and a broadsheet going soft in my fist, and I felt something I had not felt since childhood. Not fear. Fear I knew intimately; fear and I had shared a bed for twenty years and were practically married. This was the other thing, the hot thing, the thing that comes up from underneath fear like fire through a floorboard. Anger. Not the flare of it — the *banked* kind, the kind with a long burn, the kind that keeps.

Someone had named me. Someone had been naming me for years, feeding my life to the crown a spoonful at a time, and today that slow meal had ended under a crossbeam with a candle-seller who never hurt anyone except, possibly, the key of every hymn she sang.

"Quentin." Jude's voice, low, at my elbow. They had followed me into the open, which they shouldn't have, which a careful person would not have done. Their eyes went to the broadsheet, and I watched them read it, and I watched — I will swear this to any judge, any god, any thorn-hung door at the end of the world — I watched something move behind their face. Quick as a fish under ice. There and gone. Guilt, maybe. Or recognition. Or nothing at all; grief makes readers of us all, and every page says what we dread.

"That could be anyone," Jude said. "Half the charm-sellers in three provinces fit that description."

"It says long-standing source."

"It says a lot of things. Broad sheets are written to sound certain. Certainty is cheaper than proof." They put their hand on my arm, warm through the pilgrim's rough wool, steady, familiar, *practiced* — and for the first time the word practiced landed wrong, like a note Anselma would have missed. "We need to move. The herald's lists travel faster than we do. There'll be a copy of that sheet in every town from here to the coast."

"Who reads them?" I asked. "These lists. Who compiles them? Where do the names come from?"

Jude paused. Just a pause — a hair's breadth, a held breath, a gardener deciding whether a plant needs water or has had enough. "Handlers, I suppose," they said. "Palace clerks. How would I know?"

How would I know. Three words, innocently said, and I have turned them over so many times since that they've worn smooth as river stones, and I still cannot tell you what lay at the bottom of that pause. That is the cruelty of suspicion: it doesn't show you a lie. It shows you a door, and then it stands next to you forever, whispering, *you didn't open it.*

"Anselma Voss sold candles for thirty years," I said. My voice came out flat and strange. "She never stole anything bigger than an hour of her neighbors' mornings with her singing. She wouldn't come forward because coming forward meant admitting to a crime she hadn't committed, and she was too proud to lie badly." I looked at the crossbeam. "The crown didn't kill her for magic. They barely pretended she had any. They killed her as an advertisement. As a *toll* — this is what the road costs now. And somewhere there's a person whose signature is on the toll-book, collecting."

"Quentin—"

"Don't," I said. Not cruelly. Almost gently. "Please don't say anything comforting just now. I couldn't tell comfort from a knife today, and I don't want to blame you for the difference."

Jude went quiet. We walked out of Ferrow's Rest with our pilgrim's staves and our pilgrim's patience, eastward, toward the river and the bridge and the hills beyond, and behind us the town folded its morning away and began telling itself it had seen weather.

I did not speak again until dark. But all that day, in some locked room at the back of my mind, I set the broadsheet on a table opposite a memory of Jude's face — that flicker, that fish under ice — and I sat down between them, and I began, with the patience of my trade, to wait for one of them to confess something.

By the time we made camp, I knew which one I was hoping for.

By the time the fire was lit, I was no longer sure I'd survive either answer.
