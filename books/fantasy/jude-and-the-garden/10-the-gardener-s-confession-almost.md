## 10. The Gardener's Confession, Almost

I have lied to better people than Quentin Joubert. That's not a boast; it's an inventory.

There was the widow on Candlemaker's Row whose husband I reported for a charm against fever — he'd bought it from a different seller, but I needed to meet my quota that month, and the crown doesn't pay for near-misses. There was the ferryman who kept a luck-knot under his seat, and the girl in the tannery quarter who could make dye take color it had no business taking, and old Master Ferren, who taught me grafting and who could, I'm nearly certain, coax a cutting to root overnight with nothing but his hands and a hummed note. Four years of names. I keep them the way other people keep prayer beads, turning them over at night, one by one, feeling for the grooves worn into each one by my own thumb.

Quentin is just another name on the string. That's what I tell myself, anyway, sitting across the fire from them while they pick apart a loaf of road bread with the concentration of someone defusing it.

We've been three days on the Salt and Ash Road, posing as pilgrims bound for the shrine at Vell's Crossing, which is convenient because neither of us has to invent a destination we can describe without sweating. Quentin plays a pilgrim beautifully — solemn, dusty, hands folded around a tin charm they bought from *themselves* two markets ago, which strikes me as either penance or irony, and with Quentin it's usually both. I play the spouse, because playing siblings requires agreeing on a childhood, and I already have too many invented histories rattling around in me. I hold them like borrowed coats. I'm always afraid someone will ask about the pockets.

The fire is small. We're in the lee of a collapsed waystation wall, half a day shy of Vell's Crossing, and the checkpoint there will be the worst yet — full registration rolls, name-by-name, a clerk with a voice like wet paper reading the hunt lists aloud to anyone who'll stand still long enough. Quentin doesn't know I know how those readings go. Quentin doesn't know I've stood at four of them holding a lantern for the clerk, close enough to see the sweat on the condemned man's lip, close enough to nod when the clerk asked *is this the one?*

*Is this the one.* Four years, and that question still lives behind my teeth like a fishbone.

"Your bread," Quentin says, "has opinions about you."

"It's ration bread. It hates everyone equally."

"No." They turn the heel of the loaf toward me, where a pale crescent of dough has baked into something almost like a face. "It hates you specifically. Look. It's making a judgment."

I laugh before I can stop myself, and the laugh comes out wrong — too loud, too relieved, the laugh of a man standing on a frozen pond listening to the ice talk. Quentin notices. Quentin notices everything; that was the first thing I learned about them, back when noticing things was my job description. The way their eyes go still when a sound doesn't fit. The way they catalog a stranger in three seconds flat: threat, mark, or weather. In the shop they used to do it behind a smile soft as flour sifting, and I told myself the smile was the real thing and the stillness was habit. Now I sit on the far side of that stillness and understand it was never habit at all. It was survival, worn so smooth it looked like temperament.

"You're quiet," Quentin says.

"I'm eating."

"You're performing eating. There's a difference. Your jaw does this thing when you actually chew."

"Gods preserve me from charm-sellers turned naturalists."

But they don't drop it, because they never drop anything. They feed the fire a stick and watch me through the smoke with that level gray attention, and I feel the confession rise in me like water rising in a flooded cellar — slow, patient, indifferent to my opinion of it. Three days I've been carrying it up the road. Three days of checkpoints and flinches and Quentin's gaze resting on the back of my neck every time a soldier laughs too loudly. They've seen me flinch. Of course they have. I flinch like a professional, because I am one, and somewhere between the second checkpoint and this fire, Quentin started doing the arithmetic.

So here is the truth I cannot say: *I was sent to find you. I found you in six weeks. I stayed four years because the handler told me to stay, and then somewhere in the third year I stopped needing him to tell me, and I have been lying to you every single day since, including right now, including this sentence.*

And here is the truth beneath that one, the one with teeth: *If I tell you, you'll leave. And if you leave, they'll take you within a week, because I am the only reason you've survived this road, and the crown knows your face now anyway, and — and —*

And none of that matters, says a smaller, meaner voice, because the reason I haven't spoken isn't strategy. It's cowardice wearing strategy's coat. I don't want Quentin to look at me the way Master Ferren looked at me, at the end, when the soldiers walked him past my stall and I kept my eyes on my seedlings like a good little informant. I don't want the look. I would rather keep the lie warm than earn the look.

The water keeps rising.

"Quentin," I say, and my voice comes out strange, scraped. "Can I ask you something?"

"You just did."

"A real one."

They set down the bread. Wipe their hands on their knees. Something in the set of their shoulders shifts — not wariness exactly, but readiness, the posture of someone who has spent twenty years being asked real questions by people who wanted something. "Ask."

I should say it. This is the moment; even the fire seems to lean in. Say it plain: *I'm the crown's. I've been theirs since before I met you. There's a handler named Corvess who owns my sister's medicine and my silence in equal parts, and everything you trust about me was installed on purpose.*

Instead what comes out is:

"What would you do — " I stop. Start again. "Someone lies to you. Not a small lie. The kind that — the kind where every kindness they ever showed you might be part of it. Where you can't untangle the true things from the planted ones. But the lie saved your life. Maybe more than once. What would you do to them?"

The words hang over the fire like smoke with nowhere to go.

Quentin is quiet for a long time. Long enough that I count the sounds of the night separately: wind in the broken wall, an owl two valleys off, my own heart doing its clumsy work. I watch them decide whether this is a test, or a riddle, or a stranger's idle philosophy, and I watch them arrive — as they always do — at the answer underneath: *it doesn't matter what shape the question came in; only that it came.*

"If they lied to save me," Quentin says slowly, "then part of the lie was true."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the foundation of one. Give me a moment; I'm building on it." They turn the tin charm over in their fingers — their own workmanship, a cheap fever-warden, the kind they sold by the dozen in the market, harmless as a button. Or so I believed, once, before I understood what harmless buttons were worth to a hunted person. "Here's what I know about liars, Jude. I've met a few. Sold charms beside them. Run from them. I may have been one once or twice in ways I don't fully — " a flicker there, some private door closing, " — anyway. A liar who lies to protect you is still lying. But a liar who lies to protect you is also telling you something true, whether they mean to or not. They're telling you where their treasure is."

"And where's that?"

"You. Presumably. If we're talking hypothetically."

My throat closes. I examine the fire very closely, because it is the only thing in the world I can currently look at.

"Hypothetically," I manage.

"So what would I *do* to them?" They tilt their head. "Nothing, probably. Nothing violent. I'd be angry — I want to be honest about that, I'd be furious, the kind of angry that takes weeks to burn down. I'd want to know why. Not the excuse version of why. The real one, all the way to the bottom, even if the bottom is ugly. Especially if the bottom is ugly." They poke the fire, sending up a fistful of sparks. "And then — I don't know. I think I'd ask them to do the hardest thing, which isn't punishment. It's staying. Anyone can run after they're caught. Staying and being known — that's expensive. I'd make them pay that."

"Known," I repeat. My mouth is dry. "That's the price."

"That's the whole economy, Jude. Being seen accurately and staying anyway. Everything else is haggling." They glance up, and their expression goes gentle in a way that frightens me more than any soldier on this road has managed. "Why are you asking?"

Because I'm the crown's dog, Quentin. Because I led Ferren to the block and took the coin and went home and slept. Because I was planted in your shop with a forged order for conservatory charms and a handler's list of tells to watch for — the way you touch charms before selling them, the way your eyes track hands, the way you go quiet when criers pass — and I filed every single one in a report that is probably sitting on a desk in the palace right now, and I omitted only one detail to buy you time, and I have hated myself in forty different dialects since.

"Practice," I say. "For the checkpoints. They ask questions. I like to have answers ready."

"At midnight? By a fire? About hypothetical betrayers?"

"The clerks keep irregular hours."

Quentin looks at me for a long moment, and I know — I *know*, with the sick certainty of a man watching his own footprints fill with rain — that they don't believe me. That they've assembled most of the picture already and are simply waiting to see whether I hand them the last piece or make them take it. And still they let it go. Still they tear the judged half of the loaf in two and pass me the bigger piece, which is either forgiveness or reconnaissance, and with Quentin it is usually both.

"Eat your judgment face," they say.

I eat it. It tastes like ash road and unspent confessions.

* * *

I take first watch, which means I take the hours in which I hate myself with the best light available. Quentin sleeps curled against the wall with their cloak pulled to their chin, and in sleep their face loses the shopkeeper's polish entirely and becomes something younger, wearier, more astonished by the world — the face of a person who has been afraid for twenty years and has gotten very good at arranging fear to look like calm. I did that to them. No — the hunt did that to them. No — I fed the hunt for four years and called it keeping my head down. Every distinction I draw is just a slightly nicer cage.

Somewhere past middle-night I hear the owl again, closer, and then boots — no, not boots, hooves, distant, on the road behind us. Two riders, maybe three, moving fast toward Vell's Crossing. Toward tomorrow's checkpoint. Toward the rolls and the clerk and the lantern-holder who nods when asked *is this the one?*

I get up and wake Quentin with a hand on their shoulder, gently, the way I imagine you wake someone you haven't betrayed yet today.

"Riders," I whisper. "Behind us. We should cut east before dawn, take the shepherd track. It adds a day but skips the crossing."

Quentin blinks awake and — this is the part I will remember for the rest of whatever life I'm granted — they don't question it. They look at my face in the firelight, read whatever is written there, and nod. Trust extended blindly, a second time, to a man counting his betrayals like coins he can't spend.

They begin rolling the blankets, and I stand there with the confession still lodged sideways in my chest, and I think: *tomorrow.* Tomorrow, after the shepherd track, after the next safe distance, I'll say it. Tomorrow I'll pay the price Quentin named — be seen accurately and stay anyway.

Then Quentin pauses, half-turned, blanket bundle under one arm, and says quietly:

"For what it's worth — whoever your liar turns out to be, Jude — I hope they're worth the anger."

And walks toward the dark edge of the ruined wall to take their turn sleeping standing up, because they've done that before, I realize; they've slept upright against walls in twenty years of nights I knew nothing about, and the thought lands in me like a stone dropped down a well I can't see the bottom of.

Tomorrow, I promise the dark. Tomorrow I'll tell them.

Behind us, on the road to Vell's Crossing, the riders' hoofbeats fade — and beneath them, faint as a second heartbeat, I hear something else: bells. Small, many, the kind the crown's proclamations carry through the night. Someone is reading names in the dark ahead of us, and I would bet my sister's life — which is, after all, the only currency I have left — that one of the names is ours.
