## 11. Cartographer's Daughter

The archive sat in the crook of the mountains like an old tooth that had refused to fall out, and I understood immediately why: nobody had wanted to pull it. It was a squat building of grey stone, half-swallowed by the slope itself, so that its upper windows looked out over a valley of pine and its lower windows, if you could call them windows, looked directly into rock. The people of the village below — eleven houses, one shrine, a suspicion of strangers that you could spread on bread like butter — called it the Remembering House, which was either charming or ominous depending on how much you trusted places that named themselves after their function.

I did not trust it. But we needed maps, or something better than maps, because every map I'd bought since Salt and Ash Road had shown the same insult: the east simply stopped. Rivers ran off the parchment's edge into nothing. Mountains were drawn as decorative squiggles with no names. One cartographer, when pressed, had told me cheerfully that beyond the pass there was "weather, mostly," as though he'd personally checked.

So it was Jude who suggested the archive — of course it was, Jude knew everything about where things were kept, that was half of what being the crown's gardener meant, knowing where living things were stored and how they liked their light — and it was Jude who talked us past the archivist, a man shaped like a question mark who took our coin and asked no questions, which in my experience is what coin is actually for.

"You wait here," I told them at the door.

"Quentin—"

"I mean it. There are books in there older than both our sins combined, and if any of them wake up cranky, I'd rather it ate me first."

Jude held my gaze for a moment longer than necessary — they'd gotten good at that lately, holding my gaze like it was a door they were deciding whether to knock on — and then said, "Don't be long," in the tone of someone who has decided not to say three other sentences.

The interior smelled the way all old archives smell: dust, leather, tallow, and the faint sweet rot of knowledge nobody had come to collect. Lamps burned low in alcoves. And in the third alcove, sorting folios with hands that trembled but did not fumble, sat an old woman.

She was small. That was the first thing. Small and neat as a folded letter, silver hair pinned with what I would later realize was a compass needle, wearing a coat too heavy for the season because she was cold the way old people get cold, from the inside out. She looked up at me with pale eyes and went back to her folios, and I thought, *good, another person who has mastered the art of pretending strangers don't exist*, and I moved past her toward the map cases.

I found the eastern wall within the hour. Or rather, I found where the eastern wall should have been. The map case was there. The drawer slid open. Inside: dust, a brass rod, and a rectangle of cleaner wood where a chart had lain for years and been removed — recently, by the look of it, the outline sharp as a shadow under a lifted book.

"They come for those," said a voice behind me. "Every few years. Soldiers, clerks, men with soft voices and hard eyes. They take the eastern charts and burn them in the courtyard, and we catalogue the ash." A dry sound that might have been laughter. "I do the cataloguing. It's very thorough work. Ash is ash."

I turned. The old woman stood there holding a single folio against her chest the way you hold a child, or a wound.

"I'm looking for anything east of the pass," I said. "Roads. Rivers. Ruins. Anything."

"Why?"

"It's a long story."

"I'm very old," she said. "Long stories are the only ones I can still afford."

There was something in her voice — a flatness, a patience worn down to the grain — that made me answer honestly, which is a thing I almost never do. "We're running. From the hunt. We need somewhere the crown's maps end."

Something moved behind her eyes then. Not recognition. *Anticipation.* Like a fisher seeing the line go taut.

"The crown's maps end where I tell them to end," she said. "Or where they tell me to. It varies by decade." She set the folio down on the reading table with great care. "Show me your hands."

I blinked. "I beg your pardon?"

"Your hands. Show me."

It was such an absurd request, and delivered with such authority, that I nearly complied out of sheer bewilderment — twenty years of selling charms will teach you that bewilderment is a doorway, and I've made a living walking through other people's. But I caught myself, and instead I laughed and said, "Madam, I'm just a charm-seller passing through—"

"Yes," she said softly. "You would say that. You'd have said it beautifully, too. You always did."

The lamp flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft.

"What did you say?"

She opened the folio. Inside, protected between sheets of waxed linen, was a page covered in diagrams — circles nested in circles, lines radiating from centers like the spokes of terrible wheels — and annotations in at least four hands, some centuries apart. At the bottom, in ink gone brown, was a sketch of two cupped palms, and rising from them, drawn as a cartographer draws wind, a series of fine parallel lines curving upward and outward, each one labeled in a dead shorthand.

"This is a survey," she said. "Not of land. Of taking. My mother drew it. She was a cartographer of a country that no longer exists, and in the last year of that country, before it fell, she documented the thefts. What was taken. Who took it." Her finger came to rest on the sketched hands. "The thief left marks. Every memory pulled leaves a signature on the one who pulls it — a curl in the lines, here—" she tapped the diagram, "—and here. No two alike. My mother catalogued forty-one signatures in that final year. Forty-one thieves sent across the border to strip us bare before the soldiers ever came."

The lamp flame leaned again. My mouth had gone dry in the specific way it goes dry before I steal, except I wasn't stealing, I was only standing there, and yet the room felt *harvested*.

"Forty-one signatures," I said carefully. "And you're telling me this why? You think I'm one of these thieves? Madam, I'm fifty years too young—"

"Am I saying that?" She tilted her head. "No. I'm saying the signatures persist. In families. In lines of inheritance, sometimes. In—" and here her voice did something complicated, "—in students. Teachers leave marks in their pupils; pupils leave marks in theirs. The gift runs downhill like water and pools in low places, in poor quarters, in market stalls, in *charm shops*—" she said the word with a precision that told me she had already asked the archivist exactly who had paid him and what they'd claimed to be, "—and my mother wrote that a trained eye can read a signature even secondhand. Even through a lie. So show me your hands, charm-seller, and hold them palm-up, and if you're innocent you may laugh at me all the way back to your little life."

And here is the thing I have never confessed to anyone, the thing I am confessing now to no one, to this page, to the dark: I showed her my hands.

Because some part of me — the part that has spent twenty years flinching at criers and burning ledgers — some part of me already knew. Knew the way you know a room has been searched before you see the drawers open. The way you know, on certain grey mornings in the market, that you are being watched, and turn and find no one, and feel *disappointed*.

I held my hands palm-up over her mother's diagram.

The old woman bent close. She produced a lens — a real lens, ground crystal, the kind of thing that gets people executed in the cities — and studied my palms for a long, long time. The lamps hissed. Somewhere above us, in the rock, water dripped with the patience of geology.

Then she straightened up and looked at me, and I saw that whatever she had expected to find, she had found worse.

"There," she whispered, and touched the air an inch above my left palm, tracing something only she could see. "The curl. The double curl. Signature thirty-one." Her hand was shaking now, properly shaking, the folio rattling against the table. "My mother drew it nine times. Nine thefts. The same thief, again and again, working the garden districts, working the *palace gardens*—" 

"Stop," I said.

"—taking from gardeners, from groundskeepers, from the royal conservatory staff who had come east to study our flowers, our *living collections*, and the thief took what they saw and gave them back smooth blanks and sent them home smiling, and my mother traced that signature until the week the sky went white and everything—"

"*Stop.*"

I said it louder than I meant to. Louder than is safe in a country that executes magic-users. The word cracked off the stone walls and came back to me smelling of my own fear.

But she didn't stop. She stepped closer instead, close enough that I could see the veins in her ancient hands, and she said, very quietly, very clearly:

"You helped erase my country from every map. You helped erase it from every *mind*. And you're standing here asking me for a map of it."

The silence that followed was total. Even the drip of water seemed to pause out of respect, or spite.

"That's impossible," I heard myself say. My voice sounded like someone else's — reasonable, steady, the shopkeeper's voice, the voice I use on customers while my fingers do the real work. "I've never been east of the pass. I've never stolen anything but small memories, small ones, willing ones, pennies for charms, I keep accounts, I've *always kept accounts*—"

"Have you?" she asked. "Can you show me the account of your own life? Every year of it? Every—"

"Yes." And then, because I am honest in the worst moments, the moments when honesty is most expensive: "Yes. Almost. There are... gaps. Everyone has gaps."

"Gaps," she repeated. She tasted the word like wine she suspected was vinegar. "How large are your gaps, thief?"

And I reached, the way I reach when I take from others, inward instead of outward, into my own self, into the rooms of my own memory where I keep myself — and I found the gap she meant. I had known about it forever, the way you know about a bricked-up door in your own house. The years before the market. Before the city. Before Quentin Joubert, charm-seller, a name I had somehow always had and somehow never questioned. On the far side of that bricked door: childhood. A mother's face. A reason. A *why*.

Nothing. Smooth blank. The kind of blank I have given back to a hundred customers, given back smiling, given back *smooth* —

Oh.

Oh, no.

The old woman watched my face do whatever it did, and nodded slowly, as though I were a student finally arriving at the lesson.

"Now you understand," she said. "You didn't erase us and walk away whole. Someone erased *you*. Or you did. The signature works both ways — my mother wrote that too. The thief who takes too much begins to take from themselves. The lines curl inward eventually. Every thief's does, given time." She closed the folio gently, the way you close eyes. "Signature thirty-one. The double curl. The one that worked the gardens." She looked up at me, and there was no triumph in her face at all, which was somehow the cruelest part. Only a tired, terrible certainty. "I have waited sixty years for that signature to come back east. I used to dream it would arrive in chains. Instead it arrives asking me for directions."

"I don't remember," I said. My voice was very small. "I swear to you, on whatever you hold holy, on my own grave, I don't *remember*—"

"No," she agreed. "That's rather the point."

* * *

I don't know how long I stood there. Long enough that my knees learned the stone floor. Long enough that the lamps burned lower and the shadows of the shelves grew taller around us like a jury filing in.

Somewhere in the middle of it, the practical part of me — the part that has kept me alive through twenty years of criers and checkpoints — started making lists. *Jude is outside. Jude is waiting. Jude flinches at checkpoints like a professional. Jude knows the roads, knows where things are kept, knew about this archive, brought us here, suggested it, insisted—*

I shut that thought away fast, slammed it behind a door, told it we would not be needing it tonight. Suspicion is a luxury. Right now I had a larger problem, which was that I appeared to be standing inside my own crime.

"Tell me," I said at last. My voice came out scraped. "If you're going to curse me, or denounce me, or send for soldiers — I'd rather know first. I won't run. I couldn't outrun my own hands anyway." I looked down at them, those ordinary palms, those harmless market-stall hands that had plucked a thousand small memories like petals. *He loves me, he loves me not.* "Just tell me what I did. All of it. I'll carry it. I've carried worse. I think I've carried worse. I don't — I don't actually know what I've carried."

The old woman studied me for a long moment. Then, without a word, she gathered her folios, tucked them under her arm, and jerked her chin toward a stair I hadn't noticed, cut deep into the rock behind the map cases, descending.

"Not here," she said. "The walls in archives have ears, and mine have been listening to the wrong masters for sixty years. Come down. I'll show you what my mother saved. And then, thief—" she paused on the top stair, small and neat and cold-eyed in the lamplight, "—you can decide whether you're the knife or just the hand that held it. Though I'll warn you now, my mother never managed to decide either."

I followed her down into the mountain.

The stairs went further than any cellar should. The air changed — cooler, then warmer, then strange, carrying a smell I knew and could not place, green and wet and old, the smell of—

Of growing things. Of a conservatory. Of *gardens*.

My feet slowed on the steps without my permission. My heart was doing something embarrassing. Because that smell went through me like a bell struck from inside, and for one vertiginous moment I was five years old, or six, kneeling in warm dirt while someone sang, and there were flowers I could almost name, flowers that grew nowhere on any surviving map, and the feeling lasted precisely as long as a match burns and then guttered out, leaving me standing on a staircase holding nothing but the smoke of it.

The old woman glanced back at me and saw my face.

"Yes," she said quietly. "It does that. Down here, near what's left." She pushed open a door at the bottom — iron-bound, older than the archive above, older maybe than the mountain — and lamplight spilled out onto shelves upon shelves upon shelves, thousands of folios, jars, seed-boxes, charts rolled tight as sleeping snakes. A library. A vault. An ark.

"My mother was the royal cartographer's daughter," she said, stepping through. "Before the end, she understood what was coming. So she hid what could be hidden. Maps. Records. Seeds." She ran her hand along a shelf with something like tenderness. "Sixty years I've kept it. Sixty years waiting for the signature to come home." She turned and fixed me with those pale eyes. "Well, thief. Here is everything your hands helped unmake. Look at it. Look at all of it. And then you can tell me what you intend to do about it."

I looked.

And on the nearest shelf, in a jar of cloudy glass, sealed with wax and sixty years of patience, something green stirred — faintly, slowly, like a sleeper turning over — and turned toward me as I passed.

As though it remembered me.
