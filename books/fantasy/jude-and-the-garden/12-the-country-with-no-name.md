## 12. The Country With No Name

I had always assumed that if I ever learned what I was, it would come to me in a moment of revelation — trumpets, lightning, some grand unsealing of truth. Instead it came to me over barley tea in a mountain archive that smelled of dust and mouse, from an old woman who looked at me the way you look at a stain you've been trying to scrub out of a favorite coat for forty years.

"Drink," she said. "You've gone the color of the paper."

The tea was terrible. I drank it anyway. Jude sat across from me with their hands wrapped around their own cup like it was the only warm thing left in the world, and watched me with an expression I couldn't read — which was becoming a habit I didn't care for.

"You said you recognized my work," I said. "My *signature*."

"I did." The old woman's name was Sef, and she moved like someone who had spent decades being careful with fragile things, except when she looked at me, and then she moved like someone deciding whether to drop one. She reached beneath the reading table and drew out a wooden box, older than the box it sat in, and opened it with the reverence of a priest and the reluctance of a debt collector.

Inside lay a strip of linen, folded small. Even before she unfolded it, I felt it — the way you feel a draft from a door you thought was shut.

"This came to the archive sixty years ago," Sef said. "A relic from before the Erasure. Look at the border."

She spread the linen flat. It was embroidered — vines, leaves, something flowering at each corner — worked in thread so fine it might have been painted. And along its edge ran a line of stitching unlike the rest. Tighter. Deliberate. A signature in thread.

"That's mine," I said, before I could stop myself. My mouth knew it before my mind did. "That's — I do that. When I take something, I leave a hem. A neat little hem so nothing frays."

"Yes," Sef said. "You do."

"But this is sixty years old. Older. I'm thirty-four."

"So you've told yourself." She folded her hands. "Tell me about your childhood, charm-seller. Quickly, and in detail. Every name, every street, every face."

I opened my mouth to oblige — it should have been easy, twenty years of guarding my past meant I'd rehearsed it often enough — and found nothing there. Not blankness exactly. Blankness would have been honest. This was worse: a smoothness. A wall painted to look like a window. I could tell her I'd grown up in Marrow Lane because I had documents saying so. I could describe the house because I'd walked past it once and memorized its facade the way a forger memorizes a seal. But when I reached for the child who had actually lived there —

"There's no one," I said. My voice came out strange. Flat as the painted wall. "There's paperwork where a person should be."

Jude made a small sound across the table. I didn't look up. If I looked up I'd see pity, or worse, confirmation, and I wasn't ready to be confirmed.

"Now," Sef said, not unkindly, which somehow made it unbearable, "you understand why I recognized your hem."

"Explain it to me anyway. Slowly. As though I'm simple."

"You are simple, in this one thing. We all are." She touched the edge of the embroidery, tracing the stitched border without touching the thread itself, the way you'd hover a hand over a sleeping animal. "Before your crown drew its maps, before your criers cried anything, there was another country east of here. Past the salt flats, past the pass you'll have crossed coming north. It had a name. No — that's wrong. It had *names*, dozens of them, one for every village, because it never agreed with itself about anything, which is a very healthy quality in a nation."

"What happened to it?"

"It ate itself," Sef said. "Or rather — it fed on memory. That was its nature. Its soil remembered rain better than rain falls. Its houses remembered their inhabitants after the inhabitants died; people lived in walls that loved them back. And its people — its people had a gift, the way your magicians have gifts. They could take a memory out of a head and plant it, and it would grow. Grief planted in spring, harvested as peace in autumn. Joy shared like seed grain. A whole economy of remembering. Travelers who crossed the border came home lighter and happier and unable to say why, and the country kept the difference, and the difference kept the country alive."

"And your crown," Jude said quietly, "didn't like that."

Sef glanced at them. "Your crown — forgive me, I don't know what you are to each other — saw two problems. The first was practical: memory is wealth, and wealth flowing out of a kingdom through its eastern border looks, to a king, like theft. The second was theological, if you like. A country whose people can set down their griefs and pick them up again at will is a country that cannot be ruled by fear. You cannot threaten a man with exile if he can give away the memory of home. You cannot hold a grudge over generations if anyone can harvest it and compost it into forgiveness. Fear needs roots. Their gift pulled fear up by the root."

She paused. Somewhere above us, in the archive's upper floors, a shutter banged in the wind, and all three of us flinched — Jude fastest, I noticed, Jude recovering slowest, hands still white around the cup. I filed that away. There was a growing pile of such things, filed and unexamined, and I was starting to suspect the pile had a shape.

"So they erased it," I said.

"They erased it," Sef agreed. "Not with fire. Fire leaves ash, and ash leaves stories, and stories leave longing. They erased it the way you'd erase a figure from a ledger — completely, entry by entry, until the arithmetic balances and no auditor alive can prove a theft occurred. Cartographers redrew the mountains. Priests rewrote the prayers. Mothers were given new memories of how many children they had borne." Her voice didn't shake, but it went thin, the way rope goes thin before it parts. "And the instrument of the erasure — the tool that reached into ten thousand heads and unpicked ten thousand hems —"

"Was someone with my gift," I said.

"Was someone with your gift," she said, "whose hems I have spent sixty years collecting. Do you know how many fragments survived? Scraps of linen, lines of song, a child's drawing on a slate that no adult could explain. Each one carries a signature. The same hand. The same tidy, careful, *considerate* hem — because whoever did it was gentle even in the doing, gentle the way a surgeon is gentle, and it will disgust you someday to learn how much that gentleness accomplished."

The tea had gone cold in my cup. I looked at my hands. Ordinary hands. Charm-seller's hands, ink-stained, coin-counting, careful. Hands that had spent twenty years taking small things from willing strangers and leaving everything neat behind them, and had never once asked why the taking felt less like theft than like *homesickness*.

"I remember none of it," I said.

"No."

"Is that my work too? Did I unpick my own—"

"No one knows," Sef said. "Perhaps the country spared you. Perhaps you spared yourself. Perhaps the crown took your payment in advance and called it mercy. What I know is this: a girl arrived at the western edge of that country in the last year, weeping, half-starved, carried part of the way by soldiers who had orders no one would repeat aloud. And when the erasure came, everyone within reach of it forgot, and everything within reach of it dissolved — except the girl. Except you. Either the erasure could not touch you, or it chose not to, and either answer means the same thing." She leaned forward, and for the first time her eyes weren't looking at a stain. They were looking at a person, which was worse. "You may be the last inheritance of a nation that no longer exists. Everything it was — its gift, its gardens, its way of feeding on memory without starving anyone — lives now in whatever is left inside you, behind that painted wall where your childhood should be."

I stood up. I don't remember deciding to stand; my body simply declined to continue sitting inside a sentence like that. The archive tilted pleasantly. Jude rose too, half-reaching, then thought better of it.

"The Door," I heard myself say. "On the road east. Pilgrims talk about a door that isn't there. Is that—"

"The border," Sef said. "What's left of it. The country couldn't be killed outright — living things don't die cleanly — so it withdrew behind its own threshold and sealed it, and it will stay sealed until its debts are paid." Her gaze settled on me with a weight I felt physically, between the shoulder blades. "Debts of memory, charm-seller. Every theft has a creditor. And somewhere in you, behind your painted wall, is the account book."

* * *

Jude's hands were shaking.

They hid it well — Jude hid everything well, that was the whole architecture of them, load-bearing calm from foundation to roof — but I'd been watching closely lately, watching the way you watch weather, and I caught it now: the tremor as they set down the cup, the too-careful stillness afterward, the effort of it. Anyone else would have called it fear of the old woman's story. Fear of erasure, of kings who rewrite mothers, of borders that eat travelers.

But I had spent three weeks on the road with Jude, and I was beginning to build my own private ledger, and the entry I kept circling was this: Jude hadn't flinched at any of it the way a listener flinches. Jude had flinched the way a witness flinches. The way someone flinches who already knew how the story went and was dreading the ending.

We left the archive as the light failed, Sef pressing a scrap of linen into my hand — a fragment, a hem, proof I apparently existed before I existed — and found a room above a chandler's shop, because Jude insisted on rooms with two exits and I had stopped asking why. The bed was narrow, the floor was crooked, and neither of us slept.

I heard Jude turn over for perhaps the hundredth time, and then, in the dark, in a voice sanded low:

"Quentin. What she said. About the crown rewriting mothers."

"Mm."

"Do you believe it? Truly? That a king could reach into ordinary people — gardeners, cooks, soldiers — and change what they know? Change what they feel? Make them love a thing they ought to hate, or report on a person they ought to protect?" A pause, precisely measured, the length of a man checking his footing on ice. "Could make someone betray a friend and barely know it, if the leash were short enough?"

I turned the question over, sleepy and stupid with exhaustion, feeling only its surface.

"I think," I said finally, "that if the crown could do that, it wouldn't need executions. It wouldn't need hunts or proclamations or lists of names. Fear is expensive. Why buy fear if you can grow obedience in a garden?"

Another pause. Longer.

"Yes," Jude said. "Why indeed."

The silence that followed had a texture I'd never felt from them before — not comfortable, not companionable, but taut, like wire strung across a stairwell in the dark. I was too tired to name it. I named it anyway, later, much later, lying awake while the chandler's candles breathed below us: it was the sound of a person standing at the edge of a confession, testing the ice, and finding it thinner than they'd hoped.

"Quentin," Jude said again, softer.

"Don't." I don't know why I said it. Some animal instinct, some thief's reflex — the sense of a door about to open onto something I could not put back. "Whatever it is. Not tonight. Tonight I just want one thing in the world to be what it seems, and right now that thing is you."

The wire went slack. Or seemed to.

"Sleep, then," Jude said, and their voice was warm, and steady, and exactly what I needed, which is precisely why, staring into the dark, I could not sleep at all.

* * *

In the morning, Sef was waiting for us at the archive door with a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, and an expression that had settled overnight from reluctant into grim.

"I've been thinking about your hem," she said to me, thrusting the bundle into my arms. Inside were more fragments — thread, paper, a chip of painted tile, each one humming faintly against my palms like birds in a sack. "About how gentle it is. Gentle hands don't survive erasures by accident. Someone taught those hands, protected them, aimed them. Which means the crown didn't just erase a country." Her jaw tightened. "They kept a piece of it. Leashed it. Raised it."

"Raised it to do what?"

"To find the rest." She stepped back and looked at me the way cartographers must look at coastlines they're about to get wrong. "Erasure is never finished, charm-seller. It has to be maintained. Somewhere in your painted room there is a list of what remains — every scrap, every survivor, every door — and instructions. And whoever holds your leash—"

She stopped. Because Jude had gone white.

Not pale. White, the way paper is white, the way a page is white before the ink goes down, and their eyes had fixed on the middle distance with the particular blankness of a person reading something written on the inside of their own skull.

"Jude?" I said.

They blinked. Twice. And smiled — quick, warm, flawless, a smile I had seen a hundred times on the road and never once doubted.

"We should go," they said. "It's a long way to the pass, and the patrols move at dawn."

And because I was tired, and frightened, and holding a bag full of the bones of a dead country, I believed them.

I believed them for exactly as long as it took to walk out the gate.

Then I remembered the wire in the dark, the ice under the confession, and the way Jude had asked — so carefully, so *precisely* — whether a crown could make someone betray a friend without knowing it.

Behind the painted wall where my childhood should have been, something shifted. Like a draft from a door.

Like a door, unlocking from the other side.
