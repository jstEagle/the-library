## 13. Wolves in Livery

The pass had a name once, probably. Everything in this country has a name if you dig deep enough — every rock, every stream, every sad little shrine to a saint nobody prays to anymore. But the map Jude carried had worn away at exactly that spot, the ink rubbed pale by some previous traveler's thumb, as though even paper couldn't be bothered to remember this place.

"Greystone Pass," Jude said, when I asked.

"You just made that up."

"I did just make that up."

"It sounds like a place where bad things happen in stories."

Jude squinted up at the narrow throat of rock ahead of us, where the mountain pinched itself together and let only a sliver of road through, and said, with the cheerful fatalism I'd come to dread and rely on in equal measure, "Then we should hurry. Bad things hate punctuality."

We were three weeks out from the city by then, three weeks of pilgrim's gray and pilgrim's dust, of sleeping in ditches and sharing bread torn rather than cut because we'd sold the knife in Vellermont to pay for a night under a roof that smelled of goats and regret. Three weeks of learning each other's small habits: how Jude counted their steps under their breath without noticing, how I talked to horses as though they were creditors. Three weeks of checkpoints, and Jude flinching at each one in that too-practiced way I'd noticed on the road and told myself not to notice again.

I was getting good at not noticing things. It's a survival skill. It's also, I can now report, a way of walking blindfolded into wolves.

* * *

Let me tell you about the pass, since it turned out to matter.

It ran between two walls of granite furred with lichen, maybe forty paces long and barely wide enough for the mule track that wasn't really a track anymore, more of a suggestion the mountain had made and then abandoned. Snowmelt ran down its middle in a thin bright thread. At the far end, the road opened onto a shelf of meadow and then dropped away toward the eastern valleys — toward the border, toward whatever waited for us there, which at that point I still believed was simply distance. Safety, I thought, was mostly a matter of mileage.

I have since revised my theory of safety.

We stopped at noon to eat, because pilgrims stop at noon to eat, because performing normalcy is a discipline like any other. Jude built a small fire out of wet twigs with the patience of someone who has done hard gardening work all their life and doesn't see why fire should be easier than anything else. I laid out the bread and the hard cheese and the last of the dried plums, and divided them the way I always did — Jude slightly more, me slightly less, an accounting I kept privately and never mentioned, because love, whatever else it is, is also bookkeeping you don't show your partner.

I want to be clear about something before I go on. In that moment, sitting on cold stone with my back against warm rock, watching Jude blow on a stubborn flame, I was happy. Not safe — I hadn't been safe since the criers read the proclamation and the market emptied around me like water down a drain — but happy, in the specific way of people who are happy *despite*, who have decided that fear will get its hours but not all of them. Jude laughed at something. I don't remember what. That's the cruelty of memory; it keeps the ledgers and loses the laughter. But I remember the shape of the laughing, the way their shoulders went loose, the way they looked at me afterward like they were checking whether I'd caught it too.

I'd caught it. I'd been catching it for weeks, and pretending I hadn't, and both of those were lies I was comfortable living inside.

Then Jude went quiet.

Not the ordinary quiet. The other kind — the kind I should have recognized by then, the silence of a body listening to something its mouth hasn't agreed to say. Their head tilted, very slightly, toward the near end of the pass. Their hand found a stone beside them and stayed there, resting on it, the way a hand rests on a tool.

"What," I said.

"Pilgrims," Jude said. "Behind us. Four or five. They've been keeping our pace since the last valley."

"Pilgrims keep pace with pilgrims."

"These ones haven't passed us." Jude's voice stayed level, conversational, pitched for no one but me. "Three days, Quentin. Nobody walks behind another traveler for three days on a road this empty unless they're shy or hunting. And these ones don't talk."

"How do you know they don't talk?"

"Because when I dropped back yesterday to fill the water skins, they were close enough to hear, and they said nothing to each other. Nothing. For half a mile." Jude picked up the stone, weighed it, set it down again. "Pilgrims argue about saints. Pilgrims complain about their feet. These ones walk like soldiers off duty, which is worse than soldiers on duty, because it means they're pretending."

I looked back down the road, the way we'd come, the thread of it winding down through grey scree and brown scrub into the haze of the lowlands. Empty. Of course it was empty. Wolves in livery know how to stand behind the scenery.

"We could go around," I said. "There was a fork, back before the shrine—"

"That adds two days over open ground. Open ground is where they take you." Jude stood, unhurried, brushing off their knees, the whole performance of a person deciding nothing in particular. "We go through. We go through fast, and at the far side there's a shepherd track that drops east, and by dark we're a rumor."

"And if they follow us into the pass?"

"Then the pass does what passes do." Jude smiled at me, and it was a good smile, and I understood later that it had been meant as armor for me, not for them. "Bad things hate punctuality. Let's be early."

* * *

We ate standing up, which no pilgrim does, and left the fire smoldering, which no careful traveler does, and I remember thinking distantly that we had already begun to fail at being innocent, that guilt leaks out of you like heat, that anyone watching would read our haste for exactly what it was. Then I thought: let them read it. If they're crown hunters, innocence was never going to save me. Registration or death, the criers said, and I had burned my ledgers, and burning your ledgers is a kind of signature.

The pass swallowed us. That's the honest word for it. The walls came in and the light went grey-green with lichen-reflection and the snowmelt chattered under our boots, and sound behaved strangely, arriving from everywhere and nowhere. My own breathing sounded like someone following me.

Halfway through, Jude stopped.

They didn't turn around. They stood with their head bowed, listening, and I watched the gardeners' hands — hands I knew by then better than I knew my own handwriting — curl slowly closed.

"Quentin," they said quietly. "Keep walking."

"Don't be absurd."

"Keep walking, and when the road opens, turn east and don't stop until—"

The stones sang.

That's the only word I have for the sound a sling-stone makes when it misses your skull by a hand's width and strikes the wall instead — a high ringing note, almost pretty, and then the crack of it, and then the lichen dust drifting down like dirty snow. Something whirred past my ear and I understood, in the slow way understanding arrives when your body gets there first, that someone behind us had decided the conversation was over.

I turned. Of course I turned. You would too.

Four of them, coming up the pass in a line, and they weren't even pretending anymore. Gray cloaks over chain shirts, and above the chain, at the collar, the stitched badge of the crown hunt — a black key on white, the emblem of the Gift Registry, the lock-and-key device they'd hung in every market square since the proclamation. *All gift-bearers must register.* Beneath the key, smaller, the motto I could recite in my sleep by then: *A kingdom kept whole.*

Their leader was a woman with a face like a folded letter — everything important written inside. She had a crossbow, loaded, held at rest, which is to say pointed at the ground in a way that promised it wouldn't stay there.

"Travelers," she called, and her voice filled the pass the way water fills a cup. "In the name of the Registry, stand and be named."

And here is the thing I will carry to whatever grave I get: for one whole breath, I considered it. Stand and be named. Walk back to the city in a cart, register, sign my true name in their ledger, wear whatever token they give the licensed — a brand, a collar, a paper sewn to the coat, I'd heard versions of all of them. Twenty years I'd survived by being small. Some part of me, the part that had spent two decades selling charms and harvesting crumbs of memory and calling it living, reached for smallness the way a hand reaches for a banister in the dark.

Then I looked at Jude. Jude, who had dropped back to stand between me and the crossbow without appearing to decide to do it, the way water doesn't decide to run downhill. Jude, whose loyalty I trusted with the whole stupid weight of me, not yet knowing — though I suspected, God help me, I had suspected for weeks — that the loyalty had been purchased, that it came with a leash, that the leash ended in a handler's fist somewhere back in the city.

You can't stand and be named when the person standing next to you might be the reason for the name on the list.

"No," I called back.

The leader sighed, genuinely regretful, the way a butcher is regretful about the animal. "Gift-bearer, then. Or friend to one. Same cart either way." She lifted the crossbow to her shoulder, unhurried, professional. Behind her, her three drew steel with the soft industrial hiss of blades leaving scabbards, and the sound echoed off the pass walls and multiplied until it sounded like eight men drawing, sixteen, a company.

"Jude," I whispered. "Run."

Jude did not run.

What happened next took less time than it takes me to describe it, and I've described it so many times since — to myself, in the dark, in the years after — that I sometimes lose track of which version is the memory and which is the telling. So let me give it to you plain, the way it actually was.

The leader fired. She was good; she fired at Jude, not me, because you kill the shield first. The bolt crossed the pass in a blink and Jude twisted — twisted the way you twist away from a falling branch, gardener's reflexes, body that knows how to move around obstacles — and the bolt tore through the shoulder of Jude's cloak instead of the chest beneath it and buried itself in the wall with that singing crack again.

And then Jude moved forward.

Not backward. Every instinct in the pass expected backward. Jude went *forward*, into the drawn swords, into the four of them, and I saw them pull something from their belt — the little pruning knife, the one they'd used for cutting cheese and trimming rope and whittling useless beautiful things by the fire, a knife with a blade shorter than my hand — and I screamed something, I don't know what, some wordless thing that bounced off the granite and came back to me sounding like everyone I'd ever failed.

The first soldier swung. Jude ducked under it the way you duck under a low bough ten thousand times a day in an orchard, and came up inside the man's reach, which is the one place a sword is useless, and drove the pruning knife up under his jaw.

I have seen death. Growing up in that city, you see death; it hangs from the gibbets outside the gates like laundry. But I had never seen it made. I had never stood six feet away and watched a person I loved reach into another person and switch them off, quickly, terribly, with a tool meant for shaping living things.

The soldier sat down. That's what I remember most — he sat down, folding, almost politely, as though accepting a chair offered at table, and the blood came out over Jude's wrist and steamed faintly in the cold air of the pass, and his sword rang on the stone, and none of the other sounds in the world mattered as much as that sword ringing.

The pass went still. Even the snowmelt seemed to hold its chatter.

The remaining three stared at Jude. Jude stood over the fallen man, breathing hard, the knife red to the hilt, and their face — I need to tell you about their face, because it was the face of a person watching their own door close. Not horror at the killing. Horror at the *fact* of it, at the irreversibility, at the line lying behind them now, bright and clean and uncrossable-again. Whatever Jude had been before — informant, gardener, the crown's patient instrument planted in a charm-seller's life like a seed in a bed — that person could not exist anymore. You cannot unkill a man. You cannot prune him back into growing.

"There were others," the leader said quietly, to Jude, in a voice stripped of the official cadence. Just a person speaking to a person. "Before this one. Wren of Candle Lane. The sisters at Harrow Ford." She said it like a password, like proof she knew him — knew the history, the record, the leash. "You think running changes what you are?"

"I think," Jude said, and their voice cracked down the middle, "it changes what I am *next*."

She raised the crossbow again. Faster, this time. Less professional, more angry, and angry crossbows miss, and hers missed — wide, shrieking off the wall — because at the same moment the third soldier lunged at Jude and Jude caught his wrist and turned him, and the bolt meant for Jude's heart went into his shoulder instead, and he screamed, and the fourth soldier hesitated, and hesitation in a fight is a door, and Jude went through it.

I wish I could tell you I helped. I wish I could dress it up, give myself a role, a thrown stone, a shouted warning, anything. The truth is I stood against the wall with my heart trying to climb out of my throat, and the gift rose in me — old reflex, twenty years of hunger, reaching for the nearest mind to *take*, to pull some memory out of these wolves and stagger them — and I reached, and I found nothing. Their minds were shut to me like doors in a plague street. Trained. The Registry trained its hunters against people like me. Of course it did. It had been hunting us longer than I'd been alive.

So I did the only thing I could do. When the leader dropped the empty crossbow and drew her sword and went for Jude's exposed back, I threw myself at her legs.

It was not brave. It was clumsy and desperate and it nearly killed me; her elbow caught my temple and the world went white and sideways, and I hit the stone with my shoulder and tasted blood and snowmelt. But she stumbled, half a step, one stride lost — and a stride is a lifetime in a knife-fight — and Jude turned, and I will not describe what happened next except to say that the pass was quieter afterward, and there were fewer people in it.

Then there were three bodies on the wet stone, and two of us standing among them, and the leader gone — fled, limping, back down the road toward the lowlands, alive, which meant word would travel, which meant every hunter between here and the border would know our faces by week's end.

Jude dropped the knife.

It fell from their fingers like something hot, and they stood looking at their hands — at the red right hand, at the clean left one, as though taking inventory of a house that had burned down — and their shoulders started to shake.

"Jude."

"Don't." Their voice came out strangled. "Don't touch me. Don't — not yet. Please."

So I didn't touch them. I stood there in the green-grey light with blood cooling on the stones and my head ringing like the sling-stone's note, and I watched Jude Crane — no, that's wrong, I never learned their family name, isn't that something, months of roads and I knew how they counted steps but not their father's name — I watched Jude kneel down beside the first man, the one who sat down, and close his eyes, and say something under their breath. Not a prayer for themselves. A prayer for him. Gardeners' hands folded over a dead soldier's face.

When they finally looked up at me, their eyes were the calmest and the most destroyed I have ever seen, both at once, which I hadn't known eyes could manage.

"She knew things," Jude said. "About me. About what I was sent—" They stopped. Swallowed the rest of it like a stone.

"Sent?" I said.

The word hung in the pass. Jude looked at it, and at me, and I watched them weigh — visibly, horribly, the scales moving in their face — how much truth they could afford, here, now, with a wounded hunter limping toward reinforcements and the light already going amber on the western wall.

"Not now," they said. "Please, Quentin. Not here. But I'll — I swear to you, I'll tell you everything. Before the border. Before the Door, if the stories are true. Everything, freely given. I promise."

I wanted to demand it. I wanted to grab their bloodied collar and shake the whole confession out of them right there among the dead. Instead I heard myself say, "Walk, then. Tell me while we walk."

And Jude — fugitive now, murderer in the crown's arithmetic, exile in their own — picked up the pruning knife, wiped it on the grass at the pass's edge with the automatic tenderness of someone who cleans tools, and followed me east, down the shepherd track, into the valleys where no map bothered to remember the names.

Behind us, somewhere below, a horn sounded. One note, then two. Calling in the wolves.

"They'll bring dogs," Jude said quietly. "And mirrors. And by tomorrow, they'll bring *him*."

"Who's *him*?"

Jude didn't answer. And the silence where the answer should have been was louder than the horn.
