## 14. What I Was Sent For

There is a particular kind of silence that comes after a confession. I'd imagined it my whole life, the way a condemned man imagines the scaffold — rehearsing it in the dark until the rehearsal felt worse than the thing itself. I had pictured Quentin shouting. I had pictured them crying. I had even, in my more honest hours, pictured the part I dreaded most: the quiet.

I got the quiet.

We were in the shepherd's hut above the treeline, the one with the collapsed roof and the hearth that smoked more than it warmed, because everything in my life smoked more than it warmed. Snow had been falling since noon, fat unhurried flakes that buried the road east behind us and, I hoped, buried the hunters too. Quentin was sitting by the fire with their hands wrapped around a tin cup of melted snow, not drinking it, just holding it, the way they held everything — like it might be taken back.

I had been building to this for three days. Since the pass. Since the soldier's blood froze on the stones and I stood over it with a dead man's knife in my hand and no story left in me worth the telling. Since Quentin looked at me across that body with an expression I couldn't read, and I realized with a lurch that I had spent six years learning to read them and had never once let them read me.

"I need to tell you something," I said.

Quentin didn't look up. "You've been saying that since the pass."

"Yes. Well." I turned the cup in my own hands, though mine held nothing. "I keep hoping it'll say itself so I won't have to."

"Jude."

"My name isn't Jude."

That landed. I watched it land. Their face did something complicated — not shock exactly, because I think some part of them had been waiting for this too, the same way I'd been waiting, two people circling the same fire for weeks pretending the smoke between us was weather.

"It's Yuda," I said. "It was Yuda, before. Before the crown gave me a name that fit through doorways easier. Jude the gardener. Harmless Jude. Everyone trusts a gardener. We carry dirt under our nails and knives we only use on roses."

"Stop," Quentin said, but softly, the way you say stop when you mean *go on*.

"The palace conservatory is real," I said. "I do work there. That part was true. The order for charms was real too — I wrote it myself, six drafts, trying to make it sound like something a frightened little charm-seller couldn't refuse. The handler wanted me inside your shop. Not once. For years."

The fire popped. Outside, the snow kept up its patient work of erasing the world.

"They recruited me when I was nineteen," I said. "My sister had the lung-rot. The healers' hall wanted payment upfront, all of it, or nothing, and what we had was nothing. A man from the chancery came to the ward where she was coughing her life out and sat down next to me like he'd known me all my life. He said the crown had a position open. Gardening work. And that the pay would cover everything."

"You spied on gift-bearers before me," Quentin said. It wasn't a question. They knew. Of course they knew — they'd watched me flinch at every checkpoint, every crier, every list of names. I had thought I was hiding it well. I had been hiding it the way a lighthouse hides.

"Two before you," I said. "A woman who made lamps burn without oil. A boy who could find anything lost. The woman went to the registration lists. The boy—" My throat closed. I made myself finish it. "The boy ran. They caught him in Vell. I identified him from across a market square. That was my whole job, most days. Look at people until I found the one who wasn't a person, just a gift wearing a person's coat. And then point."

Quentin set down the cup. Very carefully, the way they did everything, as if the cup were a small animal that might startle.

"And me?"

"You were the long assignment. The prize. The handler had rumors about a charm-seller in Lowmarket whose trinkets worked too well. Grief-charms. Memory-tokens. Things that shouldn't be possible at a street stall. He said, get close. Get trusted. Confirm the gift. And when the hunt comes—" I swallowed. "When the hunt comes, lead us to them."

"How long?"

"Six years."

The word *six* hung in the air between us like a smell.

"Six years," Quentin repeated. "Six years of— you carried my crates. You fixed my awning twice. You ate at my table every festival for six years and reported on me after."

"After," I said. "Always after. Never during. I never wrote down anything while I was sitting with you. I want you to know that. It doesn't matter. I know it doesn't matter. But I used to go home and sit in the dark and make myself wait until morning to write the report, because if I wrote it while your voice was still in my ears I would have had to admit what I was doing while I was doing it. So I waited. As if the waiting made it cleaner."

"Did it?"

"No."

Quentin nodded slowly, absorbing it, the way ground absorbs rain — without comment, without change, just taking it in somewhere deep where I couldn't follow.

"The night of the raid," they said. "You warned me."

"I betrayed my handler. Yes."

"Why?" And there it was. The question I had been dreading more than all the others, because I still didn't have an answer that wouldn't sound like a lie, and because the true answer was so large and so simple it embarrassed me.

"Because I loved you," I said. "And because somewhere in the last six years I stopped being able to tell which parts of me were the crown's and which parts were mine, and loving you was the only thing that felt entirely mine. Every report I sent you was a betrayal of you. And every hour I spent with you was a betrayal of them. I lived in the space between betrayals. And then the hunt came, and the space closed, and I had to pick."

"So you picked."

"I picked."

Quentin looked into the fire for a long time. When they spoke again their voice was level, which was somehow worse than anger.

"The woman. The lamp-woman. Did she have family?"

"A daughter. In the provinces. She'll have been told her mother registered and died of fever in custody."

"And the boy. Did he beg?"

"He ran first." I closed my eyes. "Then he begged."

Quentin nodded again. Then they stood up, unhurried, and took their cloak off the peg by the door.

"Where are you going?" I asked, and hated how fast my voice came out, how young.

"For a walk."

"It's snowing. Quentin, it's a whiteout up there past the ridge, you'll lose the road—"

"I know the road." They settled the cloak at their shoulders. "I've walked roads alone longer than I've known you."

"Please." I was on my feet now too, though I don't remember standing. "Say something. Call me a traitor. Throw the cup. Anything but—"

But they were already at the door, and they turned then, and their face in the firelight was the calmest I had ever seen it, and that calm was the worst thing I have ever seen, because it was the calm of someone closing a ledger. Final entry. Account settled.

"You answered honestly," Quentin said. "That's the only reason you're getting the walk instead of the silence forever. Goodbye, Jude. Or Yuda. Whoever wins."

And they stepped out into the snow, and pulled the door shut behind them, and left me alone with the fire and the tin cups and six years of reports I could recite by heart, filed in a chancery archive under a name that wasn't mine, testifying against a person who had fed me soup on my birthday every year I'd known them.

* * *

I don't know how long I sat there. An hour. Three. Long enough for the fire to die down to a red eye and for the cold to come in through the broken roof like something that had been waiting its turn.

I should have gone after them immediately. Any decent person would have gone after them immediately. Instead I sat in the ruin of my own making and did the thing I do worst: I thought.

I thought about the handler — Master Corvane, though he'd laugh at the title, he liked to be called nothing at all, just a soft-spoken man who appeared at your elbow with questions shaped like kindness. I thought about how he'd known about the pass. How the hunters had found us at a crossing I'd only chosen that morning. And how I had told no one that morning, told no one anything, because I'd burned my last letter to the chancery in a shrine-fire in Saltbridge—

Except I hadn't, had I. Because there was one report I never burned. One message I sent every month whether I meant to or not, sent by existing, sent by being seen at the checkpoints where the crown's eyes were always open. Six years of habit. I hadn't led them to the pass. I had just stopped being able to hide, and they had followed the only trail I've ever truly left: myself.

Or — the colder thought, the one that came last and stayed longest — maybe they'd followed Quentin. Maybe the gift leaves a scent, a wake, a shimmer in the air, and every mile east we'd traveled had been a flare burning on a hilltop saying *here, here, here.*

Either way it didn't matter. What mattered was that staying put was dying. The hunters had lost us at the pass only because the storm had covered both our tracks and theirs. Come morning, the snow would stop, and the world would be readable again, and men who read the world for a living would come up that road.

I banked the fire, shouldered my pack, and went out into the white.

Their tracks led east. Of course they led east — east was the Door, the country with no name, the archive crone's impossible geography, the whole mad pilgrimage we'd been walking toward since the cisterns beneath the city. East was also suicide, alone, in a storm, above the treeline, for a person carrying whatever Quentin carried. But Quentin had never once in six years done the sensible thing, and I loved that about them, and loving it had never once saved anyone.

I followed. What else does a tracker do? What else does a gardener do? You tend what's growing even when it's growing away from you.

The storm had other ideas about visibility. I lost the tracks within a hundred paces of the hut, found them again by luck, lost them for good near a stand of wind-bent pines where the drifts had swallowed everything equally. I stood in the roaring white and called their name — *Quentin*, and then, stupidly, *Yuda*, my own name, offered up to the storm like a coin, like proof I was handing over something real.

The storm kept it.

I found them at dusk, or rather I nearly walked into them — a shape resolving out of the blur, sitting motionless on a stone at the edge of a frozen tarn, hood back, snow gathering on their hair like they intended to stay until they became part of the mountain. Their eyes were open. Red-rimmed. They'd been crying, or the wind had done it for them; with Quentin you never got to know which, they guarded the difference jealously.

I stopped ten feet away. Close enough to talk. Far enough to be refused.

"There's a hunter's camp in the valley below the pass," they said, before I could speak. "I saw their fire from the ridge. Four, maybe five men. They're not searching anymore, Jude. They're waiting. Someone told them where we'd be."

"I didn't tell them."

"I believe you." They said it flatly, terribly, the way you believe weather. "That's what makes it worse. If you'd sold me, I could hate you cleanly. But you're just a man who was owned, and I can't hate an owned thing, and so I'm left with—" Their breath caught, and they mastered it. "I'm left with the arithmetic. Every kindness you gave me, subtracted by every report. Every warning, weighed against the boy in Vell. And the sums keep coming out different depending on the hour, and I am so tired, Jude. I am so tired of not knowing what you are."

"I know what I am," I said. "I'm a man who killed for you yesterday and confessed to you today and is going to follow you east tomorrow whether you speak to me again or not. I'm the leash the crown put on you, and I'm chewing through it. That's all I've got. There's no version of me that hasn't done what I did. There's only the version that walks east anyway."

Quentin looked at me for a long moment. Snow settled on their lashes. Behind them the tarn lay white and blind under the storm.

"The Door needs a truth freely given," they said quietly. "That's what the old woman said. I've been turning it over for days. Wondering what mine would be. What I could possibly hand over that would count." They stood, stiffly, brushing snow from their cloak. "Now I know yours. It was heavy. Thank you for carrying it here."

They started walking east along the tarn's edge. After a few paces they stopped. They didn't turn around.

"I'm not forgiving you," they said. "Understand that. Forgiveness is a door, and I'm not opening any doors today. But the Door ahead needs two people, and one of them is you, and so we walk. Apart. Together. However you want to call it."

"We'll call it walking," I said.

Something moved at the corner of their mouth. Not quite a smile. The scar tissue where a smile used to live, flexing in cold weather.

"We'll call it walking," they agreed.

And we went on east into the storm, twenty feet apart, two people bound for the same border with nothing settled between us but the miles — and behind us, far below in the valley, the hunters' fire burned steady and patient, and I understood, watching it dwindle, that we had gained a day at most, and that the crown did not send five men to catch a charm-seller and a gardener.

It sent five men when it already knew where the Door was.

Which meant someone else had paid for that knowledge.

Someone who wasn't me.
