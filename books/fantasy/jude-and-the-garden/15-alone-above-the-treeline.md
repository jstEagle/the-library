## 15. Alone Above the Treeline

The snow above the treeline doesn't fall so much as it hangs there, undecided, like a crowd that has heard something terrible and is waiting for someone else to confirm it. I walked up into that indecision for two days after Jude told me the truth, because grief is a coward's geography and mine wanted altitude. It wanted somewhere the air was too thin for thinking.

It didn't work. Thinking is a weed. It grows through anything.

I should explain what I left behind in the valley, so you understand the shape of my solitude. Jude stood in the snow outside the pass at Vell Hollow and said: *They sent me to find you. I confirmed your gift. I led them to your door.* Fourteen words — I counted, because counting is what I do instead of screaming — and then I walked away without answering, which was not dignity, whatever I told myself at the time. Dignity would have said something. Dignity would have asked *why*, or *how long*, or *did any of it mean anything*. What I did was simpler and uglier. I turned around and climbed.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about being robbed: it makes you an expert on other people's thefts. I have spent twenty years feeling the exact weight of what customers lost when I took from them — the small silver coins of memory, a first kiss, a mother's laugh, the smell of a specific rain. I could weigh them in my palm like a moneylender. And now I kept doing it with Jude. Weighing every kindness from the road against the possibility it was assigned. The shared blanket at the checkpoint. The way they always walked on my blind side. The joke about the mule. *Was that in your instructions?* I asked the snow. The snow, being snow, declined to comment.

By the second evening I found a shepherd's bothy — a stone hut the size of a coffin stood on end, half-swallowed by the mountain, with a roof that had opinions about weather and lost most of them. There was an old hearth, a pile of sheep-rotted straw, and a view of nothing but white going up forever. It was perfect. Misery loves company, but it adores real estate.

* * *

I made a fire out of fence posts I pried from the ruin, which felt appropriately transgressive, and ate the last of the hard bread I'd carried up, and then sat in the dark doing the thing I do worst: nothing.

Because here is the other thing. In the silence, with no Jude to watch and no road to watch it from, I finally noticed the hole.

Not the hole where memories should be — I've lived with that all my life, the general fog around my childhood, the sense that the years before age eleven were a room with the furniture covered in sheets. I'd stopped noticing it the way you stop noticing a limp. But tonight, alone, with the fire cracking its knuckles and nothing to steal and no one to charm, I put my attention *inside*, the way I do when I'm working — when I slip past a customer's outer thoughts and go looking for the good stuff, the warm rooms they keep locked — and I found, tucked in myself, a lock.

I don't know how else to describe it. Everyone's mind has doors; thieves learn this early. Most are cheap latches — fear, shame, habit — and I've picked thousands. But this one was in *me*. Built into me. And it wasn't built by fear or shame or habit.

It was built by craft.

My craft.

Someone had sealed a memory inside me using the same techniques I use on other people, and the hand was familiar the way your own handwriting is familiar — I knew it before I understood it. I knew it the way you know a house by the sound of its floorboards. This was my work. Younger work — crude at the edges, overcompensating in the middle, the magical equivalent of a fourteen-year-old's attempt at a mustache — but mine. I had done this to myself. I had found something inside my own head so unbearable that child-me, with the same gift I'd spent two decades pretending was only a market trick, had wrapped it in stone and thrown away the key.

And here's the part that made my hands shake, sitting alone in a dead shepherd's hut while the wind tried to renegotiate the roof: I could feel what was behind it.

Not the content. Not yet. But the *shape* of it, the way you can feel the shape of a thing through a locked door if the thing is heavy enough. It was heavy. It had mass and edges and — I pressed closer, careful as a burglar, careful as me — warmth. Green warmth. The memory behind that seal smelled, even through the stone, even through twenty years, of rain on leaves.

The green country. The one the dying refugee carried fragments of, the one the cartographer's daughter said I helped erase, the one with no name on any map because there was no map left to be nameless on. It wasn't just gone from the world. A piece of it was in here. In me. Locked away by my own hand, back when my hands were small enough to hide behind my back.

* * *

Sleep didn't come, which surprised no one, least of all me. Instead I sat cross-legged by the dying fire and conducted negotiations.

Let me tell you how a thief approaches a lock, since apparently I'm the last expert on the subject available for comment. First you study it. So I studied. The seal wasn't brute force — brute force I could break, given time and privacy and possibly a nosebleed. No, whoever built it — and fine, *I*, let's say it plainly, the child I used to be, that little professional — had built it the way I build all my best work: not as a wall but as a price. The seal didn't resist opening. It *charged* for it. You could walk through anytime. You just had to pay the toll, and the toll was set by the person who built the lock, and the person who built the lock was me, aged approximately never-to-be-forgotten-but-in-fact-totally-forgotten.

I reached for the toll the way you reach for the price tag on something you already want.

It wouldn't show me the number directly. Of course it wouldn't. My younger self was, I'm forced to admit, better at this than I am — the seal showed me the toll the way a ferryman shows you the far shore: you see it, you just can't reach it from here. But I got impressions. Fragments, like reading a bill through wet paper.

One: the seal wanted time. Hours of it, days maybe, of sustained attention — the kind of deep-work I hadn't attempted since I learned to stop attempting it, because the deeper I went into my own gift, the more questions people might ask about where such skill came from, and I had spent twenty years making sure no one asked.

Two: it wanted a truth. Freely given, spoken aloud, into the empty air of that hut — some truth I was withholding even from myself. I sat with that one a long while, turning it over. What was I withholding? That I loved Jude? I'd known that since the checkpoint, admitted it to no one including me, and saying it aloud to a cold hearth felt like paying with a coin I'd need later. That I was afraid? Everyone above the treeline is afraid; it comes with the altitude. Something older, then. Something under the sheets-covered furniture of my childhood. The seal seemed to want a truth with roots, and all my truths were cut flowers.

Three — and this was the one that made me laugh, actually laugh, a short ugly bark that echoed wrong in the tiny stone room — it wanted a memory. One of mine, freely surrendered, to balance the books. The child who built this lock had been raised, apparently, by the same moral arithmetic I practice daily: nothing taken without return, nothing opened without payment. I had taught myself ethics through thievery, and now my ethics were robbing me. There's a symmetry there that would be funny if it weren't happening inside my own skull.

I lay back on the straw and stared at the smoke-blackened rafters and did the bleakest accounting of my life.

If I opened the seal, I would learn where the Door was — because I was suddenly certain that's what it held; the green warmth, the weight of it, the way it pulled toward the east like a compass needle made of longing. The Door out of the erased country. The way through. The only escape either of us had left, assuming Jude still wanted escaping with, assuming Jude was even alive down there in the valley, assuming the hunters hadn't—

No. Stop. That's not the accounting.

The accounting is this: I don't know what else is in there. A sealed memory isn't a single object; it's a room, and rooms have corners, and corners have things you forgot you put in them. Whatever made a child take her own gift and turn it inward — that's not a location. That's a wound with a location attached. I might open the seal and find a map. I might find out why I can't remember my mother's face. I might find out what I did — what the cartographer's daughter said I did, what my hands did while I was too young to know better, or worse, old enough. I might find the reason the country fell wearing my fingerprints.

And the alternative was staying here. Alone, above the treeline, with a fire made of stolen fence posts and a heart full of a betrayal I refused to examine, waiting for the hunters to climb, or the winter to close, or the loneliness to finish what the loneliness started.

Bleak self-confrontation, the situation demanded, and bleak self-confrontation I could supply. I was, after all, the only witness.

"You absolute little coward," I said to the child-me, to the seal, to the fire, distributing blame evenly as was my custom. "You couldn't leave a note?"

The seal, being a seal, did not answer. But I swear — and I know how this sounds, a thief attributing motives to her own mental architecture — I swear it warmed slightly. Not in welcome. In *patience*. Like a shopkeeper who knows you'll be back for the expensive thing.

* * *

Dawn came gray and businesslike, and with it came the wind's morning report: snow, more snow, and — beneath the snow-smell, faint, from the valley below — woodsmoke that was too far away to be mine and too fresh to be old.

Someone was climbing.

I went to the gap in the wall that served as a window and looked down the mountainside until my eyes watered. Nothing visible. Just white, and rock, and the treeline far below stitched across the slope like a scar. But smoke doesn't lie about itself, and neither does the prickle at the base of my skull — the one I get when someone within a mile is thinking hard about finding me. Twenty years of hiding sharpens certain instruments. Mine had never been sharper than it was that morning, standing barefoot on frozen stone, watching for soldiers and listening to a lock tick over inside my chest like a cooling stove.

Hours, then. Maybe a day, if the snow slowed them. The seal wanted time I no longer reliably had, and a truth I hadn't finished identifying, and a memory I hadn't chosen. Three payments, one customer, closing time approaching.

So I sat back down by the ashes, folded my legs, closed my eyes, and began.

I started with the truth, because it was cheapest and I wanted to feel like I'd accomplished something. I cleared my throat like a fraud about to give testimony, addressed the empty hut, and said:

"I loved him. Them. Jude." The pronoun tripped me even now, after everything, and I hated that it did. "Whatever they were sent to do, whatever they took for it — I loved them by the second week and I knew it by the first month and I let them stay anyway because lonely is a kind of stupid." The wind took the words and shredded them against the mountainside, and the seal... considered. Then it adjusted the bill upward, which is exactly what would happen to anyone who dealt with me professionally, and I respected it enormously and resented it more.

Fine. Time, then. I settled into the deep attention, the thief's trance, the place below thought where locks become landscapes, and gave the seal what it asked: hours. The sun crossed the window-gap and crossed it back. My legs went numb. My nose bled a little, which is the tax on depth, and I wiped it on a sleeve already ruined beyond redemption. And slowly, patiently, with the delicacy of someone defusing their own past, I worked.

The seal didn't fight me. That was the worst part. It yielded like a debt coming due — smoothly, inevitably, almost graciously — and with every hour it loosened, more of the shape behind it leaked through. Rain on leaves. A garden. A woman's voice singing in a language I didn't know I knew. Stone walls warm with sun. And underneath it all, threaded through it like wire through clay: a child crying, and adults arguing in urgent whispers, and the word *cartographers*, said the way you say a disease.

I stopped once, when the bleeding got worse. I looked at my red-smeared hands — these hands, these clever criminal hands — and I understood, with the clarity the thin air gives you whether you want it or not, that I was not going to like what I found. That the Door's location and the reason the country fell were probably the same memory, filed together by a child who knew that someday she'd need both, and that paying the toll meant taking delivery of the whole parcel.

Below me, faintly, a whistle. Two notes. Hunters' code — I'd heard it at checkpoints all down the salt road. They'd found the hut's smoke from yesterday, or my tracks, or simply followed the mountain's only sensible route. An hour, maybe less.

I went back in.

The truth, then — the rooted one, the one the seal really wanted, the one I'd been circling like a dog around a wound. I found it where I'd known it was, under the furniture, under the sheets, and it was simple and enormous and I said it aloud to the ashes with blood on my face:

"I think I was there. When it fell. I think I *helped*."

The seal sighed open.

And what poured through the opening — the green country entire, the Door in its hedge, the child in the garden, the whole buried continent of my forgotten guilt — began to arrive all at once, too much and too fast, a flood through a door I had unlocked but no longer controlled, while boots crunched on snow outside and a voice called out, young and cracked and horribly familiar, shouting my name into the wind.

Jude's voice.

Coming up the mountain after me, into the hunters' teeth, carrying God knew what — an apology, a warning, an army — and I sat there drowning in my own returned past, unable to move, unable to answer, as the memory crested and broke over me like the country itself, rising from its grave to collect.
