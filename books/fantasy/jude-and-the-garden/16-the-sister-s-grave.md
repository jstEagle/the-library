## 16. The Sister's Grave

The grave was where they said it would be, and that was the first wrong thing.

I'd known it before I saw it, the way you know a room has been tidied by someone who doesn't live in it. The crown does nothing without paperwork. Even grief gets a clerk. So when the handler's letter told me — *your sister lies at rest in the churchyard at Vell Hollow, third row from the lych-gate, under the yew* — I knew the grave would be neat. I knew the stone would be new. I knew someone had been paid to carve *Elodie Jude* into granite with a steady hand while my sister's actual body went wherever the crown puts things it wants to keep.

I walked up from the valley road in a thin rain that couldn't decide whether to be snow, wearing pilgrim's grey because Quentin had taught me that pilgrims are invisible. Nobody searches a pilgrim. Everybody pities one. I'd eaten at three shrines on the way down and left offerings I couldn't afford, because that's what pilgrims do, and if there is one thing I have learned in nine years of being the crown's creature, it is that the costume matters more than the soul underneath it.

The churchyard sat above the village like a held breath. Yew trees, black and patient. Sheep in the lower field, indifferent to everything. And there, third row from the lych-gate, exactly as promised: a stone bearing my sister's name.

I stood in front of it for a long time.

Here is what you should understand about Elodie. She was born eleven months after me and died — officially — four years ago, of the lung-rot she caught working the dye-houses, which was the debt that put me in the crown's pocket in the first place. They paid for the physic. They paid for the warm room. And when she got worse instead of better, they offered me a choice shaped like a kindness: *serve us, and she lives; fail us, and we will see what the coughing brings.* I signed. I informed. I hunted gift-bearers through market towns for nine years, telling myself every night that it was for her, that the ledger of my sins balanced against her single life, that a man who betrays strangers to keep his sister breathing can still look himself in the eye if he never looks too long.

And then I met Quentin Joubert, who stole memories out of people's heads as gently as picking apples, and who fed half a street on the proceeds, and who looked at me across a counter full of tin charms like I was worth looking at — and somewhere on the Salt and Ash Road I started omitting things. Small omissions at first. A name left off a report. A detail softened. Then bigger ones. Then the night I burned my handler's trust to ash and ran east with a fugitive, and there was no going back, and I told myself: fine. Fine. Let them have the sister. Let them take the last thing. I am done paying.

That was the plan, anyway. That was what I carried up the hill through the rain: surrender. Go back, give myself up, trade whatever was left of my usefulness for their mercy toward Quentin — because the hunters were closing on the pass, because Quentin couldn't outrun what was coming, because someone had to stand between that person and the crown, and I had spent nine years standing on the wrong side of that line and I meant to die correcting it.

I was going to visit her grave first. Say goodbye properly. One honest thing before the dishonest end.

The stone read:

*ELODIE JUDE*
*Beloved sister*
*Taken by the rot, remembered by the crown*
*She is at peace*

*Remembered by the crown.* Who carves that? What mason takes coin for a line like that and doesn't ask questions? It wasn't a memorial. It was a signature. It was the crown signing its work the way it signs everything — politely, permanently, and in a hand that says *this is ours now.*

I crouched and touched the granite. Rain ran off my hood and dripped onto the letters, filling the carved grooves so the name shone.

"Hello, love," I said. My voice came out wrecked. Four years of not saying it, all at once. "Sorry I'm late."

And that's when the yew tree said, "You're talking to a rock, Jude."

I have been ambushed by soldiers, threatened by handlers, held at knifepoint twice and once by a woman with a hot poker. None of it stopped my heart like that voice.

She was sitting against the trunk of the yew with her knees drawn up, wrapped in a good wool cloak — too good, palace-quality, the kind of thing they issue to valuable assets — and she was older. Of course she was older. Four years. But I had built her in my head as a girl dying in a bed, seventeen forever, and here was this woman of twenty-one with sharp cheekbones and our mother's stubborn mouth and rain in her dark hair, looking at me like I was late for supper.

"Elodie," I said.

"You're thinner," she said. "They feed you badly?"

"They—" I stopped. Started again. "You're dead. You're supposed to be dead."

"Well." She shrugged, and it was our mother's shrug, the whole family's shrug, the shrug that means *the world is disappointing but we carry on.* "I'm not."

I crossed the grass and took her face in both hands like I could confirm her by touch, and she let me, and her skin was cold from sitting under a tree in the rain but it was real skin, real stubble of gooseflesh, a real pulse under my fingers. Alive. Alive alive alive.

"They told me the rot took you," I said. "Four years ago."

"The rot was real." She said it flatly, no drama. "For a while. I was very ill, and then the crown's physickers made me better, and when I asked why the crown was spending royal coin on a dyer's daughter, nobody would answer me. So I answered myself." Her eyes — my eyes, our father's grey — went hard. "I'm the leash, Jude. I figured that out about two years ago. Did you?"

"I—"

"Don't lie to me. You're bad at it and I've had time to get clever."

"Yes," I said. "Yes, I knew. I thought— I thought if I kept serving, kept them happy, you'd stay safe. Alive."

"And how's that gone?" She gestured at me — the pilgrim grey, the hollow cheeks, the mud to the knee. "Look at yourself. Look at where they've walked you. How many people, Jude? How many did you hand them to keep your leash slack?"

The rain filled the silence.

"One," I said. "Then two. Then I stopped counting because counting made it real. There was a charm-seller in the capital, a woman who read fortunes, a boy in Corvasser who could light lamps with his breath. A gardener named Quentin Joubert, who I was sent to confirm and deliver, and whom I—" my voice cracked, and Elodie watched it crack with terrible attention — "whom I love. Whom I betrayed. Who knows it now."

Elodie was quiet a moment. Then: "Quentin Joubert. The one all over the proclamations. The hunt notices say *approach with extreme caution; memory-thief.*"

"That's the one."

"And you love them."

"With everything I have left after the crown took its share."

"Good," she said, and something in her face shifted — the sharpness giving way to something rawer. "Because I need you to listen to me now, and I need you to actually hear it, because I have been sitting under this tree for six hours waiting for you to show up, and I only knew you would because I know you, and because I found this."

She pulled something from inside her cloak. A letter, sealed with the crown's sigil in green wax. She didn't hand it over. She held it where I could see it.

"This came to the village reeve last month, with instructions. It says that upon confirmation of my death — which, note, is *staged*, Jude, there is a woman in the capital who looks enough like me dead to fool a grieving brother at a distance and I have met her, she's paid off, she drinks — the crown's asset designated *Gardener* will be permitted to surrender, will be granted leniency for services rendered, and will be reassigned to a new posting. The letter lists the posting. Do you want to know where they planned to send you?"

"Tell me."

"The pass above the Door. As bait. You'd be displayed, alive, cooperative, penitent — and Quentin Joubert, who loves you, would walk into it. They wrote it down. They wrote down that your love for them was an asset they intended to spend." She let the letter drop into my lap. "Read it yourself. Every word. I want you to see the handwriting."

I read it. The rain spotted the page and I didn't care. It was exactly what she said. Worse — it was worse in the details, the little administrative touches, the phrase *emotional leverage (confirmed)*, the note that my sister's continued survival was *contingent on asset performance*. Contingent. Like weather. Like grain prices. Like whether I performed betrayal well enough to earn the privilege of my own blood continuing to breathe.

Nine years. Nine years I had balanced every sin against her heartbeat. Nine years of telling myself the ledger balanced, that the crown and I had a bargain, ugly but binding, two parties honoring terms.

There was no bargain. There had never been a bargain. A bargain requires that the other party consider you a party. To the crown I was a tool with a leash attached, and the other end of the leash was tied to a girl in a warm room, and the whole apparatus — the illness, the cure, the staged death, the grave with its smug carved line — existed to keep me pulling. And now they'd upgraded the leash. Now my love itself was the rope, and they'd written it down in green wax like a line item, and they'd done it all while letting me believe I'd finally, finally chosen something for myself.

Something broke in me. I don't have a better word for it. Something that had been straining for nine years simply tore through, quietly, the way a rope goes — no snap, just sudden absence of tension, and you realize you've been bracing against something that isn't holding you anymore.

I laughed. I couldn't help it. Elodie watched me laugh in the rain over her fake grave and her face went carefully neutral, the way it used to when Father drank.

"Jude," she said. "What are you going to do?"

"What they least expect," I said. "Which, apparently, is anything at all."

"Listen to me." She gripped my wrist, hard, our mother's grip. "They don't know I know. They think I'm a piece on their board — the hostage, kept comfortable, moved when needed. They moved me here three weeks ago for the play. The village thinks I'm the dead girl's cousin, come to tend the grave. Which means I'm the only person in this whole kingdom who is positioned exactly where their leash attaches, and nobody is watching me, because dead girls make poor prisoners." Her grin was sudden and fierce and entirely our mother's. "So. Brother. Tell me your plan, and then let me make it better, because I have had four years to study these people and I promise you I hate them more competently than you do."

And I sat down in the wet grass beside my sister's grave — beside my living sister, in front of her lying stone — and I told her everything. The road, the checkpoints, Quentin's hands in the soil, the pass, the hunters, the Door we were walking toward and the price it demanded. She listened the way she used to listen to my childhood lies: completely, catching every flaw, saying nothing until the end.

When I finished, she was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "The handler. The one running you — Vasse, the narrow one with the rings?"

"Yes."

"She'll come to the Door herself. She won't trust the play to underlings, not for a prize like Quentin. And she'll bring me — or threaten me — because I'm her final hold on you, and she doesn't yet know the leash is cut." Elodie stood, pulling the good cloak tight, and looked down at me with rain streaming off her chin and four years of stolen patience in her eyes. "So here's what we're going to do, brother. We're going to let her bring me right to where we need her."

* * *

We walked down into Vell Hollow as the light failed, two mourners and a lie between them. In the village inn, over stew neither of us tasted, we built it piece by piece: what I would say, what she would signal, which of the handler's habits she'd catalogued from two years of being furniture in the same rooms. She knew Vasse's ring-hand was false. She knew the mirror-scry schedule. She knew which guards drank and which prayed.

"You could still just run," I said. "South, over the coast road. I could get you coin."

"And leave you to walk into that pass alone?" She shook her head. "I spent four years believing my brother sold his soul for me. Turns out he sold it for a stranger who grows things, and I find I respect the purchase. Besides—" she nudged the letter across the table, the green seal glinting — "they wrote *contingent on asset performance*. I'd like to discuss the terms."

It was near midnight when I left her, at the edge of the village, where the east road began climbing toward the mountains and the pass and the ruined country beyond. She hugged me hard, quick, the way we'd hugged as children — like a blow struck jointly against the world.

"Tell Quentin," she said into my ear, "that the crown keeps its hostages alive and its promises not at all. And tell them—" she pulled back, and her eyes were wet and furious and certain — "tell them my brother is done being spent."

I climbed. The rain turned to snow somewhere above the tree line, and I let it fall on me without a hood, because I wanted to feel it, all of it, everything real. Behind me lay a grave with my sister's name on it and no sister in it. Ahead lay a pass full of wolves in livery, and a handler who thought she held every rope, and a door that ate truths.

And somewhere past all of it, a person who had every reason to let me freeze.

I walked faster.

By dawn I could see the ridge where we'd parted — and I could see, drawn up along it in the grey light, a line of riders in crown colours, and among them, small and precise, a figure with rings on her fingers, and beside her, held by the arm, a girl in a good wool cloak who was watching me climb and smiling like a knife.

Elodie had lied to me about one thing. She hadn't waited to be brought to the Door.

She'd gone ahead and gotten herself captured first.
