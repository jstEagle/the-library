## 17. Reunion by Force

**Quentin**

They took me at the ford.

I want to say I fought. I did, a little — I threw a handful of river water at the first hunter's face and called it magic, which was more insult than defense, and he laughed so hard the others had to hold him up while they bound my wrists. That's the trouble with a gift that runs on memory: when you've spent most of yours buying passage through mountain passes and forgetting where you slept last week, there isn't much left to fight with. I had maybe enough charm left in me to make a man feel briefly fond of his mother. Against six hunters in gray livery with iron-cored rope, that's not an arsenal. That's a greeting card.

The tall one — sergeant's braid, a face like a shut door — crouched in front of me after they'd cinched my arms behind my back and looked me over with the flat professional interest of a man appraising livestock.

"Quentin Joubert," he said.

"Depends who's asking," I said, because my mouth has always moved faster than my survival instinct.

"Vessic. Crown Hunt, third company." He stood. "You're worth a lot of money dead and slightly more alive. Try to be worth more."

"I'll do my best," I said. "I've always been an overachiever."

They put me in a cart. Of course they put me in a cart. Twenty years of careful living, of small charms and smaller vanities, and it ends in a cart on the Salt and Ash Road, jouncing toward the border town where lists get read aloud and neighbors get hanged from the market arch. I'd seen what they did to the ones they caught. I had helped bury one of them, or rather I had watched Jude help, and pretended my hands were too clean for digging.

Jude.

I walked away from them into the snow eleven days ago. I had rehearsed walking away for years, in the way you rehearse a fire drill — knowing you'll never need it, hoping you won't. And then the confession came, all of it, every year of coerced informing, every name delivered, mine at the end of the list like the final entry in a ledger, and I walked away because if I had stayed I would have said something unforgivable, and I have made a life out of never saying the unforgivable thing. You can't steal memories for two decades without learning that some words take pieces of you when you say them, and those are the only thefts you can't undo.

So I walked. And now here I was in a cart, wrists raw, heading to the same border town, and the last thing I'd said to the person whose face I kept seeing in the snow was nothing at all.

That's the thing about walking away. It feels like dignity at the time. Later it just feels like leaving in the middle of a sentence.

* * *

**Jude**

I found the third company at dusk, four days behind their cart, riding a horse I'd bought with the crown's own coin — the last of my handler's stipend, and I have never spent stolen money so gladly.

My plan, such as it was, fit on the back of my hand: walk into their camp, tell them who I was, and watch their faces change.

They changed. That part went exactly as planned. A gardener walks in off the Salt and Ash Road, mud to the knees, and says, "Sergeant. Jude Ferrant, crown service, twelve years' asset work, gift-hunt division," and produces the token — a little lead seal shaped like a closed eye that I had carried against my skin for twelve years like a tumor I couldn't cut out — and the faces change. Respect, mostly. A little unease. Everyone in the hunt knows the name Ferrant, or knows the work done under it. Four gift-bearers found, three delivered, one died running. My handler used to toast the numbers at year's end. Three delivered. He liked round numbers.

"Sergeant Vessic," the tall one said, and offered his hand across the fire.

I took it. His grip was dry and hard and he held on a half-second longer than courtesy required, looking at me, and I understood that look. It was the look you give a famous weapon before you decide whether it's pointed at you. Twelve years I had been the crown's instrument, and instruments don't get looked at like men. They get looked at like edges.

"You're the one who found Joubert's trail," Vessic said. "Handler Corvane speaks well of you."

"Corvane pays me to be spoken well of," I said, and some of the soldiers laughed, and something in my chest turned over and died quietly, because the laugh came easy. The lie came easy. Twelve years of practice and the machinery still worked, smooth as water, even now, even turned the other way. I sat down at the enemy's fire, ate the enemy's stew, praised the enemy's bread, and asked, casually, about the prisoner in the cart.

"Alive?" I said. "Thought your company preferred them dead."

"Worth more breathing," Vessic said. "There's a magistrate at Harrow's Ford who likes a public registration. Likes the town to see what happens." He shrugged. "We deliver tomorrow evening."

"And then?"

"And then it's the magistrate's cart, the magistrate's rope. Not ours." He said it lightly, the way men say things when they've arranged the wording specifically so it doesn't weigh anything. "You coming along? Company of a legend might steady the new boys."

"Legend," I said. "Is that what they call it now?"

"That's what they call it in the barracks." He poked the fire. "What do you call it?"

I looked into the flames and thought about a night in a flooded cistern, holding a stranger's hand in the dark, both of us blind, trusting. I thought about a garden I'd never planted, seeds sewn into the lining of a coat I stole from a palace conservatory on my way out, carried for months without knowing why. I thought about someone walking away from me into snow without a word, and how I deserved it, and how I would have crawled after them anyway.

"I call it work," I said.

The fire cracked. Nobody noticed the pause but me.

* * *

**Quentin**

They gave me bread and water through the cart slats and no conversation, which suited me, because I was busy conducting an inventory of everything I had left.

It wasn't much. Here is what twenty years of thieving and one month of spending comes down to: I could remember my mother's hands but not her voice. I could remember the shape of the shop door but not the sound it made. I remembered Jude — I remembered everything about Jude, every argument, every joke, the way they hummed when they worked the soil, tuneless and stubborn, like the earth owed them money — and I hated that. I hated that the one memory I'd never stolen was the one I'd give anything to lose, and equally the one I'd die before surrendering. Both at once. The heart keeps terrible accounts.

Toward midnight the camp went quiet around me, and I lay in the straw of the cart doing the arithmetic of escape. One guard at the wheel, dozing. Six more around the fire, thirty feet off. Iron-core rope on my wrists. No gift to speak of. Conclusion: arithmetic is a cruel science and should be banned.

Then I heard footsteps, unhurried, and a voice at the cart's side, low and familiar as rain.

"Don't shout," it said. "Don't even breathe loud. It's me."

I didn't move. I stared up through the canvas gap at the stars and felt my whole body go strange, like a struck bell.

"They'll see you," I managed. "Vessic will—"

"Vessic invited me. I'm his guest of honor." A pause, and then, drier than the Salt and Ash Road itself: "Turns out the crown's best finder wants to personally oversee the delivery of its biggest catch. Very tidy. Very official. Nobody questions it."

"You're going to deliver me."

"No," Jude said, and there was something in the word that hadn't been there in the snow — no apology, no plea, just a fact laid down like a stone. "I'm going to steal you. Thought it was fitting. You're the professional; correct me if my technique's wrong."

I laughed. It came out of me before I could stop it, a single broken sound in the dark, and the guard at the wheel stirred, and Jude's hand came through the slats and gripped my shoulder — not gently, not tenderly, just firmly, an anchor thrown in weather — until the guard settled.

"Why?" I whispered. It wasn't the question I meant. The question I meant was *why did you lie for twelve years*, and *why didn't you tell me*, and *how am I supposed to stand next to you*, but the mouth picks its battles.

"Because you walked away before I finished," Jude said. "And I don't finish sentences either, apparently, so we're going to have to finish this one together or not at all." The hand tightened. "Tomorrow evening, at the ford below Harrow's Ford, the escort crosses the shallows. Carts go slow in water. There's a gamekeeper's hut on the north bank — I scouted it this morning. Be ready to run."

"Be ready—" I said. "I'm tied to a cart."

"Working on that." And then the hand withdrew, and the footsteps moved away, unhurried, a legend returning to the fire, and I lay back in the straw with my heart going like a hammer and thought: *this is either rescue or the longest con the crown ever ran, and I genuinely cannot tell which.*

Trust, it turns out, is just suspicion with better posture. I chose the posture.

* * *

**Jude**

Here is what nobody tells you about betraying a betrayal: it doesn't feel like redemption. It feels like surgery without anesthesia. Every hour I sat at that fire, being the legend, being the edge, nodding at the new boys and letting Vessic talk strategy at me, I could feel myself splitting cleanly down the middle — the crown's Ferrant on one side, the person Quentin walked away from on the other — and the blade between the halves didn't hurt yet. It would hurt later. Everything does. But that night it just sang.

At dusk the next day we reached the ford. The river was high with snowmelt, brown and muscular, and the cart went in slow, wheels complaining, exactly as promised. Quentin sat up in the straw like a corpse remembering its appointment. Our eyes met through the slats for half a second. Half a second is a long time when you're counting.

I gave the signal. It was stupidly simple: I dropped my waterskin in the shallows, bent for it, and came up shouting that the rope on the prisoner's axle had frayed — which it had, because I'd frayed it at dawn with a knife and forty minutes of patience. Vessic waved two men forward to check it. The cart stopped mid-river. The escort bunched up on the bank, watching water instead of trees.

The hut's shadow side was thirty feet of alder thicket. Quentin went over the cart rail like a cat off a fence — I'd loosened the wrist-rope with a file during the night watch, another forty minutes of patience — and hit the water, and I hit the water beside them, and then we were both in the current and the shouting started behind us.

They were good, I'll grant the hunt that. Vessic didn't waste time on the cart. Within a hundred yards he'd read the water right, cut upstream, and come out of the alders ahead of us with two of his faster men, and the gamekeeper's hut turned out to contain exactly the shelter and none of the safety I'd promised, because safety was never on the menu. What followed was short, ugly, and nothing like the stories.

I'm not going to dress it up. I've killed before — the hunt trains its finders for it, though it prefers the paperwork of capture — but I'd never killed for anyone, only for the crown, and the difference is the difference between a tool cutting and a hand cutting. One of Vessic's men got a hand on Quentin's collar at the hut door and I broke his arm over my knee and put my knife where it needed to go, and he made a sound I will hear for the rest of my life, and I stepped over him into the hut and bolted the door with a chair.

Vessic hammered the boards twice, testing. Then, through a gap, calm as a clerk: "Ferrant. Twelve years. For *this*?"

"For this," I said.

"You'll hang," he said. "Both of you. There's no country far enough."

"There's one," I said, and didn't explain, because explaining was the one thing I refused to do through a wall. Some truths you pay in person or not at all.

He didn't break the door. That frightened me worse than if he had — a patient hunter is a hunter with reinforcements coming. We went out the back window into the last light, Quentin limping, me bleeding from a cut along the forearm I didn't remember taking, and we ran north through the game trails until dark swallowed the road behind us.

* * *

**Quentin**

We stopped when we couldn't hear pursuit anymore — a hollow under a rock shelf, dry needles, a stream talking to itself nearby. I collapsed against the stone and shook, which I'm told is normal, and Jude built a fire small enough to hide in a cupped hand and sat down across from me, and for a long time neither of us said anything.

The silence had a lot of bodies in it. Eleven days of snow. Twelve years of lies. One drowned soldier in a gamekeeper's hut whose name I would never learn. I looked at Jude across the fire — at the blood drying on their forearm, at the mud to the knees, at the face I knew better than my own mother's and trusted less than a stranger's, and loved anyway, God help me, loved with the specific helpless fury of a person watching someone else carry their own heart around carelessly.

"You killed a man for me," I finally said.

"Yes."

"Not for the crown. Not for the numbers. For me."

"Yes." Jude fed a twig to the fire and watched it catch. "I know what it looks like. I know what I am. You walked away because you finally saw the ledger, all of it, and you were right to. Nothing tonight erases a line of it. I'm not asking it to." They looked up. Their eyes were steady and exhausted and absolutely unarmored, which on Jude was more frightening than any weapon. "I'm telling you what I did. What happens next is yours."

I waited for the apology. It didn't come. And slowly, watching them sit there — not begging, not explaining, just present, the way they'd been present in the cistern, the way they'd been present through every checkpoint and cold camp and shared meal since the day they walked into my shop with an impossible errand — I understood that the missing apology was itself the honest thing. An apology would have been a transaction. *Forgive me and I'm lighter.* Jude wasn't trying to get lighter. They were just standing in the wreckage of themselves and letting me see all of it, and asking nothing, which is the hardest thing a liar can do.

I'm not forgiven-out, for the record. I'm not sure forgiveness is a door you walk through once; I think it's a garden you keep weeding. But I scooted around the fire, and I sat down next to them, shoulder against shoulder, and I took their bleeding arm and began cleaning the cut with stream water and a strip of my own shirt, and I said:

"Hold still. If you die of infection after all that, I'll be furious, and I don't have enough memory left to sustain a proper grudge."

Jude exhaled — a long, shaking breath, twelve years leaving the body all at once — and leaned into me, just slightly, just enough to feel.

Neither of us said sorry. Neither of us needed to, yet. The fire burned down. Somewhere behind us, south, a country full of lists and ropes went on with its evening, and somewhere ahead, north, past ridges I half-remembered dreaming, a door was waiting that demanded every truth I owned.

But first — sleep. Actual sleep, for the first time in weeks, with someone keeping watch who had chosen it freely.

I was nearly under when Jude spoke, quiet, into the dark.

"There's something I have to show you. Where we're going. About the sister." A pause. "She's alive, Quentin. And she's why I almost handed you over yesterday."

I opened my eyes.

"*Almost?*"

"Tomorrow," Jude said. "When I can say it without my hands shaking. Tomorrow."

The fire ticked. North, somewhere past the black ridgeline, a hostage and a door and a sealed memory all waited in a row like stones to cross a river — and I lay awake the rest of the night, learning the weight of each one.
