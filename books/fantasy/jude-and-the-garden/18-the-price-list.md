## 18. The Price List

The sealed memory did not open all at once. Nothing in my life has ever been that merciful.

It opened the way a shop door opens when you've forgotten to lock it — a crack, a draft, and then the slow, creaking realization that something has come in whether you wanted it or not.

I had been sitting by the fire for perhaps an hour, turning the sealed thing over in my mind the way you'd turn a stone with something coiled beneath it. Above the treeline, alone, with Jude's absence sitting beside me like a second, colder person. And then the lock — *my* lock, set by hands I couldn't remember owning — gave way. Not entirely. A drawer's worth. Enough.

What spilled out was not a place or a face. It was arithmetic.

I saw the Door.

Not the thorn archway itself — that came later, in fragments I'll describe as they came — but its terms. Its *price list*. Because that is what it was, written across the inside of my skull in a hand like frost forming on glass: an inventory, itemized and patient, of everything owed before the arch would unseal.

Every theft. Returned.

And one truth. Freely given.

I laughed. I want that on the record, wherever records go after we die: above the treeline, in the snow, with no one to hear me, I sat down and laughed until it turned into the other thing, because of course. Of *course* it was a price list. I have been a merchant for twenty years. I know a bill of sale when I see one. The universe, it turns out, keeps ledgers too, and somewhere along the line I had run up a tab.

Let me be precise about what I saw, because precision is the last dignity available to a person in my position.

* * *

The Door demands return. Not destruction — return. There is a difference, and the difference is cruelty dressed as mercy. If the memories could simply be burned, I could burn them. I am practiced at burning; I fed twenty years of ledgers to a stove not three weeks ago and felt only relief. But the Door does not accept ash. It accepts restitution. Each memory must travel back along the road it came from and settle into the head it was taken from, whole, and I must feel it leave — feel it the way my customers never felt it arrive, because I was careful, always careful, taking only what wouldn't be missed.

Now I know what I missed.

I sat by the fire and began, without meaning to, to make the list. It's what I do. When the world becomes unbearable I make lists; there is a comfort in columns, in the fiction that a life can be itemized. So:

The baker's daughter on Copperlane, who sold me her first kiss for two coppers' worth of charm against her father's temper. I took the memory of the kiss itself — she asked me to, said she didn't want it, said he'd made her ashamed of it. I told myself I was doing charity. I kept it anyway. Twenty-two years old, that memory, and still warm.

The ferryman who'd watched his brother drown and paid me to take the watching. I took the watching. I left him the drowning, which I reasoned at the time was the worse half, and I have repeated that reasoning to myself every year since like a prayer that stopped working around the fifth recitation.

Old Maren, who ran the dyeshop and could not stop seeing her husband's face in strangers'. I took the seeing. She smiled more afterward. Everyone said so. I collected her smile like interest on the principal.

There were hundreds. That is the part I had never let myself count — the actual number. In the trade we call them small charms, small extractions, small kindnesses, and the word *small* does the work of a hundred hired liars. Small. As if a memory were a coin and not a room in a house where a person lives.

But the Door's ledger did not say *small*. The Door's ledger said, in its frost-script patience: **ALL**.

* * *

Here is what else opened in the drawer, and here is where the laughing stopped.

I saw the terms clearly enough to understand their shape, if not their full text — the seal is still partly shut, and some lines remain blurred, like writing under water. But three things came through clean.

First: every theft, returned. All of them. Not the ones I choose. Not the ones whose owners are convenient or living or findable. All. Which means journeys. Which means years, possibly, tracking down the baker's daughter and the ferryman and old Maren and everyone between, across a country currently executing people like me, carrying each memory home like a midwife carrying someone else's child.

Second: the memories must be returned *willingly received*, or they do not count. I cannot slip them back the way I took them. The ferryman must open his hands and ask. Old Maren must want her husband's face again, grief and all. This is the Door's real cruelty, I think — not the giving up, but the asking. Every person I robbed will get to look at me and decide whether to forgive me by taking back what's theirs. Some will refuse. Some refused customers will walk away lighter than they came, and I will walk away still owing, and the Door will not care about my arithmetic.

Third: one truth, freely given. Mine, presumably — though the script blurred there, and I confess I let it stay blurred. A truth freely given. Not coerced, not extracted, not traded. Given. I have spent twenty years making sure I never gave anything freely. Giving freely is how you get caught; giving freely is how you get killed. My whole survival has been a discipline of withholding. And now a door — the only door, out of the only country, toward the only future — requires the one currency I have hoarded past all reason.

I thought about what my truth might even be. That was the humiliating part. A truth implies a self to be true about, and my self, it develops, is largely rented space. I know the market. I know which customers flinch. I know that I love Jude — that one arrived without paperwork, no receipt, fully formed, and I would give it up last of everything. But the rest? Why I came to this city. Why I chose charm-selling. What I was running from, all those years ago, when I taught myself to steal so quietly.

The seal knows. The seal has known the whole time. Somewhere behind that locked drawer is the answer to why a child ends up learning memory-theft in a city that hangs children for less, and the Door wants it, and I can't pay what I can't find.

Unless — and I sat with this thought for a long time, feeding sticks to the fire — unless finding it is part of the payment too. Unless the ledger means for me to open the rest of the seal myself, walk into whatever my younger self decided I couldn't survive knowing, and carry it out with both hands.

My younger self. I keep saying that phrase now, and each time it lands stranger. There was a version of me with enough sense to lock a memory and enough fear to hide the key inside the same house. That's not caution. That's a person who expected to be searched.

* * *

Jude came back at grey dusk, snow in their hair, smelling of woodsmoke and cold stone. They had been gone most of the day — walking, they said. Thinking. I did not ask what about. We have developed a whole grammar of questions we don't ask, and lately the grammar has been straining under the weight of everything unsaid.

"You're pale," Jude said, kneeling at the fire. "Paler."

"I remembered something."

Their hands went still over the kindling. Of course they did. Jude notices everything; it's their gift, the one they never had to hide. "Good something or bad something?"

"It's a bill," I said. "An enormous bill. Apparently the Door doesn't take coin."

They looked at me then, really looked, with that expression they get — the gardener's expression, assessing a plant for blight — and I almost told them all of it right there. The price list. The returned memories. The truth freely given. But some instinct older than my missing years held my tongue, because there was something in Jude's face that night, some fault-line of guilt running under the concern, and I thought: not yet. Whatever you're carrying, Jude, you'll put it down when you're ready, and I will pretend to be surprised.

I'm telling myself that was mercy. It may have been cowardice wearing mercy's coat. The two are hard to tell apart at altitude.

"Tell me the shape of it," Jude said instead, which was exactly the wrong thing to say, because it meant they already suspected there was a shape, which meant they knew more about Doors than a palace gardener should, and I filed that away in the drawer where I keep my suspicions of Jude, a drawer that has grown crowded these past weeks.

So I gave them the shape. Memories returned, all of them, willingly received. One truth given free. I watched their face while I said it, and I'll record what I saw: not confusion. Recognition, quickly buried, like a candle snuffed before it lights the room.

"That's steep," Jude said finally.

"The Door drives a hard bargain."

"And the memories — yours, the ones you took — you'd feel them go?"

"Feel them leave," I said. "That seems to be the point. Symmetry. The Door believes in symmetry."

Jude poked the fire harder than the fire deserved. Sparks went up and died. "Twenty years of taking," they said, mostly to the flames, "and one winter of giving back. Doesn't balance."

"No," I agreed. "It doesn't. But I suspect the Door isn't interested in balance. It's interested in emptiness." I heard the words come out of my mouth and recognized them as true in the way that certain sentences are simply *found*, whole, like coins in a gutter — true because they came from the sealed place, leaking through the crack. "It wants me emptied out. Everything taken, everything returned, everything confessed. Whoever walks through that arch won't be me. It'll be whoever's left after the accounting."

Jude was quiet for a long moment. Then, very carefully, in the voice people use near wounded animals: "And you'd still walk through?"

I considered lying. It's my profession; I'm very good at it; the lie was right there, pre-wrapped. *Of course, Jude, anything for our future.* Instead I heard myself say, "I don't know. Ask me again when I've counted what's actually mine."

Because that was the other thing the drawer revealed, the thing I haven't said aloud yet, the thing I'm setting down here where no one but this page can hear it: as I sat listing my thefts — the kiss, the drowning, the dyeshop, the hundreds — I realized I could not remember which memories were originally mine. Not the big ones. Not any of them. Twenty years of handling other people's pasts, storing them, selling around them, living among them, and the borders have eroded. Is my mother's face mine, or is it Maren's husband's? Is my fear of deep water the ferryman's, loaned out and never reclaimed? I am a shop where everything looks familiar and nothing has a price tag of its own.

The Door wants it all back. And underneath the terror of that, there is — I'll admit it here, in ink, where it can't unsay itself — there is a terrible relief trying to get out. Because if the Door takes everything, then the emptiness at the center of me finally gets acknowledged. Officially. With witnesses. All these years I have felt like a building with the furniture arranged just so and no foundation under it, and I thought that was the magic, the cost of the gift. It might be simpler than that. It might be that I was emptied long ago, before the market, before the city, before Jude — and everything since has been me furnishing rooms in a house with no ground floor.

The Door isn't demanding payment.

It's demanding honesty about the fact that I have nothing to pay with. Except the truth. One truth, freely given — and perhaps the only truth I truly own is the smallest and worst one: *I don't know who I am. I never did.*

* * *

Night came down properly. Jude slept, or performed sleeping, curled near the fire with their back to me, and I lay awake doing what I do best, which is keeping books.

I started the actual list in my head, formally this time, the way I'd start an inventory before closing the shop for good. Name, memory, location if known, likelihood of the owner still living, likelihood of the owner wanting it back. It was grim mathematics. The ferryman drowned himself two winters ago — I heard it in the market, gossip travels — so his watching dies with him, and either the Door forgives a debt to the dead or the debt transfers somewhere I don't want to imagine. The baker's daughter married and moved south, someone said. Old Maren's dyeshop stands empty; she followed her husband's face into whatever comes after, gentle and quiet, last spring.

Dead owners. Living debts. And a country full of checkpoints between me and the ones still breathing.

Somewhere in the middle of the counting I understood that this was the penance, or the beginning of it — not the giving back, which might even be a kindness to myself, shedding weight — but the *finding*. Walking back through twenty years of small transactions, knocking on doors I'd slipped away from in the dark, saying the sentence no charm-seller has ever said: *I took something from you. Here it is. I'm sorry.* Some of them will curse me. Some will weep. One or two, maybe, will take the memory back and look at me with something that isn't forgiveness but isn't far from it, and that will have to be enough, because the Door does not negotiate and neither, anymore, do I.

And then the truth. One truth, freely given, to a door made of thorns in a garden in a dead country, while hunters close in from the ridge and the air eats at what remains of me. I kept circling back to the blurred line in the frost-script, the clause I couldn't read. *One truth.* Whose? Given to whom? The Door's? Each other's?

I looked at Jude's sleeping back and thought: *each other's* would explain a great deal about why we are both here, wouldn't it. Two people carrying truths they haven't spoken, aimed at a door that accepts nothing else.

But that's a suspicion for another chapter of my misfortunes. That night I had arithmetic enough.

Toward dawn the seal shifted again — not opening, settling, the way ice settles on a roof before it gives. And through the narrowing crack I caught one more glimpse of the ledger, one more line of frost-script, and this one was not blurred at all. It was written large, the way merchants write the total.

It said: **THE LAST PAYMENT IS THE ONE YOU LOCKED AWAY YOURSELF.**

My own handwriting. I knew it instantly, the way you know your own gait in a crowd. My younger self had added a note to the invoice, left it for me like a message under a shop door, and the note said — in the same careful, frightened, determined hand I would later see scratched into a wall in the ruined capital —

*You'll want to open this when the time comes. Don't. Make the Door open it. Make it look at what it's asking for.*

I lay in the snow above the treeline and understood that the child I used to be had anticipated all of this: the flight, the Door, the price list, the moment when I'd stand before that thorned archway and consider paying with the one memory that explains everything. And the child had left instructions. *Don't open it yourself. Make the Door do it.*

Which means the child knew what was inside.

Which means the child chose to survive rather than remember.

Downslope, faint on the wind, came something that ended my accounting for the night: the sound of a horn. One note, then silence, then one note again — a hunting signal, patient as bookkeeping, coming from the ridge where the pass met the sky.

Jude's eyes opened in the dark. They had heard it too.

"We need to move," they said.

And I thought, gathering the frozen remnants of my life into my arms: yes. Down the mountain, toward a dead country, toward a thorned door with an unpaid bill — carrying every memory I ever stole, one truth I haven't found, and a sealed instruction in my own childish hand that says, essentially, *when the time comes, make them look at what you did.*

The hunt was behind us. The debt was ahead.

I have never once in my life paid retail. It appears my luck has finally run to its natural conclusion.
