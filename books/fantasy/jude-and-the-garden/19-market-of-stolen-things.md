## 19. Market of Stolen Things

The ledger I did not burn was the one I'd sewn into my coat lining, page by page, over twenty years — the habit of a woman who trusts no fire but her own. It lists every memory I ever took: name, price, date, and the shape of what was taken, described in my own shorthand. A wedding, gold light on a river. A mother's voice singing in a kitchen that smelled of onions. The taste of a peach eaten at seventeen by someone who would remember it forever after as the best thing they ever ate.

I had told myself, for two decades, that I dealt only in surplus. That people sold me their dull hours gladly, that a charm bought with a Tuesday afternoon harmed no one. Standing in the ruined chapel outside Vell with the book open on my knees, I read it differently. Two hundred and eleven entries. Two hundred and eleven small thefts I had committed against people who smiled at me while I did it.

Jude watched me from the doorway, arms crossed against the cold.

"You don't have to do this," they said.

"The Door says otherwise."

"The Door says *some* of it. You're going past some."

I closed the ledger. "Then I'll be thorough."

* * *

Vell was three days east of the pass where Jude had killed a man for me, and we entered it separately, as we had learned to travel: me first with a cart of honest tinware, Jude trailing an hour behind as my hired hand, because two pilgrims who keep glancing at each other draw more eyes than one sinner alone.

The town smelled of tallow and wet wool. Soldiers at the gate were checking hands — the Gift shows, they say, in a tremor of the fingers, though anyone who's actually Gifted knows that's nonsense. I kept my hands busy with a bolt of cloth and let them wave me through.

My first customer was easy to find, because I had chosen her carefully all those years ago and careful choices stay found. Maret Ossel, widow, kept a dyer's shop on Tanner's Row, and the entry beside her name said: *one hour, her husband alive, laughing in the rain.* She'd sold it to buy a luck-charm for her son's apprenticeship. I remembered taking it. I remembered how she'd hesitated, then laughed and said the past was dead weight anyway.

She was older now. Grey had gotten into her hair like dye gone wrong, and her hands moved through the dipping-work without pleasure.

"Mistress Ossel," I said. "You bought a charm from a seller in Corvane market. Nine years ago. Blue glass, for your boy."

She looked up, wary. Sellers come back to customers rarely, and never for good reasons. "What of it?"

"I've come to give you something back."

I won't describe her face in detail. Some things you should have to imagine. But when I took her weathered hand and opened the little door inside myself where her hour had lived, folded up and kept warm all this time, she made a sound I will hear until I die — half gasp, half sob, the sound of a woman catching a child she thought had drowned.

Her husband laughing in the rain. She lived it standing there among her dye-vats, eyes wide and streaming, and afterward she gripped my wrists and said, "Who *are* you?"

"A thief," I said, "returning goods," and left before she could decide whether to thank me or summon the watch.

* * *

It went like that, mostly, for eleven days. Eleven days of walking up to strangers whose names I knew better than they knew mine, and saying the terrible sentence: *I took something from you once, and here it is.*

A cooper named Bram received back his daughter's first steps — he'd sold the memory to pay a gambling debt, and had spent years unable to picture the moment his girl learned to walk, knowing only that everyone said he'd been there. He sat down hard on his own doorstep when it came home to him.

An old ferryman got back the day he'd swum out to save a drowning horse and failed, and cried not from grief but from relief, telling me he'd always felt there was a hole where that day should be, a tooth missing from his life's jaw, and now at last he could worry it properly.

That is the part no one warns you about, being what I am: returning a memory means living it twice. Once when I took it, seeing it through the seller's eyes, tasting their grief or joy secondhand. And again now, watching it land, watching their face remake itself around the returned piece. Every customer, twice-lived. I began to feel like a ferryman myself, poling back and forth across the same narrow water, carrying other people's lives.

And under it, quieter, the other cost. Each return thinned my gift. I felt it the way you feel money leaving a purse — a lightness that isn't freedom. The sense I'd had since childhood, that constant low awareness of the memories around me like heat off cobblestones, dimmed a little with every hand I held. By the fourth day I noticed I had to concentrate to find the little doors anymore. By the eighth, Maret Ossel's mind was simply shut to me, opaque as a brick.

Good, I told myself. Good. Let me go deaf to it. Let me end as ordinary as I pretend to be.

But there's a difference between setting down a burden and having it cut off your arms, and I hadn't decided yet which this was.

* * *

Not everyone wanted their goods back.

Toma Reeve, a fat and cheerful innkeeper in Vell's lower town, listened to my whole speech with his head cocked like a bird, then laughed in my face.

"You're the charm-seller. The one from Corvane. Ha! I wondered if you'd hang." He wiped his eyes. "What did I sell you, then?"

"An afternoon. Your father teaching you cards, the winter before he died."

His smile stayed exactly where it was, but something behind it went still. "Don't want it."

"Toma—"

"He beat me at cards every game and called me slow in front of the tavern. Best thing I ever did was sell that afternoon. Only smart trade of my life. You want to give it back?" He leaned across the bar. "Give it to someone who's short of memories. I'm full."

I stood there holding his afternoon like a parcel no one would sign for. He waved me off and poured a drink for another man, and I walked out into the street with Toma Reeve's winter in my chest and nowhere to put it down.

Jude found me later sitting on a bridge, staring at the water.

"One refused?"

"One refused. Said his memories are his to sell *and* his to refuse."

"What will you do with it?"

"I don't know. Carry it, maybe. Plant it somewhere." I almost laughed. "Listen to me. Planting memories. Twenty years selling charms and now I talk like a gardener."

"That's my influence," Jude said, and bumped their shoulder against mine, and for one moment on that bridge I let myself believe we might get away with everything.

* * *

The curse came on the twelfth day.

I had saved him for last in Vell because his entry frightened me. *Danel Foss, mason. Sold: the memory of his wife's face. Price: medicine for his daughter.* Some sales I could justify even now — a fever cured outweighs an afternoon of anything. But a man's wife's *face*. I had taken it quickly, guiltily, telling myself he'd wanted to forget the grief of her dying. He had not wanted to forget the grief. He had wanted to keep the grief and lose nothing else, and I had been too eager for the coin to ask properly.

I found him laying stone on a chapel wall above the town, working alone in the wind.

"Danel Foss."

He turned. He was gaunt now, and his daughter — I saw her later; she lived, she thrived, she had her mother's everything — his daughter was why he'd done it, so I had held onto that like a rope. But his face when he heard his own name from a stranger's mouth was the face of a man hearing the executioner read the list.

"You," he said. "I knew it. All these years I knew there was something wrong with you. Something *taken* about you." He dropped his trowel. "What did you steal from me? What do you have of mine?"

"Your wife's face. Her face, Danel. I kept it safe. I can give it—"

He hit me.

I went down on the fresh mortar and he was on me, fists and grief, screaming that I had left him ten years married to a ghost he couldn't even mourn properly, ten years of reaching for a face and finding smooth blank stone, and his daughter asking what Mama looked like and him *lying*, inventing answers, because the truth was he didn't know, some thief had scooped it out of him—

Jude pulled him off me. It took both of them and most of their strength.

I lay in the wet mortar with my lip split and gave him back her face anyway, pressed it into him while he struggled, because it was his and I had no right to hold it another hour. He went rigid. Then he stopped struggling. Then he knelt there in the wind above the town with his wife's face restored behind his eyes, and he wept the way men weep when they've been given back a debt instead of forgiven it.

When he finally spoke, his voice was wrecked and quiet.

"Keep away from my family. Keep away from every family. If I see you again — if I hear your name near my daughter — I will curse you with every word my mother taught me, and she was a woman who could sour milk at forty paces." He stood. He looked at me with his wife's face shining somewhere behind his eyes, and it made his hatred worse, not better, because now he had something to hate me *for*, whole and vivid. "May you forget yourself, thief. May every memory you ever touched rot in your keeping. May you die full of other people's lives and none of your own."

He picked up his trowel and went back to his wall.

We left Vell that evening. My face swelled; Jude held a cold cloth to my lip and didn't say *you deserved it*, which was its own kind of mercy, and didn't say *you didn't*, which would have been a lie.

"Forty-one returned," I told them on the road. "One refused. One cursed. One hundred sixty-nine to go, and the gift getting thinner every day, and hunters behind us, and—" I stopped. Laughed, a bit wildly. "Do you know what the strangest part is? I feel *lighter*. Like I've been carrying two hundred stones and calling them coins."

Jude walked a few steps in silence. Then: "There's something I need to tell you. About the crown. About what I've been carrying too."

They said it in the tone of a person stepping off a ledge — and I heard it, that tone, I'd heard it in every customer who ever hesitated before selling — and the road went very quiet, and the mountains ahead of us had never looked so far away.
