## 20. Jude's Free Truth

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that a liar learns young, and I had been doing it so long I could do it in my sleep, which is precisely where I did most of my best lying — to myself, in dreams, where I was still an honest man with a sick sister and a shovel and no debts worth naming.

The arithmetic went like this: every secret has a price, and every price has a payer, and if you keep careful books you can always know who owes whom. The crown held my ledger for nine years. My sister's life on one side of the column. Every gift-bearer I'd ferreted out on the other. I told myself for years that the columns balanced, that I was only paying what was owed, that a debt honestly incurred is not the same as a sin freely chosen.

Then I burned the checkpoints behind us at the pass, and the columns collapsed into each other, and all I had left was a single line item that would not reconcile: Quentin Joubert, asleep against a stone wall with their coat buttoned wrong, trusting me.

Trusting me. There's a word that should come with a surgeon's warning label.

* * *

The rebels found us before we found them, which is how it works when the people you're looking for have been looking longer. We were two days out from the mountain archive, following a cartographer's riddle through country where the roads had stopped pretending to be roads, when a boy of perhaps fifteen stepped out from behind a boulder with a crossbow held in the confident way of someone who had never actually fired it at anything that could fire back.

"State your business," he said, and then his eyes flicked to Quentin and widened, because even half-starved and smelling of woodsmoke and fear, Quentin has a face that people remember. A charm-seller's face. The face on every wanted bill between here and the coast, though the bills got the mouth wrong and made them look crueler than they are.

"Pilgrims," I said. "Bound east."

"Nobody pilgrimages east." He shifted his grip. "East is where things go to stop existing."

"Then we're going somewhere very quiet," Quentin said mildly, "which suits us fine," and something in their tone made the boy laugh despite himself, and then a voice came out of the trees — a woman's voice, dry as old paper — saying, "Vasik, if you shoot the charm-seller I will be very annoyed, because I've been trying to get an audience with them for three weeks."

She came out of the green like she'd been part of it since birth: gray-haired, broad-shouldered, wearing a soldier's coat with the insignia cut off but the shape of it still readable, the way scars read through skin. She looked at me first, and her look was long and unhurried, the look of a woman reading a confession I hadn't written yet.

"Jude," she said. Not a question.

"Ma'am."

"Palace gardener. Nine years' service. Handler's name Orlane. Sister named Sefa, formerly of the fever ward, currently—" she paused delicately, "—a guest of the crown." She let that sit. "You're wondering how I know."

"I'm wondering whether to run," I said.

"Don't," she said. "I know it because we've been reading Orlane's couriers for two years, and because your name appears in his ledger under an entry I've been waiting my whole career to see written down." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile. "*Asset compromised. Subject fled. Asset's value now negative.*"

Behind me, Quentin had gone very still in the way they go still — the market-stillness, the stillness of a person counting exits. But they didn't run. They stood beside me, shoulder almost touching mine, and listened to a stranger recite my crimes.

"You're the informant," Quentin said quietly. It wasn't a question either. They'd known since the pass — since the flinches at the checkpoints, since the practiced way I bent my head under soldiers' gazes. Since the night I killed a man in livery to save them and crossed the one border that doesn't appear on maps. "Since the beginning."

"Since before the beginning," the woman said helpfully, and I wanted very much for the ground to open up, except that this country had opinions about things disappearing into the ground, and I wasn't sure I'd survive its review process.

"Yes," I said. To Quentin. Only to Quentin. The woman could wait. "Yes, and I'll tell you all of it, but not here, and not for free."

That surprised her. Good. I'd spent nine years being surprising on command; it felt better to do it for myself.

"The door needs a truth," I said. "Freely given. Your archivist friend explained it — or tried to, before Quentin went white as chalk and stopped listening. One truth, given freely, by each of the ones who ask passage." I looked at the woman. "I know what mine will be. But before I give it, I want to spend the other truths I'm carrying. The crown's. Orlane's. All of it. Because if I'm going to burn my bridge home, I intend to burn it thoroughly enough that the smoke can be seen from the palace."

The woman studied me for a long moment. Then she turned to Quentin.

"And them? Do they consent to hear it? Because some truths are weapons, and handing a weapon to someone isn't always a kindness, whatever the giver tells himself."

"That's the thing about me," Quentin said, and there was something in their voice I hadn't heard before — not the market warmth, not the wariness. Something older and tireder. "I collect things people give me. It's apparently a family trait." They glanced at me. "Give it to me, Jude. Whatever it is. I'd rather carry it than wonder about it."

So we went with the rebels.

* * *

Their camp was a day's walk deeper into the hills, tucked into a fold of land where a stream ran backward through a meadow — or seemed to; Vasik swore the water flowed uphill on certain mornings, and I'd learned by then not to bet against the geography of this region. There were perhaps forty of them: deserters, smugglers, two former registrars who had grown consciences, a cook who had once fed the Duke of Marre and remembered exactly which wine he feared.

The woman — Captain Ansel, though she waved off the title like a fly — put us in a tent and brought tea that tasted of pine and iron, and then she sat across from me with a blank book and a pen and said, "Whenever you're ready. Slowly. I write fast but I like to watch people decide."

Decide. As if it were a decision. As if I hadn't been deciding since the flooded dark beneath the city, since the moment I chose a fugitive over a handler and felt the strange lightness of it, like setting down a load I'd forgotten I carried.

I started at the beginning, because that's where the poison pools deepest.

Nine years ago I was twenty-two and my sister was dying of a lung-rot that the charity wards treated with prayers and cold compresses. I was a gardener's apprentice with calluses and no prospects. And a soft-spoken man named Orlane came to the hospital with an offer dressed as mercy: the crown would pay for Sefa's treatment — the real treatment, the foreign physicians, the whole expensive miracle — and in exchange I would keep my eyes open in the places gardeners go unnoticed. The conservatories. The palace grounds. The markets, later, when they learned I had a face people trusted.

"They called it observation," I said. Ansel's pen scratched. "For the first year it mostly was. Which nobles were buying love-charms. Which servants were stealing silverware. I told myself it was harmless, that everyone spies on everyone in a palace, that I was a pair of ears, not a hand."

"And then?"

"And then they asked me about a charm-seller in the Lanternmarket." I looked at my hands. Gardener's hands, honest-looking, which had always been the joke. "Small shop. Harmless trinkets, mostly. But Orlane had a report from a customs clerk about a customer who bought a forgetting-charm and then couldn't remember why she'd bought it. He wanted confirmation the seller had the Gift."

I hadn't known, then. That was the truth I clung to for nine years — I hadn't *known*. I'd gone into the shop on a pretext, ordered charms for the royal conservatory, an impossible errand designed to make the seller reveal themselves. And I'd liked them. God help me, I'd liked them within ten minutes, this careful, wry person weighing dried lavender behind a counter while the whole city sharpened knives outside their door.

"So you confirmed it," Ansel said.

"No." I set down the cup. "That's the omission. The one detail I never gave him. Orlane needed proof — a demonstration of power, something deniable, something I witnessed directly. I gave him everything else. The shop's location, the hours, the suppliers, the customers, the layout of the rooms above. Nine years of inventory. But I never confirmed the Gift itself. I wrote reports full of maybe. *Perhaps a trick of suggestion. Perhaps nothing.* I kept the word *perhaps* alive for nine years, and it was the only honest thing I did, and it was also the most cowardly, because it let me feel clean while I did everything else."

Quentin had gone still again, that listening stillness. When I glanced over, their expression was unreadable, and I understood suddenly why they were such a good thief of memories: they kept their own behind glass.

"Continue," Ansel said gently.

So I continued. I gave her the courier routes and their timing. I gave her Orlane's private name for the hunt — Operation Ledger, because the man had a sense of humor like a filing cabinet. I gave her the names of every gift-bearer I'd helped find before Quentin, seven of them, and I said each name out loud in front of witnesses because I had decided they deserved witnesses, even dead ones, even posthumous ones. A lamplighter in Corve. Two sisters who sold luck at the horse fairs. A midwife whose crime had been easing pain with a touch. I watched Ansel write them down and I thought: these names are the truest thing in any book the rebellion owns, and I am the ink, and the ink does not get to be proud of the page.

I gave her the garrison schedules along the eastern road. The passwords, rotating every ninth day, current as of our flight. The location of the holding cells where registrants waited for trial — the cellar of the customs house in Vell, because the prisons were full and nobody wanted magic-users visible. I gave her the fact that the trials were theater, that the sentences were decided before the accused walked in, that Orlane had a quota and the magistrates knew it and signed anyway.

And then I gave her the last item, the one I'd been circling, the one that made my mouth taste like copper:

"The Door is real," I said. "Orlane knows it's real. He has a map fragment — pre-Erasure, recovered from the archive your people raided last spring — and the crown's scholars believe the Door leads somewhere the Gift cannot be hunted. Somewhere the hunt ends. Orlane isn't chasing Quentin to execute them." I met Ansel's eyes. "He's chasing them because Quentin is the key, and the crown wants the Door locked from the far side forever — or opened, and everything beyond it claimed. I don't know which. He told me neither. But he told me enough that I understand why the hunters won't stop at the border. There is no border for them. There's only the key."

Ansel stopped writing. For a moment the only sound was the stream running its uphill argument, and Vasik outside the tent loudly failing to teach another boy to play knucklebones.

"Well," she said at last. "That's worth more than everything else you've said tonight, and you've said a great deal." She closed the book. "You understand what you've done. There is no version of the crown's forgiveness after this. No pardon, no bargain, no quiet return to the gardens. You've handed us the machinery of the hunt."

"I know."

"And the sister. Sefa. If Orlane learns what you've done — and he will learn, Jude, he always learns — she becomes leverage or example. Nothing in between."

"I know that too." My voice came out steadier than I expected. Maybe because the worst of it was already spoken. Maybe because for nine years I had done arithmetic, and for the first time the numbers were simple. "But here's the thing I've worked out, captain. The crown already owns her. They've owned her for nine years. Everything I did — every report, every betrayal, every *perhaps* — I did thinking I was buying her freedom, and she was never being freed. The leash was the payment plan. There's no balance point where the debt clears. There's just the leash, shorter every year." I spread my hands. "So I'm done paying. If they use her against me, that's a new debt, and it's theirs, and there are forty armed people in these hills who now know exactly where the crown keeps its hostages."

Ansel smiled properly this time. It transformed her face from a fortification into something almost kind.

"You'd have made a terrible spy," she said. "Too much conscience. Fortunately for us, and fatally for Orlane, you made a good one first." She rose, tucked the book away, and paused at the tent flap. "One more thing. You said the Door needs a truth freely given. From each of you." Her eyes moved between me and Quentin, and I saw her arrive at the understanding a half-second before I was ready for anyone else to. "You're not planning to sell yours, are you. Not even to us."

"No," I said. "Some things aren't currency."

She left. The lantern guttered. And then it was just the two of us and the pine-and-iron smell of the tea and nine years sitting on my chest, and Quentin looking at me with the lamplight making shadows of everything except their eyes.

* * *

"You could have given it to her," Quentin said.

"Gone to the rebels with my sins, yes. Very fashionable. Everyone in these hills has a confession; it's practically a dress code." I rubbed my face. "It wouldn't have counted. The Door doesn't want a performance. Your archivist was specific — a truth given freely, meaning given to the one who needs it, not traded to whoever's buying." I laughed, and it came out ragged. "Do you want to know the joke, Quentin? I've been giving pieces of myself away for nine years and calling it loyalty. Reports. Observations. Pieces of other people too — seven names, eight, nine. And the one piece I never gave anyone was the whole of it, laid end to end. The complete accounting. I kept that. Hoarded it like you hoard—" I stopped. Corrected myself. "Like I imagine you hoard things."

"Memories," Quentin said softly. "Yes. We're both thieves, Jude. We just stole different things."

"You weren't sent to do it."

"No," they agreed. "I chose it. That's worse, probably. Don't let me off the hook to make room for yourself." But there was no edge in it. There was something almost like relief, which I couldn't parse then and understood later: Quentin had spent weeks watching me flinch at checkpoints, assembling the truth from fragments, dreading it. Being told plainly was, for a person like them, a kind of mercy.

I stood up. My knees complained. Nine years of kneeling in flowerbeds will do that, and I thought absurdly that I might never kneel in a flowerbed again, and that grief arrived sideways, in the body, in the smallest possible denomination.

"Ask me," I said. "Whatever you want to know. Everything's on the table now. Ask me anything and I'll answer, and afterward I'll say the rest out loud at that door, all of it, even the parts that make me small. Because that's the price of passage, and I've decided—" my voice cracked and I let it, because hiding the crack would have been one more theft, stealing my own honesty and replacing it with polish, "—I've decided I want to pay it. Not to earn forgiveness. I don't expect that. I want to pay it because it's the first true thing I'll have ever given anyone without a handler signing for it."

Quentin looked at me for a long time. The lantern hissed. Outside, Vasik finally lost at knucklebones with a squawk that scattered birds from the treeline.

"What was the conservatory really like?" they asked at last. "When you came to my shop with that impossible order. Did you ever go back and plant any of it?"

I blinked. Of all the questions. Of everything in nine years of ledgers — and they asked about the conservatory.

"It's beautiful," I said. Helplessly. "That's the trap of the place. Glass houses full of things from countries that don't exist anymore, ferns older than the dynasty, a wall of orchids that bloom only when someone sings to them. I used to stay after my shift sometimes just to breathe in there. It was the only room in the palace that smelled like something other than power." I shook my head slowly. "I told myself the errand was a lie, but wanting the charms for it — that was true. I wanted them. I wanted to bring something living into that glass and watch it take."

Something moved through Quentin's face then — recognition, or its ghost. Later I would learn what they were carrying: a childhood in a country that ate memories, handwriting on a ruined wall, a sealed door inside their own skull. That night I knew none of it. I knew only that they reached out and took my hand — the gardener's hand, the informer's hand, the hand that had written *perhaps* for nine years — and held it the way you hold something you've decided not to examine too closely yet, in case examination breaks it.

"You'll say it at the door," they said. "All of it. And I'll stand there and listen, and I won't interrupt, and I won't forgive you on schedule. And then—" they squeezed my hand once, hard, "—and then we find out what grows."

* * *

We left the rebel camp before dawn. Ansel gave us provisions and a guide as far as the old salt road, and a warning: Orlane's hunters had been seen moving toward the high passes, and they were traveling like men who already knew the destination. Which meant Orlane had a map fragment and a scholar and time, and we had a riddle and each other and a truth I was saving like the last coin in a collapsing currency.

At the treeline I looked back once. The camp was already smoke and absence among the hills. Somewhere south, in a customs house cellar or a gilded cage, my sister was alive — alive, and mine to fail in a new way, or free in a new way, depending on which of us reached the Door first. I would spend chapters of the road ahead learning that love is not a ledger, that you cannot pay one person's ransom with another person's truth, that the only accounts that matter are the ones you settle facing someone with your hands open and empty.

Quentin walked beside me up into the thinning trees, toward the ruins of a country with no name, toward a wall where a child had once written something in chalk that the world had been forbidden to remember.

"I keep thinking about the orchids," Quentin said, apropos of nothing, as the sun cleared the ridge and lit the frost gold. "The ones that bloom when sung to. When this is over — if there is an over — plant me somewhere with those."

"There's an over," I said. "There has to be. I've paid too much for there not to be."

And ahead of us, faint as breath on glass, the mountains parted around a valley that no map had ever admitted to, and something in that valley — patient, thorned, and waiting — began, very slightly, to wake.
