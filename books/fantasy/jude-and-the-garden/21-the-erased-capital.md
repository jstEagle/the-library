## 21. The Erased Capital

### Quentin

The road forgot us before we reached it.

That's how I would describe it afterward, when I still had an afterward to describe things to. The pass narrowed, the old woman's directions ran out at a streambed that no longer matched her memory of it, and the world simply stopped keeping track. No cairns. No cut stumps healing over. No shepherd paths worn into the hillside by generations of sheep who had never existed. We climbed through country so unmarked it felt less like wilderness and more like a page someone had scraped clean with a knife.

Jude walked ahead of me the whole day, which was either devotion or strategy, and I had stopped trying to tell the difference.

"There," Jude said, near dusk, and pointed.

I looked. I saw mountains, scree, a sky going the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom. Then I saw what Jude saw — or rather, I stopped not-seeing it. A valley below us, wide as a held breath, and in the valley a city. Or the idea of one. Walls stood where walls had decided they were finished standing; roofs sagged like tired shoulders; towers leaned at angles that would have made any architect weep and any mason reach for his tools. And everywhere — everywhere — green.

Vines the thickness of my wrist poured out of second-story windows. Trees grew through the middle of streets, splitting cobblestones into petals of stone. Moss upholstered doorways. Ivy had taken the place of curtains in windows that still held their glass, so that from outside each house looked curtained in living lace. The city hadn't been abandoned. It had been *planted*.

"It's beautiful," Jude said, and their voice came out strange, hushed, the way people talk in churches they don't believe in.

"It's wrong," I said, because someone had to, and I was already walking down toward it.

Here is what I knew, then: the country had eaten itself. The old woman in the archive had told us that much between sips of tea she kept forgetting she'd poured. It fed on memory — its soil, its air, its *architecture* — and when the crown came for it, rather than let itself be harvested, it drank itself dry. Every map burned its own ink. Every citizen forgot their own name before the soldiers could ask it. The country chose oblivion the way other countries choose war: reluctantly, completely, and leaving the cost to be paid by whoever came next.

Whoever came next was me.

We descended into the city as the light failed, and the first thing I noticed was the quiet. Not silence — cities are never silent, even dead ones, even erased ones. There was wind in the vines, water somewhere underground, the tick and settle of masonry remembering gravity. But underneath all that, a quiet like the quiet inside a seashell. Like the city was listening.

The second thing I noticed was that I couldn't remember my mother's face.

Now, understand: I hadn't been able to remember my mother's face for twenty years, not since — well, since whatever I couldn't remember. That absence was an old tenant; I'd furnished around it. But this was different. This was like reaching for the absence and finding the absence *reaching back*. I stood in a street under a tree growing through a fountain, and I thought, *my mother's face*, and what came back was a shape where a feeling should be. Grief without a picture. A hole shaped exactly like a hole.

"Quentin." Jude's hand on my arm. "Your nose."

My nose was bleeding. Of course it was. When had it started? I touched my lip and found blood already drying there, and no memory of it starting, and that frightened me more than the blood did. In this city, things happened to you and the happening didn't stay put. It slid off. The air ate at the edges of events the way damp eats at paper.

"We shouldn't sleep here," I said.

"Where else?" Jude gestured at the ruins with the practicality of a man who has slept in worse places, which he has, several times, mostly because of me. "There's a building over there with a roof that looks ambitious about lasting the night."

It was a chapel, or a granary, or a greenhouse — the city refused to say. Its walls were stone below and glass above, and through the glass the last light fell in green bars, filtered by ivy, so that inside everything looked drowned and preserved at once, like things at the bottom of a clear lake. Vines had come through a broken pane and made a thicket in one corner, flowering. White flowers, five-petaled, with a scent I knew.

I knew the scent. That was the problem. I knew it the way you know a word in a language you've forgotten you speak.

"I used to live here," I said. It came out flat, factual, absurd. "Before."

"You told me you grew up in Marrowfield," Jude said carefully. "You told me that story twice. The millpond. The eel."

"I know what I told you." I sat down hard against a wall that was warm, somehow, though the sun had gone. "I also know I've been lying to both of us for twenty years, and the interesting thing is that this city might be the only witness left who knows what actually happened. And it's not talking."

Jude crouched beside me. Firelight, when they got it going, turned their face into something older and kinder than their years. They'd been doing that a lot lately — being kind when I least deserved it and most needed it. I still didn't know what to do with it. I still didn't know what they'd done, either, but I'd stopped pretending I didn't know *something* was coiled under all that loyalty. You can love someone and keep count at the same time. Nobody tells you that. Everybody learns it.

"Eat," Jude said, pressing bread into my hand.

I ate. The bread tasted like bread, thank every indifferent god, though I noted — with the professional interest of a thief — that I couldn't quite remember what I'd been afraid of an hour ago. The checkpoint at the pass? The wolves in livery? Something about blood on snow? It was gone, or going, filed away somewhere behind a door I hadn't built. The city was eating my fear. On balance I couldn't decide if that was mercy or appetite.

"Jude," I said. "If you wake up tomorrow and I don't know your name —"

"You'll know it."

"If I don't."

They were quiet a moment. The fire popped. Somewhere out in the ruined dark, a flower opened — I swear I heard it, a small wet sound like a kiss — and the listening quiet bent slightly toward it.

"Then I'll introduce myself again," Jude said. "I'm apparently good at beginnings."

* * *

### Jude

I have lied to kings' men with a straight face for nine years, so believe me when I say I know what lying costs. Your mouth does the work and your body pays the bill. By the time we reached the erased capital I was carrying so many unpaid debts of falsehood that I'd stopped sleeping properly; I'd lie down honest and wake up guilty, as if the truth ambushed me in the dark and read itself aloud while I couldn't stop it.

The city did something worse than ambush. It *audited*.

I figured this out in the night, in the chapel-or-granary-or-greenhouse, watching Quentin sleep. The fire had burned low. Quentin's face, asleep, kept moving — not nightmares, exactly. More like a person reading very fast. Lips shaping words. Brows lifting at something remembered, falling at something lost. Once they laughed, small and terrible, at nothing.

And I lay there and tried to remember my sister's birthday, and couldn't.

Not her face — her face was safe, her face was branded into me deeper than any debt. Her *birthday*. Fourth of Thawmonth. I have known that date since I was six years old. I recited it to myself every time the handler's summons came, as a kind of anchor: *fourth of Thawmonth, fourth of Thawmonth, everything I do, I do so she sees spring.* And now I reached for it and found fog. I knew I loved her. I knew why I'd done every rotten thing. But the date itself had been skimmed off the top of me like cream, smooth and painless, and something in the air of this place had done it while I slept, and it hadn't even bothered to ask.

I sat up too fast. Quentin woke the way they always woke — instantly, silently, hand already finding the knife — and relaxed by degrees when they saw it was me.

"What time is it?"

"Middle of the night. Go back to sleep."

"You're white as chalk." They sat up too. The firelight caught the dried blood under their nose, and they wiped at it absently, frowning at their fingers. "Jude. What's wrong?"

I almost told them. Here's the thing nobody warns double agents about: the truth gets heavy. You carry it so long your shoulders reshape around it, and then one night, in a dead city that steals birthdays, a person asks *what's wrong* in a voice like that, and the truth climbs up your throat with its little knife and you have to physically swallow it back down.

"The air," I said. "It takes things. While you sleep. Small things first."

Quentin stared at me. Then, slowly, they nodded, and their expression did something complicated — relief and grief shaking hands.

"My mother's face," they said. "I went looking for it earlier. It's not gone, exactly. It's — filed. Behind something." They wrapped their arms around their knees, a gesture so young it hurt to watch. "Do you know what I think this city is, Jude? I think it's not dead. I think it's *digesting*. Slowly. Everything anyone ever brought into it. And I think we just volunteered to be the meal."

"Then we find what we came for and we leave."

"We don't even know what we came for." They laughed, short and bitter. "No — that's not true. That's the worst part. Some part of me knows exactly what we came for, and it's sitting behind the same door my mother's face is behind, and it's *locked*, and I built the lock myself. I can feel the workmanship."

I should have said something then. About locks. About doors. About the things a person builds in themselves to survive, and how they always, always charge rent later. I have expertise. Instead I said:

"Sleep. I'll take first watch."

"You'll fall asleep. You're swaying."

"I'll take first watch," I said again, in the voice I used on the handler's clerks when I wanted a lie to sound like a fact, and hated myself for using it here, on them, in this place where lies curdled fastest.

But Quentin was already lying back down, trusting me, which was the whole catastrophe of my life in miniature: everyone trusted me, and I had never once deserved it cheaply.

* * *

### Quentin

Morning in the erased capital arrived like a rumor. Light came up through the ivy-filtered glass and lay across the floor in green stripes, and dust motes hung in it, and I watched them and tried to remember what day it was and discovered the question had no handle.

Worse: I reached for the plan — our plan, mine and Jude's, the one we'd argued about for two days on the Salt and Ash Road — and found it intact except for its reason. I knew we were going deeper into the city. I knew we were looking for a Door. I could not remember *why* a Door, or what waited behind it, or who had told us, only that the knowing sat behind that locked interior door of mine, humming, patient, like a wasp in a wall.

"This way," I said, and surprised myself, because I hadn't known I knew which way. My feet knew. My feet were locals.

We walked. The city opened street after street, and I will spend whatever memory I have left trying to describe it honestly, because it deserves honesty even if I can't give it accuracy.

The buildings remembered being gardens. That sounds like poetry; it isn't; it's architecture. Walls curved where walls should be straight, following the habit of hedges. Balconies bloomed with flowers that grew out of the *mortar*, drinking the stone. A colonnade stood in the main square with columns shaped unmistakably like tree trunks — bark-textured, branch-forked at the capitals — except they were marble, veined and cold, stone dreaming it had once been alive. Fountains flowed with clear water from sources that had been dry for thirty years. I put my hand in one. The water was cold and tasted faintly of rain that hadn't fallen yet.

And the people — the absent people — were everywhere in the negative. Chairs set out facing views of nothing in particular. A child's toy in a doorway, a wooden bird on wheels, its paint faded but its wheels still turning smoothly when Jude nudged it, as if it had been oiled yesterday. Tables set for meals long digested. On one table, a bowl of fruit turned entirely to dust kept the exact shapes of pears.

"They left mid-life," Jude said quietly. "Whatever happened, it happened fast enough they didn't finish breakfast."

"They didn't leave." I heard my own voice and didn't recognize its certainty. "They were *taken out*. All of them. At once. This wasn't a plague or a war. Look — no burn marks, no arrowheads, no hurried graves. The country closed its fist around everyone it loved and pulled them somewhere, and it left the furniture."

"How do you know that?"

"I don't." I turned in the square, heart hammering, nose threatening to bleed again. "That's the horror of it, Jude. I *know* it anyway. There's knowledge in me that doesn't belong to the part of me that's allowed to use it. I'm a locked house walking around inside a locked city, and the keys are all in drawers I sealed when I was — "

When I was what? When I was young. When I was here. The sentence hit the lock and stopped, and behind the lock something shifted its weight, slow and enormous, the way a ship shifts at dock.

Jude took my elbow. "You're bleeding again."

"Let me bleed. It's the only honest thing my body's doing."

We crossed the square toward what my feet insisted was important: a palace, or its skeleton, climbing the far hill. Its gardens had won the argument with its wings — the eastern wing was simply gone, swallowed by yew hedges grown forty feet tall, dense as fortress walls, and the western wing stood roofless with saplings in its ballroom. Between the wings, a gate. Even at distance, even through the morning haze, the gate was wrong in a way that pulled at the eye: black iron wrought into the shapes of vines, and *actually* growing, the metal and the living wood indistinguishable, rust and blossom sharing the same stem.

Behind it, deep in the hedge-dark, something waited. I couldn't see it. I could feel it the way you feel a held note.

"That's it," I whispered. "That's why we came."

Jude said nothing, but their hand tightened on my elbow, and when I glanced at them their face had gone carefully blank — the blankness I'd learned, on the road, to read as *I know more than I'm saying.* Fine. Let them keep their secrets another hour. I had one of my own now, humming behind a lock, and the symmetry of it was almost funny.

Almost.

We crossed the last stretch of garden — past a sundial whose shadow fell the wrong direction, past a bench where two people had clearly sat side by side so often their ghosts had worn the wood — and I stopped before the iron gate, close enough to touch it, and the waiting thing behind the hedge waited, and the locked thing behind my ribs knocked once, politely, like a visitor.

And that was when I saw the wall beside the gate. Low, knee-high, old stone, half-swallowed by moss. Someone had scratched into it, long ago, in a child's uneven hand — the letters wobbling with effort, pressed deep, the way children press when they want the world to keep what they write —

and I knew the handwriting.

Not recognized it. *Knew* it. From inside. The way you know your own signature, your own walk, the particular crooked R you have made ten thousand times and will make ten thousand more.

The letters said: **I LIVE HERE AND I AM NOT AFRAID.**

My knees went. Jude caught me before the ground could, and the gate loomed, and behind the lock in my chest something began, very quietly, to open.
