## 22. Gardens That Remember

The city remembered being a garden the way an old woman remembers being a girl — imperfectly, stubbornly, and with a great deal of complaint about how things used to be.

I woke on our third morning in the ruins with moss in my hair and a stone archway pressing its knee into my spine, and I lay there for a moment listening to the sound of the place breathing. That is not a figure of speech. The city breathed. A long slow exhalation came up through the cracks in the flagstones every dawn, warm and green-smelling, like the whole ruin was a chest expanding under a blanket of rubble. Then it would sigh back down again at dusk. Jude thought I was imagining it. Jude thought a lot of things were imagination that week, because the alternative — that we were walking around inside something still alive, still hungry — was worse.

I had learned to stop arguing with the city about what it wanted. It wanted attention. Everything here did.

"Quentin." Jude's voice from somewhere near what had probably once been a fountain and was now a bowl of ferns the size of dinner plates. "There are stairs."

"There are always stairs," I said. "Ruins are mostly stairs wearing other buildings as coats."

"These go *down*."

That got me upright. In the erased country, everything worth finding had been found going down: the cisterns that drank rain, the vaults where the memory-eaters had kept their harvests, the roots of the hedge-palace we'd seen from the ridge, black-green and patient as a held breath. Down was where the country kept its pockets.

Jude stood at the mouth of a stairwell tucked behind a wall of flowering briars — actual flowers, open and pink and shameless, in a season when nothing else this far north should have bloomed. They'd stopped flinching at the flora by then. Mostly. They pointed with the lantern instead of their hand, which I'd noticed they did now, keeping one fist free. Old habits. Newer ones layered over them like bark over a scar.

We went down together, because by that point in our acquaintance there wasn't really a version of either of us that went anywhere alone.

* * *

You should understand what the city was doing to us, so you don't think less of what follows.

Memory came loose here. Not violently — that would have been almost merciful. It came loose the way thread comes loose from a hem: one small tug at a time, unnoticed until your sleeve hangs wrong. On the first morning I had lost the name of the innkeeper in Salt-and-Ash town who'd watered down our wine. By the second I couldn't remember whether I preferred my tea bitter or sweet, and had to try both, and Jude watched me make the face of a stranger tasting two strangers' tea and said nothing, which was its own particular kindness.

The country ate memories. That was its nature, before the crown burned it off every map. It had fed on remembering the way our cities feed on grain — quietly, massively, as infrastructure. And though it was dead, or mostly dead, or dead the way orchards are dead in winter, its appetite remained, and we had walked into its pantry.

So we had developed rituals against it. We repeated important things aloud to each other each night, names and debts and promises, trading them back and forth like coins pressed into a trusted hand. *My name is Quentin. Your sister is called Wren and she is alive and the crown lied about her grave. You killed a soldier at the pass and it was not murder, it was arithmetic.* We said these until they wore grooves in us, grooves deep enough that the city's slow tongue couldn't lick them smooth.

It worked. Mostly. Which is why what happened on the third day frightened me more than any hunter ever had — because it wasn't forgetting.

It was the opposite.

* * *

The stairs let out into a long gallery, half-collapsed, its ceiling open to the sky in a jagged seam where something had fallen centuries ago and the country had simply grown around the wound. Ferns colonized the rubble. Water dripped somewhere with the patience of a metronome. Along the walls ran a frieze — carved figures, weathered to ghosts of themselves, and I remember thinking, with the detached professionalism of someone who had spent twenty years selling trinkets, that the workmanship was extraordinary and the subject matter was gardening.

Not war. Not kings. Not gods.

Gardening. Row upon row of carved people kneeling in carved soil, planting carved seeds, and above them, in the upper register, the same figures again — older, I supposed, or dead, or both — standing among trees so large the carvers hadn't bothered finishing the crowns. A story told twice: the planting, and what the planting became.

"They buried their history in horticulture," Jude said, running fingers along a figure's face worn featureless. "No wonder the crown hated it. Imagine the archives. *Year of the Third Pruning.*"

"You joke," I said, "but I've sold charms to three different cartographers and none of them could draw a tree honestly. They always make them too small. Something about scale frightens them."

Jude laughed — short, startled out of them, the laugh they still seemed surprised to own — and turned toward the far end of the gallery where the light fell through the broken roof in a dusty column.

I stayed where I was.

Because I had looked at the frieze again, properly, the way I used to look at customers — reading the small tells, the thing beneath the thing — and I had realized the two registers weren't *planting and result*. They were the same garden, twice. Same rows. Same spacing. The lower register showed hands putting seeds in earth; the upper showed the same hands, in the same positions, pulling the finished fruit down. And between the two registers, running along the border like a scribe's rule, was a line of writing.

I knew the script. That was the first wrongness. I had never studied the erased country's language; no one alive had; the old woman in the mountain archive had needed forty years and her father's stolen maps to read a single placename. But I looked at that line of carved letters and my mind supplied sounds for them, unbidden, the way yours supplies the rest of a song after two notes.

The second wrongness was the handwriting itself.

Carved letters don't have handwriting, exactly. Stone flattens personality. But there are habits that survive chisels — the way a particular hand loops, leans, crowds its letters or gives them air. I have spent twenty years forging labels for my charms. I know hands the way a shepherd knows sheep. And the hand that had cut this line of text leaned hard to the right, crossed its letters in impatient slashes, and made its small e's like little closed mouths.

It was my handwriting.

I want to be careful here, because *vertigo* is too pretty a word for what happened next. Vertigo has edges, a top and a bottom, a promise of ground somewhere. This was more like the moment you wake in an unfamiliar room and for three full seconds you do not know your own name — except the three seconds stretched, and the unfamiliar room was my own skull, and the name that didn't come had been mine for forty-odd years.

"Jude," I said. My voice sounded borrowed.

They turned. Whatever they saw in my face brought them across the gallery fast, lantern swinging, and they caught my elbow as my knees considered their options. "What? What is it?"

"That's me." I pointed at the carved line. "That's my hand. I wrote that. When I was—" I stopped, recalculated, started again. "Someone wrote that who writes exactly like me, in a language I can somehow read, in a country that was erased before I was born, and I need you to tell me I'm wrong, Jude, because the alternative is that I'm not."

Jude looked. I watched them do the thing they did — the informant's discipline, the careful cataloguing — before the person overtook it. Their jaw tightened. "The lean matches," they said quietly. "And the crossing. Quentin—"

"Don't say it yet." I crouched, pulled the little knife I used for cutting wick-trim, and set its edge against the stone below the line of text. There was more carving there, nearly obliterated by lichen and centuries, low on the wall where a child would reach. I scraped. Lichen came away in grey curls. Beneath it:

More of my handwriting.

A full sentence this time, cut shallow and crooked the way children cut, with one letter gouged and re-cut where a small hand had slipped.

I read it aloud, because if I didn't give it to the air I was afraid it would only exist inside me, and inside me was no longer trustworthy real estate.

"*I am Q and I live behind the fig wall and Mama says the garden will keep me even if the king takes everything else.*"

The gallery dripped. Somewhere above, a bird complained about the intrusion.

"Mama," Jude repeated softly.

"I don't have a mother." The words came out flat and stupid and true. "I mean — everyone has one, technically, but I don't *remember* one. First memory I own is age nine, standing in a market square in Vairemont with no money and no name I recognized and a talent I hadn't asked for, and I built myself out of that day like a barnacle builds a shell. Twenty years, Jude. Twenty years I assumed I'd lost my early childhood to some trauma or other, the ordinary kind, the kind that happens everywhere. Bad luck. Sad story. Move along."

"And now?"

"Now I'm looking at proof that I grew up *here*. Behind a fig wall. With a mother who sang — I don't know how I know she sang, I don't remember it, I just know it the way you know the shape of a word on the tip of your tongue—" My voice cracked sideways and I hated it and kept going. "This country fell when I was six or seven. The erasure. Everyone's memories of it went into the ground or into the fire. And mine—" I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Mine didn't scatter. Mine was *collected*. Tidied. Sealed. Someone took my whole childhood and put it somewhere neat, and left me a shell that happened to be talented, and I've been calling that shell *me* for twenty years."

Jude knelt beside me. They didn't touch me, which was correct; I was in that state where touch either saves you or shatters you and no one can predict which. Instead they did the ritual. "Your name is Quentin," they said. "You sell charms, badly, on purpose. You steal memories gently and hate yourself for it. You fled with a gardener who didn't deserve you and is trying to become someone who does. Those things are true. The city hasn't eaten those."

"The city isn't eating anything," I said. "That's what I keep trying to tell you. This isn't loss, Jude. This is a *wall*. Between me and me. And somebody built it from the inside."

We stayed there a while, the two of us crouched in the green dark under a sky-seam, while the country breathed its slow breath through the stones. I put my palm flat against the carved sentence. The stone was cold. Under the cold, faintly, the way you feel a heartbeat through a floor — warmth. Or the memory of warmth. Or a country dreaming it had warmth once.

"Q," Jude said finally. "The child's name. Just Q?"

"So it seems."

"Not Quentin."

"No."

"Then where did *Quentin* come from?" They asked it gently, but questions like that one aren't gentle no matter how you wrap them. "Twenty years ago, in Vairemont. You said you had no name you recognized. So the one you picked—"

"—or the one that was picked for me," I said, and felt the floor of the world tilt another degree. Because I had always told myself I'd chosen it. Chosen it out of thin air, chosen it defiantly, the one thing the fires and famines hadn't given or taken. And now I sat in my own erased homeland reading my own six-year-old handwriting and understood that I might never have chosen anything in my life — that even my name might be a returned good, a hand-me-down, a coin some older version of me had slipped into my pocket so I'd have *something* when the sealing was done.

Behind the fig wall. I tried to imagine a fig wall. I got warmth, and a smell like honey gone slightly wine-dark, and a shadow pattern of leaves on sunlit plaster, and nothing else. A memory's ghost haunting a memory's grave.

"I want it back," I heard myself say. "All of it. Whatever it costs."

"Careful," Jude said. "You know better than anyone that everything costs. You've spent twenty years watching people pay."

"Yes," I said. "But they paid for other people's keepsakes. I'd be paying for myself. It's the first purchase I've ever actually wanted to make."

Jude looked at me for a long moment — that look they had, the one that weighed and measured and then, lately, simply *stayed*, having decided measurement was beside the point. "Then we find the seal," they said. "Locked memories have keys. You taught me that yourself, back on the road, the night you opened the dying woman's last hour. Where would you put a key, if you were hiding it from yourself?"

I almost laughed. Almost. Because the answer was obvious, and terrible, and it had been following us since the ridge.

"The palace," I said. "The hedge. The garden at the center of everything. If I was a child of this place, and this place chose to spare me — it wouldn't leave my childhood in a cellar. It would plant it." I stood. My legs had opinions but obeyed eventually. "Gardens That Remember. That's what the frieze says, Jude, the part I can suddenly read. The lower register plants, the upper harvests. *Nothing planted here is ever truly lost. It only waits for its season.*"

"Then it's a long walk to the palace," Jude said, rising beside me, lantern raised.

"It's always a long walk. That's what makes it a pilgrimage instead of a stroll."

We climbed back toward the light. At the mouth of the stair I paused, because the briar wall was flowering — those impossible pink blossoms — and as I passed, every bloom on the wall turned, very slowly, very deliberately, to follow me. Not Jude. Me.

The country knew its child was home.

And far off, beyond the eastern ridge, carried on the breathing of the ruined city like a splinter carried in flesh, I heard — or felt, or dreamed, the distinction had stopped mattering — the thin bright note of a messenger-mirror being uncovered, and a voice I did not yet know saying a name I had not yet heard, and the name was mine, and it was being offered for sale.
