## 23. The Handler's Offer

The messenger-mirror found me at dawn, which was rude of it, because I had been having a dream about my sister and in the dream she was alive and laughing and no one had ever put her in a cell to keep me honest.

I say *found* because that is how these things work. You do not summon a crown mirror. It summons you. One moment I was crouched over a cold fire in the lee of a ruined granary, trying to coax warmth out of damp peat, and the next the surface of the water in my cup had gone silver and flat as a coin, and there was Vess Corane looking up at me from the bottom of it like a fish that had learned to wear a man's face.

"Jude." He always said my name like he was checking a ledger entry. "You look terrible."

"Thank you. I've been sleeping outdoors and eating guilt."

His mouth did the thing it did — not quite a smile, just enough movement to remind you he had one and could use it if the occasion were festive enough. Vess Corane had been my handler for nine years. He had recruited me with a debt and a dying girl and a promise, and he had kept exactly enough of that promise to keep me walking. He was not cruel the way soldiers are cruel, loudly, with blood on it. He was cruel the way clerks are cruel, quietly, in columns.

"I'll be brief," he said. "You've been difficult to find."

"That was intentional."

"So I assumed. The pass at Sorrel Gap — two of my hunters dead, a third describing a gardener who fights like a man who's done it before." He let that sit between us. Water does not blink, but he managed it anyway. "I'm not angry, Jude. I want you to understand that first. Anger is for people who disappoint you by accident. You've disappointed me very much on purpose, and I respect the craftsmanship."

I said nothing. Around me the granary dripped. Somewhere above, a bird was making the sound birds make when they are the only living thing foolish enough to have an opinion.

"Here is what has changed," Corane continued. "The Door is real. We have confirmation from sources I won't name — sources you'd be surprised by, actually, but that's a conversation for after. The erased country exists, or existed until recently, and your gardener is the key to it. The crown wants that key delivered. Alive. Functional. Unharmed enough to open things."

"And if I deliver them?"

The fish-face smiled fully then, and I understood why they put him in recruitment rather than interrogation. Interrogators make you afraid of them. Corane made you afraid of yourself.

"Pardon," he said. "Full. Signed. Not the conditional kind we've dangled in front of you for nine years — the kind with your name on it and no asterisks. Your sister walks out of Whitmore Prison the same day. House, stipend, a new name if she wants one, a life that doesn't require her to pretend her brother is dead. Everything you've spent nine years earning, paid all at once."

My hands were steady. That surprised me. I had expected them to shake, and instead they were perfectly still, the way they go still when I'm pruning something delicate — when one wrong cut costs a season.

"You're lying," I said.

"Probably," he agreed pleasantly. "But here's the arithmetic. Even if I'm lying, what have you lost? You're already a fugitive. You've already killed a man wearing the crown's livery. There is no version of your future in which you stroll back into the capital and apply for a gardening post. Whereas if I'm telling the truth—" He spread his hands, or the reflection of his hands did, small and pale above the water. "One person, Jude. One person who stole twenty years of other people's lives to stay hidden. One person who, I'll remind you, erased an entire country so thoroughly that we needed nine years and a planted informant just to confirm it happened. Is that really the life you want to spend yours protecting?"

There it was. He always got there eventually — the reminder that Quentin had done something enormous and terrible, that my loyalty was misplaced, that I was choosing a thief over a nation. And every time he said it, some tired clerk-part of my brain would nod along, yes, yes, the arithmetic is sound — while the rest of me watched Quentin sleep sitting up against a wall with their coat pulled over their head like a child hiding from weather, and thought: *you don't know anything about them.*

"The sister," I said. "I want proof she's alive. Now."

Corane turned away from the mirror — turned within it, rather, the image tilting — and came back holding something pale. A letter. He held it up to whatever glass he was using on his end, close enough that I could read the handwriting, and my whole body went cold and then hot, because I knew that handwriting. I had taught it to her. Slanting left, letters too tall, the *y* with a loop like she was showing off.

*Jude. They say you can read this. If you can, please stop being stupid. — E*

"She wrote it this morning," Corane said. "She eats well, she has a window, she is alive because you are useful. Continue to be useful and she stays alive. Deliver the gift-bearer at the Door and she stops being leverage and starts being a pensioner." He set the letter down. "You have until the ridge above the palace garden. Three days. After that, my hunters take the Door by force, and I cannot promise what force does to the people standing near it."

"Why tell me this?" I asked. "Why give me three days? Why not just take them now? You know where we are — you found me."

"Because force failed once already," he said, without shame. "Thorns, Jude. The garden itself fought us. My people describe a hedge that moved like weather. Whatever your gardener is becoming, bullets and boots aren't the answer. But a friend at the threshold?" He tilted his head. "A door opens easier for someone the key trusts."

He let the silence stretch until it hurt.

"You were the best asset I ever placed," he said. "Nine years. Never missed a report. Do you know what I kept thinking, reading your last ones? *This man loves him.* Or her. I never could pin down which story you told yourself. But love is just loyalty with better lighting, Jude. Loyalty can be redirected. Love can't. So I'm asking the loyal part. Deliver the package. Go home. Be a brother again."

The mirror went dark between one breath and the next, the way it always did, leaving me holding a cup of ordinary water and a letter I couldn't touch and a pardon I didn't believe in, in a ruin full of drip and bird-noise, alone with the fact that I had just been offered everything I had wanted for nine years and had discovered, somewhere in the last minute, that I no longer wanted it.

That was the terrifying part. Not the offer. The discovery.

* * *

Quentin woke while I was feeding the fire, and I watched them across the flames the way I had learned to watch everything now — cataloguing, measuring, hating myself for the habit. They looked thinner. Everyone looks thinner on the road, but Quentin had been thinning in a different direction, inward, like a candle burning down its own wick. Every memory they gave back to the Door took something else with it. I'd started keeping count without meaning to. Four names gone from their mouth in the last week alone. Small ones. A childhood dog. A neighbor's laugh. The word for a color only they could see.

They looked at me and smiled, and my chest did the thing it does, the traitorous thing, the thing that has gotten people killed.

"Dreamed about rain," they said. "Real rain, warm rain. Can't place where. Isn't that strange? You'd think a person would remember rain."

"Maybe you invented it," I said. "You invent most things."

"Mm." They held their hands to the fire, palms open, the gesture of someone long past flinching from heat. "Your turn to fetch water."

I fetched water. This is what our days had become — a choreography so ordinary it could have been performed by any two travelers anywhere, hauling water, arguing about salt, sharing a coat. Nobody watching would guess that one of us was a fugitive dissolving into someone new and the other was an informant deciding, meal by meal, whether today was the day he fed them to the crown.

Except today the informant had been given terms, and the terms had a clock on them.

"We should move today," I said, crouching by the stream, letting the cold climb my wrists. "East ridge. There's shelter higher up — old shepherd stone."

"You hate the high paths."

"I hate falling off them more."

Quentin laughed, and I stood in the stream-cold and thought: *three days.* I thought: *he showed me her handwriting.* I thought: *if I refuse, they come anyway, with fire this time, and the thorns can't hold forever, and Quentin dies at a doorway they bled themselves hollow to reach, and Elodie dies in a cell because I was romantic.*

And underneath all of it, quieter than any of those thoughts, the one that had been growing since Sorrel Gap since the soldier's weight came off my blade and stayed off:

*I am already the kind of person who kills for them. What is one more performance?*

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about lying to someone you love: it is exactly the same skill as telling them the truth. Same voice. Same eye contact. Same casual arrangement of hands. The only difference is the direction you're pulling.

So that evening, on the ridge, under a sky doing its best impression of a hearth-fire going out, I sat across from Quentin and told the truth-shaped lie.

"They found me," I said.

Quentin's head came up slowly. Firelight moved on their face. "Found you how?"

"Mirror. This morning, while you slept." I let my hands hang loose, let my shoulders carry the right exhaustion. "It's Corane. My old handler. He knows where we are, roughly. He knows about the Door."

I watched them take that in. Watched the fear arrive and get filed away behind the calm, because Quentin files fear the way I file grief, somewhere out of sight where it can't interrupt the work.

"What did he offer?" they asked.

And here was the fork in the road. Here was where a good person would have said *nothing, he offered nothing, he only threatened.* Here was where a clever person might have said *he offered me pardon if I walk away, and I refused.*

What I said was: "Pardon. Full pardon. And my sister, freed."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere below us the valley breathed its evening breath, wind through dead country grass.

"Jude." Quentin's voice was very quiet. "What did he ask you to do?"

And I looked them in the eyes — the eyes I have crossed rivers for, lied to kings' men for, buried my better self for — and I said:

"To lead them to you. To bring you to the Door into their hands."

I told the truth. All of it except the ending. That is the trick of it; you can confess everything as long as you keep the conclusion in your pocket. I watched Quentin absorb it — the offer, the terms, the three days, the threat of fire — and I watched them arrive, as I knew they would, at the question that mattered.

"And what did you say?"

And I said: "Yes."

The word landed between us like a dropped stone. I let it sit. I let my face do what it needed to do — the shame, the flinch, the man caught between two unbearable weights, which was even true, just not true in the direction it appeared.

"You said yes," Quentin repeated.

"I said yes." I made my voice crack, and it wasn't entirely craft. Some of that crack was real; it had been waiting in me for years. "I said yes because he has my sister, Quentin. Because he showed me her handwriting and told me she'd die in that cell if I refuse. Because I am not strong enough to watch another person I love be destroyed for my principles." I dragged a hand down my face, and the hand shook, and none of that was craft at all. "I said yes because I meant to lie to him. Because the only way to buy us time is to let him believe he's bought me. Because if I said no, we'd both be dead inside the week, and if I say yes, I have three days to figure out how to betray him properly."

I looked up. Quentin was watching me with an expression I will remember until I die, however few or many years that turns out to be — an expression with no judgment in it at all. Only a kind of terrible gentleness.

"Do you know what I did," they said slowly, "when I was nineteen, and the market master found out what I was?"

"No."

"I lied. Badly. And he sold me to the charm trade instead of the executioners, and I told myself for twenty years that surviving made it clean." They stirred the fire with a stick, not looking at me. "Everyone lies to survive. The question is what the lie costs, and who pays."

"And if I pay it?" I asked. "If delivering you is the price of my sister's life — what then?"

Quentin was quiet for a long time. Long enough that the fire settled and the stars came out in that thick, reckless way they do over the dead country, as if the sky here hadn't heard about restraint.

"Then you deliver me," they said at last. "And at the Door, whatever happens, happens. I've been paying debts for weeks, Jude. I know how prices work." They looked up, and their eyes were clear, and I understood with a lurch that they believed me — believed every word, believed I had accepted the bargain, believed I would hand them over — and they were not afraid. "Just promise me one thing."

"Anything."

"When it's done — whatever's done — you go to her. You don't stand around being tragic in a doorway. You go and you live the life he promised you." A small, crooked smile. "Promise me you'll be a brother again."

"I promise," I said, and hated myself with a completeness that felt almost holy.

Because here is what I did not say, pocketed deep where even a handler's mirrors can't reach: *yes, I will deliver you to the Door, Quentin. On my oath, on my sister's grave-soil-to-be, I will walk you there myself, ahead of the hunters, straight to the archway of thorns. And then I will stand between you and everyone Corane sends, and I will keep standing there until either the Door opens and takes you somewhere no crown can follow — or I am dead on the thorn-roots and my sister learns to grieve a brother instead of burying him.*

Both promises are real. Both promises are lies. That is what it is to be a double agent: you do not choose which truth to serve. You carry them both, and you pray they weigh the same.

* * *

Corane's mirror came back that night — or his voice did, silvering the water in my cup while Quentin slept ten feet away, close enough that I could hear them breathing, which is either a comfort or a countdown depending on the hour.

"Well?" he said.

"Yes," I said. "It's done. They suspect nothing. I'll bring them to the Door."

"Good," said Corane, and I heard in his voice the particular satisfaction of a man closing a column. "Three days, Jude. The ridge path, the ruined palace garden. Don't be creative. Creativity is how assets die."

"One more thing." I kept my voice flat, bored, a man negotiating the last details of a betrayal. "When it's finished. Her release. I want it in writing, signed, before we reach the Door. Not after. Before. I've worked for you nine years, Vess. I know how 'after' goes."

A pause. Then, approvingly, almost warmly: "There he is. Yes. You'll have your paper." The water shimmered. "And Jude — welcome back."

The mirror died. I sat in the dark with a cup of cold water and a promise I intended to break and a pardon I intended to earn by destroying, and ten feet away Quentin slept on, trusting me, breathing slow.

I looked at their shape in the dark for a long time.

Then I poured out the water, ground the heel of my boot into the coals until the fire was dead, and began counting hours.

Three days. Two truths. One Door.

Somewhere beyond the ridge, in a ruined palace garden, an archway of thorns was waiting to be paid, and I meant to see it paid in full — just not with the currency Corane had ordered.

I just hoped the thorns would know the difference.
