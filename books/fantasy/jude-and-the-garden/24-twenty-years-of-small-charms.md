## 24. Twenty Years of Small Charms

The last name on my list was a woman named Odile Ferrant, and she had bought a charm against heartbreak eleven years ago, which is the kind of purchase that tells you everything about a person before they've said a word.

I found her in Verrin's Ford, three days' walk from the ruins, keeping bees. She came out to meet me with her veil pushed up and her hands gloved to the wrist, and when I said her name she went very still, the way people do when a debt they'd forgotten comes calling.

"You're the charm-seller," she said. "From the capital. The one with the little brass birds."

"The same."

"I wondered what happened to you." She looked me over — the road-worn coat, the hollowed face, Jude hovering at my shoulder like a guilty conscience made flesh. "You look like a man who's been paying his bills."

"That's more or less why I'm here."

I had learned by now not to lead with the apology. Apologies put people on their guard; debts make them curious. So I took out the little stoppered vial — one of the last four I carried — and set it on the rail of her fence between us, and told her what it was.

Her face did something complicated. Eleven years is a long time to carry a grief you can't quite locate, I've found. People build their lives around the missing piece like a tree growing around a nail. They don't always want the nail back.

"He left," she said finally. "My husband. I knew he left. I just couldn't remember *how* it felt, knowing that. Like a room in my own house I couldn't enter." She picked up the vial, turned it in the light. "And this is it? This is the room?"

"It's yours. It always was. I only — borrowed it. Badly. Without asking."

"And now you're giving things back." She looked at me with an expression I couldn't read, and then, horribly, she laughed — not unkindly, but with the weary amusement of someone who has kept bees long enough to know that everything stings eventually. "Do you know what I paid you for that charm?"

"Three coppers and a memory I didn't tell you I was taking."

"No. You charged me five." She pressed the vial into my hand and closed my fingers around it. "Keep the change. If I take this back now, I'll have to grieve him all over again, and I've only just finished building a life on top of the hole. Maybe in another eleven years." She stepped back toward her hives, and the bees rose around her like a small golden weather. "Go on, then. Go be poor somewhere else."

So that was one memory returned without being accepted, which the Door, I suspected, would count as returned all the same. A debt discharged is a debt discharged, even if the creditor refuses the coin. Though I confess I stood at her fence a while longer than I needed to, watching the bees, wondering how many other rooms in other people's houses I had bricked over, and whether any of them would ever want them opened again.

* * *

There were four vials left when we came down out of the hills, and I had spent the better part of two months working through the rest.

It had seemed simple enough, in the beginning — or if not simple, then at least orderly. A list. Names, dates, trinkets sold, memories taken. Twenty years of small charms, each one a theft so modest even I had learned to call it something else. A woman's memory of her mother's voice, traded for a luck-knot. A boy's recollection of the day he broke his arm, gone in exchange for a fever-charm that probably worked no better than prayer. Small things. Harmless things. That was the story I had told myself for twenty years, and like most stories told often enough, it had worn grooves deep enough to live in.

Then I started handing them back.

Here is what nobody tells you about returning stolen memories: you experience each life twice. Once as the thief, holding the thing up to the light, turning it, admiring the craftsmanship of someone else's joy or sorrow. And once as the witness, watching it pour back into its owner and seeing their face change — the way a house changes when you open shutters that have been painted over. Some of them wept. One man hit me, which I deserved. An old soldier took back his memory of the day he'd deserted and thanked me with a formality so complete it felt like being dismissed from service. And every single time, as the memory left the vial and left *me* — because yes, the thief keeps an echo, that's the dirty secret of the trade, you carry a copy of everything you've ever taken — every single time, something went out of me too.

Not the echo. The echo stayed; echoes are cheap. What went out of me was subtler. A thinning. Like a coat worn threadbare at the elbows, except the coat was me.

I noticed it first with colors. Standing in a farmyard outside Delve, having just returned a dairymaid's memory of her first kiss, I looked at the sunset and thought: *orange.* Not *that particular burning gold shot through with green at the edges the way the sky over the conservatory used to go in late summer.* Just orange. The word, and the color, and nothing between them.

Then it was names. I reached for the name of the street I'd grown up on and found a smooth blank place where the name should have been, the way your tongue finds the gap of a pulled tooth. Then it was the smell of my mother's kitchen — gone, though I could remember that there had been such a smell, the way you remember a rumor of a country you've never visited.

Jude watched it happening and said nothing for three days, which for Jude is a feat of heroic restraint.

* * *

On the fourth night, camped under a leaning wall that remembered being a granary, Jude finally cracked.

"You're doing it wrong," they said, poking the fire with more force than fire requires.

"There's a right way to give back twenty years of theft?"

"You're rushing. You've been rushing since the pass. Every time we stop, you've got a vial out before the tents are up, like you're trying to finish before—" They stopped. Poked the fire again. "Before there's nothing left of you to do the finishing."

I should have lied. I'm good at lying; it's practically the family trade. But the gift was thin enough now that lying felt like lifting something heavy, and besides — Jude had earned the truth the hard way, in blood and confession and a knife-fight with crown hunters, and there is a certain economy in spending truth where it's already been banked.

"It's the price," I said. "Or part of it. The Door doesn't just want the memories back. It wants them *out of me.* All of it. The taking and the taken both." I turned a vial in my fingers — the last but one, holding a cooper's memory of his daughter's christening, which I had taken in exchange for a charm against damp and had carried for nine years, admiring it the whole while the way you'd admire a jewel you had no right to. "Every theft I return takes a little of the thief with it. I can feel it going. Like water through a cracked jug."

"What goes, exactly?"

"Everything I did with it. Everything it let me do." I tried to find the words for it, the sensation of a sense closing. "You know how you can stand in a market and hear forty conversations and sort them without trying? How you can feel where everyone's attention is, the way you feel weather? That's going. It went first, actually — I haven't been able to skim a crowd since the second week. Now it's the deeper things. The reading. The reach. The—" I gestured vaguely at my own head. "The furniture. My gift didn't just let me take memories, Jude. It *was* a memory. The oldest one I have. And I'm returning it along with everything else."

Jude was quiet for a long moment. The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, the ruined granary settled with a sigh, stone remembering grain.

"So who are you," Jude said at last, "when it's all gone?"

And there it was. The question I'd been circling for two months like a moth around a lamp, unable to land, unable to look away.

"I don't know," I said. "That's the honest answer. I built myself out of that gift the way a snail builds a shell. Take it off and I don't know what's underneath. Maybe nothing. Maybe a very ordinary person who once did extraordinary harm and remembers less of it every day." I laughed, and it came out thinner than I intended. "There's a version of this where it's a mercy, you know. The Door strips me down to nothing, and whatever walks away isn't really Quentin Joubert at all. No crimes, no guilt, no twenty years of looking over one shoulder. A clean page."

"And the other version?"

"The other version is that a clean page isn't a person. It's just paper." I looked at the last-but-one vial, and the cooper's daughter smiled up at me through the glass, mid-christening, mid-joy, belonging to a man in Delve who probably still wondered why that day felt slightly out of focus. "I keep thinking about what I'll be on the far side. Whether there'll be enough of me left to love you with. Whether 'love' will even mean anything to whoever's standing there in my boots."

Jude crossed the fire and sat down beside me, close enough that our shoulders touched, and took the vial from my hand with a gentleness that undid me more than any accusation ever had.

"For what it's worth," Jude said, "the person I fell in love with wasn't the gift. The gift was the least interesting thing about you. It was the jokes. The terrible haggling. The way you talk to horses like they're owed an apology." A pause. "The way you forgave me when you had no earthly reason to."

"The jury's still out on that last one."

"The jury reached a verdict weeks ago. You just weren't listening." Jude pressed the vial back into my palm and folded my fingers over it, the same gesture Odile had used, and I wondered briefly if kindness traveled like memory, passed hand to hand until it wore smooth. "Tomorrow, then. Last one after this. And then the Door."

"Then the Door."

* * *

I delivered the cooper's memory the next morning, riding ahead alone to Delve while Jude watered the horses, because some returns ought to be private. He was easier than most — a broad, slow man with sawdust in his beard who listened to my confession with the patience of someone who has repaired many broken things and expects everything to be broken eventually.

"You gave me a good charm against damp," he said, when I'd finished. "Barrels never sweated a day after."

"That's — thank you, but—"

"Take the daughter back, then." He held out his hand for the vial. "But answer me one question first. Honest, mind. When you took it — did it hurt her? The taking?"

I considered lying. The gift was nearly gone, but the habit wasn't. "No," I said. "That's the worst part. It never hurt anyone, the taking. It only ever hurt later, in the missing. People walked around with holes in them and called the holes peace."

He nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed something he'd long suspected about the world. Then he unstopped the vial himself, tilted it, and drank his daughter's christening down like a toast, and I watched the shuttered window of him open, and heard him laugh — a great bark of surprise — and say, "*The cake!* She got icing in my beard, the little tyrant, I'd forgotten the *cake*—" and I stood in his yard and felt the echo of it drain out of me too, the last warm room in my house going dark.

And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, I felt the gift itself shift like sand underfoot.

* * *

One vial left. I stood in the road outside Delve holding it, and did not open it, because the last one was different.

The last one was mine.

I had known it was coming — the Door's arithmetic was plain enough, once you understood its grammar. Every theft returned, plus one truth freely given; Jude had paid theirs in firelight and thorns, and the thorns had drunk it. But there was a final line in the ledger, a small footnote written in my own younger hand, sealed so deep I'd only found it when the rest of the account had emptied out: a memory I had taken from no customer. A memory I had taken from *myself*, twenty years ago, on the night I fled the capital — the memory of why the erased country fell, locked behind a door inside my own skull, because the child I had been could not carry it and the adult I became could not face it.

That was the last payment. And when it went, I understood — with the clarity of a man counting out his last coins — that it would take the gift's root with it. Whatever made me able to walk through other people's heads, whatever had kept me alive in a city that burned people like me, whatever had *been* me for longer than I could now remember — it lived in that sealed room, and the Door meant to have the room entire.

I mounted up and rode back toward Jude slowly, turning it over. Who was I without it? A charm-seller with no charms. A thief with nothing left to steal, not even from himself. A name attached to a body walking east, carrying a handful of true things: that I loved Jude. That I had done harm and was sorry. That the sky over the ruins went green at the edges in late summer, and I could no longer tell you how I knew that, only that it was true, and that some truths survive the house they were stored in.

Jude looked up when I reined in, and read my face the way they always could — no magic needed, just attention, which turns out to be the rarer gift.

"It's time," they said.

"It's time." I weighed the little vial in my palm — unstoppered now, though I had no memory of opening it, which frightened me more than anything else that day. Inside it, instead of the silver shimmer the others had held, there was darkness. A small, patient dark, like a door seen from the wrong side. "Jude. If I come through the other side of this and I don't know you — if I look at you the way you look at a stranger —"

"Then I'll introduce myself," Jude said. "I'm told I make a decent first impression."

"And if there's not enough of me left to care about first impressions?"

Jude swung up onto their horse and drew alongside me, close enough to touch, and looked east toward the ridge beyond which the ruined palace waited, and the hedge, and the archway of living thorn with its appetite for accounts.

"Then I'll plant something," Jude said, "and wait for you to grow."

We rode east, and the last dark vial rode with me, cold against my chest like a second heartbeat — and far off on the ridge, where the thorns crowned the broken palace, I saw something that stopped my breath: figures. Armed ones. Moving along the skyline with the unhurried confidence of people who are not chasing anymore, because they no longer need to.

The handler had found the Door too.
