## 25. The Door in the Hedge

**QUENTIN**

The city remembered being a garden. That was the only way I could describe what I felt walking through it — not that the ruins had once been gardens, but that the ruins themselves were gardens in the middle of forgetting how.

We came down through the shattered aqueduct at dawn, when the light came gray and apologetic through a sky that couldn't decide on weather. Below us lay streets where the paving stones had buckled into ridges like plowed furrows. Vines grew out of second-story windows of houses whose walls had long since fallen, as if the ivy had decided to take over the duties of roof and rafter both. A fountain stood in a square, dry for forty years by any honest count, and yet moss climbed its basin in green velvet whorls, thick as a mattress, as though water had never left it at all — as though the stone simply refused to admit the drought was real.

"It's wrong," Jude said behind me, and I didn't argue, because they were right, and because I'd learned by now that Jude said "wrong" about things most people would call beautiful, and meant something closer to *alive*.

"It's hungry," I corrected. "There's a difference."

I could feel it the way you feel a draft from a door you can't see: the city pulling gently at me, at the edges of my mind, tasting. Every step deeper into the ruins, the tug got stronger. Little things went missing. The name of my mother's village. The tune of a song I'd been humming since the pass. Whether I'd eaten that morning or only thought about eating. I'd taken to writing important facts on my forearm in charcoal ink like a merchant's tally, which Jude found hilarious until the day I looked down and found half the list smudged into nonsense and had to ask them what my own handwriting said.

*You are Quentin,* the arm said now. *You were born somewhere. You owe a debt to a door.*

Somewhere. That word was doing a great deal of work.

"Which way?" Jude asked. They had the old woman's directions memorized better than I did, which was fortunate, because I'd read her map three times and retained roughly nothing past *follow the singing street*. The erased country had apparently organized its capital around acoustics; certain roads had been planted so their leaves caught the wind in chords, so travelers could navigate by ear even blindfolded, even drunk, even grieving. The trees were mostly dead now. But dead wood still sings, if the wind is patient enough, and the wind here was very patient indeed. It moaned through hollow trunks in long minor notes that made the hair on my arms stand up and made me want, absurdly, to weep.

"That one," I said, pointing at the street that hummed lowest. "It sounds like it knows me."

"Everything here sounds like it knows you."

"Yes," I said. "That's what worries me."

Because here is what I hadn't told Jude, or anyone, or even fully admitted to myself in the privacy of my own skull: the closer we walked toward whatever waited at the center of this city, the more familiar it became. Not the familiarity of memory — I had none, that was the entire cursed point of this expedition. It was the familiarity of a house you dream about repeatedly. You've never seen it waking. But your feet know where the loose stair is. Your hand reaches for a latch that isn't there anymore.

My feet knew this city. Something in me, some sealed and padlocked room, had walked these singing streets before, and it knew exactly where we were going, and it would not tell me why.

**JUDE**

Quentin talks when they're frightened. I don't think they know that about themselves. Twenty years of selling charms to people who'd burn you for the truth teaches you to keep your mouth moving while your heart stands very still, and so Quentin narrated our descent into the dead capital — jokes about the architecture, complaints about the moss, three separate bits involving the fountain and what they'd do to the crown's plumbing budget — and every joke landed a little further from funny, because I could see their hands shaking.

I let them talk. I'd learned that much on the road: you can't comfort Quentin directly. Directness makes them bristle like a cat presented with an unsolicited bath. But you can walk beside them, and carry the heavier pack without mentioning it, and laugh in the right places, and that adds up to something they're willing to accept.

I was also doing arithmetic I hated.

Behind us, two days back at most, rode Captain Vess and six crown hunters. Maybe more by now. The handler doesn't send six men after a prize he wants badly; he sends six men to establish contact, then however many it takes. We'd seen the smoke of their cookfires on the ridge the night before last, and Quentin had pretended not to see it, and I had pretended not to notice Quentin pretending, and between the two of us we'd constructed a whole theater of ignorance that would have gotten us both killed if either of us actually believed it.

So: arithmetic. The palace garden lay perhaps an hour ahead. The hunters moved fast when they wanted to. Which meant we had, generously, until midday to find a door that might not exist, open it with payments we hadn't finished making, and step through into a country that appeared on no map — before men with crossbows crested the ridge behind us.

I didn't say any of this. Instead I said, "Your arm's smudged again. What does it say?"

Quentin squinted at their own forearm. "*You owe a debt to a door,*" they read. Then, wryly: "Well. That's ominous but vague. Very like me."

"You could have written yourself better instructions."

"I wrote myself instructions twenty years ago too," Quentin said, quieter. "Sealed them up behind my own lock. Apparently I've always been the kind of person who hides things from themselves." They glanced sideways at me, and there was no accusation in it, which somehow made it worse. "You'd know something about that."

"Me? I'm an open book."

"You're a locked book," Quentin said, "with the key taped to the inside cover where you think nobody's looked." And they walked on before I could answer, which was a mercy, because I didn't have an answer. I'd given my truth to the thorns' cousins back at the ruined shrine — spoken it aloud into the listening dark, every betrayal, every year — and the land had accepted it, had begun to trust us, had opened paths that shouldn't have opened. But accepting a confession and forgiving the confessor are different animals entirely, and Quentin had not yet decided which animal I was.

Neither had I, honestly. Some mornings I woke up and felt like the gardener who'd come to a charm-seller's stall with an impossible order and stayed for the banter. Some mornings I felt like the crown's creature, the leash, the planted seed. Most mornings lately I felt like both at once, growing out of the same root, and I tended whichever one Quentin needed that day.

The singing street bent around a collapsed tower, and there, framed between two leaning walls, we saw the palace.

Or what had been a palace. It had grown. That's the only word. Forty years of neglect and the building had responded like a neglected orchard — gone wild, gone woody, walls swallowed under living green to the third story, windows replaced by gaps in the foliage that glowed faintly amber, like eyes. The gates were gone. In their place, a hedge. A hedge grown thirty feet tall and just as deep, impenetrable-looking, its thorns long as fingers and dark as old blood.

And set into the hedge, unmistakably, impossibly: a door.

Not a door in the hedge. A door *of* it — an archway of living thorn, woven and braided into itself in patterns too deliberate for accident, framing an oval of absolute darkness that gave off no cold, no draft, no sound. Sealed. Waiting. I'd heard the old woman describe it, and I'd thought she was describing a ruin. Standing in front of it, I understood she'd been describing a mouth.

"Well," Quentin said, in the voice of someone who has just recognized their own handwriting on the wall of the universe. "Hello, old friend."

They didn't remember knowing it. Their face said otherwise.

**QUENTIN**

Here is what I know about doors, professionally speaking: a charm-seller learns early that everything worth having costs more than it appears to. The customer sees a copper pendant that brings sweet dreams. I see the memory I lifted to enchant it, and the guilt, and the years. Doors are the same. This one had cost a country.

I approached it the way you approach a horse that has thrown you before.

Up close, the archway resolved into detail, and the detail was worse. The thorns weren't random growth; they spelled something. Rows upon rows of characters in a script I had never studied and read anyway, the way you read your own name shouted across a market square. Names. Thousands of names, grown into the hedge, each thorn a single letter, and the names kept changing — slowly, like frost forming — rewriting themselves as I watched. The hedge was remembering its people. Or mourning them. With plants it's difficult to distinguish.

I put my hand near the dark oval and the darkness pushed back, gentle as a closed eyelid.

"Not yet," I told it. "I know. I'm working on it."

Twenty years of small thefts, returned across half a countryside — done, or nearly. One truth freely given — Jude had paid that, though the door had never asked me to confirm whose truth it took, and I suspected it didn't care for my opinion on the matter. And one thing more, sealed behind my own lock: the memory of why this country fell. My younger self had hidden it from me with the same neat paranoia that hid the Door's location, and the price of opening it, the old woman had warned, was paying it — feeling all of it, all at once, in the place it happened.

Standing in the place it happened.

"Quentin." Jude's voice had changed. Not the soft voice they used on spooked animals and spooked novelists. The flat one. The soldier one. "Look at the ridge."

I turned. Behind us, above the collapsed aqueduct we'd descended that morning, the ridge line wore new ornaments: helms catching the gray light. Six of them. Then, as I counted, a seventh cresting the saddle of the hill, and an eighth, and behind those, at a distance, the small patient figures of outriders fanning along the slopes like ink dropped in water.

Vess had not sent six men to establish contact after all. He'd sent eight to close the net.

"How long?" I asked.

"They'll move when they're sure of the ground. An hour, maybe less." Jude was already scanning the garden, cataloguing cover with the ease of long practice, and I hated how easily it came, and loved them for doing it, and hated that too, a little, because love and hate had stopped bothering to stay in separate rooms months ago. "The hedge is deep. If it's a maze—"

"It's not a maze." I touched the thorn-arch again, and this time, instead of pushing back, the darkness shivered — and the hedge moved.

All of it. The whole thirty-foot wall rippled like a horse shedding flies, and the thorns rearranged, and where there had been solid green there was now a corridor, narrow and dark, winding inward. An invitation. Or a throat.

"It's not a maze," I repeated. "It's a garrison."

Jude looked at me. "Meaning?"

"Meaning the country died rather than be weaponized, and its last magic is standing in front of us deciding whether we're friends." I stepped toward the opening. The thorns held still, watchful. "Meaning when the hunters come down that hill, this garden is going to have opinions."

"And what will it cost you?" Jude asked, and there it was, the question underneath every question we'd asked each other since the cisterns, since the firelight, since the snow. What does it cost you. Because they'd watched me spend memories buying passage, buying trust, buying hours, and they knew — they alone knew precisely — what I became with each payment. Less. Smaller. Nearer to nobody.

"Ask me again when we're through," I said.

The first arrow came off the ridge then, earlier than any sane commander should have loosed it, and struck the hedge ten feet from Jude's head. The hedge ate it. The thorns closed over the shaft like water closing over a stone, and somewhere in the deep green something made a sound I will hear for the rest of my life, however much of that life remains to me: a sound like a country clearing its throat.

"Into the garden," I said. "Both truths are paid. Now it takes what I owe — and God help the men coming down that hill, because I'm not the only one collecting today."

We went in together, and the thorns closed behind us, and the siege began.
