## 26. Siege of Thorns

The garden woke at dawn on the third day, and it woke angry.

I felt it before I understood it — a pull behind my ribs, like a fisherman testing a line I hadn't known was tied to me. I was crouched by the dead fountain with my hands in the soil, doing the only useful thing left to me now that most of my gift had gone back through the Door like coins returned to a miser. The soil was warm. Not sun-warm. Warm the way a sleeping animal is warm.

"Jude," I said.

Jude came around the hedge at a run, sword already drawn, which told me everything about how our nights had been going. "What is it? Are they here?"

"No." I lifted my hands. Soil clung to them, black and rich, steaming faintly in the cold morning air. "But something else is."

The hedges moved.

Not swayed — *moved*, deliberately, branches rearranging themselves the way a man shifts his weight when he hears footsteps behind him. The great wall of thorn that ringed the ruined palace garden, the same thorn that had been dead-seeming since we arrived, drew itself up. Buds split along every branch, but no flowers came out. What came out was more thorn. Long ones, hooked ones, thorns like fishhooks and thorns like awls, unfolding from green wood with small wet sounds.

"It knows they're coming," I said. My voice sounded strange to me, doubled somehow, as if something were speaking just underneath it. "It can feel them on the ridge."

Jude stared. Jude, who had seen the Door refuse us for days, who had watched me pour twenty years of stolen memory into its roots like water into a drain, who had stood at my side while I gave away pieces of myself I would never be able to name afterward — even Jude looked afraid.

"Is it helping us?" Jude asked.

"I think," I said slowly, feeling along the line behind my ribs again, "it thinks I'm asking."

* * *

Here is what I have learned about the magic of this country, in the few days I've had to learn anything: it does not work the way mine worked. Mine — what remains of mine — was theft. A reaching out, a taking, a pocketing of other lives. Clean, if you didn't look too closely at your hands. This country's magic was never about taking. It was about keeping. Holding things so completely that when the crown burned the maps and salted the roads and ordered every mind to forget, the country simply refused. It kept *itself*. It kept its gardens growing in ruins no cartographer would ever chart. It kept a door sealed shut until someone came who could pay.

And apparently, it kept a claim on me. On whatever small residue of gift still lived in me after all the returning. A child's portion. An inheritance tax collected thirty years late.

The first attack came an hour after sunrise.

I heard them before I saw them — the particular crunch of boots on frost-killed grass, too many boots, moving in the spread formation soldiers use when they expect resistance. I counted eleven sets. Then twelve. Then I stopped counting because counting was making it worse.

They crested the ridge above the orchard — the orchard where trees grew through the roofs of fallen houses, fruit hanging heavy and wrong-seasoned, and I saw the handler's grey livery among them, and I saw crossbows, and I saw the tall shape of Captain Vess herself, the woman whose voice had read names off lists in border towns until the lists ran red.

"Hold," Vess called, in the tone of someone addressing livestock rather than people. "Quentin Joubert. Come out. Your companion's crimes die with you if you do. There's a wagon, a pardon writ, and a long life of ordinary charm-selling waiting. All you lose is—"

She gestured vaguely, and I understood she'd been briefed on exactly what I'd been losing. Someone at court knew about the Door's prices. Someone had always known. That thought went into the ledger I was keeping, the one marked *things to be furious about later*.

What happened next happened without my deciding it.

The hedge between us and the soldiers *leaned*. I felt it lean — felt it the way you feel your own arm lift, a proprioception that wasn't mine and was entirely mine at once. The thorn wall stretched forty feet in three seconds, crossing the orchard like a drawbridge closing, and the front rank of soldiers learned very quickly why the old stories say never to follow a fleeing thief into a garden.

The screaming started. Not death-screaming — worse, the specific screaming of men caught by brambles that grip. Crossbow bolts flew and the hedge drank them, wood swallowing iron the way my old gift swallowed memories, without visible effort and with, I could swear, satisfaction.

And I paid for it.

I don't know how else to describe it. It wasn't pain. It was subtraction. Somewhere in the middle of the hedge's fury I reached for a thought — my mother's face, I think, or the smell of the market on a rain day, some small coin of myself — and found a smooth blank place where it used to be. Like running your tongue over a tooth pulled in your sleep. You know the shape of what's missing. You keep checking anyway.

"Quentin!" Jude grabbed my shoulder. "You're bleeding."

I looked down. My nose was bleeding freely, and there was frost on my eyelashes though the morning wasn't cold enough for it. "Am I?" I said, wryly, because wryness is the last luxury I own outright and I intend to spend it freely. "How festive."

"What did it take?"

"That's the thing," I said. "I don't get an itemized bill. I just find the holes later."

* * *

By midday we had a rhythm, if you can call a war a rhythm.

The hunters tried fire. Of course they tried fire; soldiers always try fire eventually, it's the argument of last resort for men who've run out of arguments. They shot burning arrows into the east hedge and for one horrible moment I watched flame crawl along dry branches and thought, *well, that's the garden gone, and us with it.*

Then the fountain turned on.

It hadn't held water in decades — I'd looked into it my first morning here, at moss and cracked stone and the fossil shadow of a mermaid's tail. But when fire touched the hedge, the fountain remembered being a fountain. Water rose up out of the dry basin in a column, hung there a moment like a struck bell, and then came down across the burning hedge in a sheet. Steam rolled across the garden, thick and white and smelling of roses, absurdly, impossibly, of roses.

And I paid for that too. I felt this one go clearly, because it was big: the name of my first teacher, the charm-seller who took me in off the streets of Cael Verre and taught me which customers' sadnesses were safe to skim and which would curdle. Her name — gone. Her round face, her laugh like a cough, the way she said *small thefts, Quentin, only ever small thefts* — I could feel the shape of all of it, intact, behind a door that no longer opened for me. She was the one who'd have hated what I became. Maybe that's why she was taken first. Maybe the garden reads character.

"The water's coming from somewhere," Jude said beside me, practical as ever, because Jude processes miracles by auditing them. "Where is it coming from?"

"I don't know. Maybe the country remembers having rivers."

"Maybe." Jude's jaw worked. "Quentin. How much more can you give it?"

I looked at my hands. They were shaking, and under the dirt of the garden beds my knuckles had gone the grey-white of paper left out in weather. "That depends," I said, "on how much of me you're attached to."

"Don't." Flat. Fierce. "Don't make it a joke. How much?"

"I don't know," I said, honestly this time. "It takes what it needs. It's very polite about it, actually. No haggling. My kind of merchant, if I'm honest."

"Quentin."

"Jude." I took their hand. It was steady; mine wasn't. "Listen. Every hour the hedge holds is an hour closer to the Door being ready. The thorns are drinking my confession — no wait, that was yours. The thorns are drinking *me*, and I am letting them, because the arithmetic works. Twenty years of my life bought me this gift. The gift is buying us a future. That's not a tragedy, that's just commerce. I ran a shop. I know how to close one out."

Jude looked at me for a long moment with an expression I couldn't read, and then said, quietly, "When this is over, I'm going to teach you every single thing you've forgotten. One at a time. Even the boring parts."

"Especially the boring parts," I agreed. "Start with your sister's middle name. I seem to have lost that somewhere around the second volley."

* * *

The afternoon attacks came in waves, and each wave cost me.

The second wave, they brought ladders — absurd, brave, doomed ladders against a wall that grew. The hedge ate three ladders whole. I lost the taste of honey bread and the memory of the first time I ever successfully pickpocketed a memory, standing in a crowd watching a puppet show, a fat merchant's delight in the puppets sliding into my palm like a warm egg.

The third wave, they brought a ram made from an orchard tree they'd cut on the ridge — and oh, the garden felt *that*. The hedge didn't just defend then; it grieved. I felt the grief come through me like floodwater, the country's old wound reopening: trees felled, again, always again, the same crime committed across centuries. Thorn burst from the ground beneath the ram-bearers in spears, and men fell, and I felt sick, and I paid. That time it was Cael Verre itself — the layout of the city where I'd built my life, twenty years of street-corner knowledge, gone soft and formless in my head like a drawing left in the rain. I sat down hard against the fountain's rim and pressed my bleeding nose and laughed, because if I didn't laugh I'd scream, and screaming costs breath, and breath I needed.

"You're laughing," Jude said, dragging me upright. "Why are you laughing?"

"Because it took the city but left the shop," I said. "I can't remember how to walk home but I can remember the exact price of a luck-charm in a bad year. The garden has a sense of humor, Jude. It's cruel, but it's *funny*."

"Sit down."

"I am sitting."

"You're sitting *wrong*."

* * *

Dusk. The fourth wave never came, and the silence was worse than the noise.

We sat in the lee of the fountain, sharing the last of the waybread and a skin of water, watching the hedge stand sentry against a bruise-colored sky. The thorns had grown so high and dense that the garden felt smaller than it had — a room instead of a country, walls pressing in. Somewhere beyond them, campfires. Voices. The occasional jingle of harness. An army settling in for a siege it expected to win by attrition.

Which was, I reflected, exactly their strategy. And exactly ours. Two sides playing the same game with different currencies: they had arrows and patience; I had myself.

"How many holes?" Jude asked. They'd taken to asking it the way you'd ask about a fever — checking the progress of a disease.

"Four today." I counted on fingers that wouldn't quite steady. "My teacher's name. Honey bread. My first theft. Cael Verre." I paused. "And I think my grandmother's lullaby, though I can't be sure, because I also can't remember whether I remember it. Which is either the cruelest trick yet or a mercy."

Jude said nothing for a while. Then: "There's another way. We go over the north wall tonight, before they tighten the lines. Take our chances on the mountain. You stop spending yourself."

"And leave the Door?" I shook my head. "It opens at moonrise tomorrow, Jude. The thorns told me — told *through* me, whatever — it will be ready. One night. We need to hold this garden for one more night. After that, they can burn the whole hillside to ash for all I care."

"One more night," Jude repeated, in the voice of a person doing arithmetic they hate. "At what rate? Four memories a day? Six? What happens when the garden reaches for something and finds nothing left to take?"

"Then it stops defending us," I said, "and we find out how fast I can run these days."

"That's not a plan."

"It's a *budget*. I told you, I ran a shop." I leaned my head against Jude's shoulder, and let myself have ten seconds of that — warmth, solidity, the one thing in my inventory that was never stolen and never sold. "Ask me something," I said. "Quick. Before I lose more. Ask me something you want to be sure I keep."

Jude was quiet a moment. Then, carefully, like laying down a card: "Do you love me?"

I smiled into their shoulder. "That's cheating. That one isn't stored in the gift. That one's just mine. It doesn't live in the same place."

"Answer anyway."

"Yes," I said. "Since the checkpoint at Marrow Ford, when you lied to that sergeant with your heart hammering so loud I could hear it. Yes. Since before that, probably. Since the conservatory order that smelled like a trap and I filled it anyway because you smiled like a man committing a crime he's proud of."

"Good," Jude said, and their voice cracked slightly on it, and I filed that away too — filed it in the place that doesn't empty, the place with no doors for thieves or gardens or crowns.

* * *

Full dark. The fires beyond the hedge had settled into low orange constellations. I was half-asleep against the fountain when the change came — a wrongness in the air, the particular pressure drop I'd learned to associate with the garden spending me.

It reached for me again. I felt the reach, gentle as a librarian, and I braced for another loss —

— and instead, something arrived.

A fragment pushed *into* me, thin and cold and clear as ice water: a memory not mine. A hall of mirrors. A woman's voice, clipped and official, saying words I recognized with a jolt: *Bring me Joubert alive at the threshold, and the sister walks free. Fail, and she pays the price of your treason.* And beneath the voice, the sound of Jude breathing — Jude, in that hall of mirrors, Jude saying *yes*.

The vision folded shut like a fan. I sat bolt upright in the dark, heart slamming.

The garden had shown me. Or lent me. Or — I understood with a chill — *warned* me. It kept things. It had kept this. Somewhere in the ruined palace, in the walls, in the thorn, the country's old magic had overheard a message meant for Jude's eyes alone, a messenger-mirror communiqué flashed in the dark hours, and it had carried it to me the way a dog carries a stolen letter to its master.

Because the garden keeps things. And what it keeps, it guards.

"Jude," I said into the darkness.

Silence beside me. Too long a silence. The kind of silence a person makes when they are deciding whether the person next to them is asleep.

"Jude," I said again. "What did the mirror show you tonight?"

I heard them sit up. I heard the long breath of someone stepping off a ledge.

"Quentin," Jude said. "We need to talk about my sister."
