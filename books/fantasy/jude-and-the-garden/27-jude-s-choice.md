## 27. Jude's Choice

The mirror came at dawn, the way bad news always does.

I was in the ruined conservatory, prying a dead vine off a trellis that had once—according to the trellis itself, which hummed faintly under my palm—held grapes the size of fists. The whole erased country did this. Everything here remembered being more than it was, and it wanted to tell you about it. I'd stopped flinching at the whispering walls around day two. You can get used to anything except being happy, my mother used to say, and she'd have known.

The glass arrived folded inside a courier's satchel left on the palace steps, which meant someone had walked through the thorn wall's outer ring without dying, which meant the crown had bought or bred something that could do what no honest traveler could. The satchel was plain leather. The mirror inside was the size of my hand and cold as a debt.

I knew what it was before I turned it over. Handler Vess had shown me one like it six years ago, back when I still believed coercion had a shape you could see—a knife, a rope—and not just a sister's name spoken softly in an office that smelled of ink and lilies.

"You're supposed to be at the Door by now," Vess said, when the surface cleared.

Vess looked exactly as I remembered: gray at the temples, precise at the collar, eyes like a clerk tallying your sins against your assets. Behind her, the familiar blur of the palace map room. Somewhere behind *her*, I knew, would be the second mirror, and behind that one, a cell in the lower ward where a woman with our mother's laugh had spent six years being alive only because I kept being useful.

"Traffic," I said.

"Jude." She said my name the way she always did when we both knew I'd lied badly. "You killed a soldier at Wolfpass."

"He was going to kill Quentin."

"Yes. That's what I said. You *killed* him." She let it sit there between us, this fact I had been carrying around like a stone in my boot for weeks, pretending it didn't change the arithmetic of everything. "And then instead of delivering the gift-bearer as ordered, you vanished into a country that does not exist, with a person who is worth more to the crown alive than you will ever be worth dead. Do you understand the position you've put me in?"

"I've given you a great deal of practice explaining things to superiors," I said. "You're welcome."

Something flickered across her face—not amusement. Vess didn't amuse. But there was a tightening at the mouth that I recognized from years of watching her decide whether I was an asset having a bad week or a liability having a last one.

"I'm going to show you something," she said, "and then I'm going to offer you a bargain, and you are going to take it, because you are not stupid even though you have recently begun behaving as though you are."

She angled the mirror.

The cell behind her resolved. Not a cell—a room, actually. A nice room. They'd moved Elara somewhere comfortable since the last proof of life, which told me they'd known for months that I was wavering and had upgraded their leverage accordingly. My sister sat at a table with a cup of tea going cold in front of her, reading. She looked well. Her hair had grown back in from whatever they'd done to her in the early years—the part I try not to think about, the part I paid for with every name I ever handed Vess—and there was color in her face.

Then a man stepped into the frame behind her. Crown livery. He drew a knife with the unhurried motion of someone doing a task he'd been assigned, not a task he relished, which somehow made it worse, and laid the flat of the blade against my sister's throat.

Elara didn't scream. She went very still, the way you learn to go still when you've been a hostage for six years and screaming has never once helped. She looked directly into the mirror—at me, wherever I was, at the end of whatever chain connected us—and her expression said, with the entire eloquence of our shared childhood: *Don't you dare.*

"Don't," I said. My voice came out wrong. Flat. Smaller than I wanted.

"The blade stays where it is until sunset tomorrow," Vess said. "If the gift-bearer is delivered to the Door's threshold by crown hands before then, your sister walks out of that room and takes ship south, free, funded, forgiven. Every debt cleared. Your pardon in full. You could come home, Jude. Whatever sentiment you caught on the road, it will keep better in memory than in company. Sentiment always does."

"And if I don't deliver them?"

"Then at sunset tomorrow, Corporal Hale there stops holding the knife still."

I looked at my sister. She was mouthing something now, slow and deliberate, the way she'd mouthed words at me through the window of the debt-collector's cart when we were children and I'd run alongside it for four miles because letting go felt like dying. I couldn't make out the words. I didn't need to. It was either *don't* or *run* or *you idiot*, and all three were the same word in our language.

"Quentin would never let themselves be taken," I said. "They'd die first. You know that. So what you're actually offering me is: kill my sister's brother, murder the only person I've—" I stopped. Vess's eyebrows rose one precise millimeter, and I hated myself for giving her that. "The offer doesn't work. It's a bad offer. It's the kind of offer a bad handler makes."

"It's the kind of offer a handler makes when the alternative is worse," Vess said. "Do you want to hear the alternative? The alternative is that the crown gets the Door another way. We have scholars working on the seal right now, Jude. We have bloodlines we've been collecting since before you were born. We don't strictly need the gift-bearer. What we need is for the gift-bearer not to be standing on the far side of that door when it opens, and there are simpler ways to arrange that than delivery. Ways that leave nothing behind for the garden to remember."

She meant they'd kill Quentin at the threshold and use the body. Or use me—I'd stood close enough to the arch, touched the thorns, drunk enough of this country's air that maybe I counted as currency too. Maybe everyone who'd eaten the ruins' forgetting was currency. The thought arrived cold and complete, and I set it down carefully and did not pick it up again, because if I started inventorying what I might be worth to them, I'd start wondering what I was worth to anyone, and that way lay the kind of night I couldn't afford with a siege coming.

"So that's the choice," Vess said. "Your sister, or your thief. The crown, or a country of ghosts. Six years of your loyalty against—what? Four months? Five?" She leaned closer to the glass, and for a moment the clerk fell away and I saw the thing underneath, the thing I'd never once seen her admit to: she was tired. She'd been doing this a long time, and some part of her had noticed that the ledger never balanced, that you could collect names forever and never once collect enough. "I liked you, Jude. That was my mistake. Sentiment in the asset, sentiment in the handler. Someone should write a treatise."

"Someone should burn one," I said.

"Sunset tomorrow." She straightened. The tiredness sealed itself away behind the collar and the gray temples and the voice like a receipt. "Hale's arm is young. Young arms shake."

The mirror went dark.

I stood in the dead conservatory holding a piece of glass that weighed nothing and everything, and I did the thing I always did when the world asked me a question I couldn't answer: I found work. There was a bed of something like lavender gone woody and wild along the south wall, and I got down on my knees in the ash-soft soil and started cutting it back with the little knife I carried for grafting, and I cut and I cut, and the plant whispered its memories against my hands—*someone grew me for sleep-dreams, someone hung my bundles over a cradle, someone*—and after a while I realized I was crying, quietly, efficiently, the way you prune, and that the lavender had stopped whispering because grief is a kind of listening and plants know when they have your full attention.

Here is the truth I arrived at, kneeling in a dead country's garden with my hands full of someone else's memory of sleep:

There was no version of tomorrow in which I saved both of them. I had known this since Wolfpass, honestly. Since the moment I put a knife into a man wearing the same uniform I'd worn for six years, I had understood that the arithmetic was broken, that Vess had built a machine with no output that didn't grind someone I loved into meal. She'd built it deliberately. That was the design. That was always the design—you don't hold a leash to control a dog, you hold it so the dog learns that every direction except yours hurts.

But machines have assumptions. And Vess's machine assumed I would choose like I'd always chosen: alone, secretly, weighing one beloved life against another in the dark and telling no one, paying whatever the price was with counterfeit honesty while the real coin stayed hidden in my chest where it couldn't be taxed.

That was how I'd survived six years. That was also how I'd lost six years. Because a secret isn't a shield. A secret is just a wound you've agreed to stop cleaning, and it festers, and everything it touches goes septic, and eventually you are walking through the world looking whole and being rotten, and the rot gets into everything you touch, including—especially—the people you were keeping the secret to protect. Elara had spent six years in a gilded cell because I thought protecting her meant lying about her. Quentin had spent five months loving a lie because I thought protecting them meant lying about myself. I had lied my way into love and called it loyalty, and here was the bill, presented at dawn, itemized in my sister's throat.

So the machine assumed I'd choose alone.

I put the knife down. I wiped my face with the heel of my hand, which smelled of lavender and old grief, and I went to find Quentin.

* * *

They were at the inner wall, of course. Where else. Quentin spent most of these days with their palms flat against the living thorn hedge, feeding it small coins of memory—*the smell of the market at closing, the taste of cheap wine, the name of a customer's dog*—and the hedge drank and thickened and groaned, and each coin made Quentin a little lighter, a little vaguer, a person being slowly paid out of existence. I watched them do it and every time it was like watching someone bleed politely.

"They're getting bolder," Quentin said without turning. Their voice had developed a new texture lately, thinner at the edges, like cloth worn through at the elbow. "Third probe before noon. Soldiers at the ridge, testing the thorns with poles. The thorns took two poles and a pole-bearer's glove. I gave the hedge the memory of a hailstorm to teach it reach." A pause. "I don't remember whose hailstorm. Mine, possibly. Possibly no one's anymore."

"Quentin."

"That's ominous. You never use the full name unless it's ominous." They turned. Their face was still theirs—that was the mercy, the face held longest—but the eyes had begun to look at me the way you look at weather, cataloging rather than knowing. Another week of payments and they wouldn't know my name. Another month and they might not know their own. They were spending themselves to buy hours for both of us, and they hadn't once asked whether I thought it was worth it, because they already knew I'd say no and they'd decided my no was not the governing vote. I loved them for it. I resented them for it. Both at once, permanently, the way you carry a stone and a bird in the same hand.

"Vess contacted me," I said. "This morning. Mirror."

I saw it land. Quentin's jaw tightened, and their hands came up in the small defensive gesture they probably didn't know they made—fingers spread, palms out, the posture of someone who has spent twenty years expecting the world to ask for things back. "And?"

"My sister's alive."

The silence went on long enough that the hedge whispered into it. Then: "Alive." Not a question. A weight being tested.

"She's been alive the whole time. Six years. The sickbed story, the grave I visited—it was staged. She's their hostage. She's always been their hostage. That's the leash. That's what I've been paying with." I heard my own voice go strange, formal, like a confession being read from a document, and I supposed it was. "Every name I gave them. Every gift-bearer I confirmed. All of it was priced in her. And now Vess says sunset tomorrow: deliver you, or she dies."

Quentin looked at me for a long time. The wind moved through the hedge and the hedge murmured its grape-sized dreams, and somewhere up on the ridge a soldier shouted something about ropes and oil, and none of it mattered, none of it existed, there was only this: the two of us in a dead country's garden, and the question between us like a drawn blade.

"You're telling me," Quentin said slowly, "because?"

Because I should run. Because I should deliver you. Because I owe you the chance to bind and gag me and lock me somewhere defensible until sunset passes. I watched them assemble the possibilities, watch them brace for the worst one, and I realized—with a clarity that felt almost like the country's forgetting, a clean white space where dread used to live—that I was done choosing alone. Whatever happened next, it would happen in the open, or it wouldn't happen at all.

"Because I'm not deciding by myself this time," I said. "Not this one. This one's too big for secrets. I've kept secrets my whole life and every single one of them cost more than the truth would have. So here it is, all of it, in daylight: I love you. I betrayed you. My sister is a hostage and the price of her life is your life, and Vess thinks those are the only two weights on the scale because Vess has never met anyone who refused to weigh." My voice cracked on the last of it and I let it crack. Six years of level handler's diction, and it broke in a ruined garden, and it was the finest sound I'd ever made. "I don't know how to save her and you both. I've been trying to find the version where I can and it doesn't exist. So I'm putting the whole thing on the table. Yours and mine. Both our truths, in front of the Door, and let the damn thorns decide what they're worth. Let *her*—" I gestured wildly at the hedge, at the sleeping country, at whatever ancient attention lived in its roots. "Let this place decide. It eats truth. Fine. Then let it eat mine, all of it, and see what comes back."

Quentin stared at me. And then they did the last thing I expected: they laughed. A short, wrecked, astonished sound, half sob, the laugh of someone who has just been handed terrible news and recognized, underneath it, the shape of a gift.

"You absolute fool," they said, and their voice was wet, and they were smiling. "You beautiful, impossible fool. You waited until the siege to fall in love honestly."

"Habitually late. You knew that when you hired me to water your lies."

"The Door needs a truth freely given." Quentin's hands found mine, soil and all, and gripped hard. "One freely given, it told me. I assumed it meant mine. I've been rationing myself like a miser counting out my own past, and the whole time—" They shook their head. "Sunset tomorrow?"

"Sunset tomorrow."

"Then we have a day," Quentin said, "to figure out how to give everything away."

Behind us, deep in the hedge, something that had been asleep since the fall of a nation stirred, and the thorns began—very softly, like an instrument tuning—to sing.

Up on the ridge, the soldiers raised a cheer. They thought the singing was surrender.

It wasn't.
