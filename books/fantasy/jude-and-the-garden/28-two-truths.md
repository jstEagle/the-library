## 28. Two Truths

### Jude

The messenger-mirror hung in the air at the edge of the garden like a wound that hadn't decided to bleed.

Handler Vess looked out of it the way she always did — that patient, unhurried stare, the look of a woman who had never once needed to raise her voice because the world had learned to lower its own around her. Behind her shoulder I could see the ridge, and on the ridge, torches. Dozens of them. My old colleagues, come home to roost.

"Jude." She said my name like a key turning in a lock. "You've had your fun. The gardener's costume suits you, I'll grant. But it's time."

Behind me, Quentin stood with their hands pressed flat against the thorn arch, and the thorns were drinking from them. I could see it happening — a faint shimmer running up Quentin's arms like water climbing a stem, and with every swallow of light, Quentin swayed a little more, like a candle guttering.

"Sister's still breathing," Vess said, conversational now, almost kind. That was always the worst part. The kindness. "For now. The rebels hit the convoy two nights ago. We recovered her before they could. She asked about you, actually. She asked whether you'd forgotten her."

"I haven't forgotten her," I said. My voice came out steady, which surprised me. Somewhere in the last year I had apparently grown a spine, and nobody had sent word. "That's rather the whole problem, isn't it? I've never been allowed to forget anything. You made sure of that. Every debt itemized, every favor notarized. You built me out of ledgers, Vess."

"And ledgers can be balanced." She smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near her eyes; I don't think her eyes had windows anymore, just painted ones. "Deliver the memory-thief to me before the Door opens. Full pardon. Your sister walks free. A cottage, even — I hear you like gardens. You could have one of your own instead of tending other people's."

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out of me sideways, ugly and startled, and Quentin turned to look at me with an expression I'll be carrying to whatever grave I eventually get — half hope, half bracing for the blow.

Because here is the thing I had not yet said out loud, the thing I had confessed in fragments and near-misses and one disastrous snowfield silence: I had spent eleven years being exactly what Vess made me. And I had told myself, all that time, that coercion was a kind of innocence. That a man forced to lie was still, underneath it, honest. That the ledger wasn't mine.

Standing there, with my sister's life held in one of Vess's manicured hands and the only person I've ever loved bleeding memory into a wall of thorns behind me, I finally understood that this was the last lie. The load-bearing one. Take it out and everything I'd built came down.

"You want my answer," I said.

"I want the thief."

"No," I said. "You want me. The thief is just the excuse. You've wanted me since the day you found a gardener with gambling debts and a dying sister, because a man like that will do anything, and men who will do anything are worth ten soldiers. So here's my answer, Vess, and I'm going to give it properly. Out loud. All of it."

I walked away from the mirror.

Not toward the ridge. Toward the Door.

* * *

The arch of thorns stood maybe eight feet tall, grown out of a hedge that had no business being alive after thirty years of ash and forgetting, and it was sealed — braided shut like fingers laced together, patient as something that has waited decades and can wait decades more. Quentin had already paid it most of twenty years of stolen memories. It drank and drank and stayed shut, greedy as any crown I ever served.

"It needs more than theft returned," Quentin had said, days ago, when the sealed memory cracked open. "It wants a truth. Freely given. Not extracted, not traded. Given."

And I had known, even then, whose truth it wanted. Because I'd read the handler's files. Because I'd written half of them. The Door didn't open for payments. It opened for honesty, and honesty was the one currency I had hoarded my whole life without ever once spending.

So I put my hand against the thorns — carefully, though one of them pricked me anyway, and the prick felt less like a wound and more like a question — and I began to speak.

"My name is Jude Aravel," I said. "Son of Marisol Aravel, who died believing her son worked honestly in the palace gardens. Brother of Wren, who is alive, and who I thought was dead for six years because the crown told me so, and I believed them because the truth would have broken me and they needed me unbroken and usable."

The thorns stirred. A rustle, low, like wind through wheat. Like listening.

"I informed on people," I went on, and my voice did not stay steady for this part. "Eleven years. There was a woman named Odile Ferran who sold charms three streets over from Quentin's shop, and I told my handler she had the gift, and she was taken, and I don't know if she's alive. I told them about a boy in the tannery district who could talk to rats. He was sixteen. I told them about a midwife who eased pain with her hands. I sat in Vess's office every month and I gave her names, and I took my sister's medicine money, and I went home and slept."

Quentin had gone very still beside me. I didn't look at them. I couldn't. If I looked at them I would stop, and stopping was the one thing I could not do, because the thorns had begun to lean toward me, actually lean, the way plants lean toward light.

"I was sent to Quentin Joubert four years ago," I said. "Not to arrest them. To befriend them. To confirm the gift and mark the shop, and wait. And I confirmed it inside a week — I saw them take a headache out of a baker's daughter and I said nothing, and I reported everything, for four years. Four years of breakfasts. Four years of them teaching me which herbs keep aphids off roses. I knew their laugh before I knew their real name, and I gave the name to the crown anyway."

"That's enough—" Quentin started, and their voice cracked clean down the middle.

"It's not," I said. "It has never once been enough. That's the whole disease of me, Quentin. Nothing I did was ever enough, so I kept doing worse and calling it necessity." I pressed my palm harder against the thorns until blood threaded between the barbs. "I chose you over my handler at midnight outside your shop, and I told myself that balanced the books. It doesn't. I killed a soldier at the pass, and I told myself that bought back my soul. It doesn't. I burned my handler's letters and I called it freedom, and I was still hers, right down to the marrow, because everything I did — even the betrayals, even the rescue, even this — I did on her schedule, in her shadow, shaped by her leash. I have never in my adult life made a choice that wasn't owned by someone else. Until now."

The mirror was screaming somewhere behind me. Vess's voice, thin and furious, promising things, threatening things. Wren's name in her mouth like a blade. I let it wash past me. For the first time since I was nineteen years old and signing papers I didn't read, the voice of the crown sounded small to me. Far away. A dog barking in a yard I no longer lived in.

"Wren," I said, and here my voice finally broke, and I let it. "If you're somehow hearing this someday — I'm sorry. I love you. I am choosing not to buy your freedom with another person's life, because if I did it, I would be hers forever, and someday you'd look at me and see her looking out of my face, and neither of us could bear it. Live long. Be free of me. That's the last thing I own that's worth giving anyone."

I turned back to the thorns.

"And you," I said to the Door, to the dead country, to whatever slept inside the braid. "I know what you are. I know what was done to you, because I served the people who did it. Everything I am is made of the same rot that erased you. But I'm standing here anyway, telling you all of it, freely, asking nothing — not pardon, not absolution, just the door — and if that isn't enough, then take what's left of me. I'd rather be compost in an honest garden than a free man in hers."

Silence. Total, ringing silence, except for Vess's voice still shrilling out of the mirror and the wind coming down off the ridge smelling of torch pitch.

Then the thorns moved.

They didn't open — not yet — but they loosened, a slow unbraiding at the center of the arch, like fists unclenching, like a held breath beginning to release. Light came through the gap. Not torchlight. Green light. Growing light.

And beside me, Quentin made a sound I had never heard from them — grief and wonder tangled into one note — and reached for the arch, and I saw the color drain out of their face as the Door turned its attention, at last, from my truth to theirs.

### Quentin

Here is what nobody tells you about watching someone confess: it costs the listener too.

Jude spoke and every word landed in me like a stone dropped down a well — I heard each one hit, felt the depth it fell to. Eleven years. Names. Odile Ferran, whom I'd mourned as a neighbor lost to plague. A boy who talked to rats. A midwife. My name, given freely to the crown across four years of shared bread, and given again and again, monthly, itemized, while Jude learned how I take my tea.

I should have hated them. Some cold clerk in my chest was keeping the books, and the books said hate.

But I watched their hand bleed against the thorns and I watched the thorns drink the confession — actually drink it, the lies and shames going down into the green like rain into drought-cracked earth — and I understood, with the last clear corner of my mind, why the Door wanted truth and not payment. Payment runs out. Truth changes the payer. Jude was standing there becoming someone new in front of me, unbraiding themselves the way the arch was unbraiding, and there was nothing left in them to hide behind, and they kept talking anyway.

Honesty as sacrifice. Not honesty as strategy. They weren't confessing to open the Door. The Door was opening because they were confessing.

And then the green light shifted, and the arch's attention came around to me like a lighthouse beam, and I remembered — with the sick lurch of a floor giving way — that my account was not yet settled.

Because the thorns had drunk twenty years of small charms. Headaches and heartbreaks and one woman's memory of her wedding, all returned. But the Door had shown me the ledger's final page, there in the mountain dark, and the final page was not a theft.

It was a memory of my own. Sealed by my own childish hand, behind a door inside myself that I had locked so well I'd forgotten it existed. And the Door wanted it back. All of it. The reason the country fell. The reason I survived it.

Why a child had wept in this garden while cartographers unrolled their maps.

The gap in the thorns widened to the width of a hand, then a shoulder. Green light spilled through and touched my face, warm as a kitchen, warm as somewhere I had been before I could remember being anywhere.

Behind us, on the ridge, someone shouted an order, and the shouting stopped being far away.

"They're coming down," Jude said, turning, hand going to the knife they'd taken off a dead soldier — my death, their scar, the line they'd crossed for me and never once mentioned since.

"Let them come," I said, and stepped toward the arch, and put my hand among the thorns, and reached for the door inside myself that I had locked when I was seven years old and weeping in a garden. "There's one more debt on the books. Mine. And I think I'm finally ready to remember why."

The thorns closed gently around my wrist — not cruel, not kind, only certain — and began to pull.
