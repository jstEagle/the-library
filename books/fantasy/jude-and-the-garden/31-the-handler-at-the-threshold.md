## 31. The Handler at the Threshold

### JUDE

The Door began to open the way a fist begins to close — slowly, and with the unmistakable suggestion that when it finished, something was going to hurt.

I was standing three paces from Quentin when it happened. Three paces is nothing. Three paces is the distance between a person and everything they have ever wanted, and I watched the thorn arch shudder, and split, and show a sliver of light through its seams — light like the first hour of morning, like the inside of a pearl — and my whole chest went tight with a feeling I can only describe as terror wearing joy's coat.

Because behind us, on the ridge, someone screamed an order.

I had been listening for that sound for two days. You spend enough years around soldiers and you learn their voices the way a shepherd learns his dogs: the bark that means *hold*, the bark that means *loose*. This one meant *down* — crossbows going down onto the ridge stones, quarrels finding their rests.

"Quentin." My voice came out flat and calm, which surprised me. I'd expected it to shake. "It's time."

They turned from the Door. There was dirt on their face, and blood — not theirs, mostly — and they looked at me the way they had looked at me since the snow, since the confession, since I told them every rotten thing I'd done in the crown's name. Not forgiving yet. Not unforgiving either. Something in between, still being weighed, like fruit in a market-seller's palm.

"I know," Quentin said.

That was all. Two words, after everything. But Quentin had spent twenty years selling small charms to frightened people and had learned, better than anyone I ever met, how to say enormous things in the smallest possible packages.

We ran for the hedge.

The garden fought for us as we went. I felt it move — there's no other way to say it — the rose-thorn hedges leaning together over our heads, braiding themselves into a lattice, the old orchard trees dragging their branches down across the paths like a man pulling a blanket up against the cold. The country was dying; it had been dying for forty years, ever since it chose to erase itself rather than be eaten. But it could still spend what it had left. And it spent it on us.

Quarrels came off the ridge in a dark hissing rain. Most of them hit thorns. One of them didn't.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Let me tell it in order, because if I don't tell it in order, I will never tell it at all.

* * *

### QUENTIN

The Door did not open all at once. It opened the way a memory comes back — in pieces, each one costing more than the last.

The thorn arch had drunk Jude's confession two nights before, every betrayal spoken aloud under the ruined stars until Jude's voice cracked and kept going anyway, and the thorns had glowed faintly green afterward, the way a banked fire glows when you breathe on it. Then I had paid my own last installment: twenty years of small charms, returned one by one, until my pockets were empty of other people's lives and I stood inside myself like a house with the furniture gone. What remained was thin. A candle-end. Enough, maybe, to turn a key.

So I turned it.

I pressed my palm to the heart of the arch and gave it what was left — not memories this time, just the raw gift itself, the strange inherited hunger that had let me take what wasn't mine since before I understood it was stealing. It left me the way a fever breaks. One moment I was full of something, even if the something was only myself; the next moment there was a coolness where it had been, and a silence, and underneath the silence a question I would have to answer later, on the far side of everything:

*Who are you, now?*

Later. Later. The Door groaned, and the thorns drew back from the center like curtains, and through the gap I saw —

Light. Green-gold, moving light, like sunlight falling through water onto a field. And air coming through the crack that smelled of rain on warm stone, and cut grass, and something sweeter beneath it that I had no name for and wanted with my whole body.

"Oh," I said. Stupidly. Just: *oh.*

And then Jude said my name in the flat voice people use for bad news, and the ridge screamed, and the world came apart.

* * *

### JUDE

There were eleven of them. I counted while we ran, because counting is what I do instead of panicking; I learned it from my sister, who used to count stairs in the dark so she wouldn't be afraid of them. Eleven hunters in crown livery, grey and black, fanning out along the ridgeline with the ease of men who had done this many times to people who were not lucky enough to have a garden fighting on their side.

Ten, once the first volley buried itself in the hedge.

Nine, when the old pear tree at the path's bend dropped a limb the size of a rowboat across the trail and two of them were too slow getting around it.

But nine was still too many, and the Door was slow, and I understood — with the terrible clarity that comes exactly once in a life, if you're unlucky, or twice, if you're me — that we were going to lose. That the arithmetic was simple and the answer was no.

Vessey came down off the ridge himself.

I should explain Vessey. Handler Vessey, third chair of the Office of Gifts, a small neat man with small neat hands and a voice like a clerk reading inventory. He had owned me for eleven years. He had held my sister's debt the way other men hold a knife — loosely, comfortably, showing it off at parties. He had sat across from me in a hundred safehouses and asked, in that inventory voice, *what did the charm-seller do today, Jude, what did they touch, who came and went,* and I had answered him, God help me, for years, and he had never once raised his voice, because he had never once needed to.

He walked down through his own crossbow fire like a man crossing a courtyard to complain about a delivery.

"Jude," he said, when we were thirty feet apart. He didn't shout. He never shouted. "You're standing on crown business. Step aside."

"The crown's business ended," I said, "when your quarrel missed."

"It won't miss twice." He glanced past me at Quentin, at the opening Door, and something moved behind his face that I had never seen there before, and it took me a moment to recognize it because it was so ordinary: greed. Plain, human greed. A door to an erased country, a magic older than the throne, and the man who brought it home would be worth more than ten handlers. Worth a title. Maybe worth the sister thrown in free.

"You'll hang," he said. "Both of you. Or — " the inventory voice, the reasonable voice, the voice I had obeyed for eleven years — "you bring them to me, Jude, and I remember that you were mine, and useful, and the girl goes free regardless. You know I keep my accounts."

I did know. That was the horror of Vessey; he really did keep his accounts. Every kindness entered in the ledger, every debt carried forward, interest accruing. He had taught me that loyalty was bookkeeping.

He had taught me very well. Which is why I knew exactly which line item he'd never expect me to default on.

"No," I said.

And I stepped between him and the Door.

* * *

### QUENTIN

I want it recorded — somewhere, anywhere, even if no one ever reads it, even if the wind takes it — that I did not freeze.

People think of the giftless as helpless. I had thought it myself, those first minutes after the Door drank the last of me: *now I'm nothing, now I'm just a person.* But I had been a person for twenty years in a city that executed my kind, and being a person teaches you things magic never does. It teaches you where the heavy things are. It teaches you to throw them.

There was a broken branch near the arch, thick as my wrist. I threw it at the nearest hunter's head and hit him, because twenty years of market work gives you a good arm, and then I picked up a stone and threw that too, and then I stopped having ideas and started having Jude instead — Jude, stepping in front of me, Jude, squaring off against the neat little man with the neat little hands, and my whole chest went cold with a knowledge I couldn't argue with:

One of them was going to die here, and I knew which one I wanted it to be, and I knew which one it probably would be, and the distance between those two facts was the width of one gardener's stubborn back.

They closed. Vessey had a short blade — of course he did; the Office of Gifts armed its handlers with quiet weapons for quiet work — and Jude had a pruning hook, pulled from their belt, the tool they'd carried since the palace conservatory, curved and honest and made for cutting dead wood.

For a moment it was almost funny. Almost. Then it wasn't.

They were fast, both of them, and I was useless at thirty feet, screaming without knowing I was screaming, watching steel flicker in the Door's new light. Jude fought like a gardener — patient, economical, always working with the growth, turning Vessey's reach against him, herding him. Vessey fought like a ledger: precise, unemotional, waiting for the entry that balanced.

He found it. Of course he found it. He always kept his accounts.

Jude feinted high. Vessey didn't take the bait; he took the low line instead, the one Jude had left open on purpose, except it wasn't a trap, it was a mistake, because Vessey was faster than the last three men Jude had beaten and the blade slid past the pruning hook and went in under Jude's ribs, angled up, the way you'd gut a fish, the way you'd kill anything quickly and quietly and get back to your paperwork.

Jude looked down at it. Looked up. And smiled — actually smiled, blood at the corner of their mouth — and drove the pruning hook through Vessey's throat.

They fell together. They got up separately. Only one of them stayed up.

* * *

### JUDE

Pain, I discovered, is mostly bookkeeping too. The body enters its losses one by one: cold first, then wetness, then the deep wrong weight of a thing inside you that shouldn't be there. I sat down without deciding to. The ground was soft. Old gardens make good floors.

"Jude." Quentin was on their knees beside me, hands pressing hard below my ribs, and their face was doing something complicated, and I wanted to tell them it was fine, but honesty has been the theme of my last few weeks and I wasn't going to break streak now. "Stay with me. Look at me. Stay."

"I'm looking," I said. It was true. It was easy. They were worth looking at, especially furious and terrified, especially lit by the light of a door to another world.

"Stay," they said again, and their voice broke on it, and I thought: eleven years I served a crown for the sake of one person, and here is the other one, the one I'd have burned the crown for anyway, and isn't it just like me to schedule both debts to come due on the same afternoon.

Then there were horses.

I heard them before I believed them — hooves on the old paved road beyond the wall, many, coming fast — and Quentin heard them too, and we looked at each other with the same thought plain on our faces: *more of them, it's over, it was never going to be anything else.*

The riders came through the breach in the outer wall, and they weren't grey and black. They wore no livery at all, or every livery at once — scavenged mail, dyed cloaks, one man in a priest's coat with the symbols cut off it. Rebels. The cell from the border towns, the ones Jude had fed the crown's secrets to, the bridge burned behind us. Their leader swung down and I knew her face from wanted posters: Maren, the quarry-widow, the one with the price on her head that made mine look modest.

And behind her, half-falling out of the saddle, held upright by sheer fury —

"Sera," I said.

My sister looked older. She looked furious. She looked, against every account in every ledger I had ever been shown, alive and free and here, and she crossed the ruined garden floor in six strides and shoved Quentin's hands aside and put her own where they belonged, the way she'd pressed cloth to my scraped knees twenty-five years ago, and said, in the voice she used for counting stairs in the dark:

"Don't you dare. Don't you *dare*, Jude. I was collateral. I was *collateral*, do you understand what that means? It means you were never the hostage. It means I've spent eleven years being brave on your behalf and I am *not* spending your death too, so you will bleed quietly and live loudly, and that is an order."

"I don't take orders anymore," I said.

"Good," she said, tearing her sleeve for a bandage. "Neither do I. We're finally alike."

Maren's people spread through the garden with practiced speed, dragging Vessey's wounded up the slope for prisoner exchange, kicking quarrels into the flowerbeds, and one of them — a boy younger than I'd been when they recruited me — took one look at the Door and sat down hard in a bed of dead lavender and started laughing, or crying, possibly both.

The rebels could not follow us through. Maren said so herself, crouched by my shoulder while Sera worked: the Door had taken its payments, all of them, and it would open for exactly two, and it was closing already, the green light narrowing, the thorns creeping inward like a wound healing in reverse.

"Then stop talking about it," Sera said, "and go."

"Not without—" I started, and she pressed her hand flat over the bandage, over the wound, hard enough to make me gasp.

"Listen to me. You gave everything for this. Both of you. I watched the ledger from the other side, Jude — I know what you paid." Her voice cracked, just once, and reassembled itself. "Let me pay something back. Let me be the one who stays. Go plant something. Write me letters that never arrive. Be *happy*, damn you, it's the only revenge that works."

Behind her, Quentin stood at the threshold. The light was on their face. They were thinner somehow — diminished, nameable-no-longer, a house with the furniture gone — but they turned and looked back at me, and extended their hand across the closing dark.

No words. They didn't need any. Quentin had spent a lifetime saying enormous things in small packages, and this package was simply an open hand, offered by someone who owed me nothing and knew everything and had come, despite it all, to the same door I had.

I took it.

Sera's grip released mine finger by finger, deliberate, a ledger closing. The thorns brushed my shoulders like a blessing. Behind us the garden sighed — the whole erased country exhaling its last breath — and ahead of us the light opened like a morning that had been waiting forty years to happen.

We stepped through together.

* * *

### QUENTIN

The last thing I saw of that country was the garden letting go.

Not burning. Nothing so simple. It *thinned* — walls going translucent, sky going pale, the whole wounded nation relaxing at last after forty years of holding itself together by will alone. As the Door sealed behind us I felt it pass through the place where my gift used to live, a farewell brushing past like a stranger's sleeve in a crowd, and in that instant — one heartbeat, no more — something settled into me that wasn't a memory exactly. Smaller than a memory. Warmer.

A fragment. A woman's voice, singing, in a kitchen that smelled of this soil.

Then the Door became a thorn arch, then a hedge, then a rumor, then nothing at all, and we were through.

Ahead of us: a valley under a real sun, grass to the horizon, unmapped, unwitnessed, ours. Behind us: no border, no door, no way back for anyone, forever. Jude swayed against me — the wound, the blood loss, the sheer audacity of still being alive — and I got my shoulder under their arm and held on.

"Well," I said, because someone had to. "Now what?"

Jude looked out at all that green, and laughed, weakly, wincingly, wonderfully.

"Now," they said, "we plant."

But the valley was not empty. Far off, at the tree line, something moved — figures, small with distance, standing very still, watching the place where the Door had been.

Waiting for us.
