## 32. Through

I want to tell you I stepped through the Door with dignity. That I turned at the threshold like a heroine in one of my own old stories, hair streaming, chin lifted, and delivered some final word to the dying country that had made me and unmade me in equal measure.

I did not. I tripped on a thorn.

The archway of living briar stood open now — had been opening since Jude finished speaking their truth into it, petal by petal, thorn by reluctant thorn — and beyond it was not darkness, which I had expected, but light. Not sunlight. Something older than sunlight, the way a bell's last ring is older than the striking of it. It smelled, absurdly, of cut grass and rain that hadn't fallen yet.

"Quentin." Jude's hand found mine. Their palm was slick with blood — theirs, mine, the handler's; by that point in the night the accounting had gotten muddled. "It's time."

"I know."

"You keep saying you know things," they said, "and then you trip over them."

"That's knowing," I said. "Knowing is just falling with confidence."

Behind us, past the hedge, past the ruined palace garden where buildings remembered being green, the ridge line was black with soldiers. The handler's banners. The crown's wolves in their neat livery, come all this way for a memory-thief and a traitor gardener and whatever was left of a country no map would admit to. The thorns between us and them had been drinking Quentin all morning — my memories, spent like coins to make the garden bite. I could feel what was missing the way you feel a tooth gone: not pain exactly, but a strange smooth absence where something used to catch.

I tried to remember my mother's face and got her hands instead. I tried to remember the name of the market where I'd sold charms for twenty years and got the smell of copper and cheap incense and nothing else. I tried to remember why I'd ever been afraid of Jude and came up empty, which was either the gift thinning or forgiveness arriving early.

Probably both. They wear similar coats.

* * *

Here is what carrying almost nothing feels like, in case anyone asks, which no one will, because everyone who might ask is dissolving behind me:

It feels light. That's the obscene part. Twenty years of hoarded other people's Tuesdays, of borrowed first kisses and griefs I sold back at a markup, of the sealed room in my own skull where I'd locked the country's death — all of it returned, paid out, given back to the Door or the dead or the dirt. And what remained fit in two hands. Jude's hand. A splinter of thorn in my boot. The memory of rain, which I'd stolen from a dying woman on the Salt and Ash Road and which the Door had let me keep, perhaps as a courtesy, perhaps because even a door gets tired of taking.

And one more thing. Small. I'll get to it.

"Walk," Jude said. Not gently. Gently wasn't available anymore; gentleness had been spent along with everything else, somewhere around the third volley of crossbow bolts and the fourth wall of thorns. What Jude had left was the flat voice of a person who has decided, once and finally, and is holding the decision up with both arms. "Don't look back."

"You know I'm going to look back."

"I know you're going to look back," they admitted. "But walk while you do it."

We walked.

The Door's threshold was a seam in the world about as wide as a kitchen doorway, which felt right. All the enormous things in my life had come through openings that size. A shop door. A cell door. The gap under a door through which, at nine years old, I'd watched cartographers unroll maps of a place they intended to erase, guided there — though I hadn't known it until yesterday, until the Door made me pay the toll — by a weeping child who only wanted them to go away.

That child was me. That child had led the wolves to the garden. And the country, when it chose to die rather than be weaponized, had spared the weeping child anyway, because mercy is the kind of stupid that nations rarely manage but gardens sometimes do.

I looked back.

* * *

What I saw: the country finishing itself.

Not burning. Burning is quick and honest. This was slower — the way morning erases a dream, in patches, from the edges in. The palace behind us was already mostly suggestion. Its walls had stopped agreeing to be walls; where stone gave up, roses continued, standing in the shapes of windows, blooming out of the memory of lintels. The whole erased capital was reverting to what it had always secretly preferred to be, which was garden. Grass came up between flagstones like water rising in a flooded cellar. Somewhere a tower leaned, sighed, and became an orchard in mid-fall, apples where its bells had been, and I heard — I swear I heard, though sound may have been another thing the country was spending — a note hang in the air where the bell used to live.

Beyond the ruins, the hunters. The handler at their front, small with distance, gesturing, furious, a person shouting instructions to a landscape that had stopped taking orders. The thorns between them and us were still moving. Still hungry. But the garden was dying faster than it could fight, and I understood — with the part of me that was still me, the shrinking part — that every thorn defending us was a rose it couldn't afford to be.

They're spending themselves for us, I thought. The whole country. Last coin, last rose.

"Don't," Jude said quietly. They hadn't looked back at all. I don't know how they managed it. I think it cost them more than looking would have.

"They're paying our fare," I said.

"I know." Jude's grip tightened on my hand. Their knuckles were split from the fight at the threshold, from the messenger-mirror, from the handler's blade that had been meant for my ribs and had taken Jude's shoulder instead. The bandage was soaked through. I could smell the iron of it under the smell of rain-that-hadn't-fallen. "So don't watch. Let them spend it on someone who's walking forward."

Which was, I grant you, very good advice, and I took approximately none of it, because I stood in the threshold of a dying nation and watched it die, and if that was ingratitude then let ingratitude be entered into the record alongside theft and treason and childhood betrayal, my sins were getting crowded enough as it was.

The last thing the country showed me — whether it meant to or whether it simply forgot to hide it — was the hedge itself, seen from outside-in, the long spine of thorn running away toward mountains that were becoming foothills that were becoming rumor. Along its whole length, roses were opening white. Not fighting. Opening. Like a crowd releasing lanterns. Like a held breath finally let go after thirty years.

Then the light through the archway bent, the way light bends at the bottom of a well, and the country was behind glass, and then the glass was behind fog, and then—

* * *

Grass.

Real grass, wet, cold through my ruined boots. Under a sky with weather in it — actual clouds, doing actual work, none of this luminous timeless nothing from the far side of the Door. Wind. Wind with opinions. It pushed at us like a large friendly animal checking whether we'd fall over, and when we didn't, it pushed harder, delighted.

Jude sat down. Not gracefully — sat down the way a building sits down, all at once, with structural complaints. Their hand left mine for the first time in what felt like a season, and I discovered I'd been gripping hard enough to leave marks, and that I minded the marks leaving.

"We're through," Jude said. They said it the way you'd say "the fever's broken." Testing it. Not believing it yet.

"We're through."

"The country—"

"Gone. Or almost. Finishing." I sat down too, next to them, in the wet grass of a land that appeared on no map, because no map had ever thought to look here. My whole body had begun reporting in, item by item, like a clerk returning from war. Feet: ruined. Shoulder: bruised where the handler's second blow had landed. Heart: present. Astonishingly.

"And the Door?" Jude asked.

"Closed. Sealed. Dissolving with everything else." I said it plainly because plainly was all I had left; eloquence, like my gift, like my mother's face, like the name of the street I'd lived on for twenty years, had been paid out at the toll. "No one follows us. No one ever crosses again. Not the crown. Not refugees. Not us, if we wanted to, which—" I searched myself for wanting-to and found a smooth absence. "Huh. I can't remember if I wanted to."

"Do you?"

I considered. This took a while. Thinking without twenty years of other people's memories rattling around behind your eyes is like walking after carrying a heavy pack — you keep expecting the weight and stumbling into its absence.

"No," I said finally. "I don't think I do. I think I wanted out. I think I wanted—" and here I reached for the small thing, the one thing besides rain the Door had let me keep, and found it waiting, warm as a coal in a banked fire.

"What?" Jude said.

"A garden," I said.

* * *

Here is the small thing, since we've arrived at it. Hold it carefully; it's the only heirloom I own.

When I was — I'm guessing four, five; the memory has no edges, only light — a woman knelt with me in a garden in the country that no longer has a name, and she pressed a seed into my palm and folded my fingers shut over it, both her hands around my one small fist, and she said — I can hear her voice, I can't see her face, the Door took the face and left the voice, and I have decided not to be bitter about this, ask me again tomorrow —

She said: *"Keep this one. Not stolen. Given. Plant it where you end up."*

One seed. One memory of one seed, freely given, exempt from the accounting. The Door took everything taken and left everything given. Apparently even doors have theology.

I told Jude about it, sitting in the wet grass of the unmapped world, wind shoving us around like a playmate. I opened my fist in the telling, out of habit, out of ceremony, out of the novelist's old instinct for staging — and of course my hand was empty. Of course. Seeds don't survive thirty years in a memory. Hands don't survive them either, really. Everything I was had been spent to buy a doorway, and I was sitting in the aftermath with rain-memory and seed-voice and a partner bleeding through their bandage, and I laughed, which surprised us both.

"What?" Jude said again. They say "what" a lot. It's their best word.

"Nothing. Everything. I came through carrying almost nothing," I said, "and it turns out almost nothing weighs less than you'd think."

They looked at me for a long moment — with that expression I'd spent years cataloguing across market stalls and campfires and checkpoints, the expression I'd once suspected was surveillance and had learned, too late and exactly on time, was love wearing a disguise — and then they said, "You're going to be insufferable without your memories, aren't you. No history to keep you humble."

"Historically," I said, "I've never been humble."

"You can't know that. You can't remember."

"I choose to believe it. That's the whole trick now, isn't it? I don't have a past, so I get to pick one." I lay back in the grass. The sky went on being weathered and ordinary and impossibly, newly real. "I've decided I was witty. Devastatingly so. Everyone said so."

"Liar," Jude said fondly, and lay down beside me, and hissed when the shoulder met the ground, and I rolled toward them and put my hand over the bandage because that much I knew how to do without any gift at all: press where it hurts, stay where you're needed.

Behind us — east, south, whichever direction home had been — the horizon had a faint glow on it, the color of a country finishing its sentence. I didn't look directly. Some farewells are better done out of the corner of the eye, the way you watch a bird you don't want to startle off the fence.

"It's really gone," Jude said. "The Door. We can't ever—"

"No."

"And your sister—" I started, and stopped, because I'd been about to offer sympathy for a wound I only half remembered, and Jude shook their head against the grass.

"Rebels got her out. She's alive, she's free, she thinks I'm dead, and the letter I'd send wouldn't cross the border anyway." A pause. "I'll write it anyway. Someday. Letters you can't send are still letters."

"Writers' wisdom."

"Thieves' wisdom. Same guild."

* * *

We must have slept, because when I opened my eyes the glow on the horizon was gone and the light was the gray-gold of a world deciding whether to bother with morning. Jude was awake, watching me with the careful attention of a person watching a candle flame decide whether to gutter.

"How much is left?" they asked. No preamble. We'd earned each other directness the hard way, paid in full at the Door.

"In the account?" I took inventory. It didn't take long. "Rain. The seed. Fragments. Your face — don't look alarmed, I kept your face, it seemed load-bearing. A few market mornings. My mother's voice singing something I can't name. Pieces of the fall of a country, worn smooth like sea glass." I flexed my fingers. Nothing answered. No pull, no hunger, no sense of other people's Tuesdays waiting to be lifted. For twenty years my gift had hummed in me like a second heartbeat, quiet and constant, and now the silence where it had been was so complete it rang.

"Does it hurt?" Jude asked. "Losing it?"

I thought honestly. "You'd think it would. It doesn't. It's more like — you know when a tooth comes out? And your tongue keeps visiting the gap? It's that. Except the tooth was also, technically, me." I sat up. The wind had settled into something more companionable overnight, or I had. "Ask me tomorrow. Ask me in a year. I suspect hurt arrives on a schedule, like rent."

"And what do we do," Jude said, "with a thief who can't steal?"

I looked at them. Wet, bloody bandage, split knuckles, eyes that had chosen — publicly, catastrophically, at knifepoint and mirror-point and Door-point — to be mine instead of the crown's. A gardener. An exile. An honest person at last, which is the most expensive thing anyone can become.

"Well," I said. "There's a seed."

"There's a seed."

"Given, not stolen. Meant for planting. Wherever I ended up." I looked around at the unmapped grassland rolling away in every direction, at the distant line of hills nobody had ever surveyed, at the soil under my palms, which I pressed experimentally and found dark, cool, and — I swear this, and you may believe me or not, belief is free here — faintly, patiently expectant. Soil that had been waiting a long time for somebody with empty hands.

"So we plant it," I said.

"Just the one seed?"

"For now." I stood, and offered Jude my hand, and pulled them upright, and we stood there together in the middle of nowhere, which was now the middle of everywhere we had. Behind us, where a Door had been, there was only grass, bending in the wind like everything else, keeping no record. Ahead of us, the hills, and the light coming up over them, and — I squinted — was that smoke? A thread of it, thin and deliberate, rising from a fold in the valley below. Not wildfire smoke. Hearth smoke. Someone cooked breakfast in this unmapped world, which meant someone lived in it, which meant—

Jude saw it too. "Someone's down there."

"Someone's always down there," I said. "In my experience, that's where everyone is."

We started downhill, Jude limping slightly, me carrying nothing at all, which after a lifetime of carrying everything felt less like loss and more like traveling light for the first time. Halfway down the slope, my boot struck something soft, and I crouched, and dug with my fingers like the child in the garden-memory, and came up with a handful of dark earth — and in it, pale and patient and impossible, a seed.

I did not steal it. I checked.

I looked back once, at the top of the hill, at the unmarked grass where a country had finished dissolving sometime in the night. Then I closed my fist around the seed the way a woman once closed my own small fingers around another one, and I turned, and we walked down toward the smoke.
