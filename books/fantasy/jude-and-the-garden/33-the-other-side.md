## 33. The Other Side

I woke to the smell of green things growing, which was the first wrong thing, because the last thing I remembered was the taste of my own blood and the color of Quentin's face going white as the Door drank the light.

The second wrong thing was the ceiling. It was thatch. Living thatch, threaded through with something flowering, small white stars of it, and when I moved my head the flowers shivered like they'd noticed me noticing them.

The third wrong thing — and this is the one that made me laugh, which hurt, which made me laugh again, which is a trap I fall into more often than I'd like to admit — was that nothing hurt *wrong*. I'd taken a blade meant for someone else. I knew what that cost. I had priced it out in advance, in the way you do when you step between steel and a person you love: here is the arm, here is the rib, here is possibly everything. But what I felt instead was a deep, bandaged ache, the honest kind, the kind that means somebody sat up with you for days doing the boring work of keeping you alive. Somebody had cleaned the wound. Somebody had stitched it. Somebody had argued with fever on my behalf and won.

Somebody was humming.

I turned my head — carefully; the stitches had opinions about enthusiasm — and there was Quentin, sitting in a chair that some patient soul had dragged out into the light beside my bed, mending a shirt. My shirt. The one with the tear where the blade went in. They were sewing it with small, precise stitches, and they were humming something I didn't recognize, and they looked up at me with an expression I had spent six years learning every corner of and did not recognize at all.

It wasn't coldness. That's what frightened me. If they had looked at me with hatred, or even with the flat politeness people use on strangers, I would have known where I stood. Instead they looked at me the way you look at a book you've been told is very good and have only just begun — interested, open, holding their judgment loosely, waiting to see what kind of thing I turned out to be.

"Hello," Quentin said. "You're awake. Good. I was starting to talk to you, and you weren't answering, and I began to worry I'd invented you."

"You —" My voice came out like gravel in a tin cup. I tried again. "You don't remember me."

Something crossed their face. Not pain exactly. The place where pain would go if it lived there.

"No," they said, setting down the shirt with great care, as if it were an argument they were choosing not to have yet. "Should I?"

* * *

Here is what I learned over the next three days, mostly from a woman named Sefa who had come through the passes with us and turned out to be the only one among us who knew anything about wounds:

The country behind us was gone. Not conquered, not burned — *gone*, the way a word goes when everyone agrees to stop saying it. The rebels who'd freed my sister said the border simply stopped being crossable. Not guarded. Absent. Men sent east to find the pass came back reporting a valley that had never held a road, and maps drawn from memory contradicted each other so badly that the cartographers gave up and left the space blank, which, if you know anything about cartographers, tells you everything.

We had come through because we were paid for. That was Sefa's theory, anyway, delivered while she changed my dressings with hands rough as bark and gentle as rain. "That door wanted truths," she said. "You two paid in full. The rest of us just walked in your wake." She nodded at my shoulder. "You especially. Blood on the threshold. Old bargain, that. Doors like blood."

"I'd rather it hadn't been mine."

"Doors don't take requests." She tied off the bandage. "Be glad it took yours and not theirs. You'll heal. What they're spending, they don't get back."

*What they're spending.* I thought about that phrase a great deal in the days that followed, because Quentin was spending something, visibly, hourly, the way a candle spends itself, and there was nothing anyone could do but watch and keep the wick trimmed.

They remembered how to do things. That was the strange mercy of it. They could sew — those stitches on my shirt were better than mine ever were. They could cook, though they kept pausing mid-recipe with the look of a person following instructions from a voice that had gone quiet. They could read, though slowly, sounding out words sometimes, frowning at letters as if the letters owed them an explanation. What they couldn't do was say where they'd learned any of it. Their whole life had become a house where all the furniture remained but every portrait had been taken down off the walls, and they kept turning around expecting to see their own face looking back at them, and finding bare nails.

And me. Here is the thing I could not get past, the stone in my shoe, the splinter under the nail: they half-remembered me. Not nothing. Half.

"You're Jude," they said, the second morning, without being asked. We were sitting outside the house — a real house, timber and thatch, standing in a clearing that some earlier hand had clearly cleared for exactly this purpose, as if the land itself had been waiting for tenants — and they were watching the light come up over hills no map had ever troubled itself to draw. "I know that the way I know the stitching. In the hands, not the head. When you came through the door carrying me, I think — I think I was angry at you. Was I angry at you?"

"Yes," I said.

"Why?"

"That's a long story."

"I have," Quentin said, gesturing at the unmapped hills, the unclaimed sky, the entire blank page of their own history, "an extraordinary amount of time."

So I started telling it. And here is where I have to be honest, because honesty is the only currency I have left that isn't counterfeit: I told it badly. Not on purpose. I told it the way you tell a story you've rehearsed as confession until it has worn smooth, all its edges rounded, its teeth pulled. I gave them the shape of it — informant, coercion, the hunt, the betrayal, the choice at the door — but I gave it in summary, the way you'd report to a handler, because some part of me still flinched from the full weight of it landing on someone who no longer had the context to absorb the blow.

Quentin listened with their chin on their knees, and at the end they were quiet for a long time, and then they said, "You're leaving things out."

"I'm not —"

"You are. You tell it like a ledger. Line items. Debts incurred, debts repaid. Nobody lives inside a ledger." They looked at me, and their eyes were Quentin's eyes — I'd have known them anywhere, I'd have known them blind, I'd have known them dead — but the person behind them was reading me fresh, and being read fresh by the person you love most is either a gift or a horror depending on the hour. "Tell me the parts that embarrassed you to say."

"The parts that embarrassed me are the parts that hurt you."

"Well," Quentin said, with a ghost of the old dryness surfacing through the newness like a fish rising in dark water, "then we'll both suffer. It's traditional."

* * *

On the fourth day I could stand, and on the fifth I could walk to the stream, and on the sixth I did something unforgivable, which was to check whether the crown had followed us.

Old habits. That's all it was. Six years of counting exits, noting faces, tracking who watched whom — you don't shed that like a coat; it grows into the skin. I found myself at the tree line scanning the ridges for livery, cataloguing sightlines, calculating how long it would take soldiers to reach the clearing if they came over the western pass, and I caught myself doing it and felt sick, actually physically sick, bent double by the stream with my stitches pulling.

Because there was nothing to count. No livery. No roads worth the name. No lists, no registrations, no criers, no handlers, no mirrors carrying messages from men with soft voices. The crown ended somewhere behind us, on the far side of a door that no longer existed, and I was free of it in the way a man is free of a debt when the lender's whole country sinks into the sea.

Free. And my first instinct upon waking each morning was still to check the window for watchers.

Sefa found me there. She crouched beside me, looked at my face, and said nothing for a while, which is a skill more people should cultivate.

"They'll ask you to be the guard," she said finally. "People like you. People who spent years watching. Everyone else will want to forget there's anything to guard against, and you won't be able to, and eventually they'll make it your job."

"Is that a warning or a prophecy?"

"It's both. Everything true is." She stood, joints cracking like green wood in a fire. "Just make sure somebody makes you put it down sometimes. Watching is a hunger. It eats the watcher last, but it eats them completely."

* * *

That evening Quentin cooked. Fish, caught from the stream by means I never saw and they couldn't explain — "my hands knew where to stand," they said, baffled and faintly delighted, "the rest of me was just along for the ride" — and we ate by the fire while the unfamiliar constellations came out overhead, arranged in patterns no astronomer of the old country had ever charted, and I thought: nobody owns this sky. Nobody has named these stars. Somewhere a court astrologer is drawing charts of a heaven that ends at our backs.

"Ask me something," Quentin said suddenly.

"What?"

"Anything. Something only I would know. I want to see what's still in here." They tapped their temple. "It's like reaching into a pocket after the tailor's been at it. Some seams hold. Most don't. I want to know which is which."

I thought about it. I could have been cruel — there were questions that would have cut, questions about the market, about the customers whose memories they'd carried for twenty years, about the child's handwriting on the wall in the ruined capital. I could have been kind in the shallow way, asked about the weather in the city, the price of charms.

Instead I asked: "What did you call the garden? Your garden. Behind the shop."

Quentin went still.

For a long moment I thought I'd broken something. Then their mouth moved around a word, testing it, tasting it, and what came out was barely a whisper: "*Vesper.*"

"Yes," I said, and my throat closed, because I hadn't known they'd told me the name — I'd found it once, written on a plant marker in their handwriting, and never mentioned it, hoarding it like the thief I was trained to be. "Yes, that's right."

"*Vesper.*" They said it again, firmer. "Evening. Evening prayer. Evening star." They laughed — a startled, cracked-open sound. "I named my garden after the end of the day. After the part where the light goes and you light candles instead. Oh, whoever I was, I was *sentimental*."

"You were," I agreed. "Appallingly."

"And you loved them anyway."

It wasn't a question, but it needed an answer, and the answer stuck in my chest like a fishbone. Because here was the arithmetic of it, laid out plain: the person I loved was mostly gone. What sat across the fire from me was Quentin-shaped and Quentin-voiced, with Quentin's hands and Quentin's dry wit surfacing in fragments, but the twenty years of small accumulated knowledge that had made them *them* — the private jokes, the old griefs, the memory of me specifically, of us, of every firelit almost-confession — that had gone through the door as payment, and doors do not give change.

And I loved them anyway. That was the terrible, simple truth. I looked at this person who was meeting me for the first time, who had every reason to weigh my confessions coldly and none of the warm debt of shared history to soften the scale, and I found that I did not love them less. I loved them the way you love a garden after winter: not despite the loss, but with the loss built into the loving, part of the season, part of the patience required.

"Yes," I said. "I did. I do."

Quentin studied me across the fire for a long moment. The flames moved in their eyes.

"You look at me like I'm a grave," they said quietly. "Like you're paying respects. Stop that. I'm not dead. I'm *new*." A pause. "Or possibly just very edited. Either way, graves are for the finished, and I haven't decided what I am yet."

"What do you want to be?"

They looked out at the dark hills, at the unnamed stars, at the whole unwritten world spread out before us like fresh paper, and something in their face shifted — the grief not lifting, but making room beside itself for something else.

"Hungry," they said. "Is there more fish?"

* * *

Later — much later, when the fire had burned down to the red hour and the unfamiliar stars had wheeled halfway across their unnamed courses — Quentin spoke again, from their bedroll, in the voice people use when they've been rehearsing a sentence for hours and have finally decided to spend it.

"Jude. The name Vesper. Where did you actually learn it? You hesitated. You know things about me you shouldn't know if I never told you. You hesitate like a man deciding which lie fits best, and then you tell the truth anyway, which is worse, because it means the truth needs managing." A rustle as they turned toward me. In the ember-light their eyes were open. "I may not remember my life. But I remember how to read people. Apparently that seam held."

I lay in the dark and considered lying, and then considered that lying to this person had been the sin of my whole former life, and that if I was going to build anything on this side of the door it would have to be built without that particular brick.

"I found it written on a marker in your greenhouse," I said. "Years ago. Before I understood what it meant to you. I never told you I knew, because —" I exhaled. "Because knowing things people don't know you know was how I survived. It was the one skill the crown gave me that I kept using after I stopped working for them. I spied on you even when I wasn't spying on you. Even loving you, I collected you. Like evidence."

The silence went on long enough that I counted my own heartbeats in it. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen.

Then Quentin said, "Show me tomorrow. The way you collect things. Show me what you notice, how you notice it. All of it." And there — there it was, surfacing whole through the newness, unmistakable, the old Quentin smile with its edge of appetite and mischief: "If I can't remember my own life, I may as well get a second one, and I intend to be thoroughly researched this time. Consider yourself assigned."

"Asigned to what?"

"To being known on purpose." They rolled over. "Goodnight, Jude. Whoever you are. Whoever I am. We'll compare notes in the morning."

I lay awake a long while after that, listening to their breathing slow, watching the stranger-stars burn, and I thought about what Sefa had said — *watching is a hunger* — and about how maybe she was wrong, or maybe there was more than one kind of watching, and the kind that feeds what it watches might be the only cure for the kind that consumes it.

Tomorrow I would show them how I saw. Tomorrow they would begin deciding, stitch by stitch, who they were going to be — and I realized, with a feeling too large and too frightening to be entirely joy, that I would get to watch. Not as a spy. As a witness.

Somewhere out in the dark, in the black soil of a land that had never been mapped, something that had waited decades under the earth was beginning, very quietly, to stir.
