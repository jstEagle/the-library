## 34. Nameless Again

I woke to the smell of bread and the absence of myself.

That sounds dramatic, I know. I was a dramatist once, in a small way — a charm-seller learns to perform, to make a copper ring sound like destiny. But this wasn't performance. This was waking in a room I didn't recognize, under a blanket woven by hands I couldn't name, with a gap where my life should have been, and the gap had edges. It wasn't fog. Fog you can wait out. This was a room with the furniture removed and the floorboards swept.

I lay still for a long time and took inventory.

My body knew things. My fingers knew how to braid thread into a charm-loop before my mind caught up and asked them what they thought they were doing. My tongue held three languages, two of which I could not have learned anywhere on any map I'd ever seen, except that I couldn't have told you which maps I'd seen. There was a scar on my left palm shaped like a crescent moon, and when I pressed it, something old and green stirred behind my eyes and then politely declined to be remembered.

So: not empty. Furnished, but the inventory list was missing.

The door opened and Jude came in carrying a basin of water, and here is the thing I want you to understand, because it took me most of that morning to understand it myself: looking at Jude hurt in a way I couldn't source. Not the hurt of a stranger's face. The hurt of a song you know every note of but can't remember where you first heard it — or worse, of a person whose face your heart recognizes and files correctly while your head stands there holding a blank form.

"You're awake," Jude said. Carefully. The way you'd speak to a bird you'd rescued that might bolt through an open window. "How much — do you know who I am?"

I considered lying. Old habit. A charm-seller lies the way other people breathe; it isn't even deception after twenty years, it's upholstery. And I found, searching myself for the lie I'd naturally reach for, that I didn't know which lie would work, because I didn't know what Jude needed the lie to be. That was the strangest part of all of it. I had apparently known this person well enough to lie to them expertly, and now I stood at the counter of my own self with nothing on the shelves.

"No," I said. "And yes. You're Jude."

Relief went through their face like water through cracked earth. Then guilt, right behind it, arriving on schedule. I didn't know why yet. I want to be clear about that. At that moment I didn't know about the informing, the handler, the years of it — none of that came back, because none of it ever would come back, not as memory. What I knew was that Jude looked like someone standing in the wreckage of something they'd caused, and that some stubborn cellar-dwelling part of me wanted to comfort them anyway, and that I distrusted that impulse completely, since I had no idea what it was founded on.

"Quentin," Jude said, and then stopped, and I watched them decide something. "You said — before, you said you'd tell me if you forgot."

"Did I." I turned the basin of water between my hands. It was warm. Someone had warmed it for me. Someone had been warming water for days, probably, waiting. "Well. Here I am, telling you."

* * *

Here is what I had left, and I made a list of it that afternoon, sitting in the doorway of the house we'd been given — given, note, by strangers, in a country whose language my mouth half-knew, and I'll get to the horror of that later — because if you can't have a past, you can at least have a ledger. Ledgers were apparently my native tongue. Some things survive the fire.

One: I could do magic. Barely. A residue, a film of it, like the scent of smoke in a coat. When I held thread too long it hummed. When I touched the soil outside the door, the soil seemed to lean toward me slightly, the way a dog leans toward a hand that has fed it before. Whatever I had been, I had spent nearly all of it buying our passage, and what remained was pocket change.

Two: I loved Jude. This was not a deduction. It was a fact I found installed in myself like plumbing, functional, load-bearing, with no documentation of its installation. I watched Jude carry firewood and my chest did a thing. I heard Jude laugh at something the neighbor's child said and I had to look away. Fine. Good, even. But love without history is a strange animal — it kept trying to pay debts I couldn't find records of.

Three: I had done something terrible. I don't know how I knew this either. Call it the scar tissue principle: the wound heals, the shape remains. Every time I reached back toward my own childhood, my hand flinched away from something before I even saw it. Somewhere in me was a locked room, and I had locked it, and I had done it young, and I had done it on purpose.

Four: my name was Quentin. Or rather — everyone called me Quentin, and when I said the word to myself it fit the way a borrowed coat fits. Warm enough. Not mine.

Jude spent those first days being very careful around me, and I let them, because I was busy grieving, though I hadn't admitted yet that grief was what it was. I kept telling myself it was convalescence. Very practical. Rest, broth, gentle exercise. But grief is patient, and one evening it cornered me at the garden fence while I was watching the light go amber over hills that no map contained anymore.

Here is what grief said: *you will never remember your mother's voice.* I didn't know if I'd had a mother. I didn't know her name or her face or whether she'd sung. But I knew — the way you know a bruise is there by pressing near it — that somewhere in the drowned rooms of me there had been a voice, singing, and that I had paid it away along with everything else so that a door would open, and it was gone, gone, gone, and no amount of wishing would grow it back.

I gripped the fence rail and did not cry, mostly because I was too furious to spare the moisture. Because here was the joke, the beautiful crown-crafted joke of it: I had been a thief of memories. I had built a life, a livelihood, a whole sly careful architecture of survival on taking other people's pasts from them gently, for coin, with their trembling permission. And now the bill had come due in full, collected by a door made of thorns, and I understood — finally, personally, down in the marrow — exactly what I had sold all those customers in the market. Not trinkets. Amputations. Neatly done, kindly priced, and forever.

"I'm sorry," said Jude, who had appeared beside me at some point, because Jude always appeared beside me at some point, another piece of installed machinery I couldn't account for. "You keep making that face."

"What face?"

"The one where you're arguing with someone who isn't there."

"That's just my face," I said. "It came standard."

They laughed, and the laugh caught on something halfway out, and I realized they were crying, quietly, the way people cry when they've decided they don't have the right to but the body disagrees.

"Hey," I said. "Whatever it is you're sorry for — and I'm going to guess, from the architecture of your face, that it's a large whatever — you should know that I've taken inventory. I know that I loved you before I lost my past. The loving survived the shipping. So whatever else got broken in transit, that's load-bearing and it held."

"That's not—" They wiped their face with the heel of their hand, angry at themselves. "You don't know what you're forgiving."

"I'm not forgiving anything. I have nothing to forgive *with*. Forgiveness requires a memory of the crime, and my crime ledger is blank on both sides." I turned to face them properly. "But here's what I do have. I have today. I have a working pair of hands and a fence that needs mending and a chest that does a thing when you laugh. That's not nothing. That might, if I'm careful, be everything."

Jude looked at me for a long time. There was something almost frightened in it — hope is frightening, when you've trained yourself out of expecting any — and I recognized the fright, distantly, the way you recognize a family resemblance in a stranger.

"And if it comes back?" they said. "If some morning you wake up and it's all there?"

"Then I'll have twice as much," I said. "And I'll deal with it then. But I'm not going to spend the meantime standing in an empty house waiting for the furniture to return. Empty houses get sold that way." I looked out at the hills, at the unmapped light. "I've decided something."

"Oh?"

"I'm going to introduce myself to you. Properly. As if for the first time." I stuck out my hand, palm up, scar-side skyward, crescent moon and all. "Hello. My name is Quentin. I don't know my surname, my birthday, or my mother's face, but I can braid a charm-loop in eleven seconds, I argue with people who aren't there, and I appear to be constitutionally incapable of walking past a fence post that isn't straight. I am told I love you. I intend to verify this claim independently, at my leisure, starting immediately."

Jude stared at my hand. Then at me. Then — slowly, like ice going out of a river — they started to smile, and it was a wrecked, wet, magnificent thing.

"You're insane," they said.

"Almost certainly. It may be all I have left. Shake the hand, gardener."

They shook it. Their grip was dirt-rough and careful, and my chest did the thing again, and I filed that under *verified.*

"Jude," they said, formal as a courtier, absurd in the mud-stained shirt. "Gardener. Former liar, current — I'm working on current. I know your favorite food is honeyed pears and you hate the smell of tallow and you talk in your sleep in a language I've never heard anywhere else, and I will tell you everything I remember about you whenever you ask, even the parts that make you furious, because you made me promise once—"

"I made you promise?" Something prickled up my arms. "What did I make you promise?"

"To tell you the truth," Jude said, "even when it costs. Even when it costs *me*, especially then. You said a person who won't pay for truth hasn't got any."

"Well," I said, after a moment. "Sounds like I was insufferable."

"Completely."

"Good. Then some of me survived intact." I squeezed their hand once and let go, and turned back to the fence, and the hills, and the enormous unlit country of my remaining life. "Then here are my terms, Jude. We start from today. Today is year one. Everything before this is prologue, and the prologue is lost, and I am done standing at the edge of the loss wailing into it. Grief is a landlord I've decided to evict. He can leave his forwarding address."

* * *

That night I dreamed of rain on green leaves, and woke with wet eyes and dry cheeks, and could not have said whose memory it was — mine, the dead country's, nobody's. I lay listening to Jude breathe in the next room and performed my new nightly ritual: building myself, plank by plank, out of choice instead of history.

My name is Quentin. I choose it. Let the word be mine by occupation rather than birth, the way a gardener names a plant by tending it.

I love Jude. Verified daily, pending further review.

I was a thief. I am reformed by force, which is the only reform that ever works.

There is a singing voice somewhere in the drowned rooms, and I will not dig for it, but I will leave a lamp burning by the door in case it finds its way home.

And I am — this was the newest plank, laid only last week, and it creaked under my weight still — I am the sort of person who stays. Who mends fences. Who plants things. Whose past is a closed book whose contents, whatever they were, produced a person capable of opening a door with love and truth, and who therefore cannot have been entirely rotten, whatever terrible thing flinches away from my reaching hand.

I was still lying there, mortar-setting these planks, when I heard it.

Footsteps on the path outside. More than one set. And then a knock — polite, deliberate, unfamiliar — and a voice calling softly in the half-language my tongue knew, the one from nowhere on any map.

"Quentin of the Garden," the voice said. "We've walked a long way. We hear you can open doors."

I sat up in the dark, heart hammering, and beside the bed my hands — my chosen, occupied, eleven-second-charm-loop hands — had already begun, without asking me, to hum.
