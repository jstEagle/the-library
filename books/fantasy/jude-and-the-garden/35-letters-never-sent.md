## 35. Letters Never Sent

The first letter I ever wrote my sister was an apology. The second was a lie. Most of the ones since have been some combination of the two, which I suppose makes them honest in aggregate, the way a ledger full of errors can still balance.

This one is neither apology nor lie. It is a list of seeds.

*Dear Wren,* I write, and then sit for a long while with the pen hovering, because there is no postal service between here and the capital, no courier, no merchant caravan willing to risk the passes that closed behind us like water closing over a stone. There is nothing between this page and her except distance, mountains, the crown's displeasure, and the general indifference of the world to the correspondence of two people it has already finished using.

I write anyway. It's become a habit, the way prayer is a habit for people who aren't sure anyone's listening. You do it not because it works but because not doing it would mean admitting something you're not ready to admit.

*Dear Wren. The frost came early this year, or late — Quentin says I use the wrong word for the season because I learned weather in a conservatory where the seasons were suggestions rather than laws. They're probably right. They're usually right, which is insufferable in a person who has forgotten most of their own life.*

The ink is cheap and grey. Everything here is cheap and grey — the paper, the candle, the little house at the edge of the settlement with its walls of fieldstone and its roof that Quentin re-thatched themselves last autumn, badly, with the grim competence of someone who refuses on principle to be bad at anything without first being furious about it. But the ink holds. That's more than I can say for most things I've written.

There were letters before these. Letters I wrote in the capital, on crown paper, with crown ink, in my own hand that the crown owned as thoroughly as it owned my sister's debt. Those letters went somewhere. These don't go anywhere at all, and somehow that makes them truer.

I used to think that was backwards. I used to think a thing was only real if it reached its destination.

*You'd hate it here,* I tell her. *Or you'd love it. I genuinely cannot predict which, and that's the closest thing to joy I've felt all week — that after twenty years of knowing exactly what you would say before you said it, I now have no idea. You might complain about the mud until spring. You might be knee-deep in the garden by dawn. Both are equally you.*

* * *

Here is what I know about writing letters that will never be sent: you stop performing almost immediately.

In the old days, every word I put down was weighed. When I reported to Handler Vess — when I sat in that grey room with its grey lamp and gave up pieces of other people's lives in exchange for pieces of my sister's — I learned to speak in a particular register. Careful. Useful. Stripped of anything that might make me look like a person with attachments beyond the approved ones. Even the omissions had to be engineered. I didn't just hide Quentin's kindnesses from Vess; I hid them in ways that wouldn't leave a shape, because Vess could read shapes the way a tracker reads spoor.

A letter to no one requires none of that. There's no handler reading over my shoulder. No archive where it'll be filed and cross-referenced against my future betrayals. Just me, a guttering candle, and the enormous liberty of saying things into a void that cannot hold them against me.

So I say them.

*I killed a man at Greywater Pass.* I've never written that down before. I told Quentin eventually, in the way you tell someone something by letting them watch your face while you don't say it. But here it is, in ink, where it can't be unsaid: I killed a man, and I have thought about him nearly every day since, and what I think about most is not his face but his boots. Good boots. The kind I could never afford during the debt years. He had a family probably. He certainly had boots.

*I'm not telling you this so you'll forgive me. I'm telling you because for fifteen years everything I did, I did for you, and you never got to know what "for you" cost, and it seems unfair that I should carry the whole weight alone out of some misplaced instinct to protect you from my sins. You're a grown woman. You survived me. You can survive the accounting.*

The candle gutters. Outside, wind moves through the settlement — forty-odd houses now, maybe fifty, built by people who trickled through the passes over two years, drawn by rumors of a garden past the edge of every map. Refugees from the hunt, mostly. A few deserters. One former tax collector who weeps easily and chops wood like he's apologizing to each log individually.

None of them know what I was. Or they suspect, the way people suspect weather coming. Fugitives develop a nose for other people's guilt; it's practically a trade skill. But nobody asks, because everyone here is running from something they did or something done to them, and the arithmetic of survival doesn't require confession. It only requires that you show up at dawn with your hands ready to work.

Quentin knows, of course. Quentin knows everything and remembers almost none of their own part in it, which is either the cruelest joke the universe has played on us or its single act of mercy. Some mornings I wake before them and watch their face in the grey light — open, unguarded, a face with no history behind the eyes — and I feel something I can only describe as vertigo made of tenderness. I loved them when they remembered everything. I love them now that they remember nothing. I have never once been certain those are the same love.

*Quentin sends their love,* I write, and then cross it out, because Quentin does not send love; Quentin sends opinions. *Quentin says you'd better not still be putting honey in your tea because honey is theft from bees and bees have suffered enough. They also spent an hour yesterday explaining to Marta's twins why worms are the true aristocracy of the garden. They were extremely persuasive. The twins now curtsy to the compost heap.*

*They don't remember you. I've told them about you — the debt, the bargain, the way you used to whistle through the gap in your teeth — but it slides off. Not unkindly. It's like telling stories to a river. They listen, they nod, they say "she sounds brave," and then they go back to whatever they were doing, and the story belongs to the day and not to them. I used to grieve that. I'm learning not to. Grief for someone else's loss is just another way of keeping them.*

* * *

Last night Quentin caught me at it.

I was at the table, third letter of the month, and they came in from checking the cold frames smelling of woodsmoke and wet earth, and instead of going to bed they pulled a chair around backward and sat watching me write with their chin on the chair-back like a child at a shop window.

"You do that a lot," they said.

"I do."

"Letters to your sister."

"Yes."

"They never go anywhere."

"No."

Quentin considered this with the seriousness they bring to everything, even — especially — the small things. Their gift is gone; I watched it drain out of them at the Door, watched them pay it away memory by memory until what was left was just a person, ordinary and finite. But some habits of mind survived the paying. They still look at the world like it's a text in a language they mostly lost and are determined to relearn.

"So they're not really letters," Quentin said at last. "They're a diary with an addressee."

"That's a horrible way to put it."

"It's accurate, though." They reached over and stole a corner of my bread, which is how they show affection now, plain and physical, no charm-work required. "Why not keep a diary?"

"Because a diary is for yourself. This is for her."

"But she never reads it."

"Not yet."

Quentin looked at me for a long moment. "Jude. The border is sealed. The Door is gone. We watched the whole country dissolve behind us. There is no road back that isn't two thousand miles of mountains and a crown that wants your head."

"I know."

"And yet."

"And yet," I agreed.

And Quentin — who once, in another life, sold charms to frightened people and took their memories as payment and called it fair trade — nodded slowly, as though I'd said something wise, and said, "Good. Keep doing it," and went to bed, and left me staring at the page wondering when exactly I had become the sentimental one.

But that's the thing. That's the whole thing, if I'm honest, which this page obligates me to be: I write because the alternative is letting go, and I am not ready to let go, and perhaps I will never be ready, and perhaps that isn't weakness. Perhaps love that persists without guarantee — without reply, without receipt, without any evidence it lands anywhere softer than a mountain snowfield — is the only kind worth the name. Everything else is transaction. I spent fifteen years in transactions. I know how they end.

* * *

Today's letter, the seed list:

*Wren — I found seeds. You won't believe what kind. Remember the palace conservatory, the glass house on the south terrace where they kept the exotic stock under lock? I stole from it. Well — "stole." I liberated. I had pockets full of the crown's botanical property the night we ran, and I didn't even know why until spring, when Quentin turned a bed of ordinary-looking dirt and up came flowers that grow nowhere on this side of any map. Blue-flamed poppies. Roses that smell like rain on hot stone. Something Quentin calls a "memory pea" that may or may not actually do anything besides taste excellent.*

*They're from the erased country, Wren. From the gardens of the place I helped destroy. And they slept for decades in a royal greenhouse, waiting, and now they're coming up in our soil like they'd merely stepped out for a moment and were pleased to find the party still going.*

*If you were here — when you're here, I refuse to write "if" anymore — you would laugh at me. I plant them in rows. Rows! Like a soldier forming ranks. Quentin plants theirs in drifts and tangles and "wherever they please," and argues that order is a form of fear. I argue that order is a form of hope. We have not resolved this. We may never resolve this. I mention it so you understand that some things survive even the end of the world: arguments about gardening.*

I set down the pen and flex my hand. My handwriting has changed since the capital days. Then, it was tight and small, trained into me by clerks who believed character revealed itself in penmanship and wanted no character revealed. Now it slants. Now it sprawls toward the margins. Quentin says my letters look like a person walking downhill with good news.

There's a knock at the door — two raps, pause, one rap. Marta. She stands in the cold with her shawl pulled up and her breath steaming, holding a basket.

"Heard you were at the writing again," she says. "Brought bread. The dark loaf. I know you pretend to prefer the white."

"I do prefer the white."

"Mm." She hands over the basket anyway. Her eyes go past me to the table, to the pages stacked there — dozens of them now, months of them, tied in bundles with string. "That's a lot of letters for one sister."

"She's a lot of sister."

Marta laughs, then doesn't. She looks at the bundles again, and I watch her arrive at the truth I stopped hiding long ago: that none of these have ever been posted. That there is nowhere to post them. That I stand at a table every few nights and address words to a woman across an uncrossable distance, the way sailors' wives addressed letters to drowned men.

"You should read one aloud sometime," Marta says. "At the fire. People like hearing about other places. Even places that don't exist anymore." She pauses. "*Especially* places that don't exist anymore."

After she goes I sit with the bread cooling in its basket and the candle burning low, and I think about what she said, and I think about the stack of letters, and I have an idea so simple it embarrasses me.

Not a reading. Not yet. Something smaller.

I start a new page.

*Dear Wren. Today I did something strange: I let someone else hear a piece of one of these letters. Marta, our neighbor — you'd like her, she lies about bread preferences the way you lie about being fine — stood in the doorway and listened to me describe your teeth-whistle, your terrible tea, your habit of finishing other people's sentences, and she laughed in the right places. Which means that tonight, in a house sixty miles from mine, a woman she's never met became slightly real. You exist here now, Wren. In fragments. In secondhand anecdotes. You're becoming one of the stories this settlement tells itself, and I didn't plan that, and I can't undo it, and I find I don't want to.*

*Keep existing like that. Persistently. Without guarantee. It's the family specialty.*

I seal nothing, address nothing, send nothing. I tie the new pages to the bundle and set the bundle in the chest under the floorboard, next to the others — an archive with an audience of one, someday, maybe, if the world softens, if the passes open, if miracles are the kind of thing that happen twice.

The candle dies on its own. I don't relight it. Through the window, the settlement is dark except for one light — Quentin's workroom, where they're up late as always, doing something to the seed trays and humming a tune they don't know the origin of. Sometimes I catch the melody and my chest goes tight, because I heard it once before, years ago, drifting from the royal conservatory at dusk. A song from the erased country, carried in Quentin's blood if not their memory, hummed now over seeds from home.

Tomorrow I'll tell them about the idea Marta planted — the reading, the fire, the letters spoken aloud into the dark where anyone who needs them can reach out and take one. Tomorrow I'll argue with Quentin about rows versus drifts, and lose, and enjoy losing.

Tonight I bank the fire, and touch the floorboard over the chest once, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping shoulder.

Sleep well, Wren. I'm still writing.
