## 36. First Planting

### Jude

I have planted a great many gardens in my life, but I have never before planted one out of spite, hope, and a coat pocket full of contraband.

The seeds had been sitting in my pack for the better part of a year. I want that on the record, because Quentin refuses to believe it. When we finally turned out everything we owned onto a blanket — which did not take long, since what we owned fit on a blanket — there was a small leather pouch among my things that I genuinely could not account for.

"What's this?" I'd asked, holding it up.

Quentin went very still, the way they do when memory brushes past them like a stranger in a crowd. Then they crossed the room, took the pouch, opened it, and laughed. It was not a comfortable laugh. It was the laugh of a person who has just found their own handwriting on a wall they don't remember writing on, which — for the record — has happened to them twice now.

"Conservatory stock," they said. "Palace seal on the pouch. Jude. You absolute criminal."

"I didn't steal anything."

"You carried stolen goods across an international border that doesn't exist anymore."

"I carried them *unknowingly*," I said, "which is the legal term for it, and also the only defense I have ever had in my life that actually worked."

They weighed the little pouch in their palm. Inside, I knew, were seed packets — dozens of them, wax-sealed, labeled in a hand neither of us could read. The labels weren't in any language of ours. They were in the script of the erased country, the one that curled at the edges like frost forming in reverse. Quentin could read some of it now. Bits came back to them when they weren't looking, the way a splinter works its way toward the surface of the skin.

"Silverbell," they read, tracing one label. "Winterheart plum. Weeping amberroot." A pause. "These are from home."

"Your home or mine?"

"Both," they said, and handed the pouch back to me, which I understood — eventually, I understand most things eventually — was a kind of trust. They wanted me to be the one who carried them. Or possibly they didn't trust themselves not to lose them. With Quentin those two motives have always been indistinguishable.

So: spring. Our second spring on this side of the border no one else will cross. The ground had been thawing for weeks, and I had spent those weeks doing the thing I know how to do, which is look at soil and argue with it. The valley we'd chosen — well, that the garden had chosen for us, if you believe Quentin, and by now I mostly do — had good dark loam along the stream, stone further up, a south-facing slope that caught the morning sun like a cupped hand.

"We break ground today," I announced over breakfast, which is to say over the last of the winter apples and the first of the nettle tea.

Quentin looked up from mending a net. "You're very cheerful for someone about to spend all day being defeated by rocks."

"I'm not going to be defeated by rocks."

"Everyone is defeated by rocks. Rocks are patient. Rocks have never once been in a hurry and they've outlasted everyone who ever swore at them." They set down the net. "But fine. Yes. Today."

That was another thing about Quentin I had learned to read: the way *yes* sat differently in their mouth depending on what it cost. Some yeses cost them nothing. This one cost something — I could see it, the small flinch of a person agreeing to put living things into the ground when they have spent two years learning how thoroughly the ground can take things away. But they said it anyway. That's the whole of Quentin Joubert, if you want me to compress them into a sentence: they say the expensive yes anyway.

* * *

### Quentin

Here is what nobody tells you about losing your memory and then building a life in its absence: the body remembers more than the mind does, and the body is a terrible liar.

When Jude turned the first spade of earth, I knew the smell before I knew why I knew it. Wet loam, cold underneath, sweet on top — and something in my chest unlocked like a door I'd been leaning against without noticing. I had to stand very still for a moment and let it pass through me.

"You all right?" Jude asked, not stopping. Jude rarely stops working to ask if you're all right; they ask *while* continuing, so that you have the option of pretending you didn't hear, which I consider the single kindest conversational habit anyone has ever developed.

"Fine," I said. "The dirt smells right."

"Dirt smelling right is generally considered the minimum standard for dirt."

"It smells right *here*. Like—" I hunted for it. Most of my memories come back as fragments now, broken crockery, edges sharp where they broke. But this one arrived whole, absurdly whole, a single perfect teacup amid the wreckage: *my mother kneeling in soil exactly this color, saying, press it firm but don't crush it, the seed needs to know it's held.* "Like someone loved things here."

Jude stopped then. Looked at me across the turned earth with that expression they get — half tenderness, half alarm, the face of a man watching someone walk close to an edge. "Do you remember her?"

"No," I said honestly. "I remember a sentence she might have said. It's not the same thing. But it's more than I had last month."

They nodded slowly and went back to digging, and I picked up the second spade, and we broke ground on a garden that neither of us would live to see finished, which is the only honest way to plant anything.

* * *

### Jude

By midmorning we had the beds laid out — or rather, I had laid them out and Quentin had redesigned three of them on aesthetic grounds I couldn't follow, involving "sightlines" and "the way the light comes off the water at evening," which is the sort of thing a person says only if they once lived somewhere beautiful and their hands remember the geometry even when their head doesn't. I let them redesign the beds. I have learned when to lose arguments. It's most of marriage, in my experience, though we haven't discussed whether that's what this is. We plant together and sleep under one roof and argue about rocks. There are villages full of people who would call it marriage and be done with it, and I find I no longer object to the word, only to the ceremony, which we couldn't have anyway, seeing as we're exiles from two countries, one of which no longer exists.

The seeds were the day's real business.

We opened the conservatory pouch on a flat stone, and I'll admit my hands shook a little. These seeds had crossed a border inside a fugitive's coat. They had sat through a winter in a pack that also contained, at various times, a stolen knife, a forged pass, and half a wheel of cheese I am still embarrassed about. If they germinated, it meant the erased country wasn't entirely erased. If they rotted —

"They won't rot," Quentin said quietly.

"You can't know that."

"I can't know *anything*, that's rather my defining feature these days. But these seeds have been waiting thirty years in a sealed packet. Seeds like that aren't dead, Jude. They're patient. They learned patience from a country that erased itself rather than be used. Patience is practically their native tongue."

I looked at them. Sometimes Quentin says things like that — flat, certain, arriving from nowhere — and I've stopped asking how they know. The gift thinned when they paid it out at the Door, but thin isn't gone. What remains isn't magic, exactly. It's more like weather sense. A pressure behind the eyes. They feel what living things intend.

"Fine," I said. "Show me how your country plants."

And they did.

* * *

### Quentin

I don't know how my country plants. I want to be clear about that. What I know is what my hands did, which is a different thing entirely.

Jude dug the holes the sensible way — evenly spaced, measured by knuckle-widths, tidy as a palace bed, because Jude cannot help being tidy any more than I can help being suspicious. And when I crouched down with the first seed in my palm, my hands refused it. They turned it over. They held it against my wrist, the way you'd check a fever. Then they pressed it into the soil sideways — *sideways*, flat, like laying down a tired child — and firmed the earth over it with the heel of my hand, gently, twice, and left it shallower than any gardener in the crown's conservatory would have dared.

"That's wrong," Jude said, in the tone of a man watching someone hang a picture with nails through the frame.

"Probably."

"The packet says quarter-inch depth, vertical orientation—"

"The packet was written by people who studied these plants after they'd already forgotten them. My hands were taught by someone who hadn't forgotten yet." I looked down at my own fingers like they belonged to a stranger, which, increasingly, they do — a stranger with better manners than me and a much longer memory. "Sideways. Firm but don't crush it. The seed needs to know it's held."

Jude went quiet. I felt them looking at me and pretended not to notice, because if I noticed I would start crying into the seedbed, and I have a reputation in this household as the emotionally armored one, and I protect my reputation fiercely, it's all I have left.

"Say it again," they said.

"Firm but don't—"

"Not that part. Before it."

"*Press it firm but don't crush it,*" I said, and my voice cracked in the middle like green wood bending, "the seed needs to know it's held."

Jude set down their trowel. They reached over and took my hand — the seed-dirty one — and pressed it, firm but gentle, between both of theirs, and held it there.

"There," they said. "Now it knows."

I maintain to this day that I did not cry. There was simply a great deal of moisture in the air that morning, which happens, in valleys, in spring. Jude has agreed never to contradict me on this point and I consider it the foundation of our entire arrangement.

* * *

### Jude

We planted until the light went long and gold.

Silverbell along the stream bank, sideways in the wet earth, because Quentin's hands insisted. Winterheart plum up on the slope where the frost pools last and leaves first — "it wants to suffer a little," Quentin said, with the authority of someone quoting a grandmother they cannot remember having. Weeping amberroot by the door, though we have no door yet, only a doorway-shaped gap in the wall of the ruin we've claimed, and Quentin said, "It'll grow into it, doors are just gaps that got ambitious."

By the time we'd finished, my back had filed a formal complaint and my fingernails were permanently brown, and I stood up at dusk and looked down the slope at forty-odd patches of bare turned earth that looked, frankly, like nothing. Mud with ambitions.

"Don't judge it yet," Quentin said beside me.

"I'm not judging. I'm despairing. Different thing entirely."

"Mm. Give it nine days."

"Nine days. Very precise."

"I'm always precise about things I can't possibly know. It's my best quality."

We walked back up to the house — house is generous, it's a roofed ruin with a hearth and opinions — and I slept the sleep of the honestly exhausted, which no amount of lying awake over betrayals can match, and if I dreamed, I dreamed of green, which I mention only because I never used to dream of green.

On the ninth morning I went down to check the beds alone, before Quentin woke, because I wanted to be able to tell them either way, and I confess I wanted to be the bearer of good news if there was any. Two years of being a bearer of bad news will make a person hungry for the other kind.

The silverbell bed had split open overnight.

Not weeds — I know weeds the way I know my own sins, intimately and by name. These were pale green hooks, curved like questions, dozens of them, breaking the crust of the soil along the exact line of the row. Thirty years dormant in a sealed packet, carried across a dead country in a smuggler's coat, and they had come up in a single night as if they'd only been waiting for permission. As if they'd heard the word *held* and taken it seriously.

I stood in the cold morning with my breath steaming and my heart hammering like a boy's, and I did the undignified thing. I ran up the hill shouting Quentin's name like a fool, and they came to the doorway barefoot and alarmed, hand already going to the knife, because two years of being hunted doesn't wash out of a person —

"It's up," I said. "Quentin. It's *up*."

And I watched the alarm leave their face and something else arrive in its place, something I hadn't seen on them since before the Door, something young. They came down the hill barefoot through the frost, which I scolded them for and which they ignored, and they knelt at the edge of the bed, and they didn't touch a single sprout. Just looked. Their lips moved, counting.

"Thirty-one," they said. "There were forty seeds in that packet."

"Some didn't take. Thirty years is a long—"

"No." They shook their head slowly. "Some are still deciding. Watch. By tomorrow it'll be forty." And they smiled — not the wry armor-plated smile, the other one, the rare one, the one I'd have crossed the border twice more to see. "My mother used to say that. About the garden. That it never finished arriving, it only kept deciding to."

I knelt beside them in the frost and put my arm around their shoulders, and we watched mud with ambitions do the impossible, quietly, at its own pace, needing nothing from us but to be held.

* * *

### Quentin

Here is what I know now, in the season when things decide:

Hope is not a feeling. I thought it was, for years — a warmth, a lift, a thing you either had or lacked, like an ear for music. But kneeling in that bed with Jude's arm around me, watching thirty-one green hooks reach for a sun they'd never seen, I understood it's closer to what the seeds were doing. Hope is a decision made in the dark, in the sealed packet, in the years when nothing confirms it. You press yourself into the earth sideways. You wait to be held. And when the warmth finally comes, you split yourself open without being asked twice.

The country did that too, in the end. That's the part I gave back at the Door, and the part that stays with me precisely because I no longer own it: they chose the soil. They chose to wait. Everyone thinks erasure was a death. It was a planting.

We worked all that day and the next, Jude and I, and the beds filled, forty out of forty, exactly as my borrowed certainties promised. Winterheart plum, weeping amberroot, a dozen more whose names come to my mouth in the old script before they come in ours. By the end of the week the slope wore a faint green haze, like a rumor of itself, and I caught Jude standing at the window at dusk looking down at it with an expression I recognized from mirrors.

"What are you thinking?" I asked him.

"That we should tell someone," he said. Then he frowned at himself, as if the words had escaped before inspection. "Isn't that strange? For two years the whole point was that no one would ever find this place. And now I keep thinking — there must be others like us. Others who ran. Others who lost a country, or lost themselves, or both. And they're out there in the passes with nowhere to go, and we have a roof and a stream and a garden that's *deciding*—"

He stopped. He looked at me carefully, the way you look at someone before asking them for something large.

"And you'd share it," I said. Not a question. I'd known this was coming for a week; I'd felt it the way I now feel what living things intend, which includes, apparently, Jude.

"You wouldn't have to," he said quickly. "After everything — you paid more than anyone for this peace. No one would think less—"

"Jude." I looked out at the green haze on the slope, at the country that had erased itself rather than hoard itself, at the seeds that had waited thirty years to be generous. "I spent twenty years taking small things from people to survive. Everything I have now, I have because I gave the rest back. Do you really think I'm going to sit behind a wall counting sprouts while refugees freeze in the passes?"

He grinned. It's an unfair grin. I've told him so.

"So it's decided," he said.

"It's decided," I said. "We'll need another bed by the stream, and you'll complain about the rocks—"

"Everyone is defeated by rocks."

"—and we'll need to fix the roof, and figure out how much a person eats, and where they'll sleep, and—" I paused, because something had drifted up the valley on the morning air. Woodsmoke. Faint, wrong, and coming from the direction of the pass. Nobody burns a fire in the high passes in early spring unless they have no roof to burn it under.

Jude had gone still beside me, reading my face the way he reads weather.

"Quentin," he said slowly. "How far away is that smoke?"

"Close," I said. "A day. Maybe less." And then, because hope is a decision and I had apparently already made it: "Get the kettle on. It looks like we're about to have visitors."
