## 37. Visitors

The first one came in March, half-frozen and full of lies.

I found her at the edge of the bean rows at dawn, face-down in the cold mud like something the mountain had coughed up. For a long moment I stood there with the watering can in my hand, doing the arithmetic every fugitive learns: how far to the door, how heavy is the poker, is it worth waking Jude. Then she groaned, and the groan had a human shape to it, and I put down the can.

She was maybe nineteen. Frostbitten ears, boots worn through at the soles, a city accent I knew the way you know your own name — the market vowels of home, flattened by distance and hunger. When I got her inside and put broth in front of her, she looked at me with the particular wariness of someone who has been hunted long enough to make suspicion a religion.

"Where am I?" she said.

"Past the edge of the map," I said. "Roughly."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one that's true." I pushed the bread closer. "Eat first. Geography after."

She ate like a person who had learned not to trust meals that might be taken away — fast, hunched over the bowl, eyes up between swallows. And while she ate, she watched me the way people used to watch me across a charm-seller's stall, back when I had a stall and charms and a self to sell them from. Looking for the trick. Looking for the hook.

There wasn't one. That was the strange part. There was no hook left in me. The Door had taken it, along with my gift, my name, and twenty years of small thefts. What remained was a woman with dirt under her nails and a kettle, and apparently that was enough to keep a girl alive through a mountain night.

"My name's Odile," she said finally, when the bowl was empty and her shoulders had come down from around her ears. "They said — in the passes, there were rumors. People talked about a garden. Past the border no one crosses. They said two people lived there, and one of them—" She stopped. Squinted at me. "You look familiar."

"Do I?"

"Like someone from the proclamations." Her voice dropped. "The memory-thief. Quentin Joubert. They had your face on every wall in the capital for a year. Reward big enough to buy a street."

I considered several answers. The true one seemed simplest, and besides, I have always been a poor liar; it's why I went into theft instead.

"The reward's out of date," I said. "I'm told the thief died crossing the border. What's left is just a gardener with a bad memory."

Odile stared at me for a long time. Then, slowly, she began to laugh — the cracked, disbelieving laugh of someone who has run too far and slept too little, and it turned into crying halfway through, and I sat with her while she did both, because I have learned that this is most of hospitality: sitting still while another person comes apart, and not flinching.

* * *

Jude came back from the high pasture at noon and found us shelling beans on the step like we'd known each other for years, which, in the way of refugees, we already did.

"You found one," Jude said, and there was no surprise in it. There hadn't been surprise in Jude since the day the sister arrived over the pass with rebel escorts and a scar that mapped her whole left side. Since then Jude had simply added a third bowl to the shelf and waited, the way gardeners wait, for things to grow toward them.

"She found me," I said. "Collapsed in the beans."

"The beans forgive you," Jude said to Odile solemnly. "Mostly. They're sensitive about being trampled."

Odile looked between us — at Jude's sunburnt calm, at the scarred hands, at the easy orbit we'd fallen into without ever discussing it — and I watched her do the same arithmetic I'd done at dawn. Danger or refuge. Hook or hand.

"It's real, then," she said. "The rumor. You can just... be here. No lists. No registration. Nobody reads your name off a paper and then—"

"Nobody reads anything here," Jude said. "We can't even read the old boundary stones. Half of them are in a language that isn't a language anymore."

That night Odile slept in the loft under three blankets, and I lay awake listening to her breathe and thought about how strange it is that the thing I spent twenty years doing in secret — taking care of strangers, quietly, for payment — is now the whole of what I am, done in the open, for nothing.

* * *

The second visitor came in April, and the third in May, and by midsummer they were coming in twos and threes, and the rumor had grown teeth and legs and walked on its own.

They came over the passes the way water finds cracks — a tinker whose gift was knowing when metal would break, until he'd fixed everything he touched and someone noticed; a widow whose dead husband's voice had never stopped giving her advice, useful advice, advice about weather and horses and which neighbors to distrust; twin boys from the salt flats who could find any lost thing, and whose own father had sold that fact to the crown for a pardon he didn't live to use. They arrived frostbitten, bullet-grazed, hollow-eyed, carrying their gifts like stolen goods wrapped in rags.

And I met each one at the gate with bread and a kettle, and each one looked at me the way Odile had, waiting for the price.

Because that's what they expected. That's what the world had taught them: every kindness is a ledger. Every open door has a toll behind it. They had all, in one way or another, been commodities — informants, hostages, specimens, bait — and they arrived at my gate braced to be bought.

"There's no charge," I told each of them, and watched disbelief move across their faces like weather. "There's work, if you want it. There's soup, whether or not you want the work. Those are separate ledgers, and neither one keeps score against the other."

The tinker — Bram, with forearms like dock rope — asked me outright, on his third evening, sitting on my step with his hands around a mug: "What do you get out of it?"

It was such a direct question that I gave it a direct answer.

"I used to take memories for a living," I said. "Small ones. A first kiss, a mother's lullaby, the smell of a specific rain. Willing customers, mostly — they paid in coin and never knew what else they'd paid. I told myself it was harmless. I told myself a lot of things." I turned the mug in my hands. "Then I spent a year giving it all back, and what I learned is that a life isn't made of its big moments. It's made of soup given to strangers. Someone sitting with you while you cry. Bread that appears without being asked for. That's the actual currency, and I counterfeited it for twenty years, and now I'm trying to pay in the real thing."

Bram looked at me for a while. "That's either the most honest thing anyone's ever said to me or the best con I've ever heard."

"Yes," I said. "Both, probably. Have more soup."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about losing a magical gift: you keep reaching for it.

For months after the Door, my hands kept making the old motions — the small turning gesture, the almost-invisible lean toward another person's temple, the instinctive reading of a stranger's grief like a shopkeeper reading a scale. And every time, there was nothing. Just my own thoughts, my own single thread of a mind, knocking around in my skull like one coin in an empty purse.

At first it felt like drowning. Then it felt like quiet. Now, some days, it feels like something I don't have a word for yet.

Because here's the thing I discovered, out here past the edge of every map: when a refugee walks into your yard, you don't actually need magic to know what's wrong with them. You need eyes. The girl who won't sit facing the door. The man who flinches at birdsong because criers whistled before executions. The twins who hoard food under their pallets and are ashamed of it. Grief writes itself on people in letters large enough to read from across a garden bed, if you've spent twenty years learning the script.

I had. Twenty years of reading strangers for profit. It turns out the skill survives the gift, like a scar survives the wound that made it.

So I couldn't lift a bad memory out of the widow anymore, couldn't take the sound of her husband's voice and give her back her silence. But I could plant marigolds with her while she talked to him, and let her talk, and not once suggest she should be over it by now. I couldn't ease the twins' nightmares. But I could teach them grafting, which turns out to be a kind of spell anyway — you cut a living thing and bind it to another living thing and both survive, stronger, and the boys took to it like it was religion.

And Odile — Odile, who had crossed three ranges alone at seventeen with nothing but a rumor — Odile turned out to have a gift too, though not the kind that gets you executed. She could make anything grow. Not magically. Just stubbornly, patiently, with the ferocious attention of someone who had never in her life owned a single square foot of ground. Give her a cutting and a week and she'd have ten. By August she had doubled our kitchen garden and was eyeing the south slope with an expression I recognized from mirrors: a person planning to stay.

"You could go farther," I told her one evening, because I felt it was only fair. "Some people do. Keep moving until the crown forgets your face."

"The crown doesn't forget faces," Odile said. "It files them." She snapped a bean pod neatly off its stem. "Besides. I heard the thief died at the border. I'm staying where the gardening's good."

* * *

In September, a man came through the pass who made me understand, finally, what this place was becoming.

He was older than the others — fifty, maybe, with gray in his beard and the careful blankness of a career bureaucrat. He arrived at dusk, stood at the gate for a long moment looking at the settlement — the new cabins, the terraced slope, the smoke of six hearths — and I saw him cataloguing it all with a clerk's eye, counting roofs, estimating stores.

"We should talk inside," I said.

"No," he said. "Out here. Briefly." He glanced at the mountains behind him. "I was a registrar. Twenty-two years. Names onto lists, lists onto warrants. In the last year of the hunt, I processed four hundred registrations." His jaw worked. "Do you know what happens to a registration? Nothing good. Ever. I understood that somewhere around number two hundred, and I kept stamping anyway, because the alternative was joining the list myself." He looked at me, and behind the blankness there was a man holding himself together with procedure. "Your name was on my desk once. Quentin Joubert. Priority file. I'm the one who sent the hunters to the pass where you were nearly caught."

The yard went very quiet. Somewhere behind me, Odile had stopped working. I could feel the others gathering at the edges of the lamplight, drawn by the tone of him, the way animals gather when something is wrong.

"Why are you telling me this?" I said.

"Because I need to say it somewhere it will be believed, and punished, and then somehow survived." He straightened like a man at a sentencing. "I have no gift. I have no skills but paperwork. I know how the crown tracks people, and I can teach everyone here how to be untrackable, and I'll do it for as long as you'll keep me, and I'll never ask you to forgive—"

"Bram," I called, without turning around. "Set another place at supper."

The registrar blinked.

"You'll sleep in the east cabin," I said. "There's a hole in the roof that Bram keeps meaning to fix. You'll fix it tomorrow, because guilt is excellent motivation for roofing. And in the morning you'll start teaching us what you know, and in the spring you'll learn to plant beans, and in five years you'll be the sort of old man who complains about slugs, and none of it will erase what you did, and all of it will matter anyway. That's how it works here. That's the whole system. Supper, work, and time."

He stared at me. "Just like that."

"Just like that. I'm told I used to be frightening. I find I don't miss it."

* * *

Later that night, Jude found me on the step, watching the settlement lights — seven of them now, strung along the slope like a constellation someone had dropped and decided to leave where it fell.

"You gave away our last empty cabin," Jude said, sitting down beside me.

"He sent hunters to kill us."

"Mm. And now he'll fix Bram's roof." Jude's shoulder settled warm against mine. "You know what I keep thinking? I spent years believing loyalty was a leash. That everyone's devotion had a hand on the other end, holding it. Handler, crown, debt." A pause. "Then I watch you feed a man who tried to have you killed, and I think — no. That's not a leash. That's just a person, choosing."

"Don't romanticize it. Half the time I'm feeding people because cooking for one is depressing."

"And the other half?"

I watched the lights. Thought about the purse with one coin in it, and how the coin had turned out to be enough. Thought about my mother singing in this soil, one recovered fragment among all the ruins of me, and how that fragment was worth more than everything I'd ever stolen.

"The other half," I said, "is the half that counts."

Jude kissed my hair and went in to bank the fire, and I stayed on the step a while longer, doing the accounting I do now instead of the old kind. Seven hearths. Fourteen souls, give or take. One registrar with roofing to do. Zero names on lists.

It was, by any ledger I had ever kept, the wealthiest I had ever been.

* * *

The knock came in October, on the first morning of real frost.

Not at the gate, the way visitors came. At the door itself — three slow knocks, deliberate, spaced like a formal announcement. And when I opened it, there was no half-frozen refugee on the step, no tinker, no widow, no penitent clerk.

There was a child. Eight years old at most, standing perfectly straight in the frost with the composure of someone who had been coached, wearing traveling clothes too fine for the road and an expression too old for her face. Behind her, at the tree line, horses waited — and riders, and among the riders, unmistakable even at that distance, the gray-and-crimson of crown livery.

The child held out an envelope sealed with wax the color of dried blood.

"From the Hand of the Crown," she said, in a clear, rehearsed voice, "to the woman called Quentin Joubert — if she lives."

The wind moved through the garden behind me. Every planted row, every grafted branch, every stubborn green thing Odile had coaxed from the slope seemed to hold its breath.

I took the letter.
