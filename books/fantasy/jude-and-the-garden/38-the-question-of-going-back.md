## 38. The Question of Going Back

I have been composing the letter for three years.

Not writing it — composing it, the way a man composes an apology to someone he wronged in a dream. I know every word by heart. *Dear Sera. I am alive. I am sorry. The garden is green this time of year.* Three sentences. It has taken me three years to earn them, and even now they sit in my chest like stones I keep deciding not to swallow.

It was Quentin who noticed first. Of course it was. A person who spent twenty years reading what customers didn't say about themselves develops an ear for the unsaid, and losing that gift apparently did not lose the habit. They caught me staring north at breakfast — at the grey line of mountains where the border used to be, where the map simply gives up and becomes blank paper — and they set down their cup with the particular care of someone preparing to be brave.

"You could go," they said.

I said, "What?"

"Overland." They turned their cup a quarter-turn, a nervous tic they'd never had before the Door took their gift and left them with new ones. "The passes east of the ridge. The shepherds use them in summer. It would take two months, maybe three. You'd cross into crown territory somewhere past the salt flats, and then—" They stopped. Looked at me. "You'd find her."

The porridge went cold in front of me. I remember that distinctly, because everything else about that morning has gone soft at the edges, the way important moments do — as if memory itself is embarrassed to be caught eavesdropping.

"I don't know the way," I said, which was true, and also the smallest possible answer to the largest question anyone had ever asked me.

"I could draw you one," Quentin said. "From what travelers say. It wouldn't be perfect."

And there it was. Laid out on our scarred table between the honey pot and the chipped blue bowl: permission. My sister, alive because rebels pulled her out of a handler's custody, living somewhere in a city I had betrayed my way out of. Two months of hard walking. A life I had abandoned at knifepoint, metaphorically speaking, though there had been very little metaphorical about the knife.

Quentin watched me across the table with those eyes that no longer stole anything and therefore saw everything.

* * *

Let me tell you what you need to know about my sister, since I have been circling her for thirty-seven chapters like a man afraid of his own shadow's opinion.

Sera is four years older than me and has been saving me since before I could walk. When our father died, she held the household together with needlework and a temper. When the debt came — the debt that the crown's handlers wore like a fishing lure, dangling rescue in front of a drowning boy — it was Sera who signed herself over first. She sold her labor to a palace laundry so I could sell something worse. And when I learned, years later, that she hadn't been in a laundry at all, that the crown had kept her in a comfortable room as collateral against my obedience, that every kindness I'd ever done for the handlers had been priced against her safety — when I learned that, I burned my ledger of betrayals in a brazier and walked through a door that shouldn't exist.

She was freed anyway. The rebels got her out during the fighting at the Door. I have known this for three years the way you know a bone has healed: functionally, but with a weather that comes before rain.

I have never written to her. Not because letters can't cross — some do, smuggled in wool bales, arriving months late and half-censored. Not because I think she's dead; the rebels confirmed otherwise twice. I haven't written because the last thing I gave that woman was a choice between her brother and a stranger, and I chose the stranger, and I have not yet figured out how to ask forgiveness for a thing I would do again.

That's the ugly arithmetic nobody puts in songs. I would do it again. Given the same mirror and the same knife, I would choose Quentin every time, and Sera — who knows me better than God does — would probably want me to. But wanting to be forgiven for a sin you'd repeat isn't repentance. It's just appetite wearing a hair shirt.

So I sat at the table with my cold porridge and Quentin's terrible, generous offer hanging in the air, and I said the second-smallest thing available to me:

"Would you come with me?"

Because that was the real shape of it, wasn't it. Not *should I go*. Not *may I*. Whether I could survive the going without them. Whether the person I had become on this side of the border existed anywhere except within arm's reach of a former memory-thief who couldn't remember their own mother's face.

Quentin smiled. It was the smile they'd developed post-Door — smaller than the market charm, less practiced, more expensive. "No," they said gently. "This soil and I have an arrangement. But I'd wait. However long it took. I'd wait."

Which was somehow worse. Which was somehow the most loving thing anyone had ever said to me, and it made the leaving impossible, and I hated them for approximately four seconds, and then I loved them so much I had to go outside and weed something.

* * *

I weeded for most of a week.

This is how I process things. Quentin steals memories — stole memories — and processes the world by subtraction. I process by addition: hands in dirt, back bent, pulling up what doesn't belong so what does can breathe. By the third day I had cleared the entire eastern bed and was working on the fence posts, and Tam from the next holding came by to ask if I was angry at the weeds specifically or at weeds in general, and I told him to mind his own turnips.

On the fifth evening Quentin found me re-stacking stones along the wall for no structural reason whatsoever. They sat down on the finished part, which is a thing they do deliberately, because they know unfinished stonework makes me itch.

"You've asked everyone in the settlement except me," they said.

"Asked them what?"

"Whether the passes are passable. Whether the shepherds take passengers. What the crown's patrols look like these days." They picked up a stone, weighed it, set it down. "Old Marta says you asked her about the salt flats over eggs."

"Marta talks too much."

"Marta talks exactly enough, which is why you asked her instead of me." They looked up at me with the evening light behind them, and I thought, not for the first time, that losing their gift had not made Quentin Crane harmless. It had made them honest, which is far more dangerous. "Jude. Ask me."

So I sat down on the cold ground next to them, because standing over them felt like a strategy and I was done with strategies, and I asked.

"If I went. If I crossed. Would you still be here when I came back? If I came back?"

Quentin was quiet for a while. Long enough that a lark got bored overhead and went elsewhere.

"Do you remember," they said, "the night at the Door? When the thorns were drinking my confession out of me like wine?"

"I remember all of it."

"The last thing I felt go was my mother's voice. Singing. I'd carried it through twenty years of theft and erasure and I never once listened to it properly, and then it was gone, and I thought — well. That's the price. That's fair. I led soldiers here when I was a child; I don't get to keep songs." They turned the stone over in their fingers. "But here's what I learned afterward, gardening. Things regrow from what's left, not from what was taken. I don't have her voice. I have the fact of her singing. And the fact of it grows things in me that the voice never could."

They looked at me then, directly, and I understood they were not talking about their mother.

"You're asking whether love holds at distance," they said. "I can't promise you it does. Nobody can. What I can promise is that I will be here, and I will be myself, and whatever comes back to me — whether it's you in three months or your ghost in ten years — will find the same person waiting, tending the same beds, having kept faith the only way I know how. Which is badly, and daily, and anyway."

"And anyway," I agreed, because that word had become ours somewhere in the flooded cisterns beneath the city, back when trust was a blindfold and a hand in the dark.

Then Quentin did the cruelest kind thing in their considerable repertoire. They stood up, brushed off their trousers, and said: "Go if you must, Jude. Truly. I mean it. There is no version of this where I hold you here out of fear, and there is no version where I'd resent the road. You've paid enough debts to other people's claims. Whatever you decide next gets to be yours."

And they walked back toward the house, leaving me alone with the stones and the larks and the enormous, terrifying freedom of a man who has just been told he owes nothing to anybody.

* * *

I packed anyway.

Of course I packed. I'm not a saint; I'm a gardener with abandonment issues and a sister-shaped hole in my chest. For two days I prepared like a man planning an escape, which, given my history, is the only kind of planning my body knows how to do. I dried meat. I mended boots. I drew maps on scrap leather from everything travelers had told us, marking water, marking passes, marking the stretch of salt flat where the crown's riders were thickest. I did it at night, after Quentin slept, which fooled neither of us.

On the second night they appeared in the doorway of the shed with two cups of chicory and no commentary, sat on the seed crates, and drank with me while I worked. When I finished the map they took it, examined it, and handed it back.

"It's good," they said. "Marta's route is right. You've weighted the eastern pass too heavily — the shepherds won't move before midsummer — but the rest is sound."

"You're helping me leave."

"I'm helping you decide," they said. "There's a difference, and it matters, and I'll thank you to notice it."

I noticed it. That was the problem. Every kindness made the leaving harder, and every hardness — the Chicory Vigils, the corrected maps, the absolute refusal to guilt me — made staying feel less like cowardice and more like a thing a person might actually choose.

On the third day I walked to the top of the ridge.

It's a half-day's climb, the ridge above our valley, and from its spine you can see the whole shape of the world we live in: the garden below, small and green and absurdly defiant; the settlement's smoke; the forest going on forever; and beyond the eastern peaks, the haze where the old country's borders used to run, where the maps give up and the crown pretends nothing ever lived. Somewhere past that haze: a city. A laundry that was never a laundry. A woman with our father's stubborn chin, who has spent six years not knowing whether her brother is alive, and whose last sight of him was a public betrayal staged for a handler's benefit.

I sat on the ridge until the sun went down and the stars came out, the same stars that shine on her, obviously, though knowing that has never once comforted anyone.

And I tried to imagine the road.

Here is what I found, and it surprised me: I could see every step of it. The passes, the flats, the checkpoints, the forged pilgrim's papers I still remembered how to make. I could see Sera's door. I could see her face when she opened it — shock, then fury, then the terrible Sera-forgiveness that always cost her more than it cost me. I could see all of it, clear as a planted row.

What I could not see was the end of it. Because every version I built, every single one, ended the same way: I found her, I held her, I told her I was sorry, and then I turned around and walked back west, because the truth underneath all my packing and map-drawing and chicory-drinking was this —

My home wasn't behind me anymore. It was downhill, in a garden, asleep in a house with a crooked door, belonging to a person who had once told me, in a snowfield, with frostbite in their fingers: *you could have let them take me and gone home clean.* And stayed anyway. Chose me anyway. With full knowledge. Without debt.

Nobody had ever chosen me without debt before. Everyone else's loyalty had come with a leash attached — the crown held Sera, Sera needed bread, the handlers held the papers. Even my love for Sera, pure as it was, had been braided with obligation since childhood, since she fed me when Father couldn't. But Quentin had walked away from me into the snow rather than accept a confession offered as currency. And waited for me to come back as a free man. And then built a garden with me where every seed was a decision, renewed daily, owed to no one.

You cannot repay a gift like that. You can only tend it.

I got back to the house near midnight. Quentin was awake — they usually were, these days; sleep comes thin when you've given away your whole interior life to a door — and they took one look at my face and one look at my empty pack-frame and said nothing, which is the hardest sentence in any language, and poured me chicory.

"I'm staying," I said.

"I know."

"Not out of debt."

"I know that too." They handed me the cup. Their hands aren't steady anymore — the gift's departure left a tremor, like a bell still humming after the strike — and I have come to love that tremor more than I ever loved the steadiness. "Debt is just fear wearing bookkeeping clothes. I'd have smelled it on you."

"So what now?"

"Now," Quentin said, "you write the letter. All of it. Everything you've been carrying for three years. And we send it with the wool merchants in spring, and it may arrive and it may not, and either way it stops being a stone in your chest and becomes a seed in the world. Those are different weights, Jude. Trust me. I've carried both."

* * *

I wrote it that winter, by firelight, while Quentin mended nets and pretended not to watch.

It ran eleven pages. I told her about the Door and the thorns and the country that erased itself to avoid being weaponized. I told her about killing a soldier at the pass, and about the handler's mirror, and about choosing a stranger over my own blood, and about how I would do it again, and about how sorry I was, and about how those two facts were going to have to learn to live in the same house. I told her about the garden. About the tree we'd planted from conservatory seeds that fruited small kindnesses — literal ones, small mercies blooming like plums, which sounds like madness unless you've eaten one. I told her that if she ever wanted to come, there was a place for her here, and if she never wanted to speak to me again, that too was hers to choose, freely, the way everything should have been from the start.

Eleven pages. Then I sealed it and put it in the wool bale in spring, and the merchants took it east, and I stood in the road watching until the dust settled, feeling lighter and heavier simultaneously, which I have decided is simply what hope feels like when it's grown up.

Quentin found me at the gate afterward.

"Well?" they said.

"It's done."

"And how does it feel, sending your heart across a closed border with no guarantee of return?"

I considered the question honestly, because they deserved honesty; we'd built the whole place on it. "Like planting," I said. "You do everything right and then you wait, and the waiting is awful, and you plant anyway, because the alternative is a yard full of nothing."

Quentin laughed — that real laugh, the rare one — and put their arm through mine, and we walked back down toward the beds, where the early lettuce was coming up and the season was turning the way seasons do, indifferent and generous at once.

We did not know it yet — how could we? — but that letter was already moving east through three pairs of hands and one very curious wool merchant's daughter, and somewhere beyond the haze, in a city that still remembered my name and my crimes, a woman with our father's chin was about to read eleven pages that would change the shape of all our lives.

But that, as they say, is next year's harvest.
