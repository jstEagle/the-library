## 39. Harvest

### Quentin

I had forgotten my own name for most of a year, so you will forgive me if I am proud of remembering this one: it was the festival of the first harvest, and I was the one who suggested we celebrate.

Not that anyone needed convincing. The settlement had been talking about nothing else since the barley turned. But there is a difference between a thing happening and a thing being *claimed*, and I wanted to claim it. I have learned — slowly, painfully, like everything since I walked through that door carrying almost nothing — that claiming things matters. You can lose your gift, your history, your face in the mirror of who you thought you were, but if you stand up at the well and say *this feast is mine to organize*, then by evening you are someone who organizes feasts, and that is not nothing. That is a self, small as a seed, and seeds were rather our specialty now.

"You'll want tables," said Marta Osei, who had arrived through the passes in spring with her two daughters and a talent for bread that I can only describe as suspiciously close to magic, though of course nobody says that word here anymore unless they're laughing.

"I want tables," I agreed. "And I want them far from the fire pit, because last time Doss let children near an open flame we spent an hour fishing his hat out of the stew."

"Your hat," Marta said.

"*His* hat," I said, with dignity. "I was supervising."

The truth — and I tell it freely now, because telling truths freely turned out to be the price of a door and I've gotten in the habit — the truth is that the settlement runs on a strange economy these days. We have no coin worth anything on either side of any border. We trade in labor and loaves and, increasingly, in stories. Every person here came over the mountains or through the high passes chasing a rumor: a garden beyond the map, tended by two people, one who forgot everything and one who remembered too much. They arrive exhausted and half-starved and looking at me like I'm supposed to be something more than a middle-aged former charm-seller with dirt under her nails and a head full of holes.

I give them soup. It turns out soup is enough. Who knew? Certainly not the woman I used to be, who thought survival meant trading in other people's memories because her own weren't worth the space they took up. That woman would be astonished to learn that hospitality — the ordinary, unmagical kind, the kind with ladles in it — could hold a community together better than any gift ever held a customer's loyalty.

The morning of the festival, I was up before dawn, which was becoming a habit and an argument with my own spine. The light came up gold behind the eastern ridge, the way it does in autumn, all long shadows and honest air, and I stood in the barley field with my hands full of the first cut sheaves and felt — 

Felt.

That deserves its own sentence, what I felt. Because for a year after the Door, feeling was mostly grief wearing different coats. Grief for the gift. Grief for the twenty years of small thefts I'd returned one by one until there was almost no *me* left underneath them. Grief for the sealed memory I'd given back to a dying country — the memory of a child leading the crown's cartographers through a mountain pass, the memory that explained why everything green had chosen to forget itself rather than be weaponized. I'd paid it willingly. I'd pay it again. But paying leaves a shape where the thing used to be, and for months every feeling I had echoed hollowly inside that shape.

This was not that.

This was the sheaves in my hands, dry and warm and smelling of sun, and somewhere below the thinking part of me a tune started up — three notes, rising, then a fourth that bent downward like a branch under fruit. A humming. A woman's voice, low, singing words I couldn't catch, the melody worn smooth as a river stone from being sung ten thousand times.

I stood very still in the barley.

Here is the strange arithmetic of my condition: I remember almost nothing of my childhood in the erased country. I know the facts the way you know facts about a stranger — that I lived here, that I wept in a garden, that the country spared me when it spared no one else. But knowing is not remembering, and for a year the melody of my own life had been silence.

Now there were four notes.

"Quentin?" Jude's voice, behind me, careful the way it always was in the early days when I went still like this. Even now, two years on, even after everything, there was a watchfulness in how Jude approached my silences. Guilt has a long half-life. So does love. They decay at roughly the same rate, which I suppose is why they're so often confused.

"It's nothing," I said. Then, because honesty: "No. It's something. Someone was singing."

Jude came and stood beside me among the sheaves. I didn't look over, but I felt the particular quality of their attention — the gardener's attention, patient, willing to wait out a season for an answer.

"Someone," Jude repeated.

"A woman. Low voice." I hummed the four notes, badly. My voice cracked on the bend of the third one, and I laughed at myself, embarrassed, and then found I was crying instead, which was inconvenient and also, lately, normal. "She was standing where I'm standing. Or near. In soil like this soil. Jude, I think she was singing at harvest."

The silence between us stretched, and then Jude did the thing they always did, the thing that undid me more thoroughly than any confession ever had: they hummed the notes back. Wrong. Terribly wrong, flat as a dropped plate. And then again, correcting, until the melody sat between us, ours now, a song with no words that belonged to a mother I couldn't name in a country that no longer existed, sung by a gardener who once betrayed me, in a field I had planted with hands that remembered nothing and knew everything.

"There," Jude said quietly. "Now it's real. Songs need two people or they're just noise."

### Jude

I want to record this properly, because I have decided — late, and against all my instincts, which were trained by the crown to record nothing and report less — that some things should live outside my skull.

The morning of the harvest festival, Quentin cried in the barley field and called it laughing, and I pretended to believe them, and we both knew I was pretending, and it was the truest moment we'd had in weeks.

You learn things about grief, living beside it. You learn that it isn't one weather but many, that some mornings it's fog and some mornings hail and some mornings — rare mornings — it breaks open into something you don't have a word for, joy with roots down deep enough to drink from sorrow. Quentin's mother sang in this soil. Quentin doesn't remember her face, her name, whether she called them by a name I've never heard. But the body keeps accounts the mind cannot read, and when Quentin's hands closed around those sheaves, some ledger older than the erasure balanced itself for one bright moment.

I carried seeds out of the palace conservatory without knowing it. That's the story everyone loves here — the gardener who smuggled a dead country's flora in the lining of a coat, dormant for decades, waiting for soil that remembered how to wake it. People ask me about it like it was heroism. It wasn't. It was a young man stuffing his pockets with pretty pods because he was homesick for green things in a city of grey stone, and the crown's own conservatory happened to grow specimens from a nation on no map, labeled only with a number. I stole them out of loneliness. They turned out to be salvation. Most of the true things in my life have worked exactly that way.

What people ask less often — what only Sefa the miller's wife has ever asked me outright, one night over cards, with her sharp kind eyes — is whether I knew what I was carrying.

"No," I told her. "I didn't know anything. That was rather my defining feature."

"And now?"

"Now I know too much," I said, "and it's still not enough," and she won the hand and made me wash the dishes, which seemed fair.

The days before the festival were the good kind of chaos. Tables built and rebuilt because Doss measured nothing twice. Children drafted into garland-making who understood their job to be eating the berries. The ovens going from dawn, Marta presiding over them like a general whose war was bread. And Quentin everywhere at once — organizing, joking, lifting things heavier than their back will forgive them tomorrow — running the whole affair with the same deft competence they once used selling charms to frightened customers. Some talents survive the loss of the gift that housed them. Quentin was always, at root, someone who could make a room feel safe. Magic was just the packaging.

I watched them across the yard on the second-to-last day, standing on a crate directing the hanging of lanterns, arms crossed, mouth pursed in mock severity while three teenagers ignored every instruction, and I felt the old fear rise — the handler's reflex, the inventory instinct, *assess the asset, note the weaknesses* — and I let it pass through me and out the other side like weather. That reflex doesn't get to run my house anymore. What stayed behind was simpler: I love this person. I have loved them through treachery and snow and a wound that nearly emptied me on the stones of a garden, and I will love them tomorrow and the day after, and none of it is debt. That's the miracle I still don't fully trust. Everything else in my life was bought or coerced. This was chosen. I choose it daily, the way you water a plant — not because it wilts without you, but because watching it grow is the best thing you'll do all day.

On the eve of the festival, Sefa cornered me by the granary with a look I'd learned to dread.

"They're asking you to speak," she said. "Tomorrow. After the first fruits are brought in. Both of you."

"Both of us?"

"It's your settlement too, gardener. Half these people crossed those passes because of the garden. They want to hear from the ones who planted it." She paused, and her voice gentled, which from Sefa was more alarming than anger. "Quentin will want you to go first. Let them."

"Why?"

"Because you remember everything," she said, "and they remember almost nothing, and love means knowing which of you should carry the weight on which days." She patted my arm hard enough to bruise. "Also bring the cider. The good cask, not the vinegar you've been hoarding."

### Quentin

Jude spoke first, as Sefa had arranged without either of us admitting she'd arranged anything.

They stood up on the grain wagon in the last of the afternoon light, this person I have known as betrayer and savior and informant and beloved, sometimes all before breakfast, and looked out at perhaps two hundred faces — farmers and refugees and children born on this side of nowhere — and said:

"I used to work for people who counted things. Grain, taxes, people. Especially people. I was very good at counting." A ripple went through the crowd; everyone here knows Jude's history in outline; it's on the unofficial welcome speech Sefa gives newcomers, right after *the latrines are behind the willow*. "And standing here today, the only count that matters to me is this: every person who eats at these tables chose to be here. Nobody was conscripted. Nobody was planted. Nobody is reporting to anybody." A beat, and then, wry as ever: "Except Marta, who reports my bread consumption to Quentin weekly, and I'd thank you both to stop."

Laughter. Marta raised a heel of loaf in salute. And then Jude's voice dropped into that register they save for truths — the register I heard once at a door made of thorns, when they gave away every secret they had and the thorns drank it and opened — and they said: "We planted a garden. It grew. That's the whole speech. Eat well. You earned it."

Which left me.

I climbed up beside them and looked out at the crowd and felt the four notes sitting in my chest like a coal wrapped in cloth.

I hadn't planned to sing. I should say that plainly, because everything else about that evening I planned within an inch of its life — the order of dishes, the seating of feuding cousins, the strategic placement of Doss far from fire. But the song wasn't planned. The song arrived the way the memory arrived: uninvited, undeniable, rooted.

"Someone sang in this soil," I told them. "Before the maps forgot us. Before I forgot myself. I don't know her name. I don't know if she was my mother or my aunt or a neighbor at her own harvest, but she stood where we're standing and she sang, and today her song came back." I looked at Jude. Jude nodded, once, steady as a trellis. "Songs need two people or they're just noise. So — sing with me. Badly. That's an order from the woman who organized your tables."

And I hummed the four notes, and two hundred voices — rough, flat, wandering, wonderful — hummed them back.

Somewhere in the middle of it, in the tangle of imperfect harmony, the fragment opened further. Not a flood; I know better than to expect floods. Just another inch of the door swinging: the smell of woodsmoke and crushed apple, a hand resting briefly on my hair — small hands under mine, so I was the child, I was *the child at the harvest* — and a voice saying words I couldn't translate anymore but whose meaning arrived anyway, the way warmth arrives through a window: *grow well, little one; the ground remembers what we plant in it.*

I got through the song. Mostly. There was a moment around the fourth repetition where my voice gave out entirely and Jude's arm came around my shoulders and the crowd carried the melody for me, and I stood there weeping openly in front of the entire settlement and discovered, to my lasting surprise, that I did not die of it. That no one looked away. That a girl of maybe nine pressed a wildflower into my hand afterward with the gravity of a diplomat presenting credentials.

Later — much later, after the dancing, after the children collapsed in heaps among the sheaves like felled saplings, after the fires burned down to their red arithmetic — Jude and I walked out past the field edge, where the new orchard trees stood in their first autumn, small and stubborn, the erased country's own stock finally fruiting. Three apples hung on the nearest tree. We ate one each and split the third, standing shoulder to shoulder in the dark, juice running down our wrists like something consecrated.

"The ground remembers what we plant in it," I said.

Jude went still. "Where did you hear that?"

"Nowhere. Everywhere. Her." I wiped my eyes with the back of my hand, apple-sweet and salt-bitter. "It's hers. It's mine. I'm giving it to the ground."

We stood a while longer. Behind us the settlement glowed and murmured, two hundred lives stacked like cordwood against the coming winter, all of them possible because a door had opened for two truths and shut forever behind them. Ahead of us the dark fields ran to the mountains, and beyond the mountains, though no map showed it and no road reached it, the old country slept on, finished with dissolving, keeping its peace.

"Next year," Jude said, "we plant the south terrace."

"We'll plant the south terrace."

"And the year after—"

But whatever came after *the year after* never got spoken, because down the slope, lamplit and breathless, young Ilsa came pelting toward us from the gate, waving something white, shouting my name — my chosen name, the one I introduced myself with, the only one I have — and calling that a traveler had come down off the high pass tonight, alone, frostbitten, half-mad with walking, and that when they'd been given soup and asked where they'd come from, they had said words that stopped the whole common room cold.

They said they'd come from beyond the mountains.

They said the pass had opened.
