## 40. Jude and the Garden

**Quentin**

The travelers always arrive at dusk, because that is when the light on our hedge makes it look like something out of a story, and stories are what carry people across mountains better than roads do.

They come up the last switchback dusty and half-convinced they've been fooled, holding whatever scrap of rumor brought them — a charcoal sketch passed hand to hand in a border tavern, a pilgrim's secondhand account of fruit that tastes like forgiveness, or simply the stubborn arithmetic of refugees who have nowhere else to be. And then they crest the ridge and see the garden spilling down the hillside in green terraces, and the little stone house with its crooked chimney, and me standing in the gate with dirt to my elbows, and I watch them do the same thing every single time.

They look at me as if I'm supposed to be someone.

I never am. That's rather the point.

"You're the one," said a tinker just last week, dropping his pack in the road and staring. "The memory-thief. The one who opened the Door."

"Am I?" I said pleasantly, and handed him a plum. It's my standard answer to everything now, and I recommend it without reservation. *Am I?* It has gotten me out of more conversations than any charm ever did.

Because here is the truth of me, the whole inventory: I am a woman with dirt under her nails, an ache behind her eyes when it rains, a fondness for honey she can't explain, and a husband — though we've never bothered much with the word — who looks at me some evenings like I'm a letter he's still learning to read. I don't know my mother's face. I don't know why my hands know how to graft a cutting but not how to write anything but my own name, which even that came back slow, like a bird returning to a window it had once flown into. I gave twenty years of small thefts back to their owners, and then I gave the largest theft back to a dead country, and what remained was this: a person, unburdened, unproven, and — I'll say it, since no one else will — considerably less interesting at parties.

Jude says I'm not less interesting. Jude says I'm *concentrated*. But Jude would say that; Jude remembers too much, and most of what Jude remembers is loving me, so naturally Jude's accounts are unreliable.

Still. There are nights — I will admit this much, here, where only the beans can hear — when I wake at the hour before dawn with my heart going like a trapped moth and the certainty that I have forgotten something enormous. Not a fact. A *weather*. Something that used to fill me the way fog fills a valley, and now there's just the shape it left. On those nights I go out barefoot into the rows and put my palms flat against the soil, and the soil does what soil does, which is nothing, gloriously nothing, and by morning I have usually remembered that I am exactly where I chose to be, even if I can't remember the choosing.

That has to count for something. I've decided it counts for everything.

* * *

**Jude**

I remember everything. That is not a boast; it is a sentence I served, and in some ways am still serving.

I remember the handler's office with its map of a country that wasn't on it. I remember my sister's fever, the debt, the bargain struck in a room that smelled of ink and cold iron. I remember every name I carried to the crown, every gift-bearer I helped them find, and I remember each one in order, the way you remember the stations of a route you walked too many times. The mind insists on keeping such things. I have stopped arguing with it.

What I did not expect was that remembering would become useful again — that all that hoarded guilt could be composted, turned, broken down into something that feeds.

Because the refugees come, you see, and they come *broken* in the particular way of people who were hunted for what they are, and they arrive carrying their memories like sacks of stones they cannot set down. The woman from the salt marshes who watched her son register and never come home. The boy who hid his gift so long he forgot he had one until it burst out of him at his own wedding and the guests screamed. They come to us because the rumor says there is a garden past the edge of the maps where nobody asks what you are, and they arrive at our gate with their stones.

And I listen. That's my work now, though no one pays me and there's no title for it. Quentin grows things; I keep accounts — not ledgers anymore, never ledgers again, but the other kind. The listening kind. I hold their memories for an hour at a time while they talk, all of it, the shameful parts especially, because shame is the heaviest stone and the one people most need to put down somewhere safe.

"I remember it all," I told the marsh woman once, after she'd finished, her voice scraped hollow. "Every terrible thing I've ever done. In perfect detail." She looked at me with something like horror, and I shrugged. "It doesn't kill you. It just makes you very attentive to not adding to the collection."

She laughed. It was a wrecked sound, but it was a laugh. She stayed through harvest and left in spring with seeds in her pockets and her spine straighter, and if that isn't redemption I don't know what word you'd use instead, and frankly I've run out of patience for words that fancy themselves bigger than a straightened spine.

Quentin worries sometimes, in the way of people who cannot remember their sins, that mine will curdle in me someday. They asked me about it once, lying in the dark, their head on my chest directly over the heart that had betrayed them and then bled for them, as if checking its work.

"Do you wish you could forget?"

"No," I said, and it was true before I said it, which is the best kind of true. "Forgetting was your penance. Remembering is mine. We balance."

"And what do we balance out to?"

I considered. Below the window the night smelled of thyme and woodsmoke and the faint green breath of the hedge, that old erased magic that never entirely went out of the ground here, the way warmth stays in a hearthstone till morning.

"A garden," I said.

* * *

**Quentin**

Now. The tree.

You'll want to hear about the tree, because the tree is what the rumors are actually about, and the rumors are mostly right for once, which I find almost embarrassing.

It began three springs ago. A traveler — one of the first, back when they came singly and stood in the gate apologizing for existing — left us a gift in payment for a bed and a bowl of soup. A sapling, root-wrapped in sacking, with leaves unlike anything in either country I half-remember: silver underneath, like a fish flashing, and a scent that made Jude go very still and very quiet, the way he does when memory presses on a bruise.

"It's from the conservatory," Jude said finally. "The royal one. I watered its mother for eleven years and never knew what it was. The palace didn't know either. It predates their records." A pause. "It predates *everything*, I think. It's from over here originally. Someone took it west a long time ago."

We planted it in the highest terrace, above the vegetables, where the view goes all the way to the pass. It grew like something making up for lost time. It fruited in its second year — small round things, gold-green, unremarkable to look at.

Then the marsh woman ate one, the day before she left, and stopped halfway down the hill, and came back up crying, and kissed my hands, and said, "I remembered my mother's voice. Just — her voice, saying my name. I hadn't heard it in nine years. What *is* this?"

We didn't know. We still don't, entirely. But we've worked it out in practice, the way gardeners work everything out: the fruit gives back small things. Small kindnesses, small warmths, the minor mercies people lose along the way and mistake for gone forever. A forgotten lullaby. The smell of a specific kitchen. The feeling of being forgiven by someone long dead. Never the big wounds — the tree is modest, it deals in small coin — but enough. Enough that people started calling it the Kindness Tree, which Jude hates on principle and I secretly love, because names are just rumors with tenure.

And here is the part I have told almost no one, because I only recently admitted it to myself: I think the tree is doing what I used to do. Only backward. I spent twenty years taking memories out of people, gently, a coin here and there to buy my bread. The tree spends its whole life putting them back. If the erased country's magic ran in my blood — and the evidence suggests it ran in it like a river runs in a valley — then perhaps this is what the gift looks like when it stops being afraid. Perhaps this was always what it was *for*, and I only ever saw the wrong end of it.

Or perhaps it's just a tree, and kindness is contagious the way everything in a garden is, and the distinction doesn't matter.

I've decided the distinction doesn't matter. I've gotten quite good at deciding things. It's nearly all I have, decision-wise, and I find I'm rich in it.

* * *

**Jude**

This morning we planted another tree. That is the actual occasion for these pages, if pages need occasions, and I have learned from writing letters to my sister that they do — that memory, whether abundant or amputated, keeps better when given a shape.

The sapling is from the Kindness Tree's own seed, grown three years in our nursery bed, and we carried it up past the vegetable terraces to the far slope, the wild one, where the hedge runs ragged against the mountain. Quentin dug; I held it upright; the hole filled with that dark sweet soil that made even the cartographers lie about this place, once.

"Why up here?" I asked. "Where no one will find it?"

Quentin considered, dirt to the elbows, hair full of wind. "So it'll be found," they said. "Eventually. By someone climbing higher than they meant to. That's how all the good discoveries happen — someone climbs higher than they meant to." Then, quieter, patting the earth down around the roots: "Besides. Some things shouldn't only grow where they're guarded. That was the whole mistake, wasn't it? Theirs and ours. Hoarding the magic. Fencing the gardens. Making people into secrets."

I said nothing, because there was nothing to add, because after eight years I still occasionally stand next to this person and feel like a man holding a lantern at the mouth of a cave that turns out to be a cathedral.

Here is what the years have taught me, and I set it down plainly, since plainness is the only style I trust: loyalty is not a thing you owe. I owed the crown for fifteen years and it rotted in me like grain in a wet sack. Loyalty is a thing you plant, annually, in whichever ground you choose, and it comes up differently every season, and some years it fails, and you plant it again anyway because the planting itself is the vow. Truth works the same way. You do not pay it once at a door and be done. You pay it daily, in small coin — in saying what you noticed, what you want, what you're afraid of, what you did. Quentin, who lost the whole ledger of their life, is better at this than I am. I remind myself of that whenever I start feeling superior about my perfect recall.

At noon we sat on the wall above the new-planted slope and shared bread and the last of the winter pears, and below us the garden did its slow green argument with gravity, and beyond the hedge the mountains kept their counsel, and somewhere east of them a country finished dissolving years ago, taking its name and its door, leaving only this: two people, one tree, and the rumor spreading downhill like water finding the low places.

A traveler came up the path while we sat there — a girl this time, sixteen maybe, alone, sunburned, with the wary eyes they all arrive with. She stopped when she saw us, and I watched her decide whether to run.

"You're real, then," she said. "The garden. The two of them — you. One who forgot everything and one who remembers too much."

"That's us," Quentin said cheerfully. "Though I'd quibble with *everything*. I remember the recipe for plum preserves and exactly how annoying Jude is before coffee."

"Both of those are load-bearing memories," I said.

The girl laughed — startled out of her, the best kind — and Quentin stood and brushed off their knees and held out a hand, dirt and all.

"Well, come down, then. You're just in time. We're planting."

* * *

**Quentin**

She stayed, as they do. Her name is Wren; she chose it herself, which is the fashion among us now, the borderless — everyone here has named themselves at least once. She has a gift she's frightened of, something to do with mending broken things whether they consent or not, and she flinches when Jude raises his voice, which is rare, and when anyone touches her shoulders, which we have all quietly learned not to do. She sleeps in the loft. She talks in her sleep in a language I don't know and neither does she, and Jude wrote it down phonetically once, and showed it to the old cartographer's daughter when she visited in autumn — she comes most years now, gray as ash and sharp as a grafting knife — and the old woman read it and went pale and said only: *"Garden-tongue. The children all spoke it. Before."*

So the language isn't dead. It's been sleeping in children, the way the conservatory seeds slept in a palace cellar, the way kindness sleeps in fruit, the way a whole country slept in one weeping child who couldn't remember weeping.

Everything important sleeps and wakes. That's the whole doctrine. I could carve it over the gate.

Tonight I walked the terraces at dusk, as I do, and stopped at the young tree on the high slope, barely knee-high, its silver-backed leaves turning in the wind like a shoal of fish deciding. I put my hand on its stem. I felt — and I am a skeptic by trade now, skepticism being the last skill my old life left me — I felt the faintest pull, like a hook of warmth, the ghost of a sensation I once knew intimately from the other side. As if the tree were asking. As if it had learned from watching me, all these years, that you ask first.

"Go on, then," I told it. "Take what you need. Give away the rest. That's the family business."

Below me the lamps were lighting in the houses we've built for the ones who stay — seven households now, and more coming with the thaw. I could see Jude in the doorway of ours, silhouetted, waiting the way he waits, patient as topsoil, and I thought about how the travelers tell it — *one who forgot everything, one who remembered too much* — and how the telling is true and also misses the point entirely, because the point was never the forgetting or the remembering.

The point was the planting. It was always the planting. Two people who had paid every price there was, standing in ordinary dirt, putting things in the ground on the faith that something would come up — and something coming up, every single year, without fail, as if the world itself had been waiting all along for someone to simply *ask it first*.

I went down the hill toward the lamplight, leaving the young tree to its wind and its work, and behind me the garden breathed out its green breath into the dark, and somewhere on the far side of the closed border, in a country that no longer exists except as a hillside and a hedge and a habit of mercy, my mother — whoever she was, whatever she sang — was, I am fairly certain, humming.
