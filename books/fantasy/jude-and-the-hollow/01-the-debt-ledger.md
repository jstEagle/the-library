## 1. The Debt Ledger

The ink was the first thing Quentin Kavanagh loved each morning, and the last thing he trusted at night.

It sat in its stone well like a small black pond, perfectly still, and in that stillness it promised what nothing else in the Hollow Valley could promise: that a thing written would remain a thing written. That a debt recorded was a debt made real, countable, survivable. He dipped his pen at dawn as other men lifted their faces to pray, and he did not think himself less devout for it.

Outside the scriptorium's narrow window, Vael's Crossing lay wrapped in the grey hush that never quite lifted. Mist hung over the millpond without moving. Smoke rose from the baker's chimney in a straight, unwavering column, as if even the fire had been instructed to behave. Somewhere beyond the valley walls — beyond the border stones with their worn carvings that no one was permitted to touch, much less read — Quentin had been told the wind howled and the rivers ran wild and men did things with fire and storm that no honest hand should do. Here, nothing howled. Nothing raged. The Valley of the Hollow kept its silence the way a debtor keeps his ledger: carefully, completely, forever.

*Stillness is safety,* the temple taught. *The Hollow is the Mother's cupped hand.* Quentin had copied those words so many times he could feel the grooves of them behind his eyes. He had copied them before he understood them, and after he understood them, and somewhere in between there had been a season — brief, quickly buried — when copying them had felt less like devotion and more like digging a hole.

He did not dig holes anymore.

"Brother Kavanagh." The voice of Father Ellam came from the doorway, dry as old vellum. "Your margins are drifting again."

Quentin looked down. It was true; the last three entries leaned toward the outer edge of the page like pilgrims straying from the road. "Begging pardon, Father."

"Begging pardon costs nothing and mends nothing." Ellam crossed the room, his sandals whispering on the flagstones, and set one finger on the ledger open before Quentin. *Debt Register, Western Parishes, Year of Stillness 214.* "Straighten your lines. A crooked entry invites a crooked thought, and a crooked thought—" he tapped the page twice— "is how men end up outside the walls instead of within them."

"Yes, Father."

Ellam lingered. He was not a cruel man, Quentin reminded himself, not the way the traveling merchants' stories painted the temple fathers of distant lands. He was simply thorough, and thoroughness in the Hollow wore the same face cruelty wore elsewhere. "You are four years from freedom," Ellam said, and there was something almost gentle in it. "Eleven thousand, two hundred and six days, by my count. Your father left you a heavy book, but books close."

"Yes, Father."

"Do you resent him?"

Quentin's pen hovered above the page. The question was not kind, but neither was it unkind; it was inventory, the way Ellam inventoried everything — candles, grain, souls. "I resent the arithmetic, Father. Not the man."

Ellam considered this, and seemed to find it acceptable. "Good. Resentment of persons breeds heresy. Resentment of numbers merely breeds accuracy." He turned toward the door, then paused. "Mother Aldessa will want the western registers bound by month's end. She has begun asking after you by name."

The words settled into Quentin's stomach like a swallowed stone. Being asked after by name, in the Temple of the Hollow, was either an honor or an examination, and no one had ever mistaken one for the other until it was too late.

"I'll have them ready," he said.

When the footsteps faded, Quentin straightened his margins and went back to work.

* * *

The ledger before him belonged to a widow named Serra Mollen, of the western terraces. Her late husband had borrowed against their loom to buy seed during the blight year, and the interest had grown the way interest grows in a valley where nothing else does: slowly, patiently, inevitably. Quentin transcribed the figures in his small, exact hand — eleven crowns principal, nine crowns interest, seven years' labor owed or its equivalent in cloth — and beside each figure he wrote the temple's notation for *justly held*, which was a small closed circle.

A closed circle. A thing with no opening in it anywhere.

He had drawn ten thousand of them by now, he estimated. Perhaps twenty thousand. When he was a boy, newly indentured, he had believed that if he drew enough of them they would begin to look like something else — a door, maybe, or a ring of standing stones, anything with a gap in it. They never did. A circle was a circle. His father had died owing the temple forty-one crowns and one son, and the son had been entered into the register in the same ink, in the same hand, in the same closed circle, and there was no appeal from ink. There was only service, and the slow erosion of years, and the promise — repeated every Festival of Stillness, embroidered into every altar cloth — that a debt faithfully served was a soul faithfully saved.

Quentin was twenty-six years old. He had been a scribe since he was nine. He knew seventeen hundred pages of scripture by heart, not because he had tried to memorize them but because copying was what he did, and his memory was the sort that did not let go. Teachers had called it a gift. The temple called it fitness for office. Quentin privately suspected it was neither; it was simply a fact about him, like the color of his eyes, and facts were the only currency the valley honored.

Facts, and debts.

At midday the bell rang for the Hour of Bread, and the scribes filed down to the refectory in their grey robes. The Temple of the Hollow was not large, as temples went — it had been built small on purpose, the brothers said, because grandeur invited pride and pride invited the old sorceries — but it held the valley's records entire: every birth, every death, every crown borrowed and repented, going back past the founding, past the Peace, back to the years the archives stopped numbering and started sealing. Quentin had been in the reading rooms a hundred times. He had never been past the third door of the archive, where the shelves stood behind iron grilles and the air smelled of cold stone and older paper.

No one spoke in the refectory. Speaking during meals was vanity; the Fathers said the mouth was for bread and prayer and little else. So two hundred men ate in the clatter of spoons and the rustle of grey wool, and Quentin ate among them, and found, as he always found, a strange comfort in the silence — and beneath the comfort, as he always found it, the faint pressure of it, like water against the far side of a glass wall. As though the stillness were not empty but full. As though something enormous were being held back, just out of hearing, all the time, everywhere, by everyone, together.

He had asked about it once, when he was twelve. Brother Tammas, who taught the youngest copyists their letter-forms, had gone very quiet, and then very kind, and said: *The Hollow holds back the flood, child. Would you complain of a dam?*

Quentin had not complained again. But he had noticed, afterward, that Brother Tammas never answered questions near the windows.

* * *

The afternoon wore on. Light moved across the scriptorium floor in a pale diagonal and did not warm anything. Quentin finished the Mollen ledger and began the parish tithes, and it was while ruling a fresh column that his eye caught, for perhaps the thousandth time, the carving above the archive door — visible from his desk through the archway, half-worn, half-familiar.

It was a spiral. Not a circle. A line that began at the center and wound outward, and outward, and outward, until it reached the stone's edge and simply — continued. Off the lintel. Out of the frame. Toward wherever the stone ended and the world began.

Every scribe in the valley drew closed circles over justly held debts. Every altar in the temple bore the sign of the sealed cup, the Mother's hollowed hands. And yet the oldest carving in the building, older than any of it, older than the founding itself if the wear on the stone meant anything, was a line that refused to close.

He had asked Father Ellam about it once, years ago. Ellam had looked at the spiral for a long moment with an expression Quentin had never been able to classify — not anger, not fear, something more tired than either — and said: *Founders' ornament. Meaningless. Rule your columns, Brother.*

Meaningless. In a temple where every mark had meaning; where the width of a margin could be corrected; where a single stray word in a copyist's hand was hunted down and scraped from the vellum with a knife. Everything meant. Except the one thing oldest of all.

Quentin ruled his column. He did not look up again. This, too, was a skill he had practiced: the art of seeing a thing and letting it pass through him like weather, leaving no record, disturbing no figures. Eleven thousand two hundred days, Ellam had said. He could hold anything for eleven thousand days. He could hold a question, a doubt, a whole country of doubts. Men held worse for longer. The widow Mollen held a loom she'd never weave on again.

This was contentment. He told himself it was contentment. He was good at his work, fed and roofed and safe inside the cupped hand, and in four years the book would close, and he would walk out through the temple doors a free man with steady hands and a head full of texts, and he would take a cottage somewhere and copy whatever people paid him to copy, and none of it — none of it — would be anyone's property ever again.

The thought should have been sweet. It arrived, as always, tasting faintly of ash.

* * *

Vespers came with the bell, and the bell came earlier than Quentin expected, which meant he had lost track of the light again, which meant the day had done what valley days did: slipped past without event, indistinguishable from the day before, a coin minted from the same blank metal.

He knelt with the others on the cold flags of the nave. Two hundred grey-robed backs. The incense — juniper and ash, no myrrh, myrrh was an extravagance and extravagance invited pride — curled upward in a motionless column and dispersed into nothing. Mother Aldessa stood at the altar, small and upright as a candlestick, her voice thin and clear and absolutely certain.

*"We give thanks for the Hollow,"* she intoned, *"which keeps us. We give thanks for the Stillness, which saves us. Beyond these borders lies the flood; within these borders lies the fold. What was banished stays banished. What sleeps, let it sleep. What we bury, let it stay buried."*

Two hundred voices answered: *"Let it stay buried."*

Quentin's lips moved with theirs. His voice made the right sounds at the right times. And in the middle of the response — between *banished* and *buried*, in the space of a single breath — he became aware, with the peculiar clarity that comes only to men whose lives contain almost no events at all, that he could not remember when he had last meant a word he'd said aloud.

Not today. Not yesterday. He searched backward through the liturgy of his life — the thousands of responses, the tens of thousands of copied verses, all of it stored in his memory whole and perfect and dustless, every page retrievable — and somewhere around his sixteenth year the meaning thinned out, and around his twelfth it vanished entirely, and before that there was only a boy drawing circles, believing they would open.

He had meant them once. He was nearly sure he had meant them once.

The response ended. Aldessa raised her hands. "Go in stillness," she said, "and may the Hollow keep what is written."

*What is written.* Not *what is true*. Not *what is loved*. Quentin bowed his head with the rest and felt the phrase settle onto him like a second robe.

The congregation rose and dispersed into the evening offices — kitchen duty, gate watch, lamp-trimming, sleep. Quentin remained kneeling a moment longer than the others, the way he sometimes permitted himself, thirty seconds of stillness that was his alone, unrecorded in any register. Through the high west window the sky had gone the color of pewter, and the mist was rising off the fields again, patient and total, folding the valley in.

And then, beneath the fading bell-tone, beneath the shuffling of sandals and the closing of doors, Quentin heard something that did not belong to the order of his evening at all.

Voices. At the outer gate. Not the usual murmur of a petitioner — those were low, apologetic, brief. These voices were sharp, overlapping, several brothers speaking at once in the particular pitch they used when the rulebook failed them. And threaded through them, thin and strange and utterly wrong for the Valley of the Hollow, another sound entirely.

Someone was crying. A child, by the sound. And the words the child was making — because they were words, Quentin realized, his scribe's ear catching the pattern of them before his mind could object — were in no language spoken on this side of the border stones.

He knelt in the emptying nave and listened, and felt, for the first time in eleven years, the closed circle inside him tremble — just slightly, just once — like a line of ink remembering it was allowed to move.
