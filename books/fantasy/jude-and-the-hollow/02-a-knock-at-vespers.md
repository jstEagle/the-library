## 2. A Knock at Vespers

The bell for Vespers rang at the seventh hour, as it had rung every evening of Quentin Kavanagh's twenty-six years, and like everything else in the Temple of the Hollow, it rang with a sound that seemed to fall dead an inch from its own mouth.

He had never been able to explain that to himself properly — not to Brother Fenwick, who would have called it blasphemy, and certainly not to himself, alone at his desk with the ledgers, where explanations were his only company. The valley swallowed things. Sound most of all. A shout in the temple courtyard carried no further than a murmur; the river at the gorge ran silent as poured glass; and when the great bronze bell above the cloister swung on its rope, the note it made seemed to end before it began, clipped clean, like a word spoken behind a closed door. Visitors from beyond the pass — the few the temple permitted, wool merchants mostly, and once a year a tax assessor whose face Quentin had studied with the hunger other men reserved for beautiful women — always remarked on it. *Quiet valley,* they said. *Good for contemplation.* And the brothers would smile their thin smiles and say yes, yes, the Hollow keeps us close.

Quentin had copied the phrase so many times he no longer heard it. *The Hollow keeps us close.*

He climbed the stairs from the scriptorium with ink still staining the creases of his knuckles, three hours of ledger-work behind him — grain tithes, candle-wax allotments, the endless arithmetic of debt in which every soul in the valley figured somewhere, including himself. Fourteen years, two months, eleven days remaining on his father's account. He did not have to count. He never had to count. Numbers stayed where he put them, the way everything stayed where he put it: his memory was a room without doors, and nothing that entered it ever left.

The brothers were already filing into the nave when he reached the corridor, forty-odd men in undyed wool, moving with the unhurried shuffling gait of people for whom hurry has been bred out like a useless limb. Vespers was the same every night. The same psalms in the old cadence, the same responses, the same long silence at the center of the service during which the congregation was meant to feel the presence of the Hollow — the sacred emptiness, the blessed absence, the stillness in which, according to the liturgy, all corruption of the outer world came to die.

*We give thanks for the silence,* they would sing. *For the wall unseen. For the peace that was bought.*

Quentin sang the responses automatically, his voice one thread among many, indistinguishable, while his mind did what it always did during services: wandered the archive corridors inside his own skull, re-shelving texts he'd copied, checking spines, dusting shelves that existed nowhere but in him. It was, he supposed, a kind of prayer. It was certainly the only kind that had ever felt honest.

The candles guttered in their iron stands. Mother Aldessa sat veiled in her chair beneath the eastern window, a shape of grey wool and stillness. Somewhere among the brothers, old Fenwick's wheeze kept time like a leaking bellows. Rain had come up the valley in the afternoon, and it drummed now on the slate roof, soft and ceaseless, and the whole nave smelled of wet stone and tallow and the cold mineral breath of the mountain.

Which was why, perhaps, no one noticed the knocking at first.

It came small — three raps, spaced carefully, almost polite — half-drowned by rain and psalm. But Quentin's ear was a scribe's ear, trained to catch the scratch of a miswritten character under fifty identical ones, and his head turned before the thought formed. The sound came again. From the western door. The pilgrim door, which had not opened, so far as Quentin knew, in nine years.

Brother Halcourt, nearest the door, frowned and rose. The singing faltered, thinned, stopped. In the sudden quiet the rain sounded enormous.

Halcourt unbarred the door, and the night leaned in — wind, rain-smell, darkness — and then something small moved out of the darkness and across the threshold, and the door was shut again, and the bar dropped, and there was a child standing on the temple stones.

She was perhaps eight years old, though it was hard to say; she was so caked in mud from crown to foot that she seemed less a girl than a thing the earth had exhaled. Her hair hung in black ropes. Her clothes might once have been wool, might once have been any color. She stood with her feet slightly apart, the way soldiers stand, or sailors, braced against a motion that wasn't there, and she looked out at the assembled brothers with eyes so pale a grey they seemed to hold no color at all — eyes like the sky through the null-stone window slits, like water over slate.

And then she spoke.

The language was like nothing any of them had heard. It was not the rounded valley-tongue, nor the trade-speech of the lowland merchants, nor the liturgical Old Tongue in which half the temple's books were written. It moved differently — quick, liquid, full of sounds made at the back of the throat and behind the teeth, syllables that seemed to turn corners. She spoke urgently, both hands rising in small sharp gestures, and though none of it could be understood, everyone understood *that*: this was a message. This was a plea. This was a child delivering something she had crossed mountains to deliver.

"What is it saying?" whispered Brother Anselm, and someone else said, "It's gibberish," and someone else, "It's possessed," and old Fenwick crossed himself with the particular violence he reserved for matters of doctrine.

Mother Aldessa raised one hand, and the nave went silent — truly silent, the way only the Hollow's silence could be, total and pressurized, as if the whole building had been submerged.

"Child." Her voice came out level and cool from beneath the veil. "Do you speak the tongue of the valley?"

The girl looked up at the veiled figure. Whatever she saw there, it stopped her voice mid-word. She took one step backward, mud squelching faintly on stone, and her hands came down slowly to her sides.

"She cannot understand you, Mother," said Halcourt. "Look at her."

"Then she is lost, and must be returned to where she was found."

"She was found at our door, Mother. At Vespers. That's — there are provisions. Sanctuary—"

"There are provisions for *pilgrims*," said Aldessa, "who come in faith and in speech. This is neither. The border wardens will have let something slip through — some vagrant family's castoff, or worse than a castoff." Something in the veil turned, very slightly, toward the door, as if measuring the weather outside by sound alone. "The rain will cover her tracks. Take her out. Set her on the road below the switchback. If she belongs to anyone, they will find her."

A murmur ran down the rows of brothers — not protest, exactly. The temple bred no protesters. It was more the murmur of men agreeing to be uncomfortable in a disciplined way. Two brothers moved toward the child, and the child watched them come, and did not run. That was the thing Quentin remembered afterward, the detail that would keep him awake: she did not run. She had crossed whatever lay beyond the pass, she had come down through rain and dark to the one lit door in the valley, and when men came to put her back out into it she simply stood and waited, as if she had already spent all her fear somewhere else, somewhere worse, and had none left for this.

*Turned out,* Quentin thought. *Into the rain, in the dark, below the switchback, eight years old.*

He had been raised in this temple since his father had signed him over against his debts at age nine. He knew its kindnesses intimately: they existed, they were real, they were dispensed like medicine, in exact doses, for exact reasons, and they always, always arrived attached to a ledger entry. Nothing in the Temple of the Hollow was given. Everything was *credited*. And he felt something move in his chest that he had not felt in years — not pity, or not only pity, but a kind of professional outrage, the offense of a man who has kept accounts all his life watching someone cook the books. Turning the child out was not mercy or discipline. It was an error. It was a line item written wrong.

"Mother," he heard himself say.

His own voice startled him. Forty faces turned. Mother Aldessa's veil tilted a degree, which from her was the equivalent of another man's stare.

"The archives," Quentin said, and his voice was steadier than he had any right to expect. "Forgive me, Mother. Brothers. I have copied — I copy everything that comes through the scriptorium, as you know, and I believe—" He swallowed. Curiosity, in the Temple of the Hollow, was a vice ranked just below pride. "I believe I have heard some of those words before."

"You have not," said Fenwick flatly. "No one has. The tongue is nonsense. Or devilry."

"It is not nonsense." Now that he had begun, the words came with the strange momentum of recitation, as if he were reading them off an inner page. "The sounds she makes — the throat-stop, the falling endings, the doubled consonants — they follow rules. Gibberish doesn't follow rules. And I have seen those rules. In the sealed stacks. There are fragments, pre-consolidation documents, in a hand the catalog calls *Old Valley Script*, and the phonetic glosses appended to them—" He stopped, aware suddenly of how much he was revealing, how precisely his memory was betraying him in public. "There are glosses," he finished, more quietly. "I remember them."

Silence. The rain filled it.

Mother Aldessa sat unmoving for so long that Quentin began composing, in the reflexive way of a lifelong debtor, the apology he would need. Then she spoke.

"Scribe Kavanagh remains. Everyone else — Vespers is concluded. Go."

The brothers filed out with the rustling obedience of rooks settling. Fenwick paused beside Quentin long enough to mutter, "Mind yourself, boy. Sealed means sealed," and was gone. The door boomed. The candles burned. The rain came down.

The child had not moved. She stood in the pool of candlelight with mud drying to a crust on her cheeks, watching Quentin with those colorless eyes, and he realized with a small cold shock that she had watched him the entire time he spoke — had tracked his voice across the nave with an attention that was not the attention of a frightened child but of someone listening *for something*, testing each sentence against an inner text of her own.

Mother Aldessa descended from her chair. Up close, the veil could not entirely hide her — the long jaw, the deep-set eyes gone pale with age, the mouth that looked as though it had been sewn shut and reopened many times. She circled the child once, slowly, the way an assessor circles livestock.

"Say it again," she told the girl. "What you said when you came in."

The girl looked at Quentin.

"Say it to him, then," said Aldessa.

The child drew a breath, and spoke, and this time Quentin listened the way he had been trained to listen his whole life: totally, helplessly, with the part of him that could not forget anything.

The sounds poured past him — urgent, rising, breaking into a rhythm like verse — and underneath them, in the sealed rooms of his memory, pages turned. He had copied those fragments four years ago, a punishment assignment, forty folios of crumbling pre-consolidation vellum that Brother Osric had wanted transcribed before they disintegrated, and had then locked away unfinished because the work disturbed him. Quentin had copied what he could read. Much of it was broken. But the glosses — a later scribe's attempts to render the old tongue in valley characters, phonetic, approximate, tantalizing — those had lodged in him like splinters, meaningless for four years, waiting.

They surfaced now, one by one, matching the child's voice like keys finding locks.

*Vess...* — vessel. The word appeared twice in the fragments he remembered, once in a phrase the gloss rendered roughly as *the vessel of the vow*. She was saying it now, pointing — not at herself, he realized with a lurch, but at her own chest, at the heart of her, and then opening her hand outward, at the walls, the valley, everything.

*Serrath...* — a name, or a title. In the fragment it had appeared beside a drawing of a tower.

And then a longer phrase, which she said slowly, distinctly, looking directly into his eyes as if she knew — as she somehow could not possibly know — that he was the only person in the world who might catch even a shred of it. The glosses gave him maybe a third of the words. *The seal ... forgets ... we come ... the hollow ... is not...*

Is not. Is not what?

The phrase ended. The child lowered her hands. The candles hissed. Somewhere far above, water ticked through a roof-gutter, counting.

"She is speaking Old Valley Script," Quentin said. His voice sounded foreign to him, coming from very far away. "Or something descended from it. Mother — that language is older than the temple. Older than the Consolidation. It shouldn't exist outside the sealed stacks. It shouldn't exist anywhere. It's a dead tongue. It's been dead for six hundred years."

"Languages leave corpses," said Aldessa. "Border villages rot into old habits."

"Not like this. Not whole sentences. Not—" He caught himself, mastered his voice. "The fragments I copied were liturgical, Mother. Prayers. And the phrases she used match them. Not approximately. Match." He looked down at the child, at the mud, at the pale eyes still fixed on him with that terrible patient attention. "Where did you learn these words?" he asked her, in valley-tongue, knowing it was useless, asking anyway.

The girl cocked her head. Then she pointed — at him, deliberately, unmistakably — and pressed her muddy fist against her own chest, and said one word.

It was not a word from the old tongue. It was rough valley-speech, accented strangely, worn down at the edges like a stone carried a long way: the word for *remember*.

The rain redoubled against the roof. Mother Aldessa stood very still, and Quentin, who had spent his life learning to read silences the way other men read letters, thought: *she is afraid. She knows something. She heard that word and something in her flinched.* But the veil hid everything but the outline of it, the shape of a thought, the way the bell's sound was hidden by the valley — present, pressing, and impossible to see.

"Take her to the infirmary cell," Aldessa said at last. "Feed her. Bathe her. Do not speak to her of scripture, of the temple, or of the border. Tomorrow she goes down to the wardens at the gatehouse, and the matter ends."

"Yes, Mother."

"And Scribe Kavanagh." He stopped at the door, the child's small muddy hand already curled, astonishingly, trustingly, into his. "Your memory is a gift of the Hollow. It is noted in your file. Be careful it does not become a liability instead."

"I don't understand, Mother."

"No," she said. "Not yet. Go."

He went, down the cold corridor with the child's hand in his, her steps light beside his own, leaving faint prints of dried mud on the flagstones like the tracks of a bird that had flown in through a window that should not have been open. She did not speak again. But twice on the way she glanced up at him and touched her chest with her fist, and repeated that one worn-down word — *remember, remember* — softly, like a lesson she was rehearsing for someone.

In the infirmary cell, by lamplight, he fed her bread and broth and watched her eat with the controlled ferocity of someone who had learned not to waste a crumb, and he turned the broken phrase over and over in the sealed rooms of his mind, unable to stop, unable to leave it shelved:

*The seal forgets. We come. The hollow is not—*

Is not what?

Outside, the rain went on falling on a valley that kept every sound it was given and returned none of them, and Quentin Kavanagh lay down beside a sleeping child who spoke a dead language, and did not sleep at all.

* * *

At dawn, before the bell, before anyone else stirred, he was at the door of the sealed stacks with a borrowed lamp in his hand and his heart going like a trapped bird, telling himself he would only look, only check one folio, only confirm.

The lock, he discovered, was newer than the door. And the keyhole had been changed within the year.

Someone had gotten there first.
