## 5. The Girl Called Nothing

The cell they gave her had been a storeroom once, before the temple's hunger for space outgrew its piety. It sat at the end of the novices' corridor, past the linen press and the room where Brother Halvard kept his infamous collection of dried saints' fingers, and it smelled of dust and lamp oil and the ghost of tallow. Quentin spent the better part of an afternoon making it habitable: a cot with fresh straw, a blanket from the donation chest — the good wool one, not the scratchy hemp he would have given anyone else — a basin, a candle stub, a stool. He did these things without deciding to do them, the way a man finds himself halfway down a road before he remembers setting out.

Mother Aldessa's instructions had been precise. *Keep her. Feed her. Listen to her. Write down everything.* The erasure of three years from his ledger hung over all of it like a coin balanced on a knife's edge, and Quentin told himself, as he smoothed the blanket's wrinkles for the third unnecessary time, that this was simply what obedience looked like. That any scribe in his position would do the same. That tenderness had nothing to do with it.

He was lying to himself, and he knew it, and he did it anyway, because the alternative was to admit that something in him had already turned toward this mud-caked, silent-eyed child the way a plant turns toward a crack of light in a shuttered room.

She stood in the doorway when he finished, watching him with that unnerving stillness she carried everywhere, as though stillness were a language she was fluent in and everyone else merely stuttered through. Her hair had been washed — he had insisted on that much, standing outside the bathhouse door while Sister Maren scrubbed her, listening to the child endure it without a single sound — and it fell now in dark, damp ropes past her shoulders. Clean, she looked younger. Eight, perhaps nine. There was a thinness to her wrists that suggested hunger of long standing, and older marks beneath it, faded scars he chose not to catalogue because cataloguing them would mean asking questions he wasn't permitted to ask.

"I've made you a bed," he said, and then felt foolish, because she didn't understand valley-tongue. He tried again with his hands, patting the cot, and she crossed the room in three silent steps and pressed her palm flat against the blanket, testing it, her fingers spreading wide as if reading something written in the weave.

Then she looked up at him and said a word in her own tongue. Softly. A question, by its shape.

"I don't know," he answered, uselessly, and then, because he was a scribe and words were the only tools he owned, he knelt and took the slate from his satchel and wrote upon it, in careful block letters: *BED*. Then he pointed at the cot. *BED*, he wrote again beside it. She watched the chalk move with an intensity that made him think of the way he himself had once watched Mother Aldessa unseal the archive doors — hunger and caution in equal measure.

She took the chalk from his hand. Her grip was small and certain. Below his letters she drew a shape: quick, economical strokes, and when she finished he saw it was a bird. Not a dove, not the temple's ubiquitous dove that stared down from every lintel with its plaster eyes closed. This was a smaller thing, round-bodied, sharp-beaked, with a tail cocked at an angle of unmistakable insolence. A wren. He knew it from the hedgerows behind the scriptorium, where the little birds scolded him each morning as though he owed them money.

"Wren?" he said.

She pointed at the drawing. Then at herself. Then she said the word again in her dead tongue, and the two syllables were different but the shape of them, the *feel* of them, matched so closely that the hair rose on his arms.

"You're naming yourself," he said slowly. "Or — no. You're telling me the name I just found is close enough."

She tilted her head, considering him, and then did something she had not done in the four days since her arrival at the temple doors. She smiled. It was a small smile, crooked, gone almost before it arrived, but it transformed her face entirely — from something watchful and ancient into something that was, unmistakably, a child's face. And Quentin Kavanagh, who had spent twenty-six years perfecting the art of feeling nothing on purpose, felt the strange hot pressure behind his eyes that meant feeling something on accident.

*Wren,* he wrote on the slate, and underlined it twice, and she nodded once, formally, like a queen accepting a treaty.

* * *

The temple's routine absorbed her the way a millstone absorbs grain — impersonally, and with every intention of grinding. She was woken at prime whether she wished it or not; she was marched to meals; she was seated in the chapel during offices where the priests prayed aloud for the continued Stillness of the valley, and Quentin watched her sit among the bowed heads like a stone in a river, unmoved by currents that bent everyone else.

It was during those hours that he studied her, guiltily, the way he'd once studied the sealed texts — knowing he shouldn't, unable to stop. She never fidgeted. Children fidget; it is their nature and their proof. But Wren could sit motionless for a full office, hands folded, gaze fixed on nothing, and when the bells finally rang she would rise and look at Quentin as if to say, *is that all your god requires of you?*

At night he came to her cell with the slate and a stub of chalk and they built their bridge across the silence plank by plank. He taught her valley words — *bread, water, sleep, no, yes, here* — writing each one and saying it aloud until she could repeat it in her low, rough voice, which was hoarse from disuse and had a strange doubled quality, as though two people spoke through one throat. In exchange she drew.

Her drawings were extraordinary. Not childish at all, or rather childish in the way that cave paintings are childish — direct, essential, stripped of everything except what mattered. She drew the temple courtyard from memory after seeing it once, accurate down to the number of flagstones. She drew faces: Sister Maren with her kind drooping eyes, Brother Halvard mid-scowl, Quentin himself rendered with such unsettling precision — the ink stain on his left middle finger, the cowlick at his crown — that he laughed aloud, alone in his own cell later, thinking about it.

But some nights she drew other things. And those things frightened him.

* * *

On the ninth night she drew the ship.

They had progressed to simple exchanges. *HUNGRY? YES. SLEEP? NO.* Wren slept badly; he had heard her through the wall, pacing, sometimes making small sounds that were not words and not quite weeping. So when he knocked and entered with the slate, she was sitting cross-legged on the cot, awake, waiting, as though she had known he would come.

She took the chalk and drew without hesitation, as if the picture had been standing behind her teeth all day.

A vessel — no, a ship, with a curved hull and a steering oar, of a design Quentin had never seen in any text permitted to him. Upon its sail she marked a symbol: a circle bisected by a vertical line, and within the upper half, three descending points like falling rain or dripping blood.

She tapped the symbol. Looked at him. Tapped it again.

Quentin's breath went shallow. He had seen that mark. Not on a ship, not anywhere in the living world — but in the sealed stacks, in the margins of the pre-kingdom liturgy he had copied into his memory at three o'clock on a morning that now felt like someone else's life. The sigil of the *Vessels of the Hollow Word*, the marginal note had called them, in a hand so old the ink had gone the color of dried blood. The last royal line of the drowned coast. Extinct, the archivists' gloss had said. *Extinct for nine hundred years.*

Except extinct things did not draw their own sigils on slates in temple storerooms.

"Where," he said, and then caught himself, and wrote instead: *WHERE DID YOU COME FROM?*

Wren took the chalk back. Beneath the ship she drew a coastline, and beyond the coastline a road, and along the road small figures — dozens of them, marching in a column, and above the figures a larger shape that she worked at for a long time, shading it in with fierce little strokes until it became a darkness with edges, a darkness shaped almost like a man but wrong, too tall, its hands too long, its head a blank oval with no face at all.

And beneath the dark figure, in her own tongue, she wrote a word. Two syllables. She said it aloud as she wrote it, and her voice cracked on the second syllable, and she said it again, harder, as if daring the word to frighten her.

Quentin did not know the word. But he knew its cousin. He had read it in the sealed liturgy, in the final folio, in a passage the old archivist had defaced with a cross-hatched pen-stroke so violent it had torn the vellum: *...lest the Unbinding come again, and the Hollow be filled...*

"The Unbinding," he whispered, translating without meaning to, the way one flinches.

Wren went very still. Then she pointed at the word on the slate. Pointed at his mouth. Pointed at the word again.

"You know it," he realized aloud, stupidly, in a language she couldn't follow. "You're checking. You want to know if I understand."

She nodded, slow and grave, and then she did the thing that undid him more thoroughly than anything before or since. She reached out and took his hand — his writing hand, the scribe's hand, ink-stained and larger than her whole face — and pressed it flat against the slate, over the dark figure, and held it there, and looked up at him with eyes that were not a child's eyes at all in that moment but something older and wearier and terribly, terribly afraid.

*This,* her gesture said, plainer than any tongue. *This is what I ran from. This is coming.*

He sat with her until the candle guttered out. He did not write the report that night. He told himself he would write it tomorrow, faithfully, everything, as Aldessa required. He told himself that a debt honestly served was the only freedom a man like him could ever hope for, and that curiosity was a luxury purchased with other people's safety, and that the sealed archives were sealed for reasons, and temples were temples because the world needed walls.

He told himself all of it, walking back down the novices' corridor in the dark, and none of it took root, because in his mind's eye — and here was the curse and glory of a scribe's memory, that nothing ever faded, nothing ever blurred, everything stayed sharp as the moment it was cut — he kept seeing the defaced folio, the torn vellum, the violence of that pen-stroke. Someone had wanted that passage destroyed. Someone had wanted the word *Unbinding* buried so deeply that even the memory of burying it was buried.

And a child had walked out of the sea-mist carrying the word in her mouth like a coal.

* * *

By the twelfth day she had thirty valley-words and used them with a thrift that would have shamed a miser. By the fifteenth she could make herself understood in emergencies, and by the twentieth — cold rain hammering the temple roofs, the whole cloister smelling of wet stone — she managed her first true sentence, assembled from her hoarded vocabulary with the fierce concentration of someone building a fire in a storm:

"No. Go. Back. No."

She said it while pointing at the eastern wall of the valley, though of course no wall was visible — the borders of the Hollow were invisible, a fact every child learned before learning letters, the way they learned that bread was shared and magic was nowhere. Beyond those invisible lines lay the world, and the world, the temple taught, was a wound the Stillness had been stitched over. Nothing crossed in. Nothing crossed out. The Peace held, had always held, would always hold.

Wren stood at the cloister's edge in the rain, pointing east, saying *no go back* with tears cutting clean channels through the rainwater on her face, and Quentin understood two things at once, the way one understands lightning and thunder as a single event.

The first was that whatever pursued her was real, and near, and east.

The second was that he had stopped taking notes for Mother Aldessa eleven days ago, and had instead begun keeping a private record — coded, in the old shorthand novices used for drafting, illegible to anyone but him — of everything Wren showed him. Every drawing. Every fragment. Every word of the dead tongue, transcribed phonetically in his flawless memory and duplicated in lead pencil in the back pages of his own prayer book, where no priest would think to look.

He was committing treason. Small treasons, chalk-sized, deniable. But treason compounds like interest, and Quentin Kavanagh, who had spent his life calculating debts to the last copper, could feel the new account opening inside him, could feel its balance growing, and knew with a certainty that bypassed all his careful reasoning that someday soon it would be called due.

That night, after lights-out, he sat at his desk with his prayer book open to the hidden pages and added the day's entry: the sentence *no go back*, and beneath it, the sigil of the Vessels of the Hollow Word, drawn from memory, exact.

And beneath that, because his hand seemed to move of its own accord, he wrote the question that had been forming in him for days, the question he had not dared think in daylight, in the temple, under the closed plaster eyes of the doves:

*If the vessel lives, what else have they lied about?*

Somewhere below him, in the dark of the storeroom cell, he heard Wren begin to pace.
