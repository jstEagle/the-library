## 6. What the Valley Forgets

*The silence is not empty.*

That is the first thing Wren knows here, and the first thing no one else seems to know at all.

They think the valley is quiet the way a room is quiet when everyone has left it. They think stillness is the same as peace. They light their little lamps and say their little prayers to a hollow god and they do not hear it — the pressure, the pressing, the vast weight of everything that is *not allowed in*, leaning against the edges of the world like water against glass.

Wren hears it. Wren has heard nothing else since the border stone.

She lies on the narrow cot in the scribe's room — his room, the one he gave her, the one that smells of ink and candle-tallow and the dust of old paper — and she presses her palm flat against the wall and listens with her skin.

*Pressing. Pressing. Pressing.*

The magic does not sleep outside the borders. The magic waits.

* * *

In the days before — and *before* feels far away now, farther than it should, as if the valley has stretched time itself thin as vellum — there was sound everywhere. Not noise. Sound. The hum under the roots of things. The green song of growing wheat, which is not a metaphor but a fact, a high silver thread you could follow through a field if you were born to hear it, and she was born to hear it, she was born from it, made of it, *made for it*.

Her mother sang to her once — or hummed, or breathed the notes into the crown of her head while the world burned behind them — and the note was the same note as the river. That is what home was. A place where every note agreed.

Here nothing agrees. Here everything is held apart, forced apart, pressed down under a great flat hand of nothing, and the hand never tires because the hand is not alive, it is *built*, and built things do not tire, they only wait to be unmade.

Wren knows about unmaking. She has seen it done to cities.

* * *

The child who arrived at the temple door cannot speak their tongue. This is what they believe, and they are wrong, and she lets them be wrong because being mute is safer than being what she is. But the truth is stranger and worse: she can understand them perfectly well. Their words arrive in her ears like stones dropped down a well — she hears each one strike, hears its meaning ripple out — but when she opens her mouth to answer, what comes up is the old tongue, the deep tongue, the language that was old when their temple was young, and she has learned what happens to children who speak the old tongue in places where the new one is law.

So she says nothing. She folds her silence around herself like a cloak, and inside the cloak she screams, sometimes, quietly, in a language only the dead remember.

The scribe — Quentin, the tall one with ink-stained fingers and eyes like a man reading something always just over your shoulder — the scribe is different. He watches her mouth move when she dreams aloud. His lips shape her words without knowing they do, the way a mirror shapes whatever stands before it. Once, half-asleep at his desk, he murmured back to her in her own tongue, badly, beautifully, like a child learning to walk in shoes too large.

He has read the forbidden pages. She knew it before he told anyone. She knew by the way he looked at her — not with fear, not yet, but with recognition, which is more dangerous than fear and rarer than rain in the dry months.

* * *

At night the pressing grows louder.

She has mapped it already, though she has walked hardly anywhere in this small captive country. It is strongest at the border stone, where the road climbs out of the valley — a wall there, a wall like the side of the world, so thick with withheld magic that standing near it makes her teeth ache and her blood sing a note of pure grief. Weaker along the eastern cliffs, where the rock itself is tired and thin and something leaks through in threads: a warmth in the moss there, a stubbornness in the wildflowers, a fox whose eyes held a flicker of old amber cleverness. The western marsh barely holds at all; she felt it the day she arrived, dragged past it in the wardens' cart, a soft spot in the glass like a thumbprint in ice.

And beneath the temple — beneath the very floor where the priests kneel and pray to the Hollow — the deepest press of all. Something vast lies under the valley, something the founders pushed down and pinned with the sky itself, and the null-field is not a blessing spread over the land like honey. It is a *lid*. It is a hand holding a mouth shut. It is the whole valley kneeling on the chest of something buried alive, and the people above it building their festival of Stillness on the grave and calling the silence holy.

Wren lies awake and feels it breathe.

Slowly. So slowly. Once an hour, maybe, the buried thing inhales, and for one terrible moment the null-field thins everywhere at once, like a sheet pulled taut, and the magic outside leans in close enough to touch the glass, and Wren's heart answers it, beats in time with it, *weeps* for it —

— and then the exhale ends and the lid comes down again and the valley forgets, the way it always forgets, the way it was made to forget.

* * *

She dreams of the sea of grass.

Not the real sea — she has never seen it — but the sea her mother described, the one beyond the mountains where the vessels used to gather before the hunting began, where the ground itself was so full of magic that walking on it was like wading in warm water and children learned to fly before they learned to fear. In the dream the grass sings in four-part harmony and her mother's hands are warm and there is no border anywhere, no glass wall, no pressing weight, and Wren is so happy in the dream that she wakes crying, every time, because happiness is now a foreign country and she has been exiled from it twice.

The worst part — the part she will never tell Quentin, never tell anyone — is that the dream is also a warning. Because the sea of grass is gone. She has seen what remains of it. She walked for nine days through ground that had been scoured down to grey glass and bone-dust, and the singing there was not singing anymore, it was screaming slowed down ten thousand times, and the things that did the scouring wore the faces of men.

Home would kill her. Home *did* kill everyone like her, one vessel at a time, patiently, thoroughly, the way water wears away stone. Her mother died so Wren could run. Her grandmother died so her mother could run. Somewhere behind her, strung back through the generations like beads on a wire, a whole line of women died running, and the running has brought her here, to the one place in the world where the thing inside her blood goes silent — and the silence is almost worse than the hunt.

Because in the silence she cannot feel her own gift at all. It is there — she knows it is there, it presses back against the null-field from inside her like a second heartbeat, a caged bird beating its wings against her ribs — but she cannot touch it. She is a lamp with the wick drowned. She is a bell wrapped in wool. She is *herself* with the self taken out, and the people here call this peace.

* * *

The girl called Nothing — that is what the temple servants call her when they think she cannot understand, and she understands every syllable — learns the rhythms of the valley anyway. She learns them the way a prisoner learns a yard: precisely, resentfully, completely.

Vespers at dusk, when the bells ring a pattern that is almost, almost the opening phrase of the old evening liturgy, worn smooth and emptied of meaning, a shell with the animal long dead inside it. She almost weeps the first time she hears it. Almost. She has learned not to weep where priests can see.

The festival banners going up in the market square, white cloth with a black circle, and she knows the black circle too — knows it as the sign the hunters painted on doors, knows it as the seal-sign, the lid-sign, the mark of the hand pressing down. Here they hang it from every eave and dance beneath it and give it sweets and songs. They have made a god of the prison door.

The ledger room where Quentin works, which hums — not with magic, never with magic here, but with the ghost of purpose, the memory of hands, and even that faint warmth is more kindness than the rest of the valley shows her.

And the border stone, always the border stone, visible from the high pasture like a white tooth in the green jaw of the hills. She looks at it the way other children look at the horizon: measuring, longing, afraid.

Because here is the truth she carries folded inside her silence, smaller than a seed and heavier than a mountain:

The glass is cracking.

She felt it the morning the bird fell. A hairline tremor through the whole field, a shiver in the pressing, and the buried thing under the temple rolled over in its sleep and sighed, and somewhere far beyond the hills something answered — something patient, something hungry, something that has spent a hundred years listening for exactly that sigh.

*They are coming.* She tried to say it that night, standing in the doorway of the scribe's room while Quentin trimmed his lamp. *They are coming to finish it.* But her mouth made only the old sounds, the deep vowels, and he tilted his head like a bird himself, trying, trying, and she saw him reach for a scrap of charcoal and she saw that he wanted to understand, and wanting-to-understand is such a rare and precious thing in this world that it nearly undid her.

Soon. Soon she will have enough of their words. She is learning fast — faster than fast, because the vessel-blood remembers languages the way rivers remember rain — and every day the cloak of silence weighs a little less and the danger underneath it weighs a little more.

* * *

Tonight the pressing is louder than she has ever felt it.

She sits up on the cot in the dark and puts both palms flat against the wall and the wall is cold and the cold is wrong, it is midsummer and the cold is coming *through* the null-field, leaking backward, the buried thing's breath frosting the underside of the lid. Outside the window the stars stand too sharp, too still, like nails driven into black cloth. The magic outside the border is not waiting anymore.

It is gathering.

Wren presses harder against the wall, as if she could push her hearing through the valley's skin, out past the fields, up the road, over the hills, and she strains and strains until her head aches — and there, at the very edge of what she can feel, at the outermost rim of the pressing, something crosses.

A small hole in the glass. A single thread slipping through, thin as spider silk, carrying a flavor she knows: iron, oath, old blood, a broken vow worn like a stone in the shoe of the soul.

Someone has come into the valley who should not have been able to come.

Someone carrying a sigil she last saw burning on a banner above a burning field.

The thread fades. The glass heals over it, slow as ice refreezing. But Wren stays sitting up in the dark long after, hands still pressed to the wall, heart drumming the old war-rhythm her body was built to keep.

The valley sleeps on. The lid holds. The buried thing breathes.

And down the corridor, in the room that smells of ink and tallow, the scribe who reads forbidden pages turns over in his sleep and murmurs a word in a dead tongue — and does not know that the child three doors away hears him, and understands, and is afraid for him most of all.

*They are coming,* she thinks, in the language of her mothers, in the language of ash. *And the valley has forgotten how to be afraid.*

The frost creeps a finger's width further across the window glass, midsummer-bright moonlight caught and frozen in it, and Wren watches it grow, and counts her heartbeats, and waits for morning to teach her whether she has found a protector or another kind of hunter.
