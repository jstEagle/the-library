## 8. Ledger Entries

The ink was the same ink. That was the thing that kept catching in Quentin's throat as he sat at his desk in the scriptorium's cold morning light, the quill balanced between fingers that had not trembled since he was nine years old. The ink was drawn from the same oak-gall vat, mixed with the same iron salt, poured into the same horn pot with its chipped lip. It did not know what it was being asked to do. It would carry a lie as smoothly as it carried a psalm.

*The child sleeps soundly,* he wrote. *She has begun to imitate common words — bread, water, door. Her understanding grows daily. Nothing of significance has passed her lips.*

He read the sentence back three times. It was good work. It was even true, if you held it at an angle and squinted. Wren had said bread. She had said water. She had said door, pointing at the door of the cell they'd given her in the hospice wing, and then she had said a great many other things besides — things in the dead tongue of the sealed stacks, things that made the hair on his arms rise when he lay awake cataloguing them against half-remembered folios — but Aldessa had not asked about those. Aldessa had asked for *everything*, and so everything was precisely what he was withholding, one careful omission at a time.

He signed the page. *Quentin Kavanagh, scribe, keeper by assignment.* His hand did not shake. That frightened him more than shaking would have.

* * *

Teaching her was harder than lying, which surprised him. He had assumed language would be the easy part — he was a man of words, after all; words were the one inheritance his father's ruin had left him intact. But Wren learned the way water learns the shape of a channel: patiently, inevitably, and always finding the cracks you didn't know you'd left.

They worked in the hospice garden because Mother Aldessa's order confined the girl indoors, and because Quentin had decided — with the specific, deliberate quality of a man committing his first real crime — that confinement could be quietly reinterpreted. *Fresh air aids her health, and her health is the temple's concern.* He had written that justification down too, in his private ledger of small treasons, and found it fit neatly beside the others.

"Sun," he said, holding up his palm to the pale light that came through the valley's ceiling of cloud like light through wax paper. "Sun."

Wren looked at his palm. Then at the sky. Then at him, with an expression of such patient, unblinking assessment that he felt less like a teacher than a specimen under consideration.

"Sul," she tried. The word came out rounder than his, older somehow, as though it had been buried a long time and she'd dug it up rather than borrowed it.

"Close enough," he said, and something in her face shifted — a flicker, quickly suppressed, of what might have been triumph. She was proud of herself. She was proud of herself and hiding it, and he recognized the habit instantly because it was his own. They were both people who had learned early that wanting things visibly was dangerous.

They worked through the morning. *Stone. Grass. Cold. Warm.* She collected valley-words the way he imagined her people — whoever they had been — might once have collected river-stones, turning each one over, testing its weight. And all the while she gave things back. When he pointed at the temple bell tower and said *bell*, she pointed at it again and said a word that meant, judging from the liturgy fragments he'd smuggled out of the archive in his memory, *the voice that gathers*. When he said *wall*, she touched the hospice's grey stone and said something soft and compound that his memory, treacherous, faithful memory, translated before he could stop it: *the keeping-place of sorrows.*

"You can't just say things like that," he muttered.

She tilted her head. *Say,* she repeated, tasting the word. And then, in the dead tongue, a question — he caught only the shape of it, the rising lilt, but the root-word was one he knew from a funeral rite nine hundred years out of use: *why do you keep?*

"I don't know what you're asking."

She did not believe him. He could see that she did not believe him, and worse, he could see that she understood why he lied, that she recognized the architecture of necessary silence because she lived inside one herself. A child of perhaps eight years, fluent in the grammar of concealment.

That evening he added a line to his false report: *She shows no interest in the temple's history or rites.*

It was the first entry he wrote knowing it to be false rather than merely incomplete. His quill paused over the page for the length of two heartbeats. Then he finished the word *rites*, blotted it, folded it, and carried it to Mother Aldessa's receiving tray like a man carrying a live coal across a wooden floor.

* * *

Mother Aldessa received him in the small audience chamber off the cloister, where the light came down through a single high window and fell on nothing but the table between them. She never let it fall on her face. Quentin had copied enough temple portraiture to know this was deliberate; the founders' icons all showed the abbots and mothers of the Hollow with light full upon them, faces open, *nothing hidden*. Aldessa preferred the shadow side of every room. Whether this was humility or strategy, he had never determined, though he was beginning — uncomfortably — to suspect there was less difference between those two words than the temple taught.

"She is learning?" Aldessa asked.

"Daily." He kept his eyes on the middle distance, on the wall behind her shoulder where a faded fresco showed the Peace — the great stillness descending on the valley like a benediction, the founders kneeling with their ledgers open. Even now, knowing what he knew from the sealed stacks, the image tugged at some obedient place in him. That was the cruelty of it. The lie was beautiful. The truth, as far as he'd uncovered it, was ash and murder, and beauty was doing most of the temple's work.

"And her tongue? This — dead speech of hers. Has she used it?"

"No, Mother."

The pause lasted exactly as long as it takes to decide whether to believe someone. He felt it pass over him like a draft under a door.

"No?" Aldessa said mildly. "A child alone in the world, speaking nothing else, and she simply... sets it aside?"

"She guards it," Quentin said, and heard the truth slip out before he could dress it as falsehood. He covered fast: "Children guard what they have left. I thought — you should know that she may speak it when she trusts us more. Progress may be slow."

Something eased very slightly in the room. He had offered her patience framed as diligence; zealots, he was learning, could forgive almost anything except the suspicion that they were being hurried.

"Slow is acceptable," Aldessa said. "Haste is how vessels break." She folded her hands. "You are doing well, Quentin. Better than well. There are few in this temple who could have been trusted with a charge so delicate."

*I am lying to your face,* he thought, *and you are thanking me for it,* and the thought arrived without triumph, without fear — just a flat, cold clarity, like reading his own name in a stranger's ledger.

"There is one more matter," Aldessa continued. "The Festival of Stillness approaches. Nine days hence. The girl will be kept within during the rites — the crowds unsettle children, and the festival is not..." a fractional hesitation, the first he had ever heard from her, "...not a spectacle for outsiders. You will remain with her. Consider it part of your charge."

"Of course, Mother."

But as he bowed and withdrew, the hesitation stayed with him, snagging like a burr in wool. Aldessa did not hesitate. Aldessa spoke of the festival the way she spoke of vespers and debt-ledgers and the mercy of the Hollow, in tones worn smooth by decades of repetition. For half a syllable, the tone had cracked, and beneath it he had heard — or thought he'd heard — something that sounded almost like grief.

Or guilt.

* * *

That night he could not sleep, and so he did what he had always done on sleepless nights: he took up his pen. But the private ledger was full, its pages crowded with the small treasons he'd been recording since Wren's arrival, and when he turned past them to the blank leaves at the back, his hand stopped.

Because here was the arithmetic that had woken him. Every lie in the ledger was small. *She sleeps soundly. She shows no interest. No, Mother.* Each one, weighed alone, was a feather. But feathers had weight, and he had been trained since boyhood to count weight precisely — it was the whole discipline of his life, the whole meaning of the ledger: that debts accumulate, that nothing is forgiven by being forgotten, that the total comes due whether or not you have kept track. Somewhere in the temple there was a doctrine for this, he was sure of it. Somewhere in the stacks he had not yet dared enter twice there was surely a treatise on cumulative sin, on how a man does not fall but *descends*, stair by stair, each step reasonable.

And below the stairs — what? He thought of the founders' names he'd found scratched into the margin of the sealed liturgy, the ones crossed out so violently the parchment had torn: *Maren of the Third Voice. Ossian, who set the stones. Ilbeth, last hand.* Murdered by their own temple, the record implied, once the seal was set and they knew too much. Had they started here? On a night like this, with a pen in their hands and a small false report drying under their fingers, each deception a single reasonable step?

He got up, dressed, and stood a long time in the doorway of Wren's cell before entering.

She slept curled toward the wall, one fist closed, breath shallow and quick — the breathing of a child perpetually ready to run. In the moonlight through the slit window her face looked carved rather than asleep. He had named her Wren for the small brown birds that lived in the temple eaves, ordinary and quick and easy to overlook, and the name had stuck because she seemed to accept being overlooked as a survival strategy. But watching her now, he thought the name had been a lie too, a comfortable little falsehood filed in the same ledger as the rest. She was not a wren. She was whatever slept inside the sealed stacks, bound in iron clasps behind a door the temple pretended not to remember building.

On the floor beside her pallet lay the slate he'd given her, wiped clean except for one drawing she'd done that afternoon and not erased. He crouched to look at it, though he already knew what it showed: the border stone. The grey standing stone at the valley's edge where the world went quiet, which she had never seen and yet had drawn perfectly — and around it, in her careful childish strokes, a ring of figures with their arms raised, pressing outward against an invisible wall.

Beneath the stone she had written a word in her own tongue. He knew it now. Three weeks of stolen lessons, and his traitor memory had assembled enough of the dead language to read a child's slate.

*Still trapped.*

Quentin sat down on the cold floor beside her sleeping form, put his back against the wall, and understood, with the calm of a man watching the last number of a column come clear, that he had crossed something. Not when he burned the first false report. Not when he lied to Aldessa. Here, on this floor, in this moment — because he had just realized what he would do, and had felt no fear at all.

If they moved against her — if the temple's quiet arithmetic ever produced a total that required Wren's blood — he would take her and run. Beyond the border, into the loud bright magicless-breaking world he had been taught to pity and fear. He would forfeit twenty-two years of counted servitude, four years short of freedom. He would lose the scriptorium, the oak-gall ink, the ordered days, the whole architecture of the life that had contained him like a page contains a line of text.

And the terrible, clarifying peace of it was that he could not make himself grieve.

He rose, took up her slate, and — instead of wiping it clean, as he should have, as a keeper reporting everything would have done — he added a line beneath her drawing, in careful valley letters she was only beginning to read:

*Nine days. Then we talk.*

He set the slate back where she would find it at dawn, and went out into the corridor with his heart beating slow and steady, like a man walking out of a burning archive with the only book worth saving held against his chest.

In the morning, the bells would ring for matins, and the valley would go on pretending to be at peace. In nine days, the whole valley would gather to celebrate the Stillness — and Quentin Kavanagh intended to be there, standing among the faithful with a liar's calm face, watching the rites he had copied ten thousand times as if for the first time.

Watching for what the celebration was really remembering.
