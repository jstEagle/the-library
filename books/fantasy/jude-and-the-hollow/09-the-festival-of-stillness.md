## 9. The Festival of Stillness

The bells did not ring.

That was the first thing Quentin noticed every year, and the thing that unsettled him most deeply, though he could never have said why. The Temple of the Hollow owned nine bells — he had copied their inventory himself, in his third year of service, each one weighed and inscribed in the ledger of sacred goods — and on the Festival of Stillness, not one of them was permitted to sound. They hung silent in their tower like drowned things. The whole valley, in fact, went quiet in a way that was not peace but performance: no cart wheels on the roads after noon, no dogs loose, even the blacksmith's hammer stilled by order of the Mother. The silence was manufactured, laid over the land like a shroud laid over a face, and everyone called it holiness.

Quentin had always called it that too. Until this year.

He stood at his post near the temple's western door, Wren's small hand swallowed inside his own, watching the procession assemble in the square below. She had been with him six weeks now — long enough that she'd stopped flinching at raised voices, long enough that she slept through the night in the cot beside his hearth instead of wedged against the wall — and still the temple had not decided what to do with her. Aldessa's summons had not come again. His reports continued, three per week, written in his careful hand: *The child ate well. The child spoke little. The child drew birds.* Each report was a small act of treason now, each omission a brick pulled from a wall he was no longer sure he wanted standing. He had stopped thinking of them as lies. He thought of them as mercy, which frightened him more.

"Stay close," he murmured to Wren. "When the chanting starts, don't look at the pyre."

She looked up at him with those grey eyes that seemed older than the valley itself and said nothing — she almost never spoke, and when she did it was in that dead tongue that lived only in the sealed stacks where he had no business having read — but her fingers tightened around his. That was language enough between them now.

Below, the procession formed. First came the acolytes in undyed wool, bearing the Nine Candles of the Peace, unlit — for fire, the liturgy said, remembered the old burning. Then the choristers, then the wardens in their grey, then Brother Coram carrying the Ledger of Stillness on a cushion of white linen, and last, robed in silver-grey that caught the autumn light like frost, Mother Aldessa herself. The crowd knelt as she passed. Quentin knelt too, pulling Wren down gently beside him, and felt the child go rigid as a snare-wire under his hand.

*It's only the festival,* he wanted to tell her. *It's only a day of quiet and candle-ash and sweet bread.*

But he knew, now, what the festival commemorated. He had spent four nights in the sealed stacks since the night he'd found the pre-kingdom liturgy, reading by stub-candlelight with his heart hammering like a fist on a door. And he had learned what the founders' chronicle called the day they sealed the valley. Not the Peace. The liturgy called it the Peace; the festival hymns called it the Peace; the ledgers called it the Peace. But the oldest record — the one written in a hand that shook, dated nine days after the valley's founding — called it something else.

It called it the Drowning.

*They came to us singing,* the shaken hand had written, *and we told them the hollow would keep them safe, and they believed us because we wore the robes they trusted. Four hundred souls crossed the border stone at dawn. By dusk there were none who remembered how to pray aloud. We set the seal while they slept. Forgive us. Forgive us. Forgive—*

The page ended there, mid-word, as if the writer had been interrupted, or had simply run out of the strength required for forgiveness.

Four hundred people, lured across the border and drowned — not in water, but in nullity, their magic smothered in their sleep like flames pinched out by wet fingers. The founders had needed vessels, living anchors, to set the seal. The chronicle never said plainly what became of the four hundred. But Quentin had noticed, in the inventory ledgers, an entry from the founding year that made his skin crawl every time he recalled it: *Nine bells cast. Alloy: bronze, silver, and remainder.* Remainder. As if human bone were something you wrote off as scrap.

He had not slept properly since.

Now the procession reached the border road, and the crowd rose and followed, and Quentin followed too with Wren's hand in his, toward the great border stone two miles east where the valley's nothing met the world's everything. The Festival of Stillness always ended there, at the stone, with the Nine Candles finally lit and the Hymn of Quiet sung into the border itself — a thanks-offering, the priests said, to the hollow that kept them safe from the madness beyond.

*Safe from the madness beyond.* Quentin walked with the founders' shaking handwriting burning behind his eyes and understood, at last, that he had grown up inside a lie so large it had its own holiday.

Wren stumbled, and he caught her, and saw that she was staring east toward the border stone with an expression he had never seen on a child's face — an expression like recognition, like grief arriving ahead of schedule.

"It's all right," he said, and heard how thin the words were.

She looked at him. And in that dead, beautiful, buried tongue, she said a single word — soft as ash falling — that he had read only once, in the margins of the pre-kingdom liturgy, glossed in the founders' hand:

*Mourning-day.*

* * *

The border stone stood alone in a mown field, a grey monolith taller than three men, carved all over with script so worn that most villagers believed it was merely decoration. Quentin knew better now. It was the same script as the liturgy. It was Wren's tongue. The seal itself, written in the language of the people it had drowned.

The crowd gathered in a wide crescent, hundreds strong, breath steaming in the late-afternoon cold. The acolytes arranged the Nine Candles in their iron ring before the stone. Coram opened the Ledger of Stillness upon its stand, and Mother Aldessa lifted her arms, and five hundred people fell into a silence so complete that Quentin could hear the grass moving.

He had stood in this silence every year of his life since he was six. He had always found it beautiful — the whole valley holding its breath together, the sense of being one body, one devotion. Today he stood inside it and felt it for what the founders' chronicle had taught him it was: the sound of a held-down lid.

Aldessa began the Hymn of Quiet, her voice thin and clear as a wire. *O Hollow, keep us. O Silence, hold us. What sleeps beneath, let sleep forever.* The crowd took it up, the harmonies braiding, rising, and Quentin sang too, automatically, the way one does anything done ten thousand times — and then he felt Wren's hand go slack in his.

She wasn't singing. She was listening. Listening the way he imagined a drowning woman listens to rain — hearing in the hymn something the singers did not know they were saying. Her lips moved, shaping translations. He leaned close, and caught fragments, and his blood turned to ice water as the meaning assembled itself in him, line by line, because the hymn — the beloved, harmless, childhood hymn — was not a prayer at all.

It was a lock-verse. A maintenance chant. Every word of it existed in the pre-kingdom liturgy he had read in the sealed stacks, in the section headed, in the founders' hand, *Words for the Keeping of the Seal.* The congregation of the Festival of Stillness renewed the seal every year without knowing it. Five hundred voices, singing themselves back into prison and calling it grace.

And the candles — the Nine Candles, lit at the hymn's end, one for each bell, one for each — Quentin's mind supplied the arithmetic before he could stop it. Nine bells, alloyed with *remainder*. Nine candles. Nine anchors. If the bells held bones, the candles held names, and the names —

The acolytes touched flame to the wicks. Nine small fires bloomed in the windless air.

Wren began to cry.

Not the crying of a frightened child — Quentin had heard that crying, weeks ago, in the first nights, and it had been loud and animal and young. This was different. This was soundless. Tears ran down her mud-brown cheeks in steady lines and her mouth stayed closed and her eyes never left the candles, and she wept the way the very old weep, the way people weep at graves of those no one else remembers. Around her the crowd sang on, faces lifted, eyes bright with festival joy, and not one of them saw the small girl mourning in their midst. Not one of them knew there was anything to mourn.

Quentin put his arm around her and drew her against his side, and looked up at Mother Aldessa, radiant in her silver-grey, and hated her — suddenly, wholly, with a purity of hatred that shocked him — and then hated himself more, because Aldessa did not know either. She sang her hymn in honest faith. They all did. That was the horror of it, the true horror, the horror that would keep him awake for the rest of his life if he let it: the massacre had been so complete that even its monument was loved. The victims' descendants decorated their own cage with flowers once a year and thanked it.

The hymn ended. The silence returned, deeper than before, and in it — Quentin would swear this until his dying day, however short or long it proved — in it he heard something answer.

Faint as a heartbeat through a wall. Faint as a knock. From beyond the border stone, from the other side of the nothing, something vast pressed against the seal the way a sleeper presses against a locked door, testing it, patient, and the seal — thinned by six weeks of frost in midsummer, weakened by whatever slow rot was eating the valley's edges — the seal creaked.

Only he heard it. He was almost sure only he heard it.

Then Wren's hand clenched his, hard enough to hurt, and he understood that she had heard it too, and had been hearing it for six weeks, every hour of every day, the way you hear water dripping in a house no one else notices is flooding.

* * *

They walked home in the blue dusk, ahead of the crowd, because Quentin could not bear another minute of the singing. The festival fires were being kindled in the villages behind them; ahead, the road lay empty and the first stars were coming out over the hills — the ordinary, faithful, magicless stars of the valley.

Halfway home, on the low bridge over the millstream, Wren stopped.

She pointed at the water.

Quentin looked. The stream ran black and quick under the bridge, catching starlight — and along its surface, drifting downstream toward them, came light. Not reflected light. Emitted. Small gold motes, dozens of them, moving against the current, gathering where the girl stood as if the water itself had recognized her. They clustered around her ankles like fish come to be fed. When she laughed — a tiny, wondering, broken laugh, the first he'd ever heard from her — the motes spun upward and hung in the air around her head, a crown of drifting sparks, and then, one by one, went out.

The valley was magicless. The valley had been magicless for three hundred years. Quentin stood on the bridge with his heart slamming and knew that he had just watched the impossible happen, watched the null-field fail for half a breath around the last vessel of the bloodline it had been built to drown.

"Wren." His voice came out strange. "Did anyone else see?"

She shook her head slowly. Down the road, the festival crowds were still distant, still singing.

Then she tugged his sleeve and pointed again — not at the water this time, but east, back toward the border stone, invisible now beyond the darkening hills. And she said, in the valley-tongue he had taught her, carefully, in her small cracked voice, the first full sentence she had ever spoken to him:

"The knocking is getting louder."

Behind them, far away, one of the temple's nine bells — the bells that were never permitted to ring, the bells that had not rung in three hundred years — rang. Once. A single deep bronze note rolling across the valley floor like a stone dropped into a well, silencing the festival fires, stopping the harvest songs, raising every head in the valley at once.

Quentin took Wren's hand and ran.
