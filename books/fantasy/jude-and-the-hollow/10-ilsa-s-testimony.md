## 10. Ilsa's Testimony

Ser Ilsa Vane had not slept in a proper bed since the night she was cast out of her order, and the narrow pallet behind Quentin Kavanagh's cell in the temple's guest quarters — a charity so thin it was nearly an insult — reminded her why she had stopped trying. Beds made a soldier soft. Beds also gave her dreams, and her dreams had teeth.

So she sat awake instead, on the floor with her back to the wall and her sword across her knees, listening to the valley breathe its breathless silence. That was the thing no one here seemed to hear anymore: the absence. Beyond the border stones, the world hummed like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing. In here it was all felt and wool and candle-smoke, a silence so complete it pressed against the eardrums like deep water.

She had been in the Temple of the Hollow for nine days now. Nine days of watching the scribe Quentin carry the child Wren through his careful, frightened routines. Nine days of watching Mother Aldessa smile with her mouth and count with her eyes. Nine days of deciding whether she owed this place anything at all.

She had decided, finally, that she did not. But she owed Quentin something. He had pulled her half-drowned from the millrace in autumn, when any other man would have let an exiled knight sink for the sake of his ledger standing, and Vane's word was the one possession the order had left her when they took everything else. She paid her debts the way she fought: directly, and without much care for what broke.

Tonight, then. While the temple slept and the wardens made their slow circuit of the outer wall.

* * *

She found him where she knew she would find him — the scriptorium, after midnight, by the light of a single lamp trimmed low enough to pass as embers if a warden glanced in. Quentin sat hunched over a copying desk with his stylus moving in small, precise strokes, though when she came closer she saw the page was blank. He was writing nothing. Just moving his hand in the shape of letters, the way a man paces when he cannot sleep.

"You'll ruin your eyes," she said quietly.

He flinched hard enough to knock the inkwell; she caught it before it went over, set it down, and lowered herself onto the bench beside him. Up close he smelled of lamp oil and cheap soap and the faint mineral tang of the archive dust that clung to him no matter how often he washed. A scholar's smell. A buried man's smell.

"Ilsa." He kept his voice below the hearing of the stone. "You shouldn't be out of your cell."

"Neither should you be writing nothing at midnight."

He looked at the blank page as if noticing it for the first time, and something moved across his face — shame, or fear, or both wearing each other's clothes. "I keep composing the report," he said. "The one I'll have to give Aldessa tomorrow. About Wren. What she drew today." His hand closed around the stylus until the knuckles whitened. "And I keep finding that every honest version of it damns her."

Ilsa studied him. In the lamplight he looked very young, though he could not be less than five-and-twenty — young the way men are young who have never had to choose between two terrible things and so have never truly chosen anything. She remembered being that kind of young. It had taken a burned village and a direct order to cure her of it.

"Then don't write the report yet," she said. "Write nothing. Listen instead. There are things you need to know before you decide which side of that page you stand on."

His eyes came up. Grey eyes, tired, too intelligent for his own comfort. "What things?"

"What lies beyond the stones," Ilsa said. "What I walked through for three years to get here. What's coming, whether you open your eyes to it or not." She turned on the bench to face him fully. "You've never left this valley. Neither has anyone in it who still draws breath. You think the world outside is like this one — quiet, magicless, governed by ledgers and prayer and the slow tyranny of good bookkeeping. Am I wrong?"

"It's what we're taught," Quentin said carefully. "That the Peace holds everywhere. That the Hollow was—"

"The Hollow is a room with the windows painted black," Ilsa said. "Sit still. This will take a while, and I'd rather tell it once than twice."

* * *

She began, as all soldiers begin, with the road.

"When they stripped my spurs and put me out of the Order of the Pale Standard, I went east, because east was the direction I'd been ordered to burn and I wanted to see what my refusal had saved. Do you know what I found?" She watched his face. "I found that the war never ended, scribe. It just learned better manners.

"There's a kingdom called Vessany, three weeks' ride past the eastern stones. They've rebuilt the old aqueducts — the ones your archives will call 'the works of the First Age' — and they run them with bound elementals, water-spirits chained into the stonework, turning the wheels and lifting the water day and night. The spirits scream, Quentin. You can hear them at night if you sleep near the channels. The citizens have stopped hearing it. They call it the singing of the pipes.

"In the Marches of Tor there are hedge-knights — men and women sworn to lords who feed them battle-magic through collars. Not the crude fire-throwing of the old tales. Subtler. Courage poured into a soldier like wine until he feels no fear and no mercy and no cold. They fight all winter without fires. They die smiling, because the magic doesn't let them do otherwise.

"And in the south, in the cities along the Salt Road, they've rebuilt the colleges. The ones your founders sealed away. They teach children to read the old tongue — the same tongue your little Wren speaks — and they teach them what the vessels are for."

Quentin's stylus had gone still. "Vessels," he repeated. "Wren used that word. Or drew it. A cup, always a cup."

"A vessel is a person born with the old blood," Ilsa said. "A living key to the sealed works. When the founders locked the great magics away — the war-magic, the binding arts, everything your Peace pretends never existed — they didn't destroy it. You can't destroy power like that. You can only lock it, and every lock wants a door. So they made doors of flesh. Bloodlines that carried the key in their bodies, generation to generation, dormant, harmless, as long as no one knew to turn them." She paused. "Everyone knows now.

"For three hundred years the vessels hid among ordinary folk and mostly lived ordinary lives and died unremarked. But the seal is old, scribe. Old and thinning — you've seen the signs yourself, even if you haven't named them. And when the great powers of the outer kingdoms understood what a vessel could do — that the right bloodline, properly prepared, could open the seals and take the war-magic for themselves — the hunting began."

"Hunting." He said the word flatly, the way men repeat a thing they refuse to feel.

"Families," Ilsa said. "Whole family lines, tracked across borders, taken alive. Because a dead vessel is a broken key. They keep them. Feed them, clothe them, educate them — some of the great houses treat their captured vessels better than their own children. And when the moment comes, when one kingdom decides it's time to claim the old powers before its rivals do—" She shrugged, and hated how casual the shrug sounded. "They use them. The last war between Vessany and Tor started over exactly that. Two years of it. I walked through the aftermath. I won't describe it. Some things a scribe shouldn't have in his memory, and yours is perfect, so guard it."

The lamp guttered. Neither of them moved to trim it.

"They told us," Quentin said at last, very quietly, "that the valley was spared all that. That the founders built the Hollow as a refuge. That the nullness at the borders is mercy — that magic cannot cross, so the wars of the outer world cannot follow us in."

"That may even be half true," Ilsa said. "Mercy wears many uniforms. But ask yourself, scribe — mercy for whom? If the null-field keeps magic out, it also keeps the vessels in. A vessel inside the Hollow can't be used. Can't be hunted. Can't be *spent*. The valley isn't just a refuge. It's a vault." She leaned closer. "And someone has just carried the last key into your vault through the front gate, and your Mother Superior knows exactly what she's holding. Think about why a woman like that lets a foreign child stay."

"She said it was charity."

"Mother Aldessa has never done a charitable act in her life," Ilsa said. "I've met her kind before. They don't break rules; they own them. Whatever she intends for that girl, she'll dress it in scripture and call it kindness while it happens."

Quentin was silent for a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped further, gone rougher. "You said the seal is thinning. How do you know? What did you see?"

* * *

Here Ilsa hesitated, because what she had seen was the thing that had broken her career, and telling it aloud to a temple man felt like handing a knife hilt-first to the enemy.

But he had pulled her from the river. And debts were debts.

"On my way here," she said, "I crossed the borderlands north of the Hollow. Wild country — the old battlefield, or what's left of it. The maps call it the Ashmoor. Three centuries ago it was farmland, before the final battle of the Unbinding War, before the founders sealed the field itself under the greatest working ever laid down. Your festival celebrates the Peace that followed. Well. I stood on the Peace.

"The ground there is wrong, scribe. Grass grows in patterns — spirals, sigils, half-formed words. Birds fly around certain hollows the way water flows around a stone. And in the center of the moor there's a depression, maybe half a mile across, where nothing grows at all, and the air above it shimmers like heat-haze in dead winter." She wrapped her hands around her knees. "When I camped there — I know, I know, a fool's campsite, but I was wounded and it was sheltered — I heard it. Under the ground. A sound like a choir holding one note forever, except sometimes the note *slips*. Slides a half-step, like a singer losing breath. Each year, the people in the nearest village told me, the slipping gets worse. They measure it by the church bells. Bells ring true elsewhere and go sour over the moor, and the sourness spreads a little farther every spring.

"The seal is failing. Not breaking — failing. Like a rope that's frayed down to its last strands. And everyone beyond those stones with any learning knows it. The kingdoms aren't preparing for *whether* the old powers wake. They're preparing for *who controls them when they do*. Every faction, every crown, every college and cult is racing to gather vessels before the seal gives. Because whoever holds the most keys when the lock opens owns the next age of the world."

"And Wren?"

"Wren is not one key among many." Ilsa held his gaze. "Her bloodline is the founder's line. The first vessel-line, the master key, the one the others were cut from. I recognized her sigil the day I arrived — the wren-and-thorn mark on her drawings. My old order wore a version of it. We were sworn, three hundred years ago, to protect that line, and we failed, and the order dissolved into the very kingdoms it was meant to guard against, and somewhere along the way we became hunters ourselves." Her jaw tightened. "That's what my refusal was about, in the end. They ordered me to take a vessel family — a mother and two children hiding in a mill town — and deliver them to a Vessanite college. I refused. They burned the town anyway, with the family in it, to punish the insubordination. So yes, scribe. I know what's coming for your girl. I've watched it happen to others, and I helped, once, by doing nothing fast enough."

Quentin had gone very pale. "If she's the master key — if the seal fails and she's here, inside the vault —"

"Then whoever takes her out controls the Unbinding," Ilsa finished. "Or—" She stopped. Looked toward the dark doorway of the scriptorium, listening, but only the temple's night sounds answered: settling timber, distant footsteps of the wardens, wind worrying the shutters. "Or she's returned to the seal itself and given back willingly, and the founding rite renewed, and the whole rotten race for the old powers starves out. That's what remains of my oath, scribe. One duty left in the whole broken covenant: get the last vessel to the seal before anyone else gets to her. The Pale Standard's original charge, from before the rot. Return the key to the lock."

* * *

Quentin sat with his hands flat on the blank page, as though feeling for a pulse in it.

"You're asking me to help you take her out of the valley," he said.

"I'm telling you what must happen," Ilsa said. "Whether you help is between you and whatever conscience the temple has left you. But understand the arithmetic. Outside these walls there are perhaps a dozen living vessels left in the world, and every crown and college is gathering them like coins before a famine. Wren is the coin that buys the whole treasury. If the temple keeps her — if Aldessa does whatever Aldessa means to do — the valley becomes either the fortress that guards the last key or the altar it's spent on; I genuinely don't know which, and neither, I suspect, does Aldessa. If the outer powers learn she's here — and they will learn, the signs of a thinning seal travel faster than rumor — they will come, scribe. Not with armies at first. With pilgrims, traders, missionaries. Then with armies. The valley's null-field won't save it. Null-fields don't stop knives, and they certainly don't stop siegecraft, and the kingdoms have three hundred years of grievance stored up against the place that stole the world's magic and called it peace."

"So the choice is to hand her to the seal," Quentin said slowly, "or watch the world come through the walls to take her anyway."

"That's the shape of it."

"And what does she want?" His voice cracked on the last word, and he steadied it with visible effort. "Has anyone — has *anyone*, in all your arithmetic, in all the oaths and orders and crowns — asked the child what she wants?"

It was, Ilsa thought later, the moment she began to believe the scribe might actually be worth the risk. Not the cleverness, not the archive knowledge. The question. No one else had asked it. She hadn't asked it herself.

"No," she admitted. "No one asks vessels what they want. Wanting is not considered relevant to their function." She rose from the bench, joints aching, and stood looking down at him in the dying lamplight. "Ask her, then. Tonight, tomorrow, whenever you next sit with her over your drawings. Ask her what she wants. And then weigh her answer against everything I've told you, because I'll be honest with you, scribe, as honestly as I know how: if her answer conflicts with the return to the seal, I don't know yet which way I'll fall. Duty says one thing." Her hand drifted, unconsciously, to the sword across her lap. "Conscience has been screaming at me for three years. I'd like to hear them agree, just once, before I die."

She moved toward the door, then paused, her hand on the frame.

"One more thing. The reason I came to this valley at all, after the burning — besides the debt I owed the river, besides the oath. On the Ashmoor, the night I heard the seal slip, I also saw lights. Out on the moor, in the dead center, above the hollow where nothing grows. Lanterns. A whole procession of them, moving in a pattern no pilgrim walks. Someone is already performing rites on the failing seal, scribe. Preparing it. Loosening it, God help us all, or tuning it like an instrument. And the sigils I could make out on their banners were Vessanite." She let that settle. "They don't know the master key is sitting in your scriptorium's keeping. Yet. But the seal slips louder every season, and the whole outer world has its ear pressed to the ground."

"How long?" Quentin whispered. "How long do we have?"

"If the slipping continues at the pace the bell-ringers measure?" Ilsa shook her head. "Two years. Perhaps three. Perhaps less, now that she's here — a vessel of her line changes the arithmetic just by breathing near the stones. The clock started long before either of us was born, Quentin. All we get to choose is whose hands are on it when it strikes."

She slipped out into the corridor's darkness, past the sleeping cells, past the wardens' distant tread, and left the scribe alone with his lamp and his blank page and the first honest weight he had ever been asked to carry.

Behind her, through the stone, she thought she heard the scratch of a stylus beginning — at last — to write something real.
