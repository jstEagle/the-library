## 13. Wren Speaks

The frost came again on the ninth night of midsummer, and this time it did not confine itself to the garden.

Quentin woke before the bell to a cold that had no business being in the world. His window was furred white at the edges, fern-patterns of ice climbing the glass from the sill as though something outside had pressed its palm against the pane and left its print in winter. He lay still beneath his blanket and listened to the temple breathe around him — the creak of old timber, the settling of stone — and told himself, as he had told himself for nine nights, that the wardens would handle it. That Mother Aldessa's prayers were handling it. That the valley had stood silent for three hundred years and would not break now, not over one mud-caked child who drew pictures of burning towers on scraps of vellum.

He did not believe himself. He had stopped believing himself somewhere around the third night, when he had watched a sparrow fall out of a clear sky over the barley field and land stiff as a carved thing, and the farmers had crossed themselves and looked, all of them, toward the temple where Wren slept under Aldessa's "protection."

Protection. The word sat wrong in his mouth whenever he used it now. It had begun to taste like the word *ledger* — like something that measured what you owed rather than what you had.

He rose in the dark and dressed by touch. The dormitory corridor was cold enough that his breath showed, which it never did in midsummer, never in the whole of his twenty-six years in the valley. The Hollow kept its own weather, mild and gray and unchanging, the way a locked room keeps its air. Weather that changed was weather that came from somewhere else.

Wren's chamber was at the end of the novices' wing, the room they had given her when Aldessa assigned her to Quentin's keeping — a word he had come to understand meant *custody*, though neither of them said so aloud. A candle burned behind the door's seam. She still did not sleep well; she had not slept well any night since the festival, since she had stood among the celebrating crowds and wept at the rites of Stillness while everyone around her sang.

He knocked softly, two fingers, the way he'd taught her meant *only me*.

"Come," she said.

Not *come in*. Just *come* — clipped, valley-tongue, one of the forty-odd words she had gathered into herself these past weeks the way a magpie gathers buttons and foil. But she said it, and it was her voice doing the saying, and every time it happened Quentin felt the small astonishing weight of it: a girl who had arrived at the temple door mute as a stone speaking at all.

She was sitting cross-legged on the bed with her back to the door, watching the frosted window. Her drawings were spread across the blanket — dozens of them now, weeks of them, the same images recurring like verses in a liturgy: the tower with the crown of smoke. The circle of figures with their hands joined. The crack. Always the crack, splitting whatever page she drew it on, jagged as a lightning scar, running through towers and hills and once, memorably, through a careful rendering of the temple itself, which Quentin had chosen not to report to Mother Aldessa.

"You saw it too," she said without turning. She touched the window. "The cold."

"The frost, yes." He came and sat at the foot of the bed, careful of the drawings. "It's been three nights now—"

"Nine." She turned then, and in the candlelight her face was grave in a way that made her look far older than her eight or nine or ten years — no one knew, not even her, perhaps. "Nine nights. You only counted three because you only started looking when the dead bird fell." She held up nine fingers, then folded them down one at a time, deliberate, showing her work the way he showed his when he taught her numbers. "I counted from the first. I know what counting means. Counting is how you know how much time is left."

Something moved through Quentin's chest, slow and heavy, like water finding a new channel. "Wren. How long have you been able to say all that?"

She considered the question with the seriousness she brought to everything, head tilted, eyes dark and steady. Then she shrugged, one shoulder, an oddly valley gesture she must have learned from the kitchen girls.

"Always could. Mouth didn't want to. Now it wants to." She smoothed one of the drawings flat on the blanket — the tower, the smoke, the joined hands. "You wanted me to talk. You waited. Nobody else waits." A pause, and then, quieter, with the particular bluntness of children: "Mother Aldessa doesn't wait. Mother Aldessa *watches*."

The words went through him like a needle finding a seam. *Report everything she says.* He had filed eleven reports. Eleven neat, faithful summaries of a child's drawings and fragments, each one softened, each one sanded down, each one a small treason dressed as diligence. He had told himself the softening was kindness. Sitting here now, with her dark eyes on him and the frost climbing the glass behind her like a hand spreading its fingers, he understood that the softening had been for himself — that he had been building, entry by entry, a version of Wren harmless enough that he could bear to hand her over.

"Wren," he said. "What you've been drawing. The tower. The people holding hands. The—" he touched the crack. "I need you to tell me. In words. All of it. Can you do that?"

She looked at him for a long moment. Outside, somewhere in the frozen dark, a bell that should not have been ringing rang — one low, confused note, the vesper bell struck by wind or by a warden's nervous hand, and they both flinched, and in the flinching something settled between them, some last hesitation giving way.

"I can," she said. And then, in a voice that was not quite her own — older, cadenced, the rhythm of it rising and falling like the pre-liturgical chants he had found in the sealed stacks — she said:

"They are coming to finish it."

* * *

For a moment neither of them moved. The candle flame leaned in a draft that should not have existed indoors, in a sealed valley, in a world without magic, and righted itself.

"Finish what?" Quentin asked. His voice came out thin.

"The war." She said it plainly, the way children say terrible things, having no practice yet in cushioning them. "The one your books don't have. The one your temple took out." She tapped the floorboards with one bare heel, twice, meaning *down*, meaning *beneath*. "Your valley isn't peace. Your valley is a lid."

Quentin's mouth was dry. He thought of the archive of ash, the records of the founders — Scribe-First Maren and her nine companions, whose names appeared in the public histories as blessed architects of the Peace, and in the sealed marginalia as something else entirely: nine voices cut off mid-entry, the final pages of the founding ledger written in a different, hurried hand. Murdered by their own temple once the seal was set. He had carried that knowledge alone for a month now, turning it over in the dark like a stone with a warm side and a cold side, unable to set it down.

"A lid on what?" he asked, though part of him already knew. Part of him had known since he first matched her sounds to the pre-kingdom liturgy and felt the floor of his world tilt.

"On the fighting." She reached for the drawings and began arranging them on the blanket, quick and certain, laying out a sequence the way he laid out pages of a text. "Before, there was magic everywhere. Big magic. War-magic. People threw fire the size of hills. People made soldiers out of stone and grief. Whole countries went black in an afternoon." She placed the tower drawing first. "This was the last city. My mother's mother's city. They fought until there was almost nothing left to fight *with* — not armies. *People.* The land itself was dying of it."

Her valley-tongue faltered on some of it — she reached for words and sometimes found gaps, and filled them with gestures, or with the old tongue's cadences, or with a patience that let him supply the word and nod when it was right. But the shape of it came through, relentless, and Quentin sat very still and let it pour over him like cold water.

"So the ones who were left — the wise ones, the tired ones — they did a great working. The biggest one ever done." Her hands came together, palms pressing, fingers interlaced, binding. "They couldn't kill the war-magic. It doesn't die. It's too old and too angry. Killing it would be like killing a river by drinking it. So they *sealed* it. They pushed it down into the deep place — under your temple, maybe, or under the mountain, the drawings don't say — and they locked it with blood." She touched her own wrist, the inside, where the veins run blue. "Their blood. The blood of the vessels. Nine families gave a daughter each, and the daughters' blood became the lock, and the lock passed down the daughters' lines, mother to child, child to child, forever."

"And the valley," Quentin said slowly. "The silence. The reason no magic crosses our borders—"

"Is the lock." She nodded. "Your whole valley is the keyhole turned. The null-place isn't protection." She used his own word back at him, gently, like setting down something fragile. "It's the *inside* of the seal. The quiet place they built around the wound so it could close." Her brow furrowed, searching for the next piece. "But locks aren't free. A lock has to be *fed*. The vessel line keeps the lock strong just by living — our blood remembers the working. As long as a vessel breathes inside the seal, it holds. That was the bargain. The nine families scattered to the corners of the world, and each generation, someone carried the blood, and the seal held, and the war stayed asleep."

She stopped. Her hands, which had been moving constantly, shaping the story in the air, went still in her lap.

"But someone found the last vessels," she said. "Found them and killed them. All but me." She held up one finger. "My mother put me on the road when I was small. Told me: *run to the quiet valley, the quietest one, run until you can't hear your own heart, and stay there.* Because inside the seal, the blood goes quiet too. Inside, I'm just a girl." She smiled then, a small crooked smile with no joy in it. "Outside, I'm a key."

"The Unbinding," Quentin whispered. The word he had pried out of her weeks ago, syllable by syllable, and reported to Aldessa in his fourth letter, carefully labeled *a word of unknown meaning, possibly ritual.*

"The Unbinding." She repeated it, and the old cadence came into her voice again, and for a moment she sounded less like a child than like a door opening onto a very large darkness. "There are people who want the war-magic back. Not all of them are evil. Some of them lost things in the old war and want their power back to *fix* everything, and some of them just want power, and after enough years those two kinds look the same." She began stacking the drawings, tower on tower on tower. "They hunted the vessel lines for generations. Killed eight. My line was the last, the strongest, the one the founders trusted most — my grandmother broke the pattern, hid us, raised my mother outside the temples, outside everything. It worked for one generation." One finger folded down. "Then they came for us."

"When?"

"I don't count that high." Flatly, without self-pity. "I remember fire. I remember my mother's hands pushing me into a hole in the ground. I remember running for a long time and eating things I don't want to say in your holy house." She glanced up at him, and the corner of her mouth twitched. "And I remember the men with the white cords on their wrists, singing while they burned the village. Singing. Like your temple sings."

Quentin felt the hair rise along his arms. The Temple of the Hollow wore gray. But he had seen the sealed liturgies, the pre-kingdom chant-forms, and he had heard — God help him, he had *heard* — echoes of them in the temple's own evening office, worn smooth by three centuries of repetition into something the priests no longer recognized. Songs outlive their meanings. Songs outlive their crimes.

"And if they take you," he said. "If they bring you to the seal. What happens?"

"They finish it." She said it simply. "They open the lock with my blood, and the war wakes up, and everything your valley forgot comes back. The fire big as hills. The soldiers made of grief. Everything." She looked at the frost on the window. "Or worse. The seal is old. Old locks don't open clean. Maybe it breaks instead of opens, and then nobody wins, not even them. Maybe the magic just *tears out* of the world like a tooth pulled wrong, and takes the land with it."

"And the frost," Quentin said. "The dead birds. The cold in midsummer."

Her jaw tightened. For the first time since she'd begun, she looked her age — a frightened child trying very hard to stand where her mother had stood.

"That's me," she said. "Being here. Being near the lock, but not *of* it — I was born outside, I grew up outside, my blood woke up on the roads. Now the lock doesn't fit me right anymore. It's like..." she searched, frustrated, "...like a shoe grown too small. Every night it pinches harder. Every night the seal slips a little more, and a little of the cold underneath leaks out, and your valley feels it and calls it omens." She spread her hands, helpless, furious. "I am the leak. Me. The thing keeping the war asleep and the thing letting it wake are the *same blood*."

The candle guttered. Somewhere below them, in the temple's depths, a door boomed shut, and both of them heard it, and both of them knew what it meant at this hour: wardens changing watch. Wardens who had begun, these past nights, to walk past her door more often. To linger.

"They're coming to finish it," Quentin said. Not a question. Understanding, at last, what her first sentence had truly been — not a prophecy, not a warning from the old tongue, but a plain report from a child who had been carrying intelligence of her own destruction since before she could speak it. "The ones with the white cords. They're coming *here*. To the valley."

"Of course they're coming here." She looked at him with something close to pity. "Everything they want is here. The lock is here. The key is here. Your temple has spent three hundred years sitting on top of the most important thing in the world and calling it holiness." She picked up the last drawing — the one she had never shown him, the one she had kept folded in her sleeve since the festival — and opened it, and laid it before him.

It was the valley. Drawn with a child's precision and a cartographer's instinct: the river, the border stones, the temple at the center. But over the temple she had drawn the crack — the same crack, larger than on any other page, running down through the temple's foundations and deeper, deeper, off the edge of the vellum into nothing. And around the valley's rim, small and patient, she had drawn figures. Dozens of them. Standing at the borders like herons at the edge of a frozen pond.

Waiting for the ice to break.

"How many?" Quentin asked. His voice sounded far away to himself.

"I don't know. Enough." She took the drawing back, folded it, and pressed it into his hands — pressed it hard, both of her small hands over his one, and looked up at him with eyes that had seen villages burn. "You asked me to talk, so listen. There is no version of this where I stay hidden and everyone is safe. The lock is failing because of me. The hunters are coming because of me. Your Mother Aldessa looks at me like she's measuring me for something, and it isn't a dress." Her grip tightened. "So you have to choose what you are, Quentin-of-the-ledgers. Before they come. Before she decides. Because I can't choose anything. I'm the key. Keys don't get to pick which door."

The word *Aldessa* in the child's mouth — flat, knowing, unafraid — landed in him like a hammer on a chisel. She knew. However much he had softened his reports, however faithfully he had lied, she had read the watching for what it was. Children always read the watching. It was adults who taught themselves not to see it.

"Wren," he said. "I—"

The bell rang then, properly this time, the matins bell, and with it came footsteps in the corridor — measured, unhurried, two sets — and a shadow broke the candlelight under the door and paused there. Paused. Moved on.

Neither of them breathed until it faded.

When Quentin finally spoke, he spoke barely above the frost's silence, and every word cost him something he would not get back.

"No more reports," he said. "Whatever you tell me from tonight — none of it goes to her. None of it goes in any ledger. You have my word."

Wren studied him for a long moment, the way she studied everything, and then she nodded once, solemn as an oath-taker, and reached for her chalk and slate. But instead of writing, she handed the slate to him.

*Then you need to decide faster,* she had written, in the careful letters he had taught her. *Because I counted nine nights. And tonight the frost got inside the walls.*

Through the window, beyond the temple grounds, beyond the sleeping barley fields, Quentin could see the border stones on the far ridge — faint in the starlight, pale as teeth. And on the ridge, for just a moment, in the space between one heartbeat and the next, he thought he saw a light move. Small. White. Patient.

Like a lantern carried by someone who knew exactly where they were going, and exactly how much time was left.
