## 16. Night Flight

The knife went into her boot first, because everything else was a lie but the knife would not be.

Ser Ilsa Vane crouched in the shadow of the temple's tannery wall and checked the blade the way her order had taught her — thumb along the spine, never the edge — then slid it home against her ankle where a warden's pat-down would not think to look. A knight disarmed is only half disgraced. A knight unarmed is nothing at all.

Above her, the Temple of the Hollow slept in the way great institutions sleep: badly, and with one eye open. Lamplight moved behind the clerestory windows where the night brothers kept their vigils. Somewhere on the eastern walk a warden coughed, the sound carrying flat and clear across the still air, because this valley did not know wind. It never had. In eleven years beyond its borders Ilsa had forgotten how silence felt when it was enforced rather than earned — how it pressed down like a hand laid gently on the back of the neck.

She had told herself, riding through the gate at dusk with dust of three kingdoms on her cloak, that she had come back for sanctuary. That was even mostly true. But sanctuaries were for people who intended to stay, and by the second bell after midnight she had already broken into the scribes' dormitory, already put her hand over Quentin Kavanagh's mouth before he could wake enough to cry out, and already said the one sentence that mattered:

*The temple means to kill the girl. We leave tonight.*

He had not argued. She had expected argument — the man was made of ledgers, of entries and balances, of debts tallied to the last copper — and instead he had looked at her with those tired clerk's eyes and said, "I know," as though he had been waiting weeks for someone to say it out loud so he could stop saying it to himself. Then he had gone to a chest beneath his cot and taken out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth: bread, dried apples, a skin of water, a child's wooden bird. He had packed for flight the way other men packed for a market day. Which meant he had been packed for weeks. Which meant Ilsa was not rescuing him at all.

She found she did not mind. There was a particular relief, she had learned in her years of exile, in discovering that the person you came to save had already saved himself. It meant you could spend your courage on the parts that actually required it.

* * *

They went down through the cloister garth, where the moon lay white as spilled milk between the colonnades, and Wren came out of the dark to meet them like something summoned.

Ilsa had seen the child only twice before — once across the temple yard, once at the festival, small and still among all that terrible rejoicing — and both times she had felt the old reflex stir in her chest, the reflex that had gotten her exiled in the first place: *that sigil. I know that sigil.* It was inked inside the girl's left wrist, a coiled line like a root or a river, and Ilsa had worn its twin burned into her own shoulder for six years before her order fell. The Order of the Verdant Oath. Sworn not to any crown but to the vessels themselves — to keeping safe the bloodline everyone else wanted to use up.

Her whole order had died keeping that oath, more or less. She had survived it by breaking a different one, refusing an order she still could not speak aloud without tasting iron, and the shame of the two acts had braided themselves together so tightly she could no longer tell which betrayal she regretted.

Wren stopped an arm's length away and looked up at her. The girl's eyes in the moonlight were the color of deep water over stone.

"You're the knight," Wren said. Her valley-tongue was still slow, each word set down carefully like coins counted out, but she spoke it now. Three months of Quentin's patient chalk-dust lessons, and she spoke it.

"I'm no one's knight anymore."

"You have the mark." Not a question. The girl turned her wrist up, showing the coiled line. "You have it too."

Ilsa knelt, which her knees protested and her pride permitted. "I did. Once." She pulled her collar aside far enough for the faded scar to show, old burn-tissue gone pale as candle wax. Wren touched it with one fingertip, light as a moth landing, and something passed across her face that Ilsa recognized because she had worn it herself for years: the look of someone finding one living thing in a burned field.

"We go now," Quentin murmured. He stood a pace off with the bundle hugged to his chest, looking — there was no kind way to say it — like a man about to be sick. His eyes kept going to the temple's dark bulk above them, to the windows where Mother Aldessa's own lamp still burned. Ilsa had watched him look at it three times since midnight. Each time his jaw tightened a little more, and each time he looked away first.

"Quentin." She kept her voice low. "Whatever you're leaving in that building, it will keep until morning. And by morning we'll be past caring."

"It isn't things I'm leaving." He said it quietly, almost to himself. Then he squared his thin shoulders, and Ilsa thought: *there. There's the spine under the ink stains. Good.* "Lead on, Ser Vane."

* * *

There were four ways out of the temple close, and Ilsa had spent the evening learning them the way she'd learned castle walls her entire adult life: by walking them slowly in daylight with a pilgrim's slouch and counting what she saw.

The main gate was suicide — barred, warded, and overlooked by the watchtower. The postern by the stables opened onto the mill road and would be watched precisely because it was obvious. The drainage culvert beneath the infirmary stank of lime and ran too narrow for her shoulders. Which left the gatehouse of the old pilgrims' court on the temple's western flank, abandoned these thirty years, its arch bricked halfway up, leaving a gap barely wide enough for a grown man to squeeze through sideways — if the man were willing to leave everything behind him and trust the dark beyond.

She had pried the loose bricks loose two hours ago and laid them back in place like teeth in a gum. Now, in the black hour before dawn, she drew them out again one by one, silent, and felt the outside air come through the gap smelling of wet grass and distance. Real distance. The smell of somewhere else, which was the only incense Ilsa had ever trusted.

"Quentin. Through. Take her hand and don't stop moving until the orchard swallows you."

"What about you?"

"I'll follow." The lie sat easily in her mouth; she'd had practice. "Someone has to put the bricks back and drag a sack of meal across the court so the morning watch finds tracks pointing the wrong way. Go."

He hesitated — of course he hesitated — and she saw him weigh it, the ledger-man weighing a debt he couldn't balance: her risk against the child's safety, and no column in which both numbers came out whole. Wren solved it for him. She took his hand and pulled, once, gently, the way you pull someone waking from a dream, and Quentin Kavanagh turned sideways into the gap and was gone, folded through the brickwork with his precious bundle crushed against his ribs, and then only his hand reappeared through the hole, reaching back for the girl.

Wren did not go to it at once. She looked back at Ilsa.

"Come with us," she said. "Both of us. Or neither."

And there it was — the oath, spoken again by a seven-year-old in a borrowed smock, in a dead tongue's daughter-speech, and Ilsa felt it land in her chest like a hand pressed flat over the old scar. For one full breath she was tempted. Truly tempted. To squeeze through that gap and run west with them, past the border stone, into whatever wild unmade world waited beyond the valley's silence — to be a soldier again with a charge worth guarding instead of a ghost haunting the ruins of her own refusal.

But the gap was narrow, and the watch changed at the fourth bell, and two figures moving in the dark doubled the chances of everything going wrong. She had done this arithmetic before, the night her order burned. Arithmetic was all she had left of her vows.

"I'm right behind you," she said, and gave the girl a small push toward the reaching hand, and hated herself with a clean and professional thoroughness as the small fingers slipped out of reach and vanished into the dark beyond the wall.

Then she dragged the sack of meal across the flagstones in a long diagonal toward the stables, scuffed her boots backward over part of it, replaced three bricks, and stood in the ruined pilgrims' court listening to the valley hold its breath.

That was when the lamp appeared on the eastern walk.

* * *

It came fast — too fast for a routine round, the bobbing flame of a storm-lantern carried at a jog, and behind it the low voices of men who had found something they didn't like. Ilsa counted shapes in the light's spill: four wardens, chain shirts over their nightclothes, and leading them a tall figure whose gait she knew from the festival dais. Brother Coram. Aldessa's second. The man with the voice like a well — deep, and you never heard the bottom.

They had found the culvert grate ajar, she realized. Or the stable boy had talked. Or the temple simply breathed suspicion the way other buildings breathed dust, and some sleeping brother had dreamed of flight and woken certain. It didn't matter. What mattered was the geometry: the lantern party was cutting directly across the garth toward the pilgrims' court, and between them and the gap lay forty paces of open moonlight, and somewhere beyond that wall two fugitives were still moving through the orchard, slow as syrup in the cold.

Ilsa stepped out of the shadow of the arch.

"Halt!" Coram's voice cracked across the court, and the lantern swung toward her, and she walked into its light with her hands loose at her sides and her chin up, every inch the disgraced knight presenting herself for judgment. Let them look at her. Every heartbeat they spent looking was a heartbeat the orchard swallowed another few yards of children.

"Brother Coram." She pitched her voice to carry, bored, faintly insolent. "Your watch keeps late hours."

"Ser Vane." He came closer, lantern high, and his face above the flame was exactly as she remembered it: long, gray-eyed, unreadable as a closed book chained to a lectern. "You returned to the valley yesterday begging sanctuary. Sanctuary has rules. One does not wander the cloister at the third bell."

"One does not sleep either." She shrugged, letting the motion pull her cloak open, letting him see the sword she was not wearing, the armor she did not have. "Eleven years on the roads teaches habits no abbot can pray out of a body. I walk when I can't rest. Ask your watch — I've crossed this court four times tonight already."

Two of the wardens glanced at each other. It was a good lie because it was half true; she *had* walked the court earlier, memorizing it, and the night brother on the eastern tower might well have marked her pale shape making its rounds. Coram studied her. Behind his eyes something moved — she would remember this later, would turn it over on the long bad nights afterward — something that was not quite belief and not quite doubt, a man standing on a floor he suspected was rotten.

"The scribe," he said. "Kavanagh. His bed is empty."

"Is it?" Genuine surprise was beyond her acting, so she offered indifference instead, and found indifference fit her like her old gauntlets. "Clerks keep strange hours. Perhaps your archives finally ate him."

"He has been seen with the vessel. He files reports on her speech, her dreams, her words. Tonight those reports are due, and tonight he is gone, and the same night a stranger who arrived asking after the girl also fails to sleep." Coram took another step, and the lantern-light climbed Ilsa's face, and she knew — she had known since she heard the running footsteps — that the arithmetic had failed, that he had already assembled the truth out of its pieces and was only deciding what to do with it. "Where is she, Ser Vane?"

"Gone, I should imagine," Ilsa said. "If your god has any mercy in him at all."

The word landed between them like a dropped coin. Coram's jaw shifted. And Ilsa understood, with the sudden clarity that comes only in the last moment before a thing becomes unavoidable, that she was not going to talk her way out of this court — and that talking had never been the plan. The plan had always ended here, in this pool of lamplight, buying seconds at the price of everything.

So she spent them.

Her hand went into her boot and came out with the knife, and she did not throw it at Coram — she threw it past him, into the lantern itself, and the glass cracked and the flame drowned in its own oil and the court went black as the inside of a grave.

Then she ran — not toward the wall, not toward escape, but *at* them, shoulder down, into the tangle of blinded men, and the night filled up with shouting and the beautiful stupid chaos of bodies colliding in the dark. Someone's elbow found her cheekbone; she gave it back with interest. A chain-shirted arm closed around her throat and she stamped the instep behind her and twisted free. In the ringing blackness she heard Coram roar *"Hold her — hold her, do NOT cut her"* — an order that told her more about Brother Coram than all his sermons ever could, and cost him precious seconds, and she used every one.

She got the courtyard gate open. She got through it. She even got ten strides down the lane before the net came over her from behind — literally a net, fisherman's weave weighted with lead corners, the temple's answer to everything it could not convert — and the ground came up and knocked the breath out of her and the stars wheeled overhead, indifferent, gorgeous, very far away.

Boots around her. Lanterns relit. Coram's face descending into view, upside down, gray eyes narrowed.

"The orchard path," one warden gasped. "Tracks. Two sets, small and large, heading west—"

"Toward the border stone." Coram straightened. For a long moment he looked down at Ilsa with an expression she would spend years failing to name — anger, yes, but under the anger something almost like grief, the grief of a man watching a door close that he had maybe, secretly, hoped would open. "You understand what you've done. That child carries the end of the peace. Whatever comes through that seal when it breaks — plague, war, the old fires — it comes because of you."

"No," Ilsa said, spitting grass. "It comes because of what your temple buried. I'm just the fool digging it up."

He signaled, and the wardens began dragging her upright, and she let them, because her work here was finished. Beyond the wall, beyond the orchard, somewhere in the wet dark, two small figures were running west, and every minute they gained was hers, bought and paid for. Eleven years of exile, one broken order, one refused command, and now this — trussed like a poacher in the temple close while the sky went gray at the edges. It was, she reflected, the most honest night's work she had done since taking her vows.

Coram leaned close, so the others could not hear.

"Which way did they truly go?" he asked, quiet as confession. "Not for the Mother. For me. Answer me that much, Ser Vane, and I swear by anything you care to name I will not ride hard after them."

Ilsa looked at him for a long time. Then she laughed — short, rusty, real — and shook her head.

"You already know," she said. "You just want me to say it, so it stops being yours alone to carry."

Something flickered in his face — the floor-rot acknowledged, if only for a breath. Then he stepped back and became Brother Coram again, Aldessa's well-deep voice calling orders, horses, the western road, and Ilsa Vane was hauled away through the waking temple with her wrists bound and her cheek bleeding and her heart, absurdly, lighter than it had been in eleven years.

They had no farewell, she and the scribe and the child. She was glad of it. Farewells were for people who believed in endings.

Beyond the walls, the dawn was coming up the color of a struck flint, and somewhere past the orchard, past the fields, past the old border stone where the world forgot itself, Quentin Kavanagh was walking toward the truth with a child's hand in his — and Ilsa closed her eyes and gave them to the morning, and began, silently, the count of minutes until the valley learned what it had been keeping out all along.
