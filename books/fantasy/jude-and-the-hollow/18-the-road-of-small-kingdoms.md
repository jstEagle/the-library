## 18. The Road of Small Kingdoms

The first village they came to was called Bracken Hollow, and it broke Quentin's heart in the space of an afternoon.

They had walked three days from the border stone, following a road that wound down out of the hills through country he had never seen and had no map for. The valley's roads were all drawn on temple charts, every mile of them accounted for, blessed, recorded. This road belonged to no chart Quentin knew. It simply went where people needed to go, worn into the earth by generations of feet, and that alone felt like a small heresy.

Wren had been quiet since the crossing — not the frightened silence of the girl who had arrived at the temple door caked in mud and speaking a dead tongue, but something new: a listening silence, the way a person goes quiet when they step into a house where music is playing. She kept stopping on the road to put her hand against things. Fence posts. Standing stones half-swallowed by bramble. The trunk of an old rowan whose branches moved without wind.

"It's louder here," she signed to him, when she deigned to sign at all. Her valley-speech had grown in leaps these past days; her hands still reached for the old gestures first, but the words came easier now, as though the land itself were helping her translate.

"Louder than what?"

"Than anywhere." She looked at him with an expression he was beginning to know well — the patient look of someone explaining water to a fish. "You lived your whole life inside the deafness. You don't have a word for hearing yet."

Quentin had wanted to argue. He was a scribe; words were his trade, his hoard, his pride. But there was no arguing with the way the light lay on the fields here, gold and thick as honey, or the way the crops stood in their rows with a vigor that seemed almost deliberate. He had copied a hundred temple treatises on the blight of magic — how it withered honest soil, how it made crops grow crooked and cattle birth monsters, how the Peace had saved mankind from a world gone rotten with power. He had transcribed those passages so many times he could recite them in his sleep. And now he was walking through a countryside that treated those passages as a joke told very slowly.

* * *

Bracken Hollow announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke and bread and, underneath both, something green and strange that Quentin couldn't name until he saw the woman working in the garden at the village's edge.

She was old, bent nearly double over her rows, and she was singing to the plants. Not a song with words — a low, humming melody, and as Quentin watched, frozen on the roadside with Wren's small hand gripping his sleeve, the garden answered. Beans climbed their poles faster than climbing should happen. A bruised-looking cabbage straightened its leaves like a soldier squaring shoulders. The woman hummed a little higher, and the whole patch seemed to lean toward her, listening.

"Hedge-witch," Ilsa had said, when they'd planned this leg of the journey around the inn fire two nights back. She'd said it flatly, the way you'd say *wolf* or *flood*. "They're everywhere out here. Harmless, mostly. Don't stare."

Quentin stared.

Every treatise he had ever copied said such women were abominations. Every sermon of his childhood had described the rot that spread outward from a hedge-witch's cottage — the blighted fields, the twisted children, the slow moral collapse of any village foolish enough to harbor one. Mother Aldessa herself had preached a festival sermon on it once, her voice ringing off the temple stones: *Where the craft takes root, death follows, as sure as night follows day.*

The garden in front of him was the most abundantly alive thing Quentin had ever seen.

"Stop gawking and help me up," the witch said, without turning around. "You've been standing there long enough to be either thieves or pilgrims, and thieves don't gawk."

* * *

Her name was Maren Ostwick, and she fed them barley soup and dark bread and asked no questions for the better part of an hour, which Quentin found more disarming than interrogation would have been. Wren ate like a small wolf. Ilsa ate like a soldier, methodically, one eye always on the door. Quentin held his bowl and tried to reconcile the kitchen he sat in — warm, ordinary, smelling of thyme — with everything the temple had taught him such a kitchen must contain.

"You're from the valley," Maren said at last. It wasn't a question. "I can tell. You keep flinching."

"Flinching?"

"At everything." She nodded at the window, where the light was doing something soft and impossible through the late-afternoon leaves. "At the light. At the garden. At me. Valley folk cross the stone maybe twice in a generation, and when they do, they walk around like men waking from surgery. Everything hurts them because everything works."

"The temple teaches—" Quentin began, and stopped, because he didn't know how to say *the temple teaches that you are a plague* to a woman who had just fed him soup.

"That we're a plague?" Maren smiled without much humor. "Oh, they teach that. They've taught it for three hundred years. Funny thing about the valley, though, scribe." She leaned forward, and her eyes were sharp and brown and not unkind. "We get valley folk out here sometimes. Runaways, traders' children, the odd apostate priest. And do you know what they all say? They say the same thing your face is saying right now. They say: *I was told the world was dying.*"

"And it isn't."

"Look out the window and tell me what you see."

Quentin looked. Beyond the garden, the village of Bracken Hollow went about its evening. Children ran between houses chasing a hoop. An old man repaired a roof while the shingles — Quentin blinked and looked again — the shingles seemed to settle themselves under his hammer, finding their places with a willingness wood did not naturally possess. Two young women argued amiably over a loom that wove, when neither of them touched it, threads of color that did not exist in any dye-merchant's shop.

"They say the Peace keeps the valley safe," Quentin said quietly. "That outside, the old war-magic still burns, and kingdoms tear each other apart with it."

"Some kingdoms do," Maren admitted. "There are bad lands east of here, and worse ones beyond. Magic's like weather — it kills careless folk. But it also grows the beans." She jerked her chin at the garden. "Your temple took the flood and the harvest and told you only about the flood. That's not teaching, boy. That's editing."

Quentin thought of the sealed stacks. Of the pre-kingdom liturgy in a language the temple pretended had never existed. Of the founders' records, the murdered scribes, the seal built on a buried truth. *Editing,* he thought. Three hundred years of it, and he had spent his life copying the edited text in a perfect hand, proud of his fidelity to a lie.

* * *

They stayed two nights in Bracken Hollow, because Wren asked to stay, in her way — tugging Quentin's sleeve and pointing at the village, then making the gesture he had learned meant *a little more*. It was the longest sentence she'd ever offered unprompted, and he could deny her nothing.

And she changed, those two nights, in ways that hurt him to watch because they were so plainly good.

The Wren of the temple had been a creature of corners. She sat with her back to walls. She flinched from raised voices and from sudden kindness alike, having learned that both could be preludes to harm. She hoarded food in her pockets even after weeks of full meals, and she slept curled tight as a fist.

The Wren of Bracken Hollow sat in Maren's garden with dirt on her knees. She followed the old witch around like a duckling, watching everything, and Maren — who professed to be too old and too tired for children, loudly and often, while slipping the girl honey-cakes whenever she thought no one saw — let her watch. On the second evening Quentin came looking for her and found her standing among the bean rows with her hands raised, hesitating, the way she had once hesitated over her first spoken words of valley-tongue. Maren hummed her low note. And a single bean flower opened under Wren's fingers, white and perfect, unfolding like a held breath released.

Wren laughed.

Quentin stood at the garden gate and felt something crack in his chest that he suspected would never fully close. Because he knew what that laugh cost her. He knew she had spent her whole life — whatever length and terror that life had been — hiding exactly this. The vessel of an ancient bloodline, hunted across kingdoms, taught from her cradle that the smallest exercise of what she was would be the death of everyone near her. In the valley she had been mute by necessity even before she was mute by trauma. And here, in a stranger's garden, guarded by a soup-making witch, she had opened a flower.

One flower. It was nothing. It was the bravest thing he had ever seen.

"She'll want to learn more than flowers," Maren said, appearing beside him at the gate with the soundlessness of her profession. "Blood like hers, it won't stay small forever. You know that."

"I know."

"You're not her father."

"No."

"You're not her keeper either, whatever you tell yourself." The old woman looked at him steadily. "Keepers keep things in boxes. What are you going to be?"

Quentin watched Wren show Ilsa the flower — the knight kneeling in the dirt in her travel-worn mail, examining it with grave military attention as though it might be a new species of siege engine — and he found he didn't have an answer. Only the growing, frightening knowledge that whatever he was becoming, it had already cost him the man he used to be, and would cost more before it was done.

"I'm going to be the one who doesn't lie to her," he said finally.

Maren snorted. "Ambitious."

* * *

They left Bracken Hollow on a bright morning and took the road south, and the road taught Quentin lesson after lesson, each one another small demolition of his life's architecture.

In the market town of Ferrow's Cross, he watched a hedgewise apothecary set a broken arm with a touch and a murmured phrase, the bone knitting visibly beneath the skin while the child's mother paid with eggs and thanks in equal measure. The temple taught that healing-craft devoured the healer and rotted the healed; Quentin had transcribed the testimony of a dozen witnesses to just such horrors, testimony he now understood had been written in this same office he worked in, by hands like his own, ordered by superiors like Aldessa.

At a river ferry, the ferryman kept a knot of braided reeds hanging from the prow that turned the current gentle around his hull. He charged Quentin nothing extra for it. He charged nothing extra for anyone. It was simply part of the boat, like the oars.

In a hamlet called Thistledown — eleven houses and a shrine to no god Quentin recognized, some local saint of wells — the whole community gathered at dusk to sing their fields asleep for the season. Quentin stood at the edge of it with Wren's hand in his and listened to farm families harmonize their livestock into calm and their orchards into rest, and he thought: *we sang too, in the valley.* The Festival of Stillness. We sang our emptiness and called it holiness. We commemorated a massacre and called it the Peace, and we made children carry candles to honor the men who built the prison, and I carried one every year of my life and never once asked what was on the other side of the flame.

"Grief looks strange on you," Ilsa observed that night, as they camped in a hay barn. She was sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening, her usual occupation when thinking. "Like a suit cut for someone bigger."

"I'm mourning a worldview, Ser Vane. It's heavy."

"Mm." She tested the edge against her thumb. "When my order cast me out, I mourned for a year. Thought I'd lost my faith." She glanced at him. "Turns out I hadn't lost my faith. I'd lost my object of worship. Different injury. Heals differently."

"And what do you worship now?"

"Ilsa Vane," she said, "worships keeping her word. Which is why I'm sitting in a hay barn instead of a garrison hall. Ask me if I regret it."

"Do you?"

"No." She sheathed the knife. "But ask me again when the queen's men find us. Or the temple's. Mourning's a luxury, scribe. Enjoy it while it's cheap."

From the loft above, Wren's head appeared over the edge, upside down, hair hanging in a dark curtain. "I made a light," she announced, in clear valley-tongue, and opened her cupped hands to reveal a mote of gold hovering above her palms, steady as a candleflame but warm-colored, alive-colored, pulsing faintly like a small heart.

Neither of them had taught her the word for that. Neither of them had taught her anything about it at all.

"I saw," Quentin managed.

"Bracken Hollow style," Wren said, with enormous satisfaction. "Maren showed me the humming. The humming helps." She closed her hands and the light went out, and her grin — wide, gap-toothed, entirely unguarded — was the single most subversive thing Quentin Kavanagh had ever witnessed. Somewhere behind them, temple wardens might already be riding. Somewhere ahead, a queen waited who wanted to make this laughing child into a weapon. And here in the hayloft, Wren held light in her hands like it was a toy, because for one week, in one stretch of country, it could be.

"Get some sleep," Ilsa said gruffly, and Quentin heard in the roughness of her voice that she was fighting the same losing battle he was.

* * *

It rained on the sixth day, and they sheltered in the eaves of a roadside chapel that was older than any building Quentin had ever seen — older, he realized as he studied its worn carvings, than the temple itself. The stonework showed figures: a circle of people, hands joined, and above them a sun rendered not as a disc but as a web, rays connecting everything to everything.

He knew the iconography. He had seen it in the sealed stacks, in the margins of the pre-kingdom liturgy. It was the emblem of the founders — the true founders, the murdered scribes — and here it was, carved in open air, worn by centuries of weather and touching, on a chapel that any traveler might pass.

They never destroyed it out here, he thought. They couldn't. Out here, the truth was never sealed away. It was just... scattered. Ordinary. Carved into chapels and sung into fields and braided into ferryman's knots, waiting for someone to come along and read it.

Wren traced the circle of joined hands with one finger. Then she looked up at him with an expression he hadn't seen before — solemn, ancient, the expression of the dead tongue rather than the living child.

"This is what my mother showed me," she said. "Before. She said the circle was the whole secret. Everyone holding on. Not one person holding everything." She paused, choosing words with care. "The bad ones want one person holding everything. That's why they want me."

The rain drummed on the chapel roof. Quentin knelt beside her in the doorway and thought about the temple's great chain of custody — every text flowing upward to Aldessa, every debt flowing downward to the indentured, every secret held at the top by one woman's conviction. One person holding everything. It had looked like order his whole life. It had looked like holiness.

It looked, from this side of the border, like the shape of the trap.

"Wren," he said carefully, "when we reach the capital—"

"I know what they want there." She said it simply, without drama, and somehow that was worse. "Everyone wants something. The temple wants a lock. The queen will want a sword. Maren said the hedge-folk would want a well." She shrugged, a small, weary gesture far too old for her shoulders. "But the circle isn't any of those. The circle is everyone."

Somewhere down the road, muffled by rain, Quentin heard a sound that pulled him out of the moment like a hook: hoofbeats. More than one horse. Moving fast.

Ilsa was already on her feet, knife out, body positioned between the road and the child with the automatic economy of a lifetime's training. "Scribe. Up. Now."

Through the rain-haze, riders emerged — four of them, cloaked against the weather, and Quentin's stomach dropped as he caught the grey-and-white of their tabards even through the wet. Temple colors. Wardens, this far from the valley, moving hard.

But they did not turn toward the chapel. They passed at speed, heading south along the road Quentin knew led toward the capital, and as the last rider went by, Quentin saw the lead warden's face under his hood — young, grim, and haunted in a way Quentin recognized, because he had worn that same look in the archive of ash, the look of a man carrying evidence against his own faith.

The hoofbeats faded southward. Ilsa lowered her knife slowly.

"Coram's outriders," she said quietly. "Or men wearing his colors. Either way — they're ahead of us now, scribe. Whatever they're riding toward, they'll reach it first."

Wren's hand found Quentin's. Her small fingers were cold.

"Then we walk faster," she said.
