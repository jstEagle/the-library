## 19. Pursuit

The rain had followed them out of the mountains for three days, and Brother Coram had begun to think of it as a companion — an unwelcome one, chattering constantly, soaking through every layer, but honest in its way. It did not pretend to be anything other than what it was.

He crouched at the edge of the farm road and studied the mud, letting the others wait behind him. Four wardens under his command, all of them temple-sworn, all of them armed with the short iron-banded staves the Order permitted within the valley's borders and the long blades they carried everywhere else. Beyond the border stone, the rules were less clear. Coram had made a study of unclear rules his whole life. It was how he had survived Aldessa's favor.

"Tracks again," he said. "Two sets. The knight walks heel-first — she was trained on hard ground, probably courtyard drills. The scribe favors his left foot."

"He's no soldier," said Warden Pell, the youngest of them, with the particular eagerness of a man who had never yet been asked to do the thing they were sent to do.

"No," Coram agreed. "He's a scribe." He did not say the rest: that Quentin Kavanagh had copied more of the temple's texts than any man alive, that his memory was a vault the Mother herself had called irreplaceable, that the theft of such a mind was in its way a greater crime than the theft of the child. He did not say that he had liked Kavanagh, in the distant, careful way one likes a colleague whose competence makes one's own work easier. There was no use saying it. They both knew it would not change what came next.

"And the girl?" Pell asked.

Coram straightened. His knees ached. Forty-one years old and his knees ached, and somewhere behind them, three days' ride back, the Temple of the Hollow stood in its perfect stillness while the valley outside it went strange — frost in midsummer, birds falling dead at the stones, hedges blooming out of season. The null-field was thinning. Everyone felt it now, even the ones who pretended otherwise. Even Coram, who had spent twenty years telling himself the stillness was holy, could feel the difference like a missing tooth: a faint pressure where nothing pressed before, a whisper at the edge of hearing that might have been wind and might have been something older, waking.

"The girl is the mission," he said. "The scribe is incidental. The knight—" He paused, watching Pell's face. "The knight you leave to me."

* * *

They made camp that night in the shell of a burned tithe-barn, one of the many structures along this road that told the same story: the kingdoms beyond the valley had not been idle while the Hollow slept. Coram had crossed the border only twice before in his service, both times escorting tithe-collectors to the near villages, both times with his eyes down and his heart locked like a strongbox. Now there was no locking it. Now he saw everything.

A shrine stood across the road from the barn, a little thing, moss-eaten, its niche empty. In the valley they taught that shrines beyond the border had all been desecrated by magic-mad mobs in the years after the Peace. Standing here in the dark, Coram looked at the niche more closely than he had ever allowed himself to look at anything, and saw that the statue had not been torn out. It had been weathered away. Rain and frost and two hundred years of patience had worn a god smooth as a river stone, and someone — recently, by the freshness of the candle stub — had left an offering of bread and wildflowers anyway, to the smoothness, to the absence, to whatever remained.

He thought about that for a long time after the others slept.

It was not doubt, exactly. Doubt was a luxury of men who had not given their lives to something. What he felt was closer to vertigo — the sensation of standing on ground that had always seemed solid and discovering, without any visible change, that it sloped gently toward an edge.

He took out his ledger — every warden kept one; accountability was the temple's second sacrament — and wrote the day's entry in the small, disciplined hand Aldessa praised. *Day four. Trail leads southeast toward the river villages. Subjects in good health, moving cautiously. No contact with local populations observed.*

All true. He paused, pen hovering.

*Subjects* was a careful word. So was *no contact*. The hedge-witch at the ford two days ago had seen them pass; she had stood in her doorway with her hands folded and watched them with eyes that missed nothing, and she had said nothing either, which was itself a kind of speech. And the word the Mother used, when she spoke of the fugitives in her cold clear voice, was not *subjects*. It was *the vessel*, spoken like the name of a disease, and then, once, in a register Coram had never heard from her before, softer and worse: *the key.*

He closed the ledger and sat listening to the rain.

Killing a child. He turned the phrase over the way he'd once turned over suspicious coinage in the tithe-house, looking for the false ring in it. But there was no false ring. That was the horror of it. She was a child — small, silent, mud-caked, with eyes too old for her face — and the Mother's orders, read plainly, required her death or her capture, and capture meant return, and return meant the blessing, and everyone above the rank of warden knew what the blessing was. Coram knew. He had helped prepare the chamber for it once, years ago, for a different purpose entirely, and he remembered the drain in the floor and had spent years persuading himself it was for water.

There was no water ritual in the temple. There never had been.

Pell stirred by the fire. "Brother? You're keeping watch?"

"I'm thinking."

"About the heretics?" Pell's voice held the bright, uncomplicated hatred of youth. "Pell had been born inside the valley. He had never seen a working of magic. He believed in the Peace the way he believed in stone."

"Not the heretics." Coram looked into the fire. "Go to sleep, Warden."

* * *

They found the campsite at midmorning, abandoned since before dawn, and it was there — crouched over the cold ashes, reading the story written in them the way Kavanagh read texts — that the vertigo finally tipped him over the edge.

Three bedrolls. A cook fire, banked carefully, doused properly — the scribe's work, tidy even in flight. And beside the ashes, half-trampled, a drawing scratched into the dirt with a stick, sheltered by chance under an overturned pail so the rain hadn't reached it.

A bird. A small bird drawn with astonishing confidence for a child's hand, round-bodied, sharp-eyed, with lines beneath its feet suggesting motion, flight, joy. Beside it, in the valley-tongue, laboriously formed letters that had clearly been traced over many times by a guiding adult hand: *WREN.*

Coram knelt there for a long moment.

He had expected to find evidence of corruption. Of the scribe's fall, of sorcery, of some sign that would make the work ahead clean. Instead he found a child's drawing of a bird with a name, and the name spelled in a careful teacher's letters, and something in his chest that had been armoring itself for twenty years simply failed.

"She named herself," he said quietly.

"Brother?" Pell had come up behind him. "What have you— oh." The young warden's face hardened. "Sorceress scribbles. Markings. Could be spellwork."

"It's a picture of a bird, Warden."

"Birds are their symbols. The old faiths—"

"The old faiths left us two hundred years ago and the birds stayed." Coram stood. His knees cracked. He looked at the drawing until he had memorized it — he was a warden, observation was his discipline — and then, deliberately, he stepped on it, grinding his boot until the dirt was only dirt, because Pell was watching and because some things should not be entered into evidence.

"Break camp," he said. "We're close. They'll make for the river villages — the knight will want food and the witch-country will hide them." He heard his own voice go flat and official, the voice of the ledger, and hated it a little. "And Warden Pell. When we take them — *if* we take them — the child comes to no harm on the road. Whatever the Mother intends at journey's end, our charge is delivery. Am I understood?"

"Yes, Brother." A pause. "Though Veyne says—"

"Warden Veyne says nothing. Veyne follows orders, and my orders stand." He let a little iron come into his voice then, enough to settle the matter, and watched Pell swallow it. But later, checking the pickets, he caught Veyne watching the treeline with a look Coram recognized from chapel — the look of a man performing devotion, and feeling nothing.

Veyne wanted permission. Coram understood that too. Somewhere on this road, if the hunt went long, Veyne would ask for it, and Coram would have to decide, in front of witnesses, what kind of man he was going to be.

* * *

They nearly had them that evening, at the crossing place above the mill.

The mill was derelict — the wheel long rotted, the race choked with reeds — and the knight had chosen it exactly as Coram would have chosen it, for the sightlines and the single approach. He spotted her first: a silhouette against the last grey light, motionless on the loading platform, patient as a gargoyle. Ilsa Vane. He had read her trial records; he knew what she had refused to do, and why she wore exile like a uniform she refused to take off. He had thought, reading those records in the warm archive, that she was a fool. Standing now in the wet grass with his blade loose in its sheath, he thought: *she was the only one who obeyed the actual law.*

He signaled. Two wardens swung wide through the reeds. Two held the road. He himself walked forward openly, hands visible, because he wanted to see her face when she saw him, and because some part of him — the part that had stepped on the drawing — wanted this to be a conversation before it was anything else.

"Ser Vane," he called. "Brother Coram, of the Temple of the Hollow. I'd speak with you."

Her answer was an arrow that buried itself in the millpost a hand's width from his ear.

Then chaos — torches, shouts, the reeds exploding with movement as the flanking wardens met the knight's prepared ground and learned, painfully, that she had prepared it. Coram shouted the hold order, the recall order, saw Veyne break left through the reeds toward the mill's dark interior where — he knew it, saw it in the same terrible instant — the child must be hidden. Saw the scribe step out of the darkness to bar the way, unarmed, arms spread, placing his slight body between Veyne's blade and the doorway.

"NO!" Coram's voice tore out of him rawer than he intended. "HOLD! All of you — HOLD!"

And they held. Because he was Brother Coram, and his voice was the temple's voice on this road, and for three heartbeats the whole night hung suspended — torchlight shivering on wet steel, the knight's second arrow drawn to her cheek, the scribe standing with his eyes shut and his arms spread like a man awaiting absolution, and somewhere behind that door, a small bird-named child breathing in the dark.

Coram walked forward, slowly, through his own frozen battle, until he stood between the drawn bow and the scribe.

"Lower it, Ser Vane. Nobody dies tonight. Not on my order." He turned his head. "Kavanagh. Look at me."

Quentin opened his eyes. Up close, by torchlight, he looked hollowed, terrified, resolute — a man three days past the end of his old life and not yet born into his new one.

"Brother Coram," he said. His voice shook. "You've come to take her back."

"I've come to bring you all back. That's my charge." And then, before the ledger-voice could finish speaking, Coram heard himself add, quietly, in his own voice: "Is it true? What you found in the sealed stacks? Tell me it isn't true, Kavanagh. Give me a reason to deliver you with a clean heart."

The scribe stared at him. Something moved behind his eyes — fear, recognition, and underneath it, unbelievably, hope.

"You know about the stacks," Quentin whispered.

"I keep the keys to the stacks." Coram's throat was tight. "I have kept them for eleven years. Do you understand what I'm asking you?"

Behind the mill door, faintly, a child's voice began to hum — tuneless, wavering, a song without words, the kind of sound a frightened child makes to fill up silence. And in the silence after it, Coram stood in the wet grass with his whole life balanced on a knife-edge, waiting for the scribe to speak, knowing that whichever way the answer fell, the man who had walked onto this road would not be the man who walked off it.
