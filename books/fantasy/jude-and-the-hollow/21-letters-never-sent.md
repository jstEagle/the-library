## 21. Letters Never Sent

The hedge-witch's cottage had a back room no larger than a confessional, and Quentin claimed it the way he had once claimed the last carrel in the temple scriptorium: by arriving early, being quiet, and making himself so much a part of the furniture that no one thought to move him. There was a stool, a shelf of jars whose contents he chose not to examine, and a window the width of his two hands that looked out on a hen run and, beyond it, the dark shoulder of the hills that led — eventually, if one walked long enough and did not die — to the valley.

He had asked Maren — the witch; she insisted on her name, said titles were for people hiding something — for paper and ink, and she had looked at him for a long moment with eyes the color of pond water before fetching both without a word. The paper was good stock, rag-made, soft as skin. The ink was walnut-brown and smelled of autumn.

He sat with the first blank sheet before him for the better part of an hour.

It was not that he did not know what to say. That was the terrible thing. He knew exactly what to say, in what order, with which words weighted and which left bare. He had spent twenty years learning how to make sentences carry weight without buckling. What he did not know was whether anything he said would be true by the time it arrived — or whether it had ever been true at all.

*Dear Mother.*

He wrote it, then looked at it for a long time. Two words. The easiest words in any language, and the hardest he had ever set down.

Outside, Wren was laughing.

The sound came through the little window thin as thread, but he knew it instantly, the way you know your own name spoken in a crowd. She was laughing with Maren out among the hens, and the laugh was new — days old, no more. In the valley she had made sounds, small ones, a kind of chirring when she was pleased, but laughter had taken crossing the border to learn. Magic ran through this land like water under grass, and whatever it touched, it loosened. It had loosened Wren. Some mornings Quentin watched her run through Maren's herb rows just to feel the wind move against her, and he understood that she was tasting something the rest of them had been starved of so long they'd forgotten hunger.

And some mornings — most mornings, if he was honest, which was the whole trouble with him, he was always honest, it was the one virtue the temple had ground into him so deep it survived even treason — some mornings he woke with his heart hammering and the taste of ash in his mouth, because he remembered the valley. Not the way the valley remembered itself, all festival bells and blessed stillness. He remembered it truly now, and truly was unbearable.

*Dear Mother,* he wrote again, and forced himself onward.

*I don't know if this letter will ever reach you. I am writing it anyway, because I have learned in the past weeks that there are things a man must say even into a room where no one stands. Perhaps especially into such rooms.*

*I am alive. I want you to know that first, because everything after it will frighten you, and I need the frightening things to stand on something solid. I am alive, and I am well, and I am fed, and I am sheltered by a woman who practices openly what our temple burned people for, and she is kinder than half the priests I have copied ledgers beneath.*

*Here is what I know now, Mother. Here is what I could not say in the valley, because saying it there is death, and here I cannot stop saying it because silence has begun to feel like complicity.*

*The Peace is a lie.*

His pen hovered. Even here, even alone, even on contraband paper in a witch's back room, writing those four words felt like setting fire to the scriptorium. Twenty years of muscle memory fought him — *the Peace is holy, the Peace is protection, the Hollow keeps us safe from the wild magic beyond* — twenty years of copying that sentence in a hundred hands and a thousand ledgers until it lived in his fingers deeper than his own heartbeat.

He kept writing.

*Not the peace itself. I believe there was peace, once, or something near it. But the story we are told — that the founders sealed away a great evil, that the valley's emptiness is a shield, that we are the protected remnant of a righteous victory — none of it holds. I have read the founders' own records, Mother. The ones the temple sealed. The scribes who built the seal were murdered by their own order once their work was done. Murdered, and written out of the histories, and replaced with saints who never existed. Every Festival of Stillness is a memorial wearing festival clothes. We have been dancing on graves for nine hundred years and calling it gratitude.*

*You will want to tell me I am ill, or deceived, or ensorcelled. I have imagined you saying each of these things. I love you enough to let you say them. But I ask you one thing only: go to the border stone. Any border stone. Stand there at dusk, when the light comes low across the fields, and watch the air above the marker. Watch how it bends. Watch how the birds refuse it. The valley is not protected, Mother. It is imprisoned. And the bars are old, and they are failing, and when they fail — and they are failing now, the frost in midsummer, the dead bird at the boundary — everything the temple buried will come up like floodwater unless someone does something that the temple would rather die than allow.*

*There is a child here. You would love her. She is small and fierce and she draws hawks on every flat surface, and she has been hunted since before she could speak for what runs in her blood. The temple wants to kill her and call it renewal. The queen beyond the mountains wants to use her and call it necessity. Everyone who looks at her sees a key, Mother. I look at her and see a girl who laughs at hens.*

*I am going to try to save her. I do not know yet what it will cost. I suspect it will cost me everything I was, and I find — this is the strange part, the part I keep turning over like a stone — I find I am willing. The man you raised, the scribe with the perfect memory and the clean ledger and the debt counted down to the day — I think he may not survive this. I think I already mourn him. Forgive me. Or don't. Either way, know that everything he was built toward this, though neither of us saw it. A memory that perfect was never a gift, Mother. It was preparation. Someone, somewhere, intended it for exactly this.*

*Your son,
Quentin*

He read it through twice. Then he did what he had known he would do from the moment he dipped the pen: he folded it, and held it over the candle, and watched his mother's name curl brown and vanish into smoke.

Maren had told him the roads east were closed — temple wardens moving in pairs, checking travelers against descriptions. A letter bearing his hand would be found, read, and answered with a visit to whoever received it. His mother lived alone above the bindery. He would not put wardens' boots on her stairs.

So the letter went into the candle, and the grief of it went into his chest, and he noted — with the terrible precision that never left him — that this was the second thing he had ever burned, and that burning was becoming a habit of his heart.

* * *

The second letter took three days.

Not because it was harder to write — though it was — but because he kept starting it wrong. He began it as an accusation and tore it up. He began it as an apology and tore that up too. He began it as scripture, quoting chapter and verse back at her the way she had taught him, and got four lines in before he heard how it sounded: a boy reciting lessons to prove he'd been paying attention.

Mother Aldessa did not deserve a lesson. She deserved the truth, and the truth was that he did not hate her, and that was somehow worse than hatred would have been.

On the third evening, Wren came to the door of the back room while he sat glaring at the blank page. She didn't knock — she never knocked; doors, in her experience, were things that other people came through with bad intentions — she simply leaned in the frame and studied him with those gray eyes that missed nothing.

"You're doing the face," she said. Her valley-tongue had grown fast, quickened by the living magic of this land, words coming to her now almost faster than he could teach them. "The one where you're angry at the paper."

"The paper has done nothing wrong."

"You always say that." She came in and sat cross-legged on the floor beside the stool, in the spot she had decided was hers, and pulled a charcoal stub from her pocket to draw on the floorboards — a hawk, inevitably, mid-swoop. "Who's it for?"

"No one."

"Letters to no one," she said, with the gravity of a scholar, "are the most important kind. Maren says so. She writes them to her sister who died." She glanced up. "Is yours dead?"

He considered lying to her. He found he couldn't. "No. Just unreachable."

"That's worse," Wren said, and went back to her hawk.

She was right, of course. She was nearly always right, in the blunt way of children and the ancient way of vessels, and he sat with the truth of it while she filled the floorboard with wings. Unreachable was worse than dead. Dead was a wound that healed over, badly, but healed. Unreachable was a door that stayed shut forever with the person you needed standing on the other side of it, alive, listening to nothing.

When she had gone to bed — she slept now in the loft above the main room, and had stopped, in the last week, sleeping with her back to the wall — Quentin dipped his pen again.

*Mother Aldessa.*

*You taught me my letters. I want you to remember that as you read this, if it ever reaches you, which it will not, because I am choosing that it will not, because I am — despite everything — still trying to protect you.*

*You summoned me to your study when I was eleven years old and told me that a perfect memory was a sacred trust. You said the temple did not collect texts; it collected truth, and that truth required vessels who could hold it without spilling. I believed you. I believe, even now, that you believed it. That is the thing I cannot forgive and cannot stop loving about you: you are sincere. Brother Coram lies to himself and knows it. The wardens lie to others and enjoy it. But you, Mother — you have built a cathedral of conviction so complete that even murder fits inside it without scraping the walls.*

*I found the founders' testament. I know you know I found it, because you knew I had been in the sealed stacks before you ever called me to your study; you always know; it is your gift and your curse, that you see the shape of a thing before its parts. And I know what you will say, because I have heard you say it in my head every night since: that the seal must be renewed, that sacrifice is mercy, that one child weighed against thousands is arithmetic any merciful god would bless.*

*But here is what your arithmetic leaves out, Mother. Here is what the founders wrote, in their own hands, in the ink they mixed with their own blood: the seal was never meant to be permanent. They built it as a tourniquet, not a tomb. They wrote — I can recite it still, I will always be able to recite it, that is the curse you gave me — "We bind the war-magic not to end it, but to pause it, until such time as the world grows wise enough to unmake it. To those who come after: do not worship the binding. Do not build your houses upon it. And above all, do not spill innocent blood upon the seal, for the seal drinks what it is given, and grows hungry."*

*It grows hungry, Mother. That is why the field fails. That is why frost comes in summer. You have been feeding it for nine hundred years, and it has learned to expect the meal.*

*I am taking the child beyond your reach. I will not apologize for this. I will tell you instead what I told the candle: when the seal breaks — and it will break, with your help or without it — the only question that will matter is whether there is anyone left standing there who remembers what the founders actually intended. I intend to be that person. My memory is the last true archive your temple ever made, and I am taking it out of your keeping, and I am sorry, and I am not sorry, and I do not know how to hold both of those in one heart except by writing them side by side, the way I once wrote debits and credits in your ledgers.*

*May you live to see yourself clearly. It is the cruelest thing I can wish anyone, and I wish it only because I loved you.*

*Q.*

He sat back. The candle had burned down to a thumb's width, and the wax had pooled into a shape like a small kneeling figure, and he found he could not look away from it.

This letter he did not burn. He could not have said why — except that he could, and the reason sat in his chest with the weight of a ledger stone: because Aldessa was not his mother. Because Aldessa had known what the seal drank and had chosen to feed it anyway. Because somewhere in the temple's deepest vaults there might, someday, be a reckoning, and when it came, someone should find this letter and know that a scribe had seen clearly and said so, in ink, dated, signed.

Evidence. Even his rebellion took the form of documentation. Even his treason filed itself neatly.

He folded it small and hid it inside the lining of his coat, against his ribs, where it rode like a second heartbeat.

* * *

He slept badly, and woke before dawn to a sound he had learned to dread in the past week: hooves.

Two sets. Coming up the forest track at a walk, unhurried, the pace of men who did not need to hurry because they believed no one could outrun them.

Quentin was down the ladder and through the main room before his mind finished the thought. Maren stood at her hearth, one hand resting on the mantel where — he had noticed and never remarked upon — a sprig of dried rowan hung crossed with iron. Wren appeared at the foot of the loft ladder, awake, silent, her eyes already wide.

Ilsa Vane came through the front door without knocking, mud to the knee, sword loose in its scabbard, and her face told him everything before she said a word.

"They've found the road," she said. "Coram's wardens, six of them, maybe more behind. They turned a peddler at the ford this morning and showed his own daughter's likeness to him, drawn from someone's description." Her gaze flicked to Wren, then back. "They're a day behind us. Maybe less. The witch's track north is still open, but it won't be tomorrow."

Maren snorted. "My track is older than their temple, knight. It'll be open long after their temple is dust."

"It'll be open today," Ilsa said. "That's what matters."

Quentin looked down at Wren. She had gone very still, the stillness she wore like armor, the stillness of a girl who had been found before and knew exactly what being found meant. But her chin was up. Her hand had found the charcoal stub in her pocket.

"We leave within the hour," Quentin said. His voice sounded steadier than he felt. "I need to fetch—"

"Nothing," Ilsa said. "You need to fetch nothing. You carry everything that matters inside your skull, scribe. That's the whole point of you."

He almost laughed. It caught in his throat and became something else — grief, sudden and sharp, for the coat lining against his ribs, for the unsent letters, for the man in the valley who had owned a desk and a chair and a place where his things stayed where he left them.

One thing, though. One thing he would not leave.

He went back to the little room, and took the walnut ink and the last three sheets of rag paper, and rolled them tight, and bound them with string. If there was a road ahead, there would be nights on it, and nights meant letters, and letters meant that somewhere in him the scribe was still alive — the man who believed that words set down truly were a kind of salvation, even when no one would ever read them.

Especially then.

When he came out, Wren was waiting at the door with Maren's hand on her shoulder, and the sky behind the eastern hills had gone the color of a fresh-cut ledger page, pale and blank and waiting to be written on.

"Ready?" Ilsa asked.

"No," Quentin said honestly. "But I'm coming."

Wren slipped her small hand into his. "That's the same thing," she said. "Maren told me."

And they stepped out together onto the northward track, three fugitives and a dead man's memories, while behind them the hens muttered and the cottage door swung slowly shut, and ahead — far off, at the tree line's edge, where the morning mist still clung — something glinted once, briefly, like steel catching light, and was gone.
