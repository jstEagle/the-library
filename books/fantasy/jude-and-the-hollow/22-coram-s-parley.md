## 22. Coram's Parley

Brother Coram had been a hunter for eleven years before he became a warden, and in all that time he had learned one truth above the others: the ones you catch are never the ones you were hunting. The quarry that matters always slips the net by a hand's width, and what you drag back instead is some lesser creature — a courier, a cousin, a sympathizer — whose capture satisfies the ledger and nothing else.

He had been chasing Quentin Kavanagh and the child for nineteen days.

Nineteen days of wet roads and worse inns, of tracing hedge-witch hearths burned cold behind them, of reading sign like scripture: a boot-print too long for a girl's stride because she had been carried; a coin spent at a crossroads shrine where no temple man would think to look; the particular way fugitives stopped lighting fires after their third night of flight, as if darkness could hide them from a God who saw everything anyway. Coram did not believe the Hollow-God watched. He had stopped believing much of anything on the night Ser Ilsa Vane refused her order and he had been made to carry out the order without her. But he believed in sign. Sign had never lied to him. People lied constantly, beautifully, with conviction — but sign only ever said what it said.

And the sign said they would come to the ferry at Marrow Ford.

It was the only crossing left to them. The bridge at Two Wells was down, the mountain passes were already snow-locked, and every other ford on the Greywater ran through temple land where wardens waited in threes and fours. Marrow Ford alone sat in the seam between jurisdictions — half the queen's writ, half nobody's — which meant it was exactly the kind of place a clever scribe would choose and exactly the kind of place a desperate one would be forced into regardless.

So Coram dismissed his six remaining wardens. He told them to sweep the eastern bank, three miles wide, and report at dusk. He watched them go with their lanterns bobbing like fireflies drunk on duty, and then he sat down on a fallen willow at the edge of the reed-beds, wrapped his cloak around himself, and waited alone in the grey hour before dawn for the people he had sworn to bring home in chains.

The fog came off the water the way it always did at Marrow Ford, slow and white and smelling of iron. Somewhere across the river a heron cried once and thought better of it. Coram counted his breaths, the way the old drill-masters had taught him, and tried not to think about the letter in his breast pocket — the one from Mother Aldessa, written in her small precise hand, which contained a single sentence beneath the seal of her office:

*Bring me the vessel alive. The scribe may be returned to the ledger or to the ground, as convenience dictates.*

As convenience dictates. Eleven years a hunter, four years a warden, and still those words sat in him like a swallowed stone. He had killed men. He had killed men who deserved it and men who did not, and he had learned to carry both kinds the same way, in the same quiet room behind his ribs. But *as convenience dictates* was not an order to kill. It was permission to stop thinking about it altogether, and that was a different thing entirely, and Aldessa knew it was a different thing, and she had written it anyway, in her small precise hand, because she trusted him not to flinch.

He was beginning to suspect she had misjudged him.

He heard them before he saw them — the particular rhythm of two people moving carefully through reeds, one heavy and one light, the light one set down and lifted again. Then the fog thinned, and there they were at the water's edge: the scribe, gaunt and road-worn, his scholar's hands cracked and dirty; and the child on his hip, small and dark-eyed, watching the fog the way other children watched festival fires.

Coram stood up out of the willow-shadow and said, quietly, so as not to startle the girl:

"Brother Quentin Kavanagh. We should talk."

* * *

To his lasting surprise, the scribe did not run.

Coram had prepared for running. He had prepared for screaming, for bargaining, for the scribe to put the child down and put himself between her and the warden's blade with the doomed arithmetic of a man who knows he cannot win but has decided the losing will cost something. What he had not prepared for was Quentin Kavanagh turning slowly, setting the girl on a flat stone above the waterline, and looking at him with the exhausted clarity of a man who had been expecting this conversation for nineteen days.

"You sent your wardens east," Quentin said.

"I did."

"Why?"

"Because I wanted to speak with you alone, and my wardens are honest men who would have made that difficult." Coram kept his hands visible, open, away from the sword at his hip. He had learned long ago that the first ten seconds of any parley decided everything, and that the fastest way to lose those seconds was to reach for steel. "I am not going to seize you, Brother Quentin. Not yet. Not until you've answered one question."

"And if I refuse to answer?"

"Then I take you both back to the valley, and Mother Aldessa does whatever she intends to do, and I spend the rest of my life wondering." He let that sit in the fog between them. "I'd rather wonder the other thing."

Quentin's eyes narrowed. Up close, Coram could see how far the man had come from the careful, ink-stained functionary he remembered from the archive corridors — the scribe who moved through the sealed stacks like a mouse through a cathedral, present and invisible. This version had hollows under his eyes and a burn scar on his right palm he hadn't had before, and when he looked at Coram he looked at him the way one soldier looks at another across a truce line: measuring, wary, unwilling to waste the moment on hatred.

"What question?" Quentin asked.

"The one I've been carrying since Two Wells." Coram took a breath. "You were in the sealed stacks. You read things none of us are permitted to read. And then you burned your ledger page and ran, taking with you the one person in this world whom Mother Aldessa wants more than she wants the valley itself. I have served the temple my whole life, Brother Quentin. I have done its hard work and its harder work, and I have told myself every night that the hard work was necessary, that the walls we keep are load-bearing, that if we ever looked too closely at what holds them up the whole roof would come down on everyone we love." His voice stayed level, but barely. "So my question is this. Prove it. Prove that the temple lied. Because if you can't, then you're a thief and an apostate and I'm doing my duty this morning. And if you can—"

He stopped. The heron cried again, farther off.

"—then I need to know it," he finished, "more than I need my duty."

* * *

For a long moment the scribe said nothing. The child — Wren, the reports called her, though Coram suspected the name was Quentin's invention — watched the exchange with an attention that unsettled him. Children flinched, fidgeted, hid behind adults. This one simply watched, dark and patient, like something old wearing a child the way a traveler wears a borrowed coat.

"You understand what you're asking," Quentin said at last. "If I show you, you can't unshow yourself. Whatever you do afterward — arrest us, let us go, kneel in the mud and weep — you'll do it knowing. There's no ledger entry that unwrites knowledge, Brother Coram. Believe me. I've looked."

"I know what I'm asking." Coram found, to his own surprise, that his voice had gone rough. "I've known since Two Wells. Do you know what happened at Two Wells, Brother Quentin? No — of course you don't, you were already gone. A family of hedge-folk, three generations in the same cottage, harbored you for a night. When we traced you there, the standing order was to burn the hearth and question the survivors. An old woman, her daughter, a boy of six." He watched Quentin's face change. "Yes. That's the order I was given. That's the order *she* gave. I reported the cottage empty and paid the family out of my own purse to disappear for a season, and I filed false testimony for the first time in eleven years of service, and I have not slept properly since. So don't lecture me about unwritable knowledge, scribe. I've been carrying mine for three weeks. I'm asking you to make it worth the weight."

The fog was thinning. Light came up off the Greywater, grey-gold and cold, and in it Coram saw the scribe make his decision — saw it happen in the shoulders first, the way decisions always showed in the body before the face agreed to them.

"Sit down," Quentin said. "This takes a while. And Wren—" he touched the girl's hair, gently, "—stay where I can see you."

* * *

What followed, Coram would remember for the rest of his life — and later, when everything else had changed, this memory would remain fixed and whole, the way a nail remains fixed in a wall after the picture is taken down.

Quentin Kavanagh did not produce documents. He produced memory.

"I was indentured at nine years old," he began, sitting on the willow-log with his ruined hands folded, "for debts my father incurred before I could walk. My debt was twenty-two years of service. I had six remaining when the child arrived at vespers. You should understand what that means, Brother Coram. Every choice I made afterward, I made against six years of freedom. Nobody runs from six years of freedom unless what they've learned is worse than the cage."

He spoke of the sealed stacks — the liturgy in the dead tongue, pre-kingdom, older than the temple's own founding texts. He recited fragments of it aloud, and Coram felt the hair rise on his arms, because the cadence of it was wrong in a way he had no words for, ancient and rhythmic, like a tide pulling at a language.

He spoke of the founders' records. The original scribes who had built the valley's seal — twelve of them, names struck from every public register, surviving only in a folio hidden behind a false spine in the deepest stack. He recited their names, one by one, and watched each name land on Coram like a small stone.

"Aldessa taught us the founders ascended," Coram said slowly. "That they were taken up into the Hollow as reward for sealing the war away."

"They were murdered," Quentin said. "All twelve. Within a year of the seal being set. By the temple's first wardens, on the orders of the first Mother. Their testament survives because one of them — Scribe Maren, the youngest — hid a copy before they came for her. I have it entire. Every word. Do you want it?"

Coram wanted to say no. He wanted, with a desperation that frightened him, to stand up and draw his sword and end this here, on the willow-log, in the clean simplicity of duty. Instead he heard himself say: "Give it to me."

And Quentin gave it.

He recited for the better part of an hour. Coram had served beside temple archivists his whole life; he knew what rote memorization sounded like, the stumbling recitation of a man reconstructing text from frequent use. This was not that. This was the folio itself speaking through a man's mouth — word-perfect, margin-notes and all, including the ink blots Quentin described because his memory held even the flaws of the page. And the testament of Scribe Maren said, in language that grew plainer and more terrible as it went:

*We did not seal the war to protect the valley. We sealed it because the war could not be won, and because our masters preferred a lie that could be governed to a truth that could not. The null-field is not a blessing. It is a lid. And lids are maintained by the hands that fear what is beneath them. If you are reading this, we are dead, and the temple you serve was built on our graves by the people who dug them.*

There was more. Much more. The true history of the Festival of Stillness — not a commemoration of peace but a memorial for the villages beyond the border that had been emptied to feed the seal's first casting. The accounting of vessels: bloodlines tracked, marriages arranged, children born into the temple's custody for generations, each one a key kept close to the lock. The reason the valley's magiclessness was called protection in every text Quentin had ever copied, and why the word *prison* appeared nowhere, and why its absence was the loudest thing in the archive.

When Quentin finally fell silent, the sun was fully up. The fog had burned away. The Greywater ran bright and indifferent past the ford, and the child Wren was still sitting on her stone, watching Coram with those old dark eyes, and Coram realized his face was wet and had been for some time.

* * *

He did not kneel. He wanted to — some part of him, the part raised on psalms, understood that this was the moment kneeling existed for — but he was fifty-one years old and a hunter by trade, and his body had forgotten how. So he sat on the cold ground at the edge of the reeds with his sword across his knees, and he wept the way soldiers weep, silently and with great irritation, and neither Quentin nor the child remarked upon it, which he would later count among the mercies of his life.

"My brother," he said at last, when he could trust his voice. "My younger brother. He was born in the valley, in the year of the great frost. The midwives said the stillness saved him — that anywhere else, the sickness that was in the air that winter would have taken him. My mother offered him to the temple because of it. Gave her second son to the Hollow in gratitude." He laughed, and there was nothing in the laugh. "A thank-offering. To the lid. For holding."

"It doesn't make the temple evil," Quentin said quietly. "That's the trap of it. Most of them believe. Aldessa believes — I'm sure of it now, more surely than I'm sure of anything. Belief is what makes it work. A prison run by cynics would fall in a generation. This one runs on people like you, Brother Coram. Good men doing hard work and calling the hardness necessity."

"Yes." Coram wiped his face with the heel of his hand. "And what am I now, do you suppose? Now that I've heard Maren's testament? Still a good man? Or just a man who knows better and hasn't decided yet?"

"That decision's yours. I can't copy it into you." Quentin almost smiled. "Believe me. I've thought about trying."

Coram looked at the child. She looked back. And there — he could not say how he knew, but he knew, the way he knew sign and weather and the weight of a lie in a man's mouth — he saw that she understood every word that had passed between them. Not the valley-tongue, perhaps. Something deeper. She had listened to the testament of her own ancestors' murderer's heirs recited on a riverbank, and she had not looked away once.

"The letter from Aldessa," Coram said. He drew it from his breast pocket, unfolded it, and read it aloud in full, because it seemed suddenly obscene to hold anything back: *Bring me the vessel alive. The scribe may be returned to the ledger or to the ground, as convenience dictates.* He held the paper out over the water, and let the wind take it, and they watched it settle on the current and turn twice and sink. "My wardens regroup at dusk, eight miles east. They believe I'm sweeping the western bank. I can give you until tomorrow's dawn before anyone thinks to check the ford — less if the temple sends riders, and Aldessa will send riders when I fail to report."

"Then we cross now," Quentin said, standing, lifting the child to his hip. "Thank you, Brother Coram. I know what this costs you."

"It costs me everything I was yesterday." Coram rose too, and his hand went to his sword out of forty years of habit, and then — deliberately, watching himself do it — unbuckled the scabbard belt and dropped the whole of it into the reeds. "Keep to the north bank past the shrine at Hallowmere. There's a warden post there, but it's Sister Ede's, and Ede owes me a life-debt from the plague winter, and Ede asks questions before she obeys. Tell her Coram vouches. After that—" he shook his head, "—after that I don't know what the road looks like for any of us. But I know where it ends. They'll all converge on the capital eventually. The queen wants the vessel, the temple wants the vessel, and there's a third party in the city that isn't either, and they frighten me more than the other two combined."

"The bells," Quentin said. His face had gone pale. "Wren dreams about bells."

"Then you understand better than I do." Coram looked at the child one last time. "Go. Cross while the light's low. And Brother Quentin—" he hesitated, and then said the thing he had walked nineteen days to say, though he had not known it until now: "When you write this down someday — and you will, men like you always do — write my name in it honestly. Not as a hero. Just as a man who asked the right question too late, and did the next right thing anyway."

The scribe nodded once. Then he waded into the shallows with the child on his hip, and the Greywater took their weight, and Coram Vane stood alone on the bank of the ford he had guarded all night, watching two fugitives grow smaller in the morning light — knowing that when dusk came and his wardens returned, he would have to invent a story, and that the story would be the first stone in a new life he could not yet see the shape of.

Behind him, far to the east, faint as a memory, he thought he heard bells.

They were ringing the wrong number of times.
