## 23. The City of Bells

The city announced itself long before they saw it.

Quentin heard it first — a sound like the sky clearing its throat, low and resonant, rolling across the plains in waves. Wren stopped on the crest of the hill, her small hand tightening around his, and tilted her head the way she did when she was listening to something no one else could hear.

"Bells," he said. "Hundreds of them. The capital is called Vael Amaranth — the City of Bells. Every temple, every tower, every guildhall has its own set. They ring for births and deaths and coronations and market days. I read about it once." He had copied an entire treatise on the city's architecture during his third year of indenture, his quill moving over vellum while his mind wandered through streets he'd never seen. "I never thought I'd stand here."

He had imagined this moment differently. In his imaginings — the few he'd permitted himself, stolen between ledger lines — arriving at the capital had always felt like an ending. Freedom at last, the debt paid, the gates opening onto a life that belonged to him. Now he stood on a dusty road with a dying child's hand in his and a forged pilgrim's pass in his pocket, and the bells sounded less like celebration than like counting. Ring after ring after ring, marking something down.

"They know we're coming," Wren said. Her valley-tongue had grown fluent these past weeks, but she still spoke slowly, as if each word were a stone she had to lift and place with care. "The bells are wrong."

"Wrong how?"

She frowned, searching for the shape of it. "They're not ringing *for* anything. They're ringing *at* everything. Like eyes."

Quentin looked at the city again — the white walls flushed gold by the late sun, the hundred towers, the great cathedral spire rising at its heart like a needle through the world — and felt, for the first time since crossing the border, a longing for the valley's silence. At least in the Hollow nothing had watched him. Here, everything did.

* * *

They entered through the Pilgrim's Gate at dusk, in a river of travelers: wool merchants and hedge-priests, a troupe of mummers with painted faces, farmers driving geese to market, two women in the gray robes of some order Quentin didn't recognize who smiled at Wren as they passed — smiled too long, he thought, their eyes lingering on the girl with an attention that made his skin crawl.

Inside the walls, the city swallowed them whole.

It was nothing like the treatise had described, because the treatise had described streets and buildings and Quentin now understood that a city was none of those things. A city was a *sound*. The bells overhead layered upon one another until the air itself seemed to tremble; beneath them ran the roar of ten thousand voices, cart wheels, hooves on cobblestone, a street preacher shouting about the end of days, a fiddler, a dog fight, a woman selling honey cakes whose cry rose above it all like a gull. Smells stacked on smells — woodsmoke, horse dung, roasting meat, incense drifting from a shrine on every second corner, tallow, river mud, and under it all something faintly sweet and rotten that Quentin couldn't name and couldn't stop noticing.

Wren pressed against his side. She had faced down temple wardens and border stones and the slow unraveling of her own blood without complaint, but the city frightened her in a way he could see plainly, though she would never say it. Too many people. Too many bells. And — he realized, watching her flinch as they passed a shrine where a hedge-witch was reading charms into a copper bowl — too much *magic*. Loose magic, ambient and unsealed, threading through every street like water through gravel. To a vessel of her bloodline, the capital must have felt like standing in a river with a wound.

"We need lodging," he said. "Somewhere quiet. Away from the shrines."

"The witch said—" Wren began, then stopped. The old woman at the hearth, three villages back, had given them a name and an address written in charcoal on a scrap of linen: a rooming house in the Weavers' Quarter run by a widow sympathetic to her craft. "The Bellringer's Rest," Wren recited. "Ask for Maren. Say the ash-elm sent us."

* * *

The Bellringer's Rest turned out to be a narrow building leaning companionably against its neighbors, four stories tall, its ground floor a tavern loud with the arguments of dyers and weavers come off their shifts. Maren was a broad, gray-haired woman with forearms like a blacksmith's and eyes that measured them both in a single glance — the tired young man, the quiet child, the road dust, the way Quentin kept Wren behind him without seeming to.

"Ash-elm, is it," she said, when he gave the phrase. Something in her face shifted — not warmth exactly, but a settling, the look of someone deciding a risk was worth taking. "Two rooms. Back of the house, top floor. You don't leave by the front door after dark, you don't ask what my other lodgers do, and if anyone comes asking after you, I never saw your faces." She slid two keys across the bar. "Supper's till ninth bell. Eat. You both look half-starved."

The room was small and clean and had a window overlooking a light-well rather than the street, which Quentin suspected was deliberate. Wren claimed the bed nearest the wall immediately, curled up facing the plaster, and within minutes her breathing deepened into sleep — the heavy, unnatural sleep that had been coming over her more often lately, the burning-out making itself known in longer and longer darknesses.

Quentin sat on the windowsill and let himself feel, for one unguarded moment, the full weight of how far they had come and how far there was left to go.

The witch's words were still with him, worn smooth by repetition like a stone carried in a pocket. *The vessel burns out. The Unbinding will happen whether or not anyone intends it — unless the vessel is emptied willingly.* And beyond that, the thing she hadn't said outright but had looked at him when she thought of: *emptied willingly* meant Wren choosing to die, or something very near it. And every power in the world was converging on this city to decide for her.

He took out the scraps he carried — the linen with the witch's other marks, a copy of the founders' testament in his own hand, folded small — and was reviewing them when he heard the bells change.

All across the city, mid-swing, every bell fell silent at once.

The silence lasted three heartbeats. Then a single bell began to toll — deep, slow, from the cathedral at the city's heart. One. Pause. Two. Pause. Three.

Wren woke instantly, sitting bolt upright, her eyes wide and fixed on nothing.

"What is it?" Quentin whispered. "What's happening?"

"That's not a bell," she said. Her voice had gone strange, flat, the voice she used only when the blood in her was speaking louder than the girl. "That's a *call*. Someone is ringing a call." She pressed her hands over her ears and rocked once, twice. "It's looking for me. It goes through the walls. It goes through *me*."

Down in the tavern, the noise had died. Through the floorboards Quentin could hear Maren's voice, low and hard, ordering someone to bar the door.

The tolling stopped at nine strokes. In the silence after, Quentin heard — or thought he heard, or perhaps felt in the marrow rather than the ear — a second sound underneath the first, rising from somewhere across the city. A chanting. Hundreds of voices, rhythmic, rising and falling in a pattern that made his scribe's memory snag and shudder, because he *knew* the pattern. He had seen it inked in a sealed folio in the Temple of the Hollow's restricted stacks, catalogued under a heading he had never been able to forget:

*Rites of the Unbinding — proscribed.*

Someone in this city was performing a fragment of the Unbinding liturgy. Not the queen's court, then. Not the temple wardens. A third party — the rival cult the hedge-witch had warned them of, the ones who wanted the war-magic released, who wanted the war *finished*, who needed a vessel of the old bloodline to do it.

And they had just rung a bell that reached inside Wren's chest and called her by name.

"It knows I'm here," Wren whispered. She wasn't crying; that was somehow worse. She said it the way she'd say *it's raining*. "It always knew. The bells aren't for the city. They've been calling since we crossed the plain."

* * *

Maren came up near midnight with bread, cheese, and a lamp, and stayed to talk with the door barred behind her.

"You'll have heard the nine-stroke," she said. She lowered herself onto the room's single stool with the groan of a woman whose knees had opinions. "That's the Choir of the Opened Veil. They rent the Old Chantry off Candlemaker's Row twenty years ago and nobody's been able to shift them since. Rich. Patient. They buy people — aldermen, gate captains, bell-ringers." She spat neatly into the rushes. "Nine years ago they tried to buy *me*. Wanted word of any traveler who came asking quiet questions, especially ones traveling with children. I told them what they could do with their coin."

"And now?" Quentin asked quietly.

"Now they've raised the price." Maren looked at him steadily. "There are men asking at the gates tonight. Temple robes under traveling cloaks — your lot, or close enough. And separately, courtiers' men, wearing livery so fine it squeaks. Both asking after a man and a child off the mountain road. The temple's offering silver. The court's offering *pardon*." Her eyes flicked toward Wren's bed, where the girl lay feigning sleep with the skill of someone who had learned young that survival meant hearing everything. "So. Which are you running from, scribe? Or should I guess: both?"

"Both," Quentin admitted. "And a third you haven't named."

"The Veil." Maren nodded unsurprised. "Then listen, because I'll say this once. This city has three hands reaching for that child, and every one of them thinks it's the hand that saves her. The temple wants her dead-or-bound — sacrifice dressed up as mercy, I've seen that robe before. The court wants her *weaponized* — the queen's been hunting a lever against the temple for a decade, and a living key to the old war-magic is the biggest lever ever forged. And the Veil wants her *opened* — they think releasing the sealed war will burn away the false kingdoms and bring back the true age. None of them will ask her what she wants. Children of her sort never get asked."

"How do you know all this?"

"I'm old and I listen and I dye cloth for three different masters, which means I hear three different sets of secrets." Maren rose, joints cracking. "One more thing. There's a fourth party in this city, small and poor and mostly ignored: folk like me, and the ash-elm, and the hedge-witches, and a few priests gone quiet with shame. We don't want the child for anything. We just don't want her *taken*." She paused at the door. "But understand, scribe — wanting a thing and being able to protect it are different animals entirely. Get some sleep. Tomorrow I'll take you through the cloth market to the ash-elm's cousin, a woman who can move people out of cities the way water moves downhill. Quietly."

After she left, Quentin sat a long time in the dark, listening to the bells resume their ordinary counting, and found that Maren's words had done nothing to comfort him. Because he had already known it, hadn't he? Had known it since the border stone, since Aldessa's blessing-that-was-a-sacrifice, since the queen's name first surfaced in Ilsa's warnings. He had simply hoped the city would prove kinder than the valley.

It hadn't. It was the same architecture at larger scale: everyone wanted Wren, and no one wanted *her*.

He looked over at the bed by the wall. Wren's eyes were open in the dark, watching him.

"I heard," she said.

"I know."

"She's right, though. About the fourth party." Wren sat up, hugging her knees. "But Quentin — the fourth party is small, and the other three are large, and large things crush small things without meaning to. Like carts crushing flowers. Nobody even looks down." She said it without self-pity, which made it unbearable. "So we shouldn't wait here to be crushed. We should go to the largest one first."

He stared at her. "Go *to* them?"

"If we hide, the Veil finds us, or the temple finds us, and we're just prey. But if we walk into the light — if we go to the court ourselves, on our own feet, before they come for us — then we're not prey anymore. We're a petition." Her chin lifted, and for a moment he saw not a child but something older looking out through her eyes, something that had inherited a war. "My mother taught me that. The last lesson before the end. *Never be found. Always arrive.*"

Outside, the bells rang the hour — midnight, twelve strokes, the whole city shivering with sound — and Quentin understood, sitting in the dark of a stranger's room in a city that wanted the child beside him for three different kinds of consumption, that he had brought her out of one prison only to deliver her to the gates of another.

And yet her plan was better than his. That was the worst part. His plan had been hiding, and hers was courage.

"All right," he said finally. "Tomorrow. But we go on our terms, and we keep the testament hidden, and if the queen's hand closes, Ilsa—"

"Ilsa will find us," Wren said, with the certainty of someone stating weather. "She always does."

Below them, muffled by floorboards, the tavern sang its last song of the night. Above them, the bells counted on, patient as ledgers, and somewhere across the sleeping city, Quentin was certain, someone was writing their names into a book of a very different kind — and every faction in Vael Amaranth believed itself the author.

He lay down beside the door, between Wren and the world, and did not sleep.
