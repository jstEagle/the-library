## 24. Court of Whispers

The summons had come on vellum, which was the first warning. Ilsa Vane had spent enough years among soldiers to know that a commander who wanted obedience sent orders on cheap paper, stamped and blunt, the kind of thing a man could burn without regret. But the queen's invitation — *invitation*, that was the second warning — arrived on thick cream vellum, sealed in green wax, with her own hand inked beneath the chancery's careful script: *Ser Ilsa Vane is graciously desired at court.*

Graciously desired. As if Ilsa were a spice, or a hound of good breeding, something to be kept because it might prove useful.

She had come anyway. Not out of loyalty — she had buried whatever loyalty she owned in a ditch outside a temple gate, along with four wardens and most of her illusions — but because the letter contained one line that made refusal impossible:

*The child and the scribe are guests of the Crown. Their welfare is assured.*

Welfare assured by a crown. In Ilsa's experience, nothing needed assuring so badly as what a powerful person intended to take.

So she rode through the capital gates in the gray hour before dawn, past walls that still bore scorch-marks from a war everyone pretended had ended two hundred years ago, and presented herself at the palace under a sky the color of old pewter.

* * *

They gave her a room instead of a cell, which told her how much they already knew. A small chamber in the east wing, high enough to see the river, low enough that no one below could pretend not to hear everything said within it. On the bed lay clothes: a doublet of dark green wool, a sword belt of oiled leather, boots that would fit her exactly.

That was the third warning, and the sharpest. Someone had measured her. Someone had asked questions about her height, the length of her stride, whether she favored her left shoulder since the gate. Courts did not measure you unless they were deciding where you fit into their plans — like a beam fitted into a wall, or a knife into a slot.

She dressed because refusing would only tell them she had something to protect. She kept her own sword, though, and let their belt hang on the peg. Small disobediences. She had learned to live on those the way a garrison lives on stores.

A servant came for her at midmorning and led her down corridors that grew quieter as they went, until Ilsa realized the silence itself was a kind of architecture — thick doors, tapestries heavy enough to smother sound, guards placed not to stop intruders but to mark the boundaries of what could be overheard. The servant stopped at last before a door of black oak carved with bells. Dozens of them, ringing outward from a single point, and at that point, worn smooth by centuries of touching hands, a small blank circle.

"The queen will receive you in the Whisper Gallery," the servant said, and knocked once, softly, and was gone before the door opened.

* * *

The gallery was long and narrow and lit entirely by clerestory windows set high above, so that the light fell in pale slanted columns onto a floor of green marble. Along both walls stood niches, and in each niche a bell — bronze, silver, glass, clay — hundreds of them, none of them hanging, all of them silent, mounted on stands like specimens in an apothecary's cabinet.

Ilsa had heard of this place. Every soldier who had ever served near the capital had heard of it. The Bells of the Old Accord, the guides called them. Cast before the kingdom was a kingdom, when the valley was founded and the great sealing done. They were supposed to have rung together, once, on the day the Hollow was made — a sound so vast it was felt in the bones rather than heard, and after it faded, magic never crossed the valley borders again.

Now they hung mute in their niches, and queens walked between them when they wanted to say things no wall should remember.

Queen Maren IV stood at the far end of the gallery, alone, studying a bell of cloudy glass no larger than a fist. She did not turn when Ilsa entered. She let the silence stretch until it became a test.

"Ser Vane." Her voice was unhurried, pleasant, the voice of a woman who had never needed to raise it. "You held a gate against your own order's wardens. Four dead. You let two fugitives slip beyond pursuit." She touched the glass bell with one finger; it gave no sound. "Most knights would call that treason."

"I'd call it arithmetic," Ilsa said. "Four wardens against a child."

"And now you've accepted my invitation. What does that arithmetic make you?"

"A guest who read the vellum carefully, Majesty. Guests who refuse queens tend to become prisoners, and prisoners can't do anyone any good."

Something moved at the corner of the queen's mouth. It might have been approval. It might have been the expression of a woman noting a debt in a ledger she kept behind her eyes.

"You survived the temple gate," Maren said. "Coram's report reached me — yes, even Coram reports to me now, in his way; men who doubt their masters always find their doubts drift toward a throne — and then you vanished for a month. I had you looked for." She turned at last, and her eyes were the pale gray of frost forming on a window. "I found you in a hedge-witch's house in Marrowfen, asking questions about vessels and bloodlines. Asking about the girl."

Ilsa kept her face still. Inside, cold water poured down her spine. Marrowfen was three hundred miles away. Someone there had sold her name for less than a night's lodging.

"I was making sure she was alive," Ilsa said. "I owed her that much. I owe Quentin Kavanagh more."

"Quentin Kavanagh." The queen tasted the name. "Yes. The scribe. My people tell me he has a memory like a cathedral — every text he ever copied, every page, every seal, filed away in that careful head. Is that true?"

"It's true."

"And the girl. Jude, they're calling her now. Or Wren. The child with the blood of the founders in her veins." The queen walked slowly along the row of bells, her fingertips hovering just above them, never quite touching. "Do you know what she is, Ser Vane? Truly?"

"I know what the temple says she is. I know what the kingdoms beyond say. I know she's a child who watched people she loved die hunting her, and that every power I've ever seen wants to hold her up and cut something out of her." Ilsa let the words sit. "Majesty, if you've summoned me here to dress your intentions in better cloth than the temple's, you should know I'll notice the stitching."

The silence that followed had weight, like the pause before a bell is struck.

"You are refreshingly rude," the queen said. "Come. Walk with me. There is something I want you to see before we speak of intentions."

* * *

At the gallery's end stood a door Ilsa had taken for decoration, banded in iron older than any smithy she knew. The queen pressed her palm flat against it — some ward waking with a faint taste of ozone, a smell Ilsa hadn't encountered since crossing the valley border and feeling the world flood back to life — and the door swung inward onto darkness.

Stairs. Down. The air grew cooler with each step, and older, and at the bottom the queen lifted a lamp whose flame burned a color flame shouldn't be, a soft gold-green, and light fell across a wall.

Not a wall. A record.

Stone stretched away into shadow on either side, and carved across it, in ranks upon ranks, were names. Tens of thousands of names, in scripts Ilsa half-recognized from Quentin's frantic midnight explanations — pre-kingdom liturgical glyphs beside early royal script beside temple shorthand, generation after generation of hands adding lines to a list that had never stopped growing.

"What is this?" she asked, though part of her already knew. Part of her had known since the gate, since Wren had pressed her small hand to Ilsa's gauntlet and Ilsa had seen the sigil of her old order branded into the girl's palm.

"The Roll of the Sealed," Queen Maren said quietly. "Every vessel of the founder's blood since the valley was made. Every one of them taken into keeping by someone — a temple, a crown, a cult, a conqueror. And every one of them dying young." She raised the lamp higher. "Look at the marginalia, Ser Vane. That's the interesting part."

Ilsa stepped closer. Beside many of the names, in cramped later script, ran small annotations. *Taken by the Temple, age six. Died age nine.* *Held by the Crown, age four. Died age seven.* *Ransomed by the Ashen Choir. Died the same night.* Page after page, column after column, three hundred years of children passing from keeper to keeper like coins too dangerous to spend, and every entry ending the same way.

"They burn out," Ilsa said. Her voice sounded strange in the underground cold. "The witch in Marrowfen told us. The vessel burns out around the magic inside it. Keeping them doesn't protect them. It kills them faster."

"Yes." The queen set the lamp down. "And yet every institution that has ever held one believed, sincerely, that it was protecting the world." She smiled, and it was the loneliest smile Ilsa had ever seen on a monarch. "I have read this wall every week since I was crowned. I promised myself I would not add a name to it. Do you believe me?"

"No," Ilsa said honestly. "But I believe you want to."

"Good. Wanting is where honesty begins." The queen took up the lamp again. "Here is my situation, plainly. The temple is coming — Aldessa's zealots, and worse, the ones even Aldessa fears. The Ashen Choir wants the Unbinding performed so they can harvest what wakes. Half my council wants the girl weaponized against the temple; the other half wants her handed to the temple to buy peace. And somewhere beneath this city sleeps a war that never ended, waiting for her blood to open it." She paused. "I intend none of those things. I intend to keep her safe, studied, and — when the time comes — given a choice, which no one in three hundred years has offered one of her blood. For that, I need people whose loyalty is to her and not to me. People who have already proven they will commit treason for a child." She met Ilsa's eyes. "I am offering you a place at court, Ser Vane. Rank, pardon, purpose. Guard the scribe and the girl. Report to me. Protect them from everyone — including, if it comes to it, from me."

It was well made. Ilsa had sat through enough officers' speeches to admire the construction: the confession, the flattery, the enemy named, the escape hatch left visibly open. A speech built by someone who understood that the best cages are the ones the bird believes it chose.

"Majesty," she said slowly, "may I ask one question?"

"You may."

"When your people found me in Marrowfen — who did they buy my name from? Because whoever it was knew I'd sheltered fugitives, knew my route, knew where I slept. If they can find me that fast, they can find Quentin and the girl. And if they can find the girl, Majesty, then either your protection is thinner than you claim—" she held the queen's frost-gray gaze— "or you already knew where they were before you ever sent for me. Before you needed a guard who'd already proven her treason. Because a guard who's loyal to the child is useful. But a guard who thinks she chose the child herself?" She shook her head once. "That's a guard who'll never wonder why the cage door was open."

For a moment — just a moment — something flickered behind the queen's composure. Not guilt. Regret, perhaps, of the efficient kind: regret that the truth had been noticed.

"You were measured for those doublets before you ever entered my city," Maren said at last, evenly. "Yes. My people have watched them since the City of Bells. They are safe. Safer than they have ever been. Everything I told you is true — but understand, Ser Vane, that truth in a court is a tool like any other, and I did not become queen by leaving tools lying about unused." She gestured toward the stairs. "Think on my offer. You have until the evening bell. When you decide, come to the Whisper Gallery and tell these old bells. They keep secrets better than any living minister I possess."

Ilsa climbed the stairs with the Roll of the Sealed burning behind her eyes, three hundred years of short lives and marginalia, and the queen's voice following her up like smoke: *everything I told you is true.*

Everything true, and arranged. That was the thing about courts, she thought, pushing open the door into the bell-lit gallery. The temple lied with iron and fire, plainly, so you could hate it cleanly. The court lied with hospitality and pardons and open doors, and by the time you found the bars, you'd already been calling them home for months.

* * *

She found Quentin that same afternoon, in the palace library — where else. He sat at a scarred oak table with Wren beside him, both of them bent over an atlas of the old kingdoms, and for a moment Ilsa simply stood in the doorway and watched them.

Quentin had changed since the valley. He carried himself differently — shoulders back, chin level, the cringing scribe's stoop burned away somewhere on the road. But his hands still moved like a copyist's when he explained things, tracing routes in the air, precise and reverent, and Wren — Jude — leaned into his elbow with the unguarded trust of a fledgling that has decided, finally and irreversibly, whose branch is home.

They looked safe. That was what undid her.

"Ser Vane." Quentin saw her and his whole face opened — relief, gratitude, the uncomplicated joy of a man greeting the person who bled for him at a gate. He rose. "We heard you'd been summoned. We hoped—"

"I need to talk to you," Ilsa said. "Both of you. Somewhere with thin walls, ironically. These ones listen too well."

Jude's eyes went from Ilsa's face to the queen's green doublet, which Ilsa still wore because she had nothing else, and the child's expression closed like a shutter. Children who had been hunted learned to read clothing the way soldiers read terrain.

They walked in the outer gardens, among hedges clipped into shapes of heroes no one remembered, and Ilsa told them everything. The gallery of bells. The wall of names. The offer, word for word, and then the thing underneath the offer — the measurements taken before she arrived, the watchers in Marrowfen, the queen's own admission: *my people have watched them since the City of Bells.*

"She's not lying to you," Ilsa finished. "That's the worst part. She means the protection. She probably even means the choice, someday, in some form. But Quentin — you've spent your life in archives. Look at the structure of it. A vessel, taken into keeping by a powerful institution that sincerely believes it's different from all the others. Guards chosen for their proven attachment to the child, so they'll never question the keeping. Time bought with comfort while the queen decides what a choice looks like when a crown is the one offering it." She spread her hands. "I've stood in that exact structure before. It had a different roof and it called itself a temple."

Quentin was very quiet. The garden breeze moved the hedge-heroes; somewhere beyond the wall, the city rang its afternoon bells, and Ilsa thought of the mute ones underground, three hundred years of silenced witnesses.

"She saved us from Coram's wardens," Quentin said finally. "Her letters of passage got us through three checkpoints. If we run from her, we run with nowhere to run *to*."

"I know."

"If we stay, we're the next entry on that wall. Just with kinder marginalia."

"I know that too."

He looked at Jude. The girl had crouched at the base of a hedge, and she was doing something Ilsa hadn't seen her do in weeks — pressing her palm flat against the earth, eyes half-closed, listening to something none of them could hear. Since the border, since the magic had come flooding back into the world, she did this often. Grounding herself, the hedge-witch called it. Feeling where the land was awake.

When she looked up at them, her face had gone pale and set in a way that made Ilsa's hand drift toward her sword hilt out of pure reflex.

"She isn't going to give us time to choose," Jude said. Her valley-tongue had grown fluent and quick, but she still spoke it carefully, each word laid down like a stone in a stream. "The queen. I felt the wards wake when we passed the inner gates this morning — the *old* wards, the deep ones. They're being re-tuned. Re-keyed." She stood, brushing dirt from her knees. "Someone is fitting this palace to hold me. Not to guard me. To *hold* me. The work started days ago." Her eyes found Quentin's. "Before Ilsa was ever summoned. Before the offer. The choice was decided before it was offered, Quentin. The queen just wanted us calm while the cage was finished."

The garden suddenly seemed very small, and very quiet, and full of listening hedges.

Quentin's face did something complicated — grief and fury and, beneath them, the terrible clarity of a man watching a pattern complete itself one final time. Then he straightened, and Ilsa saw the scribe and the fugitive arrive at the same conclusion in the same breath.

"Then we don't wait for the evening bell," he said. "Ilsa — how quickly can you get us out of a palace that's expecting us to stay?"

Isla was already turning toward the gardens' service gate, counting guards, counting shadows, doing the arithmetic she did best.

"One way to find out," she said. "We move tonight — before the wards finish re-keying. Because once they do, Majesty's gracious hospitality becomes exactly what it was always going to be." She glanced back at them both, at the scribe and the last vessel of a murdered bloodline, and felt the old familiar weight settle onto her shoulders, heavier than any armor she'd ever worn.

"A cage with the door painted open."
