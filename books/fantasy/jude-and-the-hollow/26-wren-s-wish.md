## 26. Wren's Wish

The queen's city smelled of smoke and honey and old stone, and Wren had learned to hate it in the way she hated all beautiful things that were not hers.

She stood at the window of the chamber they had given her — a fine chamber, with tapestries of hunts and harvests and a bed wide enough for four children to sleep abreast — and watched the bells of the capital swing in their towers. They rang at every hour here, sometimes for no reason at all. In the valley there had been one bell, and it had rung only for vespers, and its voice had been flat and small, like everything else the temple permitted. Here the bells were greedy. They rang and rang as if they could fill the silence the way water fills a cup, but Wren knew better than anyone alive that some silences could not be filled. She carried one inside her chest. It was shaped like her mother's voice, which she could no longer remember, and like the word *Unbinding*, which she remembered too well.

Below, in the courtyard, servants were carrying lanterns toward the great hall. The queen was holding another feast. There had been three feasts since they arrived, each one warmer than the last, each one attended by more men and women who looked at Wren the way farmers look at weather — measuring, calculating, deciding whether she would be rain or flood.

They thought she did not understand their speech yet. That was the first lie she had let them keep.

Quentin had taught her well. Too well, perhaps. He had sat with her through that first gray winter in the temple infirmary, drawing pictures on slate because neither of them could speak to the other, and somewhere in the space between his careful hand and her hungry eyes, the language had poured into her like light into a jar. Now she understood the kitchen girls gossiping about the stablemaster's new wife. She understood the guards complaining about their boots. She understood, two nights ago, through the door of the queen's council chamber where she had been sent to wait with her escort pretending not to listen, the words *weapon*, *leverage*, and *when the girl is ready*.

She understood that everyone wanted something from her blood, and no one wanted anything from her.

Except perhaps Quentin. And Ilsa, wherever she was — summoned back into the court's orbit like a stray dog whistled home. And maybe even Coram, the warden with the tired eyes who had knelt in the mud outside the hedge-witch's cottage and said, *I was wrong*, which was more than any priest had ever said to anyone.

Wren pressed her palm against the cold glass. Far to the south and west, past mountains she had crossed half-dead, lay the valley where magic went to be forgotten. She had felt it the moment she crossed its border, all those months ago: the sudden deafness, the muting of the world's great song, like plunging underwater after a lifetime of breathing wind. The others had thought her strange for weeping when they arrived. They hadn't understood. To her, the null-field had not been peace. It had been a bandage wrapped over a screaming wound, and everyone in the valley had lived their whole lives pressing their ears against the bandage and calling the muffled quiet *holiness*.

But she had also felt what lay beneath the bandage. Even muted, even sealed, the war-magic had hummed against her skin like a trapped bee. It knew her. It had been waiting for her the way winter waits at the edge of autumn.

*Come,* it had said, in the old tongue, the tongue of her mothers going back further than any kingdom. *Come and open me.*

And now the hedge-witch Maren's words came back to her in the dark hours, the ones she had not repeated to Quentin because she had seen what fear does to his kind face: *the vessel is burning out, child. Whether or not anyone opens you, you will open. A vessel that cracks spills everything.*

She had been dying slowly since before she ever reached the valley. She could feel it now if she paid attention — a warmth under her sternum that was not fever, a brightness behind her eyes that made ordinary lamplight seem dim. Her blood was a lamp burning down its oil. When it guttered out, everything it held would spill at once, unshaped, unchosen, and the world would get the Unbinding anyway — wild, blind, the war resuming itself like a muscle remembering an old motion.

Unless someone chose first.

Wren turned from the window.

*

She found Quentin in the scriptorium the court had granted him — because of course they had; the queen had learned quickly that the scribe's memory was a library that walked, and libraries were meant to be shelved where their owners could reach them.

He sat bent over a desk by candlelight, copying. He always copied. Even now, even free of the temple, his hands needed the work the way other men's hands needed prayer or drink. Tonight he was writing out, from perfect recollection, the founders' testament — the true history of the seal, the murdered scribes, the war that had never ended. Copies for the queen, he'd said. Copies for Coram. Copies, someday, for everyone.

He looked up when she came in, and his whole face changed the way it always did — the careful court-mask falling away, leaving just Quentin, the man who had named her after a small brown bird because, he said, wrens sang louder than their size should allow.

"You should be asleep," he said. "You've got the presentation tomorrow. The queen wants you—"

"I know what the queen wants." She said it in valley-tongue, their private country of a language, and watched him flinch slightly at how easily she wielded it now.

He set down his pen. "What did you hear?"

"Everything," Wren said. "I always hear everything. You taught me to listen."

He rose and closed the scriptorium door, though the corridor beyond was empty. When he turned back, his face had gone careful again, the way it did when he was frightened and trying not to show it. "Whatever you heard — whatever they're planning — we'll find a way around it. Ilsa and I. We've gotten you this far."

"That's what I need to talk to you about." She climbed onto the bench across the desk from him, the way she had in the temple infirmary a hundred nights ago, knees drawn up, chin on her arms. "Getting me *further*. Everyone keeps talking about getting me further. Further from the temple, further from the queen, further from the Unbinding. As if there's a place far enough away that I stop being what I am."

"Wren—"

"My name isn't Wren."

The words fell between them like a dropped glass. Quentin went very still.

She hadn't meant to say it. Not tonight, not first, not before the other thing. But it came out, and once it was out she could not call it back, and she found she didn't want to. "My mother named me before the riders came. Before the fire. I was too young to remember it — I lost it with everything else, the way you lose a word that's on the tip of your tongue. But I found it again." She touched her own collarbone, where beneath the shift the old sigil-mark sat, the vessel's mark, warm as a coin left in the sun. "It comes back to me in dreams. Jude. My name is Jude."

"*Jude,*" Quentin repeated softly, tasting it.

"It means *praised*, in the old liturgy. The one you found in the sealed stacks. My mothers' tongue." She almost smiled. "The temple sealed away the language my name comes from. Isn't that funny?"

"Jude." He said it again, and this time his voice cracked on it, and she saw his eyes shining in the candlelight, and she loved him so much in that moment that the next words nearly died in her throat. But they could not die. They had waited long enough.

"Quentin. I have to tell you what I'm going to do. And you're going to try to stop me, and I need you to hear all of it before you start."

*

He listened. She would remember that about him forever, in whatever life came after: he listened. His hands gripped the edge of the desk until the knuckles whitened, but he did not interrupt, did not rise, did not call her a child or a fool. He gave her the same courtesy he gave texts — total attention, withholding judgment until the last line was read.

So she told him.

She told him about Maren's reading, the truth she had kept folded away like a letter too painful to open. The burning-out. The deadline living in her blood. She told him what the warmth under her ribs really was — not the war-magic sitting in her like water in a jug, but a lamp, consuming her, and every day the oil lower, and every day the flame finding new things to burn: her stamina, her sleep, the color in her cheeks, the years she should have had.

"I am not a girl who carries a bomb," she said, using the plainest words she knew. "I am a fuse that dreamed it was a girl. That's what a vessel is, Quentin. That's what my whole family was, generation after generation — fuses, passed down, lit a little more with each birth, until someone like me gets born all spark and no wax. The Unbinding doesn't need a ritual. It needs nothing but time and my death. The ritual everyone fears — the queen's version, the temple's version, the cult's version — they're all just arguments about *who lights the match*. Nobody is arguing about whether the fire happens. Because nobody thinks there's any other way."

"There has to be another way," Quentin said quietly. It was not conviction. It was hope, worn thin, and she hated herself a little for what she was about to do to it.

"There is. Maren saw it when she read my blood. She didn't say it aloud because she was afraid, and then she died before she could say it at all, and I have carried it alone ever since, and I can't anymore. I can't carry it alone. That's why I'm telling you." She took a breath, and the warmth under her sternum stirred, listening. "A vessel can be *emptied*. Willingly. Not broken — emptied. Given away instead of spilled. The rite exists. It's in my blood the way my name was — I've been dreaming it since the hedge-witch's hearth, whole verses of it in the old tongue, and when I finally translated them with the piece of your archive-memory you shared with me—" she saw him startle; yes, she had borrowed his recitation of the founders' testament weeks ago, turning his spoken words over in her mind like beads, "—I understood. The founders wrote it themselves. The original seal wasn't meant to be permanent. It was meant to be *returned*. Slowly, over generations, given back to the land a little at a time, so the land could digest it. That's why the first scribes built the valley. Not a prison. A *compost heap*, Quentin. A place where the worst magic of the war could rot down into soil."

"But Aldessa—"

"Aldessa's ancestors murdered them and nailed the lid shut instead. Sealed the valley, stopped the returning, called it holy." Her voice stayed level, though her hands had begun to tremble; the warmth under her ribs had begun to sing. "And so the magic never rotted. It just waited. And waited. And bred vessels to hold the overflow, because the seal leaked, because a thing nailed shut always leaks, until here I am. The last drop of a flood that should have soaked into the earth forty generations ago."

"And the rite you dreamed—"

"Gives it all back. All at once. Every drop, in every vessel, everywhere — there are others like me, Quentin, scattered and hunted and hiding, I can feel them faintly, little lamps guttering across the world — the rite calls to all of us, and empties all of us, and pours the whole war into the ground where it can finally become nothing. No Unbinding. No weapon for the queen. No renewal for Aldessa's seal. No vessels, ever again — no more children born as fuses." She met his eyes. "No more me, either. Not the part that makes me what I am."

The candle flames leaned, though there was no draft.

"What do you mean, *no more you*," Quentin said, and his voice had gone very soft, and very dangerous, the way it did when he quoted the temple's cruelest passages back at himself in the dark.

"The vessel is the lamp. The rite gives away the fire and the oil together. Maren saw it — a vessel emptied willingly doesn't survive as a vessel. Maybe nothing survives. Maybe just—" She shrugged, and tried to make the shrug light, and failed. "Maybe the part that's left is small. Maybe it's like your memory, Quentin. You told me what you're planning — don't look surprised, you talk in your sleep — you're planning to take the pattern into yourself and lose everything else. Your mother's face. Your father's debts. Everything that made you *you*. You've been walking toward that sacrifice since chapter one of your own story, and I love you for it, and I will not let you do it. Because there's a version of the rite where the vessel takes it all — where no one else has to burn — and that version costs exactly what yours costs, except I'm the lamp, so the cost is mine by right."

"No."

"You haven't heard—"

"*No.*" He was standing now, palms flat on the desk, and the candle flames stood straight again as if ashamed of themselves. "Jude—" he stumbled over the name, new and precious and terrible in his mouth, "—Wren. You are nine years old."

"Ten in spring."

"You are ten years old, and kings and priests and queens have spent your entire life deciding what your blood is for, and you are *not* going to finish their work for them by deciding it's for dying. That's not freedom, that's just picking your own executioner, and the executioner is you."

"The alternative," Wren said, "is that I crack. On some ordinary afternoon, in some ordinary year, in a market or a bed or a field, with no rite, no witnesses, no choice — and everything in me spills out raw, and the war wakes up with no mind guiding it, and the world burns the way it burned the first time, except worse, because the first time there were still people alive who remembered how to fight it." She unfolded herself from the bench and stood, small in her borrowed nightdress, and let him see her — truly see her, the way no one at court ever bothered to look past the sigil. "I have thought about this every night since Maren's hearth. I have lain awake feeling myself burn down, oil by oil, and I have chosen, every single night, to keep choosing. That's what you don't understand about me, Quentin. Everyone looks at me and sees a child to be saved or used or sacrificed. But I am the only person alive who has already decided. Kings decide things about me. *I* decide things about the world. Do you see the difference?"

He did not answer. His jaw worked. His eyes were wet and furious and full of the exact shape of her, and she knew that he saw the difference, and hated it, and would fight it anyway, because he was Quentin, and fighting doomed things on behalf of small people was simply what he was.

"The rite needs preparation," she went on gently. "Days of it. The old verses have to be spoken at the seal chamber itself — the real one, under the palace, above the first battlefield. Which means tomorrow, when the queen presents me to her court, when she offers you whatever velvet bargain she's been sharpening — I need you to stand beside me and help me steal the one thing everyone in this city agrees I should never touch." She smiled, and it was the smile of a wren indeed: small, brown, louder than its size. "Help me end the war, Quentin. Help me give it back. And if the cost is me — then help me pay it *choosing*, instead of cracking."

For a long moment the only sound was the bells, ringing out some midnight hour, greedy and golden and far away.

Then Quentin closed the testament he had been copying, squared its pages with the precise, heartbroken neatness of a lifelong scribe, and said:

"Tell me the verses. From the beginning. If we're doing this, we do it perfectly — I won't have your last act be an error in transcription."

And Wren — Jude — laughed, one short bright note, and pulled the stool up beside him, and began to speak the oldest words in the world while the candles burned down around them like lifespans.

Neither of them heard the soft tread in the corridor outside, nor saw the shadow that paused beneath the scriptorium door — a shadow wearing the grey habit of a temple warden, belonging to a man who had come seeking proof of lies and was now standing very still, listening to a child recite the end of all wars.

Brother Coram did not knock. He did not leave, either.

He listened — and his hand, resting on the door, slowly closed into a fist.
