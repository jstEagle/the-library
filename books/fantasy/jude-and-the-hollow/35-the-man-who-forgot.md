## 35. The Man Who Forgot

He woke to the sound of water.

Not the dry silence of the valley — he did not know yet that it was a valley, or that its silence had ever been a thing worth naming — but the true sound of a river moving over stones somewhere below the window of whatever room this was. Light came through shutters in long golden bars, and dust drifted through them, and each mote of dust seemed to him a small miracle, because he could not remember having seen dust before.

That was the first wrongness: not an absence but a strangeness. Everything was new. Not unfamiliar — *new*, as though the world had been made overnight and he had been set down in it still warm.

He lay on a narrow bed under a wool blanket that smelled of lavender and smoke. His body ached in a way he had no words for, a deep-bone weariness like he had carried something enormous a very long way and set it down only moments ago. His hands, when he lifted them into the light, were a scribe's hands — ink-stained at the nails, a callus on the middle finger of his right hand shaped precisely for a pen. He knew what the callus was for. He knew that if someone put a quill in his grip, it would fit.

He did not know why he had one.

*Quentin,* said something inside him, faint as an echo down a long corridor. *Your name is Quentin.*

He held on to that. It was the only thing the corridor had left behind.

He sat up slowly. The room was small and clean, a healer's room or a guest room; there was a basin of water on the stand beside the bed, and a chair, and in the chair —

In the chair was a girl, asleep, her chin dropped to her chest, her hands folded in her lap like a small old woman's. She was perhaps ten years old. Her hair was dark and uncombed and there were shadows under her eyes deep enough to hold candlelight. On the back of her right hand there was a mark — not ink, not bruise — a pale silver tracery like frost on glass, or like the veins of a leaf seen against the sun.

When he looked at the mark, something moved in him. Not memory. Memory would have been a door opening. This was more like standing outside a house he had once lived in and finding every window shuttered, but hearing, from within, the faintest sound of a fire still burning.

The girl opened her eyes.

She looked at him without surprise, without fear, with a kind of terrible patience, the way one looks at a convalescent one has watched for days. Then she stood, crossed the floor, and took his hand — the callused one — in both of hers.

"You're awake," she said. Her voice was rough from disuse or weeping. "You've been asleep for four days."

"Where—" He stopped. Speaking felt like walking on a frozen pond; he tested each word before trusting it. "Where is this?"

"The City of Bells. Under it, then out of it. Ilsa carried you." She said the name *Ilsa* carefully, the way one sets down something breakable. "Don't worry. You'll ask about everything. Everyone does. But first—"

She climbed onto the edge of the bed, which put them nearly eye to eye, and studied his face with a gravity no child should own.

"Do you know your name?"

"*Quentin.*"

"Do you know mine?"

And here was the second miracle of the morning, stranger than the dust motes, stranger than the river-sound: he looked at this girl with the silver mark on her hand, this child who had clearly sat vigil over him for four days, and he knew — absolutely, instantly, the way he knew how to hold a pen — that her name was Wren.

"Wren," he said.

Her face crumpled. Not with grief — with relief, so sudden and so violent that she had to press both fists against her mouth to keep it from becoming a sob. When she lowered her hands her eyes were wet and shining.

"You kept it," she whispered. "Out of everything — you kept *me*."

"I don't understand," Quentin said. "I don't understand any of this. I know my name. I know yours. I know how to write — I can feel it, I know I could copy out a page right now, I'd know all the letterforms, I'd know how to mix ink, I'd know—" He faltered. "But I don't know why I know these things. I don't know where I learned them. I don't know—" He looked around the room, at the shuttered light, at the basin, at the small grave-faced girl. "I don't know where I come from. I don't know if I have a mother. I don't know if I've ever seen a river before today, though I can hear one and it sounds like—" His voice cracked. "It sounds like something I've been missing my whole life, except I don't have a whole life. I have a name and a callus and you."

Wren took his hand again.

"That's because you gave the rest away," she said. "To save me. To save everyone. And it worked, Quentin. It worked."

* * *

They told him in pieces, over days, and the telling was like being read a story about a stranger who wore his hands.

Ilsa came first — a tall woman with grey coming into her braid and a sword-callus of her own, who stood in the doorway of his room the way soldiers stand in doorways, weight forward, ready to leave. She looked at him a long time before speaking.

"You don't remember me," she said.

"No. Should I?"

Something flickered across her face — hurt, quickly mastered, and beneath the hurt a fierce gladness she didn't try to hide. "I dragged you out of a collapsing undercroft on my back," she said. "So yes. A little. But it's better this way, maybe. The man I knew owed too many debts." She set something on the table by his bed: a ledger book, old, its cover scorched at one corner. "Yours. Coram pulled it from the archive ruins. He thought you'd want it."

Quentin touched the cover and felt nothing. It was a stranger's book. But when he opened it, his own handwriting flowed past page after page — neat, disciplined, beautiful in its restraint — recording debts and dates and names, and among them, in entries that grew shakier toward the end, a girl called Wren, and a knight called Vane, and a temple, and a lie.

"It's a good book," Wren said quietly, watching him. "Even if you can't remember writing it. It's how I'll tell you. Page by page. However slow you want."

* * *

The city taught him the rest by simply existing.

He walked in the afternoons now, Wren at his side, Ilsa a watchful pace behind. The City of Bells rang its hours in voices that overlapped and argued, and every bell seemed to Quentin like a celebration of the same thing: that sound could travel, that messages could cross distances, that the world was *connected*. Because that was what everyone kept saying had changed. The null-field — the Hollow, the great silence that had lain over his valley for three hundred years — was gone everywhere, dissolved all at once like frost in morning sun, and magic had come back into the world the way water comes back to a dried riverbed: finding every old channel, filling every crack.

He saw it in small things. A lamplighter whose flame leapt gladly to his taper from a foot away. A baker whose bread rose twice as high as any oven should allow, and who had stopped charging extra for it because it cost him nothing now but joy. Children in the street playing a game where they passed a spark of light between their palms like a ball, shrieking when it popped.

Once, in a market square, an old woman paused in front of Wren, looked at the silver tracery on her hand — faded now, Quentin noticed, fainter every week — and bowed. Actually bowed, low, in the dirt of the marketplace. Wren tugged her hood down and hurried on, and later, sitting on the wall of a bell-tower with her legs swinging, she tried to explain it to him.

"They think I gave it back," she said. "The magic. Some of them want to worship me for it. Ilsa keeps them away." She kicked her heels against the stone. "But it wasn't me. It was you. They just don't know what to do with a hero they can't see."

"I don't feel like a hero," Quentin said. "I feel like a man reading a very good book about one."

"That's what Ilsa said you'd say." Wren smiled — the first real smile he'd seen from her, quick and crooked and young. "She said you were insufferably modest even when you could remember being clever."

"Tell me what I was like," he said. It had become his most frequent request, and she never tired of answering it. "Before."

Wren considered, her feet stilling.

"Frightened," she said at last. "All the time frightened, but you hid it inside the rules. You thought if you copied everything perfectly and filed everything perfectly and owed exactly what you owed, the world couldn't hurt you." She glanced sideways at him. "You were wrong. It hurt you anyway. And then you chose to be hurt worse, on purpose, so I wouldn't have to be hurt at all." She reached over and took his hand, the callused one, and pressed her thumb into the scribe's callus like pressing a seal into wax. "So that's what you were like. That's what matters."

* * *

At night the dreams came, and the dreams were the closest thing he had to memory.

Not pictures — he dreamed no faces, no rooms, no scenes. What came instead was language. He dreamed in texts. Whole passages unfurled behind his eyes in scripts he could not read waking but understood sleeping: liturgies, ledgers, letters, marginalia, the testament of murdered founders written in a dead tongue that somehow he could hear rather than see. He woke some nights with entire paragraphs in his mouth, and Wren, who slept on a pallet by his door — she refused to leave, and Ilsa had given up arguing — would sit up and write them down for him by candlelight, her hand steadier now than it had been, her valley-tongue fluent.

"This one's the founders' testament," she said one such night, squinting at the page. "The part you recited to Aldessa. 'We built the lock knowing we would not live to see the door.'" She looked up. "Do you know what you're dreaming?"

"No. It's like listening to a voice through a wall. I can hear every word and none of it is mine."

"It's yours," Wren said firmly. "It's all yours. You just paid for it with the rest." She blew out the candle and, in the dark, said the thing she had begun saying every night, the way other children say their prayers:

"I'll remember for you. Everything. As long as it takes. That's the bargain now — you carried my secret, and I carry your life. Fair trade."

"Is it?" he asked the darkness.

"No," she said, and he heard the smile in it. "You got the bad end. But you always did."

* * *

On the ninth day, they brought him a desk.

Coram himself carried it up the stairs — a broad, grey-templed man with a warden's bearing and a convert's careful eyes, who set the desk down in Quentin's room, squared it to the window, and stepped back as though presenting evidence.

"The chapterhouse sent it," he said. "What remains of the chapterhouse. There's talk of rebuilding the order on new foundations. Honest ones." He hesitated, and Quentin saw that this man, whoever he had been to the man he no longer was, found the conversation difficult. "There are those who would like you to lead it. Your name carries weight now, even — especially — among people who never met you."

"I can't lead an order," Quentin said. "I don't remember what an order is for."

Coram almost smiled. "Then you're the only honest man available. Which is precisely why they want you." He bowed — stiffly, but he bowed — and left.

Quentin sat down at the desk. Through the window the bells were ringing noon, and the light fell across the wood in bars, and dust drifted through it, and he watched the dust a while, because it still seemed miraculous to him, and he suspected — though he could not have said why — that it always would.

Wren brought paper. Real paper, good paper, and a pot of ink, and a quill, and she laid them out before him with ceremony, arranging each item like an altar.

"Well?" she said.

He took up the quill. It fit his hand the way his own hand fit his hand. He dipped it, and the ink caught the light, and he found — this was the thing that undid him, this small thing after all the vast things — that he knew without thinking how much ink a quill should carry, how to tap the excess, how to angle the first stroke.

He wrote: *My name is Quentin.*

The letters came out perfect. Of course they did. Whatever else had burned away, the writing remained — the one craft, the one devotion, the one prayer his mind had refused to surrender. He stared at the two words a long time. They looked like a stranger's. They looked like his.

"What do I write?" he asked. "If I'm going to start a ledger — a real one, a true one — what's the first entry?"

Wren climbed onto the chair beside him, folded her hands on the desk's edge, and regarded the blank page with the gravity of a co-author, which, he was beginning to understand, was exactly what she intended to be.

"Start with the debt," she said. "That's how your old book started, Ilsa says. So start the new one the same way, but honest." She tapped the page, once, beside his name. "*Entry the first: I owe everything to a girl named Wren, who is teaching me who I was.*"

"And who signs it?" he asked.

"You do. And then I do. Both of us. Every page." She took a second quill from the pot — she had brought two; she had planned this, she had probably planned it for days — and waited.

So Quentin wrote the entry, in his perfect stranger's hand, and beneath it signed his name, and slid the page across to the girl, who added her own signature underneath with the fierce concentration of a child signing something that mattered, which it did, which perhaps nothing had ever mattered more.

Two names on one line. A new kind of ledger.

Outside, the bells rang the quarter hour, and the river went on talking to itself below the window, and somewhere in the city a lamplighter was lighting lamps that needed no lighting anymore, purely for the joy of the flame. Quentin looked at the page, at the two names drying together in the afternoon light, and felt the shape of the empty places inside him — the mother-shaped place, the father-shaped place, the valley-shaped place, all of them dark windows in a house he used to live in — and found that the emptiness did not hurt the way he had expected it to.

Because in every dark window, if he listened, there was that faint fire still burning. And beside him sat its keeper.

"There's one more thing," Wren said quietly, when the ink was dry. She was looking at her hands, at the fading silver tracery, and her voice had gone careful in the way it did when she was about to say something large. "Ilsa had a letter yesterday. From the palace. From the queen herself." She looked up. "She wants to meet us. Both of us. Tomorrow."

Quentin felt the first cold thread of the old fear — the fear he could not remember earning but recognized anyway, the way one recognizes weather.

"A queen," he said slowly, "wants to meet a scribe who remembers nothing, and a child whose work is finished."

"Yes," said Wren. And then, with the crooked smile that was becoming the truest thing he knew: "Which means it isn't finished. Nothing's ever finished. That's what ledgers are *for*."
