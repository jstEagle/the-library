## 36. Reckonings

The queen had learned, in thirty years of court, that the most dangerous negotiations were the ones where you had already lost. She sat behind the long table in the Amber Hall and let the silence do its work while the delegation from the Temple of the Hollow stood in their grey wool like men attending a funeral — which, in a sense, they were. Their god had died in an undercroft beneath the palace three days ago, and they were here to discuss the estate.

Brother Coram sat at the center of them. He looked tired. He looked, the queen thought with a flicker of unwilling respect, like a man who had slept badly for exactly the right reasons.

"You understand," said Chancellor Vess, her oldest advisor, "that we are prepared to recognize the temple's authority over matters of doctrine only. The valley's borders are dissolved. The null-field is gone. Whatever your order was guarding there—"

"Was never ours to guard," Coram finished quietly. "We know. We have read the founders' testament now. All of it." He set a slim bundle of vellum on the table between them, tied with temple cord. "You should read it as well, Majesty. It concerns your kingdom more than ours."

The queen did not touch it. Not yet. Touching things too eagerly was how kings lost arguments. But her eyes went over it once, twice, cataloguing the script, the age of the skin, the way certain passages had been scored out by hands that had wanted very badly to erase them and failed.

"And the girl," she said.

Coram's jaw tightened. "Jude," he said. "Her name is Jude."

"Jude, then." The queen let the name sit in her mouth like a coin she was testing for weight. "She carries nothing now? The vessel is empty?"

"So far as any can tell. The witch at the border read her blood and found it quiet. The land took what she carried back into itself. There is no weapon left in that child, Majesty. There never should have been."

"No weapon." The queen leaned back. Outside the amber-tinted windows, the city rang with bells — it had been ringing with bells for three days, celebration and fear tangled together in every peal, because the whole world had felt magic come home the way a body feels blood return to a sleeping limb: painful, tingling, undeniable. In the market districts, hedge-workers who had hidden their craft for generations were lighting charms openly in their doorways. In the poorer quarters, people were afraid. In all quarters, people were asking questions the crown had spent two hundred years teaching them not to ask.

"Let me be plain with you, Brother Coram," the queen said. "Three weeks ago your temple marched on my capital under seal of a holy writ I was given no chance to contest. Two days ago your Mother Superior knelt in a hole in the earth before a scribe and a child. You will forgive me if I am not eager to treat with you as equals."

"We don't ask to be equals." Coram's voice stayed level. It was maddening. "We ask to be useful. The temple has libraries, scribes, records going back further than your dynasty. If the world is changing — and it is changing, Majesty, whether either of us wills it or no — someone must write down what is true. We would like to be the ones who write it honestly. That is all."

"That is *not* all," Chancellor Vess began, but the queen raised one finger and he subsided.

She studied Coram for a long moment. A hunter's hesitation, her spymaster had called it, in his report from the road — the warden who caught the fugitives and let them go. She had expected a zealot. Zealots were easy; you gave them an enemy. This man was something worse and better: a believer who had watched his faith crack open and had decided, apparently, to live inside the ruin rather than pretend it still had a roof.

"The temple's lands," she said slowly, "its tithes, its jurisdiction over its own order — these we can confirm. Provisionally. In exchange, the archives open. All of them. To my scholars and to any honest historian the valley sends. And the festival—" she paused, choosing the word carefully, "—the Festival of Stillness becomes a day of mourning. Officially. With the truth of it spoken aloud."

Coram closed his eyes for half a breath. When he opened them, they were wet, and he did not trouble to hide it. "Mother Aldessa asked me to say one thing before we signed anything," he said. "She is confined to the temple infirmary. She will not leave it again by choice. She said: *tell the queen the founders built the seal out of love, and love was the wrong material.* Those are her words. She wanted them entered into the record."

The queen found, to her surprise, that her throat was tight. "Enter it," she said. "All of it."

They signed at dusk. There was no ceremony worth the name — wax, ink, four witnesses, the bells ringing through it all like weather. As Coram rose to go, the queen spoke once more.

"Kavanagh," she said. "The scribe. What does he ask?"

"Nothing," Coram said. "That is what frightens everyone. He asks for paper and a desk and the girl's company. My advisors think he is playing a longer game. I think—" he hesitated, and for a moment he was not a negotiator but a soldier remembering a parley in the dark, "—I think some men spend everything they have and find they're glad of the empty hands. Your majesty would do well not to mistake him for a rival. He isn't one. He may be the only man in this whole affair who isn't."

After he was gone, Vess muttered about sowers of disorder and dangerous precedents. The queen barely heard him. She was looking out at the city, at the small lights kindling in windows that had been dark for centuries, and thinking: *the world rearranging itself, and not one hero left standing in it.* Perhaps that was what peace actually looked like. No one had ever shown her.

* * *

The refectory of the Temple of the Hollow had never been full at dawn before. That was the first thing Coram noticed, walking in with the treaty scroll under his arm: the benches crowded, the brothers and sisters talking — actually talking, voices overlapping, argument and laughter braided together — where for two hundred years there had been the rustle of pages and the disciplined hush of mouths that knew silence was devotion.

It sounded, he thought, like a house whose master had died and whose children had finally started speaking to each other.

He took the head seat and did not want it. The seat had been Aldessa's. Before her, it had belonged to eleven other Mothers and Fathers of the Hollow, and every one of them had sat in it believing the same lie so completely that they had murdered the truth-keepers to protect it. Coram sat down anyway, because someone had to, and because the alternative was a schism, and schisms in a temple built on a buried massacre would end in fire.

"The treaty is signed," he told them. His voice carried without effort; years of shouting across courtyards had trained it. "The valley stands. The borders are gone. We retain our houses, our lands, our order — reformed. From this morning, our mandate changes. We are not wardens of a silence anymore." He unrolled the founders' testament on the table before him, the true text, unscored. "We are keepers of what was buried. Every record opens. Every lie we told gets written down beside the truth of it, in the same hand, on the same page. If anyone cannot serve under that charge, there is a gate. It is unlocked. It has been unlocked since the night the field fell, though I notice none of you have used it."

No one moved toward the gate. Some of them wept. Old Sister Maren, who had taught novices their letters for forty years, laughed — a cracked, astonished sound — and said, "Two hundred years we kept the graves swept and called it holiness."

"We called it what we were taught," Coram said gently. "That is not the same as innocence, but it is not the same as malice either. Go. Open the stacks. Start with the ledgers of the first scribes. Read their names aloud. They have waited long enough."

They went, streaming out into the corridors with something like joy, and Coram remained alone at the long table with the testament and the morning light coming through the high windows — light that felt different now, he realized. Warmer. As though the valley itself had been squinting for two centuries and had only just opened its eyes.

There was one more duty. He had been putting it off for three days, and the scroll under his arm would not let him put it off any longer.

He climbed to the infirmary tower in the late afternoon. Aldessa sat by the window in a plain robe, her white mother's veil folded on the sill like a shed skin. She was watching the gardens, where wildflowers — real ones, seeded from beyond the old border by wind that had never before been permitted to cross it — were already forcing up through the gravel paths. She did not look surprised when he entered. She rarely had.

"You bring the treaty," she said.

"You know that."

"I know everything you'll tell me and much you won't." She turned her face from the window. Her eyes were calm, and that calm frightened him more than any fury could have. "Read me the parts that matter, Coram. I am done pretending I can still read small print."

He read. She listened without interruption until he reached the clause about the festival, and there she closed her eyes and her lips moved silently, as if in prayer, or rehearsal.

"They will hate us," she said at last. "The villages. The families of those who crossed the border stone and were turned back to die outside it. The descendants of the ones who were made examples of. They will hate the temple, and they will be right to."

"Yes."

"Good." She said it flatly, and meant it, and Coram understood in that moment why she had ruled so long and fallen so completely: she had always been willing to pay any price except the one called doubt, and now, having paid it at last, she paid it gladly. "Hatred that knows its reason can be answered. Hatred that doesn't becomes us." She looked at him. "Interim, they're calling you."

"Until the convocation elects properly."

"Don't let them elect you properly. You'd make a fine keeper, Coram, and a terrible idol, and this order has had enough idols." She smiled — a thin, genuine, terrible smile. "Now go. And send me the child's drawings, when you can get copies. The ones Kavanagh kept. I want to see what she saw. I owe her that much curiosity."

At the door he paused. "Aldessa. Do you regret it?"

She considered the question with the seriousness she gave all questions, gazing out at the flowers breaking the gravel apart.

"I regret that mercy was ever a thing that needed building walls around," she said. "The rest is arithmetic. Go."

* * *

Ser Ilsa Vane stood in the antechamber of the royal audience hall wearing borrowed court clothes that fit her like an accusation, and waited to be offered back the life she had thrown away.

She had rehearsed the refusal on the ride from the border. She had rehearsed it again in the guest rooms, again at dinner, again this morning while the chamberlain fussed over her collar. Rehearsal, she had learned in twenty years of soldiery, was how you kept your mouth honest when the moment came.

The herald called her name — her *old* name, her full style, *Ser Ilsa Vane, Knight-Captain of the Order of the Vigilant Dawn, restored to honor by decree of the Crown* — and she walked in under it like a woman walking under a banner she'd burned.

The queen received her alone. Small mercy, or large one; Ilsa hadn't decided.

"Knight-Captain." The queen gestured to a chair. Ilsa remained standing. "You are aware of the contents of the decree."

"I am."

"Your exile is voided. Your rank restored, with seniority. Command of the eastern garrisons, if you want it — and I'm told the eastern marches need steady hands badly, now that every hedge-charm in the kingdom is waking up and half the local lords are frightened of their own shadows." The queen's voice was dry, almost warm. "The court remembers why you were exiled, Ser Ilsa. Refusing an unlawful order is not the stain it was thought to be three months ago. Times change."

"Times change," Ilsa agreed.

"Then take the commission."

"No, Majesty."

Silence. The queen studied her the way she probably studied maps: looking for the flaw in the terrain. "May I ask why?"

Ilsa had carried the answer a long way, through a gate held against temple wardens, through a border that sang, through a city of bells that never stopped reminding her the world had changed shape. She gave it plainly, as she'd practiced.

"Because I was a good soldier for an order that turned out to be rot, and I was an exiled knight for telling the truth about it, and both of those happened because I kept taking commands and calling it conscience." She met the queen's eyes steadily. "If I take the garrison command, within a year I'll be refusing another unlawful order, and within five I'll be the one giving lawful-seeming ones, and I'll believe in them just as hard as Aldessa believed in the seal. I've seen where conviction goes, Majesty. I stood next to it in a hole under the earth while a child burned. I'm done being *given* things to defend. What I have left, I mean to choose."

"And what will you choose?"

"I'm going to the valley," Ilsa said. "There's a scribe there who writes the true history now, and a girl who remembers things the rest of the world forgot, and a temple full of penitents learning to sweep actual floors instead of graves. Someone should stand watch over people like that. Not because anyone ordered it. Because I choose it." She paused, and allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "Also, the scribe can't remember his own childhood, and the child is nine, and neither of them can cook. It's less a garrison than a household. But it's mine."

The queen was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the bells rang the hour, and Ilsa noticed the queen flinch slightly at the sound — a small human motion, quickly mastered, and Ilsa understood suddenly that the throne was lonelier than any exile she had ever known.

"Refused commissions make poor politics," the queen said at last. "But refused *well*, they make good stories, and good stories are the only currency this court has ever truly respected." She rose, extended her hand, and Ilsa — after a heartbeat's hesitation — took it. "Go to your valley, Ser Vane. But hear me. If the world breaks again — and the world, I find, is much more breakable than we believed — I will send for you. Not as your queen. As a woman who needs someone who chooses."

"Then send a letter," Ilsa said, "and not a writ. I'll answer letters."

She was three steps from the doors when the chamberlain hurried in, breathless, a courier's mud still on his boots, and the queen's face did the thing that faces did in this new age when bad news arrived — a tightening around the eyes, a bracing, the look of a ruler who had learned in one season that the impossible was simply the schedule running early.

"Majesty," the chamberlain said, "riders from the western passes. The mountain fastnesses — the old war-cults, the ones the histories said were extinct — they've lit beacon fires. Three of them. And they're sending envoys." He swallowed. "They're demanding the vessel. They say the Unbinding was stolen from them, that the magic belongs to their god, and that they are coming to collect."

Ilsa stood very still in the fine borrowed clothes. Somewhere far to the west, in a green valley where wildflowers were breaking ancient gravel, a scribe was probably setting type, and a girl called Jude was learning to be ordinary, and neither of them knew yet that the war they had ended had heirs.

"Ser Vane," the queen said quietly, without turning. "How soon can you ride?"
