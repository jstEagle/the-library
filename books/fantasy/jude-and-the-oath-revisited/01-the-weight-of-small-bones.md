## 1. The Weight of Small Bones

The dead ferryman's name was Oskit Brann, and he had been dead for three days before anyone in the guildhouse thought to mention it.

Casper Dunmore learned it the way she learned most things—sideways, through a crack in the world. She had been grinding bone meal at her workbench when the smell of river water came through the wall that had no river behind it, and then the voice, low and patient as silt settling: *You're holding me wrong.*

She set down the pestle. On her bench lay the small parcel that had arrived that morning with no sender's mark: a finger bone, a knucklebone, and a sliver of skull wrapped in oilcloth, along with a card bearing a single line in the guild's cipher — *memorial bead, standard rate, expedite.* Someone wanted him remembered, or someone wanted him *handled*, which in the guildhouse of impossible things amounted to much the same service.

"I'm holding you exactly right," Casper said to the empty room, because answering ghosts aloud was a habit she'd never managed to break, and because the collectors on the floor below were arguing loudly enough that no one would hear her talking to herself. They argued about inventory the way other men argued about weather — endlessly, competitively, with no expectation of resolution.

*Too high,* said the ghost of Oskit Brann. *You always cut too high on the first pass. Everyone does. You have to take off less than you think and then less than that.*

Casper looked down at the finger bone in her left hand and the fine saw in her right, and adjusted her grip by a quarter inch. The ghost made a sound that might have been satisfaction, if satisfaction could be conducted through river water.

She worked the way she did everything: quietly, precisely, without any gesture that might draw the eye. This was not modesty. It was architecture. In a building full of people whose trade was the impossible — bottled storms, second chances, the last breaths of kings sold by the vial — the most valuable thing a person could be was forgettable. Casper had spent eleven years becoming so. She knew which floorboards creaked under which collectors' boots; she knew which ledgers were kept in which script and which scripts were read by whom; she knew how to stand in a doorway so that eyes slid past her like water off waxed cloth. Useful and invisible. That was the whole of her strategy for staying alive in a city built on forgetting, and it had served her well enough that she had almost stopped noticing the loneliness underneath it, the way a person stops noticing a stone in their shoe until they take the shoe off.

Below her, the argument crested again.

"—three jars of grief, not two, I counted them myself—"

"You counted them *before* Pellworth's shipment. Two of his were mislabeled sorrow, which is a different tariff entirely—"

"Mislabeled! Or doctored!"

"Doctored grief. Listen to yourself."

Casper tuned them out. The bead required all of her attention, because memorial beads were the guild's most honest product and therefore its least profitable, and because the dead took a personal interest in their own beads in a way the living rarely did.

A memorial bead was carved from the bones of the one remembered — a single bead, drilled and strung, worn by whoever paid for it. The guild's catalogues called them *continuances*. What they actually were was small enough to hold between two fingers and heavy enough to sink a heart. Casper had carved four hundred and six of them in eleven years. She kept no list. The list kept itself, in the part of her mind where she stored things she did not want but could not put down.

*Not there,* said Oskit Brann, as her drill-bit kissed the bone. *Left. My mother wore hers on the left wrist. Everyone on my run did. River folk, we wear our dead on the left, closest to the pulse.*

"Left," Casper murmured, and moved the drill.

He was a good ghost, as ghosts went — undemanding, concrete, interested in craft. Some of them screamed. Some of them recited debts. One had spent nine days narrating, in exhaustive detail, the contents of a house fire he had failed to escape, and Casper had carved his bead with bleeding ears and hands that shook for a week after. But ferryman-ghosts were patient. They had spent their lives waiting on banks for people who were late.

*How long?* he asked now, as the drill bit found its channel. *How long does it take?*

"As long as it takes."

*I mean before someone wears me. Before I'm somewhere instead of nowhere.*

Casper paused, the bit hovering. Through the floor came the collectors' voices, and beneath them the particular hush of the vault levels, where the guild kept the things too dangerous or too sad to display. She thought about the honest answer. The honest answer was that most memorial beads went into drawers within a year, because grief got heavy and the living set it down, and the guild resold them quietly at a discount marked *pre-loved*, a word Casper despised with her entire being.

"Somebody will wear you," she said instead. "I'll see to it."

It was not quite a lie. It was more like a door held open.

* *

The guildhouse of impossible things stood at the crook of Candlewick Street where it bent around the old customs hall, five stories of grey stone with windows that reflected weather from days that hadn't happened yet. Inside, it smelled of tallow, cold iron, and the faint green scent of preserved time. Collectors came and went through its doors with crates and cages and expressions of professional exhaustion. Clerks logged arrivals in ledgers bound in leather that had once been something else. And everywhere, threaded through it all like wire through wax, ran the accounts.

Casper kept the ledgers. Not all of them — no one kept all of them; the guild's books went back further than the guild admitted — but the working volumes, the ones that mattered week to week. Debts in hours. Debts in names. Debts in years taken from one life and credited to another. She wrote in a hand so small and even that auditors had twice accused her of using a printing press, and she balanced columns the way other people prayed.

By mid-afternoon she had finished the bead and taken it down to the front counter, where Master Collector Vess was conducting his daily war with the inventory.

"Three jars," Vess was saying to a junior collector named Ivo, jabbing a finger at a manifest. "The manifest says three."

"The shelf says two."

"Then the shelf is lying, or the jar is walking, and I have personally never met an honest jar of grief in my life."

"Could check the cellar," Ivo offered.

"I am not checking the cellar. The cellar has opinions." Vess turned, saw Casper, and his face performed the small complicated maneuver it always did — recognition arriving a half-second late, followed by relief, followed by the immediate delegation of whatever she represented. "Dunmore. Good. Is that Brann's?"

"Finished." She set the bead on the counter. It was grey-white, shot through with a vein of blue that river water had laid down in the living bone, and it caught the lamplight like a piece of overcast sky.

Vess picked it up, weighed it out of habit, and put it down again. "Payer wants it couriered to the widow on Gull Row. Standard discretion."

"It's done, then." Casper turned to go.

"Dunmore." He said it the way he said everything, as though the word cost him money. "There's a crate came in this morning. No manifest. Unmarked wood, wet through, smells like—" he waved a hand, searching, "—like low tide. Nobody can open it. Pellworth tried a crowbar and the crowbar came back rusted. Ivo here thinks it's cursed. I think it's mislabeled. Either way, it needs logging, and everyone competent is busy, and you—" he paused, and Casper watched him arrive at the sentence he always arrived at, the one that was half compliment and half instruction, "—you're very good at knowing what things are."

Behind him, Ivo mouthed *the cellar has opinions* with the solemnity of a man passing on scripture.

"I'll log it," Casper said.

She collected the crate from the receiving room. It sat alone on a table, dark wood swollen with damp, and up close the smell was worse than low tide — it was low tide plus something older, something like the inside of a sealed room, like air that had been waiting a long time to be breathed and resented the wait. There was no lock. There was no seam. The boards had grown together the way roots grow together, and when Casper pressed her palm flat against the lid, the wood was warm.

And beneath the warmth, faint as a word spoken underwater, she heard humming.

Not the crate. Something inside it. A tune in a minor key, rising and falling like water against pilings, and though Casper had never heard the melody before in her life, some chamber far back in her chest opened its door, and the tune walked straight in, and she knew — the way you know your own name, the way you know a face across a crowded market — that she had heard it before. Long ago. Before she was allowed to remember.

Her hand came off the crate as if the wood had bitten her.

In the ledger, in her small even hand, she wrote: *One crate, unmarked, origin unknown. Contents unverified. Logged pending.* Then, after a moment, because the guild's rules required a best guess and because her hand seemed to move without consulting her:

*Contents possibly: a song.*

Vess would hate that entry. Vess would make her amend it. But the quill had already lifted, and somewhere in the walls — or in her own blood, which amounted to the same address — the humming continued, patient as a tide, waiting for her to remember the words.
