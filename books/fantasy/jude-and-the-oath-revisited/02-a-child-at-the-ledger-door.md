## 2. A Child at the Ledger Door

The rain came down on Grayharbor like a debt being collected, and Casper Dunmore counted it the way she counted everything—patiently, without comment, in the margins of a ledger no one else would ever read.

She was at the front desk that evening because Maren Hale wanted the receiving book updated before the collectors came back from the Saltmarket, and because the front desk was where Casper went when she wanted to be overlooked. The guildhouse of the Ledger and Key had many rooms—the vault with its cold iron shelving, the appraisal room where impossible things were weighed against possible prices, the long gallery of sealed cabinets that hummed faintly if you pressed your ear to them—but the front desk was hers in the way that a shadow belongs to whatever casts it. No one looked for her there. No one looked *at* her there. That was the arrangement she had spent thirty-one years perfecting: useful as a hinge, memorable as one.

Outside, the harbor fog had come up the street thick enough to chew. The lamps along Quay Lane burned halos around themselves. Somewhere a ship groaned against its moorings, and beneath even that—a sound like the sea remembering something.

Casper dipped her pen. The last entry of the day read: *One compass, brass, needle pointing at nothing. Seller wept. Paid double.* She was blotting it when the knock came.

Not a knock, exactly. More like something small and exhausted leaning against the door until the door gave up its argument. The guildhouse door was oak, banded in bronze, older than the guild's charter, and it did not give up arguments easily. But it opened, because the door knew its business, and what stood on the threshold was a child.

Mud-soaked barely covered it. The child was mud the way a candle is wax—all the way through, apparently, from bare black feet to the matted hair plastered flat against a small skull. A girl, Casper thought, though the mud made even that uncertain. Perhaps eight years old. Perhaps younger; hunger edits people downward. Her eyes were the only clean thing about her, wide and dark and fixed on Casper with an expression that wasn't fear exactly. It was recognition. The way you look at a house you've been told about your whole life but never seen.

Behind her, the fog breathed in the street. Nothing else. No mother hurrying up through the rain, no hand pulling her toward shelter. She had arrived the way objects arrive at the guild: unclaimed.

"You shouldn't be here," Casper said, and reached to close the door.

The child spoke.

It was one word, maybe two—a short phrase, wet and cracked, half-swallowed by shivering—and it passed through the gap in the closing door and into Casper's ear and then, horribly, into somewhere behind her ear, some locked room she had not known her skull contained.

*Vellith enna.*

And Casper understood it.

Not translated it. There was no pause, no turning-over, no reaching for meaning across a gap. The words simply *meant*, the way her own name meant, the way the smell of tallow meant evening. *Don't close it.* That was what the child had said. Don't close the door. Please don't close the door.

Casper's hand stopped on the oak.

She had never heard Vellish spoken aloud. She was certain of this the way she was certain of her own ledgers—every line entered, every line accounted for. Her grandmother had sung sometimes, low nonsense syllables over the washbasin, but Grandmother Seraf had died when Casper was six, and whatever language those syllables belonged to had gone into the ground with her, or so Casper had always assumed. The family did not speak of Vell. The family did not speak of much. They were Dunmores, and Dunmores were from nowhere in particular, which in Grayharbor was a kind of wealth.

But her mouth knew the shape of the reply before her mind caught up. She felt her tongue move toward it, felt the old sounds gathering like water finding a channel cut long ago and left dry—

She bit it back. Swallowed the word whole. It went down hard, like a fishbone.

"Inside," she heard herself say instead, in Trade Common, flat and unremarkable. "Quickly."

The child crossed the threshold trailing river-smell and something older underneath it—brine and green stone and, faintly, smoke. Casper shut the door behind her and stood with her palm flat against the oak for a moment longer than necessary, listening to the rain, listening to her own heart doing something irregular and unwelcome.

*Vellith enna.* Don't close it.

Thirty-one years of careful unremarkableness, and one wet phrase had put a crack straight down the middle of her.

* * *

The receiving room was empty at this hour, which was a mercy. Casper sat the child on the appraisal stool—it was the only chair not claimed by a collector's coat—and crouched to look at her properly. Up close the mud resolved into layers: harbor silt, clay, chalk dust, and a fine grey ash that Casper's hands recognized before her head did. Bone dust. Carvers' bone dust. Where had a child been rolling in bone dust?

"I'm going to find you something to eat," Casper said. "Can you tell me your name?"

The child watched her with those dark, unhurried eyes. Then she answered—not in Common, not in the gutter cant of the Saltmarket, not in any of the four languages Casper could place by ear after fifteen years at the ledger desk. She spoke in a stream of soft, rounded syllables that seemed to bend the air differently, vowels like the inside of shells, consonants worn smooth as river stones.

It was the same language. Of course it was. And Casper understood all of it, every word, and understanding it felt less like knowledge than like drowning gently in warm water.

*Ilo,* the child had said. My name is Ilo. Ilo of the—of the—here the words did something Casper had no translation for, a construction that folded back on itself, naming a place by describing its absence. *Ilo of the country that is under the water now.*

The room tilted very slightly. Casper gripped the edge of the desk.

Under the water. Vell. The drowned country. The one her grandmother had hummed about over the washbasin, the one the family walked around the way you walk around a grave in a field you're trying to plow. Casper had grown up knowing only three facts about Vell: that it had fallen, that it had flooded, and that no Dunmore mentioned it. She had assumed the silence was grief. Standing in the receiving room with river-water dripping off a child's hem onto guild floorboards, she understood for the first time that silence could also be a policy.

"Where are your parents?" she asked in Common, and Ilo cocked her head, and Casper realized with a lurch that the question itself might be the cruelty. She tried again, carefully, in the other language—her first attempt clumsy, her second better, her third arriving whole and fluent out of a mouth that had never been taught. *Where is your mother?*

Ilo's face did something terrible then. It didn't crumple. It *settled*, the way a house settles, slowly, into a shape that would hold. *The water took the road,* she said. *I walked the part that was still above it. Then I walked the rest.*

Casper did not ask what *the rest* meant. Some entries you decline to record.

She got bread from the kitchen—stale, but the child fell on it like a gull—and watered wine, and while Ilo ate, Casper stood at the window with her arms wrapped around herself and tried to think like a ledger clerk instead of like whatever she was now. Facts. Enter the facts. An unaccompanied child, found at the guild door, speaking an unplaceable language. Any collector who heard it would log her as contraband curiosity within the hour. The guild bought strange things; that was the whole of its business. A child who spoke a dead language was not a child. She was inventory.

And worse—far worse, in a corner of Casper's mind where a door she'd never noticed was now standing open—worse was the way the language fit in her own mouth. Languages weren't inherited like eye color. You learned them or you didn't. Unless someone had made sure you never learned them, and yet the learning was still there anyway, packed away under the floorboards of you, waiting.

Her grandmother's humming. The washbasin. The low nonsense syllables.

*Nonsense.* Casper pressed her knuckles to her lips. It had never been nonsense. It had been a message in the only safe container available: a song too broken for anyone to arrest. And it had lodged in a six-year-old's memory and waited twenty-five years for a mud-soaked girl to knock on the right door.

*Vellith enna.* Don't close it.

"Casper?"

She turned. Ilo was watching her, bread crumbs on her chin, and she said something in Vellish that Casper understood instantly and completely and wished she didn't:

*You speak like the ones who left. The ones who were allowed to forget.*

The fire popped in the grate. Down the hall, a collector's boots rang on the flagstones, coming closer, and Casper moved without deciding to move—stepped between Ilo and the doorway, arranged her face into its usual nothing, and became, with the ease of long practice, a woman standing near a desk.

Maren Hale filled the frame of the door, shaking rain off her oilskin, her eyes already doing inventory of the room. They snagged on the stool. On the small muddy shape on it. On Casper, positioned just so.

"Hale," Casper said. "Found a stray at the door."

Maren's gaze stayed on the child a moment too long. "Strays don't usually knock," she said. "What's it saying? It's been muttering since the street. Sounds like—" She paused, and Casper watched, from a great calm distance, the chief collector's professional interest kindle. "What language is that?"

Casper opened her mouth to say *I don't know.*

Instead, from the stool, clear as a bell across still water, Ilo spoke again—that soft, shell-voweled stream—and Casper understood every word, and Maren Hale understood none of them, and the child had said:

*Don't tell her. She smells like the men who burned the names.*

Casper kept her face perfectly, professionally blank, and inside her chest, something old and drowned turned over in its sleep and began, very quietly, to wake.
