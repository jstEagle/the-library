## 3. What the Ledgers Bought

The sealed volumes lived on the lowest shelf of the deepest cellar, behind a door that required three keys and a lie.

Casper had all three keys. She had carried them for six years, since old Master Ellery's hands had grown too knotted with gout to turn them, and he'd pressed the ring into her palm with the words, *You're the only one who counts things properly.* It was not a compliment. It was a sentence. And the lie—the one she spoke every time a collector asked what she kept down there—was simple: *Ledgers. Old ones. Nothing worth your trouble.*

Tonight, after the child had been fed and hidden in the bone-cellar above, after Maren Hale's footsteps had faded up the stairs and the guildhouse had settled into its nightly creak of settling timber and dissatisfied ghosts, Casper lit a tallow stub and went down to break her own rule.

She told herself it was diligence. A child arrives speaking an impossible language; a careful keeper of accounts checks the records. That was all. That was professional. Her hands did not shake as she fitted the first key. Her breath did not catch at the second. By the third, she had stopped pretending to herself entirely, and simply turned the lock because she needed to know.

The air beyond the door tasted of dust and tallow and something older—stone that had been wept on. The shelf stretched back farther than the lamplight reached, and the spines were not leather but vellum, actual vellum, scraped skin, and some of them were warm. Casper did not let herself think about whose skin. In this trade you learned early which questions not to ask, and then, if you were honest—and she had always prided herself on a bookkeeper's honesty—you learned that the not-asking was itself a kind of theft. You stole from yourself, mostly. Small pieces. Over years.

*Vell,* said a voice in the dark, and she nearly dropped the lamp.

It was only the ferryman. He'd been dead three weeks and he still hung about her like river damp, correcting her measurements, complaining about the price of pitch. But tonight he wasn't complaining. Tonight his voice came out thin and strange, the way ghosts sounded when they remembered being afraid.

"That's not your business," Casper told him.

"Nothing down here is anyone's business," the ferryman said. "That's how they got so much of it."

She found the volume by touch before she found it by sight: a spine stamped with no title, only a small carved sigil she knew better than she should have—a wave over a closed eye. She had seen it once before, scratched into a stone on her grandmother's mantel, and been told it was a *good-luck charm, girl, don't scratch at it.*

Her fingers knew the shape of it anyway. Her fingers had always known.

* * *

The ledger opened onto paper the color of drowned things, and the handwriting was beautiful. That was the worst part, Casper thought later. That was the thing she could never explain to anyone who hadn't seen it. The worst crimes in the guild's books were recorded in the finest hands, flourished and ruled and balanced to the farthing, because beauty was how the guild forgave itself in advance.

The earliest entries were doors.

*Item: one (1) door, blue-painted, iron-banded, removed intact from the harbor district of Vell-by-the-Sea prior to inundation. Purchased from unnamed seller. Price: 400 crowns and one (1) safe conduct. Condition: sound. Sealing: complete. Notes: still knocks sometimes; waxed the hinges; recommend relocation to deep storage.*

Still knocks sometimes. Casper read the line four times. Somewhere beneath the city—or in whatever deep storage meant when the guild used those words—a blue door stood in the dark, and knocked, and knocked, on nothing.

She turned pages. The entries grew more intimate.

*Item: one (1) name, "Serevan Ondile," purchased from bearer, price agreed. Name burned per contract. Bearer reports subject will not remember himself. Subject confirmed docile within the week.*

*Item: names plural, forty-one (41), village of Lowmarsh-upon-Vell, sold collectively by headman under duress of famine. Erasure executed over nine days. Village now remembers itself as "a fishing hamlet, unremarkable." Recommend no further inquiry; inquiries have caused regressions.*

Nine days to erase a village. Forty-one names. She tried to imagine it—fort-one people waking one morning without themselves, smiling politely at their own children, calling their own houses *that place*—and her mind flinched away from the arithmetic the way a tongue flinches from a broken tooth. But she was a bookkeeper. Arithmetic was her religion. She made herself sit in it.

Forty-one. And the guild had recorded it the way they recorded everything: *condition sound, sealing complete, recommend no further inquiry.*

"You didn't know," the ferryman said behind her. Not a question. A kindness, maybe, or a diagnosis.

"I suspected," Casper said. Her voice came out flat, professional, the voice she used for inventory. "I keep books for people who trade in impossible things. You don't keep honest books for dishonest trade unless you've decided not to look at certain columns."

"And now?"

Now the column had a face on it. Now the column had a child sleeping in her bone-cellar, mud to the knees, speaking a language that predated kingdoms, and asking—not in words, Casper understood that now, the child hadn't asked in words—but asking with her whole small existence: *where did my country go?*

Casper turned another page, and the lamplight found a name, and the name was *Crane.*

* * *

The entry was short. That was its cruelty. Whole villages got paragraphs; her family got four lines.

*Item: one (1) household name, "Crane of Vell," surrendered voluntarily by bearer, one Merren Crane, widow, price waived in consideration of services rendered. Services: testimony regarding the whereabouts of persons listed below. Persons located. Persons erased. Household granted permanent exemption from all future erasures. Condition: invisible. Sealing: permanent.*

Below, in a neat sub-column, eleven names. Neighbors. The fishwife who'd taught her grandmother songs, probably. The man who'd mended nets. Children, likely; there were small marks beside three of the entries that Casper's trained eye read instantly as *minor dependents, no separate fee.*

Voluntarily. Waived. In consideration of services rendered.

Casper sat very still in the cold cellar and felt the floor of her life tilt.

All those years of being unremarkable. All those decades of slipping through the city like water through cracks, unnoticed, unasked-after, safe. She had thought it was temperament. She had built a whole self out of it—the quiet competence, the useful invisibility, the pride of a woman nobody bothered to look at twice. She had believed her anonymity was hers. Earned. Chosen.

It was a contract. It was a *bequest.* Her grandmother had bought it with eleven lives and the guild had honored the purchase down four generations like any other perpetual clause. *Permanent exemption from all future erasures.* She was not forgettable. She was *paid for.*

And the child upstairs—the child whose language rose off her tongue like smoke from a drowned fire—that child was walking contraband in a house that had made its fortune making sure no one could ever go home to Vell again. No wonder the collectors couldn't place the language. They weren't ignorant. They were *warded*, the way the ledgers were warded, the way the whole city was warded against remembering. The forgetting hadn't been an accident of history. It had been inventory management.

"Merren," Casper said aloud, testing her grandmother's name like a coin bitten for gold. It rang false. It had always rung false; she'd just never had a scale to weigh it on.

The candle guttered. Down the long shelf, somewhere past the reach of light, something knocked—slow, patient, three times and a pause, three times and a pause. The blue door, or another like it. Or a hand.

The ferryman's ghost drifted close enough that she felt him the way you feel a change in weather. "What will you do?" he asked.

"What I always do," Casper said. "Count."

But her hands, when she copied the entry into her private memorandum—because she was who she was, because the truth deserved a second ledger even if it could never survive in the first—her hands shook so badly the numbers came out crooked.

Above her, through the floors, faint as a pulse through a wall, she heard the child singing in Vellish. And beneath her, deeper than the cellar, deeper than the river's pilings, something answered.

Footsteps were crossing the floor above. Measured ones. Unhurried ones. The particular tread of Maren Hale coming down toward a door that had no reason to be unlocked.
