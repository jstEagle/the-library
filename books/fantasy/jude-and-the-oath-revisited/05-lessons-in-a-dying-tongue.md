## 5. Lessons in a Dying Tongue

The bone-cellar smelled of tallow and cold stone, and Casper had always found it the most honest room in the guildhouse. Everything here had been someone once. Ribs stacked in crates like kindling. Skulls sorted by size, by age, by the guild's cold arithmetic of worth. She had spent eleven years among these shelves, carving names into small bones for the families who could afford remembrance, and she had never once thought of the cellar as anything but a workshop.

Now it was a hiding place. Now it was a school.

"Again," Ilo said, and the word came out in Vellish—*senna*, *again*—with the particular stubbornness of a child who has decided that a thing will be done properly.

Casper drew her burin across the sliver of rib bone. She was teaching Ilo the old way of writing, the carved way, because paper burned and ink faded but bone held its mark for centuries if you asked it kindly enough. The character was *thel*—*river*—a single downward stroke branching into three, like water finding its way to the sea.

"*Thel,*" she said aloud, and the syllable felt strange and sweet in her mouth, like fruit from a country she'd been told didn't exist.

"*Thel,*" Ilo repeated, then laughed—a bright, startling sound in the dimness—and pointed at the ceiling, where somewhere above them the drains of the city carried the river's voice. "*Thel vashen. The river is angry.*"

"I know what the river is angry about," Casper murmured, and set down the burin.

She knew more than she wanted to. That was the trouble with these lessons: every word Ilo taught her unlocked another door in her own head, and behind each door stood something she could not account for. How did she know that *vell* meant both *home* and *the place you cannot return to*? How did she know the lullaby whose melody kept surfacing in her thoughts like a body rising in still water? Her grandmother had died when Casper was six, taking her secrets with her—or so Casper had believed. Now it seemed the old woman had left them everywhere instead, sewn into the lining of the family's silence, waiting for a child speaking mud-and-river Vellish to come along and pull the threads loose.

Ilo sat cross-legged on an upturned crate, wrapped in Casper's spare coat, looking smaller than a child should look after three days underground. Mud still darkened the creases of her ears; Casper had offered water for washing and the girl had refused it. "River gave me this skin," she'd said. "It'll take it back when it wants." Old words. Older than kingdoms. Casper wrote that one down too, in the ledger of things she couldn't explain.

"Your turn," Ilo said now, switching to the trade-tongue with the ease of a child raised between languages. "Teach me a word of yours."

"I don't have words of mine. I have inventory."

"Liar." Ilo grinned. "You have a hundred words for bone and not one for *missing*. I've been counting."

Casper looked at her—at this scrap of drowned country sitting in a cellar full of dead people's parts, grinning as though the world were a game she intended to win—and felt something crack gently inside her chest. Tenderness, maybe. Or terror wearing tenderness's coat. Above them, footsteps crossed the floor of the counting-room, and they both went silent, listening, until the steps faded toward the street.

"Maren Hale," Casper said quietly. "Chief collector. She comes to check on me."

"You said she thinks I'm contraband."

"She thinks you're *memory*. Which is worse." Casper picked up the burin again. "Memory is the only cargo the guild can't price. It drives them mad."

"Then let's drive them mad," Ilo said, "properly. Sing something."

"I don't sing."

"Everyone sings. Dead people especially. You'd know that better than me—you talk to them all day." Ilo leaned forward, suddenly serious in the way children are serious, without apology or warning. "You do. I hear you talking to nobody in your workroom. And the nobodies talk back."

*

The dead had been getting louder since Ilo arrived.

Casper noticed it first as a hum under the floorboards, a vibration she mistook for the pipes. Then the ferryman's ghost—the same one whose bead she'd carved a week ago, the one who corrected her measurements—began standing at the top of the cellar stairs every night, watching them through the gap in the door with eyes like wet slate. He didn't speak. He *listened*. And when Ilo sang, his mouth moved.

"He knows it," Ilo whispered one evening, mid-song. She had stopped singing and was staring at the doorway. "That song. He knows it. He's singing it wrong, but he knows it."

"That song is older than him. It's older than everyone here."

"It's not about age." Ilo hugged her knees. "It's about hunger. You can starve on bread if the bread isn't yours. They've been starving a long time, haven't they? Your ghosts?"

Casper didn't answer. She thought of the skulls on their shelves, sorted and priced and nameless. She thought of the ledgers upstairs, which she had begun reading again after midnight—reading the way you probe a wound to learn how deep it goes. Whole villages bought and struck. Names purchased and burned. A language, itemized.

"They have," she said finally. "And I think we're feeding them."

"Good." Ilo's chin lifted. "Let them be hungry. Hungry things remember why they're hungry."

That night Casper taught her the carving for *mother*—*ama*, a curve like two arms—and Ilo taught her the second verse of the lullaby, the one about the river keeping count of everything it takes and giving back nothing but songs. And from the stairwell, faint as breath on glass, the ferryman's ghost hummed along, and beneath his voice Casper heard others gathering, dozens of them, hundreds perhaps, rising up through the foundations of the guildhouse like water seeking a crack.

They did not say *thank you*. The dead rarely did. But they pressed close to the door all night, and the cellar stayed warm, warmer than any fire could make it, and in the morning the skull-shelves were dusted clean, every one, as if by hands that had nothing else to give.

*

On the fourth day, Ilo had the nightmare.

Casper woke to the girl thrashing on her pallet, crying out in Vellish—words tumbling too fast to parse, except the last ones, which repeated: "*Ama. Ama. Thel vashen. Ama.*" Mother. Mother. The river is angry. Mother.

Casper gathered her up and held her, rocking, the way her grandmother had rocked her once in a house that no longer appeared in any record. "You're safe," she said in Vellish, clumsily, and the clumsiness made it truer somehow. "You're under the ground and the ground is holding you."

"The water took her." Ilo's fists twisted in Casper's shirt. "The collectors came and she put me in the reeds and the water took her and I *ran*, I ran the way she told me, I ran and ran and the city ate itself around me until I found your door—" She stopped. Went rigid. "Your door. Why does your guild have doors, Casper? Doors aren't things. Doors aren't *cargo*."

The question hung there, and Casper, who had lied beautifully to Maren Hale and lied adequately to herself, could not lie to this.

"Because a door is how you go home," she said. "And the guild sells homes to people who can never use them. It's crueler than burning. Burning leaves ashes. This leaves *wanting*."

Ilo pulled back and looked at her with eyes far older than eight years old. "Then you'll open one."

It wasn't a question either. Children of drowned countries didn't ask; they stated the shape of the world as it ought to be and waited for adults to catch up.

"I don't know how."

"Yes, you do." Ilo touched Casper's sternum, right over the heart. "You keep saying you don't remember. But you hum in your sleep. In *Vellish*. Nobody hums a language they don't have." She lay back down, pulling the coat over her shoulders, conversation finished. "Teach me a new word tomorrow. Something strong. Something with teeth."

Casper sat in the dark a long while, listening to the child's breathing slow, listening to the dead settle against the cellar door like cats against a warm wall.

*

It was nearly dawn when she climbed to her workroom and found the message waiting on her desk, folded and sealed with the guild's grey wax.

She recognized Maren Hale's hand before she broke the seal—neat, economical, a hand that wasted nothing. One line:

*The inventory count is short one child's weight. Come to my office before the bells. Bring nothing you want to keep.*

Below the stairs, the humming stopped.

Every ghost in the guildhouse went quiet at once, the silence rushing up through the floors like a tide withdrawing, and Casper understood that they had heard it too—that whatever came next, the dead already knew the shape of it, and they were holding their breath on her behalf.

She tucked the note into her apron, checked that Ilo's pallet was hidden behind the shelving, and started up toward the counting-room, where the chief collector waited with a smile like a locked drawer.
