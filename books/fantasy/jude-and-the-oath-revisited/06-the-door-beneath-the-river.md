## 6. The Door Beneath the River

The ledger gave up its secret the way old men give up confessions: reluctantly, and only at night, when no one was watching.

Casper sat in the bone-cellar with Ilo asleep against her shoulder, the child's breath slow and salt-sweet, and turned the pages of the oldest volume by the light of a single tallow stub. The paper was vellum—of course it was vellum, everything in this guild was made of something that had once been alive—and the ink had gone the brown of dried blood. Most of the entries were dull as any ledger's: sums, weights, dates in a calendar nobody used anymore. But in the margin of the forty-first leaf, written small and slanted, as though the hand had been ashamed even of its own shorthand, someone had noted a delivery.

*One (1) door, riverine, sealed. Placed beneath the pilings at Greywater Stair. Ward set per contract. Key: truth.*

*Key: truth.*

She had read those two words a dozen times before they stopped looking like nonsense. A door warded with a question. She thought of the ferryman then—Old Bramm, whose memorial bead she had carved three weeks ago, whose ghost still drifted through the guildhouse correcting her measurements with the fussy precision of a man who had spent fifty years reading water—and she thought of what he had murmured to her just last evening, standing at her elbow while she pretended to inventory whalebone.

*You're looking for the low stair,* he'd said, in the toneless way ghosts speak, as if reciting from a book they can't put down. *Under the pilings where the ice never quite goes. My father poled barges there when the river had another name. Ask me again tomorrow. I like being asked.*

Ghosts liked being asked. It was nearly all they had left.

Casper closed the ledger, eased Ilo onto the pallet of wood-shavings she'd made between the shelving, and tucked the child's carved word-beads into a pouch at her hip. Eleven beads now. *Water. Door. Home. Mother.* Words Ilo could hold in her fist when sleep went bad. Then she banked the tallow, took her coat and her knife, and climbed the cellar stairs into the grey throat of the morning.

*

Greywater Stair was not on any map worth trusting. It lay at the end of Rope-Walk Lane, where the city's ambition ran out of paving stones and gave itself over to mud and mooring-posts, and it descended in eleven steps—the eleventh always drowned—to a landing so old that the guild's own surveyors had listed it as *structure, pre-founding, provenance unknown*. Casper had walked past it a hundred times in nine years of unremarkable life and never once looked at it properly. That, she understood now, was the whole trick of this place. The guild didn't hide things behind locks. It hid them inside the ordinary, where eyes slid off like rain off oiled cloth.

The river ran high and brown-green with snowmelt, pushing its shoulder against the pilings. Above, gulls argued over something dead. The fog came off the water in slow ropes, and Casper stood at the top of the stair and let herself be cold for a moment, gathering courage the way other people gathered kindling—one piece at a time, and never enough.

*Bramm,* she said silently. *I'm asking.*

The fog thickened. The temperature dropped the particular few degrees that meant the dead were near, and then Old Bramm was beside her—or the shape of him, translucent as breath on glass, his hands still curled around a pole that wasn't there. He looked down the stair with an expression she'd never managed to carve into bone: longing without hope, which is the hardest thing there is.

*Eleventh step,* he said. *But you knew that. You always were one for knowing.*

"Tell me how," she whispered aloud, because speaking aloud made it real, and because Ilo wasn't here to hear.

*My father poled barges under the city before the founding,* Bramm said, patient as tide. *He said there was a country on the other side of the water once, and the water kept the door of it, and the door kept the name. When the contracts came—when your people sold the names—the door was warded shut. Not locked. Warded. There's a difference.* He turned his faded face toward her. *A lock keeps out strangers. A ward keeps out liars. Or rather—it keeps out those who cannot pay the toll, and the toll is the same coin every time.*

"Truth."

*You've been reading the ledgers.* Something moved across his face that might have been a smile. *Good. Dead men hate being the only ones who know things.*

She descended the stairs. Ten dry steps, and then the eleventh, black and streaming, half a hand below the brown water. She crouched at the edge and looked down through the murk, past her own distorted reflection—a thin woman, unremarkable, useful, invisible—and saw it.

The door was set into the stone beneath the landing, angled upward like a coffin lid, framed in timber gone black with centuries but sound as the day it was hung. Carvings covered every surface: interlocking waves, a tree with roots above and branches below, letters in a script that made her chest ache before her mind could read it. Because she *could* read it. That was the horror and the gift of it. Vellish rose in her like water in a well, unasked for, undiluted, as if the language had been waiting all these years in the sealed rooms of her blood.

*THAT WHICH WAS DROWNED IS NOT LOST,* said the lintel. *IT ONLY WAITS TO BE NAMED.*

And beneath the ring-pull, smaller, almost humble:

*SPEAK WHO YOU ARE, AND ENTER.*

Casper knelt on the wet stone and put her hand flat against the door. It was warm. Everything else in this city was cold, and the door was warm as a sleeping animal, and she felt it notice her the way a dog notices a familiar footstep several streets away.

"Well," she said softly. "Hello."

The warmth pulsed. And then the voice came—not from the door exactly, but from the stone around it, from the river, from somewhere older than either—in Vellish, round and deep and patient as a grandmother asking after your supper:

*Who are you?*

It should have been easy. Four syllables' work. She opened her mouth.

Nothing came.

Not because she didn't know the words. Because the moment she reached for them, she felt the lie coiled underneath every possible answer, the way you feel a rotten plank before you step on it. *I am Casper Dunmore, bone-carver, third of her name.* True enough—but Dunmore was a bought name, a shell name, purchased along with anonymity by people who wanted to stop being who they were. Her family hadn't merely survived Vell's fall. They had *purchased their survival*, and what did that make her? A woman built on a transaction? A ledger entry wearing a coat?

*I am a descendant of Vell.* Also true. But the ward would taste it and find it hollow, because she had never once spoken the language until a child arrived carrying it like a wounded bird. She had let it starve in her for thirty years. What kind of daughter of anything does that?

*I am no one.* The truest answer of all, and therefore the worst. Because *no one* was precisely what her ancestors had paid for. No one could not open doors. No one had nothing to say when asked who they were. The ward was not barring her out of malice; it was barring her out of arithmetic. It required a self, and she had spent her whole life spending hers down to nothing—being useful, being quiet, being invisible—until the account stood empty.

*Who are you?* the door asked again, gently, the way you ask someone weeping.

"I—" Casper's voice cracked on the fog. "I don't know."

The warmth dimmed. Not angrily. Almost sorrowfully, the way a lamp dims when the oil runs low. Through the grain of the ancient wood she glimpsed, or dreamed she glimpsed, the other side: green country, terraced fields going down to water, lights in windows of houses that no longer existed anywhere except here, behind a door under a stair, waiting.

Then it closed like an eye, and she was kneeling alone on wet stone with the gulls screaming overhead and her palms bleeding a little where she'd pressed them too hard against the frame.

Bramm's ghost stood at the top of the stairs, watching her with terrible kindness.

*Told you,* he said. *Toll's the same coin every time.*

"I don't have it." She heard her own voice go small. "I spent it. They spent it for me, before I was born, and I've been keeping the balance at zero my whole life because zero is safe. Zero doesn't get collected." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "I'm the best liar in this city, Bramm. I lie by existing. I've been lying since the day I was born, and I didn't even know it was a lie."

*No,* said the ferryman, and his voice had an odd weight to it, the weight of a message he'd carried a long way and was finally allowed to set down. *That's not what the ward asks. Listen closer. It doesn't ask who you pretend to be. It doesn't ask who they made you. It asks who you are.* His translucent hands tightened on the pole that wasn't there. *I poled the river fifty years. I know what's under a boat and what's under a lie. Under both, it's the same thing—just water, just truth, holding everything up. You don't have to be clean to cross, girl. You have to be honest. Even about the dirt.*

Casper looked back at the dark seam of the door, warm and silent and waiting.

Even about the dirt. She thought of the sealed ledgers upstairs, the burned names, the page she had found with her grandmother's name on it—*Dunmore, purchased; original name struck per contract*—and the confession she hadn't yet been able to make herself finish reading. Whatever her grandmother had done to earn that erasure, it was down there in the ink, waiting, and the door wanted all of it. Every coin. Truth weighed honestly, not laundered.

She stood. Her knees ached. Somewhere upstream a bell rang the hour, indifferent as arithmetic.

"Tomorrow," she told the door, and the word felt like both promise and cowardice. "I'll come back tomorrow, and I'll bring the whole ledger. All of it. Even the pages I can't stand."

The door did not answer. But the warmth under her palm lingered a heartbeat longer than it needed to, and Casper chose—deliberately, the way you choose to keep breathing—to believe that was forgiveness, or at least patience.

She climbed the stairs. Behind her, under the brown water, something that was not quite a voice hummed four notes of a song she almost remembered, and did not turn around to hear the rest, because turning around would have meant admitting she was already crying.

At the top of Greywater Stair, Maren Hale was waiting in the fog with her collectors' badges hidden and her smile not, and she said, pleasantly, terribly:

"Casper. We need to talk about the child."
