## 7. Interrogation of the Unremarkable

Maren Hale had spent thirty years learning to read people the way Casper Dunmore read bone, and she had never once been wrong about a liar.

Liars were loud. Liars overfilled their silences, decorated them with too many details, blinked at the wrong moments. Casper Dunmore did none of these things. That was what troubled Maren most as she climbed the guildhouse stairs to the bone-carver's workroom, the evidence folded in oilcloth against her ribs like a knife she hadn't decided to draw.

Casper had lied to her. Three days ago, standing in this same corridor smelling of tallow and river damp, she had said — calmly, usefully, with that terrible helpfulness Maren had relied on for a decade — *"I haven't seen the child since the night she came."*

And last night, one of the cellar ghosts had sung.

The singing was what had undone everything. Ghosts in the guildhouse hummed, muttered, recited debts and dying words, but they did not *sing*, not together, not in rounds, not in a language old enough to predate the city's founding stones. The night-watch had heard it through the cellar grate and come to Maren pale as milk, saying the dead below were singing in a tongue that made his teeth ache, and Maren had gone down herself and listened, and understood two things at once: the child was still in the building, and the carver was teaching her something.

Now she stood outside Casper's door, listening to the small industrious sounds within — the rasp of file on bone, the tap-tap of a mallet no bigger than an egg. Unremarkable sounds. Casper Dunmore was very good at unremarkable sounds. It was, Maren thought, the woman's single greatest talent, and it had taken Maren far too long to recognize that such talent was itself remarkable.

She knocked once and entered without waiting.

* * *

The workroom was exactly as always: bone shavings on the floor like pale snow, memorial beads strung in rows along the wall, each one tagged with a ledger number. Casper sat at her bench with her back half-turned, finishing a bead, and she did not look up right away. That was new. Casper always looked up. Casper always watched the door the way a debt-collector watches a debtor.

"Maren," she said, setting down her tools. "I have the ferryman's bead nearly finished. His ghost is particular about the grain."

"I know about his ghost."

"Of course you do."

Maren crossed the room slowly, letting her boots announce her, and laid the oilcloth bundle on the bench between them. She unwrapped it with the ceremony the moment deserved: a candle-end, burnt to a specific height; a wooden whistle, river-reed, child-sized; and a scrap of parchment bearing a line of script that curled like waterweed.

"Candle stub from the cellar stair," Maren said. "Burned three nights running, though the cellar has lamps. A whistle carved from reed that doesn't grow within forty miles of this city — it grows, if my sources are correct, on the drowned coast of Vell, where the marshes took the orchards. And this—" she touched the parchment without picking it up "—is a copy of a page from Volume Nine of the sealed accounts. The volume you signed out eleven days ago under inventory pretext, and which you returned four hours late."

Casper said nothing. Her hands lay flat on the bench, still as carved things themselves. Maren had interrogated smugglers, forgers, a necromancer who'd tried to sell the same grandmother twice, and none of them had ever frightened her the way this silence did — because Casper's silences were honest, and honest silence meant she wasn't going to bother denying any of it.

"You lied to me," Maren said. "You told me you hadn't seen the child."

"Yes."

No excuse. No trembling. Just the word, set down like a bead on the bench between them. Maren found she had prepared six speeches and every one of them now fit badly.

"Do you know what I do to people who lie to me?"

"You promote some of them," Casper said. "The useful ones."

Despite herself — despite everything — Maren laughed. It came out short and hard, a bark in the quiet room. "Yes," she admitted. "That's true. That has been true." She pulled the stool from the corner and sat, which changed the shape of the conversation; standing, she was an officer of the guild. Sitting, she was only a tired woman of fifty-three whose knees ached and who had spent her life carrying other people's impossible things up and down stairs. "Sit down, Casper. This isn't an interrogation. Well. It is. But not the kind you're braced for."

Casper sat.

* * *

"I'm going to tell you why the guild exists," Maren said, "and then I'm going to make you an offer, and you're going to hate yourself for how long you consider it. That's fair warning. I consider myself a fair woman."

"The guild trades in impossible things."

"The guild trades in things the world can't afford to remember." Maren leaned forward. "Do you think we seal away doors and names out of malice? Out of greed? Child, I've been chief collector for nineteen years, and in nineteen years I have learned one law: memory kills. Not metaphorically. A country remembers a border and ten thousand men die. A family remembers a feud and the blood goes on for generations. A *language* remembers itself, and suddenly there are people who want their country back, and their country is at the bottom of a river, and they will drown cities trying to raise it." She tapped the parchment. "Vell didn't fall because the sea rose. The sea rose because Vell remembered something the rest of the world had paid us to make it forget. We are not villains, Casper. We are the dam."

"And the child?"

"Is a crack in the dam." Maren let that sit. "A living speaker of a dead tongue, walking around loose in a city full of ghosts who used to speak it. You've heard the singing. So have I. Every ghost in that cellar is pressing toward her like moths at a lamp, and moths burn, Casper, and when the burning starts it won't stop with the cellar. There are collectors in three countries who will pay more for that child than this guildhouse is worth, and there are worse buyers than collectors. What we do with her is cruel. What happens otherwise is a massacre. I've seen the difference. I prefer cruel."

Casper's eyes — gray, steady, infuriatingly unreadable — held hers. "What's the offer?"

* * *

Maren drew a slim black book from her coat. It was not a guild volume; it was older, bound in leather gone soft as cloth, and its pages were blank except for one.

"This is the Book of Given Names. Before your time. Before mine, actually — it hasn't been opened in sixty years, because no one has earned it." She turned it so Casper could see. On the open page, written in faded iron-gall ink, was a single entry: *Given: the name Aldous Merrin. Received: silence, in perpetuity.*

"Aldous Merrin was a guild archivist who stole a sealed door-key and tried to reopen a country called Sarnthat, which burned itself rather than be forgotten properly. He was caught. And instead of killing him — we don't kill, Casper, whatever you've imagined — we gave him a name. A new one. Clean, unremarkable, belonging to no history and no debt. He walked out of this building a different man, and he lived forty more years as a fishmonger in Lowmarsh, and when he died, nobody came to the funeral, and it was the gentlest death I have ever catalogued." She closed the book gently, reverently. "True anonymity. Not the anonymity of being ignored — the anonymity of being *finished*. No past reaching for you. No ledgers with your name in them. No grandmother's crimes sitting in your bones. A long life, safe, quiet, empty as fresh snow."

She watched Casper's face as she said it, and she saw it land — saw the exact moment the offer stopped being words and became a picture. She knew that picture intimately; she had been shown her own version of it once, thirty years ago, when she was a young collector who had grown too curious about a sealed crate. She had considered it for three days. She still sometimes considered it, on bad nights, and hated herself a little less each year for how much she wanted it.

"All you do," Maren said softly, "is bring the girl up from the cellar. Tonight. Before the singing spreads above stairs and the senior partners take an interest — and Casper, if the senior partners take an interest, there will be no bargain and no mercy, only procedure. Bring her up, hand her to me, sign nothing, say nothing. By morning you'll have a new name, and by winter you won't even remember wanting the old one. That's not rhetoric. The name-change works. It's the only mercy this guild has ever perfected."

Silence. The file-lamps hissed. Somewhere below, faintly, impossibly, a ghost began humming a tune with a shape like water, and Maren saw Casper's hands curl slowly closed on the bench.

"There it is," Casper said quietly. "That's what my grandmother must have felt."

The words struck Maren sideways. "What did you find in Volume Nine?"

"Her signature." Casper looked up, and for the first time in ten years of knowing her, Maren saw her plainly — saw the loneliness under the competence, the grief under the calm, the whole architecture of a woman built to be overlooked. "Page after page. Neighbors sold for erasure. Doors bought and bricked. And her name at the bottom, trading everyone she loved for the privilege of becoming nobody." A pause. "It worked, Maren. Nobody noticed us for three generations. Isn't that the definition of success?"

"It is," Maren said, and hated how gently her voice came out. "And it can work for you. One signature. One child. Snow, Casper. Clean snow, all the way down."

For a long moment the carver said nothing, and Maren let her have the silence, because she knew — she had always known — that this was the moment the whole trade turned on. Everyone wanted to be free of their name. Everyone wanted the debt forgiven. The offer was almost irresistible; it was designed to be; it had been designed, generations ago, by people who understood that the cruelest bargains are the ones the victim would make gladly.

Then Casper stood, and picked up the ferryman's unfinished bead, and turned it in her fingers.

"When Aldous Merrin died," she said, "did anyone carve him a bead? Did anyone mark the shelf where his name used to be?"

"No."

"That's the offer, then." She set the bead down. "Not a new name. No name at all. A funeral with no mourners and no marker." She met Maren's eyes, and Maren saw that she had already chosen — had perhaps chosen the night the child first spoke at the ledger door — but was taking one more breath of the clean snow before walking out of it. "Give me until dawn, Maren. Then come to the cellar, and I'll show you what I've decided."

Maren rose, knees aching, and gathered the evidence into its cloth. At the door she paused, because thirty years of reading liars demanded a final look back — and what she saw was not a liar at all, but a woman already halfway to somewhere Maren couldn't follow, holding a candle stub in her pocket and a question she intended to answer honestly.

"Dawn," Maren said. "Not a minute past."

Below them, faint through the floorboards, the dead of the guildhouse had begun to sing again — and this time, Maren realized with a collector's cold dread, they were singing a word she recognized.

They were singing Casper's name.
