## 8. Grandmother's Hand

The burned-name contracts lived in the deepest drawer of the ledger vault, in a box made of wood that had been treated with something that refused to remember it had ever been a tree. Casper had handled the box a hundred times in her years of keeping the guild's accounts—had carried it, dusted it, catalogued its contents by number and never once by reading them. That had been the arrangement. That had been the whole quiet architecture of her survival: she kept the ledgers of impossible things, and the impossible things kept themselves.

But tonight she unlocked the drawer with Ilo's word still warm in her mouth—*hjem*, home, the syllable falling open like a door—and tonight the box felt less like furniture and more like a mouth.

There were forty-one contracts inside. She knew the number because she had written it herself on the inventory slip eleven years ago, in her small, unremarkable hand, the hand of a woman who was useful and invisible and therefore safe. Each contract was folded three times and sealed with grey wax that smelled faintly of river water. Each bore a number instead of a name. *Contract 9.* *Contract 22.* And where a name should have been signed at the foot of each page there was only a scorch—a neat, deliberate burn, round as a coin, as though someone had pressed out the letters with the bowl of a spoon while the ink was still wet.

Casper laid them out on the vault table beneath the lamp and began, for the first time, to read.

*Item: one door, oak, iron-banded, recovered from the Vellish quarter before demolition. Purchased from unnamed vendor. Condition: sealed. Buyer's instruction: no resale, no viewing, no record of origin.*

*Item: one name, female, adult, belonging to a resident of the Salt Row tenements. Name surrendered willingly. Compensation: passage, papers, silence.*

*Item: names, plural, seventeen in number, belonging to the congregation of the Tide Chapel. Vendor: one (1) individual, identity withheld under clause six. Consideration rendered in full. All records of the above persons to be expunged from civic registries, parish books, and common memory. The guild guarantees forgetting.*

She stopped. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above her, through the floorboards, the collectors were arguing about an inventory of bottled weather, their voices muffled and ordinary, and the ordinariness of it made her stomach turn over.

Clause six. She turned back through the pages until she found the reference. *Vendor identity to be withheld in perpetuity; vendor's descendants to be held blameless; vendor's lineage to receive erasure gratis, all fees waived, as consideration for services rendered.*

Services rendered. She read the phrase four times. Her eyes kept snagging on it the way a thumb snags on a splinter.

The seventeenth contract in the stack was thinner than the others, older, the paper gone the colour of old teeth. The wax seal had been broken long ago and resealed badly, hurriedly, by someone whose hands were shaking. When Casper unfolded it, a pressed flower fell out—a sprig of sea-lavender, flattened to a ghost of itself, still holding, after all these years, a thread of salt smell. Vellish flower. She knew it without knowing how she knew it, the way you know your own heartbeat.

The contract was not for a door or a name. It was a confession, written in a hand Casper recognized down to its smallest habit—the way the ascenders leaned left, the way every *r* ended in a small defiant hook. She had seen that handwriting on recipe cards. On the flyleaf of a songbook. On the note that had been tucked into Casper's first pair of work gloves when she was nine years old: *Measure twice, little wren. Cut once. Be exact, and no one will look twice at you.*

Her grandmother's hand.

*I, Maren Crane née Vell-something-that-was-burned-out, do declare under seal what follows,* the page began, and then the burn mark took the name itself, a perfect round hole punched clean through the paper, so that even the confession could not say who had confessed.

*We did not drown with the rest of them. We were offered a way out, and we took it, and the price was this: we told the collectors where our neighbours slept.*

Casper sat down. The stool scraped. She did not hear it.

*The guild came to the quarter in the autumn of the fall, buying up everything—doors first, then names, then the tongue itself, piece by piece, because a country cannot be drowned properly while anyone can still say its name. Most people refused. Pride, they called it. I called it love. But I had a daughter, and my daughter had a daughter coming, and love is a heavy thing to carry into winter.*

*So I went to the guildhouse on the fourth night of the frost and gave them the Salt Row. I gave them the Tide Chapel congregation, all seventeen, because I knew where each of them would be sleeping on the night the collectors came. I gave them Old Sef and his ferry ledger. I gave them the widow Aune, who had fed me through two bad winters. They wrote it all down in a book I was not allowed to see. Then they took my name out of my mouth like a fishhook and told me I would live.*

*They were true to their word. I have lived. That is the punishment no one warned me of.*

Below that, the writing grew smaller, cramped, as if the page itself were trying to shrink away from what it carried:

*To whoever reads this—if anyone reads this—I do not ask forgiveness. Forgiveness is a door and I am not standing at it. I ask only that you know. We were not unlucky. We were not spared. We bought our lives with other people's deaths and the guild kept the receipt, because the guild keeps everything, because forgetting is not free. Nothing here is free. Check the ledgers. It is all written down somewhere, always. That is the only mercy left in this city: everything is written down somewhere.*

At the very bottom, half-hidden by the burn hole that had eaten her name, was a single line in a different ink, added later, the letters shakier:

*The child is born. I named her nothing. Forgive me. I named her nothing so that nothing would come looking for her.*

*Casper*, her grandmother had named her. Nothing, dressed up in three syllables.

The lamp flame bent sideways. There was no draught in the vault; the air was sealed as carefully as any contract. But the flame bent, and the salt-smell of the lavender thickened until the whole room tasted of tide-flat and cold morning, and Casper understood, with the calm horror of someone watching ice crack across a pond in slow motion, that she was no longer alone.

"You read it, then."

The voice came from behind her left shoulder, dry and low, the voice of a woman who had spent sixty years speaking quietly enough that no one would look twice. Casper did not turn around. Turning around would make it real, and some stubborn animal part of her wanted one more moment in which her grandmother was only a memory of flour-dusted hands and humming in the kitchen.

"You always said the dead don't come to this city," Casper said. "You said they couldn't find their way. You said that's why it was safe."

"I said a great many things." The ghost came around into the lamplight slowly, the way fog comes off water. She looked as she had at the end—small, upright, her white hair pinned with a bone comb—but she also looked, impossibly, younger at the edges, as if death had begun returning to her everything that erasure had taken. Around her shoulders hung a shawl worked in patterns Casper had never seen and yet recognized instantly: wave-knots, tide-marks, the stitched language of a coast that no longer existed. "I said them because they were true, and then the world changed its mind."

"The ghosts," Casper said. "In the cellar. They've been restless for weeks. Humming songs I didn't know I knew. Because of the child."

"Because of the child," her grandmother agreed. "And because of you. You've been carrying our tongue in your mouth your whole life like a stone in a shoe, girl, walking careful around it, pretending it isn't there. Words leak. Even unspoken ones. Especially those." She looked down at the contracts spread across the table, at the neat round burns, and something moved through her face that was too complicated to be grief alone. "I used to sit where you're sitting. Did you know that? Before they gave me my new name and sent me north. I sat in this chair and I watched them burn my name out of that page, and I thought: good. Let it hurt going. At least it will be gone."

"It wasn't gone." Casper's voice came out strange, flat, distant. "It's right here. Forty-one times over, it's right here. You're in these books, Gran. You've been in these books the whole time. Every year I dusted this box. Every year I numbered these pages." She heard her own voice rise and hated it. "You let me keep them. You knew I'd take this post, you recommended me for it—did you? Did you want me to find this?"

"I wanted you safe." The ghost's hands twisted together, an old woman's gesture, worn smooth by repetition. "Safe and small and unremarkable. I paid for that. Do you understand what I paid? Aune's face. Sef's daughters. Seventeen sleepers in a chapel who woke to lamplight and strangers. I paid for your safety with their erasure, and the guild threw in my own name free of charge, and I thanked them, Casper. I stood in the rain outside this building and I thanked them." Her voice cracked on the last word, and the lamp guttered. "And now there is a child at your door speaking the language we sold, and the collectors are circling, and I can feel it happening again. The same bargain, wearing new clothes. They will come to you with kindness. They will offer you exactly what they offered me. Silence, safety, a life with nothing in it."

"They already did." Casper thought of Maren Hale's soft voice, the offered protection, the warmth of complicity like a blanket on a cold night. "I lied to her, Gran. First time in years. It felt—" She searched for the word and found the wrong one first. "*Good.*"

"Of course it did. That's how it starts. That's how it started with me." The ghost drifted closer to the table, to the thin contract with the hole in its heart, and her finger hovered over the burn without touching it. "I have waited thirty years to ask you one thing, and I need you to answer honestly, because I will not be able to bear it otherwise." She lifted her eyes, and they were young eyes in an old face, frightened eyes. "When they come for the child—and they are coming, girl, they are already coming—which will you be? The woman who opens the door for the collectors? Or the woman who pays?"

"That's not a fair question."

"No," her grandmother agreed softly. "It wasn't fair when they asked it of me, either. That's rather the point."

The silence between them filled with the sound of the river moving overhead somewhere far above, patient and enormous, waiting the way rivers wait. Casper looked down at her own hands—carver's hands, ledger-keeper's hands, exact and steady—and saw in them, for the first time, her grandmother's hands, and beyond those, dimly, a chain of hands reaching back to a coast she had never seen, each one gripping the next, each one letting go of something else.

"I found a door," Casper said quietly. "Under the pilings. It asked me a question and I couldn't answer it."

Her grandmother went very still. Ghost-still, more still than the living can be. "What question?"

"*Who are you.*"

The ghost closed her eyes. When she opened them again there was something new in them—not fear, not quite hope, but the terrible tenderness of someone watching another person walk toward the edge she herself had refused. "Then listen to me, and listen well, because I can only tell you what I know and I have spent a lifetime not knowing it: that door does not want your guilt, and it does not want your innocence. It wants the truth, and the truth has a shape, and you cannot carve it in advance." She reached out—not quite touching, but close, so close Casper felt cold move along her arm like a current. "I begged them to burn my name. Don't you dare give yours away for free."

Above them, through the vault ceiling, footsteps crossed the ledger hall—heavy boots, unhurried, stopping directly overhead. Three sets. Then a knock, polite and terrible, at the top of the stairs.

"Casper Dunmore," Maren Hale's voice said pleasantly through the door. "We need to talk about the cellar."
