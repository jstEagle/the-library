## 9. The Question at the Threshold

The river had two skins, Casper thought as she ran the pilings in the dark. The one everyone knew lay on top, grey and patient, carrying barges and drowned dogs and the city's refuse out to the sea. But underneath it there was a second river, older and blacker, and she could feel it now through the soles of her boots—a slow cold pulse, like something sleeping with one eye open.

Ilo rode against her shoulder, small and burning-hot with fever-fear, arms locked around her neck. The child's breath came quick and shallow against her ear, and every exhale carried words Casper no longer had to translate.

*They are coming,* Ilo whispered in Vellish. *The ones who smell like sealed rooms.*

*Yes.*

*Will they catch us?*

*Not tonight.* She said it because the child needed to hear it, not because she believed it. Behind them, back through the tannery lanes and across the fish-market square, lanterns were blooming one by one—Maren Hale's collectors moving in their terrible quiet way, spreading out along the waterfront like ink dropped into water. Casper had heard them before she'd seen them: three of the guild's hounds, the ghost-dogs that tracked memory the way bloodhounds track blood, and behind the hounds, Maren herself, walking with that unhurried stride of a woman who had never once needed to run.

Casper's arms ached from carrying Ilo down the ladder-streets. Her lungs burned. Her mind, traitorously calm, kept returning to the ledger page she'd torn out and burned rather than let it fall into Maren's hands—the page with her grandmother's confession on it, the words still glowing behind her eyelids like an afterimage.

*We told them where the Harrow family slept. We told them about the miller's daughters, about old Sef and his boat. For this we were given silence. For this we were made no one.*

She had read those lines forty times since finding them, and each reading had stripped away another layer of the comfortable story she'd built around her own life. She was not unremarkable by nature. She was unremarkable by purchase. Every quiet year, every unnoticed day in the guildhouse, every time a collector looked straight through her—that had been bought and paid for, in a currency she was only now learning to count.

And now the bill had come due, and it was standing right in front of her wearing a child's face, speaking the language her family had helped bury.

*Here,* the ferryman's ghost said, flickering at the edge of the pilings where the planking rotted into black water. He had led them true, dead man that he was, drawn up out of whatever rest he'd earned to walk ahead of them all night like a lamp with a grudge. His finger pointed down. *Under. It was under when I ferried for the last king's tax-collectors, and it will be under when your guild is dust.*

Casper set Ilo down on the planks and knelt. Below her, between the barnacled legs of the pilings, in the space where the tide breathed in and out, something caught the moonlight that shouldn't have been there. Not metal. Not stone. A seam—a vertical line of faint silver light running down through the dark water, straight as a plumb line, from the underside of the dock into depths the lamps never reached.

A door.

*It opens on the ebb,* the ferryman said. *When the river draws back its skirt. You have perhaps the length of a slow song.*

Casper looked at Ilo. The child looked back—mud-streaked, hollow-eyed, six years old or seven, carrying a whole country in her mouth the way other children carry stones in their pockets. And Ilo smiled at her. Small and fierce and trusting, in the way only children trust, having decided somewhere in the bone-cellar among the carved words and the humming ghosts that this scarred woman with the chisel-calloused hands would not fail her.

Casper had failed people before. Her family had made a trade of it.

"Not this one," she said aloud, in Vellish, and her voice cracked in the middle of the word like green wood.

* * *

Getting down was the worst of it. The pilings wore a beard of broken shell, and the tide-slick timber gave nothing to grip. Casper went first, lowering Ilo hand over hand, the child's weight swinging above the black second-river until her bare feet found the cross-brace below the waterline. Then Casper followed, tearing her sleeve, opening a long shallow cut along her forearm that she did not feel at all. Above them the collectors' lanterns swept the dock-planks, and once a hound's voice rose—not a bark but a sound like a page being torn slowly in half—and Maren Hale's voice came after it, level and unhurried:

"Casper Dunmore. I know you can hear me."

She could. That was the cruelty of it. Maren knew exactly how well she could hear.

"You're a sensible woman," Maren called down through the planking, conversational, almost kind. "You've spent your whole life being sensible. Bring the child up, and tomorrow none of this ever happened. That's the bargain we've always had, you and I. Silence for safety. You know how much that's worth."

*I know exactly what it's worth,* Casper thought, wedging her back against the piling, water rising past her knees as the ebb began. *My grandmother priced it. She left the receipt.*

"Come up," Maren said, "and keep your name."

And there it was—the whole trap laid out in a single sentence, so simple, so reasonable. Keep your name. Stay Casper Dunmore, bone-carver, keeper of minor ledgers, a woman so forgettable that even her own reflection seemed to glance elsewhere. Live another forty safe years and die in a warm room, unmourned and unremembered and untouched by any of this.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed it with a strange detachment, watching her own fingers tremble against the wet timber.

Then Ilo's small hand found hers in the dark and squeezed it, and the child began—softly, barely a thread of sound—to sing. One of the cellar songs. The one the dead had hummed along to until the very walls of the guildhouse had seemed thin as paper. And from somewhere above them, drifting down through the gaps in the planks, Casper heard it answered: the ferryman first, then others, the drowned and the forgotten who haunted the waterfront, joining the tune in voices like wind through reeds. The dead were singing her down the last few feet of her old life.

The door stood before her now, fully revealed as the ebb pulled the water away from it. Silver-seamed, ancient, its surface carved with the same script Ilo had scratched into the bone-cellar floor—the script of Vell, flowing like water frozen mid-pour. In its center, where a lock or a handle might be, the carvings gathered into a single line of deeper cutting, and as Casper pressed her palm against the cold surface, the line lit.

Words rose through her hand, through her arm, into her skull. Not a voice. A question, worn smooth by centuries of asking.

**Who are you?**

The ward's question. The one the ledger had warned of. *Warded against the descendants of the traded,* the account-keeper had written, four hundred years ago, in ink gone brown as old blood. *For those who bought their absence cannot answer honestly, and the door knows a lie the way the tongue knows rot.*

Casper opened her mouth—and the lie was already there, waiting, smooth as riverstone. *I am no one. I am Dunmore's daughter, nameless, harmless, a carver of beads.* All she had to do was say it.

The door waited. The lanterns moved overhead. Ilo watched her with eyes too old for her face.

"No," Casper whispered.

She thought of her grandmother then—not the ghost, not yet, but the woman as she must have been before the fall: young, frightened, standing in some burning doorway making the worst choice of her life and calling it survival. Casper carried that woman in her blood. She always had. Every easy anonymity of her life had been built on the bones of the Harrows and the miller's daughters and old Sef and his boat.

So she would not lie. And she would not hide behind Ilo either—would not claim innocence, would not say *the child is the Vellish one, not me, I'm only helping.*

She drew a breath that tasted of salt and old iron.

"I am Casper Dunmore," she said, and heard her own name in Vellish for the first time in her life—*Kasparra Dunmorren*—and it sounded like a key turning. "Daughter of Mereth, daughter of Sable, daughter of Yeva of Vell." Each generation spoken aloud, each name a stone laid back onto a wall someone else had torn down. "And my grandmother's mother betrayed her neighbors to the collectors of her day. She sold the Harrows to the knife. She sold old Sef and his boat. She bought our lives with theirs, and she bought our silence, and we took it—we took it for four generations, and I took it too. I kept the guild's ledgers. I saw what they bought—doors, names, bones, villages—and I said nothing, because saying nothing was warm and saying something was death."

The ward-light pulsed, steady, listening.

"I am the granddaughter of a betrayal," Casper said. "I am the complicit one. I am the coward who learned better too late, standing here in the cold with a stolen child in a stolen language, asking a door to open for the kind of person it was built to keep out. I don't ask you to believe I'm innocent. I'm not. I only ask—" and here her voice finally broke, all the way through, and she let it break— "I only ask that you take the truth, because it's the only thing I have that isn't stolen."

Silence, except the river breathing and Ilo's song, still going, thin and stubborn as a candleflame in wind.

Above them, boots crossed the dock directly overhead. Lantern light bled down through the plank-gaps in long gold stripes. Maren's voice, closer now, almost gentle: "Last chance, Casper."

The ward's question came again—but softer now, almost curious, the way the ferryman's ghost had sounded when she'd finally stopped fudging his measurements.

**Who are you?**

And Casper, weeping without shame, dripping riverwater and honesty, answered a third time: "I am Kasparra Dunmorren. Guilty. Here. Mine."

The silver seam split.

Light poured up from beneath the second river—not white, not gold, but the deep sea-green of Vell remembered, the color of the country's waters in the songs Ilo sang. The door swung inward on darkness full of stars, and a smell came through it that dropped Casper to her knees on the cross-brace: rain on hot stone, cedar smoke, bread baking somewhere far off, the impossible smell of a country that had been underwater for four hundred years.

Ilo made a sound Casper had never heard a child make—grief and homecoming in the same breath.

Behind and above, the hatch above the pilings banged open, and lantern-light flooded down, and Maren Hale's voice lost its gentleness all at once: "Stop them. *Now.*"

The collectors started down the pilings, hounds first, their torn-page cries splitting the night.

Casper got her feet under her, lifted Ilo onto her hip, and turned toward the open door.
