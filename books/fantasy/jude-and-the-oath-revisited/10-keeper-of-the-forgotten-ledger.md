## 10. Keeper of the Forgotten Ledger

The door did not close behind Ilo.

That was the first thing Casper understood, standing in the cold green dark beneath the river pilings with water seeping through her boots and her hands still raised as though they could hold the shape of what had just passed through them. The ward's question hung unanswered now—answered, rather, so thoroughly that it had nothing left to ask—and the door stood open like a held breath.

Beyond it was not a country. She had known it would not be a country. Vell was under the water and had been for three hundred years; no door could un-drown a nation. What lay beyond the threshold was something else: a country remembered. Hills that were only hills because someone had loved them enough to describe them to a child. A harbor with gulls that sang in Vellish, because every gull in memory sang in Vellish. Light the color of the inside of an eyelid on a summer afternoon. It was not a place you could farm. It was not a place you could die in, either. It was the shape of home kept warm by everyone who had ever carried it, and it was enough—it would have to be enough—for a mud-soaked girl who spoke a language older than kingdoms.

Ilo stood just past the threshold, small against all that soft light, and looked back at Casper.

*You're coming too,* the child said in Vellish, and it wasn't a question. Children never believed that adults might stay behind. The world was supposed to move for them, whole and entire.

Casper knelt in the wet stone so their eyes were level. Her knees ached. Everything ached—the confession she had spoken into the ward still sat in her chest like a swallowed coal, her grandmother's crime and her own complicity and her true name, *Sefa Vellane*, spoken aloud for the first time since it had been burned out of every book that ever held it.

*I can't,* she said. *Someone has to keep the ledger.*

*What ledger?*

She almost smiled. Of course the child didn't know. No one knew. That had been the entire architecture of the thing.

*The one where your name gets written down so it doesn't get lost,* Casper said. *Every language needs someone keeping accounts. Otherwise the guild wins by default. They don't even have to do anything. They just wait, and forget things, and eventually the forgetting does their work for them.* She reached out and tucked a strand of Ilo's river-damp hair behind her ear, the way her grandmother had once done for her, before Casper understood what those hands had bought. *In there, you'll find people. Not alive people. But they remember. They'll teach you everything the songs left out. And as long as one person speaks it, the tongue isn't dead. It's just waiting.*

Ilo's chin did the stubborn thing it did. *And when I've learned everything?*

*Then you come back to the door and teach me,* Casper said. *I'm not going anywhere.*

It was a lie of a kind—she was going somewhere, somewhere very specific, a small stone room with a lamp and a desk—but it was the kind of lie you told children so they could walk forward, and she found she could carry its weight without shame.

Ilo considered her for a long moment with eyes far too old for her face, eyes that had watched collectors come for her family, that had walked out of a drowned history alone into a city built on forgetting. Then she did the most Vellish thing Casper had ever seen anyone do. She stepped back across the threshold, took Casper's face in both muddy hands, and kissed her forehead—the old blessing, the one the ferryman's ghost hummed, the one that meant *you are seen, you are counted, you are not lost.*

*Keeper Sefa,* Ilo said gravely, testing the name like a new coin.

Then she turned and walked into the remembered light, and the light folded around her like water closing over a dropped stone, and she was gone—not lost, gone the way a letter is gone when it has been delivered—and the door stayed open, patient as a doorway is, waiting for whoever came next.

* * *

Maren Hale came down herself. Casper heard her boots on the stairs before she saw her, that particular measured tread of a woman who had made an art of arriving exactly when arriving mattered.

Maren stood at the foot of the stairs and looked at the open door for a long time. The light from it moved on her face like water. Whatever she saw there, she did not flinch from it, which was more than Casper had expected.

"You opened it," Maren said.

"I answered it."

"Same thing, in the end." She came closer, stopping just short of the threshold, and Casper noticed she kept her hands clasped behind her back, the way you do near an edge. "The ledgers will show a breach. The senior partners will want a reckoning. Names taken, contracts invoked, the usual machinery." She paused. "Unless."

"Unless," Casper said.

"There's a form for this. There's always a form. Voluntary dissolution of identity. Strike the name from every register, every contract, every index the guild keeps—which is every index the city keeps, though we're not supposed to say so." Maren's voice was level, professional, and underneath it, very faintly, almost tender. "No collector comes for you. No debt attaches to you. You simply stop being anyone the books can find. Most people who sign it don't survive a year. Not because anything kills them. Because being nobody is heavier than it sounds."

"I know how heavy it is," Casper said. "My family signed it generations ago. We just called it surviving."

Something moved behind Maren's eyes—recognition, maybe, or regret, or the particular loneliness of two women who had spent their lives making themselves useful to an institution that ate names. "You understand what you're choosing. The child goes free, the door stays open, and you sit here in the dark keeping accounts until—" She didn't finish it.

"Until it doesn't matter anymore," Casper said. "Yes."

"And your name? The real one. The Vellish one." Maren tilted her head toward the open door. "Do I strike that too?"

"No." Casper said it before she had fully decided to, and then felt the truth of it settle through her like sediment finding a riverbed. "Strike Casper Dunmore. Every copy, every cross-reference, every bead I ever carved for the guild inventory. Let the bone-carver vanish; she was only ever a place to hide. But write *Sefa Vellane* in the oldest volume you have, in the margin, in pencil if you have to. Not as a member. As a debtor." She smiled, and felt the smile reach all the way up into her eyes for the first time in years. "I owe Vell a debt. Someone should keep the account."

Maren studied her for a long moment. Then she took out her notebook—the small black one, the one collectors used for things that never went in official ledgers—and wrote something in it, and showed it to Casper: *Sefa Vellane, debtor, door-keeper, river gate. Account open.*

"Signed?" Maren asked, offering the pencil.

Casper signed. It was the first time in her life she had written the name without flinching.

Maren closed the notebook, and for a heartbeat her composure cracked—not much, just enough to show the woman underneath, tired and old and wondering what she herself had traded away and whether anyone would ever keep her account. "For what it's worth," she said, "the child was the best thing this guild ever failed to collect." She turned and climbed the stairs, and her tread faded, and the machinery above began, slowly, to grind out the erasure of Casper Dunmore.

* * *

The room behind the door was small and dry and had probably been a toll-house once, back when the river had a Vellish name and the pilings were young. Casper swept it. She found a desk, or something desk-shaped, and a lamp that burned without oil because remembered countries are generous with light. From the cellar she brought her tools, her bone-blanks, her unfinished memorial beads. She brought the ledgers—all of them, even the sealed volumes, especially the sealed volumes, because a keeper who hides the shameful pages is just a collector with better manners.

She set up the ferryman's bead on the corner of the desk where he could see it. He approved. He had opinions about everything now that he'd stopped correcting her measurements and started singing instead.

Because that was the other thing. The ghosts had come.

They arrived the way fog arrives, gradually and then completely, drawn down through the city by something they couldn't name but recognized instantly: their language, spoken aloud, written in a ledger, given a room. Old men who had died pretending to be from Harrowmere. Women whose names had been sold and burned and who had wandered the guildhouse for decades as nameless as the furniture. Children. Always children. They crowded the little toll-room and spilled out along the pilings, and when Casper opened the first fresh page and wrote *Vellish Lexicon, Volume One* at the top, they made a sound she had never heard ghosts make.

They sang.

Not the thin, mournful humming she had caught in the bone-cellar, half-swallowed by years of silence. Real singing, full-throated, harmonies stacking on harmonies the way water stacks on water, songs about harbors and grandmothers and the specific blue of a certain door in a certain street that no longer existed anywhere except here. The dead pressed close to watch her hand move across the page, calling out words—*no, the word for door is older than that, write the older one*—and she wrote them all down, and corrected, and wrote again, and her pen never quite kept pace and she had never been happier.

Through the open door, sometimes, the remembered hills glowed at dusk, and once—only once—she saw a small figure running down a remembered hillside with a remembered dog, laughing in Vellish, and she put down her pen and let herself watch until the light took it.

Her own name was already fading. She could feel it going, the way you feel a tooth stop aching—not absence exactly, more like a room in the house finally quiet. Somewhere above her, clerks were striking lines through registers. Somewhere in the city, people who had known Casper Dunmore would pause over her name and feel it slide off their memory like water off waxed cloth. By spring, no one would be able to say who kept the little lamp lit beneath the river. The guild's own records would show only what Maren had penciled in the margin of the oldest book:

*A keeper. Unnamed. Account open.*

On the first page of the new ledger, beneath the title, Casper—who had been many things and was now, at last, precisely what she chose—wrote the entry that would govern all the others:

*This ledger exists so that nothing which was drowned may be forgotten twice. All debts recorded. All names honored. The door stands open.*

Outside, under the pilings, the river moved in the dark toward a sea that had swallowed a country, and the dead sang, and the lamp burned, and someone was coming down the stairs—a child's footsteps, quick and sure, speaking a language older than kingdoms, coming home to be taught.

Casper dipped her pen, and began.
