## 1. Charms for Copper

The trouble with selling magic in a city that had outlawed it, Bram Vane had learned, was that you had to be very good at pretending it wasn't magic at all.

"Third knot from the left," she said, turning the little cord over in her brown fingers for the fishwife who'd been hovering at her stall for the better part of ten minutes. "Red thread for luck on the water, blue for calm at home. Don't mix them up. The red one's been blessed by a priest of the Tidewife."

"There aren't any priests of the Tidewife left," the fishwife said.

"That's what makes it lucky." Bram tied off the knot and held it out. "Two coppers."

The fishwife paid without further argument, which was the whole art of it, really. People wanted to believe. They just needed you to give them a story sturdy enough to hang their believing on, and the Watch didn't arrest anyone for telling stories.

Bram's stall sat at the crooked elbow of Copperlane Market, wedged between a pickle-barrel cart and a man who sold secondhand boots with the confidence of a man who had never once checked whether the pairs matched. Her awning was patched in three places. Her sign — *CHARMS & KNOTWORK, HONEST PRICES* — had faded to a color that could generously be called "weathered" and less generously called "the color of old dishwater." It was perfect. Nothing about it said *witch*. Nothing about it said *arrest me*.

Under her sleeve, wrapped twice around her left forearm beneath the linen bandage she never removed, the oath burned like a coal banked in ash. It always burned a little. On cold mornings it burned more. On days like this — grey sky pressing down on Greyhollow like a lid that wouldn't quite close, the first true bite of autumn in the wind off the harbor — it burned enough that she had to keep her hands busy so they didn't shake.

Three years since she'd spoken a sword-word. Three years since the night the edicts came down and the practitioners' quarter went up in smoke, and Maren had put both hands on Bram's face and said, *They can't burn what they can't find. Be small, girl. Be so small they look right through you.*

Maren had been very good at being small, at the end. Small enough to fit in an unmarked grave.

"Lady Vane!" piped a voice at knee height, and Bram looked down into the gap-toothed grin of Pip Tanner, age seven, terror of the pickle-cart, who had appointed himself her unofficial apprentice sometime last spring and could not be dissuaded by anything short of actual lightning. "Did you make any new ones? The kind that really work?"

"Pip." She kept her voice light, but her eyes flicked past him out of habit — left, right, the watch-post at the fountain, empty, good. "How many times have I told you? It's knots. String and patience. That's all."

"But Mrs. Hobbs says her fever-charm cured her boy when the physicker couldn't—"

"Mrs. Hobbs's boy got better because children get better, and because Mrs. Hobbs believes very hard, which is free medicine." She fished a scrap of knotted red thread from her basket and pressed it into his grubby hand before he could argue. "For your mother's mending basket. So she doesn't lose her temper when you track mud through the kitchen. Now go on, before she does anyway."

He ran off clutching it like a crown jewel. Bram watched him go and felt the familiar ache settle behind her ribs — not the oath's ache, the other one, the one shaped like a small boy calling her *Lady Vane* as though she were something worth the title.

She was good at this. That was the worst part. She was good at being small.

* * *

Midmorning brought the rush: the shift-change crowd from the ropewalks, all elbows and hunger, wanting charms against rope-burn and bad lungs and wives who counted the wage-purse too carefully. Bram sold fever-knots three for five coppers, luck-strings for the lottery runners, a "blessed" thimble that was in fact simply a very nice thimble she'd gotten cheap off the boot man. A young dockhand bought a courage-knot for his first deep-water run and blushed the entire time. An old soldier with a game leg haggled her down to one copper for a pain-charm and then, when he thought she wasn't looking, tucked two extra onto the table.

"You'll ruin my margins, Harl," she called after him.

"You'll survive," he said. "You've got the look of a survivor."

If only he knew what the look cost.

By noon the sun had made a halfhearted attempt at breaking through and given up, and the market smelled of brine, frying onions, and the particular damp-sweat smell of a city holding its breath. Because that was the thing about Greyhollow nobody said aloud anymore: everyone was holding their breath. Three years of the Edicts of Clean Streets, and the whole city had learned to speak in code. Charms instead of spells. Knotwork instead of witchcraft. Everyone knew. Nobody said. The Watch knew too, probably, and looked away for a cut of the take, and everyone pretended the pretense was real, because the alternative was remembering what happened to the quarter, and to the forty-one names read out on the steps of the Assize Hall, and to the woman who had taught Bram everything and died in a cellar that smelled of wet stone while Bram held her hand and could do nothing — nothing — because using her gift would have killed them both faster.

Be small. Be so small they look right through you.

Bram was so tired of being small that some mornings she woke up furious about it, and had to lie there and let the fury drain out through the floorboards before she could get up and tie knots for coppers.

She was reaching for her lunch — half a loaf and hard cheese, the meal of queens — when the bells started.

Not the hour bells. The *other* bells. High, fast, ugly, from the direction of the fountain post, and every head in Copperlane turned at once like a field of grim flowers following a black sun.

"Raid," someone breathed, and the word moved through the market faster than the bells did.

Bram's hands were moving before her mind caught up. Luck-knots into the false bottom of the basket. Fever-charms under the folded blanket. The boot man was already kicking his cart into motion; the pickle-cart fled; the whole market performed its practiced little dance of becoming innocent, stalls folding into ordinary wares, witches becoming seamstresses and tinkers in the space of ten heartbeats. Bram flipped her sign over to its blank back, swept her remaining stock into the basket, and was adjusting her shawl into respectable widowhood when the Watch came around the corner.

Six of them, in slate-grey coats, with the new badges — the eye-and-anvil of the Office of Purity, stamped fresh and shiny, because nothing said *purity* like a well-funded badge. And behind them, trotting to keep up, a man in a black coat with a white collar and a ledger under his arm. A Reader. The Watch broke doors; the Readers decided what was behind them worth burning.

"Inspection," announced the sergeant, in the tone of a man announcing weather he personally found distasteful. "All goods displayed. All persons remain where they stand."

Bram stood. Bram was excellent at standing. She arranged her face into the mild, faintly put-upon expression of a widow whose morning was being inconvenienced, and she did not look at the fountain post, and she absolutely did not look at—

At Pip.

The boy had frozen mid-dash, three stalls down, still holding the red thread she'd given him, directly in the path of the Reader. He was looking around with the wide-eyed panic of a child who has just realized the game has stopped being a game, and his fist — his stupid, brave, seven-year-old fist — was clenched around a charm that Bram had, in a moment of weakness she would now pay for, actually *made work*. Not much. A thread of warmth woven in, barely a candleflame of the craft, enough to genuinely soothe a temper. Enough for a Reader's senses to catch like a hook in a thumb.

The Reader stopped. Turned. Looked down at Pip the way a man looks at a stain on a fine coat.

"Well," said the Reader. "What have we here?"

"He's just a child," said the pickle-woman, who had come back for her cart and immediately regretted her heroism.

"They're always just children," the Reader said mildly, "until they're not." He extended one gloved hand. "Show me what you're holding, boy."

Pip's chin came up. Bram saw it happen — saw the fear curdle into defiance, saw him plant his feet the way children do when they've decided the world is wrong and they are right, and she thought, with terrible clarity: *he is going to say something. He is going to say it's a magic charm and that it works, because he's seven and he loves me and he doesn't understand.*

And the Reader would take his hand. Or worse. The Office of Purity had opinions about children who *believed*, and the opinions involved long interviews and longer ledgers, and mothers who signed papers swearing their children would be examined quarterly until majority.

Bram calculated the distance. Six paces. The sergeant between her and them. Two watchmen idling near her stall, close enough to see whatever she did next.

Every sensible part of her — the part that had survived three years, the part Maren had built with three years of *be small, be small, be small* — told her to stay still. To let it happen. To be small. One child's red thread was not worth forty-one names' worth of fire.

But the child was looking at her. Not at the Reader. At her. With absolute, unbroken faith, as if the lady with the stall full of string could surely fix this, because she fixed everything, because her charms worked.

Bram exhaled, and reached under her shawl, and touched the oath.

It flared against her palm like a live thing glad of the attention. Three years of banked coals, and the heat of it nearly took her breath away — the sword-oath, the oldest binding, the words that lived in her marrow waiting to be spoken. She didn't speak them. She couldn't; a sword-word would split the street open and bring half the Watch garrison running. But the oath was a door, and behind the door was everything Maren had ever poured into her, and she opened it just a crack. Just a hairline crack. Just enough.

"*Still,*" she whispered — not a sword-word, not even a spell-word, just intent pushed through the crack with the precision of a woman threading a needle in the dark — and she sent it out along the red thread, along the bond she'd tied into it that morning without letting herself think about why, and the thread warmed in Pip's fist.

Warmth, not power. Calm, not command. The gentlest working she knew: the one that made a frightened heart remember it was allowed to beat slowly.

Pip blinked. His shoulders came down from around his ears. When the Reader repeated, "Show me what you're holding, boy," Pip opened his hand with the easy indifference of a child showing off a bit of string.

"Just thread," Pip said. "The lady gives 'em out. For mending."

The Reader stared at the thread. Bram held her breath and held the working steady and felt sweat start cold between her shoulder blades, because the warmth was *there*, faint as a hearth across a room, and if this Reader had any talent at all—

The Reader plucked the thread from Pip's palm, rubbed it between gloved fingers, and frowned.

"Warm," he said.

"It's wool," said Bram.

Everyone turned. She hadn't meant to speak. But here she was, stepping forward, shawl clutched, voice pitched at exactly the register of a woman whose patience had been tried once too often today. "Wool holds warmth, sir, that's the whole point of wool. My late husband was a dyer. I sell knotwork for luck and mending-thread for practical purposes, and if the Office of Purity intends to confiscate my mending-thread, I'll thank you for a receipt, because I've a stall to restock and no husband to earn for me."

Silence. The sergeant's mouth twitched — the ghost of sympathy, quickly murdered. The Reader looked at her, and she met his eyes with the flat, bored courage of the utterly harmless, and inside her skull the oath roared like a sea against a seawall, and she thought, *I am going to die in Copperlane Market over a piece of red thread, and Maren will haunt me forever.*

Then the Reader dropped the thread.

"Hmph." He consulted his ledger, wrote something — a flourish, nothing, she'd bet her awning on it — and snapped it shut. "Nothing here. Move along." He pointed his glove at Pip. "Boy. Go home to your mother."

Pip went. Fast. Bram watched him vanish down the Tanner's Row alley and only then allowed herself to breathe, slow and even, while the raid rolled on past her stall — a knock on the boot man's shutter, a shouted question, the market exhaling around her.

Her hands, once the Watch rounded the far corner, would not stop shaking. She gripped the edge of the table and waited it out, jaw tight, and hated — quietly, thoroughly, with three years of practice — how good the magic had felt. Just that crack in the door. Just that sliver of herself, and her whole body sang with it like a struck bell. That was the trap nobody warned you about: not the burning, not the risk. The *wanting*. How after three years of being small, one whisper of stillness could make you feel more yourself than all the honest knotwork in the world.

"Close call," said the boot man, sidling back with his mismatched cart.

"Mm," said Bram.

"That boy. Yours?"

"No." She began retying her sign to its nail, needing her hands occupied. "Just a customer."

"Customers like that'll get you hanged someday."

"I know," Bram said, and smiled at him, because that was the other thing she was good at now — smiling while the coal under her sleeve burned holes in her composure. "Buy a luck-knot. You look like you need one."

* * *

Evening came down grey and early, the way it did now, winter gathering itself somewhere north like a debt collector. Bram packed the stall by feel, her thoughts elsewhere — on red thread, on Pip's upturned chin, on the word *still* still humming faintly in the air around her like a plucked string refusing to go quiet.

She was blowing out her lamp when the wind changed.

It came off the harbor with teeth in it, colder than the season had any right to be, and the oath under her bandage answered it — flared, sharp, insistent, the way it hadn't flared in three years. Not the dull banked ache. This was a pull. Northward. Like a compass needle waking up.

Bram stood very still in the emptying market, her basket on her hip, and stared down the length of Copperlane toward the darkening water.

That was when she saw the woman.

She came out of the alley by the tannery wall at a stumbling walk, coatless in the cold, frost in her hair though it had not yet snowed anywhere in Greyhollow. Frost on her eyelashes. Frost on her lips. She walked like someone carrying something breakable and precious, and her eyes — wild, glassy, searching the shuttered stalls one by one — found Bram's lamp and locked onto it.

She changed course. She came straight for the stall, faster now, and Bram's hand drifted toward the knife in her basket without her permission.

"Please," the woman rasped, and her breath came out as fog, too much fog, cold pouring off her like an open cellar door. "They said — the knotseller. The one whose charms—" She laughed, a cracked, awful sound. "Whose charms *work*."

"Ma'am, you need a physicker—"

"No." The woman seized Bram's wrist — her bandaged wrist, the oath roaring at the touch — and her grip was freezing, impossibly freezing, cold sinking through linen and skin straight to the bone. Up close her eyes were the worst part: not frightened. *Relieved.* "No time. Listen. Listen to me. I sold it. Years ago, I sold it, and it's called due tonight, and I got away, I got *out*, but it follows the debt—" She was fumbling at her breast, dragging something out — a roll of oilcloth, stained dark at one corner, stained in a way Bram recognized with a lurch she'd spend years trying to forget. Blood. "Take it. Take it *north*. Don't open it till you're past the passes. Promise me you—"

Somewhere behind her, down the lane, boots. More than two pairs. Moving fast.

The woman's eyes went wide. She shoved the oilcloth roll into Bram's arms with the last of her strength, and then she did the strangest thing: she smiled. Wide and warm and utterly genuine, the smile of someone setting down a weight carried twenty years.

"Oh," she whispered. "Oh, it's warm *here*. I'd forgotten things could be warm."

She slid down against the stall post, and the frost climbed her cheeks like ivy, and Bram — kneeling, shouting for help that wouldn't come fast enough, pressing her palms to a chest going cold as river-stone — felt the oath under her bandage pull northward, hard, like a hooked fish.

The woman's lips moved one last time. No sound came out. But Bram, swordspeaker, keeper of dead words, read the shape of them easily:

*Beneath. Beneath the mountain.*
