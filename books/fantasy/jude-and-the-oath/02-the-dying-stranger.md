## 2. The Dying Stranger

The frost came early that morning, and with it the smell of tallow and wet wool that meant the market would be thin. Bram Vane set out her wares anyway: luck-knots in three colors, fever-charms wrapped in waxed linen, a small tin of "blessed" salt that was, in truth, salt. She arranged them on the board she'd planed smooth herself, angled so the weak grey light caught the knots' bright thread. Presentation mattered. In Greyhollow, where magic had been outlawed for eleven years and everything else taxed besides, presentation was the only spell left to the honest merchant.

Not that her charms were entirely honest.

She kept the lie small and told it carefully. A luck-knot worked because a person believed it might — that was true enough, and belief did more good in this city than most prayers. The fever-charm was willow bark and elderflower under the wax, which any herb-wife could sell legally at twice the price. The knot itself was just cord and patience. The warmth a customer felt in their palm when they closed their hand around it —

Well. That was the part Bram didn't examine too closely, the way you don't press on a bruise to see how bad the day will be.

Under her left sleeve, from wrist to elbow, the oath sat coiled like a sleeping adder. Three years since she'd spoken a sword-word. Three years since Master Corvin had gone north and not come back, and she'd taken the last thing he'd left her — the binding, the discipline, the long slow burn of an oath sworn in the old tongue — and folded it down small enough to hide beneath a merchant's cuff.

*Small charms for copper,* he'd have said, if dead men could laugh. *You were meant to hold a door shut against the dark, girl, and instead you're selling string.*

"String's honest," Bram muttered to him now, as she did most mornings. The empty stall across the aisle didn't answer. It never did, but talking to it kept her from talking to the ache in her arm, which answered far too readily.

* * *

By midmorning the market had found its bones. The fishwife two stalls down argued cheerfully about herring. A boy sold roasted chestnuts whose smoke smelled like every winter Bram had survived. She'd moved four luck-knots and one fever-charm to a cooper's wife who pressed her hand over her heart and said the charm had already helped, and Bram had smiled the smile that fit over the truth like a lid over a well.

That was when the stranger came.

Bram noticed feet before faces — a stallholder's habit, watching what walked toward you. These feet were wrong. They dragged. One boot was missing its laces entirely, tied with a strip of what looked like curtain hem, and the woman wearing them came up the aisle like a ship taking water, listing hard to starboard.

She was perhaps forty, though frostbite and hunger made age a guess rather than a fact. Grey hair escaped a hood crusted with rime. Her lips had gone the blue-grey of old pewter, and her hands — Bram's eyes went to her hands and stayed there — her hands were bad. White at the fingertips, mottled black between the knuckles. Frostbite past saving.

"Charms," the woman rasped, stopping at the stall. Her voice sounded like a door opening in an empty house. "You sell charms."

"Luck and health," Bram said, easy as breathing. "Nothing dramatic. The fever-charm's my best seller this time of year."

The woman looked up. Her eyes were the shock of it — clear and brown and utterly present, while the rest of her was already halfway gone somewhere colder. She looked at Bram the way people look at a lighthouse when they've spent all night on the rocks.

"No," she said. "No, you don't. You sell *real* ones."

The words dropped through Bram like a stone through pondwater. Around them the market went on — herring, chestnuts, a child shrieking joyfully about something — oblivious. Bram's hand drifted, casual as adjusting her sleeve, to make sure the cuff sat low.

"Madam, I sell knots," she said pleasantly. "Cord. Very nice cord."

"You're her." The woman swayed. "The swordspeaker. Corvin's girl. He said — he said if I ever got desperate enough, if they came for me, I should find the charm-seller in Greyhollow with the crooked awning." Her mouth twitched toward something that wanted to be a smile. "Your awning's crooked."

It was. Bram had been meaning to fix it for two years.

"I think you've confused me with someone else," Bram said, and hated how smooth it came out, how practiced. Eleven years of outlawry taught you to deny yourself fluently. "Sit down before you fall down. There's a stool."

The woman didn't take the stool. She took the stall.

Her knees buckled and she caught the counter's edge, dragging the whole board sideways with a screech that turned heads along the aisle. Luck-knots skittered. Bram grabbed for them, grabbed for the woman, and got an armful of freezing flesh as the stranger collapsed against her — light, terribly light, the weight of someone whose body had been spending itself faster than it ate.

"Please." The whisper came right against Bram's ear, urgent as a blade laid flat against the throat. "Listen. There's no time and you won't want to hear it and I need you to hear it anyway."

"Water — let me get you—"

"*Listen.*"

And Bram listened, because there are voices that command it, and dying is one of them.

"They know I ran. The Wardens. They'll come dressed as watchmen, they always do, they'll ask questions and search your things and you must not let them find—" The woman's hands clawed at the front of her coat, at an inner pocket stitched against the lining. Her blackened fingers couldn't work the buttons. "I sold it. Do you understand? Not my labor, not my years. My *soul*. To the court beneath the mountain. And the debt's come due, and I ran, and running doesn't work, nothing works, except—"

"Except?"

"Corvin tried. Years ago. He went down to bargain them back — all of us, everyone they'd bought — and he failed, but he learned things, and he wrote them down, and he gave the map to me because I was already theirs, because they can't read what they own." A sound came out of her that might have been laughter, cracked down the middle. "They can't see their own chains drawn by their own debtors. Stupid. Arrogant. Their one flaw, and it's ours now."

"My name isn't—" Bram started.

"It doesn't matter what your name is. It matters what your arm says."

Bram went still. The oath beneath her sleeve, quiet for three years, stirred — a slow heat rising from wrist to elbow like dawn coming up behind mountains. The woman felt it through Bram's coat; Bram watched her feel it, saw her nod as if confirming a suspicion.

"He bound you before he left," the woman whispered. "So no one could compel you. So the court couldn't reach in and turn your hand. Smart man. Stubborn man. Dead man, I think, but stubborn even dead."

The market noise seemed to come from very far away. Bram was aware of the fishwife glancing over, aware of the chestnut boy watching with open curiosity, aware most of all of the woman's hands working at that inner pocket with the terrible patience of the frozen.

"Take it," the stranger said. "I'm giving it. That matters — remember that it matters. Given, not taken, not stolen, not owed. *Given.* The court respects gift above everything; it's the one law older than they are. If they ask, you say it was given freely by a free woman, and let them choke on it."

"I don't want it." The words came out of Bram flat and honest. "Whatever it is. I keep my head down. I sell cord. I've spent three years being nobody, and it's worked, and I am not—"

"Nobody?" The woman's clear brown eyes fixed on her, and for a moment there was such fierce pity in them that Bram's denial died in her throat. "Child. You've been somebody your whole life. You're just the only one pretending."

Then the buttons gave, and the map came out.

* * *

It was oilcloth-wrapped, bloodstained — his blood or hers or older blood, Bram couldn't tell and didn't want to — and heavy for its size. The woman pressed it into Bram's hands and folded Bram's fingers over it herself, both ruined hands around Bram's two, and the cold of her was shocking, deep as well-water.

"North," she breathed. "Follow the border ink. He hid the true road in the borders — you'll know his hand. You'll know it." Her breath was going ragged now, shorter and shorter, each word costing more than the last. "Winter comes early this year. Early and wrong. When it seals — a hundred years, the mountain sleeps a century, and everyone they hold sleeps with it. Everyone I—"

She stopped. Her gaze went past Bram's shoulder, up the aisle, and whatever she saw there drained the last urgency out of her voice and replaced it with something worse: calm.

"They're here," she said. "Watchmen. Don't run. Running confirms. Sell me your charms, child. Loudly. Be dull. Be boring. Be nobody, since you love it so."

Bram turned her head, slowly, the way you turn toward a sound you dread.

Two figures in watch grey were working up the aisle. Market patrol, ordinary enough — except ordinary patrols didn't scan faces quite so systematically, and ordinary sergeants didn't wear gloves indoors in autumn, and ordinary men didn't move with that particular economy, like wolves who'd studied how dogs walk.

"Charm for a headache!" Bram announced, loud and cheerful, hauling the woman upright and propping her against the stall like any tired customer. "Best fever-charm south of the river, madam, and I'll throw in a luck-knot for twopence —"

"Kind of you," the stranger murmured. Then, closer, barely air: "Say it was given. Freely. By a free woman."

The watchmen arrived. Up close they smelled of snow, though no snow had fallen yet in Greyhollow — a high, clean, wrong smell, like the inside of a glacier. The taller one had a face like a closed ledger.

"Trouble here?" he asked.

"Fainted lady and pushy commerce," Bram said. "The usual tragedy of market day."

He looked at her a moment too long. Bram held the look, bored and bland, and thanked every stubborn god that fear, properly managed, looked exactly like irritation. Behind her, the woman had gone very still against the stall.

"We're looking for a runaway," the second watchman said. "Woman, grey-haired. Frostbitten. May have passed this way."

"Haven't seen her." Smooth. Instant. Bram heard her own lie arrive without a stammer and marveled, distantly, at what a competent criminal she'd become. "Lots of folk about, though. Could check the soup line by the fountain — cold weather brings 'em in."

The tall one's eyes moved past her. Down to her hands. To the oilcloth bundle she was holding, bloodstain side out, because there had been nowhere to put it and no time.

"What's that?"

"A purchase." Bram lifted it, displayed it, let him see the whole shapeless wrapped lump. "Dried herbs. For the fever trade. Very glamorous."

"Open it."

There it was. The moment, arriving on schedule, the way moments did. Bram felt the oath stir again under her sleeve, hot and eager, whispering in the old tongue the way it always did when her heartbeat climbed — *one word, one word and they sleep, one word and they forget* — and she pressed it down, down, three years of practice, and smiled her merchant's smile.

"Course," she said, and began unwrapping with exaggerated care. Underneath the oilcloth she had perhaps three seconds of improvisation, and she was already reaching for the fever-charms, already planning to bury whatever lay within in a fuss of waxed linen and patter—

A hand closed over the bundle first.

The stranger. Standing straight now, somehow, swaying but standing, her ruined hands lifting the map out of Bram's grip with the tenderness of someone picking up a sleeping cat.

"Don't trouble the merchant," she said clearly. "It's mine."

Every head nearby turned. The fishwife paused mid-argument. The chestnut boy stopped selling. The two watchmen went from bored to focused with a speed that confirmed every ugly guess Bram had made about them.

"Is it," said the tall one.

"I stole it," the woman said, "from the court beneath the mountain. And I'll thank you not to touch me, because I'm marked, and anything that touches a marked soul answers to the mark." She smiled then — wide, wild, luminous — and Bram understood suddenly that the smile wasn't madness. It was triumph. Small, bitter, complete. "Go on. Fetch your masters. Tell them Sera Voss sends her regards and keeps her debts."

For one suspended moment, the tall watchman actually stepped back.

Then the second one moved, fast, grabbing her arm — and the moment his glove touched her skin, Sera Voss cried out, a short sharp sound, and folded to the cobbles like a cut puppet.

What happened next happened quickly and was witnessed, later, by half the market and believed by almost none of them. The watchmen knelt over her, shouting about fits, about contagion, about clearing the crowd. Bram knelt too, because kneeling was natural, because anyone would, and got one hand under the woman's shoulders and heard, close against her ear, a whisper with the last of the air in it:

"Given. Freely. By a free woman. Now *keep* it, you stubborn girl, and finish what he started."

And then, with the watchmen's hands full of her and the whole market watching, Sera Voss died — smiling, genuinely smiling, her clear brown eyes fixed on some middle distance where perhaps a debt had finally been paid — and the strangest thing happened, the thing Bram would turn over for nights afterward:

The frost on her hood melted. All at once, all of it, everywhere on her, in a single soft exhalation, as if the cold that had ridden her for miles had simply been dismissed from service.

The watchmen felt it. Bram watched them feel it — watched them exchange a glance over the body that had nothing to do with fits or contagion, watched the tall one rise and look directly at Bram, and Bram looked back with the flat, incurious stare of a charm-seller who has seen a hundred strangers die in a city that eats strangers daily, and after a long moment he nodded to his partner and they carried the body away, and the market exhaled and resumed its noise, and the chestnut boy asked Bram if she was all right, and she said yes, and it was the third lie she'd told that morning and nowhere near the largest.

* * *

She didn't open the map until nightfall, in her cellar rooms behind the shop, with the door barred and the lamp turned low and her heart hammering like a fist on a door.

But she felt it all evening. Lying on the counter among the luck-knots, wrapped in its oilcloth and its blood, it radiated a presence she had no honest word for — the way a hearth radiates heat, the way a grave radiates silence. Customers drifted away from it without knowing why. Once, passing her hand near it, Bram felt the oath under her sleeve lean toward it, unmistakably, the way a plant leans toward light.

*You'll know his hand,* Sera Voss had said. *You'll know it.*

Bram lit the lamp properly. Barred the door a second time, though once was plenty and twice was superstition. Sat down at her worktable with the bundle in front of her and her sleeves pushed up, the oath visible at last — the fine silver scarring that traced her forearm in characters older than the kingdom, dormant, waiting, aching faintly toward the north.

She unwrapped it.

Oilcloth, then leather, then paper — good paper, heavy stock, the kind cartographers used. And spread flat under the lamplight it showed... a map of the northern reaches. Mountains, passes, a river system rendered in careful blue. Ordinary. Competent. Utterly unremarkable, except for the borders.

Bram stopped breathing.

The borders were decorated — maps always were, vines and flourishes, the pretty nonsense that filled the margins of official work. But these vines weren't nonsense. She knew the pattern. She knew the little conventions hidden in it, the way a leaf curled into a *th*, the way a root split into a double stroke that meant *turn back*. She'd spent six years watching those fingers draw them, correcting her own copies against them, learning a secret script disguised as ornament because her teacher had trusted beauty to hide what plainness couldn't.

Master Corvin's hand. Alive in every margin, woven through every flourish, threading the true road through the pretty parts like a vein of ore through worthless stone.

"Oh, you *bastard*," Bram said softly, to a dead man, in a cellar, alone. "You knew. All this time, you knew, and you went anyway, and you left me a knot to untangle and three years of cord to sell."

The lamp flickered. Under her sleeve, the oath burned — not painfully, not yet, but steadily, insistently, a compass needle finding its north. Toward the map. Toward the mountains. Toward the court beneath.

Toward the door her mentor had sworn her to guard, and apparently, secretly, desperately, meant her to open.

Outside, far to the north, the first snow of the wrong winter was beginning to fall.
