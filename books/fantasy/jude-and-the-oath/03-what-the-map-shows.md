## 3. What the Map Shows

The cellar beneath Bram's shop smelled of tallow, dried yarrow, and the particular damp that came with living below the waterline of a city that had decided magic was a crime and then built its foundations on top of an old witch's spring anyway. Greyhollow was full of contradictions like that. It outlawed the gift and still bought luck-knots by the dozen, so long as the seller called them "decorative."

Bram sat at her worktable with three candles burning — one more than she needed for light and exactly as many as she needed for courage — and unrolled the dead woman's map.

She had not wanted to bring it down here. She had wanted, very sincerely, to burn it. There was a small iron dish by the door that had seen the end of several dangerous things: a letter from a debt collector, a lock of hair she'd once been paid to enchant and hadn't dared return, and on one memorable night, a pair of boots that turned out to belong to a watchman who'd come in plainclothes to buy a fever-charm and stayed to ask too many questions. The dish was well-practiced. The map would have gone into it easily.

But her hands hadn't let go of it.

That was the trouble with oaths, she thought now, turning the parchment under candlelight. They didn't live in your head where you could argue with them. They lived in the body. Her right arm, from wrist to elbow, held the swordspeaker's binding like a brand laid under the skin — old scar tissue shaped like script no living language used anymore — and when she'd tried to feed the map to the flame that afternoon, the oath had *pulled*. Not pain, exactly. More like a dog on a leash catching a scent and leaning, all its weight suddenly going in one direction, dragging the hand holding the leash along whether the hand agreed or not.

So the map was here instead of ash, and Bram was alone in her cellar at midnight, talking to herself because there was nobody else to talk to.

"Right," she said. "Let's see what you are."

The map showed the north country. That much was ordinary enough. She traced the coast first out of habit — Odile Fen had drawn the standard coastal charts every schoolchild memorized, all clean lines and confident little waves, and Bram had spent enough years checking charms against geography that her eye went to shorelines the way other people's went to faces. This coastline was wrong. Or rather, it was right in ways the official charts weren't: the fjords were deeper, the islands numbered differently, and there, two-thirds of the way up, a mountain range that appeared on no guild chart at all, rendered in ink so fine it looked less drawn than grown.

*The Teeth*, someone had labeled them, in handwriting that made Bram's chest do something complicated.

She knew that handwriting.

She knew it the way you know footsteps on your own stairs — before thought, before reason, straight into the bone. The tall ascenders. The way the *e* leaned back like it was eavesdropping. The little hook he put on his capitals because he'd learned to write left-handed during a winter in the Salt Marches when frost took the feeling from his right side and never entirely gave it back, and he'd joked about it afterward, always joked, *a man should keep at least one hand honest,* and Bram had laughed every time because laughing was easier than saying what she actually felt, which was that she would have followed that man into a fire if he'd asked, and eventually did follow him into something worse than fire, and lost him there.

Corvess. Master Corvess Aldane, last teacher of the swordspeakers, three years dead by official count and eleven years dead by any count that mattered, because the last time Bram had seen him alive was the day he walked north and told her to stay behind and she'd obeyed him, which remained the single great regret of her life, ranked comfortably above the time she'd enchanted a tax assessor's quill to write only insults.

"No," she said aloud, to the cellar, to the map, to the dead. "No, that's coincidence. Half the country writes like that."

Half the country did not write like that.

She made herself be methodical, because methodical was the wall she built between herself and the thing clawing at the back of her ribs. She fetched her lens. She trimmed a candle closer. She pinned the map flat with four river stones from the windowsill garden she kept for reasons she'd have described as *practical* and everyone else correctly identified as sentimental.

And then she looked at the borders.

Maps had borders the way books had margins — space for ornament, for flourishes, for the cartographer to show off. This map's border was a running vine of interlace, knotwork of a style Bram recognized from charm-craft: continuous line, no beginning, no end, the kind of pattern that wasn't decoration at all but containment. A border like that didn't hold a picture in. It held something else.

She followed the line with her fingertip, slowly, and began to read.

It was script — the same script that burned along her forearm, the oath-script, the words of binding that swordspeakers carried in their skin because paper could burn and stone could crumble but flesh remembers. She couldn't read most of it; the oath was given whole, not taught piecemeal, and Corvess had died before teaching her the deep grammar, if he ever intended to teach it at all. But some words repeated, and repetition is how anyone learns anything, and by the third circuit of the border Bram could pick out four phrases recurring like a refrain:

*The door beneath. The debt unpaid. The court that weaves the winter. The hand that swore.*

Her finger stopped.

"The hand that swore," she whispered.

The candle flames leaned all together, though the cellar had no draft. Down at the bottom edge of the map, worked so small and so cleverly into the vine-work that she'd taken it for a leaf, was a signature. Not a name. A mark — a stylized sword crossed with a quill, and beneath it, tucked into the flourish, two letters:

*C.A.*

Corvess Aldane.

Bram sat back so fast her chair legs shrieked against the flagstones, and in the silence after, the cellar felt very large and very cold and extremely occupied by everything she'd spent three years not thinking about.

"You knew," she said. Her voice came out rough. She cleared her throat and said it again, steadier, because Corvess had hated whining and she'd inherited that along with everything else. "You knew about the mountain. You drew this. You—" She stopped, started again. "You sent that woman, didn't you? Dying at my stall. Of all the stalls in Greyhollow. Of all the cities."

The map said nothing. Maps were good at that. Corvess had been good at it too — eleven years of questions answered with jokes, with deflections, with *you're not ready, Bramble, and that's not a punishment, it's a promise.* She'd thought the secrecy ended with him. She'd thought grief was the last thing he'd leave her holding.

Apparently he'd left her homework.

She laughed, and it came out wet, and she wiped her face with the heel of her hand and got back to work, because that was also inherited: feel it later, work now, later will keep.

* * *

The hours went strange after that, the way hours do in cellars with no windows.

She copied the border-script onto scrap parchment, matching stroke for stroke, and set her copy beside the one artifact of Corvess's teaching she still owned: a single page of practice script he'd given her at fifteen, when her oath-mark was new and angry and itched like a healing burn. *Copy this until your hand knows it better than your name,* he'd said. *A swordspeaker who can't read her own arm is just a girl with a scar.* She'd copied it four hundred times. She could reproduce it blind, drunk, or mid-argument, and had done all three.

The scripts matched. Not similar. *Matched* — same hand, same pressure, same left-leaning eavesdropping e. The border of the dying stranger's map had been written by Corvess Aldane's own fingers, and then — Bram bent close, breath fogging the lens — over-written. Someone else had worked atop his lines. A second script, finer, colder, threaded through his like wire through wool. Where Corvess's writing pulled toward the mountain, the second script pulled away from it, and the two together created a tension in the parchment she could feel with her fingertips: the faintest vibration, like a plucked string still ringing.

"It's a ward," she murmured. "No. It's a *cage*. He warded the knowledge in." She sat with that. "Or someone caged it after."

Which raised the question she'd been circling all night and finally had to look at straight on: why would Corvess Aldane — who had taught her that the first rule of survival was *never write down what can be spoken, never speak what can be sworn, never swear what can be refused* — draw a map? A map was testimony. A map was evidence. A map was the least Corvess thing imaginable, unless the map itself was the message and the territory was beside the point.

Unless the map wasn't meant to show the way to the mountain at all.

Bram looked at the mountain range, at *the Teeth*, at the fine black peak at their heart marked only with a symbol she now understood: the same closed-door sigil that stood at the head of her oath-script, the sign swordspeakers bore meaning *that which is bound.* The stranger had pressed this map into her hands and said *sold beneath,* and Bram had assumed *beneath the mountain, go there.* But maps had layers. Cartographers knew that better than anyone. And if the border was a cage, then maybe the center was bait — or maybe the center was the lie, and the truth ran all around the edges, hidden in plain sight, waiting for exactly one person in the world to recognize the handwriting.

Waiting for her.

"He wanted me to read the border," she said slowly. "Not follow the map. Read it." Her heartbeat picked up. "Because the border tells you what the door is. The border says *debt.*" She touched the phrase again: *the debt unpaid.* And beneath it, smaller, almost swallowed by the vine: a fifth phrase she'd missed on every previous circuit, worked into a corner knot where the line doubled back on itself.

*The oath is the payment.*

The cellar went very quiet.

Bram looked down at her arm. Under the sleeve, under the bandage she wore over the mark when she went to market, the oath-script had begun to warm. She pushed the sleeve up. In the candlelight the old scar-lines stood dark against her skin, and along their length, faint as dawn behind cloud, they were glowing — not flaring, not burning, nothing so dramatic. Just warming. Waking. Leaning north the way a compass needle leans, if compass needles ached.

"Oh, don't you start," she told her arm.

Her arm continued starting.

It wasn't unbearable. That was almost the worst part. It was a low, patient pull, a tuning-fork hum in the marrow, and it said, in the wordless grammar of oaths: *this way. this way. the debt stands. the hand that swore. come and pay.* Three years she'd kept it quiet with routine and restraint and small harmless magics that barely qualified — and one night with a corpse's map open on her table, and the binding had found its heading again, precise as a hawk.

She wrapped the map away from herself, deliberately, the way you'd close a door on an argument you weren't finished having. The ache dulled but didn't stop. She banked two of the candles, kept one, and climbed the stairs to sit in the dark of the shop proper among her shelves of decorative luck-knots, holding her aching arm like a child holds a hurt one, and let herself finally, finally feel it.

Grief, reopened, is worse the second time, because the first time you at least have shock to cushion it. Now she had three years' worth of questions she'd taught herself to stop asking, and every one of them came up at once, armed. *Why did you go north without me. What did you owe them. Did you sell something, Corvess, did you sell someone, is that why I'm bound, is my oath the payment, am I the coin you left on the table?* He had loved her. She'd stake her life on that; she had, effectively, staked her life on that, daily, for fifteen years. And he had also lied to her by omission for fifteen years, and those two facts sat inside her like stones in the same shoe, and no amount of walking was going to wear them smooth.

Somewhere in the middle of it — somewhere between the third hour and whatever hour comes after, the one with no name — she heard the knock.

Three soft raps at the shop's front door. Paused. Then three more, in the rhythm market folk used to mean *I know you're awake, I watched your candle.*

Bram went still. It was long past the hour when honest customers knocked. The watch knocked louder. Bounty men didn't knock at all.

She rose, took the iron poker from the cold hearth — not for the fire, which was dead, but because a poker in hand made even a lie about being closed sound sincere — and cracked the door to the width of a knife.

Standing in the fog on her step was a woman she half-recognized: sharp-faced, travel-stained, with ink stains to the second knuckle and eyes that had the particular exhausted alertness of someone who had been awake for two days making very bad decisions on purpose. She glanced up and down the empty street once, quick and professional, then looked at Bram.

"You're the charm-seller the frostbitten woman died in front of," the woman said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I'm closed."

"You're also holding a poker behind the door. I can see your reflection in the window glass. Very menacing. Well done." The woman shifted her weight. Somewhere across the city a watch bell rang the hour, muffled by fog. When she spoke again her voice dropped, urgent and quiet. "My name is Odile Fen. Formerly of the Royal Cartographers' Guild, formerly employed, formerly respectable, currently none of those things. And I need you to let me inside, Ms. Vane, because I know exactly whose handwriting is on that map you're hiding — and in about six hours, so will the people who killed the woman who carried it."

The ache in Bram's arm pulsed once, northward, like a second heartbeat answering.

She opened the door.
