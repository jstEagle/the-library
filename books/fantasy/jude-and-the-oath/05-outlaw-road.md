## 5. Outlaw Road

The northern passes out of Greyhollow were called the Shepherd's Teeth, which told you everything you needed to know about them: they bit, they were indifferent to your suffering, and somebody had once thought the name was charming.

Bram had walked them twice before — once as an apprentice, trailing Master Corren with a pack full of surveying tools she wasn't allowed to touch, and once on the worst day of her life, coming south alone with a box of his effects and nothing left to say to anyone. Both times the road had been kind. The early snows hadn't come yet, and the wind had been at her back.

Now the snows came early, and the wind was in her face, and she had company.

"Stop," Odile Fen said for the eleventh time that morning.

Bram stopped. She had learned by now not to ask what for. She turned and found Odile crouched at the edge of the trail with her sketchbook open against her knee, drawing — of all things — a rock.

"It's a cairn," Odile said, catching her look. "Or it was. Somebody knocked it over."

"Somebody knocked it over," Bram repeated.

"That's what I said." Odile's pencil moved fast, sure, recording every fallen stone with the same care she'd give a cathedral. "Cairns don't fall over by themselves. And travelers don't knock them down unless they want other travelers lost." She glanced up, and there was something underneath the dryness of her voice that Bram was learning to read as fear wearing a sensible coat. "Someone came through here ahead of us. Someone who didn't want to be followed. Or someone who wanted *us* followed."

"The watch doesn't leave Greyhollow," Bram said. "They can't afford to. Half their pay comes from market bribes, and bribes don't collect themselves."

"No. But bounties do travel." Odile closed the book and stood, brushing grit from her trousers with brisk, unnecessary motions. "There's been a standing purse for unregistered practitioners since before I left the guild. It's small. Enough to make a hard winter comfortable. There are men who follow the passes every autumn hoping someone like you will come north looking guilty."

"I don't look guilty."

"You look like a woman walking very fast toward a mountain with a forged map in her bag," Odile said. "In this weather, that's practically a confession."

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a shelf of granite that some long-dead shepherd had walled into a rough bothy, half ruin, half shelter. Bram got a fire going small — small enough to hide, big enough to boil the barley — and sat with her hands out while Odile laid out the map between them and studied it by firelight, muttering coordinates under her breath like prayers.

It still unnerved Bram how much the map unnerved *her*. Three days out from Greyhollow and she'd caught Odile checking it four times, running her thumb along the border where Corren's handwriting hid in the weave of the lines, tiny letters braided through the ink like thread through cloth. *The door remembers what the oath forgets.* Over and over, around the margin, patient as water.

"You knew him," Odile said suddenly. Not looking up. "Corren Vasquez. That's your name too — Vasquez. I checked the guild rolls when I decided to find you. He trained you."

"He did."

"What was he like?"

Bram fed a twig to the fire and watched it catch. "Stubborn. Kind in ways that made people angry, because kindness that expects nothing back makes people suspicious. He hummed when he worked. Badly." She smiled despite herself. "He used to say a swordspeaker's oath isn't a weapon, it's a promise that learned to fight. Took me years to understand what he meant."

"And now?"

"Now I think about it every day," Bram said, "and I still don't."

Odile nodded slowly, as if this were a satisfactory answer, which it could only be if satisfaction meant shared confusion. Then she rolled up the map, said "First watch is yours," and lay down with her boots on, because she was the sort of person who slept ready to run.

The night was quiet. Too quiet, Bram would think later. The mountains should have been full of small noises — fox, wind, stone settling. Instead the silence pressed close around the fire like something listening.

She almost woke Odile twice. She didn't. She sat up with her back to the wall and watched the dark, and somewhere past midnight she heard it: voices, far off but closing, carried on the wind the way sound carries before snow.

Four of them. Maybe five. Coming up the pass.

* * *

"They're bounty men," Odile said at first light, crouched beside Bram behind the bothy wall. Below them, the trail switchbacked down through grey scree, and along the trail moved five figures wrapped in mismatched furs, unhurried, spreading out the way men spread when they've done this many times. One of them held something up to the light. Paper. A bill of notice.

"'Wanted: unregistered practitioners,'" Odile murmured. "Dead preferred, alive worth double. They'll have a binder's collar on one of them — iron, cold-forged. Puts out a practitioner's gift like pinching a candle." Her jaw tightened. "I saw one used once. At the guild, on a mapmaker who drew a coastline that hadn't been surveyed yet. He drew things that weren't there, you see. Accurate things." Her voice went flat. "They collared him and marched him through the guild hall so everyone could learn."

Bram's stomach turned. Below, the men had stopped. One crouched at the ruined cairn — Odile's rock — and looked up-slope. Straight at the bothy.

"They know we camped here," Bram said.

"They knew last night. Those voices you heard — that was them letting us hear. Herding." Odile was already moving, stuffing the map inside her coat. "There's a goat track behind the shelf. Steep, but it cuts across the shoulder of the ridge and meets the pass above them. If we move *now*—"

The whistle cut her off. Short, sharp, deliberate. From the ridge *above* them.

Bram turned. Two more figures stood on the skyline against the pale morning, and even at that distance she could see the iron glinting — a collar hanging from one man's fist like a lantern waiting to be lit.

"Seven," she breathed. "Seven for two women and a satchel."

"For a practitioner," Odile corrected grimly. "You're worth triple alive. Congratulations. You're the most valuable thing on this mountain, and I'm the map that proves where you're going." She laughed — a short, bitter sound with no humor in it anywhere. "I told you honesty would cost me. I didn't think it would cost this much, this fast."

The men below started up the switchbacks. The men above started down. Somewhere a third whistle answered from the east, where Bram had thought the track was clear.

"We run anyway," Bram said.

"We run anyway," Odile agreed, and they ran.

* * *

The mountain fought them both ways. Scree slid underfoot; wind drove ice crystals sideways into their eyes; the goat track narrowed until they moved single file with three hundred feet of nothing on the left. Bram's lungs burned. Her scarred arm burned worse — a deep ache under the oath-mark, the old sword-words stirring like coals raked open, waking because her blood was up and her body remembered what it was for.

*Not yet,* she told the arm. *Not unless.*

But the men were faster than they should have been. They knew the ground. Twice Bram caught glimpses of them flanking, cutting angles, patient as weather, and by the time the track spilled onto a broad saddle of bare stone — no cover, no walls, just sky and rock and the wind screaming through — the seven had become a ring, and the ring was tightening.

The leader stepped forward. Big, grey-bearded, unhurried, with the easy stance of a man who had done violence often enough to stop performing it. In his left hand hung the collar: two iron半-circles on a hinge, etched with suppression sigils that made Bram's teeth ache just to look at.

"Ladies," he called over the wind, pleasantly. "No need for anyone to get tired or frostbit. You come down quiet, the mapmaker walks free once the purse clears. Simple sums."

"She's not registered," Odile shouted back, "because registration means the guild owns your hands! You're selling a woman!"

"I'm buying bread, missus. Same as anyone." He shrugged, genuinely, horribly reasonable. "World's made of sums."

And that — that stupid, gentle shrug, that arithmetic — was what broke something loose in Bram.

Because he was right. That was the obscene part. The world *was* made of sums, and she had spent three years living inside them: charms sold cheap, magic hidden, grief folded small enough to fit in a market stall. She had made herself a sum. Charm-witch, minor, harmless, worth copper. And here was a man with a collar doing the same arithmetic out loud, and the answer came to eight silver for her freedom and none for her dignity, and everyone on this mountain thought that was simply how things were.

Her arm was burning.

*The oath is a promise that learned to fight.*

Master Corren's voice, in memory, humming. And beneath the burn, the words themselves — her oath, sworn at nineteen with his hand on her shoulder, the true sword-words that lived in her skin like seeds under bark. Three years she had kept them buried. Three years of fever-charms and luck-knots, magic rationed down to a whisper, power treated like a disease in remission.

The nearest hunter was ten paces away, moving low, hand out with the collar.

Bram spoke.

"*Stand.*"

One word. The oldest word, the first word, the word the swordspeakers had carried since before Greyhollow had walls. It left her mouth and it was not a shout and it was not magic in any way the outlaw city would have recognized — no light, no thunder. Just a word that landed on the world like a blade landing on a whetstone.

The hunter stopped. All of them stopped. Seven men froze mid-stride on bare stone, breath steaming, eyes wide, bodies no longer entirely their own — because the oath-word did not command flesh, exactly; it commanded the truth of a moment, and in that moment the truth was that Bram Vane stood between these men and the woman behind her, and truth, properly spoken, has weight.

For three heartbeats the whole saddle belonged to her.

Then she released it, gasping, and the men stumbled as if waking from a fall, and the leader looked at her — really looked, the arithmetic falling out of his face — and said, quietly, "Swordspeaker," the way another man might say *plague*.

"Walk away," Bram said. Her voice shook. She hated that it shook. She hated far more that underneath the shaking, the word still sang in her chest like a struck bell, warm and enormous, filling spaces in her that three years of hiding had let go hollow. It felt *good*. That was the trap of it. That was what Corren had tried to teach her and she'd been too young to hear: the oath feels good precisely when you most need to be careful, because it is a promise, and promises are sweetest right before they own you.

"Sums have changed," the leader said. He backed away, then turned, and the seven of them went down the slope fast, not running, but not pretending anymore either.

Odile stared at her. Wind whipped her hair across her face. "Three years," she said. "You hid *that* for three years? In a city full of informants? Selling *fever charms*?"

"It seemed safer."

"It seemed — " Odile pressed her mittened hands to her face, dragged them down, and produced, improbably, a laugh. "Right. Fine. Yes. I tracked down the most dangerous woman on the northern passes and asked her to trust me. That's — that's very funny, actually. I'm going to be furious about it later."

"We should move," Bram said. "They'll come back with more, or with a warden's writ, or—"

"Yes. Moving. Good." Odile hoisted her satchel, then paused, and her voice gentled into something rare for her. "You hated it. Saying it. I saw your face."

Bram looked out over the pass — north, toward the mountain that waited at the end of Corren's handwriting — and told the truth because Odile had earned it. "I loved it. That's the problem. For three heartbeats everything was simple. Stand, and the world stands. No sums." She flexed her scarred hand, watching steam rise off it in the cold. "A person could get used to being right like that. And then one day you're not protecting anyone. You're just speaking, because the silence got uncomfortable."

Odile considered this. "So don't get used to it."

"That's the plan."

"Plans fail," Odile said, already trudging north. "That's why I draw maps. Come on, swordspeaker. If those men report what they saw, every hunter between here and the Whitefen will be on our scent by tomorrow. We need distance today, and shelter tonight, and—" She stopped.

Bram nearly walked into her.

Ahead, at the far end of the saddle where the track dipped toward the high valley, smoke rose thin and grey against the snow-light. Not a campfire's careless sprawl. A single column, disciplined, tended — rising from the broken roof of what might have been a shrine, if shrines survived this far north.

And leaning against the boundary stone at the shrine's edge, propped upright like a man who had put himself there carefully before collapsing, was someone in torn fine clothes, red-dark stains down one side, one hand raised — whether in greeting or surrender, Bram couldn't tell.

Odile sighed the sigh of a woman whose plans were failing ahead of schedule. "Well," she said. "Either that's a trap, or that's trouble, and on this road I've stopped believing there's a difference."

Bram's arm ached toward him — toward the smoke, toward the north, the way it had ached since the map first opened in her cellar. She started walking.

"Stay here," she said, "and if it's a trap—"

"If it's a trap," Odile said, following her anyway, "we'll both be idiots together. Move."
