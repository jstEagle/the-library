## 6. The Runaway Lord

Cassian Merrow had been running for eleven days, and in that time he had learned three things about himself.

First: he could not start a fire. Not once. He had the flint, the steel, dry tinder, a book of instructions copied out in his own neat hand — and still, every night ended with him huddled in his own cloak, blowing on embers that died like his dignity.

Second: nobility did not survive contact with the wilderness. His boots were wrong. His coat was wrong. The way he walked was wrong. He had passed two woodcutters on the third day and both had looked at him the way one looks at a cat wearing a hat — with pity, and a faint suspicion of witchcraft.

Third: when it came down to it, he would bleed for a stranger.

That last one had surprised him most. It had surprised him so much that he hadn't noticed he was doing it until afterward, which is how he'd come to be lying in a roadside shrine with a knife-wound under his ribs and someone else's blood drying on his knuckles, staring up at a ceiling fresco of a sky that some long-dead painter had gotten almost right.

The shrine was small and old, tucked into the shoulder of the north road where the pines thickened. It had probably been dedicated to something respectable once. Now its altar held a chipped bowl of rainwater, three candles burned down to stubs, and Cassian's entire remaining fortune, which amounted to four coppers and a hair ribbon that had belonged to Liss.

He was trying very hard not to think about the ribbon.

He was failing.

"Idiot," he told himself, in the voice his father used, which was the only voice available. "Absolute idiot."

The knife-wound made an argument in reply. It was a shallow argument, as wounds went — the man with the knife had been drunk and Cassian had been lucky — but shallow arguments from knives still tended to win. Blood seeped through his shirt in slow, patient pulses. Above him, the painted sky had a sun in one corner and clouds that looked suspiciously like sheep, and Cassian thought: *if I die here, they'll find me under a painting of weather. Father will hate that. He hates anything imprecise.*

Which was, of course, the whole problem, wasn't it. Precision. His family had been precise for six generations. Precise ledgers, precise marriages, precise debts. And somewhere in those ledgers, in ink so old it had turned brown, someone in House Merrow had written down the price of a soul and called it *acquisition*.

Liss's soul. Well — not Liss specifically. A Merrow daughter, three generations back, sold beneath the mountain before she was even born, and every daughter after her, because the debt renewed like a lease. Cassian had found it in the archive room on a Tuesday. He'd been looking for tax records. He had instead found the reason his sister woke screaming some nights, though she'd never once said why, and the reason the mountain road near their estate was always cold, always, even in July.

He had packed a bag by midnight. He'd taken the second half of a map that had been split and sealed in the family vault — a map he was fairly sure he wasn't supposed to understand but did anyway, because cartography ran in the blood on his mother's side — and he'd run.

Eleven days ago. And now this.

* * *

The trouble had started an hour past dusk, when a girl — sixteen at most, mud to her knees, a bundle of stolen bread under her arm — had come pelting into the shrine with two men behind her. Bounty hunters. Cassian knew the type; Greyhollow paid well for "unregistered practitioners," which was a legal phrase meaning *anyone the watch disliked*. The girl had ducked through the shrine door, seen Cassian lying there, and frozen.

"Please," she'd said. "They'll take me south. Please."

And Cassian — who had never thrown a punch in his life, who had been schooled exclusively in fencing with blunted foils against opponents instructed to let him win — had stood up off the floor and put himself between her and the door.

He still didn't know why. That was the thing. There had been no decision in it. His body had simply concluded, without consulting him, that this was what a person did.

It had gone badly, briefly, and then luckily. The drunk one had lunged, Cassian had grabbed the first thing to hand — a candlestick, brass, blessedly heavy — and swung with the full untrained fury of a man who read too many adventure novels. The man had gone down. The other one had hesitated just long enough for the girl to bolt out the back window, and then he'd put a knife into Cassian's ribs on general principle and left, dragging his friend, muttering about waste of an evening.

So: here he was. Alone. Bleeding. Rich in candlesticks.

"You're going to die in a chapel," he informed the ceiling, "and your last possession will be a hair ribbon, and history will remember you as nothing at all."

"I wouldn't count on dying yet."

Cassian's whole body attempted to leave through the top of his head. He twisted toward the doorway, one hand pressed to his ribs, the other scrabbling on the flagstones for the candlestick.

Two figures stood silhouetted against the last grey light. One was tall and sharp-featured, wrapped in a traveling coat that had been good five years ago, carrying herself with the particular posture of someone who had once been important and resented it. The other was broader, quieter, hooded, standing slightly behind — the stance of a person who had learned the hard way to keep exits in view.

"We saw them leaving," the tall one said. She stepped inside, and the candlelight caught her face: tired eyes, ink-stained fingers, an expression that suggested she catalogued everyone she met and had already filed him under *problem*. "A man dragging another man, and a girl vanishing over a wall. We thought we'd check whether anyone needed burying." Her gaze dropped to his ribs. "Burying may still be on the table."

"I'm fine," Cassian said, and fainted.

Not entirely. He fainted about ninety percent, which is to say the world went soft and sideways and he found himself on his back again with no memory of getting there, and the quiet hooded figure was kneeling beside him with a flask at his lips.

"Bite down," she said. Her voice was low and roughened, like a blade kept in a drawer. "It tastes like regret and pine resin. Those are its two main ingredients."

He bit down. It tasted exactly as promised, and then the pain pulled back from a scream to a grumble, and warmth spread out from the wound like a rumor of summer.

"Swordspeaker's work?" the tall one asked quietly.

"For a knife-wound? Charms and poultice." The hooded woman — Bram, the other one called her, and the name snagged something in the back of Cassian's mind — worked quickly, efficiently, hands that had done this a hundred times. "Hold still."

"I am being extremely still," Cassian said. "This is my stillest. I was trained in stillness. There were lessons."

Odile — the tall one, apparently — snorted. It was not a kind sound, but it wasn't cruel either. More the sound of a woman discovering, against her will, that something was funny.

"What were you trained in, precisely?" Odile asked, crouching to look at him properly. Her eyes were doing something methodical to his face. Reading him. "Because you have the accent of the capital's better districts, boots worth more than my horse, and you're bleeding out in a roadside shrine defending a bread thief. Either you're a very committed criminal or you've recently fallen a very long way."

"The second one," Cassian admitted. "Though I'm given to understand the fall started generations ago."

Something changed in Odile's face. Subtle — a stillness, a sharpening. "Generations," she repeated.

"My family has a debt." He said it flatly, because he'd rehearsed saying it flatly, and rehearsal was all that kept his voice level. "To the court beneath the mountain. It's paid in souls. Daughters, specifically. My great-great-grandmother signed something, and the signature renews." He laughed, and hated the sound of it. "I found it in the archives. I was looking for tax records. Do you know what it's like to find out your whole house is built on — on *that*? On a ledger entry?"

Neither of them answered. But Bram's hands had gone still on his bandage, and when he glanced over, her hood had slipped back, and she was looking at him with an expression he couldn't name — something raw and recognized.

"And the girl?" Odile asked, gently redirecting. "The bread thief?"

"She got away?"

"Over the wall, yes."

"Good." And he meant it, and was pleased to discover he meant it. "Then it was worth the knife. Mostly. Ask me again when I can sit up."

"You can't sit up."

"I know. I was making conversation."

* * *

They stayed the night in the shrine, because Bram pronounced him unfit to move until morning and neither Odile nor Cassian was fool enough to argue with her. Cassian drifted in and out while the fire — Bram's fire, lit in thirty seconds flat while he watched with genuine grief for his own failed attempts — threw shadows across the painted sky.

At some point he woke to low voices.

"—the map, then. You're sure."

"It matches. The border script, the watermark, the way the mountains are drawn wrong on purpose." A pause. Paper rustled. "There's supposed to be a second half. The western approaches. This only shows the eastern face of the mountain and the upper galleries."

"That's why we need him."

"He's a *noble*, Odile."

"He's a noble who *ran*. There's a difference. I was a royal cartographer, Bram, and I can tell you the ones who stay are the dangerous ones."

Silence. Then Bram, quieter: "His wound needs tending for days. We don't have days. The snows are early."

"Then ask him. Tonight. While he's grateful."

"While he's *bleeding*, you mean."

"While he hasn't had time to become a liability."

Cassian lay very still and listened to them discuss him like weather, and found — to his mild surprise — that he didn't mind. It had been years since anyone discussed his future without also discussing his inheritance, his marriage prospects, or the family name. Being discussed as a potential liability felt almost like freedom.

He cleared his throat. Both voices stopped.

"Just so you know," he said to the ceiling, "I can hear you, and I agree with the tall one, and I have the other half of the map."

Another silence, longer. Then footsteps, and Bram crouched into view, firelight on a face that was younger than her voice and older than her years, marked along one forearm by a scar that was not a scar — a pattern, deliberate, burned in like a brand in the shape of words he almost, almost could read.

"You have the western half," she said.

"In my coat. Inner pocket. Left side. It's sealed — wax, and a sigil that opens to Merrow blood, which I assume is why they used wax and a sigil rather than, say, a lock, since locks can be picked and bloodlines cannot be argued with." He paused. "I talk a lot when I'm wounded. My tutor called it verbal spillage. I've decided to call it charm."

Odile appeared over Bram's shoulder, eyes bright with a hunger he recognized instantly, because he'd seen it in mirror-glass whenever maps were mentioned. "The western approaches. Drawn by whose hand?"

"A Merrow. Three generations back. Why?"

"Because I forged a copy of that map," Odile said, "seven years ago, for a man who paid me in silence, and I have spent seven years wondering what the original showed." She sat down beside him like a woman settling in for a siege. "Show me."

* * *

The seal took his blood, as advertised — a small nick on his thumb, pressed to dark wax, and the fold fell open like something exhaling. Odile spread both halves together on the flagstones and went silent in a way that made Bram glance at her twice.

Cassian watched them study it and felt the strangest sensation: that the paper was warmer than the room. That the ink had shifted since the last time he'd looked. He decided not to mention this, on the grounds that mentioning it would require admitting he'd stared at the map for hours in the dark like a madman, and he had a reputation to build.

"The route goes under," Bram said finally. Her finger traced a line through the western galleries, down and around, avoiding the grand stair entirely. "Whoever drew this wanted a way in that the Court wouldn't watch."

"Our ancestor did more than sell a soul," Cassian said. "She tried to buy one back. There's a letter in the vault — half a letter. She writes about a spindle." He looked up. "I don't know what it means. But the map's annotations are all about the loom halls. Where the guards thin. Where the threads are stored."

Bram had gone very pale. Not wounded-pale. *Recognized*-pale, as if he'd named something she carried too.

"You know what a spindle means," Cassian said slowly.

"I know what it costs to find out." She stood, abruptly, and walked to the shrine door, and stood looking out at the falling dark.

Odile caught his eye and shook her head — *later, don't push* — and Cassian, who had spent twenty-two years learning exactly when not to push, nodded.

Instead he reached into his coat's other pocket and drew out the hair ribbon. Faded blue. He turned it over in his fingers.

"Liss," he said, before he could decide not to. "My sister. She's alive — I'm nearly certain she's alive, the debt keeps the soul, it doesn't spend it, the letter says so — but she doesn't sleep whole nights anymore, and she's thread-thin, and our parents tell her it's nerves." His hand closed around the ribbon. "I'm going to get her back. I don't know how. I have a map, a knife wound, four coppers, and no ability whatsoever to make a fire. But I'm going."

The fire crackled. Outside, the first flakes of the season's real snow began ticking softly against the shrine roof.

Bram came back from the door. She looked at him a long moment — at the ribbon, at the map halves joined on the stones, at whatever it was she saw when she looked at people who had lost things.

"We leave at first light," she said. "You'll slow us down."

"I will absolutely slow you down."

"You'll complain."

"Constantly. I have a gift."

"And if the Wardens find us—"

"Then they'll find a man with a candlestick," Cassian said, "and they should fear him accordingly."

Something happened to Bram's mouth then — brief, unwilling, gone in a heartbeat — that on any other face would have been called a smile. Odile rolled her eyes at both of them, gathered the map with the tenderness of a mother collecting infants, and announced that someone had to take first watch and it might as well be her since clearly no one else in this company could be trusted awake.

Later, wrapped in his cloak by Bram's fire — his first successful fire in eleven days, and it hadn't even been his — Cassian watched the two of them settle into the dark corners of the shrine and thought about the word *family*, which had meant, for his entire life, a building and a ledger and a weight.

It was maybe the fourth or fifth thing he'd learned about himself on this road, and the one that frightened him most: that he already didn't want to do the rest of it alone.

Above him, the painted sky shed its painted light. And far below the mountain, in halls that smelled of wool and cold, something turned over in its sleep — and began, patiently, to weave.
