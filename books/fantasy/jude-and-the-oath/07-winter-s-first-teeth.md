## 7. Winter's First Teeth

The snow began as a rumor at midday and became a verdict by dusk.

Odile Fen had spent eleven years reading maps the way priests read scripture, and she knew what the sky was telling them: *you are not making the pass tonight.* The clouds over the northern shoulder of the Grey Teeth had that flat, bruised look she'd learned to fear during her survey years — the look that meant the mountain intended to bury something, and hadn't yet decided what.

"There," she said, pointing with her chin, because her hands were both buried to the wrist in her coat and had been for an hour. "Waystation. See the chimney?"

Bram Vane squinted through the whiteout. "That's not a chimney. That's a dead tree."

"It's a chimney with ambitions. Come on."

The third member of their party said nothing, because Cassian Merrow had been saying very little for two days now — ever since the shrine, ever since he'd pressed his half of the map into Bram's hands with blood still tacky on it and asked, in the smallest voice Odile had ever heard from a man wearing a noble's ring, whether they'd mind if he came along. He walked where they told him to walk and stopped when they stopped and looked, mostly, at his own boots.

The waystation was exactly what Odile had promised: low, stone-walled, built by some long-dead road-warden who had understood that the mountains eat travelers and that a roof is the only mercy worth building. The door stuck. The hearth smoked until Cassian climbed onto the roof and cleared a bird's nest out of the flue, which was the first useful thing any of them had seen him do, and when he came back down gray with soot and quietly pleased about it, Odile decided — provisionally, pending further evidence — that she liked him.

By full dark the wind was screaming against the shutters like something owed money, and the three of them were arranged around a fire that Bram had coaxed out of damp wood using, Odile suspected, a charm small enough to deny afterward.

"Right," Odile said, unrolling her bedroll with the crisp authority of someone who has slept in worse places on purpose. "Ground rules. If we're snowed in together, we're going to be snowed in *honestly*. I've spent two days watching you two lie by omission at each other, and it's exhausting. It's like watching two people play chess by mail. Stories first. Then sleep."

Bram raised one eyebrow. It was, Odile reflected, a very good eyebrow. She'd have made a fortune drawing it.

"You want us to trade secrets," Bram said. "Around a fire. Like children."

"I want us to trade secrets like adults, which means we'll pretend we're doing it ironically so nobody has to cry." Odile held up her hands. "I'll go first, since I'm the disgraced one and therefore have nothing left to lose but my dignity, and frankly the watch took that years ago along with my seal and my drafting table."

* * *

She told them about the map.

Not this map — the *first* map. The one she had drawn at twenty-four with steady hands and a guild commission and no idea what she was actually holding.

"The court's chancellery sent me north with a survey team. Standard work. Rivers, passes, elevation lines. And then one day the senior warden — Hollow Warden, though I didn't know the word then, they wore plain gray and smiled too easily — brings me a partial chart and asks me to finish it. Copywork, he says. A cartographer's dream, being trusted with something old." She turned her hands toward the fire. "So I finished it. Beautifully. I matched the hand, I aged the ink, I filled in the gaps the way you fill in a damaged fresco — with what *ought* to be there. Borders woven with knotwork. A door beneath a mountain. Paths that only make sense if you already know where they go."

"And you didn't know," Bram said.

"I didn't know. That's the part I can't forgive myself for, actually. Not that I lied — lying was the job, eventually. That I did the finest work of my life for people whose names I never learned, and it took me four years to understand what I'd drawn." Her voice stayed level; she'd practiced that. "A girl went missing from my street that winter. Just one girl, one street, nothing anyone connected. But I keep thinking: my map went somewhere. Things move along maps. That's what maps are *for*."

The fire popped. Outside, the wind found a loose shutter and worried it like a dog with a bone.

"So when I saw your stranger's map in that pawnshop window — the border knotwork, the door — I knew my own stitching the way a mother knows a child's cough," Odile finished. "I've been trying to buy it back ever since. You beat me to it by about six hours and a dying woman."

Bram was quiet for a moment. "You could have just stolen it."

"From *you*?" Odile snorted. "You sleep with a sword under your blanket and your eyes open. Besides—" and here she gestured vaguely at the room, the storm, the shared danger, "—this seemed more efficient. Honesty in exchange for passage. I told you that in Greyhollow and I'm telling you again under oath of blizzard."

"That's not a real oath."

"All oaths are real if the weather enforces them."

Cassian made a sound. It might have been a laugh, strangled young in its cradle. Both women turned to look at him, and he ducked his head, soot still in his hairline, and said, "Sorry. It's just — my tutor used to say that about contracts."

"Your tutor sounds smarter than most nobles' tutors," Odile said, and then, because fairness cut both ways and she'd started this: "Your turn, Lord Merrow. Or whatever you'd rather be called. We've got all night and possibly several days."

* * *

He didn't want to talk about Liss. Odile could see that from across the fire the way she could see a false contour line — the little hesitation before the name, the way his hands went still in his lap.

But he talked anyway, which Odile privately filed away as the single most important thing she'd learn about him that night.

"She was older than me by two years. Which meant, in our house, that she was responsible for everything and allowed nothing." He stared into the flames. "The Merrows have an arrangement with — I didn't know what to call it. Nobody called it anything. There's a ledger. Old. My father showed it to me when I turned nineteen, the way his father showed him. One entry per generation. A name, a date, a payment received. I thought it was a mortgage record or something. Some land debt." His jaw worked. "It was Liss's great-grandmother. And then her grandmother. And then — the last page, the newest entry, the ink barely dry —"

"Liss," Bram said softly.

"Liss." He said it like setting down something heavy. "My father didn't even argue when I confronted him. That was the worst part. I'd rehearsed the argument for days. And he just looked tired and said, *it keeps the rest of us safe, Cassian. It has always kept the rest of us safe.* As if that settled it. As if safety were a thing you could pay for with someone else." He shook his head. "I ran that night. I'd been running for a month when you found me. I kept telling myself I was going back with proof, with leverage, something. Mostly I was just going in circles, bleeding."

"The shrine," Bram said.

"I thought shrines were safe. Old ones. Nothing watches an old shrine." A ghost of a smile. "Apparently something does. Something with a knife."

"Old gods get bored," Odile offered. "Or hungry. Or they simply object to being used as a barn."

That got a real laugh out of him — brief, surprised out of him, gone again — but it was real, and Odile counted it.

"Do you think she's alive?" Bram asked. Gently. The way you'd ask about weather you couldn't change.

Cassian was quiet long enough that the wind filled the silence for him. "Her letters stopped two winters ago. Before that they were getting strange — short, like she was writing with cold hands. The last one said she'd taken a position in the north. House business. She signed it *L*, not *Liss*. She always signed Liss." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious about it. "I don't know what I believe. I know what I'm going to do regardless."

"Well," Odile said, and pulled her blanket tighter, "that's the healthiest delusion I've heard all year."

* * *

Bram's story, when it finally came — after Odile had fed the fire twice and Cassian had fallen into the doze of the genuinely exhausted and been prodded awake — came out sideways, the way water comes out of a cracked jug.

She talked about her mentor the way you talk about a house you grew up in: specific, unsentimental, and clearly beloved.

"Marek. Marek Solenne. He taught me swordspeaking — the old craft, the words you put into steel. Except by the time I met him, speaking a word could get you hanged in Greyhollow, so what he actually taught me was how *not* to speak. How to hold it. Three years I trained with him and he never once let me say a whole oath aloud." Her mouth twisted. "I used to think it was cruelty. Restraint as punishment. Now I think he was terrified of something, and I was too young and too angry to ask what."

"What happened to him?" Cassian asked, blinking sleep away.

"He went north. Said he had unfinished business with an old debt. Told me to mind the shop, which was a joke, because we didn't have a shop, we had a room above a dyer's that smelled like vinegar." Bram's voice went flat and careful, the voice of someone carrying a full cup across a crowded floor. "He never came back. Two years later the oath on my arm started aching whenever I faced north, like a compass needle made of scar tissue. And then a dying woman put a map in my hands written partly in his handwriting, and now I'm in a waystation in the Teeth following it."

Odile watched her. "You think he's alive."

"I think the map wants me to think he's alive." Bram poked the fire harder than the fire deserved. "Which is worse, if you want to know. A dead man can't be bait."

"A living man can't either, technically," Odile said. "People aren't bait. They're just used as it. There's a distinction and I intend to hide behind it."

For a moment — one unguarded moment, there in the firelight with the storm sealing them in like wax on a letter — Bram laughed. Short, rusty, real. Odile thought: *there you are.* Three years of hiding, and it was still in there, the person under the oath-scar and the market stall and the luck-knots sold for copper.

Then Bram caught herself laughing, looked faintly betrayed by her own mouth, and said, "Sleep. Someone should take first watch, and I nominate whoever isn't me."

"Democracy in action," Odile muttered, and took the first watch, because she'd never once in her life won that vote and saw no reason to start pretending otherwise.

* * *

Here is what Odile would not say aloud, then or perhaps ever:

Watch was the part she loved.

Not the cold, and not the responsibility, and certainly not the wind performing its opera through every gap in the masonry. What she loved was sitting awake while other people slept, and letting herself feel — briefly, cautiously, like testing ice with one foot — the unfamiliar weight of *not being alone.*

Eleven years since the forgery. Four since the guild stripped her seal. In all that time she had traveled with caravans, shared inns, drunk with strangers, and trusted precisely no one, because trust was a contour line and hers had been erased. But tonight two near-strangers had handed her their worst stories like coins across a table, and the strange thing — the thing she kept turning over like an unfamiliar coin herself — was that it hadn't cost her anything. It had *paid*. Warmth against the cold. That was all. That was everything.

She checked the map.

They kept it wrapped in oilcloth inside Bram's pack, but Bram had left it out on the table that evening, spread flat under a stone, because — Odile suspected — none of the three of them could quite stop looking at it. She lifted the stone now and held the parchment to the firelight.

And stopped.

The route they'd drawn — Odile's route, inked in her own hand two nights ago in Greyhollow, showing the pass, the waystation, the glacier crossing beyond — had changed.

Not smudged. Not faded. *Changed.* New lines threaded through the border knotwork, fine as hair, in an ink that gleamed faintly blue-gray in the firelight. They branched off their marked path and wound northward, up and over, through country Odile knew for certain was unmapped, because she had *tried* to map it once and the survey party had turned back citing weather that had never existed.

Three paths. She counted them twice. Three lines, converging on the same point beneath the mountain's crown.

She stood there a long time, holding the map, listening to Bram breathe and Cassian murmur something in his sleep — a name, maybe, or half of one — and feeling the warmth in her chest cool by careful degrees into something older and more familiar.

Unease. The cartographer's oldest companion. The feeling that the territory was moving under the map.

She woke Bram without ceremony, because some things you didn't sit on alone.

"Vane. Your map's been drawing."

Bram came awake the way Odile imagined she did everything — instantly, silently, one hand already finding the sword under her blanket. She crossed to the table, took the parchment, and went very still.

"That's not our route," she said.

"No."

"Those lines weren't there."

"No."

Bram traced one path with a fingertip, and Odile watched the oath-scar on her forearm flush faintly red through the sleeve, glowing toward the north like a banked coal remembering fire.

"They shift when we sleep," Bram said quietly.

"Then tomorrow," Odile said, taking the map back with hands that were steadier than she felt, "we stay awake longer, and we find out who — or what — has been doing our drafting for us."

Outside, the storm screamed. Inside, the fire burned low, and on the table between them, patient as a spider, the map waited for morning.

Neither of them slept much after that.
