## 8. The Village That Remembers

The village appeared out of the snow like something remembered rather than built.

Bram saw it first as a smudge of woodsmoke against the grey afternoon — a cluster of slate roofs huddled in the fold of the mountain's shoulder, terraced fields buried under drifts, a single crooked bell tower leaning east as if it had spent centuries listening for something. Goats watched them from a snow-crusted wall with the flat, unimpressed stares of animals who had seen every kind of traveler and been impressed by none of them.

"Please tell me that's real," Odile said. She was walking with her map case hugged to her chest like a child, her lips blue at the edges. "I've been navigating by optimism for two days and I'm running low on stock."

"It's real," Cassian said. "Or we've all started hallucinating the same village, which would at least be efficient."

"Keep talking and I'll leave you in the next drift," Bram said, without heat. It was the longest conversation they'd managed since the waystation, three days of climbing through passes where the wind spoke in a language of teeth. The map — the shifting, breathing, ink-that-moved-at-night map — had led them here, its lines crawling northward with an insistence none of them could ignore and none of them liked discussing.

She'd stopped discussing it after the second morning, when she woke to find the route drawn in what looked suspiciously like her mentor Aldric's looping hand, though he'd been dead three years, and dead men didn't update their cartography.

The village had one street, packed snow over old stone, lined with houses whose doors were painted with symbols Bram didn't recognize — spirals, mostly, wound tight as knots. A woman splitting firewood straightened when they passed, set down her axe with deliberate care, and looked at Bram's left arm.

Not her face. Her arm.

"Grandmother," the woman called toward the nearest house, not taking her eyes off Bram. "You'll want to come out."

* * *

They were given tea before they were given answers, which Bram decided was either good manners or a strategy. The grandmother — Maren, they called her, though she corrected no one and answered to nothing — was small and iron-haired and sat by her hearth with the stillness of someone who had learned to conserve everything, including opinions. Half the village crowded into her kitchen behind them, pretending not to listen and doing it badly.

Odile drank her tea like a woman being paid to do it. Cassian held his cup in both hands and tried to look like a person who belonged in a mountain kitchen rather than a noble's son in an expensive coat gone threadbare at the elbows. He failed charmingly.

"You came up the Wraith Pass," Maren said.

"We did," Bram said.

"No one comes up the Wraith Pass. Not in autumn. Not in any season, really." The old woman turned her hands around her cup. "Show me your arm, swordspeaker."

The word went through the room like a dropped glass. Bram felt every eye land on her, felt the oath under her skin stir — it did that lately, a slow turning-over, like something waking from a long sleep. She'd kept it covered since Greyhollow. Sleeves, wraps, a story about an old burn if anyone asked. Nobody in the city ever asked twice; magic was illegal there, and curiosity about it was only slightly less so.

But these weren't city people. And the map had dragged her here for a reason.

She pushed back her sleeve.

The mark ran from wrist to elbow: a blade rendered in fine silvered lines, its edge written through with words in a script she couldn't read but had memorized anyway, letter by letter, in the years since Aldric had pressed his thumb to her forearm and spoken her binding into her skin. The oath-script caught the firelight and seemed, as always, slightly warmer than the rest of her.

The room made a sound — not quite a gasp. More like a held breath finally released.

"So it didn't die out," Maren said softly. "We were told it died out."

"Aldric Vane was the last," Bram said. "He raised me. He's dead now." Three years, and saying it still had a splinter in it.

"He was never the last," Maren said. "He was the last who *left*. There's a difference." She looked up, and her eyes were pale grey and very old. "Sit properly, girl. You're going to want to hear this sitting down. All of you. Even the lordling."

"I'm not—" Cassian began.

"You have a Merrow face," said a man near the door. "My father sold wool to your grandfather. I know a Merrow face."

Cassian closed his mouth.

* * *

Maren told it the way old people tell stories that are also warnings: slowly, with pauses for tea, letting each piece settle before laying down the next.

Before Greyhollow, before the outlawing, before the cities got frightened and wrote their fear into law — there had been the door. Beneath this mountain, beneath all these mountains; the villages had different names for it and agreed on none except that it opened downward into a court where winter was woven. The Pale Court, the elders called it. They wove the seasons' cloth, or had once — spring and summer and harvest too, in the old days, whole bolts of weather spun out on looms of bone and light.

"But they grew greedy," Maren said. "Or hungry. Hungry is worse. Greed takes things; hunger takes *people*. They began buying souls. Not stealing — they paid, always, some bargain sweet enough to make a fool sign. And every soul they bought was another thread on their loom, and every thread made the winters longer and colder, until the long winter nearly swallowed the world."

"And the swordspeakers?" Bram asked. Her voice came out strange.

"The swordspeakers were sworn to stand at the door and keep it shut." Maren's gaze fixed on the mark. "That's all. That's the whole of it, girl. Not to open it. Not to go down and bargain. To guard. Your order was a lock, and the words on your arm are the mechanism. Spoken whole, they seal the gate so hard that not even the court can pick it. That's why the court feared you. Not your sword-words — those are just noise, pretty noise. They feared the *closing*."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, a goat complained about the snow.

"That's not what Aldric taught me," Bram said slowly.

"What did he teach you?"

"To speak. That the oath was power. That a swordspeaker's word could unmake wards, break bindings, open what was shut." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "He taught me the opening. Every lesson, the opening. I thought— I thought the mark pointed down because that's where my enemies were."

Maren studied her for a long moment. "And where did he learn it?"

"He never said. He talked about everything else. His teachers, his travels, the wars. Never the court. I thought it hurt him too much to remember." Bram turned her arm over, looking at the underside of the mark, as if Aldric might be hiding there. "He told me once — drunk, the only time I ever saw him drunk — that he owed a debt he couldn't pay and had spent his life making sure I wouldn't inherit it. And then he bound me with the biggest debt there is."

"He didn't bind you to the debt," Maren said. "He bound you to the *door*. Those are different chains, whatever they feel like." She leaned forward. "Tell me how he died."

"Fever. In Greyhollow. Ordinary fever, ordinary death. He'd been sick for months and hid it, and then one morning he wasn't hiding it anymore." Bram wrapped her hands around her cooling tea. "His last words to me were 'don't go north.' So naturally I've spent the past week going north."

The old woman almost smiled. "Aldric Vane always did give instructions designed to fail. It's how he apologized without apologizing."

"You knew him." It wasn't a question. Bram knew it wasn't, from the way the word landed.

"He stayed here. Winter of the year the pass closed — you'd have been a baby, or not born. He came down from the mountain white as bone and half-mad, and he stayed with us a month, and he told us what he'd done." Maren's voice flattened. "He'd gone below, swordspeaker. Through the door he should have sealed. He went down and bargained with the Pale Court, alone, and whatever he traded them — we never learned what — he came back up having broken faith. The first swordspeaker in nine generations to open instead of close. We struck his name from the remembering-songs. He asked us to."

The kitchen was very quiet. Odile had stopped drinking. Cassian had gone carefully, professionally blank — the expression of a boy raised in a house where feelings were furniture you moved out of guests' sightlines.

"What did he buy?" Bram heard herself ask.

"We don't know. He wouldn't say. But he came up the mountain weeping, and he took a child's boots from our stores because his own had burned away below, and he walked south and never came back." Maren reached across and touched Bram's marked arm, one finger, feather-light. "Whatever he bought, he spent the rest of his life paying for it. And then he gave the bill to you."

* * *

They slept that night in the village's guest house, a low room above the goat barn that smelled honestly of goats and dishonestly of lavender. Odile fell asleep mid-sentence, sitting up, her sketchbook open on her knees. Cassian lay staring at the rafters, and Bram pretended not to notice that he was mouthing his sister's name over and over, a rosary of guilt.

She couldn't sleep. She climbed down to the common room and found Maren there, awake, feeding the fire, as if old women simply grew out of hearths at a certain age.

"The map," Bram said, and laid it on the table between them.

Maren looked at it for a long time. Her face did something complicated. "Where did you get this?"

"A dying woman put it in my hands. She said her soul was sold beneath."

"And the borders? Look at the borders again. Properly."

Bram looked. She'd looked a hundred times already, at the interlaced knotwork framing the mountains, at the tiny woven letters threaded through it — Aldric's hand, unmistakable, the same hand that had written her lessons and her birthday notes and one apologetic, illegible last letter. "It's his work."

"It's his *debt*." Maren traced the border without touching it. "This is a court-map, girl. Drawn below, in the court's own style. The court gives such maps to those it wants walking its road. Your teacher didn't draw this. He *signed* it. Whatever bargain he made down there — this is the shape of it. The court has been holding his payment all these years. And now it's holding yours."

The fire cracked. Bram thought of the vision waiting at the sealed gate — the thing she hadn't told the others yet, the glimpse the mountain had shown her through stone: Aldric alive, bent over a bargaining table, his voice worn down to gravel. *Hope is the cheapest coin,* she'd thought then. Now she understood the exchange rate.

"He's alive," Bram said. "Down there. Isn't he."

"I think," Maren said gently, "that the court keeps what it's owed, and shows it to whoever carries the bill. That isn't mercy, child. That's a shopkeeper arranging his window."

Bram stared at the map until the knotwork blurred. Legacy, she thought. Everyone talked about legacy like it was a gift — a sword handed down, a name, a song. Nobody mentioned that it was also a rope, tied around your waist before you could walk, with the other end somewhere you'd spend your life falling toward.

"My oath," she said. "If I speak it whole at the gate, it seals the door forever. Yes?"

"Yes. And the court knows it. Which is why they'll do anything to stop you reaching the gate — or anything to make sure you reach it *willingly*, on their terms, with their price in your pocket." Maren banked the fire. "Get some sleep, swordspeaker. The pass opens tomorrow, and the snows are coming early this year. The long winter's finishing its fall. Whatever you mean to do below, you'll want to do it before the door freezes shut for a century."

* * *

Bram went back up to the loft and stood a while in the dark, listening to Odile breathe and Cassian murmur Liss's name, and thought about a drunk old man crying on a mountain with burnt boots.

*Don't go north.*

She touched the mark on her arm. It was warm. It had always been warm. She had spent three years thinking of that warmth as a gift, and one evening learning it was a leash — and now, lying in the dark above a goat barn at the top of the world, she decided that leashes pulled both ways.

If the court had kept Aldric alive all these years as bait, then they had made one mistake, the same mistake every creditor makes eventually: they had shown her the collateral.

In the corner of the loft, the map shifted in its case with a sound like pages turning themselves. The ink was moving again. Drawing the road down.

Bram watched the new line crawl northward through the dark — through the pass, toward the glacier, toward the door carved with her oath — and understood, finally and completely, that she was not following the map.

The map was following *her*.

And somewhere beneath the mountain, something ancient and patient had just felt a swordspeaker's mark turn toward home.
