## 9. Hunters on the Slope

There is a particular kind of silence that lives on a glacier, and I had always assumed it was peaceful.

I had read about glaciers. That was the trouble with my entire upbringing, really — I had *read* about everything. Glaciers, duels, grief, love. I could recite the melting points of mountain ice and the proper form for a cavalry saber and six different poems about heartbreak, and not one page of any of it prepared me for the sound a glacier makes at dawn: a deep groan somewhere under the world, like an enormous animal turning over in its sleep and deciding, grudgingly, to keep dreaming.

"It's settling," Odile said, when Bram flinched at the sound. "The ice. It does that."

"Does it ever *stop* settling?"

"Not while the winter keeps falling." She said it lightly, but her eyes went north, where the sky had been the wrong color for three days now — a pale, bruised grey that never quite committed to snowfall and never quite let the sun through. The long winter finishing its descent. We were racing it, and losing.

We crossed the ice field in single file, which was Bram's rule and which she enforced with the particular grimness of someone who had once watched a man step into a crevasse because he wanted to walk beside his friend. Bram led, her oath-scarred arm bare despite the cold — she claimed the mark needed air, though I suspected she simply liked watching it. The old swordspeaker's brand ran from her wrist to her elbow like a river system seen from very high up, and on cold mornings it glowed faintly gold, as if remembering fire. Odile walked second with her maps wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like a second heartbeat. I took the rear, because I was the heaviest, because I was the best-fed, and because — though nobody said this part aloud — if the ice broke, it should break under the one of us who had spent his life being useless in comfortable rooms.

I want to be honest here, since honesty has become something of a discipline among us, practiced daily like scales on a fiddle and about as pleasant. On that morning, walking the glacier with the wind combing frost into my beard, I was thinking about my sister.

Not Liss. I am trying to think of Liss less as a wound and more as a destination; Odile says this is called "coping," and Bram says it is called "getting on with it," and both of them say it in voices that suggest they have considerably more experience with the exercise than I do. No — that morning I was thinking about Liss when she was small, when she used to hide frogs in Father's slippers, and how she would laugh so hard she hiccupped, and how the last time I saw her whole and warm she was seventeen and waving from a window, and I did not wave back because I was angry with her about some trifling thing. A broken vase. I cannot even remember which vase. That is the cruelty of it. Seventeen years of sisterhood, and the final image is me scowling over a vase.

So absorbed was I in this that I nearly missed what Bram caught instantly.

She stopped so abruptly that Odile walked into her back, and Bram's arm came up — scarred side toward the wind — and her hand closed around the hilt of a sword she did not carry. Three years of selling luck-knots will teach you to keep your hands empty. Some habits survive anything except wanting them to.

"What is it?" Odile whispered.

"Smoke." Bram tilted her chin. "Behind us. Two fires' worth, banked cold. Someone camped last night and didn't want to be seen."

"Travelers," I offered. "Pilgrims. Very tidy pilgrims."

Bram looked back at me, and there was no unkindness in her face, only arithmetic. "Tidy pilgrims don't bank their fires under slate. That's a warden habit. My mentor taught it to me — he learned it fighting them." She paused. "He also told me they never travel in fewer than six."

*Six.* The number sat down in my stomach and stayed there.

* * *

We made perhaps two more miles before the mountain gave them their opening.

It was a place where the glacier narrowed between a wall of blue ice and a shelf of black rock hung with frozen waterfalls — beautiful, in the way that everything up here was beautiful and hostile, like a court portrait of someone who hates you. Bram hesitated at the mouth of the narrows, chewing her lip, clearly weighing whether to climb the rock shelf instead. Then the light changed.

That is the only way I can describe it. The bruised-grey sky dimmed by a single shade, all at once, evenly, as if a hand had passed over a lamp. And out of that new shadow, on the ridge above the ice wall, figures stood up. They hadn't been lying down. They had simply decided, in unison, to exist.

Hollow Wardens. Six of them, in mail the colour of old snow, and on their breasts, stitched in silver thread gone slightly green with age, the crest of House Merrow: a heron standing in shallow water, spear upright.

My family's colors. Worn by men who sold souls to the dark under a mountain.

"Cassian." Bram's voice came flat and low. "Cassian, look at me. Not them. *Me.*"

I was looking at them. I want that recorded accurately, because afterward — in the waystation, in the village, in every retelling — there was a version of events where I fought bravely and a version where I fell heroically, and neither is true. What happened is that I looked at the heron on that first warden's breastplate and my body made a decision without consulting me, and the decision was *no.*

I knew these men. Not by name — or rather, yes, by name. The tall one on the left with the crossbow was Ser Aldric Vane-Holt, who had eaten dinner at our table four times a year for my entire childhood and once taught me to whistle through a blade of grass. The short one beside him was Corren, my father's master-of-wards, who had carried me on his shoulders through the harvest festival when I was five. These were not monsters out of a story. These were men who knew my favorite dessert. They wore my house on their chests, and they had come across a hundred miles of killing cold to bring back the runaway heir and burn whatever he'd attached himself to, and my legs —

My legs would not hold a stance. My hand found my sword grip and stayed there, resting, like a guest who won't commit to sitting down. Somewhere very far away Bram was shouting something — *down, get behind the rock* — and I heard it perfectly and understood none of it, because understanding requires believing you will still be alive in ten seconds, and I had stopped believing that somewhere around the moment Ser Aldric raised the crossbow.

Here is what cowardice actually feels like, since the ballads get it wrong. It doesn't feel like running. Running is at least motion, and motion is at least a choice. Cowardice feels like glass. Like being sealed inside a bell jar while the battle happens on the other side, close enough to touch, silent enough to watch. You see your own hands shaking and you think, distantly, *how embarrassing,* and then you think nothing at all, because thought has left too, and all that remains is the animal arithmetic of *not yet, not yet, not yet.*

The first bolt hit the ice a hand's width from Odile's ear and burst into glittering powder.

"MOVE!" Bram roared, and Odile moved, and I — I stood there, in the open, in the narrows, wearing the expression I imagine a rabbit wears when the hawk's shadow arrives and the rabbit's entire ancestry rises up through its spine and whispers *freeze, we've survived worse than this by doing absolutely nothing.*

The wardens came down the slope in a line, unhurried, professional, boots crunching in eerie unison. Ser Aldric reloaded as he walked. Corren drew steel. And I watched them come with my sword half-drawn and my feet rooted in the ice like something planted, something decorative, something that had never in its life been asked to be brave and had answered accordingly.

Odile saved my life. I have had years to arrange that sentence into humility, and I still cannot say it without my ears going hot, so let me at least say it properly.

She threw herself off the rock shelf.

Later, reconstructing it, I understood the geometry: the wardens were converging on the narrows, funneling toward Bram and me, leaving the low flank by the frozen waterfall briefly unwatched — briefly, a few seconds, the kind of gap a careful person would note and dismiss as insufficient. Odile Fen, disgraced cartographer, liar, forger of the map that started all of this, looked at those seconds and jumped. She slid down the ice slope on her hip with her satchel clutched to her chest, hit the hard pack at the bottom, rolled, came up on her knees directly in Corren's path — and drove her knife, not at him, but at the rope of frozen waterfall beside him, striking exactly where a hairline crack already glowed faintly blue.

Twenty tons of ancient ice came off the falls.

I remember the sound. Everyone remembers the sound. It was not a crash; crashes end. This was a long white roar, a held organ-note, the mountain clearing its throat, and then the world was snow-smoke and tumbling shards and Corren vanishing under an avalanche he had walked beside for thirty years without ever learning its temper. The concussion knocked the remaining wardens from their feet. It knocked me from mine, which at least solved the problem of my legs.

And Odile — Odile knelt in the settling powder with her right hand buried to the wrist in the drift where Corren had fallen, because she had reached for him. After everything. After he'd helped forge the warrant for her arrest. Her hand had gone out to catch a falling man, and the fall had closed over it instead, and the cold up there is not cold as the lowlands understand it; it is a living hunger, and it had taken hold of her fingers and begun, quietly, to keep them.

Bram got her free. Bram always gets people free; it is the family business she actually chose. She hauled Odile backward out of the snow, and Odile screamed — a short, furious, embarrassed sound, the scream of someone angrier at the noise than at the pain — and when Bram stripped the mitten away, the hand underneath was white as the drifts, the fingers waxy and stiff, five small sculptures of themselves.

"Frostbite," Bram said, in the voice of a physician delivering news she has delivered too often. "Deep. We need heat, now, or we lose the fingers."

"The map," Odile said. Her teeth were chattering so hard the words came out in pieces, but she was already, even then, even kneeling in the ruin of the avalanche with her hand dying, patting at her chest with her good arm. "Is the — is the map—"

"You absolute *fool*—" Bram began, and stopped, because there was no insult adequate. There never is, for Odile.

* * *

Ser Aldric was dead. So were two others; the falls had taken them without malice or preference. The remaining three withdrew up the slope, dragging Corren — alive, I learned later, half-buried and howling — and I stood amid the wreckage with my sword finally, fully, uselessly drawn, and watched them go, and felt nothing whatsoever, which frightened me more than the fight had.

Bram built a fire in the lee of the rock shelf with hands that did not shake, because of course they didn't. She cut away Odile's sleeve and packed the white hand against the warmth, working slowly, murmuring the whole time — not charms, just talk, a low steady stream of nonsense about the market in Greyhollow and the price of turnips and the terrible poetry of a client named Master Hobb, because she knew what I did not: that the injured listen for your voice to know if they are allowed to be afraid.

I sat on my hands and watched, and hated myself with a thoroughness I can only describe as literary.

"I froze," I said, when the fire was high and Odile had stopped shivering quite so violently. "You both saw it. Say it plainly. I'll feel better if someone says it plainly."

Bram didn't look up from Odile's hand. "You froze."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet." She glanced up then, and her eyes were not cruel but they were not soft either; they were the eyes of a woman who had buried a mentor and was tired of funerals. "Because here's what happens next. In a day, maybe two, we reach the door beneath the mountain, and the door does not care about your nerve. The wardens will come again, and next time they'll know our positions because *you taught them our habits for twenty-five years.* And the person standing next to you will need to believe that when it matters, your legs work." She tied off the bandage. "So don't thank me. Go be sick behind the rocks if you need to, and then come back and tell me why you froze. The real reason. Because 'they wore my colors' isn't it. Colors don't root a man to the ground. Something else does."

Odile, grey-faced and fierce, added from within her blanket: "For the record, jumping was also terrifying. I merely elected to be terrified *usefully.* It's a skill. It can be learned. I learned it from being arrested twice."

Despite everything — despite the dead men in my house's livery cooling in the snow, despite Odile's hand, despite Bram's verdict hanging over me — I laughed. A cracked, awful laugh, but a laugh. That is the thing about the three of us. We are each, in our different ways, experts in disgrace, and disgrace makes excellent company.

But Bram's question stayed with me through the night watch, through the wind moaning in the falls, and near dawn I finally found the true answer, and it was worse and better than cowardice.

I froze because some traitorous chamber of my heart had looked at Ser Aldric Vane-Holt whistling grass-blades with a boy and thought: *these are my people. Whatever they've done, whatever the house has done, they are the only family I was issued, and if I raise this sword against them, I am alone in the world forever.*

That is the lie, of course. The elegant, inherited lie. Family is not a uniform. I had learned it watching Bram bind Odile's hand with turnip-market gossip; I had learned it watching Odile jump for a man who'd ruined her. But knowing a lie and refusing its shelter are different skills, and I had only just begun the second lesson when the morning came, and with it, the mountain.

We climbed out of the narrows into a valley that ended — impossibly, inevitably, the way a sentence ends — in a wall of black stone rising out of the ice, older than any quarry, carved with letters worn nearly smooth. Bram went ahead, slow, one hand pressed to her bared arm, where the oath-mark had begun to glow like a coal breathed upon.

She stopped at the base of the wall and read the words aloud without seeming to decide to, her voice strange, doubled, as if something under the stone were reading along with her:

*"By blade and breath and blood sworn true, the door remembers you."*

And deep beneath our boots, patient as the winter, something answered: a groan of stone, a breath of warm air smelling of iron and looms and old, old dark — and the mountain opened.

Bram turned to look at us, her face lit gold by her own arm, and said, very quietly: "It wants a toll. And I think I'm the coin."

Behind us, far down the valley, the wardens' signal-fire bloomed against the snow.
