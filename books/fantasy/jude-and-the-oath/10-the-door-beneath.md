## 10. The Door Beneath

The mountain had been growing in the window of Bram's eye for three days, and she had run out of ways to pretend it wasn't.

It stood up ahead of them like a judgment, white-shouldered and patient, wearing its new snow the way a magistrate wears fresh robes. The old maps — Odile's maps, drawn from memory with hands still stiff from frostbite — called it Vael Saran, the Hollow Crown. The villagers in the last settlement had refused to name it at all. They'd only pointed north with their chins and gone back inside.

"Cheerful place," Cassian said, for perhaps the fourth time that morning. He said it every time the trees thinned and the wind came down raw off the icefields, and every time he said it Odile made a small sound like a kettle deciding against boiling.

"You could walk behind me," Odile said, "where I can't hear you."

"I find my commentary improves morale."

"Your commentary has never once improved anything."

Bram let them argue. It had become her quiet work over the past weeks, this listening — not to what they said but to the fact of them saying it. Three people who had no business surviving together, bickering up a frozen mountain because each of them owed something they couldn't put down. When Cassian's voice cracked on Liss's name, Odile pretended not to notice and handed him the tea skin first. When Odile's bandaged hand shook too badly to strike a match, Cassian lit it without comment and then complained loudly about the quality of the kindling so she'd have something to be annoyed about instead of ashamed.

And both of them, she knew, were watching her watch the mountain.

They crested the last ridge as the light went long and copper-colored, and there it was.

The door was not what Bram expected, though she couldn't have said what she *had* expected. A cave mouth, maybe. Something crude. Instead: a wall of black stone rising out of the glacier like the keel of a drowned ship, forty feet high, dressed with carvings that the snow could not seem to settle on. Snow fell toward the wall and slid away from it, as if the stone were politely declining. The gate itself stood centered in the wall — two doors of the same black stone, closed, seamless, without handle or hinge or seam that Bram's eyes could find.

But she didn't need her eyes. The oath under her skin had found the door before they did. It had been pulling for days, an ache along her forearm like a tooth remembering an old injury, and now it sang — low and insistent and *familiar*, the way a song you learned as a child is familiar when you hear it sung in a stranger's house.

The words were carved across the doors in letters taller than she was.

She knew them. Of course she knew them. She'd carried them for fifteen years, inked into her arm by a dying man's hand, recited ten thousand times in the dark of her cellar until they wore grooves into her like water wearing grooves into stone:

*I am the blade at the threshold. What sleeps below, I hold asleep. What knocks below, I turn away. While I stand, the door stays shut.*

Her mentor's handwriting had been all over the map's borders, and his words were all over the door, and Bram stood in the blue shadow of both and felt something in her chest go carefully, deliberately quiet.

"That's your oath," Odile said. Not a question. Her breath fogged and hung in the still air like the mountain itself was waiting on it.

"It's the oath." Bram walked closer, close enough that her breath bounced back off the doors. "Every swordspeaker learns the same words. We thought — I thought — it was just tradition. A formula." She reached out and laid her bare fingers against the stone. It was warm. In the middle of all that ice, the door was warm as a sleeping animal. "It's not a formula. It's a key."

"How does a key lock a door?" Cassian asked. Then, watching her face: "Or — ah. How does it *open* one?"

"The elders said we were made to guard it," Bram said. "Guarding means standing between. And you can't stand between a door and what's behind it unless—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Unless the door knows you're allowed to decide. Both things. Shut and open. The oath isn't a wall. It's a *choice*."

"So you speak it," Odile said slowly, "and the door opens."

"If I mean it."

"And do you?"

Bram looked at the words cut into the mountain, at her dead mentor's grammar, his cadence, the way he'd always insisted a sentence should be built to bear weight. Fifteen years of hiding magic in a city that hated it. Three years of selling luck-knots to people who needed real help and getting copper for lies. An oath that had burned her every day since the night it was pressed into her arm by hands going cold.

"I don't know yet," she said honestly. "Ask me again in a moment."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the wall, which was warmer than any campsite had a right to be, and neither Odile nor Cassian remarked on it, which was its own kind of courtesy.

Odile spread the map on her bedroll and weighted its corners with stones. By firelight the ink was doing its slow evening migration — coastlines drifting, passes opening and closing like breathing. Tonight the paths all bent toward the wall behind them, converging on the doors like streams running backward uphill.

"It wants us inside," Cassian observed.

"Maps don't want things."

"This one does. Look at it. It's practically wagging its tail."

Odile studied it a moment longer, and Bram watched her not-friend frown at the paper with the particular intensity she reserved for being frightened. "When we go through," Odile said at last, "I'll draw everything. Every gallery, every turning. If we get separated—"

"We won't get separated."

"If we get *separated*, Cassian, you follow the drawings. Not your instincts. Your instincts are noble and useless. The drawings will be true even if nothing else is."

Cassian was quiet for a moment. Firelight moved on his face. "You know," he said, "when I met you both, I thought nobles were supposed to be the ones who planned everything."

"You thought wrong about most things," Odile said. "Go to sleep."

Bram took first watch. She sat with her back against the warm stone and her sword across her knees and listened to her companions breathe, and she tried to feel brave.

What she felt instead was the oath, humming against the door through four inches of rock, like a voice through a wall.

*While I stand, the door stays shut.*

Then why, she asked it silently, the way she'd asked it a thousand nights in her cellar, did you let him die? Why did you let them take his soul, if you were supposed to hold the door? What good is a guard who can't—

The ache in her arm pulsed, once, hard. Almost like an answer. Almost like grief.

She woke the others before dawn.

* * *

The light was grey and sourceless when they stood before the doors. No sun had cleared the peaks; the brightness simply assembled itself out of the snow-glare, flat and shadowless, a stage awaiting players.

Odile checked the map twice more and put it away. Cassian checked his knife twice more and put that away too, which for Cassian constituted enormous restraint. Then they both looked at Bram, and looked away, giving her the room the way you give someone room at a graveside.

She drew her sword.

Not to fight. She wasn't sure what for, exactly — habit, maybe. Or ceremony. The oath lived along the blade-side of her arm, and it seemed right that steel should witness this. She pressed her palm flat to the left-hand door, felt the warmth of it climb into her wrist, up her forearm, settling into the scarred lines where the oath-ink ran like a second set of veins.

"I'm going to say it," she told them. "All of it. Out loud. I've never — it's meant to be spoken whole, at the door. That's the rite. Nobody ever taught me the rite because nobody ever expected a swordspeaker to come here." A short laugh escaped her, thin and cold. "We were all guards. None of us were ever supposed to *use* the other half."

"Bram." Cassian's voice, gentle. "Whatever happens — whatever you see in there — remember that mountains lie. Courts lie. Anything that shows you something you want, you ask it what it costs."

She nodded. She appreciated it. It did not help even slightly.

She began.

*"I am the blade at the threshold."*

The words left her mouth and the mountain heard them. There was no other way to describe it — a vast attention turned toward her, patient and old, the way a lighthouse must feel to ships. The warmth under her palm deepened.

*"What sleeps below, I hold asleep."*

The carved letters filled with light, slow as honey.

*"What knocks below, I turn away."*

Her arm burned. Not painfully — or not only painfully. It burned the way a struck flint burns, purposeful, spending itself.

*"While I stand, the door stays shut."*

Silence. The whole mountain held its breath, and Bram stood before her own words and understood, finally, what the elders had been trying to tell her in the village kitchen with its smell of barley bread: the oath was never a leash. It was a hinge. And a hinge turns both ways.

"While I stand," she said quietly, in her own words now, her own plain Greyhollow market-voice, "the door *opens*. For me. For those I choose. Let me pass, and let me pass back."

The doors swung inward without a sound.

Beyond them: stairs, descending into a dark that was not empty. It had texture. Weight. It breathed cold air upward that smelled of stone and loom-oil and, faintly, unbearably, of woodsmoke and dried lavender — the smell of her mentor's cottage, the smell of the years before everything went wrong.

Bram's eyes stung. She blinked it back. Later. Grief later. Work now.

"Stay behind me," she said. "Both of you. And if anything speaks to you down there, if anything offers you anything — you come get me first. Promise me."

"Promised," said Cassian.

"Noted," said Odile, which from Odile was the same word.

They started down. The stairs were wide enough for six abreast and worn smooth in the centers, worn by feet, by centuries of feet, and Bram did not let herself think about whose. The dark thickened around their single lantern. Somewhere far below, faint as a pulse, came a rhythmic sound — a great slow *clack... clack... clack* — that Odile identified in a whisper as a loom, though how she knew that, and why her voice shook saying it, she didn't explain.

They had descended perhaps a hundred steps when the toll came due.

Bram felt it first as a tightening behind her eyes, a pressure like deep water. Then the stairwell was gone.

* * *

She stood in a kitchen.

A small kitchen, low-beamed, cluttered, smelling of lavender and bread and the sharp green scent of healing herbs hung from rafters. Rain on the roof. A cat — grey, disreputable, one ear chewed — asleep in a basket by the stove. Everything exact. Everything right down to the chip on the third shelf and the way the window caught afternoon light.

And at the table, writing a letter, sat Corren Hale.

Alive. His beard more grey than she remembered, his shoulders a bit more stooped, a burn scar on his wrist she'd forgotten she knew. He looked up as though she'd only stepped out to fetch water, and smiled the smile that had raised her, and said:

"There you are. Put the kettle on, love, I'm at the difficult part."

Bram could not move. Could not speak. Her body was twenty-four years old again and safe and the rain was real, she could *feel* it was real, the drumming on the roof, the warmth of the stove —

No.

*Cassian's voice, from somewhere very far away:* anything that shows you something you want, ask it what it costs.

"What does it cost?" she said aloud, and her voice came out cracked and young and hateful.

Corren set down his pen. He looked at her with such kindness that it was worse than cruelty would have been. "That's not how you greet your old teacher?"

"You died," Bram said. "I buried what we found. I grieved. Three years, Corren. Three years I starved myself of everything I was because you weren't there to say it was allowed. So either you're a ghost, or you're a lure, or—" and her voice broke despite everything, despite the discipline of a lifetime, "—or you're alive, and you *let me believe*—"

"I'm alive," he said gently.

The world tilted. The kitchen held steady around him like cupped hands.

"I'm alive, Bram. Below. They keep me. They've kept me these three years because I bargained poorly and they bargain well." He rose, crossed to her, and stopped just short of touching — a courtesy, or a rule. "I came down here to buy souls back. Yours was next, after mine. I signed the debt over to save you and I failed anyway, and I've regretted it every day since. But you're here now. You spoke the oath. Do you understand what that means? You can finish it. You can buy them all back. Every soul on those looms. You have the key."

"How?" Her heart was hammering hope into every corner of her, and she hated herself for how fast it filled. "How do I finish it?"

"Come down. Come to me. The court will deal with a swordspeaker who holds the door — it's the one bargain they've wanted for centuries. One agreement, sealed properly, and everyone walks free. Liss Merrow. All of them. Me." He smiled again, tired and warm and true, or wearing truth like a coat. "You only have to leave the others at the gate. They can't come further. Court rules. But they'll be safe up there, Bram, and you'll have me back, and it will be *over*. No more hiding. No more copper charms. Kettle's already on."

Somewhere beneath the kitchen — beneath the vision, beneath the lie or the truth or whatever it was — she could hear the loom. *Clack. Clack.* And underneath that, almost lost: voices. Hundreds of them, murmuring, thread-thin.

Liss was down there. Liss, who didn't know her brother's face. And Corren was asking her to leave the people who had dragged her up a mountain at her back, alone, at a bargaining table with a court that had spent three years holding her mentor as leverage.

*He's alive.*

The thought arrived whole and shameless, and Bram let it sit in her chest for one full breath, two, three. She looked at his face. She memorized it. The grey beard. The stoop. The scar.

"You're at the difficult part of a letter," she said.

His smile flickered — barely, a hairline crack in porcelain. "What?"

"When I came in. You said put the kettle on, you're at the difficult part. Corren, you never wrote letters at that table. You said they cramped your hand. You wrote in the window chair." She stepped back, and the kitchen's warmth pulled at her like undertow. "Whoever you are — whoever sent you — you got everything else right. The scar. The cat. Even the rain." Another step. Her heel found the top of the stairs; cold air rose against her ankles like a tide. "You got one thing wrong. And I'm going to come down there, and I'm going to find out whether the real Corren Hale is at that table or in a cell, and if he's in a cell I'm getting him out. But not by leaving my family at the door."

"Bram—"

"And I'm not putting the kettle on."

The kitchen shattered like sugar-glass.

* * *

She came back to herself mid-stair, on her knees, lantern guttering, Cassian gripping one shoulder and Odile the other, both of them shouting her name. The loom-sound pulsed far below. The dark waited, patient as a creditor.

"Bram. Bram, look at me. What did it show you?" Odile's voice, sharp with fear she'd deny to her grave.

Bram pushed herself upright. Her legs shook. Her eyes were wet and she let them be, for exactly as long as it took to wipe them, and no longer.

"My mentor," she said. "Alive. Down there. Asking me to come to terms with the court — and to leave the two of you above."

Cassian and Odile exchanged a look over her head, the look of people doing arithmetic and hating the total.

"It's a trap," Odile said.

"Probably," Bram agreed. She picked up the lantern. Its light steadied as her hand did, and she found, somewhere in the wreckage of that false kitchen, a coal of fury that burned clean and useful. Whatever the court had shown her, it had shown her its own fear: it needed her willing, needed her parted from her people, needed her at that table believing she had something to lose by refusing.

Which meant she had more power at that table than she'd thought.

"But it also told me he might be alive," she said, and started down the stairs. "So we're going to go ask the Pale Regent ourselves. Politely. With witnesses."

The dark below received them, and the loom kept its slow count, *clack... clack... clack,* like a heart counting down to something.
