## 11. Descent

Odile Fen had drawn maps for kings. She had charted the Amber Straits in a storm, ink freezing in the pot, and she had once redrawn the entire northern coastline of Vail because a duke's vanity required the harbor to be larger than his rival's. She knew what a map was: an argument about the world, made in lines, and she had always won her arguments.

The galleries beneath the mountain were not interested in arguing.

They were interested in forgetting.

She noticed it first as a slip of the pen — a small thing, forgivable, the kind of error she would have torn the sheet up over in her old life. They had been walking perhaps two hours past the gate when Bram asked how far to the next junction, and Odile looked down at her careful survey of the descending stair and found that somewhere in the last hundred paces she had stopped drawing the ceiling. Not simplified it. Stopped. The space above the corridor was simply blank, white, unconsidered, as though she had forgotten that anything existed above their heads at all.

Her hand shook. She fixed it. She drew the vaulting back in, rib by rib, from memory, and made herself note the height — *forty feet? thirty?* — with a question mark that felt like a small confession.

"Problem?" Bram asked. The swordspeaker walked like a woman entering a church she intended to rob: reverent and resentful about it. Her scarred arm was wrapped tight against her ribs, and every so often her fingers moved along the oath-marks as if counting them, or reassuring them.

"No," said Odile. "The ceiling is forty feet. Probably."

"You don't know?"

"I'll know when I finish the map."

Bram looked at her a moment too long, the way she did when she was deciding whether to be kind or merely quiet, and then mercifully said nothing. Cassian was ahead with the lantern, holding it high the way he'd held everything since the shrine — carefully, apologetically, like a man who expected the world to invoice him for it. And behind came Liss Merrow, thread-thin and silent, walking only because Cassian held her hand and because, Odile suspected, some part of her soul still remembered being told where to go.

That was the thing about this place. It was full of people who had been told where to go for so long they had forgotten they were ever anywhere else.

* * *

The upper galleries announced themselves by sound before light: a vast, arrhythmic *clack-clack-whirr*, like a thousand looms operated by a thousand hands that could not quite agree on the hour. Then the corridor opened, and Odile stopped drawing, because there was nothing in her training for this.

The gallery was a cathedral turned inside out. Galleries upon galleries rose into darkness on both sides, tiered like an amphitheater the size of a weather system, and every tier was lined with looms. Hundreds. Thousands. Each loom worked by a single figure, each figure gray as old ash, each one weaving — Odile squinted, adjusted her spectacles, squinted again — *weather*. That was the only word for it. The threads ran out silver-white and cold-looking, and where the finished cloth wound onto its beam, frost bloomed across the wood. Above the whole impossible hall, where a ceiling should have been, snow fell upward, slowly, patiently, into the dark, as if the sky itself were being rolled up like a carpet.

"That's the winter," Bram breathed. "All of it. Three years of it. They're *weaving* it."

"Every flake has a pattern," Odile said quietly, because it was true and because saying true things helped her feel anchored. "Look at the selvage. Someone designed this. It repeats." Even now, even here, her eye went to the structure first. Structure was safe. Structure could be drawn. "It's a map," she added, surprising herself. "The weave. If you read the warp right, it's a map of Greyhollow under snow. Of everywhere. They're not making weather, they're *drafting* it."

"Then you can read it," Bram said, turning to her with that sudden fierce attention she got when hope presented itself wearing work clothes. "You can find the master spindle."

"I can read a great many things." Odile uncapped her ink — the good ink, the black-black ink she'd been hoarding — and began, on a fresh page, to draw the gallery. Tier by tier. Loom by loom, in shorthand. She drew until her wrist ached and then kept drawing, because here was the thought that had been sitting in her chest since the gate, cold and patient as a spider:

*If I stop drawing, I will forget the sky.*

Not metaphorically. Not in the way anyone forgets anything. She had watched the weavers as they passed — the way their eyes didn't track movement, the way one woman's mouth shaped words with no sound, over and over, like a prayer worn smooth. And she had seen the newest ones. There were newer ones; the court must take souls the way a mill takes grain, steadily, without ceremony. The new ones still looked *up*. The old ones didn't. Somewhere between the gate and the hundredth loom, a person stopped looking up, and after that, Odile would have wagered her guild seal, they couldn't have said what a sky was if you paid them.

She drew the sky. Right there in the margin of the gallery survey, small but precise: the particular bruised violet of dusk over the northern passes, three birds, one of them slightly wrong because she'd never been good at birds. She dated it. She wrote beneath it, in her smallest hand: *Remember this is real.*

Cassian caught her doing it. He didn't say anything — he was carrying Liss's arm through his, guiding her past a loom whose weaver had paused to watch them go by with the mild interest of a cat at a window — but later, at the rest-stop, he sat down beside her and said, "What are all the margins?"

"What?"

"In your book. Every page has drawings in the edges. Stars. Clouds. A sun, once. You've never once drawn a sun in your life, Odile Fen, I've seen your atlases."

"It's a professional exercise," she said.

"Is it."

"Cassian. I am a disgraced royal cartographer who forged a map for a soul-court and has spent nine days underground with a swordspeaker who bites and a lordling who apologizes to furniture. My professional exercises are the only thing standing between me and becoming a woman who weaves snow for a century." She said it lightly. She had learned long ago that the trick to saying terrible things was to say them in the tone you'd use for complaining about inn prices. "So yes. I draw the sky. In the margins. Every hour. If you see me stop, poke me."

He looked at her for a moment, and then, instead of laughing or pitying her — either of which she could have borne — he took out his own little notebook, the one where he recorded Liss's clear hours, and wrote something in it.

"What are you doing?"

"Margins," he said. "Liss used to sing while she rode. Badly. On purpose, mostly, to annoy our father. I'm writing down the songs so I don't forget which ones she'd choose." He glanced at Liss, sleeping against her own knees a few feet away, gray-tinged even in the lantern light, her fingertips translucent as sea glass. "Everyone should get margins."

Odile found, inconveniently, that her eyes stung, and covered it by blowing her nose with great authority and returning to her survey.

* * *

They went deeper. The route Odile plotted threaded between the working tiers, through maintenance corridors the weavers never left — she deduced this from the wear patterns on the floors, from the little alcoves where bowls of gray porridge appeared and vanished, from the absence of doors. There were no doors anywhere. That was the detail that frightened her most, and she was a woman who had once been frightened by a compass that pointed slightly wrong. No doors meant no one here had ever needed to be kept out. Or kept in. The whole underworld was a machine running smoothly enough that locks would be an insult.

Her map grew. Twelve pages, fourteen, sixteen. Junction 9, Junction 10. She named the corridors after things above: *Gullwing Passage, Thaw Street,* and once, when Cassian wasn't looking, *Merrow Walk,* which she denied afterward with a straight face.

But the slips kept happening, and they got worse.

On the second day below — or what she called days, having instituted a schedule of lantern-dimming that everyone obeyed because Bram threatened to and nobody wanted that conversation — Odile looked up from her page and realized she could not remember the color of the evening sky over Greyhollow. Not the exact violet. She knew she had known it. She had *drawn* it yesterday, in the margin, dated and captioned. She looked at the drawing and it told her: violet, three birds. But knowing it secondhand was like being told a stranger's dream. The actual color, the one she had seen ten thousand evenings of her life, was simply — gone. A drawer opened in her mind and found clean, swept, ready for new contents.

Her heart went off like a trap. She sat very still and made herself breathe, and then she took out the ink and redrew the sky, larger this time, and wrote underneath: *violet, you fool, VIOLET, you have seen it every summer of your life.* And then, because terror makes honest women of cartographers, she wrote a third line: *How much else has already gone quietly?*

She started testing herself after that. Names of streets. The smell of rain on hot stone. Her mother's voice singing — no, her mother hadn't sung; her mother had *hummed* while kneading bread, off-key, a habit Odile had found mortifying at twelve and would have given both hands to hear again now. She tested and tested, and each test came back with holes, small ones, neat ones, like moth-holes in a coat you keep meaning to mend.

That was how this place worked, she understood at last. It didn't take your soul all at once. It unraveled you politely, thread by thread, and let you keep the shape of yourself long enough that you didn't notice the draft.

* * *

It was Bram who caught her, on the fourth night below, in the act.

Odile had woken from a dream of white — just white, featureless, warm, oddly pleasant, which was the horrifying part — and found herself sitting up with her pencil moving on a fresh page, drawing the gallery. Just the gallery. Tier upon tier, loom upon loom, beautifully, accurately, lovingly rendered, and in the margins of that page: nothing. No sky. No violet. No birds. She had been drawing the underworld in her sleep, and her margins had gone blank as a weaver's eyes.

"Odile." Bram's voice, low. "Put it down."

"I'm mapping."

"You're practicing." Bram crouched in front of her and took the pencil from her fingers with a gentleness Odile had not known the woman possessed. "Look at your page. Look at the margins."

"I know." Her voice cracked on the second word, and she hated it, hated being seen mid-unraveling, hated more that the tears came anyway, fast and furious. "I know, Bram, I *know*, but I can't stop, because if I stop I'll forget the sky, except it isn't working, do you understand, I've drawn it forty times and today I looked at my own drawing and it was like reading someone else's postcard—"

"Hey." Bram took her by the shoulders. Up close, the swordspeaker's face was all planes and exhaustion and, buried deep, a stubborn banked kindness she clearly resented carrying. "Say it to me. Don't draw it. Say it."

"The sky is violet at dusk over the passes," Odile whispered. "There are three birds. One is wrong. Rain smells like hot stone. My mother hummed off-key when she baked. I'm Odile Fen. I forged a map, and I'm going to un-forge it, and then I'm going to see the sky again, and it will be violet, and I will complain about it, because it's never quite the right violet, and that's — that's mine, that complaint is mine—"

"Yes," Bram said. "Keep that one. The complaint. That's the most you thing you've ever said."

A wet, undignified laugh escaped her. From across the dead fire, Cassian stirred and mumbled, "Is everything — are we — should I be awake for this?"

"Sleep," both women said, and he subsided, and Liss slept on beside him with her thread-thin fingers curled around his sleeve, and Odile sat in the dark between her two unlikely keepers and made herself hold the whole sky in her head without any paper at all, for one full minute, shaking the entire time.

Afterward Bram pressed the pencil back into her hand. "Draw," she said. "But draw us a way *out* first, every morning, before you draw anything else. Start every page with the door we came in by. Make the exit a habit."

It was good cartography and better medicine. Odile adopted it on the spot, and privately resolved that if they all survived, she was going to teach a seminar on it.

* * *

On the fifth day below, following her own lines down through Gullwing Passage toward the lower galleries where her reading of the woven map said the spindle vault lay, Odile rounded a bend and stopped so suddenly that Cassian walked into her back and apologized to it.

Ahead, the corridor ended at a landing overlooking the deep gallery — and the deep gallery was different. The looms below were larger, older, black-framed, and they did not weave snow. They wove *darkness*, dense and heavy, cloth of absolute night, and the weavers there stood rather than sat, and none of them were gray. They were pale as the Regent himself, and they all, every one, lifted their heads at once when Odile's lantern-light touched the rail.

Not hostile. Worse. *Recognizing.*

One of them raised a hand — not in threat, in greeting, the way you wave to someone whose name you almost remember — and called out in a voice like wind through keyholes:

"Cartographer. You came back. You left your signature here once. We kept it."

Odile's blood went to ice water. Because she knew — she had always known, in the guilty locked room of herself — exactly which signature they meant. The forged map. Her finest lie, drawn years ago in a guild workshop under a duke's orders, and delivered down here, and *used*. And if the weavers kept signatures the way they kept everything else —

Then somewhere in those lower galleries, woven into the pattern of the long winter, was a piece of Odile Fen. Already spun. Already hers.

Behind her, Bram's hand found her shoulder, steady as a doorframe.

"Tell me," the swordspeaker said quietly, "that there's another way down."
