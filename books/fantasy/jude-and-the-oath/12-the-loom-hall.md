## 12. The Loom Hall

Cassian had imagined the underworld a hundred times as a child — usually while being tutored in heraldry he did not want to learn. In his imagination it had been grand: vaulted halls of black marble, torches burning cold blue fire, courtiers with faces like winter moons. He had imagined it because his sister used to dare him to. *Bet you can't picture where they took her,* Liss would say, ten years old and fearless on the nursery floor, and Cassian would try, and Liss would laugh at whatever he came up with, even the versions with dragons.

The reality was worse than any of them, and also somehow smaller. It was a corridor of rough-hewn stone that smelled of wet wool and tallow. And at its end, when the passage opened out like a held breath finally released, it was a hall full of people weaving.

That was all. That was the horror of it.

* * *

The loom hall stretched farther than Odile's maps said it should, which Cassian had privately begun to suspect was a general rule of this place. Rows upon rows of great looms rose out of the gloom like the ribs of drowned ships, and at each loom sat a weaver, and none of them stopped working. Not one. The sound was the first thing that got under his skin — not the clack and hush of shuttles, exactly, but something underneath it, a low continuous tone like wind through a keyhole, like a note held so long you forgot it had ever been struck.

"That's them," Bram murmured beside him. Her voice came out flat, pressed thin, the way it always did when she was keeping something from spilling over. "The threads. You can hear them."

He could. Now that she'd named it, he couldn't stop hearing it. Every thread that passed through every loom sang faintly, and the songs were different, and some of them — this was the part that made his stomach turn over — some of them sounded almost familiar. Like tunes from childhood. Like a lullaby half-remembered.

Odile stood a pace behind them both, hood drawn low, her bandaged hand cradled against her chest. She hadn't spoken since the gate. She kept flexing the fingers of her good hand, opening and closing, opening and closing, the way a man checks his purse in a crowd.

"The wardens change watch," she said at last, very quietly. "I counted the intervals on the way down. We have perhaps an hour before they pass the gallery mouth again." A pause. "Less, if any of you do anything stupid."

"Define stupid," Bram said.

"Anything you're currently planning."

Cassian didn't answer, because she was right, and because his eyes had already left them both and gone back to the looms, going row by row, hunting. He had told himself — firmly, in the voice he used for household accounts and other unpleasant necessities — that he would be methodical. That he would walk the hall's edge with the patience of a man counting inventory, because Liss could be anywhere among hundreds of weavers and panic would only make him miss her.

Then he saw a woman three rows in whose shoulders moved when she worked, actually moved, the whole small architecture of her shifting with each throw of the shuttle, alive in a way the others weren't, and methodical went out the window like a cat escaping a bath.

"Liss," he breathed, and Bram caught his sleeve.

"Wait. *Wait.* Look first. All the way."

So he looked, hating her, knowing she was right. He looked the way he had once looked at his father across a negotiating table, cataloguing everything, letting nothing show. The woman was thin. Thin wasn't the word. She was *thread-thin*, as if someone had taken the ordinary substance of a person and drawn most of it out into string, leaving just enough at the center to hold a shape. Her hair was white where it had been dark. Her hands — Liss had always had quick, impatient hands, hands that drummed during sermons — those hands flew through the work without hesitation, without weariness, without anything at all. They wove the way water flows downhill. No choice in it whatsoever.

And the cloth coming off her loom was beautiful. That was the worst discovery of all, and it arrived slowly, the way ice arrives on a window. The fabric unrolling from her beam shimmered with colors he had no names for, and woven through it — he leaned, and his breath fogged in air far too warm for breath to fog — woven through it were pictures. Snow falling on a garden. A dog shaking rain from its ears. A boy and a girl on a nursery floor, and the girl laughing, and the boy trying and failing to draw a dragon.

He knew that day. He knew the color of the rug. He knew that his own attempt at a dragon had looked, in Liss's merciless opinion, like "a lumpy sock with ambitions."

"She's in there," he said. His voice cracked on the second word. "Bram. That's — I remember that morning. She's *weaving our memories.*"

"Not memories," Bram said softly. "Her. Everything she was. It comes off the loom and goes—" She pointed, and followed the finished cloth with her eyes as it wound away into the dark on some vast spindle-driven reel, toward the heart of the mountain, toward the pattern. "It goes into the winter. Every flake of it. Every frozen field. She's not imprisoned, Cassian. She's being *spent.*"

* * *

They waited for the wardens' interval, and then they went in.

Odile had worked out the approach with her maps and her maddening, precise mind: a service lane between two ranks of looms, blind to the gallery mouth, close enough to the workers to reach them but shadowed enough to hide three intruders. The soul-weavers did not look up. That was the thing Cassian could not stop noticing, the thing that scraped at him with every step. Nobody looked up. Nobody turned their heads. The weavers sat at their work like furniture that occasionally breathed, and when he passed close enough to see faces, what he saw made him want to sit down in the aisle and refuse to move until someone explained how the world allowed this.

They weren't sad. None of them were sad. There was no despair in the loom hall, and no joy either. There was only *attention*, total and vacant, the look of a person reading a book they cannot put down and cannot remember starting.

"They've been here a long time," Bram whispered. "The ones who remember being unhappy are already mostly unwoven. What's left doesn't mind."

"Doesn't mind," Cassian repeated. Something hot climbed his throat. "My father told me she'd be *at peace.* That's what the brokers said when he signed. Peace. Rest. A gentle sleep beneath the mountain." He laughed, and hated the sound of it bouncing off the stone. "He paid for her to become a lamp wick."

"Cassian." Bram touched his arm, brief and hard. "Row seven. Go."

Liss sat at the seventh loom in the fifth rank, and up close she was both more terrible and more herself than she had been at a distance. Terrible, because now he could see the places where the thread-thinness showed worst: the hollows at her wrists, the way her collarbones stood like the frame of a tent with too little canvas. Herself, because there was a smudge of ink still on the side of her right hand — the same ink smudge she had carried through her entire girlhood, because she bit her pens and wiped her fingers carelessly and never once in her life noticed.

He had planned words. Of course he had; he was his father's son, raised on speeches, drilled in address. He had composed three openings on the road down, polished two of them, discarded both somewhere around the glacier.

What came out was: "Hello."

She didn't look up. Her hands flew on. The shuttle went *clack*, and the song under the song bent slightly, and he realized with a lurch that he recognized this thread's tune too. It was the melody Liss used to hum while doing sums, badly, on purpose, to annoy their tutor.

"I'm sorry to disturb you," he said, because apparently his mouth had decided to improvise. "I'm — that is — I'm a buyer."

Nothing changed in her face. But her rhythm stuttered, one beat, two, and then resumed, and Bram made a small sharp sound behind him that meant *keep going.*

"A buyer," Liss said. Her voice was hers and wasn't — thinner, sanded smooth, like driftwood. "We don't get buyers here. Only takers."

"Then I'm the first." He swallowed. "I'm looking for — quality work. The finest in the hall. They told me the best weaver is on row seven." This was a lie. No one had told him anything. He had simply walked toward her the moment he saw her shoulders move.

The shuttle slowed. Slowed further. And then — and this undid him so thoroughly that he had to grip the loom's post to stay upright — Liss turned her head and looked at him.

Her eyes were grey, the exact grey of theirs, of home, of the slate roof of the east wing after rain. There was recognition in them, or the ghost of recognition, or possibly only politeness; he could not tell, and the not-telling was a knife he suspected he would carry the rest of his life.

"Finest," she repeated, considering. "The finest weave goes to the Pattern. Nothing leaves."

"I'm told exceptions can be purchased."

"Everything can be purchased." She smiled, and it was her smile, the crooked one, the one that meant she found a foolishness charming rather than contemptible, and Cassian nearly broke apart on the spot. "That's rather the point of this place, isn't it? Everything has a price. Most people just don't read the bill until it arrives." Her head tilted. "You talk like my brother talks when he's lying about cards."

The world stopped.

Behind him, Bram had gone very still. Odile's breathing had stopped entirely.

"Does he?" Cassian managed. His voice came out in one piece, which he considered a miracle on par with anything the old faith had ever claimed. "This brother of yours."

"Mm. Cassian." She turned back to her loom, and her hands resumed, and the shuttle went *clack*, and he understood dimly that he had been dismissed, gently, the way you set down a book you mean to return to. "He lies about cards because he hates losing. He lies about feelings because he hates needing. Otherwise he's honest to the point of being tedious." The thread ran gold through her fingers. "You should meet him sometime. If you find him down here, tell him—" The rhythm faltered. Her brow creased, a tiny fold, like paper remembering it had once been folded. "Tell him the sock-dragon was lovely. I never told him that. I should have."

"I'll tell him," Cassian whispered. "I promise."

"You have kind eyes," Liss said, not looking at him. "Buyers don't usually. That's why we don't get buyers."

* * *

They withdrew to the service lane, and Cassian discovered that his legs required negotiation.

He got his back against the wall between two looms and slid down it until he was sitting, knees up, in the manner of a child hiding from a scolding, and he did not care. Bram crouched beside him. She didn't say anything for a while, which was one of the things he had come, without ever discussing it, to rely on about her. She had a talent for silence that wasn't empty.

"She remembered the sock-dragon," he said finally.

"Yes."

"She called it lovely. She spent twenty years telling everyone within earshot it looked like a sock with ambitions."

"Yes."

"And she doesn't know me." His voice did something humiliating on the last word, and he let it, because there was no one in the world left who hadn't heard him fail at composure except possibly the wardens, and they could add it to the list. "She's in there, Bram. She's *all* in there. Every memory I have of her is coming off that loom in gold thread and going into snowflakes, and the last of her — the last actual *her* — is sitting at a bench smiling at strangers because we're the only conversation she's had in—"

He stopped. Breathed. Somewhere above them, miles of stone and frost away, the sky was doing whatever skies did when no one watched.

"We get her out," Bram said.

"Do we? Do we actually?" He looked up at her, and her face in the loom-light was grim and certain and young. "Because I did the sums, Bram, and I'm better at sums than Liss ever pretended to be. She's been here eleven years. Eleven years of her, woven into the winter. Even if we cut her free tomorrow, what walks out of here isn't my sister. It's a stranger wearing her handwriting. We came to steal a soul and what we're actually stealing is *remnants.*"

"Then we steal the remnants," Bram said, "and we give them back to someone who will spend the rest of her life learning them again." She said it simply, the way she said true things, without ornament, and it landed in him like a coin dropped in a dry well. "You lied to her beautifully, by the way."

"I lied to my own sister that I don't know her. Beautifully."

"It was the kindest lie I've heard in three years." Bram glanced down the lane toward row seven, where the small figure worked on, unaware or pretending, humming her sums-song through thread. "She gave you something, though. Before she turned away. Did you catch it?"

"The sock-dragon."

"Before that." Bram's eyes were sharp. "*If you find him down here.* She knows someone might come. Some part of her, down under all that weaving, has been waiting — and she sent you a message through a stranger, because it was the only door open to her." She stood, and offered him her hand, scarred arm and all. "Which means she's still in there making choices, Cassian. That's not remnants. That's your sister, holding the line."

He took her hand and got up, and did not trust himself to speak for a moment.

* * *

It was Odile who ruined the plan, in the end, and saved it in the same motion, which Cassian would later decide was simply how Odile operated, like weather.

They had agreed — a whispered conference in the lane, three heads together, absurd conspirators — to withdraw and regroup, to map the route to the Pattern's heart before attempting anything so reckless as extraction. Very sensible. Very Odile. Cassian had even begun to believe in it.

And then, as they turned to go, the loom hall changed.

It began at the far end, a ripple running down the ranks like wind through wheat — but wrong, inverted, a hush instead of a rustle. One by one, the weavers' hands slowed. The song under the song bent upward into a single clear note, and every thread in every loom shivered, and the light — there was no light source he could name, but there was light, tallow-pale and sourceless — dimmed as though the mountain had blinked.

On the great reels at the hall's end, the finished cloth stopped winding. Then, with a sound like a sigh played backwards, it began to unwind.

"What is that," Cassian whispered. "Odile. *What is that.*"

"I don't know." For the first time since he'd met her, Odile Fen sounded frightened, and it was worse than any scream. "The pattern's — pulling back. Something's drawing thread upstream. Something's *demanding*—"

Down the central aisle, unhurried, came figures in grey, and though Cassian had seen Hollow Wardens twice before — once at distance on a glacier, once too close in a doorway — he had never seen so many moving together, nor moving with this particular quality, which was not menace but *purpose*, the purpose of men coming to collect a specific debt.

And every warden's head turned, in perfect unison, toward row seven. Fifth rank. The finest weaver in the hall.

"No," Cassian said. "No, no — she hasn't done anything, she *can't* do anything, they keep them empty—"

"The stranger," Liss had said. *You have kind eyes. Buyers don't usually.* And her rhythm had stuttered, one beat, two — and the song under the song had bent, and maybe, just maybe, a thread eleven years deep had pulled hard enough to be felt at the top of the mountain.

Bram's hand found her sword-hilt, oathless and desperate. Odile was already calculating sightlines, exits, odds, muttering numbers like prayers. And Cassian Merrow, who had run from his family, from his title, from every fight that mattered, stepped out of the service lane into the open aisle with his arms spread wide and his heart hammering like a fist on a door.

"Gentlemen," he announced, in the voice he'd been trained in since birth, the voice of a house that sold souls and called it stewardship. "I believe you're looking for a buyer."

Three rows back, at a loom on row seven, a thread-thin woman paused her weaving — and smiled.
