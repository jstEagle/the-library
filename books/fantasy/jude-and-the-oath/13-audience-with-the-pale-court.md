## 13. Audience with the Pale Court

They came for Bram in the hour when the loom hall's lamps should have been lit but never were — the Hollow Wardens, six of them in Cassian's house colors gone grey as ash, moving with the terrible courtesy of servants who have been told to break something gently.

"Lady Vane," said the tallest one, which was wrong on two counts and probably deliberate. "The Regent will see you now."

"I'm not a lady."

"The Regent will see you *anyway*."

Bram set down the fever-charm she'd been pretending to work on — the wardens had left her tools, her little bundle of thread and bone needles, everything except her sword, which they'd taken at the door like it was an umbrella — and followed them through galleries that breathed.

That was the thing about the underworld no map captured, not even Odile's, not even the true one bleeding its slow ink across the borders: the mountain was not a place you entered. It was a body. The passages had the warm hush of a throat. The walls sweated. Somewhere far below, a rhythm Bram refused to call a heartbeat kept time with the looms.

The wardens walked in step. Of course they did. Everything down here walked in step; even the sold souls at their looms moved in a rhythm so uniform it made Bram's teeth ache. She thought of Liss, thread-thin, weaving winter into cloth with hands that no longer remembered how to hold anything else. She thought of Cassian's face when he'd lied to his own sister. *I'm here to buy thread.*

She thought of Odile's last words before the wardens separated them: *"If they offer you anything, count what it costs first. I'm serious, Bram. I once forged a map of this place for a man who traded his daughter's name for a bridge. He got the bridge."*

"Lead on," Bram said.

* * *

The court received her in a hall that had clearly once been a cavern and had been persuaded, over centuries, into architecture. Pillars of black stone rose into darkness where stalactites hung like chandeliers nobody would ever light. The floor was smooth as poured glass, and it reflected not the room above but something else — snowfields, Bram realized. A frozen lake under stars. The ceiling's memory, or the mountain's dream of one.

And seated at the far end, on a throne carved from a single vein of white stone that ran up out of the floor like frost given a spine, was the Pale Regent.

Bram had expected a monster. She'd spent three days underground expecting a monster — something with too many joints and a voice like grinding rock, something she could hate cleanly. What sat on the throne was worse. It looked like a woman who had died of old age and then gotten bored of being dead: small, neat, wrapped in robes the color of unlit candles, with a face so ordinary that Bram caught herself thinking *someone's grandmother*, and hated herself for it.

"Bram Vane," the Regent said. Her voice was dry paper and river stones. "Swordspeaker. Last of your line, though your line was never really a bloodline, was it? More of a… succession. A candle passed hand to hand." She smiled, and it was a kind smile, and Bram felt the oath under her skin turn over like a dog hearing its name. "Sit."

There was no chair. When Bram didn't move, the floor obligingly raised itself into one, which was the single most unsettling act of hospitality she had ever experienced.

"I'll stand."

"You'll stand until your knees give out, and then we will have this conversation while I watch you rub your legs like a woman twice your age. Sit. Courtesy costs nothing, and I have paid for everything in this room already."

Bram sat. The stone was warm, damn it. Comfortable, even.

"You've come about the girl," the Regent said. "Merrow. Lissandra Merrow, sold by her grandfather's grandfather, four generations of interest accrued, currently valued at—" she paused, and somewhere below them the great heartbeat stuttered, as if the mountain were checking a ledger— "considerably more than you can pay."

"I wasn't planning to pay in coin."

"No," the Regent agreed pleasantly. "You were planning to pay in oaths. That is, after all, what your order existed for. The swordspeakers were our bankers, Lady Vane. Promises made flesh, debts written into living skin so they could not be forged or forgotten. Do you know why there are no more of you?"

"My mentor died."

"He died," the Regent said, "because he tried to buy back every soul ever sold beneath my mountain. All of them. Four hundred years of accounts, settled in a single afternoon, by one man with one sword-word left in him." She folded her hands. "It was very nearly the most beautiful thing I have ever witnessed. And it failed, and he knew it would fail, and he did it anyway. Sentimental men make poor bankers."

The words went into Bram like a blade finding the gap between ribs. She'd known Aldric was dead. She'd carried the grief three years, worn smooth as river stone. But this — *he tried to buy back every soul* — was a grief she hadn't known she owed, arriving all at once with interest.

"That's a lie," she said, because it had to be, because the alternative was unbearable.

"It is not. You may check. That's rather the point of oaths, isn't it? They cannot be spoken falsely." The Regent leaned forward, candle-colored robes whispering against white stone. "Here is my offer, swordspeaker, and I will make it only once, because unlike some parties in this negotiation I keep my books clean."

Somewhere in the dark above, water dripped. The reflected snowfield gleamed on the floor between them, and Bram understood suddenly that the Regent sat facing it deliberately — that the dead woman liked to look at winter she hadn't woven.

"I will return Aldric Marlowe to you," the Regent said. "Not a ghost, not a shade in a loom — *him*. Flesh, breath, memory, that appalling laugh of his that made my wardens flinch for a decade. His debt was paid in full the day he died; his payment remains in my keeping, and I am empowered to refund it. And I will release you. Your oath, burned off clean, your binding ended, your magic yours again — free, whole, unencumbered. You could walk out of my mountain tomorrow and charm fevers openly in Greyhollow until the day you die of old age instead of a warden's blade."

"And in exchange?"

"You leave the others. The cartographer stays — she has debts of her own to settle, and I have use for honest liars. The boy stays — his family's accounts run deep, and Lissandra's soul is collateral against them. And Lissandra stays." The Regent's voice gentled, which was somehow the cruelest thing she'd done yet. "One girl, Bram. Thread-thin already. Half-unraveled. Even if you cut her loose tonight, do you know what you'd carry home? A stranger wearing her brother's memories of her. She would look at Cassian Merrow and see a kind man she'd met once, at a shrine, bleeding."

"I know what unraveling looks like," Bram said quietly. "I watched it happen to a man named Tovan in the loom hall. He wove for sixty years before anyone came for him. His daughter found him and he asked her politely if she was a buyer."

"Yes," the Regent said. "Sad. Inefficient. Which is precisely why my offer is the merciful one. One life restored, fully, against half-lives lost partially. Any banker worth her salt takes that trade. Any *duty*-bound soldier does." She spread her small hands. "You swore to protect people, swordspeaker. I am offering you the chance to save someone completely instead of failing everyone equally. That is not cruelty. That is arithmetic."

Bram looked at her hands. The oath-mark lay along her forearm, the old scar-words her mentor had inked into her skin at seventeen, burning faintly the way they always did down here, close to their origin.

*Aldric alive.* She let herself want it for exactly four seconds — long enough to feel the whole shape of it. His voice in her shop. His terrible tea. Someone to tell about the passes, the ambush, the way Odile drew the sky from memory on a cell floor because she was afraid of forgetting it. Someone to say *I know, I know, I know what they took from me* —

"You're doing it again," Bram said.

The Regent tilted her head. "Doing what?"

"Dressing duty up as mercy. My mentor used to warn me about that. He said the worst bargains always come pre-wrapped in kindness, because then you feel grateful while they're robbing you." Bram stood, knees steady after all. "He also told me about a man who traded his daughter's name for a bridge. Got the bridge. Found out later the bridge was built from her."

Something flickered behind the Regent's ordinary face. Not anger. Something older and more tired.

"Aldric Marlowe," the Regent said slowly, "told you that story?"

"He told everyone that story. Usually drunk. Always twice."

For a moment the throne room was silent except for the drip of water and the mountain's slow pulse. Then the Pale Regent laughed — a real laugh, short and rusty, like a door opening in a house long shut.

"He *knew*," she said, half to herself. "Three hundred years I've sat this seat, and every swordspeaker who stood where you're standing believed themselves clever, and that infuriating man trained you to quote himself back at me before you'd even arrived." She rose. Up close she was smaller still, and her shadow on the wall was much larger than she was, and Bram decided firmly not to think about that. "Then you refuse."

"I haven't said that yet."

"You're going to. But I find, tonight, that I would like to hear the reasons. Call it professional curiosity. Call it sentiment." The Regent descended one step from her throne, and the reflected snowfield rippled around her feet like disturbed water. "Indulge an old woman who has been dead a very long time. Why refuse?"

And Bram — exhausted, magicless-feeling, her arm aching toward a mentor three years dead and possibly one bargain away from breathing — found that the answer was simple enough to fit in a sword-word.

"Because you counted the cost of everything," she said, "except what it does to the person holding the scales. Aldric alive means Aldric learning what I spent to get him. Me free means Cassian watching his sister dissolve alone. You're not offering me a rescue. You're offering me a *witness position*. And I didn't come three hundred miles under a mountain to watch."

The Regent studied her for a long moment, head tilted, the way Odile studied a coastline that refused to match the charts.

"No," she said at last. "I don't suppose you did." She turned, robes whispering, and began to climb the steps back to her throne. "Then hear the terms of your refusal, swordspeaker, and understand that I keep clean books in both directions. You may go. Take the girl's location from my wardens' minds — they will show you the spindle vault's outer gate, since you will die there anyway, and I dislike wasting hospitality. The cartographer and the boy will be released to join you, because I want you all in one place when the vault closes for the winter. It saves my wardens the walking."

Bram's stomach dropped. "You're letting us try."

"I am letting you *arrive*." The Regent settled onto her throne, and her shadow folded around her like wings. "There is a difference, and you will learn it in approximately one hour. Oh — and Bram Vane? One more thing, freely given, no charge."

"What."

"The man who traded his daughter's name for a bridge?" The Regent's smile returned, kind and cold as the snowfield at her feet. "Aldric Marlowe never told you the ending. Ask yourself why a man who quoted that story *always twice* never once finished it."

Behind her, the doors of the throne room opened onto darkness, and the wardens were already waiting, and somewhere below, faint as a heartbeat, the looms changed their rhythm — faster now, urgent, the sound of a pattern being rewoven ahead of schedule.

The long winter was falling early.

And Bram, walking out of the presence of the Pale Court with her refusal still ringing in her own ears, finally remembered where she'd heard that story's missing ending — not from Aldric at all, but scrawled on the border of a dying stranger's map, in handwriting she now recognized as his:

*The bridge held. Check what it was built over.*

She broke into a run.
