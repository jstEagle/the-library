## 14. The Bargain Refused

The Pale Regent's throne room was not what Bram had expected, though she could not have said what she had expected instead. Caves, perhaps. Darkness and dripping stone and the kind of squalor that festered beneath mountains in old stories.

Instead there was a hall of white marble veined with silver, so smooth the ceiling reflected like still water, and lit from no source she could find — the light simply *was*, even and colorless, the light of a day that never quite committed to being morning. The court sat in a crescent of thrones carved from something that might have been bone or might have been ice or might have been neither, and every figure upon them was beautiful the way frost on a window is beautiful: precise, patterned, and utterly without warmth.

The Pale Regent stood at the crescent's center. She was tall in the way that made you aware of your own smallness, robed in grey that moved as if underwater, and her face was kind. That was the worst part. Bram had prepared herself for cruelty. She had not prepared for a smile that looked genuinely glad to see her.

"Swordspeaker," the Regent said, and her voice filled the hall the way water fills a cup. "Three years we have watched you knot charms for copper in Greyhollow. Three years you have starved your gift in a city that fears it. And now you come down to us at last, wearing his oath on your arm." She descended one step. "You must be tired."

"I'm fine," said Bram, who had not slept properly since the waystation, whose boots were soaked through with glacier melt, and whose arm had been singing a low, hot note ever since the door beneath opened. Lying to authority figures was one of the few skills Greyhollow had sharpened rather than dulled.

The Regent's smile deepened by a degree. Somewhere behind Bram, Cassian shifted his weight — she heard the whisper of his coat, the careful breathing of a man trying very hard to be furniture. Odile was somewhere too, presumably, though Odile had a gift for being nowhere when it mattered.

"You came to bargain," the Regent said. "For the thread of the girl called Liss Merrow. Sold by her own house four generations past, woven into the winter's hem. A small thread, as these things go. Easily cut. Easily returned." Another step. Her bare feet made no sound at all. "I am prepared to give you far more than that."

Bram kept her hands loose at her sides, where they could reach either hilt or pocket depending on how the next minute went. "I'm listening."

"Your mentor," the Regent said, "is alive."

The hall did not go silent, because the hall had never been anything but silent; the silence simply gathered itself around the word like a held breath. Bram felt it land in her chest and crack something open that three years of careful living had scarred over.

"That's a lie," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Corren Vane died eleven years ago. I buried him. I dug part of the grave myself because the ground was frozen and the priest's back was bad."

"You buried what he left you," the Regent agreed gently. "His cloak. His sword-word, passed into your keeping. His debts." She descended the final step, close enough now that Bram could see her eyes, which were pale as the underside of ice and held, unmistakably, sorrow. "But Corren Vane himself stands below us yet, in the deep galleries, where those who bargain with this court and fail do not die. They remain. As payment. As promise. As proof that we honor our agreements — even the ones our guests regret."

*Hope is a weapon,* Odile had said once, mapping their route by candlelight, *and the mountain knows how to use it.* Bram had nodded along, thinking it was about maps.

"He tried to buy souls back," Bram said slowly. The thought arrived whole, the way sword-words arrived, before the mind could flinch. "That's why he went under. Not to break faith. To buy them back."

"Every soul his oath was sworn to guard," the Regent said. "He came to us with his sword-word, his years, his craft — everything a swordspeaker owns that can be owned. He offered it all for the release of the stolen dead. It was a noble offer. We nearly wept." Her expression suggested she remembered weeping fondly. "But a single swordspeaker's life does not balance four hundred threads, child. The arithmetic refused him. So he asked instead that his payment be held in trust — against the day another swordspeaker came down wearing his oath, strong enough to finish what he began."

"And you've been waiting," Bram said. "Eleven years. Holding him like a coin in a drawer."

"We hold all payments in trust," said the Regent. "It is what distinguishes a court from a thief."

Behind her, Cassian made a sound — small, involuntary, the sound of a boy hearing his family's name spoken in a room full of enemies. The Regent's gaze slid past Bram toward him, and Bram stepped sideways, deliberately, breaking the line of sight.

"Here is my offer," the Regent continued, as if the interruption had been weather. "Leave the Merrow girl's thread where it lies. Leave the loom unbroken, the winter unfinished, the long season sealed below as it has been sealed these hundred years past. Walk out through the gate above, and I will release Corren Vane into your keeping. Whole. Alive. With his debt paid and yours forgiven — you will be the first swordspeaker in history to walk away from the mountain owing nothing." She spread her pale hands. "You may grow old together. You may quarrel about tea. You may ask him yourself why he never told you any of this. Is that not worth more than one thin thread of one forgetful girl?"

*One forgetful girl.* Bram thought of Cassian in the shrine, bleeding and furious and terrified, saying *she used to steal my boots and fill them with river mud, and I would give anything — anything — to have cold wet feet again.* She thought of Liss at the loom, thread-thin, looking through her brother as if he were weather.

And she thought of Corren. Of course she thought of Corren. Eleven years of grief did not politely step aside just because grief turned out to be premature.

She thought of his hands, cracked and ink-stained, teaching her to braid a luck-knot. *A charm is just an oath you can hold,* he'd told her, age nine. *Small promises, kept often, add up to a life.* She thought of the map in his handwriting hidden in the borders of a forged chart — he'd known, even then, even dying, that someone would come down here after him. He'd left her a trail and trusted her to follow it.

He'd also, apparently, left her as collateral. Which was very like him. Corren Vane had never once in his life done the sensible thing when a stupid, generous thing was available.

"No," Bram said.

The Regent tilted her head. "You have not considered—"

"Oh, I've considered." Bram's voice came out low and even, and underneath it she felt the oath wake along her arm, heat rising like a struck match. "You're offering me my teacher back if I abandon a girl who doesn't remember her own brother's face. You're offering me mercy that costs me nothing and costs everyone else everything. And you dressed it up kindly, which means you knew exactly how it would look, which means—" she took a breath, "—you needed it to look kind. Which means you were worried I'd say no anyway."

Something flickered across the perfect face. Not anger. Something older and more careful than anger.

"Your mentor made the same error," the Regent said softly. "He mistook sentiment for strength."

"No," said Bram. "He mistook love for a currency. And so do you. That's the whole problem with this court — you think everything can be weighed. Four hundred threads against one. One teacher against one girl. But that's not how any of it works." She lifted her chin. "You want to know what he taught me? Every lesson, all of them, come down to one sentence. *You keep the promises you can keep, and you keep them completely.* Not the profitable ones. Not the ones that balance. The ones in front of you."

"The girl will fade regardless," the Regent said. "Her thread is worn near through. Even freed, she keeps only fragments—"

"Then she keeps fragments," Bram said. "Fragments are hers. You don't get to decide what's worth saving. That's not a bargain, that's bookkeeping, and I didn't come down here to do your books."

The temperature of the hall changed. Bram felt it against her skin, a pressure like the moment before lightning, and every figure on the crescent of thrones turned its head in unison, and the Regent's kindness finally, gratefully, fell away.

"You refuse," the Regent said. It was not a question. It was a door closing.

"I refuse."

"Then understand what you refuse. The winter falls tonight. When it seals, the gate above closes for a hundred years, and everything below — every gallery, every loom, every soul — belongs wholly to us until the thaw. Your mentor sleeps in the deep galleries. He will sleep through your whole life, swordspeaker, and through your children's lives, and no one will remember he waited at all." The Regent leaned close, and her breath smelled of snowmelt and old stone. "Grief, once given, is not refunded."

"Yeah," Bram said. Her heart was hammering and her eyes were burning and her voice, miraculously, held. "You know what else isn't refunded? Time. Liss Merrow has lost forty years of mornings. You don't get to keep her evenings too." She rolled her shoulders back. "So here's *my* offer, and it's not really an offer. Cut her thread. Open your ledgers. Or I'll do it myself, and I promise you — I *promise* you — I'm worse at negotiating than I am at everything else, and everyone says I'm terrible at everything else."

For a long moment the Regent simply looked at her. Then, horribly, she smiled again — and this smile was real, this one had teeth in it, this one was almost admiring.

"Corren said something like that," she murmured. "Word for word, nearly. It must be a teaching." She straightened, and the hall's colorless light seemed to dim by a fraction, as if the mountain itself were drawing a breath. "Very well, swordspeaker. You have refused mercy. Receive justice instead. The court withdraws its patience. Take your thread if you can find it — the spindle vault is guarded, the wardens are waking, and the winter falls within the hour. If you reach the loom before it seals, you may attempt the cutting. If you fail—" the pale eyes glittered, "—you will join your mentor in the deep galleries, and the two of you can spend eternity explaining to each other why pride was worth it."

Guards were already moving at the edges of the hall — tall figures in grey, faces smooth as eggs, moving with the terrible unhurried grace of things that had never once been late.

"Now," the Regent said, turning back toward her throne, "be escorted out. Or don't. It makes no difference to us."

* * *

They ran.

Cassian caught her arm at the hall's edge, pulling her down a side corridor that Odile had marked on her stolen sketch-map with a small anxious asterisk — *servants' passage, probably, PROBABLY.* Behind them the great doors boomed shut, and the sound of it followed them through the dark like a heartbeat.

"Bram." Cassian's voice was ragged. "What he offered you. Your mentor. You said no. You actually—" He couldn't finish it. He didn't need to.

Bram kept running, boots slapping wet stone, the oath burning up her arm like a brand finally allowed to speak. And here was the strange thing, the thing she would turn over for years afterward: she wasn't sad. Underneath the fear and the fury and the grief-with-a-reprieve, there was a clean bright coal of certainty, and it warmed her all the way down.

Rage, it turned out, was just love that had found its target.

"He taught me better than that," she said. "And when we get out of here, I'm going to tell him so. Loudly. At length. Possibly with diagrams." She glanced sideways at Cassian's white, set face, and softened. "We're getting him out too. After Liss. Both of them. Write it on your map, Odile — *two* threads to cut tonight."

From somewhere ahead in the dark, Odile's voice floated back, tight and dry and steady as a plumb line: "Already drawn, dear. Now run faster. The wardens are awake, the winter's falling, and I have it on excellent authority that the spindle vault locks from the outside."

Somewhere far above them, the first true snow of the long winter began to fall on the mountain's crown — and somewhere far below, in a cell of white stone, a man with ink-stained hands lifted his head as if he'd heard his name spoken after eleven years of silence.
