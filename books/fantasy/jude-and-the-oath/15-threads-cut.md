## 15. Threads Cut

Cassian had learned, in the galleries beneath the mountain, that grief had a texture. Upstairs it had been silk — smooth, private, something you folded away before breakfast so the servants wouldn't see. Down here it was wool. Coarse. It caught on everything.

He sat on a loom-bench in the third gallery with his sister's hand in both of his, and her hand felt like a bird's bone wrapped in cold silk.

"You're staring," Liss said.

"I'm allowed to stare at you."

"You're staring like I'm going to evaporate." She smiled, and it was almost her smile — the crooked one, the one that had gotten them both thrown out of the conservatory when they were children because she'd convinced him the music master's wig was haunted and he'd screamed loud enough to crack a window. "Cass. I'm fine."

She was not fine. Three days ago she had known his name. Two days ago she had known it only when he said it first. This morning she had looked at him with the polite blankness of a stranger at a market stall, and then — mercifully, terribly — something had surfaced, some deep thread of her that remembered being loved by him even if it could not remember loving him back.

"I know where you keep your courage," she'd whispered, gripping his sleeve. "You keep it in your left boot. You always have. Don't ask me how I know."

He had not asked. He had gone behind a pillar and wept like a child, quietly, the way he'd been taught.

Now the looms clattered around them, endless, patient, weaving winter. The soul-weavers worked in shifts that never ended, pale hands drawing pale threads through pale frames, and every finished length of cloth went into the great pattern that fell over the peaks as snow. Liss's own frame stood empty beside them. Her thread was not on the loom anymore. The court had taken it down.

That was what Bram's refusal had bought. That was what *no* sounded like, down here: a spindle turning faster, a girl unraveling.

"Cassian," Liss said again, and this time her voice had an edge he recognized — the edge she used on tutors, on suitors, on him whenever he tried to protect her from things she'd already figured out. "Look at me. What did they do?"

"Nothing. Nothing yet."

"Liar." She squeezed his fingers with strength that surprised them both. "I can feel it. Like hemming. Like someone unpicking a seam from the inside. I forgot the name of my horse this morning. I had a horse, didn't I? A terrible one. Chestnut. Bit everyone."

"Bramble," Cassian said, laughing wetly. "Bramble bit everyone including you. Especially you."

"Bramble." She said it like a prayer, holding it. Then her face crumpled, just for a moment, before she smoothed it — their mother's trick, that smoothing, and he hated their mother a little more for teaching it to her. "It's going fast now, isn't it."

"Yes," he said, because she had never once forgiven him for lying to her, and he would not give her a reason to start at the end.

* * *

He found Bram in the shadow of the gallery's great arch, arguing in whispers with Odile, whose bandaged hand was tucked against her chest like a wounded bird of its own.

"She doesn't have days," Bram was saying. "Maybe hours. The Regent means to make an example—"

"Then we take her now," Odile said. "Four against a garrison. I've seen worse odds. I've *drawn* worse odds."

"With one hand?"

"One and a half. And a truly vicious temper."

Cassian cleared his throat. Both women turned. He was aware, suddenly and completely, of how he must look — Merrow colors still faintly visible in the embroidery of his coat, the house sigil he hadn't been able to bring himself to cut out, a stag pierced by a silver needle. His family's mark. The mark worn by every warden hunting them through these halls.

"There's something I should have told you," he said. "Both of you. Before we go any further. Before you—" he gestured vaguely at Odile's plan, which involved at least two felonies and probably a third, "—do anything brave on my account."

Odile raised an eyebrow. Bram just waited, arms crossed, the way she always waited, as if she could out-stillness a mountain. Which, he supposed, she could.

"My family didn't sell my sister's soul generations ago," he said. "My father renewed the contract. Eleven years ago. There was a drought; the eastern valleys were failing; the court offered rain in exchange for... maintenance. A tithe, they called it. My father signed it in the library with the good brandy open, and I watched him do it. I was fourteen. I thought it was a land deed." His voice cracked and he let it. "Liss found out three years ago. That's why she ran. Not me. She ran *first*, and she ran alone, and I stayed in the house for two more years eating off plates bought with her thread, and then I came after her, and I have been telling myself ever since that coming after her cancels out staying. It doesn't. Nothing cancels out. I just wanted you to know who you're dying in tunnels for. A man whose whole life is a debt someone else took out in his sister's name."

The looms clattered. Somewhere far off, a warden's boot rang on stone.

"Well," Odile said at last, "I forged a royal map that got three surveyors killed, so if we're keeping score, you're winning."

"I knew," Bram said.

Cassian blinked. "What?"

"The night in the shrine. Your blood on the altar stone — oath-blood leaves a residue, even old oaths, even broken ones. Yours was all over that shrine. House Merrow's binding-signature. I've seen it before, on my mentor's papers." She unfolded her arms. "I've known since the day we met you."

"And you didn't say anything?"

"You were bleeding and terrified and you'd carried half a map across half a country to save a sister you clearly loved. I figured whatever you'd done, you'd already paid for it." She shrugged. "Also, Odile was lying about her name for the first week, so the bar was low."

"It was a *professional* alias," Odile muttered.

Cassian laughed — a broken, startled sound, tears still on his face, laughter arriving anyway like weather. He understood, dimly, that this was the thing he'd been starving for his entire gilded life: people who knew the worst of him and stayed anyway. Family was not what you inherited. Someone had told him that once, or he'd read it somewhere, or maybe he was only now inventing it, standing in hell with two liars who'd decided he was worth keeping.

"So," he said. "The plan."

"The plan is bad," Bram said. "But it's ours."

* * *

They went back to Liss together, and Cassian sent the others to watch the gallery mouth, because what came next belonged to him and no one else.

Liss was sitting up now, and though her eyes had gone glassy at the edges, there was a ferocity in her face that made his chest ache. She looked, for one moment, exactly like their grandmother — the iron one, the one who'd run the estate through two floods and one scandalous elopement.

"They're taking me apart," she said without preamble. "Aren't they."

"Yes."

"Good."

"*Good?*"

"Good that you said it out loud, idiot." She grabbed his wrist. Her grip was frighteningly strong for someone becoming thread. "Listen. I don't have much clear time left, and I can feel where things are, still — the way you can feel a splinter under skin. When they pulled my thread off the loom, I saw where they took it. Where they take all the master threads." Her breath hitched. "There's a vault. Below the Pale Court itself, below the throne-gallery, below everything. The Regent keeps the master spindles there — the originals. Every contract ever signed upstairs has its anchor down there. Burn the spindles and the contracts don't just break. They *never were*. Every debt called in, every soul held — released. All of them, Cassian. Not just me."

"How do you know this?"

"Because I've been weaving winter for six years, little brother, and thread remembers what it passes through." She smiled, and it was entirely her smile now, crooked and wicked. "The vault door is behind the Regent's dais. There's a stair disguised as a cistern channel — dry, they drained it centuries ago, arrogance is useful. The lock answers to spoken oath. Any true oath. It doesn't care whose." Her fingers tightened. "Which means yours will do. Or Bram's. Or anyone's, if it's true enough."

"Liss—"

"Shut up and listen. Third turning past the drowned chapel, there's a seam in the wall that isn't a wall. Wardens can't see it — it's warded against the bound, and every warden down here sold something once, that's how they're kept. But you—" she tapped his chest, right over the stag-and-needle, "—you never signed. You watched him sign. You're the only unbound Merrow alive. The seam opens for you or not at all."

"Why are you telling me this instead of resting?" he whispered. "Save your strength—"

"My strength is leaving anyway. I'd rather spend it." She pulled him close, forehead to forehead, and her voice dropped to something fierce and small. "You came for me. I know. Even when I forget your name, some part of me knows that, the way a body knows warmth. So finish it. Get the others. Burn the spindles. And Cassian — when you find my thread—" her voice finally broke, "—don't look at it too long. Promise me. Promise you'll burn it fast, without reading it. Some of it's going to be ugly. I was angry for six years and thread holds anger like dye."

"I promise."

"Good boy." She kissed his cheek, once, hard, the way she had when they were nine and he'd lost a fistfight defending her honor against a stableboy twice his size. "Now go. I can hear boots."

So could he. Lots of them.

* * *

The wardens came into the gallery in a wedge of grey and silver, twelve at least, halberds catching the loom-light, and at their center walked a tall figure Cassian recognized with a sickness that started in his teeth: Warden-Captain Vess, his father's oldest friend, the man who had taught him to ride. Vess wore the Merrow needle at his throat like a trophy.

"Lord Cassian," Vess said, and his voice carried the awful gentleness of a man delivering sad news he'd rehearsed. "Your father grieves. Come home. The girl is beyond keeping — but you aren't. The Regent is generous with those who kneel early."

Cassian looked at his sister, small on the bench, watching him with eyes already going distant. He looked at Bram, sword-word gathering light along her scarred arm like heat lightning. He looked at Odile, who had produced, from somewhere in her ruined coat, a knife and an expression of pure professional offense.

"No," Cassian said.

Vess sighed. "No?"

"No. I'm not kneeling. I'm not coming home. And I'm not 'a lord' anymore — I renounced it an hour ago, in front of witnesses." He nodded at Bram and Odile. "Two witnesses. That's legally sufficient in Greyhollow, I checked."

"It is *not*," Odile hissed, "but go on."

"Take them!" Vess snapped, and the wedge broke forward—

—and Bram spoke.

Not the full oath. One word of it, one syllable of the old swordspeech, and the air in the gallery turned to a struck bell. The nearest four wardens went down as if the floor had leaned. Lanterns guttered. A loom snapped a warp-thread with a sound like a scream, and every soul-weaver in the hall flinched and looked up, and Cassian saw in their faces — hundreds of faces, pale and thread-thin — the first hope he'd seen anywhere in the underworld.

"Run," Bram said hoarsely, shaking her hand as if it burned. "Everyone run. Now. *Now.*"

They ran. Odile hauled Cassian by the sleeve toward the seam Liss had described, shouting directions she was mapping aloud as she invented them — "left, arch, *down*, mind the loom-frame, Cassian if you stop to fight I will haunt you personally" — while Bram covered the rear, speaking single words of power like a miser spending coins, each one costing her something visible.

Behind them, the wardens regrouped. Behind them, Liss was lifted from the bench and carried away toward the Pale Court, and she did not struggle, because she had spent her last clear hour on something better than struggling.

At the mouth of the passage, Cassian stopped — one heartbeat, one — and looked back. Liss saw him looking. She couldn't have known his name in that moment. But she raised her hand anyway, palm out, the way she had at every departure of their childhood: *go on ahead, I'll catch up.*

He caught up to the others with his lungs full of knives.

"The cistern stair," he gasped. "Behind the dais. There's a seam — I'm the only one who can pass it. And Odile—" he grabbed her shoulder, "—I need a map of a room nobody has ever mapped, drawn in the next hour, from my memory of a five-second glance through a door I saw when I was fourteen."

Odile's eyes lit with a terrible, wonderful fire.

"Oh," she said, flexing her bandaged hand, "you sweet, doomed boy. Do you have any *idea* what I can do with an hour?"

Above them, muffled through miles of stone, the mountain shuddered — Bram's spent words still ringing in its bones — and somewhere below, the master spindles waited, and the long winter above began, very slightly, to falter.

Cassian put his hand flat against the hidden seam in the wall, said the truest thing he knew — *"I came back for her"* — and felt the stone begin, slowly, grudgingly, to open.
