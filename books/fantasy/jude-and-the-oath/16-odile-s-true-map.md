## 16. Odile's True Map

They took her compass first.

That was the insult of it. Not the manacles — she'd worn those before, in a different life, when the Guild still pretended its tribunals were about truth rather than embarrassment — and not the rough hands at her arms or the hood smelling of tallow and wet stone. No. It was the compass. A Warden with grey eyes lifted it from her coat pocket, turned it over twice as if he expected it to bite him, and then dropped it on the flagstones and ground his boot heel down until the brass cracked like an eggshell.

"I had that calibrated in Vellamark," Odile told him, because dignity was a habit you kept even when no one paid you for it. "Do you know how long the passes take to calibrate? Three days. In a cold room. With a man named Hobb who talks."

The Wardens did not answer. Wardens, she was learning, were selected for their refusal to be interesting.

* * *

The cell they gave her was better than she expected, which meant it was worse than she feared. A room that expects nothing of you is a room preparing to ask for everything. There was a bench bolted to the wall, a bucket behind a screen — someone down here had manners — and a lamp burning behind thick green glass, so that everything wore the color of deep water. The light never flickered. That was the tell. Real flame flickers; this was fed by something patient and underground, something that wanted her to lose count of the hours.

Odile Fen had spent eleven years making maps, three years forging them, and one very long afternoon learning what happened to cartographers whose rulers decided accuracy was treason. She knew rooms designed to unmake people. She sat down on the bench, folded her hands, and began counting seconds against her heartbeat, because if the mountain wanted her sense of time she would damn well make it work for every crumb.

She thought about Bram's face when the wardens had come over the ridge of the gallery road — not fear, exactly. Calculation. Bram Vane calculated the way other people breathed. *Run*, her face had said, *and I'll hold them*, and Odile had run, because arguing with a swordspeaker mid-fight is a good way to die stupid. Cassian had grabbed her sleeve and pulled her left, toward the service galleries he somehow knew despite claiming — repeatedly, unconvincingly — to have never been below the snowline before. And then the floor had opened where the mountain wanted it to open, and Odile had gone down alone into the dark with two wardens holding her arms, listening to Bram shout her name in a voice that cracked right down the middle.

Nobody had shouted for Odile Fen in eleven years. She was still deciding what to do with the feeling.

* * *

They came for her after what she estimated was six hours, though the lamp argued otherwise.

The interrogator was not what stories promised. No hooks, no braziers. He was a soft-voiced man with ink-stained fingers — actual ink, the ordinary kind — who introduced himself as Master Quillon and apologized for the accommodations as though he'd personally chosen the green glass out of cruelty.

"Mistress Fen," he said, settling across from her with a portfolio. "Formerly First Cartographer Royal. Convicted of forgery in the matter of the Merrow grants." He said it gently, the way one mentions an old scar. "You understand my position. I cannot simply believe you're here by accident. Accidents do not carry half-maps drawn in a dead man's hand."

"It wasn't a dead man's hand," Odile said. "It was mine. He dictated. I drew. That was the whole arrangement, and it's why I'm here instead of him."

Something moved behind Quillon's mild expression. Interest. Good. Odile had learned long ago that you survive interrogation the way you survive winter: by giving away heat slowly, and only ever in exchange for something.

"Tell me about the map," Quillon said.

"Which map? You've taken three off me. One is a fake. Two are misdirections. I'd tell you which, but then you'd stop feeding me, and I've seen your ration schedule through the wall seams. It's grim, Master Quillon. Even by mountain standards."

He almost smiled. She catalogued that too. Every smile was a door, and doors went both ways.

"The vault," he said. "The spindle vault. Your companions are looking for it. I want to know if you can draw it."

"And if I say yes?"

"Then we discuss what your cooperation purchases."

There it was. Odile let the silence stretch just long enough to look like consideration, though she'd made her decision somewhere around the third hour, staring at the seamless green light, doing sums.

Here were the sums. Bram and Cassian were loose in the upper galleries with a dying girl's directions and perhaps a day's lead. They could not read the underways; nobody could, the galleries rearranged themselves like a dog turning in its sleep. Without a map they would wander until the wardens found them, and then Liss's thread would stay on the loom until the winter sealed, and the mountain would win the way mountains always win: by being older and more patient than everyone else's plans.

Unless the wardens stopped looking. Unless the wardens were all, every one, standing in a vault corridor staring at a beautiful, detailed, utterly fictional map, checking alcoves that didn't exist and counting paces to doors that led nowhere.

"You can draw it," Quillon said again. Not a question this time.

"Master Quillon," Odile said, "I once forged a royal grant so convincing that the King's own archivist wept over it, and then had me arrested, and then privately asked if I'd do another one. Drawing is the only honest thing I've done in years. Yes. I can draw it."

* * *

They brought her vellum. Real vellum, calf-skin, smooth as a lie — and pens, and ink mixed with something faintly luminous that she pretended not to notice. They cleared a corner of her cell and set a table. They even, after negotiation, restored her lamp privileges to a warmer amber.

Quillon watched her work the first day. He stood too close and breathed too evenly and asked questions shaped like small talk: *Where did you train? Who taught you hachures? Is it true the Guild burns a failed journeyman's first plate?*

"Yes," Odile said, hatching the shadow of a stairwell. "Mine burned for four hours. Very moving. There were speeches."

"You don't seem like a woman who regrets her craft."

"I regret plenty. Just not the craft." She blew a hair off the vellum. "A map doesn't care who holds it. That's the tragedy and the decency of the whole profession. A map is true for whoever needs it true. Kings, smugglers, lost children. The paper doesn't check your papers."

"And the forger?"

"The forger decides who deserves to be found." She glanced up. "You should write that down. It'll look good in your report."

On the second day, Quillon brought tea, which she interpreted as either respect or poison and drank anyway, because if the mountain wanted her dead it had cheaper options. On the third day he did not come, and she worked faster.

Because here was the thing about the finest work of your life: you know it's the finest while you're making it, the way you know a fever breaking. Odile had studied the vault's real layout for exactly ninety seconds — through Liss's thread-thin description, through Cassian's half-memory of his family's old deeds, through the geometry of the galleries' drift, which she'd mapped obsessively since the descent because mapping was how she prayed. Ninety seconds of truth, and now five days of beautiful invention wrapped around it, dense as amber around a fly.

She gave them the true approach corridor — they'd verify that much, and the verification would buy the fiction its weight. Then she branched it into splendor. Counterweights marked as load-bearing stairs. A secondary entrance that would require them to drain a cistern that did not exist. The master spindle itself placed behind a triple seal in the north vault, when Liss's whisper had said south, south, near the warm looms, near the sound of the river under the world. She lettered it in her best Guild hand. She added a scale bar. She added, out of pure professional spite, a charming little compass rose, since they'd broken hers.

By the fifth day it was perfect. By the sixth she hated it, because perfect things you give away stop being yours.

* * *

Quillon came at last with two wardens and a document case, and laid the trade out in plain terms, because beneath everything he was a bureaucrat and bureaucrats, bless them, put things in writing.

Her freedom — such as it was; escorted to the gate, released above the snowline, watched thereafter — in exchange for the completed vault map and whatever intelligence she could offer regarding her companions' intentions.

"My companions intend," Odile said, "to steal a soul back from a court that stole it first. I intend to help them. Those are my intentions, fully disclosed."

"Noted," Quillon said, without irony, actually noting it. She liked him a little. It was inconvenient.

She rolled the map, tied it with the ribbon they provided, and held it a moment longer than she needed to.

"A condition," she said. "When your people search the north vault and find nothing but my excellent penmanship — and they will find nothing, Master Quillon, because there is nothing — you'll be angry. You'll want to come find me. Don't. Not because you can't, but because by then it won't matter, and chasing a forger through melting galleries is beneath an institution that wove a hundred-year winter. Take the humiliation. Frame it. It'll match the rest of your décor."

Quillon looked at her for a long moment. "You know we'll catch them regardless."

"Everyone says that," Odile said. "The snow said it. My tribunal said it. My mother said it about marriage." She handed over the roll. "I have excellent references for being underestimated."

They walked her up through the galleries, past the loom hall — she kept her eyes forward, mostly; the threads sang, and she refused to learn the song — and to a gate where the air changed and tasted of iron and distance. Above the snowline the sky was doing something obscene and grey, snow falling upward in the wind, the whole mountain exhaling its long winter in ragged gusts.

A warden unlocked her manacles. Another pointed downhill with the enthusiasm of a man pointing at weather.

Odile Fen, disgraced cartographer, walked free into the white.

She got perhaps four hundred paces before her legs informed her of their opinion, and she sat down in the snow beside a leaning waymarker and laughed until it turned into something else, the ugly kind, the kind you keep quiet even when no one's there to hear it. Because the plan was good — it was genuinely good, the north vault would eat a full search party for two days at minimum, maybe three, and Bram only needed one night — and because the plan required her to be here, alone, having traded the only thing she'd ever made that mattered.

Sacrifice, it turned out, looked exactly like sitting in snow with wet knees. No music. No applause. Somewhere below her feet, her best work was being admired by men hunting her friends.

"Well," she said to the waymarker, which had no face but seemed sympathetic. "At least the map's good."

* * *

They gave her a hut. Of course they did; the village at the snowline had been emptied by the wardens' passage, and the wardens, thorough to a fault, had left the smallest structure unlocked — a shepherd's bothy with a soot-blackened hearth and one window facing east. Someone had decided a cooperative prisoner shouldn't freeze. Someone else had decided she shouldn't be comfortable. The result was a blanket, a stool, a locked door, and a view.

Odile sat on the stool in the amber afternoon and looked at the floor.

Flagstones. Rough, uneven, mortared by someone in a hurry a century ago. And blank — that was the thing, blank as fresh vellum, and she was a woman with ink-stained fingers and no ink, no pen, no vellum, no compass, no sky.

No sky for eleven days. That was the number her heart-count gave her, and she trusted it the way she trusted hachures: completely, because everything depended on it. Eleven days of green glass lamps and corridors that curved wrong and ceilings low enough to dream about. She had mapped the entire descent in her head, every gallery, every drift of the underways, because stopping meant forgetting, and forgetting the sky was the first stage of becoming one of those pale thread-people at the looms. She'd seen it happen to a weaver who paused mid-row and stared up at nothing with an expression Odile recognized from mirrors: the face of someone trying to remember a word in a language they used to speak.

So: the floor.

She had no charcoal. She had, however, a heel of bread gone hard as slate, and the bothy's hearth, and patience — patience she had in industrial quantities, refined over three years of tribunals and exile and pretending to be merely a very dull traveling surveyor.

She burnt the bread crust black in the hearth, crushed it fine, mixed it with a spoonful of tallow scraped from the lamp the wardens had grudgingly left, and made ink. Bad ink. Ink that would smudge if anyone breathed on it. Perfect.

Then Odile Fen knelt on the flagstones of her prison, in a shepherd's hut above a mountain that had stolen her freedom and her friends' names and nearly her sky, and she drew the sky from memory.

She started with the horizon line, because all honesty starts with a horizon. East-facing, so: dawn country. The Greyhollow rooftops she used to see from the Guild tower, chimneys crooked as bad teeth. Then the clouds — high cirrus over the northern passes, the mare's-tails that meant weather coming in three days, and she drew them accurately, because somewhere in the world it was still three days from raining. Then the sun's path, arced in careful dashes, solstice to solstice. Then — kneeling, aching, laughing a little at herself — the constellations. The Plough. The Shepherd's Eye. The one the sailors called the Drowned Lantern, which wasn't official and wasn't on any Guild chart and which she drew anyway, slightly wrong, the way she'd always drawn it since she was nine and her father pointed it out from a ferry deck and lied about its name.

The stars took hours. Her knees filed formal complaints. The ink smeared every time her sleeve dragged, and she let it, because the sky is allowed to smear; that's most of what it does.

When she finished, she sat back on her heels in the lamplight and looked at the ceiling of her world rendered on the floor of her prison, and found that the tightness behind her ribs had loosened by perhaps one degree. Not healed. Loosened. Grief, like cartography, was a matter of tolerable margins.

"They'll come," she told the painted stars. "Bram will cut the thread, Cassian will cry about it in a dignified noble way, and they will stomp up this mountain and find me drawing on the floor like a madwoman, and I will never hear the end of it." She touched the Drowned Lantern, smearing its edge. "And it will be worth it. That's the part nobody warns you about. It's worth it, and it's lonely, and both things are true at once, and there's no map for that."

She slept that night beneath her own sky, on the floor, because the bench felt like surrender.

* * *

She woke to boots in the snow outside — many boots, moving fast, and the sharp whistle-code of wardens calling an alert, three rising notes she'd memorized from the galleries. She was on her feet with her ear to the door before her mind caught up with her body.

Shouting, downhill. The crunch of running. Then, cutting through the wind, a voice she knew — hoarse, furious, unmistakably alive:

"—she's in the hut, the little one with the crooked chimney, move—"

Cassian Merrow. Which meant Bram. Which meant—

Odile wrenched at the door, found it locked, and did the only reasonable thing: she picked up the stool.

She woke to boots.

Many boots, hammering through the snow outside, and the sharp whistle-code of wardens calling an alert — three rising notes she'd memorized in the galleries, the signal for breach. She was at the door with her ear pressed to the wood before her mind caught up with her body.

Voices downhill, ragged with urgency. The grind of some vast mechanism far below, felt through the floor more than heard. A captain's voice cracking across the yard: *"All companies to the north vault — NOW — the intruders are past the second seal—"*

Odile closed her eyes.

Past the second seal. Her second seal. The invented one, the beautiful impossible one, the triple-locked fiction she'd lettered in her best Guild hand — except they weren't supposed to be *past* it, they were supposed to be stuck behind it for two glorious days while —

Unless. Unless Bram had read the map the way Odile had drawn it, all of it, including the errors — including the one flaw she'd buried in the hachures of the north approach, a stairway marked as collapsed that any cartographer worth her salt would know to doubt, because Odile had drawn the collapse *too neatly*. A signature. A message in the only language they shared.

*Trust the drawing. Doubt the drawer. Go south.*

"Oh, you magnificent, reckless sword-sworn—" Odile laughed, wet-eyed, alone in a shepherd's hut while an entire garrison streamed past her window toward the vault where her friends were already fighting.

The mountain had believed her lie. That was the joke. That was the whole gorgeous joke: her finest forgery hadn't deceived the enemy — it had *instructed* them, marched them by the nose to the wrong vault, and bought Bram and Cassian exactly the opening they needed. Sacrifice without applause, yes. But apparently also sacrifice *with results*, which no one had promised her.

Down on the flagstones, her ink-drawn sky caught the lamplight. The Drowned Lantern gleamed, smeared and imperfect and true.

Odile picked up the stool, weighed it in both hands, and turned to consider the lock.

"Right," she told the empty hut, the painted stars, the mountain, and anyone else listening. "Your turn to be mapped."
