## 17. The Spindle Vault

The directions Liss had given Cassian were written in the language of someone who had spent a hundred years — or what felt like it — walking the same corridors in her sleep.

*Down past the loom hall where the light comes from no lamp. Left at the pillar that weeps. Do not touch the pillar that sings. The vault door is the one that looks like nothing at all.*

"That's not directions," Cassian had said, reading it aloud in the flicker of their stolen lantern-light. "That's a riddle."

"It's a map," Odile said, with the particular offense of her profession. "A bad one. A *drunk* one. But a map."

Now, three galleries deeper than either of them wanted to be, Bram understood that Liss hadn't been drunk at all. She'd simply been describing a place that refused to hold still.

The corridor they crept along was carved from black stone shot through with veins of something pale and slow-moving, like frost trapped mid-crawl. The veins pulsed. Not quickly — once every ten breaths or so — but each pulse made the air taste faintly of iron and old promises. Bram's scarred arm ached in time with them, a dull toothache of an ache, as though the oath under her skin recognized the rhythm of this place the way a dog recognizes its own name.

"Stop doing that," she muttered to it.

"Talking to your arm again?" Cassian whispered.

"My arm listens better than most people."

He smiled at that, but it was a thin smile, stretched over fear like cloth over a frame. He'd been holding himself together since Liss's last clear hour — since she'd pressed his hand against her unraveling cheek and told him about the spindle, about the vault, about the wardens' rotation — and Bram had watched him do it the way you watch someone carry water in cupped hands: carefully, knowing it would spill eventually, hoping it wouldn't spill *now*.

She knew the feeling. She'd been carrying herself that way for three years.

*Left at the pillar that weeps.* It appeared out of the dark ahead of them, a column of stone streaked with long mineral tears, and beyond it the corridor split three ways. Liss's directions had only mentioned two.

"The map's wrong," Cassian breathed.

"The map is *honest*," Odile had said before they parted from her — Odile, who was currently two levels up, sitting in a cell of her own choosing, trading her finest forgery for their lives. "Honest maps show you what's true. True things change."

Bram chose the leftmost passage anyway, because her arm ached harder when she leaned toward the others, and she had learned — slowly, stubbornly, over twenty years of oaths and one very long winter — to trust the parts of herself that hurt.

* * *

The singing pillar nearly got them.

They heard it before they saw it: a high, sweet note threading through the silence, so pure that Bram's first instinct was to walk toward it with her eyes closed. She caught Cassian doing the same — his chin lifting, his feet drifting sideways like a man asleep on a boat — and grabbed his collar.

"No," she said. "We don't touch anything that *sings*. That was the whole instruction."

"I wasn't going to—"

"You were absolutely going to. You had the face of a man about to propose marriage to a rock."

That startled a laugh out of him — small, cracked, real. In the months since the shrine, since the bleeding boy who couldn't look at his own house colors, Cassian Merrow had learned to laugh maybe four ways, and Bram had catalogued all of them the way Odile catalogued coastlines. This one — the startled one, the one that came out sideways — was her favorite. It meant he was still in there. Still choosing to be.

They gave the singing pillar a berth wide enough to drive a cart through, and the note behind them thinned into something almost petulant, like a child denied a sweet.

Then the corridor ended, and there it was.

The door that looked like nothing at all.

It was a wall. That was the trick of it — a wall of seamless black stone, identical to every other wall in the gallery, except that Bram's arm burned now, truly burned, the oath beneath her skin straining toward it like a hound at a leash. She pressed her palm flat against the stone and felt, beneath the cold surface, a vast slow turning, like a wheel turning underwater, like a spindle turning thread.

Words rose in her throat unbidden. Her oath-words. The first line of them, the part she'd spoken a hundred times in training yards and never once in anger:

*I am the hand that holds the edge.*

The wall shivered. A seam appeared, hairline-fine, glowing faintly gold, tracing the outline of a door tall enough for a giant and narrow enough for a penitent.

"It wants your voice," Cassian said quietly.

"It can want." But she spoke the second line too, barely above a whisper — *I am the word that keeps the peace* — and the seam widened, and cold air poured out smelling of snow that had never fallen anywhere, and they stepped through into the vault of the Pale Court.

* * *

The room inside was round and enormous and utterly silent, and it was full of spindles.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. They stood in ranks like a forest of silver trees, each one taller than two men, each one turning slowly, endlessly, wound with thread that caught the sourceless light and threw it back in colors Bram had no names for. Some threads were white as bone. Some were grey as ash. One, near the entrance, was a warm brown that made her chest tighten inexplicably until she realized it looked like bread crust, like hearth-smoke, like a hundred small human comforts spun thin enough to wind around a spindle.

"The winter," Cassian whispered. "This is where they weave it."

Every thread fed upward, converging somewhere above into darkness Bram couldn't see the top of. And where the threads passed through the air, the air itself changed — she could see it, could *feel* it on her skin — growing colder, slower, heavier. The long winter wasn't falling on the world above by accident. It was being *woven*. Stitch by patient stitch, by souls who didn't remember they were souls, on spindles that never stopped turning.

Her rage came up quiet, which was how her worst rage always came. Quiet and total, like floodwater.

"Liss's thread," she said. "Find Liss's thread."

"How? They all look—"

"She told you. Think. What did she tell you?"

Cassian closed his eyes. Bram watched him reach for it — watched him walk, in his mind, back down the corridor of his sister's last clear hour, refusing to let any of it slip. His lips moved.

"*Mine is the one that hums,*" he recited. "*You'll hear it before you see it. It hums the lullaby Mother used to sing, the one about the fisherman's daughter, and I always sang it wrong and she always corrected me and—*" His voice caught. He opened his eyes. "She said I'd know it because I always sang it wrong."

"Then sing it wrong," Bram said gently. "Sing it exactly as wrong as you always did."

And Cassian Merrow — runaway lord, coward faced honestly, boy who had fled a house that sold his sister — lifted his voice in the middle of the Pale Court's vault and sang a children's lullaby off-key on purpose, and it was the bravest thing Bram had seen anyone do in three years.

* * *

The thread found them before they found it.

It came as a sound first — a humming, sweet and wavering, rising above the great silent turning of everything else. Then as a color: a thread of pale gold running through the ranks of spindles, not anchored to any of them but *crossing* the vault in a long low arc, like a bridge, like a held breath. It led toward the center of the chamber, where one spindle stood apart from all the rest — larger, older, its silver gone dark with use, turning more slowly than the others with the gravity of something that had been turning since before winters had names.

The master spindle.

Liss's thread ran straight to it.

"Of course," Bram breathed. "Of course it does. She's not just woven into the pattern. She's *anchoring* it. That's why she was unraveling so fast — they're using her—" The floodwater rage rose another inch. "They're using her as a *knot*."

"So we cut her free." Cassian's hands had gone to the short blade at his belt — the one weapon he'd carried since the glacier, the one he'd frozen beside on the slope while men in his own colors advanced. "Cut the thread, pull her out, run."

"It's not going to be that simple."

"It's never that simple. I've read books."

"Books lie," Bram said. "I write them."

But there was no time to argue about simplicity, because that was the moment the wardens arrived.

* * *

They came through the far wall, which was the way things came and went in this place — six of them, tall and grey, their armor bearing the faded crest of House Merrow: a heron swallowing a star. The sight of it hit Cassian like a physical blow; Bram saw him sway, saw the old freeze climbing his throat.

"Cassian." She kept her voice low and even. "Look at me. Not the crest. The crest is cloth. You are not."

"They wore those colors when they took her," he whispered. "The night my father signed. I remember the heron. I've always remembered the heron."

"And now the heron remembers *you*." She turned to face the wardens, putting herself between them and him, and felt the strangest thing rise in her chest — not fear, not quite courage, but the thing that lived between them, the thing she'd only ever felt standing next to people she loved. "Can you fight?"

"I don't know."

"Good. Honest. Now find out." She drew her sword — the plain, honest blade she'd carried out of Greyhollow wrapped in a luck-knot — and spoke the third line of her oath aloud, the one that made the air ring: *I am the debt that will be paid.*

The wardens paused. Even hollow things, it seemed, remembered what swordspeakers were.

Then they came on, all six at once, and there was no more time for talking.

* * *

Fighting in the vault was like fighting inside a bell. Every clash rang and rang again, the sound rolling up through the threads overhead and coming back changed, and Bram learned within three exchanges to stop listening and start feeling. The wardens fought without heat, without anger, with the terrible patience of winter itself — they did not try to kill her so much as to *outlast* her, to make her slow, to make her cold, to wait for the warmth to leave her limbs the way it left the world above.

She gave them warmth instead. Three years of banked fire, of charms sold for copper, of magic folded small and hidden — she let a little of it out, just a little, just enough, and her blade woke with a light like dawn on snow.

Cassian fought beside her, and he was not good at it, and he did it anyway. That was the whole of it. He took a warden's blow meant for her back and went down to one knee and got up again. He shouted — actually shouted, the boy who froze — and the sound bounced around the vault and came back sounding like five men, and two wardens hesitated, and Bram put them both down in the space of that hesitation.

"Go!" she called to him. "The spindle! Cut her loose!"

But when he reached the master spindle and raised his blade to Liss's golden thread, the thread *moved*. Slid aside, quick as an eel. When he swung again, it wrapped once around his wrist — gently, the way a tired woman takes a hand — and the humming lullaby faltered, and Cassian stood there with his sword arm caught in his sister's soul and his face coming apart.

"It won't let me," he said. "Bram, it knows me, it's *holding my hand*, why won't it let me—"

Because it wasn't rope, Bram thought, fighting her way clear of the last warden, her breath tearing, her arm burning brighter than it had ever burned. Because it was a person. Because you cannot cut a person loose the way you cut a knot; someone has to *unmake* the binding, and bindings like these — court-bindings, oath-bindings, soul-debt — answered to only one kind of voice in all the world.

An oath-bound voice.

Hers.

She knew it the way you know a stair in the dark. She had known it, probably, since the dying stranger pressed the map into her hands, since the first ache toward the north, since the door beneath the mountain had opened to her words like a flower to sun. Everything — the whole descent, the whole winter, the whole terrible architecture of the Pale Court — had been leading here, to this spindle, to this thread, to this single truth waiting patiently for her to catch up:

Only she could cut it. And cutting it would cost the one thing she had guarded for three years, the gift under her skin, the oath that was the last living piece of her mentor's faith.

The last warden fell. The vault went quiet, except for the turning, and the humming, and Cassian's ragged breathing.

"Bram?" He was still bound to the thread, still gentle with it, cradling his own sword arm like something wounded. "What is it? What are you thinking?"

She walked toward him across the silent floor, past the ranks of turning spindles, past the threads of bone-white and ash-grey and hearth-brown, and she looked up at the master spindle where Liss's soul was knotted into the pattern of a hundred-year winter, and she felt her oath rise in her throat — all of it, every line, the whole long vow she had never yet spoken end to end — gathering like weather.

"I'm thinking," she said, "that I finally understand what my mentor was trying to buy."

And the doors of the vault — every door, all at once, behind and ahead and in the walls themselves — began, softly, to open.
