## 18. The Oath Spoken

The spindle vault was colder than the rest of the mountain, which Bram would not have believed possible until she stepped into it and felt the cold go through her like a needle through cloth. Not wind-cold. Not snow-cold. This was the cold of a thing that had never once been warm, a cold with intention behind it.

The vault was round, and vast, and wrong in a way her eyes kept trying to correct. Threads hung from a ceiling so high it vanished into darkness — thousands upon thousands of them, silver-pale, each one humming faintly like a plucked string that refused to stop. And at the center, rising out of a floor of black ice, stood the master spindle: a pillar of pale wood taller than three men, wound with thread from base to crown, turning slowly, endlessly, with no hand to turn it.

Every rotation, Bram realized, was a day of winter. Every pass of the shuttle-arms that circled it like patient moths wove another mile of snow onto the world above.

"Gods," Cassian breathed beside her. He'd come through the gap in the wardens' line with blood on his knuckles and no memory of how he'd gotten either, and now he stood staring up at the loom-work with an expression Bram recognized from her own mirror: the look of someone seeing, for the first time, the true size of the machine that had been grinding their life.

"Don't look at it," she said quietly. "Look for her."

Liss's thread. That was the task. Liss's directions had been fragmentary — *the vault is round, the door faces east, my thread hangs low because I am nearly unwound* — but fragmentary was more than nothing, and Odile had bought them this far with her forged map and her frozen fingers, and Bram intended to make every step of that price count.

They moved along the vault's rim, keeping to the shadow of the walls, where the threads grew sparse. The humming changed as they walked — rose, fell, shifted pitch — and Bram understood with a lurch that the threads were singing to one another. Or perhaps they were screaming. At this volume, after this many years, she supposed there wasn't much difference.

"There." Cassian pointed.

It hung lower than the others, just as Liss had promised: a single silver thread, thin as spider silk, drooping toward the floor like a stem too tired to hold its flower. It trembled when the great spindle turned, and each tremor took something from it. Bram could *see* it fraying, could see little motes of light sloughing off its length and drifting down into the black ice and going out.

Liss. That was Liss, unraveling one turn at a time.

"I'll watch the door," Cassian said. His voice shook and he didn't pretend otherwise, and Bram loved him a little for not pretending. "However long you need. However many of them come through it." He drew his knife — a dueling knife, pretty and useless-looking, though he'd learned better with it on the glacier — and set his back against the eastern wall.

Bram approached the thread alone.

Up close, it hummed louder, and she could hear what the sound was made of: laughter, half-remembered; a girl's voice saying *Cassian, don't tell Mother*; the creak of a garden swing; fragments of a whole life, worn smooth by years of being woven into weather. She reached out to touch it and stopped herself just in time, because her fingers remembered what thread did to oath-bound skin, and her arm — her scarred, burning, three-years-silent arm — had already begun to ache in answer.

Because here was the truth she'd been carrying since the village elders told it, and she could not put it down any longer:

A thread bound by sale could be cut by anyone with a sharp enough blade. But a thread woven into the long winter itself — a soul threaded through the loom that spun the snows — that bond could only be severed by a swordspeaker speaking her whole oath. All of it. Beginning to end. The words her mentor had taught her at nine years old, word by word, telling her *never say them all together, Bram, never, the oath is a door and every word is a hinge* —

She had spent three years not saying them. She had hidden them under charmwork and haggling and the careful smallness of a life shrunk down to fit inside an outlaw city. And now the only way to give Liss Merrow back her name was to open that door all the way and let everything behind it burn.

"Bram." Cassian's voice, low. "Wardens. Two, maybe three, coming along the gallery. I hear boots."

"How long?"

"A minute. Less."

A minute. Well. She'd written letters in less time. She'd buried her mentor in less time, if you counted only the digging.

Bram planted her feet on the black ice, facing the trembling thread, and rolled up her sleeve.

The mark beneath was exactly as it had always been: a line of script coiling from wrist to elbow, ink-dark, faintly warm, the oath written in the old tongue that predated every kingdom and every court above or below any mountain. Her mentor's handwriting, she'd thought for years. Then she'd learned, in a village that remembered, that it was older than him — that every swordspeaker's arm bore the same words, that the oath was not his gift to her but her inheritance of a debt he had died trying to pay.

*I swear by iron and breath,* it began. Simple words. Kind words, almost. The kind of words you could carry for years without feeling their weight, the way you can carry a stone in your pocket and forget it's there until you reach for a coin.

Behind her, steel rang against steel. Cassian shouted something defiant and slightly absurd — *"You're interrupting private business!"* — and Bram smiled despite everything, and began to speak.

"*I swear by iron and breath.*"

The first word left her mouth and the mark woke.

Not the ache she knew. Something far older and hungrier — heat unspooling along her veins like a rope of embers being drawn through her, and every word that followed pulled it deeper.

"*By the door beneath and the sky above.*"

The threads around her shrieked — all of them, the whole vault, a chorus of stolen souls crying out as her oath resonated through the loom-work like a bell struck inside a cathedral. The spindle's slow rotation stuttered. Somewhere far above, she imagined, snow hung suspended over Greyhollow, confused.

"*To guard what sleeps and wake what's owed.*"

Her knees buckled. She locked them. The heat reached her heart and she discovered, distantly, that she was weeping without any of the usual grief — tears simply falling, the body doing what bodies do when they burn, and somewhere beyond the roaring in her ears Cassian was still fighting, still shouting, bless him, still holding the door with borrowed courage because courage was the one thing found family lent freely.

"*To speak the cutting word when cutting is needed—*"

The thread before her flared white.

It heard her. Of course it heard her — it had been waiting three years and more, waiting since a woman froze to death smiling outside a charm-stall and pressed a map into unwilling hands, waiting perhaps since the first swordspeaker swore these words at a door that should have stayed shut. The whole vault leaned toward Bram Vane like a crowd leaning toward a verdict.

"*—and to pay the debt with whatever remains.*"

One sentence left. She had saved it, all these years, without meaning to — the closing words her mentor had made her promise to keep whole, and she had kept them whole out of fear, out of grief, out of the simple animal wish to remain herself. They sat on her tongue now like coins she'd hoarded past all usefulness.

The eastern door burst inward. Wardens — four, five, grey cloaks and house colors that made Cassian scream — and then Cassian himself, bleeding, throwing himself between them and her, arms spread, buying seconds with his body because it was the only coin he had left.

"Bram!" he roared. "*Now!*"

She gave the power back.

That was how it felt — not losing it, not spending it, but handing it over, deliberately, the way you return a borrowed coat at the end of a long winter. She spoke the final words of the oath her mentor had died owing, and the fire in her arms became a flood became a light that filled the vault wall to wall, and she aimed all of it, every last ember of a lifetime's binding, at one thin silver thread hanging low over the ice.

"*I am Bram Vane. My word is iron. My word is given. Cut.*"

The thread parted with a sound like a held breath released after twenty years.

And the mountain came apart.

Not gently. The spindle screamed — an actual scream, a voice, the Pale Regent's fury pouring through the loom itself — and the great wheel lurched and stopped, and every thread in the vault snapped taut and then slack, and the black ice beneath Bram's feet split from wall to wall. Snowmelt burst upward through the cracks, decades of hoarded winter suddenly with nowhere to be, and the ceiling shed its darkness in falling sheets of ancient frost.

"Liss's thread—" Cassian caught Bram as her legs finally quit on her, hauling her upright, and she grabbed the cut thread's trailing end — it drifted weightless now, free, glowing softly, a soul unmoored and *rising* — and pressed it into his hands.

"It goes home," she rasped. Smoke poured off her lips when she spoke; her voice sounded scorched even to herself. "Follow it. Through the galleries. It knows the way out."

"You're coming."

"The mountain's falling, Cass. Somebody has to not slow you down."

He looked at her — really looked, the way people look when they are memorizing — and then he did the most Cassian thing imaginable: he laughed, wild and cracked, kissed her forehead like a brother, and ran, the freed thread streaming ahead of him through the smoke like a compass needle pointing at daylight.

Bram turned back toward the collapsing vault.

She should have run. She knew she should have run. But the spindle stood there dying, and wound around it, tangled in its last convulsions, were ten thousand threads that weren't sold souls at all — couldn't be — smaller, dimmer, threads of *weather*, of pattern, of the long winter's terrible design. And as the loom shattered, those threads unraveled into the floodwater and dissolved, and Bram felt it happening overhead: the endless snowfall faltering. Stopping. A century of manufactured winter coming undone all at once, which meant, she thought with the strange clarity of the very exhausted, that all of it was about to come down the mountain at once.

The water hit her like a door.

Cold took her — real cold, ordinary cold, the honest cold of snowmelt, and she had a moment to think *so this is what magicless feels like, this is what everyone else has always weighed* — and then she was tumbling in the dark with the vault breaking apart around her, deafened by the roar of the long winter dying, reaching for anything and finding nothing, because there was nothing left in her to find. No sword-word. No ember. Just a woman, mortal as bread, drowning in the wreckage of a bargain three generations old.

*So,* she thought, absurdly, as the dark closed in. *This is how it ends. Not with an oath. With arithmetic.*

Something grabbed her wrist.

Something gripped hard, calloused and certain, and hauled, and the dark tore open into chaos and torchlight and Odile Fen's face, furious and tear-streaked and frost-scarred, screaming words the water ate. Another hand — small, strong, familiar — caught her collar. And then Bram was coughing riverwater onto black stone while two people she had known for mere weeks refused, with a stubbornness no court could match, to let her die.

"*Don't you dare,*" Odile was shouting, shaking her. "Don't you *dare* die after all that, I traded a *prison cell* for you, I want that repaid in full—"

"Cassian," Bram croaked. "Liss—"

"Gone ahead." Odile hauled her arm across her shoulders. "Thread's leading them to the gate. Move, you magnificent idiot, the whole slope is coming down."

They ran. Behind them the vault folded into the dark, the spindle toppling in slow silence, and above — far above, through splitting stone — Bram saw, for one impossible heartbeat, a crack of grey daylight widening like an eye opening after a hundred-year sleep.

Then the mountain shrugged, and the three of them went up through the ruin of the underworld, running hand in hand into the falling sky.

* * *

The gate was already open when they reached it.

Not broken — *open*, standing wide for the first time in living memory, its carved oath-words dark and dead on the stone, and beyond it the passage sloped up and up toward a light that was unmistakably, gloriously the sun. Cassian stood in the threshold with Liss in his arms, and Bram's heart stopped, because the girl was so thin, thread-thin, barely a person-shaped shadow—

But her chest moved. And her eyes opened.

And Liss Merrow, who had not known her own name an hour ago, looked up at her brother's filthy, bleeding, weeping face, and frowned, and said — in a voice rusty as an unused hinge:

"...You got *tall*."

Cassian made a sound that was laughing and sobbing at once and would have embarrassed a lesser family thoroughly, and Bram, sagging between Odile's shoulder and the doorpost, felt something loosen in her chest that had been tight for three years and knew it would never tighten quite the same way again.

Then the light through the gate brightened, and the wind changed, and from somewhere high above them came a deep, rushing roar — the sound of a century of stolen winter, all at once, beginning to come down the mountain.

Odile looked up at the noise, then at the four of them, and said, very calmly:

"Run *now*, cry later."
