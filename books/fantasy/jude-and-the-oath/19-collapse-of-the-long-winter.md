## 19. Collapse of the Long Winter

*Odile*

The cell shook, and Odile Fen did not waste a single heartbeat being surprised.

She had spent three days in it, drawing the sky on the flagstones with a stub of charcoal she'd smuggled in her boot heel — because if you were going to be imprisoned beneath a mountain by soul-traders, you might as well be imprisoned with good boots — and when the first crack split the ceiling and dust rained down like grey snow, she looked up and thought, *Well. Bram always was an overachiever.*

The door did not shatter so much as give up. The lock had been warded, but wards were ink, and ink was her native language, and three days of boredom had given her plenty of time to study the sigils through the bars until she could have redrawn them blindfolded. When the mountain lurched again, the ward-ink flickered like a candle in wind — and Odile pressed two charcoal-blackened fingers to the seam of the door and spoke the counter-word she'd been composing since hour one.

The lock sighed open like a grateful debtor.

"Amateur work," she told the empty corridor, and stepped out into chaos.

The galleries beyond were screaming. Not people — stone. The long galleries of the underworld ran on loom-song, on the steady rhythm of threads pulled tight, and somewhere above — or below, or *everywhere*, the geometry down here had never made sense to her and she'd stopped apologizing for it — Bram Vane had cut the thread that anchored winter itself. Odile could feel it in the way the air moved, in the way the walls leaned, in the way the endless blue-white glow of the weave-lamps guttered like they'd been caught telling a lie.

Snow-melt was already coming down the main gallery in a brown, churning ribbon, ankle-deep and rising. The Pale Court's wardens stood in knots along the walkways, and here was the thing about soldiers who had never once been contradicted: they froze beautifully.

Odile did not freeze. She grabbed a fallen warden's staff, used it to vault a spreading crack in the floor, and started running toward the loom hall, mapping as she went — left at the weeping pillar, straight past the collapsed arch, mind sketching the route even as her boots slipped, because drawing was how she prayed and she intended to pray all the way out of this mountain.

Halfway there, a side gallery gave way with a sound like the world clearing its throat, and the flood came through.

Odile went down. The water took her staff, took her footing, took her dignity, and for one horrible moment it took her sense of direction too — which, for Odile Fen, was worse than drowning. She surfaced gasping, clawed at a root of carved stone that turned out to be a warden's outflung arm, and hauled herself onto a shelf of rock above the torrent.

A warden stared up at her from the water's edge, helmet knocked askew, utterly bewildered.

"Your court is collapsing," Odile informed him, teeth chattering. "I'd run."

And then — because she was who she was, and because some debts deserved interest — she kicked him squarely in the chest, watched him go under, and kept moving.

*That,* she thought grimly, hauling herself along the ledge toward where Cassian and Liss would be, *was for every map I ever forged under threat.* It wasn't much of a prayer. But it was honest, and honesty had gotten fashionable lately.

* * *

*Cassian*

Cassian Merrow had imagined this moment perhaps four hundred times, and in none of those imaginings had his sister weighed quite so much.

Liss was light. That was the horror of it — she was thread-thin, hollowed, her bones like a bird's — and still his arms burned, still his legs shook, still he staggered through the crumbling loom hall with her cheek against his collarbone and her breath coming in small, confused hitches.

"Cass," she said, for maybe the tenth time since he'd told her. "You said your name was Corin."

"I lied." He ducked under a falling beam, felt it clip his shoulder hard enough to see stars. "I'm sorry. I lie badly. Ask anyone."

"You have my chin," Liss said wonderingly, and then the floor tilted and Cassian lost the argument entirely to the business of not dying.

The way back to the gate was gone. He knew it was gone; Odile had drawn him the route twice, in that fierce careful hand of hers, and the second gallery — the one with the weeping pillar — had come down behind them like a held breath finally let out. Which meant the only path was the one nobody drew: upward, through the service channels the weavers used, the ones that stank of dye and old wool and, increasingly, of cold clean water pouring down from the world above.

The mountain was dying the way a dam dies — slowly, then all at once, then slowly again, in a manner designed to maximize terror.

"Liss, hold on." He shifted her weight. She wrapped her arms around his neck without being asked, and something in his chest cracked open at how easily she trusted him now, this stranger with her chin, when an hour ago she hadn't known his name. Grief and joy arrived together, tangled like thread, and Cassian decided there wasn't time to untangle them. Later. Everything later.

He found the channel mouth half-flooded and went in anyway.

Water shoved at his knees, then his thighs. Liss clung. Somewhere behind them the great hall of the looms groaned, and he heard — he would hear it in dreams for years — the sound of ten thousand threads snapping at once, a sound like the sky tearing, like a century of stolen winters losing their grip.

Then the water rose fast, black and violent, and Cassian learned what it meant to fight a river indoors.

He went down twice. The second time, the current pinned him against a grate, Liss torn half out of his arms, and he thought with strange clarity: *Father sold her for a title, and I will not lose her to plumbing.* He got his shoulder into it. He screamed — no words, no oath, just noise — and the mountain, which owed his family nothing, gave him nothing, and he won through sheer stupid stubbornness, dragging Liss onto a stair that climbed toward a grey far-off light.

Above him, muffled by tons of collapsing stone, someone was shouting his name.

Not *Corin*. His real one.

"MERROW! Up here! Follow the light, you idiot!"

Odile. Alive, soaked, furious, hanging off a broken gantry with one hand extended, and Cassian had never loved a forged map in his life the way he loved that voice right then.

* * *

*Bram*

Bram Vane had been magicless for exactly eleven minutes, and she had nearly died twice.

The gate chamber was flooding. Of course it was. The long winter hadn't simply ended; it had come *home*, all hundred years of stored snowmelt roaring down through channels the mountain had dug for it over centuries, and the sealed gate — her gate, carved with her oath, opened by her voice — stood open at the top of a staircase that was becoming a waterfall.

She'd spoken the oath. She'd cut the thread. And the price had come due instantly, thoroughly, the way debts do: the swordspeaker's fire under her skin hadn't faded, it had *left*, gone out like a lamp pinched between wet fingers, and what remained was a scarred arm and a body that obeyed the ordinary laws of ordinary flesh.

Ordinary flesh, it turned out, drowned easily.

The first near-death had been the spindle vault's ceiling coming down. The second was a warden who didn't care about collapsing courts and wanted very much to finish his assignment; Bram had beaten him with a length of broken loom-frame and hated every second of it, because there had been a time when a word would have done it — one word, clean and bright — and instead she'd swung wood like any common brawler, grunting, graceless, *mortal.*

Now the third was water.

It came down the gate stairs all at once, a white wall of meltwater, and Bram was halfway up them when it hit. The world became noise and pressure. She tumbled — struck stone — lost which way was up — and the awful, absurd clarity of it: *this is how it ends, not with a curse or a court, just water, just gravity, just a body doing what bodies do.* Her lungs burned. Her scarred arm, her dead arm, ached ferociously, as if the oath were trying to say something from beyond its own grave.

*No one escapes alone*, the elders had said. At the moment she escaped nothing at all.

Then a hand closed around her wrist.

Not her sword-hand. The other one. Small fingers, ink-stained, gripping with the desperate strength of someone who had once mapped a coastline in a storm and refused to accept the sea's corrections.

Bram broke the surface coughing, and Odile was there, wedged in a crevice of the gate's frame, hair plastered flat, shouting over the roar: "—grab the *stone*, not me, I'm slippery—"

"I've noticed!"

"Cassian! CASSIAN!" Odile shrieked upward into the grey light, and Bram, clinging to carved oath-words older than her grandmother's grandmother, saw them: Cassian in the upper stair, Liss on his back, both of them leaning down into the spray, Cassian's arm straining, reaching.

Bram reached back.

For a moment — one long, grinding moment — nothing happened except everyone failing to reach everyone, the water pulling, the stone slick, the mountain groaning its disapproval. Bram thought, distantly, that this was the whole story in miniature: four liars and cowards and exiles, all of them an arm's length short, all of them only saved by holding on together.

Cassian caught her forearm. Odile braced. Liss — thin, unravelled, *alive* — reached down and took Bram's hand, and her grip was shockingly strong, the grip of someone who had worked a loom for a hundred borrowed years and knew exactly what a thread was worth.

They pulled.

Bram came out of the water like a cork, like a fish, like a woman with no dignity whatsoever, and lay gasping on the gate's threshold with the sky — the actual sky, grey and snowing lightly and more beautiful than any loom-woven dawn — spread out above her.

"Don't," she wheezed, before anyone could speak, "don't say anything noble. I have no magic left and I *will* cry."

"We wouldn't dream of it," Odile said, and cried a little herself, quietly, facing away, pretending to check the horizon for wardens.

* * *

*Liss*

Later — much later, when the four of them had dragged themselves down the mountainside to the shelter of a leaning cairn while the world behind them thundered and settled — Liss sat with her brother's coat around her shoulders and listened to the long winter die.

It sounded like rain. That was all. A hundred years of stolen snow, released all at once across the peaks, coming down the slopes as melt and slush and ordinary weather, and the sound of it was so gentle after the loom-hall's endless rhythm that she laughed — a cracked, rusty laugh, her first in longer than she could measure, and everyone turned to look at her as though she'd performed a miracle.

"She laughs," Cassian whispered, delighted and devastated at once.

"It's raining," Liss said. "You all nearly died and the grand ending is *rain.*"

"The grand ending," Odile said primly, ringing meltwater out of her sleeve, "is that I am going to draw this mountain correctly, from memory, and the cartographers' guild can weep with shame."

"The grand ending," Bram said, "is soup."

They all looked at her. She was grey-faced, shivering, her scarred arm cradled against her chest — and she was smiling, tired and crooked and real, and Liss found she liked her immediately, this stranger who had torn a hole in a century of winter and asked only for soup.

"You don't know me," Liss said. "Why did you pull me out? You'd never met me before today."

Bram considered this with the seriousness of someone deciding an important question. "Your brother lied to a warden for you," she said at last. "Badly. With his whole heart. People like that are worth keeping."

"And you," Liss said, turning to Odile, "kicked a warden into a flood."

"He had it coming professionally."

"And you—" Liss began, toward Cassian, and found she couldn't finish, and didn't need to. He understood. He always seemed to understand, this brother-shaped stranger, and she was learning him the way Odile learned coastlines: patiently, in bad light, one line at a time.

Behind them, deep in the mountain, something vast shifted its weight and went quiet. The Pale Court, whatever remained of it, buried under a hundred years of returning weather — or waiting, patient as debt, for another door, another oath, another desperate soul to bargain with. Liss felt it the way you feel a storm passing: not gone, just moved on.

Above the cairn, through the thinning snow, the first pale stripe of evening sky showed itself — ordinary, unmagical, free.

"Well," said Odile, producing her charcoal stub and flattening a scrap of hide against her knee. "Someone should record this properly. Once, accurately, and *only once* — I refuse to forge anything ever again." She glanced up, and her eyes were bright and sharp and, for the first time since Bram had met her, unafraid. "Starting with the road home. Does anyone actually remember the way?"

Four faces looked at one another over the ruins of a stolen century.

"No," they said, almost in unison — and began, laughing and arguing and shivering, to figure it out together.
