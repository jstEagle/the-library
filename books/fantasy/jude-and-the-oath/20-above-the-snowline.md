## 20. Above the Snowline

The first thing Liss Merrow knew was the color of the ceiling.

It was not white. That mattered more than she could say, though she could not have said why. It was smoke-dark timber, silvered with age, and a spider had built its web in the corner above the window with the patience of someone who intended to stay. She lay in a bed that smelled of lavender and lanolin and watched dust drift through a bar of sunlight, and she thought: *I know this color. I have always known this color.*

She did not know her own name.

That arrived later — or rather, it was given to her later, by a girl with a voice like a cracked bell who sat down on the edge of the bed and said, "You're awake. Don't panic. Well. Panic a little, it's only sensible."

Liss opened her mouth and found her throat full of wool. "Where—"

"Mountain village. Elders' longhouse. You've been asleep four days." The girl — young, freckled, with ink stains up both wrists as if she'd been caught in an inkwell avalanche — poured water from a clay jug into a cup and held it to Liss's lips with the brisk tenderness of someone who had done this for strangers before. "Small sips. Your name is Liss. You're going to want to argue with me about that in about ten minutes, so I thought I'd get ahead of it."

"I don't—" Liss drank because her body wanted the water even if the rest of her was somewhere else entirely. "I don't remember."

"I know," the girl said, and her briskness softened into something almost gentle. "Maren. I'm Maren. I'm the one who's been changing your bandages and listening to you talk in your sleep. You talk in your sleep *a lot*. Mostly about thread. And once about a dog named Turnip, which I feel is important information I now possess forever."

"Turnip," Liss repeated. The word landed somewhere deep and rang like a struck bell. Her eyes were wet before she understood why. "I had a dog."

"You had a dog," Maren agreed, and mopped at Liss's face with a sleeve without asking permission, which Liss would learn was simply how Maren operated. "And a brother. And a house with blue shutters. And a hundred other things. They're not gone. The elders say they're not gone — they're just folded up small, like linen in a chest, and it takes time to unfold them without tearing."

"How do you fold a life?" Liss whispered.

"Badly, apparently," said Maren, "if what happened to you is any measure. But we're getting ahead of things. Drink your water."

* * *

The unfolding took exactly as long as everyone promised and nothing like the shape anyone expected.

Her name came back first, which felt like cheating — Maren had already told her, so how could Liss trust that she remembered it rather than merely borrowed it? Then came the house with blue shutters, arriving whole one morning while she was eating porridge, so suddenly that she set down her spoon and cried into the bowl for a quarter hour while the elder women pretended very loudly to discuss grain stores.

Her brother came back in pieces, and the pieces hurt.

There was a boy in her memory with scraped knees and a talent for finding trouble, and when Cassian Merrow walked into the longhouse — tall, hollow-eyed, hands bandaged from the mountain — Liss looked at him and felt the two images grind against each other like millstones. He stopped three steps inside the door. He didn't come closer. She understood, in some wordless way, that he was waiting to be recognized, and that being unrecognized again might be the thing that finally broke him.

"Cassian," she tried.

His whole face changed. "Yes."

"The boy with the scraped knees. You fell out of the pear tree trying to reach the ones I couldn't."

"I fell out of the pear tree *every summer*," he said, and his voice cracked clean through, and then he crossed the room in four strides and held her so carefully, as if she were made of the same thread-thin stuff she'd been woven from, and Liss held him back and remembered: the pear tree, the shutters, their mother singing off-key, their father's maps spread across the dinner table. Remembered loving him. Remembered, with a cold slow horror rising under the warmth, everything after.

Because the grief came back too. That was the part no one warned her about — that memory is not a gift you get to choose pieces of. Along with the pears came the ledger. Along with her mother's voice came the argument overheard through a door, the word *sold*, the shape of her own name spoken like a figure in a column. She had been eleven years old in that memory. She had been eleven years old and already owed.

"They sold me," she said into Cassian's shoulder, not as a question.

"Yes." His arms tightened. "Before either of us could understand what it meant. Before I was born, even. Grandmother signed it, and Father renewed it, and I found the papers when I was nineteen and I ran, and I'm sorry, Liss, I am so sorry, I should have come back for you, I should have—"

"You were nineteen," Liss said. "I was a thread on a loom. There wasn't anything to come back for." She pulled back far enough to look at his face, this stranger-brother with her mother's eyes. "But you came anyway. Eventually. With sword-women and map-makers and a great deal of noise. That counts."

"It doesn't feel like it counts."

"It will," she said. "Give it time. I'm told that's the cure for everything up here."

* * *

The village was called Harrowmere, though the elders pronounced it with a softness that made it sound less like a name and more like a sigh. It clung to the mountainside below the snowline, a scatter of stone houses and turf roofs and one enormous longhouse where the four fugitives were installed without ceremony and without questions — questions, the eldest woman, Sefa, explained, could wait until spring, and besides, anyone who came down off that mountain in winter had already answered the important ones by surviving.

Liss learned the rhythms of the place the way she'd once learned loom-patterns: by watching hands.

Sefa's hands were never still. She carded wool while she talked, spun while she listened, and taught Liss the spindle-work of the village in the evenings — coarse mountain wool, nothing like the fine threads of the court below, and Liss found, to her surprise, that her fingers remembered spinning even when her mind remembered nothing. The body keeps its own accounts. She spun badly at first, then adequately, then one evening produced a length of yarn so even that Sefa looked at it, looked at her, and said nothing at all, which Liss would learn was the highest praise Harrowmere offered.

The others orbited her recovery like careful moons.

Odile Fen came every day at the same hour, sat in the same chair, and drew. She was drawing the sky, Liss eventually realized — over and over, clouds in different arrangements, the sun in different positions, filling page after page of a battered notebook with a thing most people never thought to draw at all. When Liss asked about it, Odile's pen paused.

"I spent six weeks underground," she said, in the flat voice of someone reciting something rehearsed. "Mapping. If you stop drawing, you forget. So I'm practicing not forgetting." A pause. "Also your brother snores, and drawing helps me ignore it."

"He doesn't snore."

"He snores like a landslide arguing with itself."

Cassian, passing through the room with an armload of firewood, called out without turning around: "I can hear you, Odile."

"Good," said Odile, and kept drawing.

And Bram — Bram Vane, the swordspeaker whose name the elders spoke with a reverence that visibly embarrassed her — Bram came the least often and stayed the longest. She sat by the window where the light was good and whittled, or mended things, or simply existed in the room with a stillness that Liss found easier to breathe around than conversation. One afternoon Liss woke from a nap to find Bram quietly re-hemming a blanket that had come loose, working with the concentration of someone defusing a trap.

"You don't have to keep watch over me," Liss said.

Bram didn't look up. "I'm hemming a blanket."

"You've been hemming blankets for nine days."

"They're bad blankets. Village full of weavers and not one of them can finish an edge properly." But her knife-hand stilled, and after a moment she said, quieter: "Your brother nearly died getting you out. Odile gave the wardens a forged map and sat in a cell drawing the sky on the floor so she wouldn't lose herself. And I burned out everything I was to cut your thread free." She tied off the hem, bit the thread, and finally met Liss's eyes. "So no. I don't *have* to keep watch. I choose to. There's a difference, and it turns out it's the only difference that matters."

Liss turned that over for a long time afterward. *Inheritance is what you're given; family is what you keep choosing.* Someone had told her that once — or would tell her, or it was simply true and needed no telling. She was learning that her mind was full of furniture that might belong to her or might belong to whoever had lived in the room before, and that the only way through was to touch each piece and ask it directly.

* * *

The winter broke on a morning in late thaw-month, and the whole village turned out to watch it happen.

For three years, the elders said, the snows had come early and stayed late, the passes choked, the glaciers creeping downslope like something with intent. Now the peaks were letting go. From the longhouse doorstep Liss watched avalanches thunder in the high country — not menacing, but vast and inevitable, the mountain shrugging off a weight it had carried too long. The river below the village ran brown and roaring with snow-melt, and children stood on the banks screaming with delight at the sheer *volume* of water, and one of them fell in and was fished out in four seconds flat and laughed the entire time.

"Every year the melt," Sefa said beside her, squinting at the peaks. "But this year it means something. This year the winter was a lie, and lies don't hold once you've seen the seams." She glanced sideways at Liss. "You were part of the lie, girl. Part of the loom. How does it sit, knowing that?"

Liss considered the question honestly, because Sefa deserved honesty and because lying to Sefa was widely understood to be impossible.

"Like a scar," she said at last. "It aches in cold weather. But it's mine now. It happened *to* me, but it isn't what I *am* — I'm deciding that part as I go." She flexed her hands, these pale thin hands that had worked a loom beneath the mountain for years she could barely count. "Do you know what I remember most clearly? Not the court. Not the Regent. Not even my brother's face, though that's coming back. What I remember is the pattern. The winter-pattern, thread by thread, row by row. I wove frost for six years and I could weave it still, blindfolded, in my sleep." She looked at her palms. "Is that a wound or a skill?"

Sefa was quiet a moment. "Both," she said. "Same as everything worth carrying." Then she thrust a basket of raw fleece into Liss's arms. "Come on. The ewes won't shear themselves, and grief keeps better with work."

* * *

Spring came up the valley like a rumor turning true. First the crocuses through the last crust of snow, then the smell of thawed earth, then the birds — actual birds, in numbers that made Odile fill twelve pages with sketches and annotations and one marginal note reading *I had forgotten there were this many kinds of small loud things.*

Liss relearned her grief the way she relearned everything: slowly, in installments, each piece arriving with its own weather.

Some days it was manageable — the memory of her mother's laugh, bittersweet but bearable, a candle rather than a fire. Some days it ambushed her. She lost an entire afternoon to the realization that she could no longer recall the exact color of her childhood bedroom door, and wept over a *door*, and Cassian sat with her through all of it and said, "I'll describe it to you. It was green. Badly painted. I chipped it with a wooden sword in the shape of a crescent moon and blamed the cat."

"There was no cat."

"There was no cat," he agreed solemnly. "The cat was also a lie. Our household ran on lies; it's a wonder any of us turned out functional."

"We haven't finished turning out," Liss pointed out. "Ask me again in a year."

He laughed — really laughed, the first one she'd heard from him, and it transformed his face so completely that she understood she was seeing, for the first time, the boy from the pear tree.

The nightmares faded too, gradually. Where the loom-hall had been, sleep began offering other things: Turnip the dog, warm against her side; rain on the blue shutters; her own hands, older than her memories, steadying a spindle. Once she dreamed of a woman with a scarred arm standing in a doorway holding a lantern, saying nothing, just making sure the light reached where Liss was walking. She mentioned it to Bram over breakfast, casually, and Bram went very still and then said, "Huh. Weird dream," in a voice that fooled absolutely no one, and Maren kicked her under the table, and the moment dissolved into general laughter that Liss hoarded away like a coin.

She was not healed. She suspected she would spend years meeting versions of herself she didn't recognize — the girl who'd been taken, the thread who'd forgotten, the woman waking up in a strange bed under a strange roof. But the elders fed her, and the sun came through the window every morning onto the same smoke-dark beams, and her brother was learning to sleep without one eye open, and slowly, stitch by stitch, the unfolding continued.

On the first truly warm evening, the five of them — for Maren had annexed herself to the group with the inevitability of weather — carried blankets up the slope behind the longhouse and ate supper on the grass while the light went gold and then rose-colored over the peaks. Odile sketched. Cassian argued cheerfully with Maren about whether a cloud looked more like a fish or a disgraced magistrate. Bram lay on her back with her hat over her eyes, pretending to sleep, present as a hearthstone.

Liss sat among them, full of bread and tiredness, and felt the strangest sensation: the absence of urgency. For six years there had been the loom, the endless rows, the pattern that must be woven or else. Now there was nothing that must be woven. There was only this — grass, voices, the long light — and she found she didn't know what to do with a life that wasn't a debt.

"What happens now?" she asked the sky generally.

"Now?" Bram said from under her hat. "Now we go home, eventually. Greyhollow. There's a shop with our names on it, more or less. Real charms, sold openly. Odile's going to redraw half the northern passes out of spite. Cassian's going to learn a trade and complain about it constantly."

"And me?"

Bram lifted the hat brim and looked at her, and her eyes were steady and kind and completely without pity. "And you," she said, "get to find out. Slowly. That's the whole assignment. Nobody's grading you."

Liss looked at the mountains going rose and shadow, at the river running bright with honest meltwater, at these improbable people who had torn a hole in the underworld to pull her through it. Her arm ached faintly where the thread had been — phantom twinges, the healers said, would come and go for years. She pressed her thumb against the ache, acknowledging it, and let it go.

It was Maren who spotted it first, because Maren noticed everything and announced most of it.

"Someone's coming up the road," she said, shading her eyes. "From the south. Traveler, single, leading a pack-mule." A pause. "They're stopping at the gate. They're asking Sefa something." Another pause, longer. "They're asking for us. By description."

The ease went out of the evening like a snuffed candle. Bram sat up. Odile's pencil stopped. Cassian was already on his feet.

Liss stayed sitting a moment longer, looking at the road, at the small dusty figure waiting at the gate with a pack-mule and some business that had crossed a mountain range to find them. She thought: *we are not finished.* She thought: *of course we're not.*

"Well," she said, standing and brushing off her skirt. "Let's go see what the world wants this time."
