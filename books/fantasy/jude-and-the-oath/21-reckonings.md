## 21. Reckonings

The warrants came down from the watch-house door on a Tuesday, which Bram thought was fitting, because Tuesdays had always been Greyhollow's most bureaucratic day. She was buying eggs when she saw the clerk on his ladder, unpinning the papers with the weary efficiency of a man who had done this a hundred times and would do it a hundred more. He did not tear them up. He filed them. That was the part that made her laugh into her collar — not forgiveness, just paperwork.

*Warrants withdrawn pending review,* the crier announced that afternoon, in the flat voice of someone reading words he didn't believe. *In light of recent meteorological findings.*

Meteorological findings. Bram turned the phrase over all the way home, tasting it. Three years of hiding like a rat in a cellar wall, and the city's grand concession to everything she'd suffered was that the weather had gotten *suspicious*.

But it had, hadn't it. That was the thing nobody could argue with anymore. The long winter had broken across the peaks in a single catastrophic week — snowmelt flooding the lowland rivers in midwinter, glaciers calving into valleys that hadn't seen open water in a generation, and above it all, the mountain standing bare and black and *wrong*, its summit stripped of ice for the first time in living memory. Farmers who had prayed to every saint in the almanac were now praying to no one at all and getting better results. The frost-knots Bram used to sell for two coppers under the counter now hung in every window in Greyhollow, and half of them worked, because half of them had been made by people who finally dared to make them properly.

The other half were Odile's fault, but that was a different story.

"You're smiling," Odile said when Bram came in with the eggs. She sat at the workbench with her sleeves rolled up, her scarred right hand braced against the wood while her left guided a pen across fresh vellum. The guild had sent her new paper within days of her reinstatement — good paper, heavy and cream-colored, the kind she'd been denied for six years. She drew with it the way some people held grudges: carefully, and with great satisfaction.

"I'm not."

"Your mouth is doing a thing. I've catalogued your mouth-things. This is the one you do when you're pleased about something and want to pretend you're too dignified for it."

Bram set the eggs down and considered throwing one at her. "The warrants are gone."

"Filed," Odile corrected, not looking up. "There's a difference. Withdrawn means they're sorry. Filed means they're waiting to see if anyone important complains." Her pen paused. "They'll complain. Some duke will remember that unregistered practitioners nearly toppled the economy, or whatever we nearly toppled. But for now—" she gestured with the pen at the window, where winter sunlight fell honest and clean across the counter, "—for now, we're boring paperwork instead of criminals. Enjoy it. Boring is underrated."

It was strange, how small the victory felt. Bram had imagined this moment for three years — imagined walking through the market with her sleeves pushed up, the oath-scar visible, daring anyone to say a word. In the imagining it had felt like triumph. In practice it felt like taking off boots that had molded themselves to her feet: relief, yes, but also an odd, ghostly lightness, as if part of her had been amputated along with the fear.

She still caught herself hunching over her hands when she worked. Still flinched at the sound of watchmen's boots, even though the boots now belonged to men who nodded to her in passing. Old habits, Odile said, took longer to die than oaths did. Odile would know. She still woke some nights convinced she was back in the guild's punishment cell, and had taken to sleeping with a pencil behind her ear so that whatever happened, she could draw her way out of it.

* * *

Cassian renounced his title on a Thursday, in front of witnesses, which was very Cassian — he couldn't simply leave a thing alone, he had to make it *correct*.

He did it at the Merrow townhouse, before a solicitor, his mother's man of business, and one deeply confused herald who kept trying to explain that titles weren't really renounceable, they were more of a... continuum. Cassian signed four documents, initialed nine margins, and then, when the herald opened his mouth again, said with terrible politeness: "My lordship is a continuum. How lovely for it. It can continue existing without me."

His mother did not attend. His father sent a letter that consisted of one line — *You will regret this when the money runs out* — and Cassian read it aloud to them over dinner, in the voice he used for bad poetry, and then used the back of it to draft a grocery list.

"He'll come around," Liss said. She was doing better. That was the sentence everyone used, and it was true the way a road is long — true, and requiring daily travel. Her memories had come back in tides, whole decades arriving overnight and leaving her weeping into her porridge, followed by mornings when she'd call Cassian "sir" out of habit and have to sit down from the shame of it. She was relearning her own handwriting. She was relearning grief, which was worse, because grief had been waiting for her the whole time, patient as debt.

But she laughed now. Real laughs, sudden and loud, that startled her more than anyone. And she had appointed herself, without consultation, the manager of Bram's shop.

"It's not a shop yet," Bram protested, the fourth time Liss rearranged the shelves.

"It's a shop with delusions of being a cellar. We're fixing that." Liss moved a jar of fever-tea three inches left with the authority of a general redeploying troops. "Sign goes there. Window display there. You will charge actual prices, because undercharging is just pride wearing a hair shirt, and I've done six years of pride in a loom-hall and I'm *done* with it."

The shop occupied the ground floor of the building they'd all, through a series of events no one fully planned, begun living above. It had been a chandler's once, then empty for two winters, and it smelled of old tallow and possibility. Bram had stood in it the first day with her arms crossed, telling everyone who'd listen that she wasn't ready, that selling charms openly required a kind of courage she'd spent three years learning to fake rather than feel.

Then Odile had drawn the floor plan on the back of Cassian's father's letter, marked where the counter should go, and said, "There. Now it exists. All you have to do is stand in it."

That was Odile's whole philosophy, Bram had learned. Things existed once you drew them. Courage included.

* * *

The guild's restoration ceremony, such as it was, took eleven minutes and consisted of a man with a chain of office reading an apology so hedged it might have been written by a lawyer defending the concept of apology itself.

"...regretting any... administrative irregularities... surrounding the matter of Mistress Fen's former standing... while noting that the Guild acted at all times in accordance with the information available..."

"Which was nothing," Odile muttered beside Bram, "because they burned my notes and gave my commission to a man who thinks north is a suggestion."

"—and welcoming Mistress Fen back into full standing, with all privileges, rights, and responsibilities thereof."

There was applause. There was wine, which was bad. There was a moment, afterward, when the man with the chain of office gripped Odile's hand — the scarred one — and she went very still, and Bram watched her decide, visibly, not to pull away.

Justice, Bram was learning, arrived imperfectly. It arrived late and slightly embarrassed, holding paperwork. It restored what it had taken minus interest, minus apology, minus the years. Odile got her guild standing back; she did not get the six years. Cassian got his freedom; his father kept the fortune and the grudge. Liss got her life back thread by thread, and no one — not the court, not the crown, not the cartographers' guild — would ever be made to account for the loom-hall, because the court beneath the mountain had collapsed into legend, and legends don't answer subpoenas.

And Bram? Bram got a shop.

She stood in it that first morning, before opening, with the sign hanging crooked — Liss had insisted on hanging it themselves, immediately, despite having no ladder skills whatsoever — and read the letters she'd painted herself, in careful block capitals:

**VANE'S CHARMS — HONEST WORK, HONEST PRICES**

Underneath, smaller, because Odile had made her add it: **NO CURSES. NO LOVE POTIONS. ASK ABOUT OUR WINTER LINE.**

The bell over the door rang at half past eight, and her first customer was a boy of about ten who wanted a luck-knot for his grandmother's knee, and paid in buttons, and Bram took the buttons, because honest prices sometimes meant knowing what honesty actually cost.

By noon she'd sold three fever-charms, a genuine frost-knot, and one item she had to refuse — a woman wanting a binding-spell for a straying husband, whom Bram redirected toward the notion of conversation with the gentle firmness of someone who had once seen what happened to souls that got bound into things.

By evening her feet hurt and her face ached from smiling and she had made, in coin and buttons and one extremely suspicious dried fish, more than she'd made in any month of the last three years.

Odile appeared at closing with meat pies and a look of profound smugness. "Told you."

"You told me many things. Most were wrong."

"But not this one." She set the pies on the counter and leaned back against it, surveying the shop with her cartographer's eye, mapping it, claiming it. "A year ago you were selling luck-knots out of a blanket and lying about every stitch. Now look. A legitimate business. A sign. A *window display*."

"A crooked sign."

"A charmingly crooked sign. Everything about this place is crooked. The shelves lean. The door sticks. The proprietor scowls at customers." She bit into a pie. "It's perfect. It looks lived-in. Maps are the same way — the ones with coffee stains and corrections are always the truest. Nobody trusts a map that's too clean."

Bram looked around her shop — their shop, though she'd stopped fighting the word — and found that she agreed. It was crooked and warm and smelled of beeswax and drying herbs, and upstairs someone was laughing, Liss's new loud laugh, and Cassian's answering groan, which meant either cards or cooking, and both meant home.

Three years she had hidden. Three years of hunched shoulders and half-voice and magic bled out in thimblefuls so no one would notice. And now the scar on her arm caught the lamplight openly, and a customer that afternoon had asked about it — asked *what it was* — and Bram had told her the truth, the whole strange truth, and the woman had bought a frost-knot and said, "Well. Someone ought to guard doors. Glad it was you."

Someone ought to guard doors. Glad it was you.

Bram turned that over all evening, after the others went up, after the lamps were banked. It wasn't a monument. It wasn't a song. It was a stranger in a shawl saying something kind and slightly wrong and meaning well, and somehow it settled the account better than any warrant withdrawn or title renounced ever could.

The oath was gone. Burned out, spent, spent gladly. What remained was just a scar that ached in cold weather, and a pair of hands that still remembered how to work charms, and a life that fit her now the way the old hiding never had — imperfectly, warmly, with room in it for other people.

Justice had arrived imperfectly. But family, she was learning, arrived however you let it, and stayed as long as you kept the door open.

She banked the last lamp. Outside, Greyhollow glittered under an ordinary winter sky — real snow, honest cold, weather with no agenda. Somewhere north, the mountain stood bare-topped and silent, keeping whatever secrets remained to it.

And on the doorstep of the shop, half-buried in the fresh-fallen snow, something pale caught the lamplight through the window glass.

Bram frowned, pulled the door open against the cold, and bent to pick it up.

A map. Folded small, sealed with a drop of red wax, and written along its border, in a hand she knew better than her own —

her dead mentor's handwriting, and one word:

*Bram.*
