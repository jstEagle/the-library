## 22. The Family Kept

The snow came down on Greyhollow the way snow was supposed to come down now — in honest, ordinary flakes, falling from an honest, ordinary sky, piling up on the awnings of Copperlane Market and melting into grey slush under a thousand boots. Bram Vane stood at her window above the shop with a mug of spiced cider going cold in her hands and watched it, and felt nothing in her arm at all.

That was the miracle she never got tired of. Not the grand kind — not the shattering of a loom that had woven winter for a hundred years, not the cutting of a soul-thread with words that burned her magic out of her like a wick burning to its end. Those had been terrible and bright and over in a single night. This was the small one: midwinter, cold enough to crack the washwater in the basin, and her scarred forearm quiet as a stone. For three years the oath had ached every day of her life, a hot wire under the skin, a reminder of what she owed and to whom. Now it only spoke when the weather did — a dull throb on the worst mornings, like an old break predicting rain — and even that felt less like a debt than a memory. *You were this once,* it said. *You are not this anymore.*

Below her, the shop's sign creaked in the wind. **VANE'S CHARMS — HONEST WORK, HONEST PRICES**, Odile had lettered it, and then, after a week of Bram's scowling, had added a smaller line underneath: ***and no luck-knots, we mean it***. It had been Odile's idea, the shop itself. "You spent three years selling fever-charms out of a blanket in the cold," she'd said, ink-stained fingers tapping the deed, "like a woman apologizing for existing. Sell them from behind a counter instead. With a stove."

So Bram did. And Greyhollow, which had outlawed magic and then watched an endless winter fall from a mountain and break all at once, had decided — in the imperfect, grudging way cities decide things — that maybe the problem had never been charms at all. The warrants hadn't been torn up. They'd simply been allowed to expire, quietly, the way inconvenient laws do when everyone is too embarrassed to mention them. A magistrate had come by in autumn, bought a protection sigil for his granddaughter, and left without meeting Bram's eyes. That was justice arriving imperfectly, wearing good boots. Bram had learned to take it.

She heard them before she saw them — Cassian's laugh first, carrying up through the floorboards, too loud and too delighted as always, and then Liss's voice answering, dry as a January wind. A year ago Liss Merrow had been thread-thin, her memories unraveling into cloth on a loom beneath the mountain, unable to remember her own brother's face. Now she could pick apart Cassian's pretensions in four sentences flat, and did so daily, and seemed to consider it her chief occupation.

Bram went down to let them in.

* * *

The meal was midwinter proper: a goose Odile had bullied out of the poultry-man at a scandalous discount, roast turnips because Cassian insisted he liked them (he did not; he ate them anyway), black bread, honey-cakes Liss had made herself and defended against all criticism, and a bottle of plum wine that Cassian had smuggled past his own family's vineyards out of what he called "principle" and what everyone else called spite.

They ate upstairs, in the room that served as kitchen and parlor and, on nights like this, everything else. The stove ticked. Snow pressed at the windows. Odile had pinned maps along two walls — real ones, guild-stamped, her name restored to the cartographers' rolls in letters she still touched sometimes like she couldn't believe they were hers — but the third wall held other things: a child's drawing of a luck-knot, given by the girl Bram had saved in the market raid three years ago, whose mother now brought bread every solstice; a scrap of glacier-blue cloth Odile refused to explain; a pressed sprig of something that grew only above the snowline, that Liss had carried down from the peaks herself.

"So the guild master says to me," Cassian was saying, gesturing with his wine, "‘Lord Merrow, surely you don't intend to actually *survey* the northern passes yourself,' and I said, my lord, I have personally been lost in those passes while being shot at, I am uniquely qualified—"

"You weren't shot at," Liss said. "You hid in a shrine and bled on the floor."

"I was *bleeding*, which is worse than being shot at, because it implies a prior event—"

"And the shrine," Liss continued, merciless, "where you were found by two strangers who fed you, and you repaid them by lying about your name for a week."

"That was strategy."

"That was cowardice with better tailoring." But she was laughing when she said it — really laughing, head back, the sound filling the little room — and Cassian beamed like a man who had won something, because he had. He'd spent a year earning that laugh, patiently, showing up every day, renouncing his title in writing so many times the family solicitor had finally stopped sending lawyers and started sending fruit baskets. He'd told Liss the truth about everything: about the bargain their grandfather had struck, about the years he'd known and said nothing, about the lie he'd told her in the loom hall when she hadn't known him. She had forgiven him slowly, in installments, the way you forgive someone who has also forgiven himself. Some evenings Bram watched the two of them argue across the table and thought: *this is what it looks like when a family repairs itself instead of inheriting its cracks.*

Odile caught Bram's eye across the goose and raised her cup a fraction. Her right hand — the frostbitten one, the one she'd nearly lost on the glacier saving a frozen boy in house colors — was wrapped around the cup awkwardly, two fingers still stiff in deep cold, but she held it steady. She'd redrawn half the kingdom's northern charts since spring, correcting the forgeries she'd been forced to make, one map at a time, unapologizing. "You're staring," she said.

"I'm counting," Bram said. "A year ago tonight we were eating hardtack in a waystation and you wouldn't stop drawing."

"I remember." Odile looked at her own hands. "I still draw the sky sometimes. Before bed. Old habit." She shrugged, as if it were nothing, as if she hadn't spent weeks alone in a cell trading her finest forgery for their lives, asking for no credit, receiving none until Cassian had marched into the guild hall and told the whole story loudly enough that three masters dropped their pens. "It's a good sky. Worth practicing."

Liss reached over and stole a honey-cake off Cassian's plate with the ease of long practice. "To the sky," she said, lifting her cup. "And to hardtack, may it rot."

"To the sky," they echoed, and drank, and the stove ticked, and outside the snow fell straight down like it had nowhere else in the world to be.

Bram sat in the warmth of it and let herself feel the whole weight of the year — not the grief parts, though those were there, always, her mentor's name surfacing quiet as a fish — but the kept parts. Because that was the word she'd settled on, somewhere in the second month of the shop, watching Odile correct a coastline and Cassian learn to bake and Liss relearn her own handwriting: *kept*. Family wasn't what you inherited. Inherited things came with debts attached, oaths written in someone else's hand, bargains struck generations before you were born by people who never asked you. Kept things were different. You chose them daily. You fed them. You set a place for them at the table even when they were late, especially when they were late.

Her arm ached, faintly, in the cold coming off the window glass. Only in cold weather. Only a memory.

She was reaching for the honey-cakes when the knock came.

* * *

It was a soft knock — the knock of someone who didn't want to be heard knocking, which on a midwinter night in Greyhollow meant either trouble or desperation, and often both. The table went quiet. Old reflexes: Cassian's hand drifting toward the poker, Odile's eyes flicking to the window and the fire escape she'd insisted on installing ("cartographers plan exits"), Liss simply turning in her chair, calm, because she had already survived the worst thing anyone could do to her and it had made her the bravest person at the table.

Bram opened the door.

The stranger was young — younger than any of them, barely more than a girl — soaked to the knees, snow in her dark hair, standing on the doorstep with her fists clenched at her sides like someone who had rehearsed this speech the whole way across town and was about to lose all of it. Her coat was travel-worn far beyond Greyhollow's streets. Mud from the south road. Weeks of road.

"I'm looking for—" she started, and stopped, and started again. "They said in the market there's a charm-seller who's real. Who was real. Who—" Her jaw tightened. She was fighting for the words the way you fight for footing on ice. "My brother sold something of mine. To something under a mountain. And I've walked a very long way, and everyone tells me the courts are gone, the winter broke, it's over — but it isn't over, because I can still *feel* it, the thread, pulling—" Her breath hitched. "Please. I don't know where else to go."

Behind Bram, the room had gone utterly still. She could feel them there — Cassian rising slowly, Liss with her cup forgotten halfway to her mouth, Odile already moving toward the shelf where the old maps lived, because some instincts don't fade, they just change jobs.

And the stranger, seeing Bram's silence as refusal or disbelief or simple exhaustion, did the last thing Bram expected and the one thing that made the whole year fold up inside her chest like a closing hand:

She pulled a map from inside her coat. Bloodstained at one corner. Hand-inked, hurried, borders dense with tiny careful script — and even across the doorstep, even upside down, even after twelve months, Bram knew that handwriting. Knew it the way you know a voice in a dream.

"He wrote this," the girl said. "An old man helped me, near the pass. He said if I ever found the swordspeaker, give her this. He said she'd understand." She held it out with both hands, snow melting on the parchment. "He said to tell her the debt was never paid. All of it. Every soul. He's going back down to finish what he started, and he said—" her voice cracked, steadied — "he said she'd want to choose for herself this time."

The snow fell. The stove ticked. Behind Bram, Odile said softly, "That's his hand. That's — Bram, that's *his* hand."

Bram took the map.

Her scarred arm, quiet for a year, ached — not the old burning wire of the oath, not the debt, none of that was left in her. Just an ache, like weather, like memory, like a door opening somewhere far below and a lamp being lit at the bottom of a very long stair.

She looked at the girl shivering on her doorstep, at the table behind her set for four and easily made five, at the people who had chosen her and whom she chose back, daily, feeding it like a fire.

"Well," Bram Vane said, stepping aside to let the cold in and the stranger through. "You'd better come in out of the snow. We'll want a bigger goose."
