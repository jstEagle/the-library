## 2. A Knock in Old Tongue

The Guild of Improbable Trade closed its doors at the last bell, and Felix Fairweather was always the last one out.

It was not sentiment. She had learned long ago that the final hour belonged to her alone—the great ledger room emptied of clerks, the oil lamps burning low and gold, the inventory crates stacked in their numbered rows like a congregation at rest. In that hour she could reconcile without interruption: a bottled lullaby here (lot 4471, provenance disputed), a debt of shadows there (lot 4488, interest accruing nightly). Her pen moved in small, exact strokes. Numbers did not lie to her. Numbers had never once asked her where she came from or what she'd been before she walked through these doors with a forged reference letter and a hunger she'd spent fifteen years pretending was ambition.

She liked the sound of the building settling around her. Beams ticking as they cooled. The faint briny smell that never quite left the vaults, no matter how many times the floors were scrubbed—salt worked into the very grain of the place, like the Guild itself had been built on an old wound.

Felix finished the day's page, blotted it, and checked the lock on the strongbox twice. Then a third time, because the senior traders had been whispering about auditors all week, and auditors counted everything, including the things you didn't know you'd miscounted.

That was when she heard the knock.

Three strikes, soft but deliberate, against the great iron-banded door at the front of the Guild. Not the knock of a petitioner—petitioners hammered, wept, begged. This was patient. Almost formal. The way one knocks at a door one has traveled a very long way to reach.

Felix set down her pen.

The last bell had rung a full quarter-hour past. By Guild law, the doors stayed shut until morning; by Guild custom, anyone knocking after hours was either desperate or dangerous, and the Guild charged extra for both. She should have let it go. She should have climbed the stairs to her narrow room above the counting floor and let whoever it was stand in the cold until the porters arrived at dawn.

Instead she found herself crossing the ledger room, her boots quiet on the stone, and pressing her eye to the peephole cut into the door's iron banding.

A child stood on the steps.

A girl, perhaps eight years old, maybe nine—small for either. Barefoot, though frost silvered the cobblestones. She wore a shift of undyed wool gone gray with road dust, and her hair was white. Not old-woman white, not ash-white from some alchemical accident of the sort the Guild saw weekly. It was white the way salt is white, uniform and total, as if color had simply declined to live in it. Her skin was pale to the point of translucence, and Felix could see, faintly, the shadow of veins at her throat like lines of ink under parchment.

And she was not shivering. That was the detail that caught Felix first, before anything else. The cold lay on the street like a living thing, breath fogging from Felix's own lips inside the warm hall, and the child stood in it as if it were a summer afternoon.

The girl raised her small fist and knocked again. Three strikes. Patient. Formal.

"Guild hours are dawn to last bell," Felix said through the door, pitching her voice into the flat register she used for petitioners. "Come back tomorrow."

The girl tilted her head. And then she spoke.

Felix would spend the rest of her life trying to describe what happened in her chest at that moment, and she would fail every time. The words themselves meant nothing—and everything. They were not any language she knew, and yet her body knew them, the way a scar remembers the blade. The sounds were old. Not old like Grandmother's stories, not old like the founding of the kingdom. Old like stone. Old like the space between stars. Each syllable seemed to arrive slightly before her ears could account for it, as if the air itself were being asked to carry something heavier than sound.

She recognized it.

Not fluently—not even close. But fragments. A rising cadence in the middle of the second phrase. A doubled consonant that clicked at the back of the teeth. The particular way the girl's voice fell at the end of each sentence, like water finding its level.

She had seen those shapes before. Inked in a hand far older than her own, in books with covers stitched from something that wasn't leather, locked in a cabinet in a cottage at the edge of the moor, in a house she had sworn never to think about again.

*No,* said the rational part of Felix Fairweather, the part that kept meticulous ledgers and valued her position and her anonymity above all things. *No living person speaks that tongue. That is the whole point of it. That is why Odile kept those books behind three locks and made me copy out warnings I didn't understand.*

But the child was still speaking, and now Felix caught something else beneath the strangeness—a thread of urgency. The girl's voice cracked on the third repetition. Her hands pressed flat against the door, fingers splayed, and Felix saw that the fingertips were cracked and bleeding, dried dark, from walking. From knocking on other doors before this one. Doors that had not opened.

*How many doors?* Felix thought. *How many days barefoot in the frost?*

The rational part of her mind presented its objections in orderly fashion, like entries in a column. Opening this door was irregular. Irregularity attracted attention. Attention was the one thing Felix Fairweather had spent a decade and a half avoiding; her entire life was built on the principle of being precisely unremarkable, of reconciling ledgers so flawlessly that no one ever looked at her twice. There was a girl on the step speaking a dead language, which meant either sorcery or madness, and both drew appraisers the way spilled honey drew wasps.

And underneath all of it, quieter, in a voice she did not recognize as her own: *You know what the Guild does with things it cannot classify.*

Appraise. Catalogue. Lot number. Provenance. The Guild had a shelf for everything, and the shelves went down further than the clerks were permitted to know.

Felix's hand was already on the bolt.

She told herself afterward that she had weighed the decision. She had not. Some part of her—some part older than the ledgers, older than the forged name, older than the careful invisible woman she had constructed plank by plank over fifteen years—that part had decided before her conscious mind caught up, and it used her hands without consulting her.

The bolt slid back. The door opened six inches, on the chain.

Cold poured in. The girl looked up at her, and Felix saw her eyes properly for the first time: gray, the gray of sea-slate, of weather coming, and in them was a weariness no child should own. The girl stopped speaking. She looked at Felix with an expression of terrible, exhausted recognition—as if Felix were the thing she had walked all this way to find, and had feared she wouldn't.

Then the girl held up one small hand, palm open, and in it lay a sliver of crystal, clear as ice, no bigger than a thumbnail. It caught the lamplight and threw it back wrong—not refracted but *remembered*, the light moving through it like a memory moves through a mind, blurred at the edges, warm in the middle.

Felix knew what salt-crystal looked like when it had been cut in the deep places. She had catalogued enough of it. But this piece was different. This piece hummed against her awareness like a struck bell too low to hear.

"Come inside," Felix heard herself say. "Quickly. Before someone sees."

* *

Getting the girl through the hall unseen required every trick of invisibility Felix possessed, and she possessed more than most people suspected. She timed their crossing against the patrol of the night porter, whose rounds she knew to the half-minute because she had memorized the entire building's rhythms years ago, the way other people memorize prayers. She moved the girl through the archive stacks during the gap between his eastward and westward passes, one hand on the small cold shoulder, both of them pressed flat against the shelving while his lantern light swayed past at the end of the aisle.

The girl did not fidget. That unnerved Felix more than anything else might have. Children fidgeted; children were noise and motion barely contained in skin. This child stood when told to stand, moved when guided, silent as an unsent letter, watching everything with those slate-gray eyes. When the porter's light swept near, she did not flinch or hide her face—she simply became less present, somehow, the way a candle dims rather than gutters. Felix filed that observation away in the growing drawer of things she refused to examine tonight.

The ledger room received them with lamplight and the smell of ink and cold paper. Felix shot the bolt behind them, then stood a moment with her back against the door, heart hammering, listening to the building breathe.

The girl wandered—no, not wandered. She *surveyed*. She moved along the shelves of bound volumes with slow deliberation, trailing her cracked fingertips just above the spines, not touching, the way one walks through a temple. When she reached the sealed case where the oldest ledgers lived—the ones chained shut, the ones even senior traders needed warrants to open—she stopped.

And she spoke again. Softly, this time. Two words. The doubled consonant clicking at the back of her teeth. The falling cadence, like water finding its level.

Felix's mouth went dry.

Because she had copied those two words out once, fifteen years ago, in a cottage kitchen, under a witch's flint-eyed supervision, as part of a transcription exercise she had been told was merely calligraphy practice. She remembered the scratch of the nib. She remembered Odile's voice saying, in that tone that brooked no questions: *Copy it exactly. Do not speak it aloud. Some words are doors, and doors do not care who walks through them.*

The words on that page had been labeled, in Odile's cramped marginal hand, with a translation Felix had never forgotten, though she had tried for fifteen years:

*The mountain keeps its accounts.*

The girl turned and looked at Felix across the lamplit room, and said the two words again, patiently, the way one says something to a person one believes can understand. Then she pointed one cracked finger at the sealed case, and then—at herself.

Felix's stomach dropped through the floor and kept dropping.

"No," she said aloud, to the empty room, to the girl, to whatever was arranging events around her. "No, absolutely not. You are a lost child, and lost children get taken to the charity ward, and I am going to feed you soup and find you shoes, and that is the end of it."

The girl regarded her without expression. Then she sat down on the floor beside the sealed case, folded her small salt-white hands in her lap, and waited—with the patience of someone who had knocked on many doors and knew, eventually, one opened.

Felix busied herself because busying herself was what she did instead of feeling. She fetched bread from her own stores and the heel of hard cheese, warmed water, found a blanket in the porter's closet that smelled of lamp oil. The girl ate with precise, unhurried hunger—every crumb accounted for, nothing wasted, the eating habits of someone who had known real scarcity—and drank the water down in measured swallows. Frost-bitten feet, Felix discovered when she wrapped them; the girl let her tend them without a flicker of pain, which was its own kind of wrong. The skin was cold as riverbed stone and showed no sign of chilblain.

"What's your name?" Felix asked, knowing it was useless. "Can you write it? Can you write at all?"

The girl took the proffered charcoal stub and the scrap of blotting paper. For a moment Felix thought she hadn't understood. Then the small hand moved, and what emerged was not letters but figures—neat, deliberate, arranged in columns. Rows of numbers, tallied and subtotaled with a precision that made Felix's professional soul sit up and take notice even as her stomach knotted tighter. At the bottom of the column, the girl drew a single character unlike the numbers: a spiral, closed upon itself, like a shell cut in cross-section. Like a chambered thing.

Then she looked up at Felix and pointed at the numbers, and at the sealed case, and said the two words a third time.

*The mountain keeps its accounts.*

"I don't know what you want," Felix lied, because the truth was that she was beginning to suspect, in the wordless cellar of herself, exactly what the girl wanted, and it was a suspicion shaped like a door she had nailed shut a long time ago.

The lamps burned lower. Somewhere above, the porter made his midnight round, boots scuffing, keys chiming. The girl slept finally—curled against the sealed case like a cat against a warm hearth, the blanket pulled to her chin, her breathing slow and even and faintly, impossibly, smelling of brine.

Felix sat at her desk and did not sleep. She sharpened her pen. She squared her blotter. She opened tomorrow's ledger page and wrote nothing on it, because there was no lot number for what had come through her door tonight, no category, no clean line of entry. For the first time in fifteen years, Felix Fairweather faced a blank page and had no idea how to record what was true.

Her eyes kept returning to the sleeping child. To the white hair. To the spiral drawn at the bottom of the column of numbers.

And beneath the fear—beneath the cold arithmetic of risk that had governed her every choice since she was sixteen years old—something else was setting, hardening, taking shape with the inevitability of frost on glass. She examined it warily, the way one examines a new weight in one's chest, and identified it only slowly, because she had not felt it in so long she had nearly forgotten its name.

It was protectiveness. Fierce, unreasonable, entirely unbudgeted.

*They will appraise her,* Felix thought, and the thought tasted of iron. *If they find her, they will assign her a lot number, and she will go down the shelves, down past the bottling rooms, down to wherever the Guild keeps the things it cannot classify. And no one will ever come looking, because no one comes looking for inventory.*

Her hand found the key ring at her belt. Among the dozen keys there hung one she was not officially issued—the key to the sealed case, copied from Marrow's ring during an audit three years ago, a sin she had committed for reasons she'd told herself were practical and had never fully believed. Her thumb brushed its teeth.

Tomorrow, she told herself. Tomorrow, in daylight, with a clear head, she would figure out what this was. Tomorrow she would be sensible.

But the girl murmured in her sleep—a single word in the old tongue, soft as surf—and Felix, without deciding to, understood it. Understood it the way her body had understood the language from the first knock, from some buried stratum of herself she did not remember laying down.

The word was a name. And it was not the girl's own.

It was *Fairweather.*
