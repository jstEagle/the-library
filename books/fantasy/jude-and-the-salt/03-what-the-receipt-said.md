## 3. What the Receipt Said

The Sealed Archive of the Guild of Improbable Trade occupied the lowest floor of the countinghouse, a floor that did not appear on any visitor's map and that most clerks pretended, with the practiced ease of long habit, not to believe in. Felix Fairweather had keys to it. Three of them: iron, bone, and one that was neither, which she kept on a separate ring and touched as rarely as she touched her own face.

She came down before dawn because dawn was when the archive breathed. By lamplight the shelves looked like ribs—rows upon rows of them, black oak gone soft with age at the edges, each shelf holding ledgers bound in hides that had never been cow or goat or anything with a name still spoken aboveground. The air tasted of dust and vinegar and something faintly sweet, like sugar left too long in water. The sweetness was the worst part. Everyone who worked below learned not to ask what it was.

Felix set her lamp on the reading table, rolled her sleeves to the elbow, and tied her hair back with the same plain cord she always used. Ritual mattered down here. The archive rewarded precision and punished curiosity, and Felix had spent eleven years becoming the sort of person whose curiosity fit inside a ledger line.

*The child is asleep in the false drawer,* she reminded herself. *The child is asleep, and no one knows she exists, and by morning you will know why.*

That was the lie she was carrying down the stairs, and she knew it was a lie even as she thought it, because the truth was that she already half-knew why. She had known since the moment the old words fell out of the girl's mouth in that rusted, careful accent—words Felix had last seen inked in a book Odile had burned pages from rather than let them be read aloud. A soul does not learn a dead language by accident. A soul learns it by living somewhere the language is not dead.

And there was exactly one such somewhere in every forbidden book Felix had ever been allowed to glimpse: the court beneath the mountain.

So. The sealed ledgers.

Transactions involving the mountain court were not filed with ordinary sales. They were bound separately, in volumes stamped with the Guild's second seal—the salt-white circle that meant *irrevocable*—and shelved in the deepest alcove behind a gate of black iron filigree that hummed faintly when Felix's fingers neared it. She had catalogued this alcove once, years ago, as a junior clerk with nothing but inventory duty and ambition. She remembered the titles the way other people remembered hymns.

She unlocked the gate. The humming stopped, which was somehow more unsettling than the humming itself.

The volume she wanted was indexed by decade, and the decade she needed was the one she least wanted to open: the years of her own apprenticeship. Years she had spent running errands for Odile through hedgerows and hollows, learning herb-lore and word-lore and how to hold a grudge gently. Years that ended, abruptly, with Felix walking away down a frost-white road and never once looking back—or telling anyone why.

She found the spine she needed. It was colder than the others. Not metaphorically; she pressed her palm flat against it and the cold went through skin and muscle into the bone, a clean mineral chill, like holding a stone pulled from deep water. Salt-cold. Her breath fogged.

"Right," she said aloud, because saying things aloud made them smaller, and this room had taught her that trick early. "Let's see what I've done."

Reading a sealed ledger is not like reading a book. The Guild's oldest volumes are written in contracts that arrange themselves according to the reader's stake in them. A stranger flipping these pages would see only columns of figures and dates, dry as ash. But if you were party to what was recorded—if your name, or your mark, was woven into the transaction—the text woke up. It turned toward you. Felix had watched a senior trader open a sealed volume once and go grey as whey, and she had understood then why the archive was kept underground, where whatever woke up would have far to climb.

She opened the cover.

The first pages stayed inert. Columns, dates, sums in souls and shadows and one entry she did not linger over involving a river's memory of its own source. She turned pages with the ivory folder she used for documents she did not trust her hands near. Outside, somewhere far above, the Guild's night-bell rang twice. The child would still be sleeping. The child had slept the way soldiers sleep, fully dressed, one hand closed around nothing.

Then the ledger turned toward her.

It happened between one page and the next—a subtle shift, like a room going quiet when someone important enters. The ink darkened. The margins filled with small annotations in a hand Felix recognized with a lurch: her own. Her apprentice script, younger and rounder, the letters leaning slightly left the way they had before Odile beat the lean out of them. Annotations she had no memory of writing. Little marginal notes in her own sixteen-year-old voice: *confirm weight before transfer*, and *the Regent pays in years, do NOT accept years*, and, chillingly, in a smaller, hurried script jammed against the binding: *he said she wouldn't remember. he promised.*

Felix sat down. The stool received her without comment.

There was an entry dated the ninth day of the Drowning Month, nineteen years past. She read the header three times because the first two readings refused to mean anything:

*Sale of one (1) soul, juvenile, unregistered origin, language-bearing. Buyer: the Court Beneath, per standing arrangement. Seller: [REDACTED BY ORDER OF THE GRAND LEDGER]. Broker's fee remitted in salt. Witnessed and countersigned below.*

Below was a signature block. Three marks.

The first was the buyer's: a spiral pressed into the page so deeply it had scarred through three sheets beneath, the way a hot iron scars wood. The mountain court's sign, the coiled labyrinth she'd seen drawn in Odile's banned books with warnings in the margins.

The second was the broker's—a merchant's chop, some salt-house sigil, a wave over three dots. Unfamiliar. Filed under a name she'd have to look up: *Corvane & Sons, Purveyors of Fine Preserves.* Preserves. She almost laughed, and the laugh died somewhere in her chest and came out as a shiver instead.

The third mark was hers.

Not similar to hers. Not forged in imitation of hers, though God knew she had seen enough forged signatures in eleven years of auditing to fill a wing of the archive. It *was* hers—the exact loop-and-barb of her apprentice mark, the little crossbar she used to add out of vanity at seventeen, the ink sitting in the paper's grain at precisely the depth of a confident hand. And beside it, in the ledger's formal script, the attestation every contract required:

*Signed freely, in full knowledge, of sound mind and uncoerced will.*

"No," Felix said to the empty room.

The room did not answer. The archive never answered; that was its courtesy. But the entry remained, patient as arithmetic, and the sweet smell of the shelves seemed thicker now, closer, as if the whole alcove were leaning in to watch her understand.

She checked everything. That was what she was *for*. She checked the date against her own history—she had still been with Odile then, still sleeping in the loft above the drying shed, still complaining about the porridge. She checked the ink composition against known forgery patterns; it matched period-appropriate oak gall, no modern additives, no telltale chemical brighteners. She checked the mark against samples of her own hand preserved in Guild records from her hiring, eleven years ago, when she'd signed her employment contract with a newer, straighter version of the same signature.

Identical. Identical across nineteen years, across a life she had rebuilt from scratch, as though her hand had never changed at all—which hands did not do, which hands *never* did.

Which meant either someone had copied her mark with a fidelity that bordered on the miraculous, using ink and paper aged with a skill the Guild's own appraisers would weep over—

Or she had signed it.

She turned back to her own marginal notes. *He said she wouldn't remember. He promised.* Written in her hand. Referring, presumably, to the seller—redacted—and the buyer's broker. *He promised* she wouldn't remember *what?* Signing? The sale itself? The child?

Because there was a third possibility, and it was the one that made her palms sweat against the ivory folder: that the redaction ran both directions. That the Grand Ledger's order had struck the seller's name not just from the record but from the seller. That somewhere in her missing reasons—those blank, frost-bordered months at the end of her apprenticeship, the hole in her memory shaped exactly like a decision—there was a night, a table, a quill, a child.

A child whose soul was sold to the mountain while she was still, by every calendar she owned, a hedge-witch's nobody apprentice with no dealings in improbable trade.

Felix put her head in her hands and breathed the sweet, terrible air.

Her whole self had been built on a simple foundation: *I left because I had to. I don't remember all of why, but I was young, and Odile's world was narrow, and I wanted ledgers instead of hexes.* Eleven years she had stood on that foundation. Now here was a document suggesting the foundation was load-bearing in ways she'd never inspected—that she hadn't merely witnessed the Guild's trade as an outsider who later joined it, but had fed it, at sixteen, at the very beginning, one soul, one signature, one girl who maybe hadn't known what she was signing, or had known and signed anyway, or had known and been made to forget.

Every version was unbearable. She held all three at once, the way she'd been trained to hold contradictory entries until the audit resolved them, except no audit could resolve this, because the discrepancy was herself.

Above her, muffled through floors of stone and timber, the Guild began to wake. Boots on stairs. The kettle in the clerks' room. Somewhere, Marrow arriving early with his bad hip and worse coffee, and the auditors due within days, and a mute child hidden in a piece of furniture one floor up who spoke the language of the buyers listed on this page.

Felix lifted her head. Her reflection stared back at her from the ledger's polished obsidian clasps—pale, precise, a woman made entirely of careful entries—and behind that reflection, faint as breath on glass, the scarred spiral of the mountain's mark seemed to glow.

One fact cut through the horror, sharp and useful, the way facts did when everything else was drowning: the contract specified a *sale*, singular, final, irrevocable—except that the Guild's own charter, article nine, clause four, which she had helped draft the modern wording of, contained the phrase she now recited silently like a prayer: *no sale of soul-stuff is binding where the instrument bears a coerced or false mark.*

If her signature was real, she had damned a child. If her signature was false, the contract was void—and she could prove it, because she had spent eleven years becoming the finest forensic auditor of instruments the Guild possessed, and whoever had forged her mark had done it well enough to fool everyone but her.

Either way, the road forward ran through the same door: she needed to know which it was. And there was exactly one person alive who had known her hand at sixteen, who had beaten the lean out of her letters, who had burned pages rather than let this language be read—

Who had also, Felix now noticed with a fresh, cold slide of dread, annotated this very volume in the margin of the facing page, in a cramped furious script she would have known anywhere:

*Felix. If you are reading this, come home. Bring the girl. Burn nothing.*

Odile had signed the archive's ledger nineteen years ago, and Felix had never once suspected it.

She closed the book. The sweet smell faded. Upstairs, the night-bell rang the hour, and somewhere above, faintly, a small voice—not words yet, not the old tongue, just a sound—hummed a tune that Felix's body recognized before her mind did: a lullaby from a cottage she had spent eleven years refusing to remember.

She took the ledger, wrapped it in her shawl, and started for the stairs.
