## 4. Salt on the Windowsill

*white*

*first there is white, and white is not nothing. white is a room with no corners. white tastes like the inside of a shell held too long against the ear.*

she has been in the white for so long she has forgotten that other colors exist, except—

except there was a window once. or something like a window. an opening where the white went thin and behind it she saw:

*a woman's hands.*

hands moving over paper. ink on the fingers, black as beetle shells. a lamp burning low and green. numbers going down onto the page in rows, rows, rows, and a voice above the hands saying them aloud, quiet, like a prayer said by someone who does not believe in gods but believes very much in arithmetic.

*four hundred and six vials of bottled rain, condition fair. one debt of shadows, interest accruing. do not shake.*

the voice is patient. the voice never hurries. the voice belongs to someone who has decided that if everything is written down correctly, nothing can hurt her.

*(this will turn out to be untrue. but it is a beautiful thing to believe, and the child loves her a little for believing it.)*

* * *

before the white there was the mountain.

she remembers the mountain from underneath, which is the correct way to remember a mountain if you were born inside one.

the court of salt. pillars of it, grown not built, rising out of floors that glittered like frozen lightning. nobles standing in niches with their arms folded across their chests, and their faces—she tries to make the faces come, and they come wrong, they come as *shapes*, because the nobles are not flesh. they are memories wearing the idea of faces. crystallized remembering. when one of them turns toward you, you feel yourself being *filed*.

she was filed often.

she was small, and she was the only one who could speak, and this made her precious the way a well is precious in a country of thirst. the old tongue lived in her mouth. when she spoke, the salt pillars hummed. when she sang—which she did on the days the Regent commanded it—the whole court brightened, every noble flushing faintly rose-colored, the way a memory flushes when it is being loved.

they did not love *her*. they loved what she carried.

she understood this early. understanding things early is a kind of salt too; it preserves you and it stings.

* * *

there was a transaction.

she remembers it the way you remember weather—not the day itself but the pressure of it, the smell before the storm. a delegation came up out of the deep corridors: figures in gray coats with brass pins through their lapels shaped like little open books. Guild men. Guild women. Guild *somethings*, whose gender had been traded away years ago and who now spoke in the flat tone of people reading terms aloud.

the Regent stood at the center of the court, tall as a cathedral pillar, crowned with a ring of frozen first-frosts.

and the Guild-something unrolled a receipt.

paper, down here, was obscene. paper was a surface thing. but the receipt glowed faintly, the way all contracts glow when they are binding, and she watched the Regent lean down—down, down, the crown of frosts swinging—and read it.

the voice that came back was like gravel being kind.

*"sold,"* the Regent said. *"a soul. young. unspoiled. last speaker."* a pause, the terrible patience of a being who has outlasted kingdoms. *"whose name sits on this line?"*

and the Guild-something turned the receipt around, and she—small, silent, filed—craned to look, because even then she knew that names were the most dangerous cargo in any world.

the signature was a mark, not a name. a small hooked symbol like a bird's foot dragged through wet ink.

she did not know then whose hand had made it.

she knows now. she saw the same hand, later, through the thin place in the white, moving over ledgers in a green-lit room, writing numbers in rows, careful, careful, careful.

*fates are braided,* the old tongue says. *no knot knows which strand began it.*

* * *

the taking was gentle. that was the worst part.

she had expected teeth. she had expected the mountain's hunger, which she had heard about in the nobles' murmured filings. instead there was a basin of brine set before her, warm as bathwater, and the Regent's voice telling her—not unkindly—that a soul could be drawn the way a splinter is drawn, with steadiness and good light.

*"you will feel lighter,"* the Regent promised.

that was true. that was the trap of it. she felt lighter, and lighter, and lighter, until she was a small dry weightless thing looking down at a girl-shaped body kneeling in salt water, and the body's eyes were open and empty as two unwritten pages.

then the white took her.

the white is the space between ownerships. souls wait there, she learned, the way coins wait in a purse: not suffering, not sleeping, just *held*. time in the white is a rumor. she might have been there a season. she might have been there ninety years.

but the thin places come, sometimes. the thin places where the white wears through, and through the hole you can see whatever your soul is bound nearest to.

her soul, it seemed, was bound to a ledger room.

* * *

through the thin places she learns the woman in pieces, the way you learn a song from a neighbor's window—one phrase at a time, never the whole melody.

she learns that the woman drinks tea gone cold because she forgets it exists while working.

she learns that the woman counts under her breath when she is frightened, and that she is frightened more often than her still face admits.

she learns that the woman's left boot heel taps twice before she stands up from her desk, always twice, a tiny superstition or a tic or a spell she doesn't know she's casting.

she learns that the woman is alone. not lonely—there is a difference, and the woman has built a whole life on the difference—but alone the way a locked room is alone.

and one night—or one *something*, time being a rumor—the thin place opens wider than usual, wide enough that the woman looks up from her ledger, directly at the hole in the air where the white is showing through, and frowns.

not afraid. *puzzled.* as though reality has produced a smudged entry and she cannot find the correcting pen.

the woman rises. crosses the room. leans close, close enough that the child can see her eyes, which are brown and tired and sharp as awls, and there is a moment—a held breath, a suspended column of seconds—where the woman looks *into* the white and almost, almost sees her.

then the door of the ledger room opens below, voices, lamplight, and the woman steps back into her life, and the thin place closes like water closing over a dropped coin.

* * *

after that, the white begins to fail.

she feels it as a loosening, a tide going out somewhere far away. bindings unraveling. somewhere in the upper world, paper is moving—contracts shifting hands, seals being examined, someone asking questions in a flat procedural voice. she cannot see these things. she can only feel them the way you feel weather coming through a wall.

and then—

falling. or rising; in the white they are the same motion. a long soft plummet through nothing, and then:

cold. real cold. stone under her knees. night air that tastes of chimney smoke and horse and rain, the most delicious filth imaginable after an eternity of white. her own body again, small and stiff and aching, kneeling on a step before an enormous door banded in iron.

the Guild's door. she knows its smell. brass and vellum and the sour tang of souls kept too long in storage.

she should knock. some buried instruction tells her to knock, present herself, be appraised. the gray-coated logic of it sits in her chest like a swallowed pin.

instead she opens her mouth, and what comes out is the old tongue—unbidden, flooding, the whole drowned language pouring up out of her like water finding its level. words for salt and word for debt and word for *braid*. words no living throat should remember. the door does not answer. the street does not answer. a cat on the opposite step flattens its ears and leaves.

she has no voice in the common way—hasn't since the basin, since the drawing-out; her sound went with her soul and what remains is only this older channel, this salt-carved speech. so she says the only sentence she owns, over and over, to the shut door, to the indifferent city:

*the ledger is wrong. the ledger is wrong. find the hand that wrote it.*

* * *

the door opens eventually—of course it does; doors in this city open eventually—and inside there is warmth and lamplight and the smell of ink, and she is swept along corridors by people who talk about *appraisal* in voices that make the word sound like a knife being oiled.

and then the woman appears.

the ledger woman. alive, upright, three-dimensional, smelling of cold tea and lamp oil, and her eyes go wide—not with fear but with *recognition*, which is stranger, which is impossible, because they have never met, have they?

(they have. through a thin place. the woman simply doesn't know it yet.)

the woman hears the old tongue fall out of her mouth and goes pale in a specific way—not the paleness of surprise but the paleness of *remembering something she has been made to forget*. the child watches it happen: the flinch, the flicker, the hand rising unconsciously to touch the hollow of her own throat, as if checking whether a signature is still there.

*yes,* the child thinks, in the old tongue, silently, fiercely. *yes. you know it. some room in you knows it. find it.*

the woman hides her. smuggles her through corridors that smell of dust and binding glue, into the green-lit room full of shelves, and locks the door, and for the first time in longer than forever, the child is *somewhere* rather than nowhere.

* * *

night, in the ledger room. the woman sleeps badly at her desk, cheek on her folded arm, one hand still resting on a closed book as though the book might bolt.

the child cannot sleep. sleep is for bodies that trust their containers.

so she finds charcoal—of course there is charcoal; this room runs on marks—and a scrap of vellum trimmed off some ledger's edge, and she kneels at the windowsill where the moon comes in thin and gray, and she draws.

she draws the face she knows better than any face in any world.

it is hard. faces are the hardest things; they move even when they're still. but she works patiently, the way the nobles worked patience into her, stroke by stroke: the jaw set a little too tight, the habit of vigilance worn into the brow. the eyes she does carefully, twice, because the eyes are the truest thing she carries—brown, tired, sharp as awls, and underneath the sharpness something scared and stubborn and *kind despite itself*.

she draws the mouth and gives it the shape it makes when it reads numbers aloud: absorbed, unguarded, almost peaceful.

she draws the hair escaping its pins because it always escapes its pins; she has seen it do so through four separate thin places.

when it is finished, she holds it up against the sleeping woman, checking, adjusting, checking again. the likeness is good. the likeness is *true*, which is different.

and beneath the portrait, in the old tongue, in small careful characters like frost forming on glass, she writes what she has known since the court of salt, since the receipt glowed and the Regent leaned down to read the name on the line:

*this one. my debt is tied to this one. her hand signed me away, and neither of us chose it, and now we are braided until one of us pays.*

the moon moves. the charcoal dust settles on the sill like gray snow.

the child sets the drawing on the desk, weighted with the corner of the woman's own cold teacup, positioned precisely where the waking eye must find it first.

then she climbs onto the windowsill herself, tucks her knees up, and waits for morning—for the woman to wake, and see, and understand that the ledger is wrong, and begin, however reluctantly, however fearfully, to search her own past for the hand that wrote it.

far below the city, far below the earth, in a court of pillars and frosts, a Regent of salt dreams without sleeping and turns one page of an endless book, waiting for the braided strands to pull tight.

the child watches the sky lighten over the rooftops and practices, silently, the hardest word she knows.

*Fairweather,* she thinks. *one who carries storms lightly.*

*hurry.*
