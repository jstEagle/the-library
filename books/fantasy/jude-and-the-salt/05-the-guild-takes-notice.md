## 5. The Guild Takes Notice

The trouble began, as all Guild troubles began, with a discrepancy.

Felix felt it before she saw it—a wrongness in the air of the ledger room, the way a sailor feels a storm three days off. The ledgers themselves were innocent: forty-one volumes in their appointed places, spines aligned to the width of a fingernail, their brass clasps turned to the quarter-past-lock position that meant *closed and verified*. It was the silence that was wrong. For nine years the ledger room had hummed with the particular quiet of perfect order, a silence so complete it was almost musical. This morning the silence had a hole in it.

She found it at the third shelf from the window, where the sealed ledgers lived—the ones recording transactions older than the current administration, older than some of the kingdoms whose names appeared in them as signatories. Volume Twelve of the Deep Registry was missing.

Not stolen. Missing. There is a difference, and Felix knew it intimately, because she had spent nine years learning every grammar of absence the Guild possessed. A stolen book left a gap with edges—dust unbroken on one side, disturbed on another, the ghost of a spine pressed into leather. What she found instead was worse. The shelf held Volume Eleven and Volume Thirteen, and between them sat a space exactly wide enough for Volume Twelve, dusted clean, squared, *tidied*. Someone had removed the book and then arranged the remaining world around its absence so carefully that only a person who had memorized the shelf's breathing would ever notice.

Only her. Only Felix Fairweather, who had catalogued this room down to the number of moth wings caught behind the third drawer, who knew that the west window let in light at an angle that faded red ink faster than black, who had made herself so small and so precise that the Guild had stopped seeing her entirely—which was, of course, the entire point of becoming invisible.

The child slept in the crawl space beneath the map cabinets, wrapped in Felix's own coat, breathing the slow breath of someone who had not yet learned that sleep was a thing you could lose. Felix had checked on her four times since dawn. Each time, the girl's small hands were curled around a scrap of paper on which Felix had written, in careful block letters, *You are safe here. Do not come out until I tap twice.* The child couldn't read Common script—Felix was nearly certain—but she kept the paper anyway, the way you keep a charm you don't understand but trust.

*Do not come out until I tap twice.*

Felix tapped nothing now. She stood before the empty space on the shelf and did the arithmetic no one else in the building could do.

Volume Twelve of the Deep Registry contained the mountain court's accounts. Every transaction between the Guild of Improbable Trade and the court beneath the mountain—the salt courts, the memory markets, the place where years and names and souls changed hands like coin—was recorded there. And somewhere in those pages, she knew now, was a receipt bearing her own apprentice mark: a small hooked F trailing a line like a fishing line dropped into dark water. She had found it two nights ago, by candle stub, heart hammering, before she'd had the sense to copy the entry into her personal cipher-book and return the volume to its place.

Except she hadn't returned it to its place. She had returned it to *a* place—one shelf over, one slot too high, because her hands had been shaking and the candle had been guttering and she had told herself she would fix it in the morning.

The morning had come, and someone had already fixed it for her. Or rather: someone had noticed the error, drawn the obvious conclusion, and taken the evidence.

Felix closed her eyes. In the dark behind them she saw the entry again, as she had seen it a hundred times since: *One soul, female, age indeterminate, tongue: pre-dynastic. Sold to the Court Beneath. Consideration received: [entry smudged]. Appraiser's mark: [illegible]. Apprentice countersign:* and there it was, the hooked F, hers, unmistakably hers, in ink she did not remember dipping.

Nine years ago she had been Odile's apprentice, and she had signed things. Contracts of apprenticeship. Receipts for herbs. Once, a bill of sale for a jar of bottled thunder that Odile had traded to a tinker out of sheer spite. Her hand had been looser then, rounder. But the mark was the mark. Ink remembers what hands forget.

And now the book that proved it was gone, and the Guild would be asking questions, and Felix Fairweather—who had built a fortress out of being unremarkable, who had spent nearly a decade ensuring that no one in this building could describe her face if asked—was about to become the most interesting person in it.

From somewhere above, through the floorboards, came the sound she had been dreading: the great front doors of the Guild hall opening at half past eight instead of nine, and voices—several voices, and one of them belonged to Grand Ledger Corvane himself.

* * *

Corvane held his morning address in the Hall of Weights, the vast chamber where the Guild conducted its public business. Felix took her usual position: fourth pillar from the back, half in shadow, hands folded, expression arranged into the mild attentive blankness of a person whose presence required no acknowledgment. Around her, the traders gathered in their graded silks—apprentices in undyed wool, journeymen in grey, full traders in colors bought and sold like everything else. Marrow stood near the front, a mountain of a man gone soft at the edges, his trader's sash the deep amber of second-rank seniority. He did not look back at her. He never looked back at her. That was the first rule he had taught her, seven years ago, when she'd been a new hire with too-sharp eyes: *In this house, we do not see each other seeing.*

"Colleagues." Corvane's voice filled the hall the way cold fills a cellar. He was a narrow man, dry as a cancelled debt, with a face like something kept too long in a strongbox. "At dawn this morning, the audit office confirmed an irregularity in the Deep Registry. Volume Twelve has been removed from its housing without authorization."

A murmur went through the assembled traders—not alarm, Felix noted, but appetite. News in the Guild was currency, and everyone was calculating exchange rates.

"The volume concerns our historical accounts with the subterranean court," Corvane continued. "Its contents are sensitive beyond the clearance of most persons in this room, which narrows considerably the list of individuals who might have taken it—and narrows further still the uses to which it might be put." His gaze moved across the hall like a hand moving across shelves. "An internal inquiry has begun. All staff will submit to standard verification of memory-integrity. Refusal will be interpreted as confession."

There it was. Memory-verification: the Guild's favorite instrument, a minor rite that combed through the surface layers of a mind looking for recent images of restricted materials. It missed deep memories, buried ones, sold ones—but Felix had looked at Volume Twelve only two nights ago, and two nights ago was very much on the surface.

Her hands stayed folded. Her breathing stayed even. Inside, she was doing what she did best: filing. *Verification scheduled. Timeline: today, likely within hours. The child cannot be found during verification. The crawl space will not survive a thorough search. I need—*

"—and one further matter." Corvane's voice thinned. "The night porter reports an unidentified child at the Guild door at closing bell yesterday. The porter admits he admitted her—against protocol—for appraisal this morning. The child is not currently in the appraisal wing."

The murmur this time was different. Sharper. A missing soul-child was not news; it was *scandal*, and scandal in the Guild of Improbable Trade was the one commodity that always depreciated its holder.

"The porter has been questioned," Corvane said. "He recalls admitting the child. He does not recall anything after. Someone in this room," and his voice dropped into a register like a key turning, "has either taken a Guild asset or interfered with one. When I learn which, the penalty will be instructive to us all."

He dismissed them. The hall emptied in its graded order, silk before wool, and Felix drifted with the apprentices toward the records annex, her mind running calculations at the speed of panic dressed as calm.

They would verify her. They would find the image of Volume Twelve open under candlelight, the hooked F, the smudged consideration line. Unless—

Unless someone with authority reordered the sequence of the verification. The rite read memories forward from a chosen anchor point, and the anchor was set by the presiding officer. A presiding officer of sufficient rank could anchor the rite *after* the relevant hours, or anchor it shallowly, or declare the subject low-priority and skip it entirely.

There were perhaps four people in the Guild with that authority. Three of them wanted Corvane's chair and would burn her for the warmth of it.

The fourth was Marrow.

* * *

She found him in the weighing room at the back of the trade floor, where he took his mid-morning tea among the counterweights like a farmer resting among his oxen. Marrow had been a trader of souls once, in the years before the Guild reclassified that inventory as "prestige instruments," and there was something in his face that had never fully recovered from it—a settled sorrow, like silt at the bottom of a clear glass.

"You're going to ask me for something," he said without looking up from his tea. "I can tell because you're standing where you can see the door. Guilty people watch exits. You've watched that door since your first week."

"I need my verification anchored after last midnight."

Marrow set down his cup. For a long moment he said nothing, and the counterweights around them seemed to lean in, brass ears listening. "Anchor after midnight," he repeated. "Which means whatever you did before midnight stays below the waterline. Which means you did something before midnight." He finally looked at her, and his eyes were tired and kind and absolutely unfooled. "The child."

Felix said nothing. Saying nothing was, in her experience, the most eloquent sentence available.

"The porter's mind was wiped clean," Marrow said. "That's not amateur work. That's a trained hand, or—" he paused, and something moved behind his eyes, "—or a hedge-trained hand. Odile's girl. Yes. I wondered when the old woman's debts would find their way to my door." He rubbed his jaw. "You hid her in the ledger room."

"In the crawl space. Under the map cabinets."

"Felix." He said her name the way a man says the name of a river about to flood. "You hid a soul-child of unknown provenance, on the same night a registry volume concerning the salt courts went missing, in the one room of this building you know better than the auditors do. Do you understand what Corvane will do if he finds her? He won't appraise her. He'll *auction* her. Pre-dynastic stock hasn't crossed a Guild block in two hundred years. There are buyers who would trade provinces."

"I know what she's worth." Felix's voice came out flat and strange. "I've read the receipt."

Marrow went very still. "Whose receipt?"

"Mine." She heard herself say it and marveled distantly at how steady it sounded, how much like a ledger entry. "My apprentice mark is on the sale. Nine years ago. I don't remember making it. I don't remember any of it. But the ink is mine, Marrow. I know my own hand."

The silence that followed had weight, like a stone laid on a table. Somewhere above them, the Guild's great clock struck the quarter hour, and each stroke fell into the room and sank.

"That," Marrow said quietly, "is why you ran from the hedge-witch."

"It's why I became good at hiding."

"No." He shook his head slowly. "It's why you became good at *ledgers*. Hiding was just the shape it took." He stood, and his bulk blotted out the window light, and for a moment Felix felt what every debtor must feel before him: the vertigo of standing across from someone who holds your whole account in his head. "I'll anchor your verification after midnight. I'll log the porter's wipe as my own maintenance—old habit, tidying loose minds, no one questions it. The child stays hidden. The inquiry stalls for lack of a subject."

Felix exhaled. It felt like the first breath she'd taken all morning. "Thank you."

"Don't." He raised one enormous hand. "Don't thank me. Thanking implies a gift, and this is not a gift. This is a transaction, and I am about to name the price, and you are going to hate me for naming it, and then you are going to pay it, because you have no alternative. That's how this house works. It's how every house works. You taught yourself that lesson years ago; I'm merely collecting tuition."

"What's the price?"

"A favor. Open-ended. Unspecified. Payable on demand, at a time of my choosing, in a form of my choosing." He held out his hand, palm flat, in the old gesture—the trader's seal, the one that predated contracts, that predated writing, that meant *my word against my name*. "No limits clause. No refusal clause. You'll note that I am asking this of you precisely because you are clever enough to know what a terrible bargain it is, and honest enough to honor it anyway. Those people are rare, Felix. I collect them."

She looked at his hand. She thought of the child beneath the map cabinets, breathing slow, clutching a note she couldn't read. She thought of the hooked F in old brown ink, and of a mountain full of salt, and of the fact that somewhere in the world there existed a version of herself who had signed away a living soul—and that version had walked on her own legs, worn her own face, and Felix could not remember a single hour of her.

Every ally in this world cost something. The question was never whether you could afford it. The question was whether you could afford to stand alone, and she had answered that question nine years ago, alone, in the snow, and the answer had cost more than this.

She put her hand in his. His grip swallowed hers whole.

"Witnessed," Marrow said softly, to the counterweights, to the room, to whatever old powers still honored the gesture. "So mote it be entered."

"So mote it be entered," Felix echoed, and felt the words settle into her like a hook settling into flesh—painless now, she knew, and not painless later.

Marrow released her and retrieved his tea, and just like that he was a soft-edged senior trader again, mildly bored, halfway through his morning. "Verification begins at noon," he said. "Yours is scheduled for the third session. Be dull, Felix. Be the dullest thing they've ever skimmed. Think about ink viscosity and shelving angles and nothing else." He paused at the door, and without turning: "One more thing. The appraiser Vess arrived this morning. From the capital. Corvane sent for her before dawn."

Felix's stomach dropped through nine floors of the Guild and into the bedrock. "Vess? Why Vess? She audits *contracts*, not personnel."

"She audits whatever Corvane points her at. And Corvane pointed her at the Deep Registry gap." Marrow's reflection in the dark window was unreadable. "Vess has never failed to close an account, Felix. Not one. Whatever story you're telling yourself about how this ends—about the child, about the receipt, about that signature—you should know that the best instrument the Guild owns is now reading the same pages you are."

He left. Felix stood alone among the counterweights, her hand still warm where his had swallowed it, the price of her safety hanging from her like a stone she had agreed to carry without knowing the road.

Above her, muffled through floors of oak and stone, she heard the noon bell begin to ring.

Under the map cabinets, a child who spoke the language of mountains waited for two taps that had not yet come.
