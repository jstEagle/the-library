## 6. Road Toward the Hedge

Felix left the Guild by the coal door, the one reserved for ash-haulers and debts that had gone bad, and she did not look back at it. Looking back was a habit of people who believed they had something to return to.

The child came with her because there was nowhere else for the child to be. She walked a half-step behind Felix's left shoulder—close enough to be caught if Felix turned quickly, far enough not to touch—and she made no sound at all except her breathing, which was quiet as paper turning.

Felix had planned the route three times in her head while the child slept in the ledger room, folded into the space behind the cabinet of uncollectable accounts where the ledgers' leather smell was thickest and no auditor ever went. Out through the tannery district before the morning bells. Along the drainage cut until it met the shepherd's road. Then west, past the last tollgate, into country that the Guild's maps marked only with a small notation: *unprofitable*.

It was good planning. It was precise. It accounted for every variable Felix could name.

It did not account for the fact that she had never once, in eleven years of meticulous invisibility, done anything like this.

The tannery district smelled the way it always smelled—of lime and rot and money being made from something dead—and Felix moved through it with her ledger-keeper's walk: spine straight, eyes forward, pace exactly quick enough to look busy and exactly slow enough to look like she belonged. She had worn this walk like a coat for a decade. Nobody looked twice at a woman who walked like arithmetic.

But the child looked. The child looked at everything—the vats steaming their yellow stink into the grey air, the boys hauling hides, a dog gnawing something in a doorway—with an attention so total it seemed almost rude, as if the world were a page she intended to memorize. And because she looked, people began to look back. A hide-worker straightened up to watch her pass. A woman selling boiled chestnuts forgot her customer mid-transaction.

"Stop staring," Felix murmured without moving her lips. "People notice people who notice."

The child looked at Felix then, and her expression said, plainly, in the language of children everywhere: *why?*

"Because—" Felix began, and stopped, because the honest answer was *because I spent ten years learning to be nobody, and I will not have you undoing it in one morning,* and some part of her heard how that sounded even as she thought it. She bought two bags of chestnuts instead, pressing a coin across before she could calculate what it cost, and pressed the warm paper into the child's hands.

The child held them like they were documents. Important ones. She ate one slowly, watching Felix over the top of the bag, and something in the watching made the back of Felix's neck prickle—not unpleasantly. Like being read. Like being entered into a book she hadn't authorized.

"Don't," Felix said. "Don't do that."

The child tilted her head.

"You're cataloguing me." Felix kept walking, voice low. "I know what it looks like when someone does that. I've spent my whole life doing it to other people. You don't want me in your records. I'm very boring. There are pages missing."

The child considered this with grave seriousness, chewed, swallowed, and then reached out and took hold of Felix's sleeve. Just two fingers, pinching the wool, light as a clerk turning down a corner to mark his place.

Felix should have shaken her off. She noted, distantly, with professional alarm, that she did not.

* * *

They cleared the tollgate at noon, or rather they walked around it, through a gap in the hedge that Felix remembered from a lifetime ago—the year she'd come east on foot with blistered feet and a head full of things she'd been told never to say aloud. Her body remembered the gap even though she didn't remember choosing to remember. That was the trouble with roads. They kept your secrets whether you asked them to or not.

Beyond the gate the land opened up: fallow fields going gold at the edges, a line of poplars stitching the horizon, and far off, faint as a watermark, the mountains—the real ones, not the mountain, but cousins of it, the same stone family. Felix looked at them once and then made herself stop looking.

The road was empty in both directions. Empty, and yet she could not make herself believe it.

Appraisers traveled light and fast and they did not use roads; they used *valuations*, following the worth of things the way hounds followed blood. Somewhere behind her was Vess—whoever Vess was, whatever Vess wanted—and somewhere behind her, too, would soon be the machinery of the Guild waking up to a missing entry. Felix had left the sealed ledger open on Marrow's desk with the incriminating page face-down, which would buy hours, maybe a day, maybe nothing at all.

She walked faster.

The child matched her pace without effort and without complaint, chestnut bag folded neatly and tucked into her shirt, and after a mile or so of silence she began, quietly, to hum.

Felix stopped so abruptly that a crow lifted off the fence post ahead of them.

"No," she said. "*No.* Not that."

The humming stopped. The child watched her with wide, dark, entirely innocent eyes, and Felix stood on the rutted road with her heart slamming against her ribs, because she knew that tune. Three notes of it, four—it wasn't a song so much as a shape, a falling sequence like water finding stairs—and she knew it from a room that smelled of dried herbs and lamp oil, from a banned book with pages soft as cloth, from a voice reading aloud in a language that predated every kingdom whose coins she now counted.

"You can't hum that," Felix said, more gently than she meant to. "Not here. Not anywhere there might be ears. That song is—" She searched for a word the Guild would use. *Contraband. Uncollectable. Encumbered.* None of them fit. "That song is *theirs*, do you understand? If anyone hears it, they'll know what you are. They'll know where you've been. And then neither of us gets to be a person anymore. We become inventory."

Something crossed the child's face then—something old and brief and terribly tired, there and gone like a fish under ice—and Felix understood, with a lurch, that the child already knew all of this. Had probably known it since before Felix was born. Was humming anyway, because a person who has been inventory for long enough will pay any price to hear her own sound in the open air.

Felix crouched down, which put dust in her hem and cracked her knees, and looked the child levelly in the face.

"One condition," she said. "You want to sing it, you sing it to me. Only me. And you watch my hands." She raised one hand and showed her, palm out, fingers spread. "If I close this fist, you stop. Whatever note you're on, you swallow it. Can you do that?"

The child nodded. Then, after a moment, she reached out and closed Felix's fingers into a fist for her, demonstrating, and looked up with the ghost of something that on any other child's face would have been called mischief.

"That's right," Felix said, and found, to her considerable annoyance, that she was smiling. "Clerk. You'll be the death of me, and it'll be logged as my own fault."

* * *

They slept that night in the ruin of a way-shrine, one of the little roadside chapels the old kingdoms had built to gods nobody could name anymore. The roof was half-gone, letting in a sky salted with stars, and the stone god in the alcove had weathered down to a suggestion—a brow, a fold of robe, a hand held out in blessing or in asking.

Felix built a small fire because the night had teeth, and rationed out bread and hard cheese, and pretended not to watch the child eat with the steady, unhurried thoroughness of someone who had known hunger as a craft and not an accident.

"I had a system," Felix said, to the fire, because talking to fires was safer than talking to children. "Eleven years. Same desk, same ink, same lunch, same route home. I filed myself under 'permanent fixture.' Do you know what that takes? Every year I turned down promotions. Promotions. Do you understand what kind of discipline it takes to refuse a *promotion*?" She tore her bread precisely in half. "Because promoted people get looked at. Audited. Remembered. I wanted to be the woman who balanced the books and then evaporated. And it worked. For years, it worked beautifully."

The child licked cheese from her thumb and listened the way she did everything—completely.

"And then you knocked on the door," Felix said, "with your impossible language and your impossible face, and now I'm sitting in a ruined chapel eating contraband bread, running toward the one person on this earth who has every reason to turn me into a newt." She shook her head. "I want you to appreciate the scale of what's been lost here. My anonymity took a decade to build. You eroded it in a week. Erosion. That's the geological term, I think. Water wearing away stone. Very slow, very patient, doesn't look like anything's happening, and then one day the cliff is just—gone."

The child reached over and, very deliberately, tucked a loose strand of hair behind Felix's ear.

Felix froze.

It was such a small thing. Such an absurdly small thing. No one had touched her face in—she ran the calculation and abandoned it halfway through, because the answer was a number she didn't want to see. And the child did it without ceremony, the way you'd smooth a crease out of an important document, and then went back to her cheese, and the gesture sat in Felix's chest like a coal from the fire, glowing, inconvenient.

"You shouldn't get attached to me," Felix said quietly. "I'm not a good risk. Ask anyone I've ever owed anything to."

The child looked at her for a long moment. Then she pointed—at Felix, at herself, and then, with one finger, drew a slow line through the air between them, binding them together like a column in a ledger.

"I know," Felix said. "I felt it too. That's what frightens me."

The child frowned, dissatisfied with this, and tried again. She pointed at Felix. Then she pressed her own small fist against her own sternum, hard, twice—*here, inside, mine*—and then pointed back at Felix, and waited, with the terrible patience of the very young and the very old, as if the sentence were obvious and Felix were simply failing to read it.

And Felix, who could parse a contract in six languages and spot a forged seal at forty paces, sat there in the starlight and could not translate three gestures to save her life. Or perhaps she could, and simply didn't dare.

"Eat your bread," she said, gruffly, and threw another stick on the fire.

* * *

On the second day the road climbed, and the hedges closed in, and Felix began to feel it again: that prickling between her shoulders, the sensation of being *appraised*. Twice she turned and saw nothing—empty road, wheeling birds, wind combing the grass—but nothing, on the second occasion, had a quality of recentness about it, like a room someone had just left.

"We're being followed," she told the child, because lying to her seemed suddenly obscene. "Someone from the Guild. An appraiser, most likely. They're not soldiers—they're worse, in a way. They're reasonable. Everything with them is a fair price fairly struck. If we're caught, there will be forms. There will be options. And the options will all be terrible, and they will all be offered kindly." She adjusted the strap of her bag. "So we don't get caught. Understood?"

The child nodded. Then she tugged Felix's sleeve and pointed west, where the land rose into moorland, and beyond the moorland, barely visible, a smudge of green that any ordinary traveler would have taken for woods.

Felix looked at the smudge for a long time.

She had not been back in eleven years. She had built her whole careful, decimal-pointed life on the premise that she would never go back, and now here was her own two feet carrying her there at four miles an hour, with a stolen soul-child humming forbidden songs beside her and a Guild appraiser reading their worth from the road behind.

"There's a woman there," Felix said. "Her name is Odile. She taught me everything I know about things that aren't in ledgers, and then I—" The sentence hit the wall it always hit, the place where her memory went smooth and featureless as river stone. She frowned at it, probed it, found nothing. Strange. She'd never noticed the strangeness before. Lately she noticed it constantly. "And then I left," she finished. "Before I finished learning. She'll be angry. She has a right to be. She keeps bees that hold grudges."

The child smiled at that—an actual smile, small and crooked and devastating—and Felix looked away from it quickly, the way you look away from a flame.

They walked. The moor rose around them, purple and whispering, and somewhere behind them, patient as interest accruing, the appraiser walked too. And somewhere ahead, in a cottage with a crooked chimney and a garden that grew by rules of its own, a woman who was neither forgiving nor surprised was, Felix was certain, already setting out a second cup.

That certainty frightened her more than the appraiser did. Because she could not remember how she knew. And because some buried, unsellable part of her had missed it—had missed *her*—every single day, and had never once told Felix so.
