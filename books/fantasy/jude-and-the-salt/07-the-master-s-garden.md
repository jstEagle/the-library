## 7. The Master's Garden

The hedge had grown back.

That was the first thing Felix noticed, and it was the wrong thing to notice first, and she knew it. Ten years of meticulous bookkeeping had trained her to notice the significant thing—the anomaly, the unbalanced column—and instead she stood at the edge of Odile's property cataloging shrubbery like a coward.

The hedge was a boundary ward, of course. She knew that too. It had been a scraggly, half-starved thing when she'd left it, all thorn and spite, barely tall enough to catch a rabbit. Now it stood shoulder-high and dense as woven iron, its leaves a deep glossy green that no honest plant should wear in the dying weeks of autumn. It had grown back the way debts grow back: patiently, and with interest.

Behind her, the child made a small sound—not a word, not quite, but something with the shape of one. Felix turned. The girl stood in the rutted lane with her hands folded in front of her like a small merchant awaiting an appraisal, and she was looking at the cottage with an expression Felix could not read in any ledger she owned.

"You know this place," Felix said. It wasn't a question. Nothing about this child was ever a question; that was the trouble with her.

The girl tilted her head. Then she raised one hand and pointed—past the hedge, past the cottage, past everything—to the mountain.

It stood on the horizon the way it always had, gray-blue and patient, wearing its crown of cloud. From this distance it looked almost gentle. Felix had spent ten years in a city where you could forget it existed if you kept your eyes on your ledgers, and she had kept her eyes on her ledgers very carefully indeed.

"We're not going there," she said. "We're going *there.*" She pointed at the cottage, whose chimney was producing smoke, which meant someone was home, which meant—

Which meant it was time.

Felix had rehearsed this. On the road, three days of walking with a mute child who watched her with those old, old eyes, she had rehearsed it the way she rehearsed everything: precisely, repeatedly, in the hope that precision would hold the world together. She would knock. Odile would open the door. Felix would say—I'm sorry, or perhaps I need your help, or perhaps nothing at all, because what do you say to a woman whose trust you sold for a warm bed and a full belly? And then whatever came next would come next.

She had not rehearsed the hedge.

She put her hand against the leaves, expecting resistance, expecting thorns, expecting the ward to taste her name and find it wanting. Instead the branches parted. Smoothly. Willingly. Like a door held open by a servant who had been told to expect her.

The child slipped through ahead of her, quick as water, and Felix followed because the alternative was standing alone in a lane arguing with shrubbery.

* * *

The garden was the second wrong thing.

Odile had never been a gardener. Her plot behind the cottage had always been functional—a row of feverfew, some comfrey gone leggy, herbs grown for utility rather than beauty, weeded only when they interfered with the drying racks. Felix remembered it as a kind of organized neglect, which had suited them both.

Now it was magnificent.

Rows upon rows, laid out in patterns Felix's eye kept trying to parse as script. Winter-blooming things she had no names for, their petals white as salt. A bed of something silver-leafed that hummed, very faintly, when the wind moved through it. And everywhere, threading through every row like a spine, a strip of pale gravel that glittered wrong in the afternoon light—coarse, crystalline, catching the sun and throwing it back in little knives.

Salt. The garden paths were salted, deliberately, extensively, in a way that would kill anything trying to cross them.

Felix stopped in the middle of the path and felt the day tilt beneath her.

"Ten years," said a voice from the cottage door, "and you still walk like someone checking for exits."

Odile stood on her own threshold with flour on her hands and no expression at all, which from Odile was a great deal of expression. She had not aged the way people aged. There were new lines around her mouth, sure, and her iron-gray hair was longer than Felix remembered, but her eyes were exactly the same: dark, level, and patient in the way that deep water is patient.

"I—" Felix began, and discovered that ten years of rehearsal had produced nothing. "The hedge let me in."

"It did." Odile wiped her hands on her apron. "I adjusted it in the spring."

"In the spring." Felix's voice came out strange. "You adjusted it *in the spring?*"

"I have good days and bad days for divination. That was a good day." Odile looked past her, to the child standing among the winter-white flowers, and something crossed her face—quick, controlled, but unmistakable. Recognition. Not surprise. *Recognition.*

"You knew she'd come," Felix said.

"I knew *someone* would come. I didn't know which end of the story you'd be." Odile stepped off her threshold and came down the path, moving with the unhurried economy Felix remembered from a hundred lessons: how to stake a ward, how to read smoke, how to tell when a bargain is still breathing. "You look well, Felix. That's the cruelest thing about you. You look *well.*"

"I've been careful."

"Yes. You were always careful. You counted your tea leaves twice." Odile stopped an arm's length away, close enough that Felix could smell bread and rosemary and, beneath both, something mineral and cold. "I'm not going to ask you why you left. We both know the answer has teeth in it. But you'll come inside, because the light's going, and because that child needs feeding, and because you didn't walk three days through the Guild's territory with her on your back for the pleasure of my garden."

It was not forgiveness. It was not even welcome, exactly. It was logistics, offered in Odile's flat practical voice, and Felix found her eyes stinging anyway, which was humiliating, and so she nodded once, sharply, like closing a ledger, and followed her inside.

* * *

The cottage interior had changed less than the garden, which somehow made it worse. Same table, scarred where young Felix had once practiced cutting sigils into wood. Same hearth, same kettle hook, same shelf of jars labeled in Odile's cramped hand. The banned books were gone from their shelf—or rather, the shelf itself was gone, leaving a rectangle of unfaded wallpaper like a scar.

The child sat at the table eating bread and honey with the focused gravity of someone performing a sacrament. Odile watched her eat the way Felix had seen appraisers watch uncertain assets: total attention, no assumptions.

"She hasn't spoken," Felix said quietly, by the hearth. "Not a word. Not in any language. Except—" He hesitated. Saying it aloud, here, made it realer than he wanted. "Except that language. The one from your books. The ones I wasn't allowed to read."

"The ones you read anyway," Odile said. "At night. By moonlight, because you thought I couldn't see candle-glow under the door of your room." She turned. "Recite some."

"What?"

"You recognized fragments. So you remember fragments. Recite them."

And Felix, who had spent three days telling herself she'd imagined it—that exhaustion and fear had stitched nonsense out of a child's mouth—opened her mouth and heard herself say:

"*Ash velun kethra. Ash velun domm.*"

The words came out smooth and round and certain, in an accent she did not recognize as her own, and the moment they left her lips the fire in the hearth burned absolutely still. Not lower. *Still*—every flame frozen mid-flicker, the way water freezes, the way time is supposed to behave only in stories.

Then it resumed, and Odile exhaled slowly through her nose.

"That's the greeting of the court beneath the mountain," she said. "*Ash velun kethra*—I see you, salt-born. *Domm*—and am seen. It's a courtesy. It's also a claim. You don't say it lightly to anyone who might answer." She looked at Felix with those dark, patient eyes. "Where did you learn it?"

"I don't—" Felix's hands had gone cold. "I read it. In your books. I must have—"

"You read *fragments* in my books. Transliterations, broken bits, third-hand glosses. Enough to recognize the shape of the tongue, never enough to speak it. I made certain of that. I burned two volumes rather than leave them where you'd find them." Odile's voice stayed level, but underneath it Felix heard something she had never heard there before: a kind of dread being held very carefully, the way you carry a full cup across a crowded room. "What you just spoke was fluent. Native-fluent. With the court's own cadence."

The room seemed to lean in. Even the child had stopped eating and was watching Felix with those eyes, honey on her lip, utterly still.

"So either you learned it somewhere I don't know about," Odile continued, "or you learned it here, in this house, and then forgot learning it. And there are only two ways a person forgets a whole language, Felix. One is a blow to the head. The other is a sale."

The word landed in Felix's chest like a dropped coin.

"No," she said. "No, I would know. I keep ledgers. I know every transaction I've ever—my records go back to my first year at the Guild, I balance them nightly, I would *know* if—"

"Would you?" Odile asked softly. "Show me your memory of the day you left here. All of it. Every hour."

Felix reached for it, confident, automatic—and found fog.

There was the morning: she remembered the morning, the argument, the slammed door, the lane, the road south. She remembered it vividly, in perfect detail, and when she pressed backward from the morning toward the night before, the details went soft. Then softer. Then there was simply a gap, smooth-edged and seamless, like a page torn cleanly from a bound book—and the binding was so neat that until this exact moment she had never noticed the missing page at all. Her memory simply said *the night before I left* and offered nothing, and she had lived ten years believing that was normal. That grief did that. That anger blurred things.

"How much?" she whispered. "How much is missing?"

"I couldn't say. That's rather the point of taking it." Odile pulled out a chair and sat, heavily, the flour still on her apron. "But I can tell you what I know. You studied the old tongue with me for two years. You had a gift for it—you heard the grammar the way musicians hear intervals. And then, in your last winter here, something happened. Something I was never able to see clearly, because whatever it was, it was wrapped in transactions, and transactions blur when you try to scry them. Afterward you were different. Frightened. And then you were gone."

"I ran because of you," Felix said, and heard how thin it sounded. "Because you were—because the work was dangerous, because you wouldn't tell me—"

"I know what you believe," Odile said. "I've had ten years to consider what you believe. And I'll tell you the worst part, Felix, the part that should keep you up tonight: your story is *consistent*. Every piece fits every other piece. That's not what memory looks like when it frays naturally. That's what memory looks like when a professional edits it." She folded her hands. "Someone gave you a story to live inside. And whoever edits a person's past usually does it to hide what they took."

The child set down her bread. She slid off her chair, crossed the flagstones, and stood in front of Felix, looking up. Then, slowly, she reached out and touched Felix's sternum—right over the heart, right over the hollow place Felix had carried so long she'd mistaken it for her personality.

And she said, in that old rounded tongue, one word.

Felix didn't know its meaning. But Odile did. Felix saw it hit her—the color draining from her face, her hand going to the table's edge.

"What did she say?" Felix demanded. "What does it mean?"

Odile was quiet a long moment. When she answered, her voice had the careful flatness of someone reading a figure they wish were different.

"It means *sold.*" She met Felix's eyes. "In the court's tongue, it's what they write beside a name in their books. She says you're written in them, Felix. You're not the buyer here, and you're not the thief." She glanced at the child, then back, and the dread in her face finally broke surface. "You're inventory. You always have been. And I suspect the reason you can't remember your own past is that someone has already spent it."

Outside, far off across the darkening fields, the mountain wore its cloud like a hood, and the wind coming down from it tasted, faintly and unmistakably, of brine.
