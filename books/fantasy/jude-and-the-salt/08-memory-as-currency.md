## 8. Memory as Currency

The garden had gone to seed with a kind of deliberate grace, the way things did when Odile tended them. Felix recognized the foxglove crowding the fence line, the feverfew gone feral along the path, the row of bee-balm that Odile had once insisted on planting despite there being, as far as Felix could tell, no bees for forty miles. Everything grew exactly where it wanted to grow, and everything was exactly what it appeared to be, which was more than Felix could say for herself.

Odile set three cups on the table beneath the trellis. The tea smelled of juniper and something older, something that made the back of Felix's teeth ache in a way she couldn't name.

"Sit," Odile said. It wasn't an invitation. It hadn't been one in eleven years, and it wasn't going to become one now, just because Felix had arrived on her doorstep with a mute child and a Guild appraiser somewhere behind them and a signature on a receipt that didn't belong to her hands.

Felix sat. The child—Wren, because Felix needed something to call her and Wren was small and quick and had appeared at the Guild's door like a bird blown through a window—climbed onto the bench beside her and pressed close, shoulder to shoulder. Her warmth was startling. Felix kept forgetting that children were warm.

"You recognize the tongue," Odile said. Not a question either.

"Some of it." Felix wrapped her hands around the cup. "From your books. The ones you burned." A beat. "Or said you burned."

"I said I'd consider burning them." Odile lowered herself into the chair across the table, joints cracking like green wood. She was smaller than Felix remembered, or perhaps Felix had simply been smaller then, and everything about her master had seemed built on a scale that dwarfed the world. Now she looked like an old woman who kept a dangerous garden. "You studied it for two winters. You were better at it than you had any right to be. Then you stopped."

"I don't remember stopping."

"No," Odile said. "I imagine you don't."

* * *

The child drank her tea in small careful sips, watching them both with eyes that had seen too much salt. When Odile spoke to her—not in the old tongue, not yet, but in the low humming cadence hedge-witches used for animals and frightened things—Wren tilted her head and answered in fragments. Sounds that weren't quite words. The shape of language worn down by disuse, like a coin rubbed smooth by a thousand pockets.

"She's not broken," Odile said quietly. "She's emptying. Whatever they took from her, it isn't sitting still in her. It's draining out through the cracks they left." She turned to Felix. "How long since she surfaced?"

"Three days."

"Then you have less time than you think. A soul that's been handled doesn't stay whole aboveground. It wants to go home, and if it can't go home—" Odile shrugged one thin shoulder. "It goes wherever it's pulled. And there are plenty of hands in the world that know how to pull."

Felix's cup had stopped halfway to her mouth. "Pulled. By whom?"

"Anyone with the receipt." Odile's eyes were flat and gray as slate. "Which brings us to you, girl. Sit up straight. We're going to talk about money."

"We don't have any money."

"Oh, we have money." Odile smiled, and it was the smile Felix remembered from the worst lessons—the ones that ended with Felix bleeding into a poultice and understanding something fundamental about the world. "We have wonderful money. The best money there is. The court beneath the mountain doesn't trade in coin. They've never cared for it. Coin is anonymous. Coin says nothing about who you are." She leaned forward. "They trade in memory."

* * *

Felix had heard the phrase before, of course. Everyone in the Guild had. *Memory as tender, accepted at select exchanges.* It appeared in the footnotes of contracts, in the fine print nobody read. She'd catalogued a bottled lullaby and a debt of shadows; she'd entered *one (1) grandmother's recipe, recalled imperfectly* into the inventory ledger without blinking. But those were curiosities. Inventory. Things other people lost.

"They buy memories?" she asked, and heard how thin her voice came out.

"They buy everything a person is made of," Odile said. "A favorite afternoon. The face of a dead parent. The knowledge of how to swim. The feeling of being loved by someone specific. They crystallize them. That's what the nobles of the court are—crystallized memory wearing robes. They eat well, down there. They eat *lives*." She sipped her tea. "And they pay fairly. That's the horror of it, girl. They're honest merchants. They give exact change. If you sell them the memory of your mother's voice, they'll return to you the precise number of years that memory was worth, measured out in health and vigor. You'll be richer and emptier, and you won't be able to tell anyone what you're missing, because missing requires remembering."

Felix thought of the ledgers. Of the neat columns, the balanced books, the satisfaction of a page that added up. She thought of the senior trader's warning: *auditors are coming.* She thought of a signature on a receipt, in her own hand, that she had no memory of making.

"How do they measure?" she asked, because measurement was the only ground she had left. "What's the exchange rate?"

"It varies." Odile set down her cup. "Childhood runs dear. Firsts run dear. Anything tied to love runs very dear indeed. Grief is worth less—they have mountains of grief already, it's common as gravel. Joy is rare. Surprise is rarest of all. And the oldest memories—" her voice dropped, "—the ones from before you could talk? Those are nearly priceless. Nobody sells those. Almost nobody."

"And to enter the court. To bargain with them. I'd have to pay my way in."

"You'd have to pay for everything. The gate takes a toll. The audience takes a fee. Every question you ask costs, every answer you're given costs double, and if you flinch—if you show them a memory they want mid-negotiation—they'll take it off the top whether you agree or not. It's called the brine-tax. You'll feel it as a taste. Salty, on the tongue, right before something goes missing." Odile's gaze sharpened. "Most people come back from their first visit having sold more than they meant to. Most people come back not knowing what they've sold. That's not theft. That's just how the market works."

* * *

The light was going amber through the trellis. Somewhere in the garden, wind moved through the foxglove and made a sound like pages turning. Wren had fallen asleep against Felix's arm, her breathing shallow and quick, her fingers curled loosely around the hem of Felix's sleeve. Felix found she couldn't move her arm without waking her, and found, further, that she didn't want to.

"Say it plainly," Felix said. "What does it cost me to get her soul back?"

"A toll at the gate. Perhaps a season of your life—something recent, something you won't miss much. An audience fee—a little larger. Something middle-aged, so to speak. And the bargain itself." Odile folded her hands. "Whatever the Regent asks. Whatever they decide she's worth. And she's worth a great deal, girl. Last speaker of the old tongue? Living archive? They sold her soul to the surface by mistake, or by treachery, and they will want it back, but they will want *compensation* more. Souls of that weight don't move cheap."

"And if I can't pay?"

"Then you leave her there and come back alone, and you spend the rest of your life wondering what you traded to forget the trip." Odile said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "That's the standard outcome. Most people who descend don't complete their business. They get halfway through, spend a memory they needed, and wander back up missing the reason they went."

"That sounds like a system designed to keep people from succeeding."

"It is. It's a very good system. The Guild learned it from them, or they learned it from the Guild—I've never been able to establish which came first, and neither has anyone else, which should tell you something." Odile rose, joints crackling, and went to the low cabinet near the hearth. From it she took a book. Not one of the banned ones—or rather, one of the banned ones, but the smallest, the plainest, bound in undyed leather with no title on the spine. She set it on the table between the teacups. "Before you argue with me, look at this."

Felix looked. It was an account book. Old-fashioned, hand-ruled columns, entries in a spidery script she almost recognized—and then did recognize, with a lurch, because it was her own. Her apprentice hand, younger and rounder, recording transactions in a currency column headed with a symbol she'd never seen before entering the Guild and had never seen since: a circle bisected by a wavy line.

"What is this?"

"That's your practice ledger. From your last year with me. You were studying exchange magic—the deep kind, the kind the mountain uses." Odile tapped the page. "You were good at it. Frighteningly good. You understood instinctively what most students never grasp: that identity is finite. That a person is not a soul wearing memories like clothes. A person *is* the memories. Sell enough of them and there's no one left to wear anything. You wrote that down yourself, there, see? *The self is a sum that can be subtracted from.*"

Felix read her own handwriting and felt the floor tilt under the table.

"I don't remember writing that."

"No," Odile said again, softly now. "You don't remember a great many things. Do you know why you stopped studying the old tongue after two winters? Why you flinched from it? Because you'd already spent the part of you that spoke it. You paid your first debt to the mountain before you ever left this cottage, child, and you paid it in the only coin you had."

The wind turned the pages of the practice ledger. Felix watched her own young handwriting flip past like something swimming.

"How much have I spent?"

"I don't know. Neither do you. That's the trap." Odile sat back down, heavily. "Every transaction leaves a gap, and every gap seals itself over, smooth as water, because the mind abhors a hole worse than the body abhors a wound. You walk around inside yourself and the rooms all connect, and you never notice the ones that are missing, because a missing room looks like a wall until you try to open it." She reached across the table and, with two dry fingers, turned Felix's cup ninety degrees, some old habit of correction. "There are tests. Painful ones. There's a rite that can force a purchased memory back up to the surface for inspection—you hold it, you look at it, and then you choose whether to swallow it again or let it go. I can perform it. It will hurt. It may hurt more than you're prepared for, and what comes up may not be anything you want to own."

Felix looked down at Wren, asleep against her sleeve. The child's fingers tightened in her sleep, holding on.

"The receipt had my mark on it," Felix said. "My apprentice sigil, signing away a soul. I need to know if I did that."

"Yes," Odile agreed. "You do. And so do I, though for different reasons." She stood. "Tomorrow. At moonrise. Bring the girl's blanket—she'll sleep through it, and you won't, and one of you should be spared." She paused at the door of the cottage, silhouetted against the lamplight inside. "One more thing, Fairweather. Before you decide how much of yourself you're willing to spend down there—understand what the mountain really buys. Not the memories themselves. The *certainty*. Every person who descends is sure they know which memories matter, sure they can spare the right ones, sure they'll come back whole. The court has dined on that certainty for ten thousand years." Her voice dropped. "So before you go pricing yourself, ask yourself: what would it mean, if the thing you're most sure of—the thing you built your whole life on—is the first thing they take?"

She went inside. The door clicked shut with the soft finality of a ledger closing.

Felix sat in the darkening garden with a sleeping child against her arm and her own handwriting swimming on the table before her. *The self is a sum that can be subtracted from.* She had written that at sixteen, before she knew anything, when she still believed knowing a thing was the same as being safe from it.

Far off down the road, faint as a struck match, something glinted. Once. Twice. The rhythm of boots, keeping pace.

The appraiser had found the road.

And tomorrow at moonrise, Felix would open herself up like an account book and find out, entry by entry, what was left to spend.
