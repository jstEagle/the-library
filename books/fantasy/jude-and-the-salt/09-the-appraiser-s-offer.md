## 9. The Appraiser's Offer

Vess had been walking for three days, and the road had begun to feel like a sentence being read aloud very slowly.

She did not mind roads, as a rule. Roads were orderly. A road went from one place to another and did not pretend otherwise. What she minded was this particular road, which wound up out of the river valley in long switchbacks through country that could not decide whether it was farmland or wilderness—half-tended orchards gone feral at the edges, stone walls dissolving into bramble, a chapel with no roof and no congregation and one stubborn bell still hanging in its tower like a tooth that refused to fall out.

The Guild of Improbable Trade sent appraisers along established routes. Vess had walked most of them. She knew the mileage between waypoints the way other people knew prayers, and she kept her ledgers the way other people kept their consciences: balanced, current, and closed at night with a ribbon.

The discrepancy had been flagged on a Thursday. Vess remembered because Thursdays were reconciliation days, and there was something almost elegant about an error surfacing on the day set aside for finding them. Entry 4,417 — one soul, minor, provenance contested, tongue unclassified — entered into inventory at the receiving desk and then absent from every subsequent audit. The receiving clerk swore the child had been brought in at dusk. The door ledger said the child had been brought in at dusk. Every record after that said nothing at all, which was itself a kind of statement. Things did not simply vanish from Guild books. Things were made to vanish, and someone signed their name doing it, and that name was supposed to be legible.

Felix Fairweather's name was legible. It always was. That was what everyone said about her—that her columns were so clean you could eat off them, that she had never once filed a late report, that if Felix Fairweather told you a number you could take it to your grave. Which was precisely why Vess had requested the assignment personally, over the objections of two senior traders who felt it was a waste of a competent appraiser to send her after a bookkeeper.

They were wrong. You did not send a hound after a fox. You sent someone who understood why the fox ran, and Vess understood better than anyone in the building, because Vess had spent eleven years learning exactly how much order a person could buy, and exactly what it cost.

* * *

She caught the first real trace of them on the morning of the fourth day, at a shrine where the road forked toward the old forest.

Someone had left an offering—a heel of bread, wrapped in cloth, placed neatly on the worn lip of the altar stone. Beside it, drawn in the dust with a fingertip, a line of symbols Vess did not recognize. Not a script so much as a weather system: curves that seemed to bend against their own logic, marks that hurt slightly to look at, the way bright snow hurts.

Vess crouched and studied them without touching. Her training was thorough; among her competencies were forty-one recognized scripts, six dead languages of commercial significance, and the complete taxonomy of things the Guild was licensed to trade. This was none of those. This was older than licensing. This was the kind of writing that predated the idea that anything could belong to anyone.

And beneath it, half-erased where a boot had dragged through, a smaller notation in ordinary Guild shorthand—the accounting cipher taught only to clerks. A column mark. A carry-over symbol. Someone had been teaching the strange language arithmetic.

Or someone who already knew both had written both, and one hand had belonged to the child, and one—

No. Vess straightened. Facts first. Feelings were inventory too, and she had learned to keep hers in a separate ledger, sealed, rarely opened.

Two travelers. One adult, trained in Guild notation. One child, speaking or writing an unclassified tongue. Heading away from the lowlands, toward the hills, toward—she consulted her route map, then the older survey maps beneath it—toward a region marked, in faded ink, with a single word: *hedge.*

The Guild's maps did not label hedge-witches. Hedge-witches were not tradeable, taxable, or insurable, and therefore did not officially exist. But Vess had read enough marginalia to know what lived out here, in the green dark between jurisdictions. And she knew that a woman running from the Guild with stolen property would run toward exactly the kind of person who had spent a lifetime making things unfindable.

She walked faster.

* * *

She found them at dusk, in the ruin of a shepherd's cot below the tree line—an old dry-stone shelter with its roof mostly intact, smoke threading thin from a gap where a window had once been. Vess did not approach immediately. Approach was a skill. You did not sprint at a frightened animal; you arrived the way weather arrives, inevitable but unhurried, so that by the time you stood in the doorway the other party had already half-adjusted to your existence.

She banked her fire-sense down—appraisers carried certain small instruments of the trade, and Vess kept hers holstered—and knocked on the stones beside the doorway rather than the wood, a courtesy signal, non-entry. Then she waited.

Inside: silence, then the scrape of a body moving, then a voice she recognized from a hundred corridor greetings, pitched now with an edge she had never heard from Felix Fairweather before.

"If you're a robber, the Guild pays me almost nothing."

"Guild pays everyone almost nothing," Vess said. "It's how we know the numbers are honest. May I come in, Mistress Fairweather? I'd rather not conduct an audit standing in the rain."

A long pause. Rain ticked against the surviving corner of roof. Then: "You're alone?"

"I'm alone."

"You're lying."

"I'm never lying," Vess said, and meant it in the way that made people nervous. "Lying creates discrepancies. I am the opposite of discrepancies."

The bar scraped back. Vess ducked under the lintel into a small round room smelling of woodsmoke and wet wool and, faintly, of salt.

Felix Fairweather sat on a bedroll near the fire with a knife held low and casual, the way accountants hold knives—incorrectly, but with great conviction. Behind her, half-hidden in the shadow of the curved wall, a child. Small, maybe seven years old, with eyes that reflected firelight oddly, the way ice reflects it—not orange but a pale glacial blue-white, as though the flames were merely passing through her on their way somewhere else.

Vess looked at the child for exactly three seconds, cataloguing, then deliberately looked away and let her gaze rest on the fire instead. It was important, in this next part, to be a person and not an instrument. Instruments got knives thrown at them. Or worse—people ran from instruments, and Vess did not have the provisions to chase anyone across these hills.

"Felix," she said, using the given name deliberately, a small intimacy purchased at no cost. "Sit down. I'm not here to seize anyone tonight."

"You're here to seize someone tomorrow, then."

"I'm here to talk." She unbuckled her satchel—slowly, showing her hands—and drew out neither irons nor writs but a wax-paper parcel, which she set on the floor between them. Inside: hard cheese, dried apples, a twist of good salt. "Dinner first. Negotiations go badly on empty stomachs. That's not philosophy, that's documented. There's a study."

Felix didn't move. The child moved—forward, one step, nose lifting at the smell of the apples—and Felix's hand shot out and caught the girl's shoulder, gentle and absolute, and Vess watched the whole gesture with the particular attention she reserved for data points that contradicted a file.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing she had turned over for four days on the road. Felix Fairweather, senior reconciler, the most reliably self-interested clerk in the Guild's employ—self-interested in the modest, survivable way of clever people who had learned young that institutions eat the careless—had committed a catastrophic crime with no profit in it whatsoever. No sale. No skimming. No rival bought off. Just an entry erased and a child carried into the rain.

There were only two explanations Vess had ever encountered for crimes without profit: madness, and love. Felix showed no signs of madness. Her columns had been sane for nine years.

"Sit," Vess said again, softly. "Please."

Felix sat. The child sat pressed against her side, eating an apple slice with the concentration of someone relearning food, or perhaps tasting it for the first time in a long time. Vess took her own share, ate a little, and began.

* * *

"Here is the situation," she said, when the cheese was gone and the fire had settled. "As I see it. Correct me where I'm wrong.

"The Guild received a child at closing, four nights ago. Provenance unknown. Tongue unclassified—which means, under Charter Article Twelve, she cannot be appraised, cannot be priced, and therefore cannot legally be entered into inventory. An item that cannot be inventoried is, however, still an item, and unclassified items default to custodial review pending classification. Custodial review is conducted by the Office of Appraisal." Vess touched her own collarbone, briefly: her own badge, hanging beneath her coat. "Me. My office. My jurisdiction.

"But she was never entered for review. Someone removed her. The erasure bears the signature of Felix Fairweather. And Felix Fairweather has fled the jurisdiction with the asset. Under Article Thirty-One, that is theft of Guild property. Under Article Forty-Four, since the property may prove to be of unique class, it is grand theft. Grand theft carries forfeit of assets, ten years' bonded labor, and—" she paused, not because she enjoyed the pause but because accuracy required it— "in cases involving items of singular provenance, remanding of the thief's own soul to the Guild's custody for valuation and resale. To cover damages."

The fire popped. The child had stopped eating. She was watching Vess with that glacial attention, head tilted, and Vess had the uncomfortable sense of being read—not her words, her *ledger*. As though the girl could see the entries written in her.

"So," Vess continued. "That's the situation. Now here is the offer.

"You surrender the child to my custody. Voluntarily, tonight, at this location, which I will note in my report as *recovered through negotiation, minimal expenditure.* In exchange: full immunity. All charges voided. Your signature on the erasure gets attributed to administrative error—I have seen nine administrative errors this year alone, Felix, the Guild is drowning in them, one more costs nothing. You return to your desk. Your wages resume. Your clean record resumes. In five years nobody remembers this happened, because the Guild does not remember things that don't appear in its books, and I control what appears in its books."

She let it sit. It was, she thought objectively, a generous offer. It was the kind of offer that got appraisers commended.

Felix was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was careful. "And the child? What happens to her under custodial review?"

"She gets classified."

"How?"

"That depends on what she is." Vess kept her tone level, factual, because the temptation—the ugly, human temptation—was to soften it, and softening was its own kind of lie. "If she proves classifiable, she gets valued. If she gets valued, she gets listed. If she gets listed, she gets sold. That's the machinery. I don't built it. I maintain it."

"You'd be selling a child."

"The Guild would be selling an asset whose classification happens to include a child." Vess looked at the fire. "I'm aware those are different sentences describing the same event. I've had eleven years to think about the difference. I haven't resolved it."

"Why make this offer, then?" Felix leaned forward. "If you know what the machinery does—why bring me the immunity? Why not just write your report and call the retrievers?"

Vess considered several answers. The procedural one: because negotiation produced cleaner paperwork. The professional one: because a cooperative witness was worth more than a fugitive. Both true. Neither sufficient.

"Because four days ago I read the original intake entry," she said finally. "Before it was erased. I keep copies of everything; it's a failing. The intake clerk wrote a description of the child. Standard form. Age, sex, condition, distinguishing marks. And then, at the bottom, in the space reserved for *origin,* he wrote one line: 'Says she came from under the mountain. Says she was sold.' And he wrote, in the field reserved for *seller of record*—"

Vess stopped. The child had gone very still against Felix's side. Even the firelight in her eyes seemed to stop moving.

"—the seller of record was the Guild itself," Vess finished quietly. "Countersigned twice. Sealed. Which means either the intake clerk was drunk, or this child has been through our books before. Sold out and sold back. And I have been an appraiser for eleven years, Felix, and I have learned one thing above all others: when the numbers contradict themselves, it isn't the numbers that are lying. It's whoever balanced them."

She rose, slowly, and took a folded paper from inside her coat—the offer, drafted in proper form, wax and seal ready—and laid it on the floor between them, on the stone, where the firelight reached it.

"Immunity," she said. "Your life back. Your clean columns. Everything you built, restored, in exchange for one surrender. It's reasonable. I've made sure it's reasonable. Reasonable offers are the only kind worth making, because unreasonable ones get refused and reasonable ones get *chosen*, and I need you to choose, Felix. I need to watch you choose it. Because if you hand her over—if you can do it—then you're just a thief who got smart, and my report writes itself, and I sleep fine.

"But if you can't," Vess said, and here her voice did something she had not planned, dropping into a register she barely recognized, "if you stand there with your knife and your clean reputation and your nine years of careful invisibility, and you look at that child and tell me *no*—then this is not a theft. Then this is something else, something the Charter doesn't have a category for, and I will need to know what it is. For my records."

Silence. Rain. The child's cold-bright eyes moving between them like a scale weighing two weights.

Felix looked at the paper. At the seal. At the neat, reasonable, immaculately balanced shape of her own forgiveness, priced at exactly one child.

Then she looked down at the girl beside her, and Vess watched her do it—watched the exact moment the choice moved through her, visible as a tremor in the hands, a change in the breathing—and when Felix lifted her head, her eyes were wet and absolutely steady, and she pushed the paper back across the stone with two fingers, slow, deliberate, all the way, until it lay in Vess's lap.

"No," Felix said. It came out rough. She swallowed and said it again, steadier, like a figure she was checking twice because it mattered. "No."

Something in Vess's chest performed an operation she had no entry for. She picked up the contract, and the child flinched—but Vess only held it to the fire, feeding it to the flames corner-first, watching the seal blister and the reasonable language curl and blacken until there was nothing left of the offer but ash and heat.

"Then I have a problem," Vess said. She settled back down onto the stone, heavily, like a woman setting down a weight she'd carried a long way. "My orders say retrieve the asset. My report requires a resolution. And the only resolutions available to me now are ones I find—" she searched for the word, and chose it with care, the way she chose all figures— "*discrepant.*"

Felix stared at her. "What are you saying?"

"I'm saying," Vess said, pulling off her badge of office and turning it over in her fingers, catching firelight in the stamped sigil of the Guild, "that I have read the original contract, or what survives of it, and there is a countersignature on it that should not exist. The Grand Ledger's own seal, on a sale the Grand Ledger has no record of authorizing. Which means somebody very high in the Guild sold this child once already, erased the evidence, and would very much like my audit to end with you in irons and her in a vault."

The child spoke then—for the first time since Vess had arrived, in that voice like wind over ice, a single word in the old unclassifiable tongue—and pointed one small finger past them both, out through the broken window, north, toward the hills, toward whatever hedge waited there in the dark.

Vess looked where she pointed. Then she looked at Felix Fairweather, fugitive, thief, and apparently the most honest bookkeeper in the history of dishonest institutions.

"Well," Vess said, and began repacking her satchel with the brisk efficiency of a woman reclassifying her entire life mid-audit. "We'll need to walk fast. My superiors expect a report by week's end, and I intend to give them one they'll wish they'd never taught me to write."
