## 10. What Felix Sold

Odile did not ask permission. She never had.

Felix stood in the garden at dusk while the hedge-witch moved through her rows with a sickle that was more ceremony than tool, cutting stems of things Felix could not name—flowers that closed when looked at directly, herbs that smelled like other people's kitchens, a moss that hummed faintly in the key of a lullaby Felix almost remembered. The child sat on the cottage step with her knees drawn up, watching everything with the wide, unblinking patience of someone who had once watched centuries pass from inside a mountain.

"She'll need salt," Odile said without turning around. "Not yours. The child's. A pinch from her heel, where the court's mark still sits."

"The court marked her?"

"They mark everything they touch." Odile straightened, pressing the cuttings into a clay bowl. "That's how I knew what she was before she reached my gate. Salt leaves a signature, girl. You'd know that if you'd ever read your own skin."

Felix had been avoiding mirrors for eleven years. She suspected this was related.

The bowl went onto the table inside, among the candles, and Odile began arranging the herbs in patterns that made Felix's ledger-trained eye itch—nothing balanced, nothing symmetrical, every line deliberately broken. Magic, she thought, was bookkeeping done by someone who hated arithmetic.

"What you're asking me to do," Felix said, "is dangerous."

"What I'm asking you to *let* me do is dangerous. You've been asking since we got back from the well." Odile struck a match off her thumbnail. "There's a difference, and the difference matters tonight."

The plan, such as it was: the mountain court kept its purchases in trust, and anything held in trust could be *viewed*, if you had the right key. Not returned—viewed. Odile couldn't reach into the vaults below and pull out Felix's sold memories like coins from a drawer. But one memory had come back through the cracks on its own, the way water finds its way through stone. The first one. The cheapest one. The year Felix had traded away at sixteen, which the salt-merchant had apparently resold so many times, spent so carelessly, that its edges had worn thin enough to slip loose.

"It's already trying to return," Odile said. "I'm not fetching it. I'm holding the door open and making sure it comes back whole instead of in pieces. Pieces are worse. Pieces are how people end up talking to their chairs."

"That's very reassuring."

"I'm not in the reassurance business." She lit the candles one by one, and each flame burned a different color—brine-white, rust, the deep green of pond ice. "Sit down. Hands flat on the table. If you feel like running, don't. If you feel like screaming, do it quietly; the girl frightens easily."

The child did not look like she frightened easily. She looked like she was cataloguing them both.

* * *

The rite began with salt and ended with silence, and between those two things there was a sound Felix would spend the rest of her life failing to describe—a sound like a page being uncrumpled, like a debt being read aloud in a language that predated reading aloud, like something long-buried turning over in its sleep and deciding, after consideration, to wake up.

Then she was sixteen again, and starving.

* * *

The winter Felix was sixteen had no end to it. That was the thing about that winter she always forgot until she was inside it again: it simply refused to conclude. Snow fell in October and kept falling through what should have been thaw, and the roads closed, and the markets emptied, and by the time the calendar insisted it was nearly spring, the village had eaten its seed grain and begun trading things that should never have been tradeable.

She remembered hunger as a specific sensation—not an absence but a presence, a second person living under her ribs, cold and patient and increasingly articulate. It spoke to her at night. It said reasonable things.

Her master's cottage had food in it. That was the unbearable arithmetic of that winter. Odile's larder stayed stocked because Odile was careful, and clever, and had arrangements with the land itself that Felix, sixteen and proud and furious about being an apprentice rather than a daughter, refused to benefit from. She had left. She had walked out into the white world with one cloak and no plan, because pride is a currency too, and she had spent hers all at once, and gotten nothing back.

By February she was in the low town by the river, where the salt-merchant kept his shop.

Everyone knew him. He came through every few years in a cart drawn by two gray mules, selling coarse white salt in blocks stamped with a spiral, and everyone bought, because salt was life and he always had it when no one else did. He was a small man, neat, with hands like folded paper and eyes the color of nothing in particular. People said he was odd. People said his prices were strange. People said these things and then they paid, because the alternative was a slow gray death, and the human mind is very good at not looking directly at the fine print.

Felix stood outside his shop for three days before she went in.

Inside, the shelves were full of things that were not salt. A jar of someone's laughter. A ribbon tied around a shadow. A birdcage with a song in it, hopping from perch to perch. And ledgers—rows of ledgers, bound in gray leather, their spines blank.

"Apprentice of the hedge-witch," the merchant said. It wasn't a question. His voice was dry as his wares. "I wondered when you'd come. You have her stubbornness and none of her sense. Sit."

"I want salt."

"Everyone wants salt." He set a block of it on the counter between them, white as a sleeping animal. "What will you give me?"

"I haven't any money."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "You haven't. But you have other things. Everyone has other things." He opened one of the gray ledgers to a fresh page, and Felix saw that the pages weren't paper. They were something thinner, translucent, faintly warm. "A memory of your mother's face. A summer afternoon. The taste of honey. These fetch fair prices."

"I won't sell those."

"Mm. Most people say that first." He turned the page. "But you're a clever girl, or you'd be dead already, and clever girls notice that some memories cost less than others. Time, for instance. A year of your life. No pictures, no faces, no pain. Just... duration. An hour here, a season there, skimmed off the top. You wouldn't even miss it." He smiled with great kindness. "You'd simply be seventeen slightly sooner than expected. Or eighteen. Or twenty. Who counts, really? Do you count?"

And sixteen-year-old Felix—who was hungry down to the marrow, who had spent her pride and had nothing left but her body and her future, who genuinely believed she was making a shrewd, temporary arrangement, a bridge loan against better weather—looked at the block of salt and heard the thing under her ribs purr.

"One year," she said.

"For one winter's worth. Enough to get you to thaw."

"One year."

He wrote it down. She watched the pen move across the warm translucent page, and she felt nothing—that was the horror of it, the thing she would only understand decades later. No tug, no tearing, no cold wind through the soul. Just a faint lightness, like setting down a pack she hadn't known she was carrying, and the merchant's neat hand, and the salt wrapped in brown paper under her arm, heavy as a promise.

"Come again next winter," the merchant said as she reached the door. "You'll find the terms improve with familiarity."

She never went back to his shop. She didn't need to. The network found her—the clerks, the appraisers, the polite men with gray leather books—and over the following years she sold and sold and sold, always telling herself this was the last transaction, always believing she was the one driving the bargain. By the time she fled Odile's cottage for good, she had been trading pieces of herself for so long that she could no longer tell which parts of her history were real and which had been signed away. So she built new ones. Meticulous ones. Ledgers within ledgers.

The receipt for that first sale, though. She remembered the receipt. She had kept it folded in her boot for years, a superstition, proof that she had survived something—and then one day it was gone, and she could not remember losing it, and she had told herself that was nothing too.

* * *

Felix came back to the present with her face wet and her hands still flat on the table, gripping it as if the table might otherwise float away.

The candles had burned down to pools. Odile sat across from her, arms crossed, watching with an expression Felix had no name for—not triumph, not pity. Something older than either.

"Well?" Odile said.

"You knew." Felix's voice came out scraped raw. "You knew about the merchant. You knew I—"

"I knew a girl came back from the river town that spring with salt and a lie about buying bread. I knew she flinched whenever anyone mentioned the low town. I asked her once if she'd made a bargain down there and she called me a meddler and packed her things." Odile unfolded her arms. "So yes. I knew. What I didn't know was the shape of it. Now I do, because now you do, and you wear your knowledge on your face like everyone else does. You always were a terrible card player."

"A year," Felix whispered. "It was one year. One stupid, cheap, forgettable year, and it—" Her hands were shaking. She pressed them together to stop it and failed. "It was the door. Everything else walked through that door. Every sale after. The Guild. All of it."

"Yes."

"I could have starved. I should have just—" The old contempt rose in her throat, familiar as ink stains, and she let it rise, and then, for the first time, she looked at it honestly. Sixteen. Starving. A winter with no end. A merchant whose prices were designed by something older than cruelty. What exactly had that girl been supposed to do, alone in the snow, with no one willing to feed her unless she stopped being so proud?

Nothing. There had been nothing to do. The choice had been a wall painted to look like a doorway.

"She was a child," Felix said slowly, testing the words like unfamiliar coinage. "She was a hungry child, and a grown man with a gray book offered her a deal dressed up to look survivable, and she took it, and then she spent forty years hating herself for being hungry."

The child, silent on the bench by the window, made a small sound. When Felix looked over, the girl was watching her with those deep, patient eyes, and she raised one hand and touched her own chest—*here*—and then pointed at Felix—*you*—and nodded once, solemnly, the way one acknowledges a fellow survivor of the same flood.

Something in Felix's chest cracked along an old seam, and instead of breaking, it opened.

"I thought coming back here would mean facing what I did to you," she said to Odile. "That was the fear. That was the whole fear, wrapped around the journey like wire."

"And now?"

"Now I think the thing I actually sold wasn't a year of my life." Felix looked down at her hands—ink-stained, steady again, older than they should be. "It was the right to forgive myself. They kept that part in the vault. They kept *all* the parts, and charged me interest on remembering that I'd pawned them."

Odile was quiet for a long moment. Then she reached across the table, took Felix's wrist, and turned her hand palm-up, studying the lines the way she studied weather.

"The first sale leaves a mark," she said. "Deep in the palm, under the skin, where the court writes its accounts. Every trader has one. Most never see it because most never look." Her thumb traced a spiral in the center of Felix's palm, and where it passed, the skin prickled cold. "Yours spirals inward. That's rare. Inward means the debt flows back toward its origin—which means, girl, that somewhere in the mountain's books, your first transaction is still open. Unsettled. They consider you *unfinished business*."

"That's not possible. I paid. I've been paying for thirty years—"

"In salt and silence and forgetting, yes. But the original contract was never closed, because the merchant who held it never died and never forgave, and everything he held passed down to the court beneath when the network went home to roost." Odile released her wrist. "Which brings us to the interesting part. The part you're not going to like."

"I've had a full day of parts I don't like. I've developed a tolerance."

"The court doesn't sell memories back, Felix. It trades them. And it trades them only with the account holder—in person, at the gate, with the original mark presented." She tapped Felix's spiraling palm once, sharply. "If you want the rest of what's yours, if you want to understand what you signed and who profited and why a mute child arrived at your Guild carrying your handwriting on her soul's receipt—you don't send a letter. You don't send Marrow. You walk down yourself, through the salt gate, and you settle your account face to face."

Outside the window, far off in the direction of the mountains, thunder rolled across a sky with no clouds in it.

The child stood up. She crossed the room without hurry, took Felix's marked hand in both of her own small ones, and turned it over gently, studying the spiral with the gravity of an archivist confirming a catalogue number. Then she looked up, and her mouth moved, and though no sound came out, Felix understood her perfectly—understood it in her bones, in the place where the old tongue lived whether or not one consented to speak it:

*They know you're coming. They've been waiting for you since the winter you were sixteen.*

* * *

Felix slept badly, which was to say she dreamed of gray ledgers opening themselves, and woke before dawn with her decision already made, sitting fully formed in her chest like a stone that had always been there.

She found Odile in the garden, cutting morning stems by feel in the dark.

"I'll need supplies," Felix said. "And whatever you know about the salt gate. And Marrow's favor token—I sent word last night; he'll meet me at the crossroads with it and pretend this conversation never happened, because pretending things never happened is his finest skill."

Odile snipped a stem and dropped it in the basket. "And the girl?"

"Stays here. With you." Felix hesitated, and the hesitation cost her something, and she let it. "Keep her safe. Please. I know what I am to ask, given our history. I know what I did to earn your refusal. But I'm asking anyway."

Odile set down the sickle and looked at her for a long, measuring moment—the look of a woman weighing not a request but a person, and finding the scales closer to level than they had been in thirty years.

"She stays," Odile said. "Now come inside. Before you walk into that mountain, there are three more things you need to know about the merchant with the gray books—and the third one is why his ledgers were in my forbidden shelf all along."

Felix went cold. "You knew him."

"I married him," said Odile, "for six weeks, in another century. Come in, and I'll show you the divorce papers."
