## 11. Descent at the Salt Gate

The child did not want to let go of Felix's sleeve.

She didn't cry—she had never once cried, as far as Felix could tell, and there was something in that restraint that frightened Felix more than any wailing would have. She simply held on, small fingers pinching the wool of Felix's cuff, her dark eyes fixed on Felix's face with the terrible patience of someone who has already been left behind before.

"You'll be back by the third bell of morning," Odile said from the doorway of the cottage, arms crossed, her silver-streaked hair bound back with a strip of dyed linen. She said it the way she said everything: flatly, as though reciting a recipe. "If you're not, I'm not coming after you. I've done my time in that mountain. Once was enough for one lifetime."

"Understood," Felix said.

"I mean it, girl."

"I know you do." Felix crouched so that she was level with the child. Up close, the salt-pale skin had a faint translucence to it, like paper held against a candle. "I have to go get something that belongs to you," she said slowly, carefully, the way one speaks across a language barrier even when no language stands between them. "I can't carry you down there. It wouldn't be safe. But Odile will keep you, and I will come back, and then—" She faltered. Promises were a currency too; she knew better than anyone how cheaply they could be minted. "Then we'll see what you look like under open sky."

The child studied her for a long moment. Then she reached up and pressed two fingers to Felix's sternum, just over the heart, and spoke a single word in the old tongue—a low, resonant syllable that seemed to hum in Felix's teeth rather than reach her through the air.

"What did she say?" Felix asked Odile without looking away.

"'Remember,'" Odile said. "It's an imperative. A command form." Her mouth twisted. "They don't ask things politely, that language. It's all ledgers and orders. You'd feel right at home."

Felix stood. The child's hand slid from her sleeve, and the loss of that small weight felt like a page torn cleanly out of a book—the binding still intact, but the story suddenly missing a piece.

* * *

The road to the mountain ran through country that grew stranger the higher they climbed. By midday the grass had gone grey-green and brittle, and by late afternoon it stopped being grass at all, giving way to pale crystal growths that chimed faintly when the wind moved them, like a thousand tiny glasses being set out for a banquet no one would attend.

Marrow's favor token sat heavy in Felix's breast pocket. It was a disc of black iron, unmarked except for a single stamped character in the Guild's oldest script—a character Marrow had told her meant *debt acknowledged*, though she suspected it also meant *this person may be collected upon later*. He had pressed it into her hand at the crossroads outside Thornhollow with his usual expression, which was the expression of a man doing arithmetic behind his eyes and finding the sum distasteful but unavoidable.

"You understand this makes us even," he'd said. "Not friends. Even. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

"I understand."

"And if Vess asks—and she will ask—you tell her the truth. The Guild respects truth told plainly far more than it respects lies told cleverly. Lies are just bad accounting, Fairweather. They never balance."

Now Vess walked ten paces behind her, and had done so since dawn, saying nothing.

Felix had tried, in the first hour, to lose her. Old habits. She had taken the goat track instead of the road, doubled back through a stand of birches, crossed the stream twice in opposite directions. Vess had appeared each time on the path ahead or beside her, unhurried, her grey coat immaculate despite the mud, her ledger-satchel riding her hip like a holstered weapon. Finally Felix had stopped and turned around and said, "Are you going to arrest me?"

"No," Vess had said.

"Then why follow me?"

"Because the Guild sent me to observe, and observation does not require your permission." A pause. "Also because I read the original contract last night, and I have questions, and you are currently the only person walking toward the answers."

That had been four hours ago. Since then: silence, except for the chime of the crystal grass and the crunch of their boots on ground that grew whiter with every mile.

Felix found the silence unbearable, which surprised her. She had spent nine years cultivating silence, wrapping herself in it like insulation. Silence was how you survived a place like the Guild—you made yourself quiet enough, precise enough, invisible enough that no auditor ever flagged your name. But silence shared with another person was different. It had weight. It demanded filling.

"The contract," she said finally, not turning around. "What questions?"

Behind her, Vess's footsteps kept their metronome rhythm. "The signature on the sale record is yours. Your apprentice mark. I verified it against Guild records—it matches, stroke for stroke, including the little hesitation in the descender where you lifted the pen." Another pause. "Except that according to those same records, you did not receive your apprentice mark until three years *after* the date of sale."

Felix's stomach dropped. "That's impossible."

"Yes," Vess agreed mildly. "I find that impossibilities are rarely impossible. They are usually misfiled."

* * *

The salt gate announced itself long before they saw it.

First came the smell—brine and something older beneath it, mineral and mineral-adjacent, the smell of a thing that had been waiting so long it had forgotten what it was waiting for. Then the sound: a low, continuous tone, barely audible, like a wet finger circling the rim of an enormous glass. Felix felt it in her fillings, in the bones of her inner ear, in the scar tissue over her ribs where a hedge-witch's needle had once stitched her back together.

Then they crested the ridge, and there it was.

The gate was not built. That was Felix's first thought, and her second, and her third, because every other explanation failed. It was a fissure in the mountainside perhaps forty feet tall, its edges grown over with crystalline formations that caught the dying light and threw it back in colors Felix had no names for—not quite rose, not quite gold, the color of memory seen through water. And set into the rock above the opening, carved so deeply into the stone that it must have been cut before the stone itself cooled, was a line of text in the old tongue.

Felix stopped walking.

She could read it.

That was the thing that stole the breath from her lungs. Not that the words were there—she had expected the words—but that they resolved into meaning as easily as Guild script, as easily as her own name. She had never studied this language. She had never been taught it. And yet the characters arranged themselves in her mind like figures in a ledger, patient and legible:

*ALL WHO ENTER SET DOWN WHAT THEY CARRY.*

"It's a toll notice," she said aloud, stupidly.

"Of a kind," Vess said, coming to stand beside her. She had produced a small notebook and was writing in it with a pencil stub, unhurried. "The Guild has three recorded descents through this gate in the last century. Two traders returned. One did not. The two who returned both reported losing something they couldn't name—a smell, a habit, the ability to recall their mother's face. The court accepts memories as tender. Everyone knows that. What fewer people know is that the gate takes a deposit before you're even inside."

Felix touched the token in her pocket. "Marrow said this would get me through."

"Marrow's token will get you past the door wardens. It will not stop the mountain from taking its tithe." Vess closed the notebook. "Nothing stops the mountain. You can only decide what you're willing to pay before it starts choosing for you."

The light was failing. Below them, the fissure breathed its long glass note, and somewhere beyond it, down in the dark, a court of crystallized memory was keeping its books, and one of the entries in those books had Felix's mark on it, forged or otherwise, and a child's soul filed under *assets received*.

Felix thought about the child's fingers on her sleeve. About the word pressed over her heart like a seal in wax. *Remember.*

"All right," she said, and was surprised to hear that her voice did not shake. "Let's go be appraised."

* * *

The passage beyond the gate sloped downward in long switchbacks, and the walls began almost immediately to show them things.

Felix noticed it first as a flicker at the edge of vision—a glint in the crystal that might have been lamplight, might have been nothing. Then the glints took shape. Small scenes, frozen mid-motion, suspended in the translucent stone like insects in amber: a woman kneading bread in a kitchen that smelled impossibly of cardamom; two children running through summer rain; an old man laughing so hard he had to grip a table. Memories, she realized. Spent memories. The walls of the descent were mortared with them—every payment ever made at this gate, every year and name and lullaby surrendered by every traveler who had come before, pressed into the stone like flowers in a book.

And the mountain tasted them as she passed.

There was no other way to describe it. A pressure, faint but insistent, moving over her the way an appraiser's fingers move over goods—probing, weighing, pricing. It lingered on the back of her skull. It slid along the architecture of her thoughts with the impersonal thoroughness of a customs search, and Felix, who had spent nine years making herself unreadable, discovered that unreadable was not the same as unopenable. The mountain had keys. The mountain had always had keys.

*What do you carry,* it seemed to say, in a voice that was not a voice but a settling, like snow shifting on a roof. *Set it down. Set it down. Everything weighs something here.*

"My name is Felix Fairweather," she said out loud, because saying it felt like gripping a rail in a storm. "I keep ledgers for the Guild of Improbable Trade. I am here to correct an entry."

The pressure paused. Somewhere far below, Felix had the distinct impression of a very large book being opened, and a very dry attention turning toward her like a page.

Vess, behind her, had gone quiet in a new way. When Felix glanced back, the appraiser's face was pale in the crystal-light, and her hand rested flat against the wall—not bracing herself, Felix realized, but touching. Reading. Her lips moved slightly, following some record only she could see.

"Vess."

"They're beautiful," Vess said quietly. "The memories. Do you know what the Guild does with the ones we collect? We bottle them. We shelve them. We sell them to people who want to remember being happy." Her thumb traced one of the embedded scenes, the laughing old man. "But this—" She shook her head. "This is what they look like before we get our hands on them. Still attached to the people who paid them. Still *warm*."

"You sound like you're reconsidering your employment."

"I sound like an auditor who has found a discrepancy between the books and the vault." Vess withdrew her hand and straightened her coat, and the moment of naked wonder folded itself away into procedure. "Come. The gate wardens will be expecting Marrow's token, and I'd rather not explain why we loitered."

* * *

The wardens were waiting at the third switchback, where the passage widened into a vestibule of white stone.

There were three of them, and they were not entirely people. They had the shapes of people—robed, upright, roughly Felix's height—but their surfaces were salt-crystal, faceted, and where faces should have been there were smooth planes that caught the light and gave back nothing. They did not walk so much as arrive, each repositioning between blinks, and when the tallest of them extended a hand, palm up, the gesture carried the weary authority of every toll-taker since the invention of roads.

Felix took out Marrow's token.

The warden's crystal fingers closed over the black iron disc, and for a moment nothing happened. Then the facets of its face shifted—rippled, like the surface of disturbed water—and a voice came out of it that was layered, many-voiced, the way a choir sounds heard through a wall.

*"Debt of Marrow-of-the-Guild, acknowledged. One passage, one bearer."* The blank face tilted toward Vess. *"You bring a second."*

"She observes," Felix said. "She doesn't bargain."

*"All who enter set down what they carry."*

"I heard the sign."

*"The sign is a courtesy. The mountain is not courteous."* The warden returned the token, and its touch was cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature—cold like the space between entries in a ledger, like the pause before a number that will change your life. *"Bearer. The court knows your scent. It has known it a long time. There is an account opened in your name older than your name."*

Felix's mouth went dry. "How old?"

The warden's facets rippled again, and Felix would have sworn, later, that the many-voiced sound it made was amusement.

*"Older than your memory of opening it. That is how accounts work here."* It stepped aside. Behind it, the passage plunged onward and down, and the glass-note of the mountain swelled until Felix felt it in her teeth. *"Descend, bearer. Descend, observer. The Regent keeps evening hours, and the Regent dislikes waiting more than it dislikes theft—which is to say, considerably."*

They went down.

The stairs were worn concave by feet older than kingdoms. The walls thickened with memory-scenes until Felix could hardly bear to look—whole lives pressed into the stone, tenderness and terror side by side, priced and paid and shelved forever. And threaded through all of it, growing stronger with every step, came a sound that stopped her heart and restarted it sideways.

A voice, far below, reading numbers aloud.

Slowly. Rhythmically. The cadence of a clerk at closing time, counting inventory in the dark.

The child's voice, from her fragments by the windowsill. *A woman's voice, reading numbers.* Felix had assumed it was a memory of the Guild.

It was not the Guild. It was the court. It had always been the court, counting its holdings, and one of its holdings had escaped upward into the daylight carrying a language in her mouth and Felix's name in her bones.

Felix tightened her grip on the banister of ancient salt and kept walking, one step, then another, resolve hardening in her chest like cooling metal—no longer the brittle kind that shatters, she thought, but the kind that holds an edge.

Below, the numbers went on counting. And now, faintly, she could make out what they were counting toward.

Her own name.
