## 12. The Court Beneath

The gate closed behind Felix with a sound like a book shutting.

She stood in a corridor that should not have existed inside a mountain—high-vaulted, pale as bone, lit from no source she could find. The walls were not stone. She touched one and understood: salt, but not the salt of kitchens or roads. This was salt grown slow and deliberate, laid down in strata like the pages of an enormous ledger, each layer a record pressed flat by weight of years. When she dragged her fingernail across it, it left no mark. Nothing here could be altered. Everything here was kept.

Her boots rang on the floor, and the corridor answered her steps back in fragments of her own footfalls, delayed just enough to feel like mockery. Somewhere behind her, faint through the mountain's flesh, Vess's footsteps followed at a courteous distance—the Guild appraiser keeping to the letter of her shadowing order, close enough to witness, far enough to disclaim.

Felix walked on. The air tasted of brine and old promises, exactly as Odile had warned, and beneath the brine there was something sweeter, cloying, like honey left too long in a copper pot. Memory-scent. She had read about it once in a banned book with her fingers greasy from stolen bread, before she knew what such knowledge would cost her.

The corridor opened without warning into the court.

* * *

It was a cavern vast enough to hold a cathedral three times over, and it was full of people who were not entirely people.

They sat—or stood, or drifted—in ranks along terraces of white stone, and every one of them glittered faintly, the way sugar glitters when light catches its crystals. Their skin had the translucent quality of sea-glass. Some of them were old beyond reckoning, their faces worn smooth like river stones; some wore shapes so recent they still seemed to be deciding what they looked like. And all of them, Felix realized with a lurch, were made of memory.

Not metaphorically. Literally. What she had taken for flesh was crystallized recollection, grown dense and permanent, souls annealed over centuries until they could no longer be separated from what they remembered. They *were* what they recalled. To forget, for them, would be to die—and so they had arranged never to forget anything at all.

A herald stood at the base of the lowest terrace. It had been human once, perhaps; now it was mostly voice, a shimmer of salt-crystal shaped around sound itself. When it spoke, it spoke first in the old tongue—that rolling, root-deep language Felix had heard falling from the mute child's mouth—and then, obligingly, in Common.

"The Guild's clerk arrives. Felix Fairweather, keeper of ledgers, second chair of improbable inventory." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Also known by other names. We keep those too."

Felix kept her hands folded, the way she did when the auditors came. "I've come to bargain."

"Everyone who comes here comes to bargain," the herald said. "Few come to pay."

* * *

They gave her a chair.

That was the first cruelty of it, though she didn't recognize it as cruelty yet. The chair was carved from a single block of pink salt and placed at the center of the court floor, so that every noble in the tiers could look down upon her while pretending to look elsewhere. She sat because refusing would have been a statement, and statements were currency she could not afford.

The Regent had not yet appeared. In the Regent's absence, the court amused itself with her.

An elderly noble drifted down from the third terrace—a woman whose crystalline face had settled into an expression of perpetual mild interest—and circled Felix twice, the way one circles merchandise.

"You are the one," the woman said, in accented but fluent Common. "The one from the winter transaction. Sixty-one years ago." Her eyes, if they were eyes, were chips of clear crystal, and behind them Felix saw—not imagined, *saw*, the way one sees a fly trapped in amber—a small scene moving: a girl of sixteen, hollow-cheeked, snow in her hair, standing before a merchant's stall that smelled of the sea.

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"Of course not." The noble smiled with genuine warmth. "That is rather the point of you."

Felix's hands tightened on the arms of the salt chair. "I want to see the Regent."

"In time. First, introductions." The woman gestured, and other nobles descended, drifting down like snow deciding to be people. "I am Halvane. I remember being a customs officer in a kingdom that no longer has a name. These are my colleagues. We have all sold something, or bought something, or both, and we remained. That is the whole of our aristocracy: we stayed."

"We stayed," echoed the court, softly, in the old tongue, and the word rolled through the cavern like a bell rung underwater.

Halvane leaned closer. Up close, Felix could hear a faint ticking within her, like a clock made of ice. "You want the child's soul back. Everyone below knows why you've come; the mountain heard your name the moment you signed Marrow's token. But before we discuss terms, I am instructed to show you hospitality." Another smile. "And our hospitality is honesty."

"I'd prefer tea."

"We don't have tea. We have truth, served cold." Halvane raised one glass hand, and the court quieted. "Let us review the account of Felix Fairweather, for her own edification."

* * *

What followed was the worst hour of Felix's life, and she had once spent a week cataloguing bottled screams during an audit.

The nobles did not accuse her of anything. They simply *remembered*, aloud, in turn, each contributing a fragment like witnesses passing a candle down a line. And their memories were perfect—perfect in a way no ledger could ever be, because ledgers can be forged and memories here could not.

They remembered her arrival at the Guild at seventeen, half-starved, presenting herself to the intake clerk with a forged reference she had written herself in a passable imitation of a hedge-witch's hand. They remembered her first five years, when she slept in the record room because a bed cost more than food, teaching herself double-entry accounting from discarded drafts, becoming indispensable by becoming invisible.

They remembered—this from a noble who had once been a Guild trader himself, sold to the mountain for embezzlement in 1143—the night she had found her own intake receipt and read the line where her name should have been and seen, instead, a blank space, and understood that the Guild had never recorded her surname because she had never given them a true one.

"That is not a crime," the ex-trader noble observed mildly. "We merely note it. You have been erasing yourself for forty years, little clerk. It is the most consistent work you have ever done."

"I want to see the Regent," Felix said again, and her voice cracked on *Regent*, and Halvane pretended not to notice, which was its own kind of mercy.

But the worst was still coming.

A young noble—young meaning perhaps two centuries—drifted last of all, and this one carried a smell Felix recognized from her nightmares: the sea-stink of a certain merchant's stall. It settled before her chair and said, in a voice like sand through an hourglass:

"Winter of 1081. A girl trades one year of her life for bread and a roof. The merchant logs the purchase. The year is brought below, weighed—" it held up its hands, cupped, as if holding something small and trembling "—and found to be very heavy. Heavy years are rare. Most people spend their years badly. Hers were dense with wanting. The Regent drank it personally."

Felix could not breathe. Somewhere above her, in the ordinary world, she was forty-four years old, or fifty-seven, or sixty-one—she had never known which, because the arithmetic required a starting point she had sold—but here in this court, the number was known. Here, everyone knew exactly how much time she contained, to the day.

"You are younger than you believe," the young noble said gently. "Or older. The distinction stopped matterating for you decades ago. We only mention it because you will need to know your own weight when we come to terms."

"Terms," Felix managed. "You keep saying that word as though I'm the merchandise."

Halvane laughed—a bright, crystalline sound, entirely without malice, which made it worse. "Oh, little clerk. Everyone who enters this court is merchandise. So is everyone who leaves. The only question the mountain ever asks is: what are you *for*?"

* * *

Then the Regent came.

Felix never learned whether the Regent arrived or had simply always been there and become visible, the way a pattern emerges from wallpaper. One moment the dais at the far end of the cavern was empty; the next it was occupied by a figure of pure white salt, tall as three men, its surface crawling slowly with new crystal growth, like frost forming in fast motion. It had no face. Where a face should have been there was depth—a suggestion of features seen through miles of clear ice, features that might have belonged to anyone, or to everyone, or possibly to Felix herself on a bad day.

When it spoke, it did not use the herald. It spoke directly into the bones of the room, and the room translated.

"Felix Fairweather." Its voice was patient the way glaciers are patient. "Keeper of ledgers. Forger of one signature. Seller of one year. Eraser of one self. Mother—" it paused, tasting the word "—adjacent. You have come to buy back a soul the Guild sold us under seal, using your mark, in the year of your apprenticeship to the hedge-witch Odile."

"I don't remember signing that."

"No," the Regent agreed. "You wouldn't."

The words landed like stones dropped down a well. Felix waited for the splash and none came. "What does that mean? *I wouldn't?*"

"It means the mountain does not explain its inventory. It means that memory is tender, and tender is tender, and yours has passed through many hands." The great white head tilted. "But we are not here for recrimination. Recrimination is for courts that forget. We remember everything; therefore we forgive nothing and blame no one. It is very restful."

"Then let's get to it. The child's soul. Name your price."

The Regent was silent for a long moment. Around the cavern, the nobles went absolutely still, ticking faintly, watching.

"You misunderstand the nature of the offer," the Regent said at last, and its voice changed—softened, warmed, became almost kind, and that was the most frightening thing yet, because Felix recognized kindness as a sales technique because she had used it herself, on clients, for forty years. "We do not wish to sell you the child's soul. We wish to give you a place here. Permanently."

The word dropped into the silence like a coin into a deep well.

"A seat among us. Rank, even—you have the temperament. Precision. Patience. A horror of disorder that we find charming." The Regent's crystal surface crawled with new growth, and Felix realized, with distant horror, that the patterns spreading across it were *ledgers*. Columns of figures, growing forever. "You would keep books for eternity. No hunger. No aging. No fear of auditors. No fear of anything, because fear requires the possibility of loss, and nothing here is ever lost."

"And the child?"

"Would remain. As she remains regardless. Her soul is ours by lawful purchase. Yours is—" the Regent paused delicately "—partially ours already, and the portion that is not is tired. We have watched it. It wants rest. It wants to stop carrying the storm." A pause. "*Fairweather,*" it said, in the old tongue, and the word resonated through the cavern, and Felix flinched because it sounded, in that language, like an accusation. "One who carries storms lightly. You carry nothing lightly. You carry nothing at all, anymore. You have spent forty years becoming a hole in the shape of a person. We offer you the peace of finishing the job."

* * *

Here is the thing about temptation: it never looks like a trap. It looks like relief.

Felix sat in her pink salt chair and felt the offer settle over her like snowfall, soft and totalizing. No more running. No more gaps in her own history, gaping like pulled teeth. No more lying awake wondering what she had done at sixteen, what she had signed, who she had betrayed—because here, she would *know*. She would know everything, permanently, and knowing everything, she would finally be allowed to stop.

And the ache—

The ache was this: they knew her. All of her. Every fragment. The nobles in their tiers had recited her life back to her with perfect accuracy, and somewhere in that recitation, underneath the humiliation, had been a feeling she hadn't experienced since she was a child: the feeling of being *seen*. Completely. Without the option of escape.

Forty years of careful invisibility, and the first place that ever truly looked at her was the place offering to swallow her whole.

She thought of the child, hidden at Odile's cottage—mute, drawing faces from memory, remembering a woman's voice reading numbers. Numbers read aloud. Felix's voice. Had it been? Was that the missing year, the missing years, the transactions she'd performed below in a language she'd studied and then paid away?

She thought of Vess, waiting somewhere in the corridors above with procedural mercy folded in her pocket like a knife.

She thought of the smell of ink. Of the ledger room at closing time, lamps low, columns true. Of the child's small hand gripping her sleeve on the road.

"I have a counter-offer," Felix said.

The Regent waited.

"You keep the seat. I'll take the soul." She stood up from the pink chair, legs unsteady, voice steadier than she had any right to expect. "And I'll pay in the only coin I have left that you haven't already tasted. My remaining purchased years—the ones I bought back, the ones that make me whatever age I actually am. All of them. Every year I clawed back from your merchant network, returned to the mountain, in exchange for the child, whole, free, and cleared of debt."

Something moved in the depths of the Regent's face—far down, behind the miles of ice. Not quite surprise. Interest, perhaps. Or recognition.

"Bold," it said. "Reckless. Those years are the sum of your survival."

"They're the sum of someone else's theft," Felix said. "I'm just returning stolen property. I'm a clerk. Returning stolen property is the only thing I've ever been good at."

The silence stretched. The nobles ticked. High above, water dripped somewhere with the patience of geology.

"There is a complication," the Regent said finally, and its voice had gone cold again, formal, contractual. "Your years are not yours to offer. Not entirely. They were purchased through intermediaries, resold, split, collateralized. Before any exchange can be ratified, ownership must be verified against the original instrument." The great white head inclined toward her, and for one vertiginous moment Felix felt utterly, completely *read*. "Fortunately, we keep excellent records. Bring me the memory of why you ran from Odile—the reason you have never been able to retrieve—and I will trace the chain of title myself. Pay me that, and I will show you exactly who owns your years, and what your signature was for."

The court held its breath.

"The memory of why you ran," the Regent repeated, softer now, almost gentle. "Every debtor knows the memory they cannot find. You have been circling it for forty years, little clerk. It is the hole in the shape of your life. Pay it to me—and learn what you paid it to forget."

Felix stood in the white heart of the mountain, in a court of perfect memory, and understood at last that she had never come here to buy a child's soul.

She had come to buy back herself.

And the price was the one thing she had spent her entire adult life making sure she would never, ever have to look at.

"Well?" said the Regent, patient as salt.

Felix opened her mouth.
