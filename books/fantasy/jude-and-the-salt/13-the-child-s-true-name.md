## 13. The Child's True Name

Odile Vetch had kept bees for sixty years, and she had learned from them the only lesson that mattered: everything that hums is listening.

So when the child began to speak on the third morning—properly speaking, not the broken fragments and pointing of the days before—Odile did not startle, did not drop her cup, did not do any of the things a woman less careful might have done. She simply set down her honey knife, turned her back as though tending the fire, and listened with every nerve in her old body.

The words came out of the girl like water finding a crack in stone. Not halting. Not learned. *Remembered.* Each syllable fell into place the way a keystone falls—the whole arch of the sentence standing up at once around it—and Odile felt the arch stand up inside her own chest too, because she had heard this language before, once, in a book so dangerous she had burned its cover and buried its pages beneath the gooseberry bed, where even now the berries grew sweeter than they had any right to.

The tongue of the mountain court. The salt-speech. The language that predated kingdoms the way roots predate trees.

The child was sitting cross-legged on the kitchen floor among the drying herbs, talking to the rosemary.

Not *to* it, precisely. Odile corrected herself, watching. Talking *for* it. Naming it. Her small hands moved in slow, formal gestures, and each time her fingers closed around a shape in the air, the rosemary's scent deepened, thickened, became more entirely itself, until the whole corner of the room smelled like a hillside in high summer rather than a bundle cut three days ago and hung to dry.

"Ah," Odile said softly, to the fire, in Common Tongue. "There you are."

The child looked up. Her eyes were pale—not blind-pale, but pale the way salt flats are pale, all the color pressed out by weight. She had not spoken a word since arriving at Felix Fairweather's heels, mute as a stone, and Odile had catalogued the muteness alongside the other facts: no shadow that quite matched her movements, a faint brine smell that no washing removed, and a hunger for plain bread and nothing sweet, which in a child was the strangest fact of all.

Now the girl said something else—a longer phrase, addressed to Odile directly, her voice still rusty, still careful, like someone walking on a floor they remembered being solid.

"I don't speak it," Odile answered honestly, in Common. "Not anymore. I read enough of it to know I should be afraid."

The child tilted her head. Then, deliberately, slowly, she reached out and touched the table where Odile kept her account books—the ones for the cottage, hens and honey and the occasional discreet curse—and drew one finger across the wood as if writing there.

"No," Odile said, and then, because lying to children was a habit she'd never managed to acquire, "not *no*. But not here."

* * *

She took the girl out to the garden instead, because some conversations wanted open sky above them, and because Odile had buried certain pages under the gooseberries and preferred to know exactly what lay between herself and them while she thought.

The morning was thin and bright. Frost still clung to the shade side of the fence posts. Odile walked the rows with the girl trailing her like a small grey shadow—which was nearly literal; Odile had noticed on the second day that in low light the child cast two shadows, one hers and one slightly behind, slightly wrong, like an echo that hadn't yet caught up. She had decided not to think about that yet. There was a discipline to not thinking about things, and Odile had practiced it for decades.

"You were sold," Odile said, without preamble, crouching to check the winter garlic. "Sold twice, if my reading of Felix's face was right. Once by whoever put you in the Guild's inventory, and once by the Guild itself, down the mountain. Souls are their favorite stock—they hold value forever and never complain about storage." She brushed soil from a clove. "I'd like to know who sold you first."

The child knelt beside her in the dirt, uninvited, and began to help with the garlic, separating cloves with small precise fingers, and as she worked she spoke—low, continuous, a river of that old language—and whether she understood that Odile couldn't follow or simply needed to say it, Odile couldn't tell. But she listened anyway, the way you listen to rain, letting the shapes of the sounds settle into her, and gradually, gradually, patterns surfaced. Words she'd seen transliterated in that burned-cover book. Cadences. A grammar that bent time strangely, verbs that seemed to reach backward.

And then the girl stopped. Took Odile's hand. Turned it palm-up, and traced something into the skin with one fingernail: a character. Not a letter of any script Odile knew—but she knew *of* it. A sigil from the forbidden pages, one that meant, if her memory served and her memory for such things always served—

*Archive.*

Or perhaps *record.* Or perhaps, in the older strata of the tongue, *the thing that remembers so the rest may forget.*

The girl traced it again, slower. Then pointed at herself.

Odile sat back on her heels in the frost-wet dirt and let out a breath she felt she'd been holding for thirty years.

"Well," she said. "That explains the rosemary."

* * *

She spent the middle of the day doing what she always did with unbearable knowledge: she made lunch.

Soup, bread, pickled things. The child ate with the grave thoroughness of someone who had known real hunger, and Odile watched her over her own bowl and assembled the truth piece by piece, the way she assembled anything—patiently, with good tools.

A living archive. The court beneath the mountain spoke the old tongue, but speech rots; even courts rot. So they had kept a speaker. A flesh-and-blood vault, raised below, steeped in the language, carrying every word of it the way a bee carries pollen on her legs without knowing what she carries or why the flowers need it. And somewhere—recently, within the last year, judging by the girl's size and the fresh-cut look of the fear on her—that vault had been taken out of the mountain and sold upward, to the Guild of Improbable Trade, as inventory.

Which was impossible.

Odile turned her cup slowly on the table. It was impossible, because the mountain court did not sell. The mountain court only bought. That was the entire architecture of the place, the whole terrible genius of it: memories, years, names, souls—all flowing downhill, one direction, like water. Nothing came back up. If a piece of the court had come *up*—if its own living archive had been extracted and passed to surface traders—then either the court had been robbed from within, or the court had *chosen* to let her go, and both possibilities were worse than the other.

And there was a third thing. The worst thing. The thing Odile circled without landing on, the way you circle a snake hole.

Someone had brokered the sale. Someone with access to the Guild's ledgers, someone who could move a soul through inventory without it appearing as what it was. Felix had found the receipt; Felix had found her own apprentice mark on it, forged or otherwise. Forged, Odile would have bet the cottage. But forgery required a forger, and a forger required a reason, and a reason that large required a conspiracy with roots deep enough to drink from the same water as the mountain.

The Guild was not merely trafficking in stolen goods, Odile realized, looking at the child buttering her bread. The Guild was being used as a laundering house. And someone inside it—high inside, ledger-deep inside—had sold the last speaker of the oldest language in the world to the very institution best equipped to lose her in the stacks forever.

Why? Odile chewed on it. Why steal an archive and hide it?

Because archives can be *read*. Because whoever held the girl could eventually make her speak, and whatever she carried in her memory—the court's contracts, its debts, its original bargains with the surface kingdoms, the terms under which every soul below had been purchased—would become leverage. Or evidence. Or a weapon.

Or, whispered a colder voice in Odile's head: because the court wanted its archive *out* before something happened to it. Before an audit. Before a reckoning.

Odile set down her cup. Outside, a bee droned against the window glass, persistent, half-asleep with cold, wanting in.

*Everything that hums is listening,* she reminded herself, and said nothing aloud at all.

* * *

In the afternoon, she tested the girl. Odile was not proud of testing children, but pride had never been one of her operating expenses.

She laid out objects on the kitchen table: a copper nail, a sprig of yew, a chicken bone, a river stone with a hole through it, a lock of her own white hair tied with red thread. Old objects, charged objects, each with a history she knew down to the year. Then she pointed at each in turn and waited.

The girl named them. In the old tongue first, and the naming did something—Odile felt each word land on the object like a stamp, felt the copper nail become *more* copper, the yew more darkly itself. Then, watching Odile's face, the girl tried again in Common Tongue, haltingly, tasting each surface word: "Nail. Tree-that-kills. Bone. Stone-with-door." At the lock of hair she paused a long moment, and then said, carefully, "Grandmother-smoke," and Odile had to leave the room for a moment on the pretext of checking the kettle, because her mother had been dead forty years and no one living knew she'd smoked a clay pipe by the sea.

When she came back, the girl was waiting with the patience of the deeply old wearing the body of the very young.

"How much do you remember?" Odile asked. "Of below."

The girl held up her hands, palms out, fingers spread wide. Twice.

"Twenty—" Odile began, and the girl shook her head, frustrated, and then did something Odile never forgot afterward: she took the copper nail and scratched lines into the tabletop, quick and sure, a tally in the old numerals, which ran past counting into a symbol Odile recognized from the burned book's margins, a spiral closed upon itself.

*All of it.*

Every contract. Every purchase. Every name the court had ever taken, every year it had ever swallowed, every bargain struck with every desperate soul who'd climbed down the salt gate to trade. All of it, carried in a child's skull, walking around in the daylight in a hedge-witch's kitchen, eating bread.

"And they let you go," Odile murmured. "Or they lost you. Or—" She stopped. Looked at the girl properly. "Or someone stole you *for* them, and the theft is the message."

The girl's pale eyes held hers. She didn't nod. But she didn't look away either, and in that country—between assent and silence—lived the whole shape of it.

* * *

Felix and the appraiser would be back soon. Odile could feel it in her knees, which predicted weather and trouble with equal accuracy. Felix would come up from the mountain carrying whatever price she'd agreed to pay, and Vess would come carrying whatever the Guild had ordered her to carry, and both of them would walk into this kitchen believing they understood the shape of the game.

They did not understand. Odile barely understood, and she'd spent her life understanding things other people were wiser not to touch.

Because here was the arithmetic that kept turning over in her mind, refusing to settle: if the girl was the court's living archive, then the receipt Felix found—the sale record bearing Felix's forged mark—wasn't just a crime's paperwork. It was a *thread*, left deliberately trailing. You didn't forge a signature onto a soul-sale by accident. Someone had wanted Felix Fairweather, specifically, to find that page. Someone had wanted her to run down the mountain. Someone had wanted a disgraced apprentice, a procedural appraiser, and a hedge-witch with buried books all pulled toward the salt gate at the same season, like beads drawn along a string.

Which meant the conspiracy wasn't just *inside* the Guild.

It had planned for the people outside it, too. It had planned, possibly, for this kitchen. For this soup. For the exact old woman who alone in the county could read enough of the old tongue to know what the child was—and who owned, buried under the gooseberries, the only surviving copy of a book the court might very much want found or destroyed.

Odile looked at the girl, who was quietly re-naming the herbs on the windowsill, one by one, making them more themselves, keeping them safe the way you keep coals alive under ash.

"They didn't sell you away from the mountain, little one," Odile said softly, in Common. "They sent you ahead of something. You're not the goods." She wiped her hands on her apron. "You're the *receipt*. And somebody means to come collect."

The child went still. Then, for the first time since arriving, she smiled—a small, grave, ancient smile, far too old for her face—and spoke a single line in the salt-speech, and though Odile couldn't translate it, she understood it perfectly in her bones, the way you understand thunder:

*The audit has already begun.*

Down the lane, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of footsteps on frost—two sets, one heavy and hurrying, one measured as a metronome—and Odile reached over and pinched out the candle, though it was not yet dusk, because whatever walked toward her garden had already seen enough.
