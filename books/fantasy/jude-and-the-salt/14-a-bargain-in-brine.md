## 14. A Bargain in Brine

The throne room of the mountain court was not a room so much as a wound—a vast hollow carved into the salt itself, its walls veined with translucent strata that glowed faintly, like flesh held up to candlelight. Felix walked across a floor of packed brine-crystal and felt it give slightly beneath her boots, the way old ice gives, or old promises.

She had counted her steps down from the gate. Four hundred and eleven. She had counted them because counting was what she did when terror arrived wearing formal clothes. Numbers were her native tongue now; she had spent fifteen years making sure of it. But the court beneath the mountain spoke in the older language, and every syllable of it scraped against something buried in her, some sediment of memory she could not name, only feel—the way you can feel weather coming through an old scar.

The Salt Regent sat at the far end of the hollow, and Felix had expected—she didn't know what. A king of salt. A monster. Something with teeth.

What she found was a woman made of ledger-pages.

Not literally. Not quite. The Regent's body was crystallized memory given shape: layers upon translucent layers, each one a recollection pressed flat and preserved, and where they overlapped they formed features—a face like a page held too long to a flame, browned at the edges, legible everywhere. Her eyes were two entries written in a hand so old it predated ink. When she turned her head, Felix heard the whisper of turning pages.

"You are the Fairweather signature," the Regent said, and her voice was dry and patient and terribly amused. "I have been waiting for you to come collect your own handwriting."

Felix stopped three paces from the dais of grey crystal. She had rehearsed this. She had rehearsed it for six days, on the road, in Odile's kitchen, in her dreams, which had become increasingly crowded with arithmetic.

"I've come to buy back a soul," she said. "A child's. Sold to this court under entry—" she recited the number without looking at the paper in her hand, because she had not needed the paper since the second day, because the number had taken up residence behind her eyes like a lodger who paid no rent, "—and I'm prepared to offer payment."

"Payment." The Regent tasted the word. Around her, along the walls, the court assembled itself: figures rising out of the salt strata, nobles of crystallized memory, their faces smooth as sea-worn glass. They did not sit. They simply *were*, in the way that facts are. "You Guild people always say that word as though it were simple. As though value were a settled matter." One pale finger tapped the arm of her throne, and the sound rang through the hollow like a bell struck underwater. "Tell me, little accountant. What do you think a soul costs?"

"I don't deal in souls," Felix said. "I catalog them. There's a difference."

"Oh, there is indeed." The Regent smiled, and the smile moved through her layered face like light moving through water. "The cataloger is worse than the dealer. The dealer at least knows what he has done."

Something cold slid down Felix's spine. She kept her hands still. In the Guild they taught you that still hands meant a settled mind, and a settled mind meant leverage, and leverage was the only currency that never devalued. *Still hands,* she told herself. *Still hands.*

"I'm offering memories," she said. "I'm told this court accepts them as tender."

"Told by whom? The hedge-witch?" The Regent laughed, and the laugh was a sound like sand pouring through an hourglass, and every noble in the chamber leaned fractionally closer, the way creditors lean. "Yes. Odile. We remember Odile. She has never once come below. Clever woman. She knows that everything offered here is kept here." The layered eyes fixed on Felix, and Felix felt herself being read—not her face, not her words, but her *contents*, the way Vess read ledgers, the way she herself read the Guild's sealed volumes: thoroughly, professionally, without mercy. "And you came anyway. For a child whose sale bears your mark. Tell me about that mark, Fairweather. Do you remember signing it?"

"No," Felix said, before she could stop herself, before she could make it strategic. The word came out raw. "That's the point. I don't remember. I need to know—"

"Ah." The Regent raised one finger, and the whole court went silent with the suddenness of a held breath. "There it is. There is the crack in the wall. You want more than the child. You want your own missing pages back." She rose from the throne, and her movement was slow and rustling, like a book opening. "Every soul sold to this court comes with a story attached. Hers came with a particularly interesting one. Your name on the receipt. A signature in a hand we both recognize. And the seller—an intermediary, a broker of small horrors, a salt-merchant with soft eyes and soft terms—who has been selling to us for longer than your Guild has existed." She drifted closer. Up close, Felix could see that the layers of her face were not blank pages at all. They were full. Thousands of them. Millions. Every memory ever traded into this place, worn as skin. "You want to bargain with me while carrying a hole in yourself exactly the size of the answer. That is either very brave or very stupid, and in my experience the two are indistinguishable until afterward."

"The child," Felix said steadily, "did nothing. Whatever chain of transactions put her soul in your inventory, she isn't a coin. She's a person. I want her out."

"And I want," said the Regent, "to be amused. It has been eighty years since anyone came below with an interesting proposition. The last one offered me his mother's voice. I took it. He wept beautifully. It was dull."

* * *

They bargained for what Felix estimated was four hours, though time below the mountain behaved like water in a leaking ship, and she could not be sure. They sat—or rather, the Regent reclined among her strata and Felix stood, because sitting felt too much like settling—and they went through the inventory of Felix's life like two merchants haggling over a warehouse.

"Your sixth summer," the Regent offered. "The day your father left. It is a common trade. Children sell it early because it hurts, and they regret it forever."

"Not for sale."

"Your wedding, then. There was a wedding. A small one. Rain on the canvas."

Felix blinked. "I've never—"

"You have. Or you had." The Regent's amusement deepened. "You would be astonished, Fairweather, at how much of you is already in my vaults. Bought in fragments over years, through intermediaries, at moments when you were hungry or frightened or tired enough to sign without reading. You think of yourself as a careful woman. You are, in fact, a woman who has been carefully *spent*."

The words landed somewhere below Felix's ribs and stayed there, cold and expanding. She thought of Odile's face in the garden, the terrible gentleness of it. *You have already spent more than you know.* She thought of the gaps she had learned to walk around the way one walks around furniture in a dark house—knowing their shapes, never questioning why they were empty.

"My memories of the Guild," she said. "Fifteen years of ledgers. Names, dates, prices, routes. Enough to burn the whole operation down if it ever reached the surface."

"Ledgers." The Regent wrinkled her page-browned nose. "You would offer me *accounting*."

"It's the most valuable thing I own."

"It is the least valuable thing you own, because it is the thing you built to replace what you'd already lost. No." The Regent waved a hand, and the nobles along the walls rippled with something that might have been laughter, if laughter could fossilize. "We do not want your numbers. Numbers are dead memory. We want—" and here her voice changed, dropping its dryness, acquiring something almost tender, which was infinitely more frightening, "—the living ones. The wet ones. The ones that still bleed."

She named things then. The smell of bread in the first house Felix could remember. The color of a particular evening sky over the fen country, the one she had thought about for years without knowing why. The feeling of being carried asleep from a cart to a bed by someone strong and smelling of herbs. Each one Felix refused, and each refusal cost her something she couldn't itemize, because with every *no* she felt the memory in question glow brighter in her chest, suddenly precious, suddenly irreplaceable—which was, she understood with cold clarity, precisely the point. The court wasn't haggling. It was teaching her the price of herself.

Finally the Regent grew bored of the game.

"You will not give me the past willingly," she said. "Very well. Then I will name the price myself, and you will find, I think, that you cannot refuse it—because it is the only payment that covers this particular debt." She glided close, close enough that Felix could see individual pages in her cheek, could read, upside down and backwards, fragments of other people's childhoods. "The child's soul is not ordinary stock. She is the last speaker of the old tongue. She is our living archive—our library, our law, our continuity. When she was taken from us, we lost something we cannot regenerate. To release her, I must receive something equally unregenerable. Something singular. Something *yours*."

"Name it," Felix whispered.

"The memory of why you ran from Odile."

The silence that followed was total. Even the salt seemed to stop growing.

"I—" Felix started, and stopped. "I don't have that memory."

"No," the Regent agreed pleasantly. "You do not. You sold it. Or someone sold it for you—it is often difficult to tell, in cases of panic, whose hand is on the pen. It sits in my deepest vault. It is the single most requested item in my collection, and I have never once granted a viewing, because it is worth far more sealed than open. Do you understand what I am telling you, little accountant?" The layered face bent toward hers, and every page of it was smiling. "You have spent fifteen years building a person out of what remained. You have made yourself precise, invisible, orderly. All of it—all of it—was mortar over that one missing brick. And now I am asking you to pull it out and finally look at the wall."

Felix's hands were not still. She looked down at them, trembling against the grey crystal of her ledger, and felt fifteen years of discipline crack like river ice in spring.

"Why that one?" Her voice came out thin. "Why not anything else? Why does everyone circle back to it—Odile, the merchant, you—"

"Because it is the keystone," the Regent said simply. "Remove it and everything you believe about yourself shifts. Perhaps you ran for cowardice. Perhaps you ran for love. Perhaps you ran because you did something that could not be forgiven, and forgetting was the only mercy available to a sixteen-year-old with no money and fewer choices. I know the truth of it. I have read it a thousand times. It is a magnificent memory, Fairweather—one of the finest pieces in my collection. Grief and guilt and a girl's desperate arithmetic, all in one." Her eyes glittered like frost on a window. "And you want to know the exquisite part? Once you pay it, you will never know what it said. You will carry the child's life in one hand and your own unanswered question in the other, for the rest of your days, however many of those remain to you."

"That's monstrous."

"Yes." The Regent settled back onto her throne of strata, folding her page-hands. "It is also the market price. I do not set the market, Fairweather. I merely am it."

* * *

They gave her a cell to consider the terms. Of course they called it a guesting-chamber, and of course it was beautiful—a hollow of white salt hung with lamps of frozen memory that burned without heat, casting shadows of things that had never been in the room. A bed of crystal. Water in a cup that tasted faintly of tears, though whether hers or someone else's, she could not say.

Felix sat on the edge of the bed and did what she always did. She opened her ledger.

The pages looked wrong down here. The ink seemed darker, the columns less certain, as though the paper itself doubted its arithmetic. She began, as she always began, with inventory:

*Assets: one favor token from Marrow, spent at the gate. One appraiser named Vess, shadowing me, loyalties unknown, orders unknown. One child hidden in a hedge-witch's garden, forty miles above, alive because I stole her file and lied to my only friend. Fifteen years of careful, meticulous, deliberate nothing.*

*Liabilities: one soul, owed. One signature, forged or otherwise, mine. One hole in my memory exactly the size of a winter.*

She wrote for a long time. Then she stopped writing and did the thing she never let herself do, the thing the ledgers were built to prevent.

She remembered around the gap.

There was Odile's garden—the rosemary, the crooked fence, the smell of woodsmoke and feverfew. There was Odile's voice saying her name—not Fairweather, the other name, the apprentice name she had not spoken aloud in fifteen years and found she could not now recall, which should have alarmed her far more than it did. There was warmth there. There was, unmistakably, warmth. And then there was the running: the road at night, the fen mist, the salt-merchant's cart with its soft-eyed driver and its soft-spoken terms, and the desperate animal certainty that whatever was behind her was worse than whatever was ahead.

But the middle was gone. The middle—the reason, the act, the moment the door shut behind her—was a clean absence, a page torn out so skillfully the binding didn't even fray. And every time she probed it, the same terror rose, not fear of pain but fear of *meaning*: the fear that when she finally saw what filled that space, every kind thing she had ever done since would reveal itself as penance, and every careful thing as hiding, and the woman she had built would turn out to be a load-bearing fiction over a crime.

Or—worse, in the small hours, with the memory-lamps burning—that it wouldn't. That the missing piece was nothing. Cowardice, plain and squalid. That she had run from kindness because kindness asked something of her, and she had spent fifteen years mistaking her shame for mystery.

A knock at the cell door—soft, procedural. Vess stood in the corridor with a lamp, her Guild-grey coat immaculate even this far underground, her face wearing the expression Felix had come to think of as *audit-neutral*: neither pity nor judgment, only the patient expectation that everything, eventually, balances.

"They're waiting on your answer," Vess said.

"I know."

"For what it's worth"—Vess glanced down the corridor, then back, and her audit-neutral cracked just slightly, just enough to show the human underneath, the one who had burned her orders once already and might again—"in your position, I'd want to know. Even if it cost me. An unresolved line in a ledger is worse than a loss. A loss you can carry. An unresolved line carries *you*."

"That's exactly what frightens me," Felix said.

Vess almost smiled. Almost. Then she stepped back into the corridor and became procedure again.

Felix looked down at her ledger one last time. Then, deliberately, she closed it, and felt the strangest sensation—as though the closing of the book were the closing of a door she had been holding shut for fifteen years, and behind it something was stirring, waking, wanting to be known.

* * *

The Regent received her standing, among her nobles, in the great hollow where the walls pulsed with the slow light of ten thousand preserved yesterdays. The whole court had assembled to watch, and Felix understood that this, too, was part of the transaction: witnesses. Value required witnesses. That was the first lesson the Guild had ever taught her, and apparently the mountain had taught it first.

"Well?" the Regent said. "The child's soul for the memory of your betrayal. That is the term. Say it back to me, in the old fashion, so the salt can hear it bound."

Felix lifted her chin. Her heart was going like a hammer in a locked room, and her mouth was dry as the floor beneath her, and somewhere above her, forty miles of stone and fen away, a mute child was sleeping under a hedge-witch's roof, breathing, alive, waiting for a soul that Felix had somehow, impossibly, signed away before she ever knew the child existed.

"I, Felix Fairweather," she said, and her voice did not shake, which surprised her, "offer in payment the memory of why I ran—from Odile, from the garden, from the life I had before. Freely given. Fully surrendered. Let it balance against the soul of the child, entry by entry, and may the salt keep the account."

The Regent's smile spread slowly across her layered face, page by page by page.

"Oh, *magnificent*," she breathed. "Do you know, in eighty years, no one has ever said the old formula correctly on the first attempt? Not one. You studied it once, Fairweather. Long ago. Before you sold the studying of it." She extended one translucent hand, and the nobles drew breath—or performed the drawing of breath—in a single rustling sigh. "Now then. Kneel, if you like, or stand, it makes no difference. This will feel like drowning, or falling, or remembering. It varies. The vaults are opening, little accountant."

The Regent's fingers touched Felix's forehead, cool as a ledger's spine.

"Let's see," the Salt Regent murmured, and her voice was suddenly everywhere, in the walls, in the salt, in the blood, "what you did."

The world went white as brine.
