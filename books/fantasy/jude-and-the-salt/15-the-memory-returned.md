## 15. The Memory Returned

The Salt Regent held the memory out to her the way a moneylender holds a coin—flat between two fingers of crystal, catching light it had no business catching, deep beneath a mountain with no sun.

"Yours," it said. "Or nearly yours. We keep things well here, Fairweather. Better than you kept yourself."

Felix's hands did not shake. She had spent eleven years training them not to, in ledger rooms where a tremor could misalign a column and misaligned columns got people appraised. But inside, where no one could see, something was already screaming—not in fear, but in the way a body screams when it knows, before the mind consents to know, that what is coming cannot be survived intact.

She thought of the child sleeping under Odile's hedge-protection, three days' climb above them, dreaming in a language older than kingdoms. She thought of the salt-white flame waiting somewhere in the vaults below this court, small as a candle-snuff, worth more than every entry Felix had ever catalogued.

*One memory,* the Regent had said. *The one you've never been able to find.*

And she had known, even then, even before she nodded, what it would cost her. Not the memory itself. The truth underneath it.

"I'm ready," she said.

"No," the Regent said, almost gently, and its voice was the sound of tide withdrawing over shingle. "You are not. No one is. That is rather the point."

It pressed the crystal against her sternum.

---

The world went white, then wet, then cold—and then Felix was sixteen again, and starving.

Not metaphorically starving. Not the genteel hunger of a girl who'd skipped supper. The real thing: the hollow ache that starts behind the knees, the taste of boiled bark still coating her tongue from breakfast, if you could call it breakfast, if you could call a strip of willow innerwood and snowmelt food. Three days since anything else. The winter had come early that year, the way winters do when they intend to kill, and Odile's cottage at the edge of the moor was running thin on everything except lessons.

Odile had been teaching her the old tongue that autumn. Just fragments—the counting words, the words for *yes* and *no*, the word for *mine*. "Languages are doors," Odile had said, tapping Felix's forehead with one knuckle. "Most people live their whole lives in one room. I want you to have keys." And Felix, sixteen and proud and so hungry she dreamed in arithmetic—counting what they didn't have, dividing what remained—had nodded and memorized and said nothing about how the hunger made the words slide through her like water through a net.

The salt-merchant came on a Tuesday.

Felix remembered that now, with terrible clarity: it was a Tuesday because market day, because the roads were busy enough that an old man with a mule and a cart of gray sacks drew no particular attention. He was not remarkable. That was the horror of him, looking back—he was nobody. A dried-apple face, a coat brushed shiny at the elbows, a smile like a crack in glaze. He set up his little stall at the crossroads with the other traders and sold, openly, a pound of coarse salt for a copper, and everyone who passed thought: *what a dreary trade*, and no one thought to wonder why his scales never needed weights.

Felix had been sent to market for meal and had come back with neither meal nor coin, because she'd stood too long watching the merchant weigh things that weren't salt. She'd seen him take a woman's shadow once, quick as snipping thread, folding it into a square of gray cloth while she laughed at some joke he'd told. She'd seen him pour a year of someone's life into a jar—it looked like honey, moved like honey, but it smelled like rain on hot stone, like summer, like being nine years old and unafraid—and hand the jarred man three coppers and a blessing.

She should have run. Any sensible creature runs from the trap it watches close.

But she was sixteen, and hungry, and the merchant had looked up from his scales directly at her—directly, precisely, the way an arrow looks at where it intends to arrive—and smiled his cracked-glaze smile, and said:

"You're the witch's girl. The clever one. I can smell ledgers on you."

"I don't—" Her voice came out wrong. Too young. It always sounded too young in memories; she'd forgotten, these eleven years, how young she'd been. "I don't have any money."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "But you have other stock. Come, sit. Sit. I don't bite. I buy."

And here was the part Felix had never been able to retrieve, the hole in herself she'd probed for a decade like a tongue seeking a broken tooth: what he offered first wasn't years. It was simpler than that. It was bread. A loaf, actual wheat, warm, sitting on his scale-pan like a bribe from God.

He named his price while she ate. He was courteous about it. He explained, in the manner of all good merchants, exactly what she'd get and exactly what she'd give: one year of her life, drawn clean, painless, barely noticeable—a year from the middle somewhere, not the beginning, not the end, a year she'd never miss because she'd never know which one it was. In exchange: bread for the winter, coin for the spring, and a start.

"A start at what?" she'd asked, mouth full, hating herself, unable to stop.

"At leaving," he said. "At being somebody new. You think I can't see it? You sit in that cottage learning dead languages and you flinch every time the wind rattles the shutters, because you know, child, you *know*, that woman you serve keeps books of her own. Dangerous ones. Books that make men like me very interested in her address." He leaned close. His breath smelled of brine and old promises. "I'm offering you a door out of a burning house."

"The house isn't burning."

"All houses are burning," the merchant said. "Some just burn slower. Now." He produced, from inside his coat, a contract written on paper the color of bone. "One year. Sign here."

And sixteen-year-old Felix—who had been raised by a hedge-witch to know better, who had been taught the signs, the wards, the warnings, who *knew*—signed.

She signed because she was hungry. She told herself then, and told herself for eleven years after, that it was only the hunger, that anyone would have, that hunger forgives everything.

That story was about to die.

---

Because the memory didn't stop there. Memories bought and stored in the mountain court kept everything, and what Felix had paid to forget was not the signing.

It was the night after.

Odile had known. Of course Odile had known—hedge-witches know things, it is their entire profession—and when Felix came home with bread and coin and a lie assembled from spare parts (*won at cards, from a trader, honest game*), Odile had looked at her for a long moment across the table, in the firelight, with those eyes that had never once needed to be appraised to know a thing's value.

"You met the salt-man," Odile said.

"No."

"Felix."

"It was just—"

"*Felix.*" Not loud. Never loud. But the old tongue has a word for *listen*, and Odile used it, and the whole room seemed to lean in. "What did you sign?"

And sixteen-year-old Felix, cornered, ashamed, terrified—terrified not of Odile but of losing the door, the exit, the beautiful imagined future where she was somebody new and unburdened and fed—did the arithmetic. The same cold arithmetic she would later perfect in the Guild's ledger rooms. What does she know. What can she prove. What happens to me.

Odile reached across the table and took Felix's hand, and her grip was strong and rough and shaking, and she said: "Whatever you signed, we'll undo it. Do you hear me? There is no contract that can't be unwritten. But you have to tell me everything, and you have to stay away from that stall, because that man is not a merchant, child, he is a *collector*, and collectors work for the court under the mountain, and what you signed may already be filed there—"

"He'll come back Thursday," Felix heard herself say. "Market day. He said—he said if I brought him information, he'd waive the interest. On the year. So it stays one year and doesn't grow into ten."

The silence that followed lasted the length of a heartbeat and a half, and in that heartbeat and a half, Felix watched understanding move across Odile's face like weather.

"What information?" Odile asked quietly. "Felix. What information have you already given him?"

And here it was. Here, at last, at the bottom of the hole she'd spent eleven years not digging: the memory she had paid to remove, the transaction within the transaction, the true price of her survival.

She had given him the location of the cottage. Not the village—the cottage. The wards' weak gate, the hour Odile slept, the shelf where the banned books sat, the fact that Odile kept her true name written in a lead box under the hearthstone. Everything a collector's network needed to file a hedge-witch, to audit her out of existence, to sell her the way you'd sell any unattended treasure.

And she hadn't done it in panic. That was the worst part, the part that made the memory worth sealing behind a wall of forgetting. She had done it *calmly*. Over two days. In careful installments, the way you feed a fire. Because the merchant had explained, reasonably, kindly, that the debt grew if she delayed, and because sixteen-year-old Felix Fairweather had looked at the woman who fed her and taught her and called her *keys-and-doors*—and had chosen the door.

Thursday morning, Odile woke to find the bread gone from the box, the coin gone from the jar, and a note in Felix's careful apprentice hand: *I'm sorry. I was never going to be enough for this life. Don't look for me.*

And Thursday noon, the collector's men came for Odile's books, and found the cottage warded empty, because Odile—who had understood everything by Wednesday night—had already burned what couldn't be carried and buried the lead box and walked away from forty years of rootedness into exile, so that when the network's auditors arrived with Felix's forged signature on a Guild intake form, there was nothing left to seize but ash and a cold hearth.

The forged signature. Felix felt the knowledge land in her present body, kneeling on salt stone, like a blade sliding home: the signature on the receipt for the child's soul, the apprentice mark she didn't remember making—it wasn't hers. It was *copied*. The merchant's network had lifted her mark off the intake forms she'd signed that winter, and had been using it ever since, a ghost-signature, a convenient deniable instrument, filing sales under the name of a runaway girl who could never afford to surface and contest them. Every crime of the Guild's deep trade, laundered through *her* hand.

She hadn't sold the child's soul. She'd merely been made the permanent co-author of everyone's.

And Odile—Odile who had lost her home, her books, her life's root-system, who had every reason to hate her—had spent eleven years knowing exactly what Felix had done, and had opened her garden gate anyway, and had said *neither forgiving nor surprised*, because forgiveness requires surprise, and Odile had never once been surprised.

The memory closed over Felix like water closing over a stone.

---

She came back to herself on the salt floor of the Regent's hall, and she was crying without having decided to, great silent tears cutting channels through the fine dust of brine on her cheeks, and the crystalline nobles of the court had gone very still, the way rooms go still around a grief too large to be polite about.

"Now you know," the Regent said. "Was it worth it? Knowing?"

Felix wiped her face with the back of her hand, a child's gesture, and hated that too, and let it go.

"You knew," she said hoarsely. "All of you. You've known for eleven years what I did. Why show me now?"

"Because," the Regent said, and its crystal facets caught her reflection and multiplied it into a dozen small broken Felixes, "you asked for the child's soul at any price. And prices must be *true*, Fairweather. We do not bargain with people who don't know what they are." It tilted its head, tide-slow. "So. Now that you know what you are—what you did, what was done with your hand—do you still wish to bargain? Or shall I show you to your seat? We have excellent chairs. The forgetting is very comfortable. You would never feel the weight of it again."

And Felix Fairweather, kneeling in the ruins of her own story, with no self-forgiveness left to stand on, began—to her own astonishment—to laugh. Low, ragged, disbelieving laughter that echoed strangely off the salt walls.

"No chair," she said. "No seat. I know exactly what I am now. I'm the person who sold the door instead of holding it shut." She rose, knees cracking, spine straightening, something settling in her that was not peace but was harder and might last longer. "Which means I'm also the only person alive who knows how the network files its crimes. Your archive, your vaults, your precious audit trail—you built it all on my stolen signature, Regent. Which means every one of those entries is *mine* to contest."

The hall went colder. Somewhere far below, in the vaults, a flame the color of salt flickered.

"And I want my lawyer," Felix said. "Send word up the mountain. Tell Vess—the Guild's appraiser, standing very patiently at my gate—that the audit has begun."

Above them, faint through miles of stone, something in the mountain's deep ledgers turned its own page.
