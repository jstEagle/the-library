## 16. Vess Chooses

Vess had been an appraiser for nineteen years, and in all that time she had learned one lesson above every other: the ledger did not care.

It did not care about the widow who wept when her late husband's laughter was weighed and stamped at three crowns the ounce. It did not care about the boy who sold his shadow to buy medicine for a sister the Guild's own ledgers said had died two winters prior—sold it cheerfully, gratefully, because the alternative was watching her die of something the ledgers also refused to record. The ledger did not care about fairness or grief or the small human catastrophes that arrived daily at the Guild of Improbable Trade asking to be converted into numbers.

The ledger cared only that the numbers balanced.

Vess had built herself around this truth the way a pearl builds around grit. She was procedural because procedure was mercy—the same rules applied to everyone, without favor, without cruelty, without the arbitrary violence of a trader's mood. When she seized goods, she did so with receipts. When she repossessed souls, she did so with documentation. Every terrible thing she did was authorized, countersigned, filed in triplicate. If you asked her—and few did, but once, a long time ago, a hedge-witch's neighbor had asked her how she slept—she would have told you: *I sleep because I am not the one who decides. I am only the hand that writes what has already been decided.*

She believed this the way some people believe in saints: completely, and without ever testing it against the light.

* * *

The orders came down from the Grand Ledger's office on the morning of the third day, sealed in black wax with the double-key sigil that meant *do not question this.*

Vess read them standing in the waystation at the foot of the mountain road, rain streaming off the eaves behind her, her travel cloak still beaded with the wet of the climb down from the ridge where she'd lost Fairweather's trail two nights before. The orders were brief, as good orders always were.

*Appraiser Vess, Second Rank. The asset known as the mute child is confirmed to be in transit toward the mountain court under escort of the fugitive Felix Fairweather. Upon the asset's presentation below—as collateral, per the Regent's custom—you are instructed to take possession of the asset in the name of the Guild and return it to surface custody for appraisal and resale. The fugitive Fairweather is to be permitted to conclude her negotiation; her cooperation in delivering the asset constitutes partial satisfaction of her outstanding debt to the Guild. Full satisfaction will be assessed upon her return. Fail not in this, for the balance must hold.*

*By order of the Grand Ledger.*

*Countersigned, sealed, filed.*

Vess stood in the rain and read it twice.

There was nothing unusual in it, technically. Collateral was an old custom of the salt court—goods held below while bargains were struck above, a courtesy between institutions. The child was Guild property; that was settled law, entered in the ledgers years before Vess had ever held a stamp. Fairweather was a thief and a fugitive. The arithmetic was clean.

And yet.

Vess folded the orders away into the oilcloth sleeve where she kept her commission, and she found that her hands were not steady, which troubled her more than the orders themselves. Her hands were never unsteady. She had once repossessed a debt of shadows from a house full of screaming children and her hands had been steady as a mason's plumb.

She told herself it was the cold.

* * *

She caught up with them at dusk on the second day of the descent—not by following their trail, which Fairweather had obscured with a competence that Vess noted professionally, but by simply knowing where they were going. There was one road into the mountain. There was one gate. All trails converged eventually; that was the comfort of systems.

She came upon the camp in the lee of the gatehouse ruins, fire low, and she saw the thing she had been refusing to see since the child first appeared at the Guild's door.

Fairweather sat with her back against a fallen lintel, asleep sitting up, exhausted past the dignity of lying down. And the child—the asset, the mute girl with eyes like deep water—sat beside her, awake, drawing in the ash at the edge of the firelight. Drawing faces. Over and over. A woman's face, rendered again and again with the patient repetition of someone practicing a prayer.

It was Fairweather's face. Every time.

The child looked up and saw Vess standing at the edge of the firelight, and she did not flinch. She did not run. She looked at the appraiser's badge, at the black seal-case at Vess's belt, at everything that should have terrified her, and then she did something worse than any of it.

She smiled. Small, tired, knowing. As if to say: *you too?*

Then she went back to her drawing.

Vess stood in the dark a long moment. Then she unrolled her bedroll on the far side of the fire, as travelers do, and said nothing, and took first watch, and did not wake anyone, and hated herself for the softness of that even as she did it.

* * *

They reached the Salt Gate at midmorning.

Fairweather saw her and went white, stepping in front of the child with a speed that surprised them both—a hedge-clerk's body moving with a protector's instinct, badly, bravely. "Vess." Her voice cracked on the name. "You don't have to—"

"Your token," Vess said. "Marrow's favor."

Fairweather fumbled it out. The old trader's bone disc, worn smooth, worth exactly one passage and no questions. Vess examined it, nodded, handed it back.

"You're coming *with* me," Fairweather said. Not a question. Her eyes were red-rimmed and furious and afraid. "You've been following us for three days. You're going to stand there and pretend you're just—what, an escort?"

"I'm an appraiser," Vess said. "I go where the goods go."

"The goods," Fairweather repeated, and her voice shook. "She has a name."

"Names are inventory category seven," Vess said. "I know. I wrote the classification revision myself, four years ago." She heard her own voice come out flat and correct and hated every syllable of it. "Move aside, Fairweather. The gate doesn't wait."

The child tugged Fairweather's sleeve and said something—not words, not quite, a sound like wind through a hollow place, like the memory of language—and pointed down through the gate, into the dark where the mountain opened its throat. Then she pointed at Vess. Then, deliberately, she drew one finger across her own throat and shook her head.

*No,* the gesture said. Or perhaps: *not yet.* Or perhaps something older and stranger that Vess's body understood before her mind did.

Fairweather stared at the child. Then at Vess. "What did she say?"

"I don't know," Vess lied, and walked through the gate first, so she wouldn't have to see either of their faces.

* * *

The mountain tasted of brine and old promises. That was the phrase everyone used, and like most phrases everyone uses, it was true in the boring way and false in the important one. What the mountain actually tasted of, to Vess, was paper. Old paper. The dry mineral smell of archives, of vellum kept too long in the wrong air, of records that outlived the people they recorded. She had spent her life among such smells. Walking down into the salt courts felt, horribly, like walking into the Guild's deepest vault—if the Guild's vault had teeth.

The court received them in the Long Gallery, that corridor of crystallized memory where the nobles hung in their cases like specimens, each one a recollection thickened into permanence until it forgot it had ever been lived rather than stored. Vess had read about them. Reading was nothing. Standing among them, feeling them *notice* her—feeling decades of other people's memories turn their attention like lighthouse beams—was another thing entirely. One of the cases held a memory of a harvest. Another held a wedding. One, near the end, held nothing Vess could identify except a vast and patient grief, and as she passed it, her chest tightened with a sorrow that was not hers and could not be set down again.

*This is what permanence costs,* she thought. *Everything. Even the ability to let go.*

The Salt Regent received Fairweather's petition with the amusement of an entity that had watched ten thousand mortals bargain badly, and named its price, and Vess stood at the gallery's edge with her hands folded and her face arranged into professional blankness and listened to a woman negotiate away pieces of her own mind to save a child who wasn't hers.

And somewhere in the middle of it—while Fairweather's voice steadied, while the Regent leaned forward with something that might have been interest—Vess made a decision that would have been unthinkable to her nine days ago.

She asked to see the original contract.

Not the copy. The original. The founding document of the child's sale, the one held in trust by the court itself, because the salt court kept originals the way the Guild kept copies—it was the difference between them, and Vess had always believed the Guild's version was the truer one for being newer.

The Regent, amused by something, permitted it. Perhaps it wanted her to see. Perhaps everything below the mountain was a trap laid in plain sight, and the trap was simply *the truth, available to anyone willing to walk down and read it.*

* * *

The archive room was cut from living salt, lit by lamps that burned without flame, and the contract lay open on a lectern of glassy crystal as if it had been waiting for her.

Vess read contracts the way surgeons read bodies: fast, systematic, missing nothing. Date of sale. Description of asset—one soul, juvenile, unnamed at time of transaction, later designated. Seller of record: an agent of the Guild, identity redacted in the manner reserved for senior officers. Buyer: the Court Beneath the Mountain, per the ancient treaties. Consideration paid: forty years of stored time, drawn against the seller's personal account.

All standard. All clean. All horrifying in the ordinary way that Guild business was horrifying, and none of it yet the thing that stopped her breath.

She checked the seal next, because checking seals was the first thing they taught you and the last thing you trusted.

The Guild's great seal was a double key crossed over an open book. It was pressed into wax mixed with iron filings—a security measure Vess herself had designed, so that any forged impression would fail the lodestone test. Every authentic document in the Guild's history carried that iron-tempered wax. Every one.

The wax on this seal was pure.

No iron. No filings. It would fail the test it was supposed to pass. Which meant one of two things: either the seal was a forgery—and a poor one, by Guild standards—or—

Or the document predated the iron-wax protocol. Vess checked the date. Checked it again. Ran the conversion twice, because the salt court reckoned years in its own cycles and mistranslation was the oldest trick in appraisal.

The contract was eleven years old. The iron-wax protocol was fourteen.

Which meant the seal on this document had been made *after* the protocol existed, *by someone with access to the true matrix*, and made *without* the iron—which no honest officer would do, because the omission served no purpose except deniability. Plausible forgery. A seal that could be disowned.

There was exactly one person in the entire Guild with unrestricted access to the true seal matrix. Exactly one person whose signature authority superseded every rank, every check, every safeguard Vess had spent her career believing in.

The Grand Ledger.

The person whose orders were currently folded in oilcloth against her heart. The person who had sent her down here to seize the child. The person whose entire institutional existence was premised on the sanctity of the record.

*The balance must hold.*

Vess stood in the lampless light of the salt archive with her hands flat on the lectern, and she felt the pearl crack down the middle.

Because here was the thing, the thing she had never let herself think in nineteen years: the ledger did not decide anything. The ledger was written by people. People chose what entered it, what was omitted, what was forged, what was burned. *I am not the one who decides* had always been true—but someone decided, and that someone had been selling children under a false seal for longer than Vess had carried a stamp, and every repossession, every seizure, every tearful widow and shadowless boy had been downstream of that choice. She had not been the hand of the system. She had been the hand of one man's pen.

The child's sale hadn't even been a real sale. The consideration—forty years of stored time—had never been debited from any account. Vess traced the entry line with her fingernail and found the tell she'd taught apprentices to find: the ink of the consideration clause sat slightly proud of the page, applied after the surrounding text had dried. Added later. The whole transaction was a fiction wearing a contract's clothes. The child had been taken, not sold. Stolen, with paperwork.

And now the Grand Ledger wanted her to steal her back. Again. Officially.

* * *

She climbed back up through the galleries to where the negotiation was concluding, and she found the child waiting for her.

Not Fairweather—the child. Sitting alone on a step of white salt, feet swinging, watching Vess come with those deep-water eyes. Around her neck, faintly glowing beneath her skin like a coal wrapped in cloth, was the shape of the thing they had all come so far to trade for: her soul, returned, waiting to be carried home.

Vess crouched in front of her. Protocol said she should announce herself, present her commission, state her purpose. The words assembled themselves in her mouth and would not come.

"You knew," Vess said quietly. "At the gate. You knew why I was here."

The child studied her. Then she raised one hand and touched Vess's chest, over the oilcloth sleeve where the orders lay, and her small face did something old and sorry.

Then she spoke—not the wind-sound, not quite, but words, the old tongue, and though Vess could not translate them, she understood them anyway, the way one understands a bell: *You are not the hand. You never were. The hand can close.*

From the far end of the gallery came Fairweather's voice, rising—something in the negotiation turning, the Regent's tone shifting from amusement to appetite—and Vess understood with cold clarity what was happening. The court was going to demand collateral. Fairweather was going to offer the child, believing it temporary, believing in the custom, believing that a bargain struck in good faith would be honored. And the moment the child was formally surrendered, Vess's orders activated. Take possession. Return to surface. Appraise and resell. The Regent got its entertainment; the Grand Ledger got its property back; Fairweather got nothing, and would learn it too late, and the machinery would grind on exactly as it always had, with Vess as its smooth and blameless gear.

Unless.

Vess straightened. She took the oilcloth sleeve from her belt. She unfolded the orders one last time and read the signature at the bottom—that confident, institutional hand, the hand that had signed away widows' laughter and orphans' shadows and now, at the end, its own mask slipping, its own seal exposed as the forgery it had always been.

There was a brazier near the gallery entrance, burning salt-fire for the comfort of visitors. It gave no heat, only light.

Vess fed the orders into it page by page, watching the black wax bubble, watching the double keys curl and blacken, watching nineteen years of *I only write what has been decided* go up in light that cast no warmth and left no ash. The salt-fire consumed paper utterly. That was its purpose. Some things deserved to leave no record.

The child was watching her, wide-eyed.

"Don't tell Fairweather yet," Vess said. Her voice was steady. Her hands, for the first time in days, were steady too. "Let her finish her bargain thinking the rules still apply. But when they bring you below—" she crouched again, so their eyes were level, and heard herself say words that would end her career, her rank, her entire architecture of self, "—when they bring you below, you run to *me*. Do you understand? Whatever happens in that court, whatever the Regent promises, whatever your own people ask of you—you find Appraiser Vess, and I will get you out of this mountain if I have to carry you in my own arms and spend my own name doing it."

The child smiled—the same small, tired, knowing smile from the fireside—and pressed her palm flat against Vess's cheek, and the touch was warm, warmer than any fire in this cold place, warm as a verdict.

Down the gallery, the Regent's voice rose like tide, and Fairweather's answer came thin and brave and doomed:

*"Done. The child stays below as surety, until my payment clears—"*

Vess rolled her shoulders once, like a soldier taking up a weight, and turned toward the sound of the bargain being struck.

The heist, she understood, had already begun.
