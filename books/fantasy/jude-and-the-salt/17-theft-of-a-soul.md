## 17. Theft of a Soul

The Court Beneath deliberated in a way that made the Guild's ledger room look like a market stall.

Felix stood at the base of the Salt Regent's dais while the nobles of crystallized memory argued over her. They were beautiful the way frost on a window is beautiful—each of them a facet of some remembered thing grown vast and rigid, wearing their recollections as bodies. One noble was a wedding that had lasted four hundred years. Another was a betrayal so old it had become architecture. They spoke in the old tongue, and though Felix could not follow most of it, she caught the shape of the debate: whether to admit her among them as a keeper, an archivist, a woman who understood that memory was currency and identity was merely a well-kept account.

*Seat her,* one said, its voice like sugar dissolving in water. *She has already sold half of herself. She is nearly one of us.*

The Salt Regent sat above them all, tall and patient as a stalagmite, its face a smooth plane of white salt where features surfaced only when it chose to feel something. Right now it was watching Felix with what she had learned to read as amusement.

"They are deciding," it said in trade-tongue, for her benefit alone. "Take your time. Consider your answer honestly."

Felix bowed her head the correct amount. "I am honored by the offer."

"You are terrified," the Regent corrected pleasantly. "That is wiser."

Behind her left shoulder, three steps down and to the side, Vess waited with the stillness of a held breath. The appraiser had come below under Guild writ, shadowing Felix as condition of passage, and had spent the descent saying nothing at all—which, from Vess, was a kind of eloquence. Now Vess's eyes moved across the court the way they must have moved across every inventory ever audited: cataloguing exits, counting guards, weighing value. Felix did not look directly at her. Looking would be a confession, and confession down here became contract.

Somewhere beneath this chamber—she had seen it on her way in, a corridor of vaulted dark lined with shelves that went back further than light—the memory vaults held the child's soul. A small salt-white flame, the Regent had called it when Felix first asked. Kept in a reliquary of pressed glass. Catalogued under the number Felix had found in the sealed ledgers: *one soul, juvenile, unspoken tongue, sold fair.*

Sold fair. She turned the phrase over like a coin she suspected was clipped.

"May I ask," Felix said carefully, "what my duties would be, if I accepted?"

"To remember," said the noble shaped like a wedding. "To keep. To hold what others let go. Nothing decays here, little trader. Nothing is lost, nothing forgotten, nothing forgiven—" it paused delicately, "—or needed to be."

Nothing forgiven. There it was, threaded through the offer like wire through wax. They knew. Of course they knew; they remembered everything, including her first sale, including the signature on the child's receipt, including whatever it was she had paid the Regent not two days past—the memory of why she ran from Odile, spent like the last coin in her purse, and now a blank place in her chest where something enormous used to live. She could feel the absence the way you feel a pulled tooth: with your tongue, endlessly, helplessly.

She didn't know what she had done. She only knew it had been bad enough to build a whole life on top of the hole.

"I need time to consider," Felix said.

"You have until the tide of the vaults turns," the Regent said. "One turn of our inner sea. After that, your answer is either yes, or you leave the mountain as you came—without the flame, without the child's debt discharged, and with the Guild's writ still around your neck like a collar." It gestured, and a door of salt ground itself open somewhere behind the dais. "Walk if you wish to walk. The court will deliberate. We are very good at deliberating."

Felix bowed again, lower this time, and walked.

* * *

The corridor to the guest chambers curved through the living rock, and salt bloomed from the walls in slow crystal flowers. When the last noble's attention had slid off them like oil off glass, Vess fell into step beside her.

"One turn," Vess said quietly. "How long is that?"

"I asked Odile the same about the outer gates." Felix kept her eyes forward. "Down there, maybe six hours. Up here, no telling. Time is another thing they keep."

"And the plan?"

"There isn't a plan," Felix said. "There's a sequence of increasingly stupid decisions, and we execute them quickly."

Vess almost smiled. Almost. In the torchlight of the corridor her face looked carved out of patience. "I burned my orders," she said. "You know that. Whatever happens next, I am not acting under Guild authority. I'm acting under—" she searched for the word and seemed genuinely troubled not to find it immediately, "—correctness. The seal on the original contract was forged. By the Grand Ledger himself. The child was never lawfully sold. Everything else follows."

"That's the part I can't get past," Felix admitted. "Everything else follows. You read one document wrong-by-the-numbers and now you're committing theft in a court older than kingdoms."

"Not theft." Vess's voice stayed level, but something underneath it had begun to burn, low and steady, like a lamp in a locked room. "Correction. An audit is only arithmetic until you find the error. Then it becomes a knife. I have been an instrument for thirty years, Fairweather. Tonight I am the error."

They reached the guest chamber—a hollow of salt furnished with a bed of woven brine-grass, a basin of water so clear it looked like a held breath—and Felix closed the door and finally allowed her hands to shake. Vess watched her do it without comment, which Felix appreciated more than comfort.

"The reliquary is in the third vault," Felix said, once her breath steadied. "I saw the shelving when they brought me in. Third vault, western wall, row nine—the Regent showed me the catalogue entry when it thought I was considering its offer. It wanted me to see how safe the flame was. How impossible theft would be."

"It showed you the location."

"It showed off. That's different." Felix pulled the folded paper from inside her coat—Marrow's favor token, creased soft as cloth now from handling. On its face, in Marrow's cramped hand, was written a single line: *Presented for withdrawal against account.* A favor owed. Marrow had covered for her once already, back on the surface, and the price had been this: a blank check drawn on whatever Marrow valued, redeemable anywhere the Guild's word carried weight. Even, Felix prayed, down here. The courts traded with each other; debts were the one borderless country.

"This gets us into the vaults," Felix said. "Past the wardens. Debts are holy everywhere, even here. Especially here."

"And out?"

Felix looked at her hands. "That's the stupid decision part."

* * *

They went down during the change of the warden-watch, when the vault corridors filled with the sound of the inner sea turning—a vast, slow sluicing, like the mountain breathing through its stones. The favor token worked exactly as Marrow promised: the warden at the vault gate took one look at the line of ink and inclined its head with the reverence of a priest shown a relic. Debt outranked duty. Somewhere in the deep accounting of the world, Marrow's name was worth more than the Guild's suspicion, and Felix sent a silent, complicated apology upward through the rock to a man who had never once asked her why she cared about a mute child.

Then they were inside.

The memory vaults were not rooms. They were a swallowed cathedral, shelf upon shelf rising into darkness, and every shelf crowded with vessels: bottles, boxes, lanterns, loaves of bread gone hard as stone, a birdcage with nothing in it that nevertheless sang faintly when the sea turned. Each vessel hummed at its own pitch. Walking the aisle was like walking through a crowd of people all dreaming at once. Felix kept her breathing shallow. Down here, memory leaked. Stand too long near a strong one and it would seep into you through the pores, wearing someone else's grief like a borrowed coat.

Vess counted under her breath. Row seven. Row eight.

Row nine.

And there it was: a reliquary of pressed glass, small enough to cup in both hands, sitting in a niche cut to its exact shape. Inside it, a flame burned that gave off no heat—salt-white, the size of a moth, guttering gently in a wind that belonged to no room. The child's soul. The last speaker of the old tongue. The living archive of everything the mountain court was built from, reduced to inventory, to *lot number*, to a line in a forged book.

Felix reached for it.

Her fingers stopped a hair's breadth from the glass. "If I take it," she whispered, "the moment it leaves the niche, every warden in the mountain will know. The Regent will know. This isn't stealing a purse, Vess. This is pulling a thread the whole garment is sewn with."

"Yes," Vess agreed. "So we don't pull the thread. We re-write the label."

For the first time since the road, Vess smiled fully, and it transformed her face into something almost reckless. From her coat she drew a slim volume bound in grey leather—her own working ledger, thirty years of audits, every page a weapon of pure procedure. And from the reliquary's niche she lifted—not the flame—a second vessel, identical in size and shape, that had been waiting one row over: a bottled memory, unlabeled, dim, its contents a grey murk.

"A filler," Felix breathed. "A decoy."

"An unlabeled asset recovered from an unregistered cache during routine survey," Vess said, setting it into the niche with the crisp care of a woman racking a rifle. "It will not fool them forever. It will fool them for exactly as long as we need, because no one audits a thing that looks like paperwork. I have spent my life watching people walk past crimes because the crimes were filed correctly." Her hands were perfectly steady as she broke the Guild's provisional seal and wrote, in flawless official script on the reliquary's tag: *Transferred pending appraisal — Appraiser V., writ of correction.* Then, beneath it, the words that had cost her everything and freed her completely: *Seal of origin: forged. Entry void.*

"You're signing your own name to it," Felix said.

"I burned my orders," Vess said. "I might as well sign something."

Felix lifted the true reliquary. The glass was cool, then warm, then—it seemed to her—glad. The little flame leaned toward her hands like a plant toward light, and through the pressed glass she felt it flicker against her palms, feather-light, insistent, alive. Not property. Not inventory. A person, folded small, waiting to be unfolded.

"Hello," Felix whispered, in trade-tongue, and then—carefully, clumsily, the syllables dredged up from banned books and Odile's patient lessons—in the old tongue. *Little one. We're going home.*

The flame flared bright as a struck match.

Every light in the cathedral of shelves trembled. Somewhere far above, a bell of salt began to toll—one slow stroke, then another, the alarm of the deep places—and the murmur of ten thousand sleeping memories rose all around them like a congregation coming awake.

"Run," Vess said, with feeling.

* * *

They ran.

The vault aisles blurred past, torchlight strobing across the shelves, and behind them the tolling spread and multiplied—other bells answering, the mountain finding its voice. Wardens' shapes moved in the crossings ahead, tall figures of compacted brine with lamps for eyes, and Vess led them sideways through gaps in the stacks that no map should have allowed, reading the shelving like scripture, choosing the routes where the paperwork was oldest and the oversight thinnest.

"Left," she snapped. "This aisle hasn't been surveyed in a century—I can smell the dust on the tags—"

Felix followed, clutching the reliquary to her chest, the flame beating against her sternum like a second heart. And it was doing something to her, she realized; the old tongue was leaking out of it the way memory leaked from the shelves, filling her head with images that were not hers: a mountain seen from inside, a court of singing salt, numbers read aloud in a woman's voice, and—over and over, urgent as a heartbeat—a face. Her own face. Drawn in charcoal, in ash, in spilled salt on a windowsill. *The woman with the ledger,* the images said. *The woman who reads. Find her. She signed. She can unsign.*

"I've got you," Felix gasped to the flame, to the child folded inside it, to whatever could hear. "Hold on. Hold on—"

The eastern stair opened before them, the ascent toward the salt gate, toward cold air and starlight and Odile's garden and a small body waiting for its soul the way a lamp waits for fire—

And the Salt Regent was standing at the foot of the stairs.

Not striding. Standing, as it had always been standing, the way mountains stand. Its smooth face had found features now, and the features were not angry. That was the worst part. The Regent looked at the reliquary in Felix's arms, at Vess's grey ledger still open in her hand, at the tolling bells and the waking vaults, and its expression settled into something ancient and delighted and terribly, patiently amused.

"Well," the Salt Regent said, in the old tongue, and then again in trade-tongue so there could be no mistake: "Now *that* is the most interesting entry this court has recorded in a thousand years."

The stairs were blocked. The bells were ringing. The flame in Felix's arms burned brighter, as if it had been waiting its whole small eternity for exactly this moment.

"No one leaves with a stolen flame," the Regent said, spreading its hands like a host presenting a feast, "unless a debt of equal weight is paid."

Felix's heart did not sink. It rose—terrible, clear, certain—because she had known this was coming since the gate, since the road, since the first blank place in her chest. She had been carrying the payment up the mountain the whole time, wrapped in skin.

She stepped forward.

"Then let's discuss the years I owe," Felix said, "and how many I have left to give."
