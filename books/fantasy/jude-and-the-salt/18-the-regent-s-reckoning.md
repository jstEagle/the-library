## 18. The Regent's Reckoning

The doors of the memory vault closed behind us with a sound like a held breath finally let go, and for three heartbeats Felix allowed herself to believe they might actually escape.

Then the mountain sealed.

She felt it before she understood it—a pressure in her ears, a taste of brine thickening on the tongue until swallowing became an effort. The corridor of salt ahead of them, the one that had led down from the gate, simply *ended*. Not blocked. Ended. Where stone had been there was now a wall of translucent crystal, and inside the crystal, moving slow as honey, she could see shapes: memories. Thousands of them, suspended like insects in amber, turning gently in currents she could not feel. Somewhere in that glittering mass was a year of her own life, purchased back from a salt-merchant by a hedge-witch's risky rite. Somewhere in it was everything else she had ever sold.

"No," Vess said beside her. It wasn't despair in his voice. It was diagnosis. He pressed one gloved hand flat against the crystal wall the way he would press a seal into wax—testing its authenticity. "This isn't a door closing. This is a *ledger* closing."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning we're inside an entry that hasn't been reconciled." He turned to look at her, and even now, even here, his face retained that terrible procedural calm—the face of a man who had burned his orders and still could not stop thinking in columns. "The court doesn't punish theft, Fairweather. The court doesn't have punishments. It has *balances*."

The salt-white flame cupped in her hands gave no heat and no light of its own, but everything around it seemed lit anyway, as if the flame were not glowing but *remembering* light. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything. When Felix shifted her grip, she felt the child's soul settle against her palms like something sleeping, trusting, unaware it had been stolen twice over—once by whoever had signed it away to the Guild, once by the Guild itself, and now, for the third time, taken back by a woman who was beginning to suspect she had never once in her life done anything honestly.

"We keep walking," Felix said. "There were side passages off the vault corridors. Storage galleries. There has to be another way up."

"There isn't." Vess said it without apology. "I've read this mountain's architecture, or as much of it as anyone has. It's built like a ledger too. One way in. One way out. And the exit—" he nodded at the crystalline wall, where a shape was now forming, tall and thin and crowned with something that glittered like frost made patient, "—is coming to reconcile us."

The Salt Regent did not walk so much as *arrive*, section by section, the way a figure emerges from fog. Its body was salt crystal grown into the approximation of nobility: high collar, long robes, a face smoothed featureless except for two hollows where eyes should be, and in those hollows, instead of darkness, there was depth—layer upon layer of stored time, going back further than kingdoms. When it spoke, it spoke first in the old tongue, those syllables Felix half-knew from Odile's banned books, and then, obligingly, cruelly, in Common:

"*Thief.*"

"I prefer," Felix said, and her voice only shook a little, "*repossessor.*"

The Regent's hollow gaze moved to the flame in her hands. Around it, the corridor's suspended memories stirred, pressing against their amber walls like moths against glass.

"You carry what was sold to Us," the Regent said. "Sold thrice. Once by the small hand of a child who did not understand what she signed. Once by the Guild that bought her cheap and sold her dear. And once—" the hollows brightened, if brightness could be cold, "—once by you, Felix Fairweather, who took her back without paying."

"She was never theirs to sell."

"And yet they sold her. And We bought her. And in the courts beneath mountains, as above them, possession is nine-tenths of law and the last tenth is written down." The Regent raised one crystalline hand, and the walls of the corridor began, slowly, to close—not crushing, nothing so vulgar, but narrowing, the memories within them swirling faster, agitated. "You will return the flame, and you will remain, and your debt will be considered... partially discharged. Or you will refuse, and We will take the flame, and you will remain, and your debt will be considered *fully* discharged. Either way, Fairweather, you remain. The mountain is sealed. No one leaves carrying what they have not paid for."

"And if I put the flame down," Felix said slowly, working it through the way she worked through a ledger, line by line, "and walked away empty-handed?"

The Regent paused. It was the pause of an accountant encountering an entry in the correct format but the wrong spirit.

"Then you would leave," it admitted. "And she would remain. And in a hundred years, perhaps two, when the last speaker of the old tongue had faded into Our archive entirely, We might release what remained of her. We are not cruel. We are *patient*." The hollows dimmed. "But you will not do that. We have watched you since you entered Our gate. You checked the child's soul for damage eleven times in the descent. You speak to her when you think no one can hear. You would not trade her silence for your freedom. That is why you are interesting. That is why, a lifetime ago, you were permitted to survive."

The words landed like stones dropped into still water. *Permitted to survive.* Felix thought of a starving winter, a salt-merchant's warm smile, a year of her life poured out of her like water from a jug—and felt, for the first time, the shape of the trap that had been set for her at sixteen, its lines running all the way down through the years to this corridor, this moment, this choice.

Vess stepped forward. "Regent. I am Vess, appraiser second class, Guild of Improbable Trade, currently in a state of—" he hesitated over the phrase, savoring it, "—conscientious defection. I have read the original contract for this soul. The Guild's seal upon it is forged. By your own Grand Ledger, as I believe your court already knows. If any party here holds an unreconciled claim, it is not the woman. It is *you*. Your purchase was made with counterfeit tender."

For the first time, something moved across the Regent's smooth face—not expression, exactly, but the memory of expression, like wind passing over a frozen lake. "We know," it said. "We have known for sixty years. We permitted the fraud because the fraud brought Us a most remarkable asset: the living archive. Every speaker of the old tongue is dead or scattered, save one, and she sat in Our vaults and dreamed in Our language and kept it alive for Us without knowing she did so." The hollows fixed on Felix. "You did not steal from Us, thief. You stole from *time itself*. Do you begin to see why We cannot simply let you walk out?"

Felix looked down at the flame. Small. Salt-white. Sleeping.

She had spent her whole adult life believing that survival was the first transaction and every other transaction merely interest on it. Starving girl, warm merchant, one year poured out—that had been the founding entry of her ledger, the one from which all other columns descended. She had built her careful invisible life on top of it the way a city is built on top of a buried crime.

But the Regent had just told her the truth of how ledgers work, the truth she had known in her hands for twenty years and never let reach her heart: *every entry balances somewhere.*

Her first sale had balanced here. In this mountain. In this moment.

"How much," Felix asked quietly, "does she weigh?"

The Regent tilted its head. "*Explain.*"

"The debt. Her soul, sold thrice, stolen once, held against a forged contract in a court that knew about the forgery." Felix heard her own voice go flat and precise, the voice she used for inventory, the bottled lullaby, the debt of shadows—her invisibility, repurposed at last into something visible. "You want equal weight. Fine. I am a trader. Let us trade. Name the price."

"A soul of equivalent vintage," the Regent said. "Equivalent depth. Equivalent—" the hollows flickered, "—*scarcity*. There is nothing in Our vaults so rare as a living speaker. What will you give Us that matches?"

Felix closed her eyes.

She saw it immediately, the way you see a number you've been avoiding in a column you've been afraid to total. She had carried the answer out of Odile's garden, down through the salt gate, past the vaults—it had been humming under her ribs the whole descent, waiting for her to be brave enough to read it aloud.

"When I was sixteen," she said, "I sold a year of my life to buy bread through a killing winter. Odile bought that year back for me. It's mine again—it lives in me again—I've been breathing with it since the rite." She opened her eyes. "It's the only year I own outright. Every other year I have belongs to some ledger or other. But that one—" her voice caught, and she let it, "—that one I paid for. That one I *earned*."

"It is one year," the Regent said. "The flame is a soul."

"One year *of mine*. Bought at sixteen with everything I had, returned to me by the only person who ever tried to save me, carried through twenty years of a life I built on top of its absence." She lifted the flame higher, offering both things at once, the taking and the giving, the whole terrible symmetry of it. "You told me I was permitted to survive. That year is the proof. Take it back. Take the survival I never should have had on credit. Let it balance against her."

The corridor went very quiet. Even the suspended memories stopped turning.

Vess made a sound beside her—small, involuntary, the closest thing to emotion she had ever heard from him. "Fairweather. That's not how appraisals work. A year against a soul—"

"It's how *my* appraisals work." She didn't look at him. "I've spent my life pricing things the Guild called priceless. Lullabies. Shadows. Names. The Guild taught me that value is whatever someone will pay. So here is what I'll pay."

The Regent regarded her for a long moment, and in its hollows, Felix saw—or imagined she saw—a single year flickering, rendered in salt and light: a starving girl, a loaf of black bread, a door closing on warmth, snow falling on a road she'd walk alone.

"You understand what you offer," the Regent said. "Not death. Worse than death, as your kind measures it. A year less of living. One fewer spring. One fewer—"

"Yes."

"—one fewer chance at—"

"*Yes.*"

The Regent extended one crystalline hand, palm up, and waited.

Felix looked at the flame one last time. Eleven times she had checked it for damage on the descent; she did not check it now. Some things you do not inspect when you are handing them home.

She set it into the Regent's palm, and the mountain exhaled, and everywhere along the corridor the suspended memories began to turn again, slow and satisfied, like a library settling back to sleep.

Then the Regent's other hand came up—faster than she could flinch—and touched her sternum, and the year came out of her.

It did not hurt. That was the horror of it. It came out the way a splinter comes out after decades, leaving behind not pain but *space*, a strange cool room in her chest where a winter used to live, where a sixteen-year-old girl had once shivered and endured and survived. She felt herself lean forward into the emptiness, chasing the warmth of it, and Vess's hand closed on her arm and held her upright, and the Regent drew back holding a small grey-white ember between its fingers, examining it the way she herself examined inventory.

"Adequate," the Regent said.

And then, because it was a court and courts are cruel in their courtesies, it added: "The debt is discharged. The seal is lifted. But know this, Felix Fairweather, former keeper of the Guild's ledgers: the year We take from you is the year you *bought back*. Which means the original sale stands. Which means the winter you survived, you survived on borrowed time—and the time has come due." The hollows dimmed to something almost gentle. "You will not remember which spring you lost. You will only feel it gone, always, the way one feels a missing stair in the dark. That is the price. That is what survival costs when it is finally paid forward."

The wall of crystal at the end of the corridor dissolved into ordinary stone, and beyond it, far above, faint as a rumor, Felix could smell rain.

"Go," the Regent said, turning away, the flame of the child's soul cradled against its chest like something it had wanted all along. "The archive will be restored. The old tongue will live. And your name—" it paused at the edge of the light, "—your name We return to you unwritten. Spend it more carefully this time."

They climbed. Stone stairs, then rough-cut tunnel, then the long salt-bright gallery toward the gate, Vess steady at her elbow the whole way, saying nothing, because there was nothing procedural left to say. At the threshold, where the first grey daylight of the surface world fell across the floor, Felix stopped and counted her own pulse and found it one beat short of where she expected it to be—found, in fact, that she could no longer say which beat was missing, only that something was, a stair in the dark, a spring she would never stand in.

Behind them, deep in the mountain, something vast and patient wrote a final line in a book no one would ever audit.

Ahead, at the top of the stairs, small and whole and blazing with a life newly returned to her, the child stood in the rain-washed light with Odile's hand on her shoulder—and opened her mouth to speak.

* * *

What she said next was not in Common, and it was not a record, and it changed everything Felix had thought she was climbing toward.
