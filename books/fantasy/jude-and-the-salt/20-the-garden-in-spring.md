## 20. The Garden in Spring

The frost came out of the ground the week before Felix returned, and Odile spent it on her knees in the garden beds, pulling winter's dead stalks and listening to the child name every green thing that broke the soil.

Not in words Odile knew. In the old tongue—the language of the mountain court, of salt and memory, of books she had burned or buried or both. The child spoke it the way rain falls: without effort, without choice, without any awareness that most of the world had forgotten how.

"Thistle," Odile would say, pointing.

"*Sess-ka-tel,*" the child would answer, and then, after a pause, in her small hoarse voice, "thistle," because she was learning—had been learning all winter—that other people needed the bridge built for them. That speaking only in the old tongue left you alone in a room full of people who loved you and could not follow.

Her name, now, was Wren. Odile had given it to her in the first week, when the girl arrived whole and blinking at the cottage door like something newly hatched, her soul restored to her by a woman who no longer remembered doing it. Wren had accepted the name the way she accepted everything: seriously, completely, as though names were the serious things and everything else was decoration. Perhaps, Odile thought, for a child who had been sold as an archive, they were.

Wren knelt beside her now in the turned earth, pressing pea seeds into the dark with a precision that made Odile smile. The girl planted the way Felix kept ledgers—every seed accounted for, every row true.

"You're thinking about her again," Wren said.

"I'm thinking about peas."

"You do the same face." Wren pressed another seed down. "When you think about her. Like you're reading a page and the ink is smudged."

Odile sat back on her heels. Sixty years of hedge-craft had taught her patience with plants, with weather, with the slow stubborn work of mending what could be mended. It had taught her almost nothing about waiting for a woman she'd raised, been betrayed by, cursed, mourned, forgiven, and—against every scrap of sense she owned—loved.

"When did Vess say she'd come?" Wren asked.

"Vess didn't say. Vess sent a letter written in ledger-hand, which means she knows exactly when and considers it beneath her dignity to mention." Odile brushed soil from her palms. "Felix will come when Felix comes. Or she won't, and we'll have peas."

Wren looked at her with those grey eyes that had seen the inside of a mountain. "She'll come," the child said. "The salt remembers her. I remember her. She's coming back."

And Odile, who had spent a lifetime distrusting prophecy because prophecy was just certainty wearing nicer clothes, found herself believing the child anyway.

* * *

Vess arrived first, which surprised no one who understood how appraisers moved through the world—always ahead of the thing being appraised, never behind it.

She came up the lane on a mud-spattered horse with Guild tack, dismounted at the gate, and stood there a long moment looking at the cottage as if checking it against some internal specification. She was thinner than she'd been on the road, when she had hunted them with warrants and reasonable offers. Her coat was new. Her eyes were not.

"Hedge-witch," she said, by way of greeting.

"Appraiser," Odile said. "Or whatever you are now. Come in before the horse judges us both."

Inside, Wren was sitting at the table copying letters—real letters, Guild-common script, laboriously formed—and Vess stopped in the doorway when she saw her. Something crossed the appraiser's face that on anyone else Odile would have called grief, or its cousin.

"She speaks now," Vess said quietly.

"Constantly. Mostly complaints about my cooking."

"Your cooking deserves complaints." Vess took the offered chair but sat on the edge of it, a woman who had not yet learned to sit anywhere as if she might be dismissed from it. "I've taken the post. Internal audit. Grand Ledger's office is being dismantled as we speak—three tribunals, two arrests, one very public burning of falsified seals. The Guild is calling it a correction." Her mouth twisted. "I'm calling it what it is, in triplicate, filed where no one can lose it."

"And the court beneath?"

"Sealed. Truly sealed, this time—not the way they seal things when they want you to think they've sealed them. The Regent paid its own debt, in its own coin." Vess glanced at Wren. "It lost its archive. A court that trades in memory cannot survive without remembering why it trades. They'll keep their books, but the buying is over. Your apprentice—" she caught herself, corrected, "—your Felix saw to that."

"She's not mine," Odile said. "She hasn't been mine since she was sixteen."

"No," Vess agreed, and there was something careful in it. "But she's coming back to you anyway. I escorted her as far as the crossroads at Millbrook. She wanted to walk the last miles alone." A pause. "She's frightened."

"Felix doesn't frighten."

"Everyone frightens," Vess said, "once they've paid enough." She set a folded paper on the table between them—a single sheet, sealed with wax the color of old bone. "This is the audit finding on the original sale. The child's soul, entered into Guild inventory eleven years ago, signed with an apprentice mark forged by the merchant's network to protect the trail. It exonerates her. Officially, permanently, in every ledger that matters." She pushed it across the wood. "Give it to her when she's ready. Not before. Some people need to hear they're innocent from someone who isn't holding the knife."

Wren had stopped copying. She was watching Vess with her head tilted, the way she watched weather come in.

"You burned your orders," the child said suddenly. "In the vaults. I wasn't supposed to see, but the salt told me everything the salt touched. You held them over the lamp and your hands shook and you said—" Wren frowned, reaching for it, translating from a language that had no word for procedure, "*you said, let the record show I refuse.*"

Vess went very still.

"Yes," she said finally. "That's what I said."

"It's the bravest sentence in the old tongue," Wren told her solemnly. "There isn't a word for it, so we borrow one. *Tel-vara.* It means: I will be the one who remembers differently."

Vess stood abruptly, thanked Odile for nothing in particular, and left. At the gate she paused, hand on the rail, and Odile saw her look back at the garden—at the green rows, the turning earth, the small ordinary miracle of a place where nothing was for sale—and then ride away toward the Guildhall with her spine straight as a plumb line.

Odile put the exoneration paper in the drawer with the good candles, where Felix would find it, or wouldn't, depending on what kind of day courage gave her.

* * *

Felix came at dusk, three days later, walking up the lane with a satchel over one shoulder and nothing else—no horse, no escort, no ledger under her arm. Odile saw her from the kitchen window and had the sudden, humiliating urge to hide, sixty-one years old and hiding behind her own curtain like a girl.

She didn't hide. She put the kettle on instead, because there were things you could say with a kettle that you couldn't say with a mouth.

Felix knocked. Of course she knocked. Eleven years gone, a soul ransomed, a self spent down to the wick, and she still knocked at the door of the woman whose betrayal had started it all—as if this were a house she'd merely been invited to.

Odile opened it.

Felix looked—smaller. That was the first thing. Not thinner, exactly; the road had fed her well enough. But smaller in the way a house is smaller after the furniture's been sold, rooms standing open and echoing where once there had been walls of accumulated self. Her eyes went past Odile's shoulder into the cottage, cataloguing it—old habit, inventory habit—and Odile watched her find the gaps in herself the same way, reaching for familiar shelves and touching air.

"Odile," Felix said. Her voice cracked on the second syllable, and she covered it with a cough. "I—there was nowhere else. I know that's not fair to—"

"Get in before the moths do."

Felix stepped over the threshold and stopped, staring at the table, at the chairs, at the fire, at the drawer where the good candles lived, as though she were memorizing a room she'd never seen and had always known. Then Wren came barreling out of the back room and hit her at waist height hard enough to stagger her, arms locked around her ribs, face buried in her coat, and Felix made a sound Odile had never heard her make—not a word, not a sob, something older than either—and held on.

They stayed like that long enough for the kettle to sing.

Over supper—stew, bread, the cooking Wren complained about and ate three bowls of—Felix told them almost nothing, which was how Odile knew how much there was. She described the descent in flat trader's prose, the court in the clipped shorthand of someone reading from an inventory list, the bargain in numbers. What she paid. What it cost. She did not describe the memory she'd bought back, the truth of what she'd done at sixteen, and Odile did not ask, because she had done her own arithmetic across forty-five years and knew exactly which entry Felix had finally read.

Instead Felix said, at the end, hands wrapped around her cup: "I don't remember the road here. From Millbrook. Vess says I walked it, but it's—" She turned the cup. "There are holes. Whole seasons. I know I worked for the Guild nine years, because the ledgers say so, but I can't remember a single morning of them. I know I ran from you. I don't remember deciding to." A breath. "I came back to a life I apparently lived, and I have no receipt for any of it."

"What do you remember?" Odile asked.

Felix was quiet a while. The fire ticked. Outside, spring rain had begun, soft on the new-turned beds.

"The smell of ink," she said at last. "That survived. The smell of ink and the weight of a pen and—I don't know why that matters, but it does. It feels like me." She looked up. "And her laugh. Hers survived. And—" she faltered, frowning, reaching into one of the holes and finding something at the bottom of it, "—and there's a feeling. About you. I can't source it. There's just this—tenderness, sitting in my chest, and no memory attached, and I don't know if I'm allowed to feel it because I don't know what I did to earn it or what I did to ruin it."

Odile set down her spoon.

"You're allowed," she said. "Feelings aren't entries, Felix. They don't need provenance."

"That's easy for you to say. You remember everything."

"Yes," Odile said. "And I'd trade half of it for one hour of not knowing what you did at sixteen, so we're both poor in different currencies." She reached across the table and took Felix's wrist—bony, ink-stained still, some habits surviving even the paying-away—and held it. "You betrayed me. I know it. You've remembered it. We know it. And you came back anyway, having bought a child's life with the last of your own time, and if you think I'm going to spend our remaining years auditing each other, you picked the wrong witch to flee twice."

Felix's eyes were wet. She laughed, wetly, horribly, beautifully. "You haven't changed."

"I've changed enormously. I planted peas."

Later, when Wren was asleep and the rain had settled into its overnight work, Odile showed Felix to the back room—the one with the narrow bed and the window facing the garden. Felix stood in the doorway a long moment.

"There's no desk," she said.

"No."

"I always have a desk. Ledgers, pens, the—" She stopped. Put her hand flat against the doorframe. "I don't have a ledger anymore. Anywhere. I checked. The Guild struck my marks; Vess confirmed it. Every line I ever wrote belongs to them or to the mountain. I am, officially, a person with no record." Her voice wobbled on the last word. "Do you understand what that means? Forty years of keeping accounts, of making the world legible, of being the one who knew where everything stood—and I'm illegible. I'm a blank page."

Odile considered her. This woman, this brilliant ruined meticulous woman, standing in a doorway grieving the loss of her own handwriting.

"Then tomorrow," Odile said, "we'll get you a pen."

Felix blinked. "What?"

"A blank page is only a tragedy if you believe the writing's already done. I never believed that. It's why I'm a hedge-witch and not a banker." She steered Felix gently into the room, into the bed, tucking the quilt up the way she had when the girl was sixteen and feverish and furious at the world. "Sleep. In the morning there will be peas to plant, a paper in the candle drawer with your name cleared on it, and a child who has decided you're hers, which is a debt no court can collect."

Felix was already half under, exhaustion claiming her like a tide. But at the edge of sleep she caught Odile's sleeve.

"The tenderness," she murmured. "Toward you. Is it real? If I can't remember where it came from—is it real?"

Odile stayed crouched by the bed a moment, looking at the face of the girl she'd raised and lost and gotten back at compound interest.

"It's the realest thing in this house," she said. "Everything else is furniture."

Felix slept. Odile banked the fire, checked the wards out of habit, and paused at the window overlooking the dark garden. The rain had stopped. Moonlight lay along the new rows like a line of silver script, waiting.

And there, at the foot of the garden where the soil was freshest, something glinted—faint, crystalline, wrong for spring. Odile leaned closer to the glass, squinting against the dark.

A single grain of salt, large as a thumbnail, resting on top of the earth. And beside it, pressed into the mud as neatly as a signature, a second mark: small, white, deliberate.

The old tongue's first character. The one Wren had promised, weeks ago, to teach them.

The one that meant, according to the child, *paid in full.*

Except—Odile's blood went slow and cold—Wren had also told her, laughing over supper just last night, that the court was sealed, that nothing crossed the salt gate anymore, that the mountains kept their books in silence now.

Nothing should have delivered this.

Odile opened the window and looked out at the sleeping garden, at the grain of salt catching moonlight like a coin left in payment, and wondered what, exactly, the mountain thought it had just settled—and whether the account, despite everything, was truly closed.
