## 1. The Bone That Sings

The rain came off the mountains in the late afternoon, as it always did in Greymarrow, and Hazel Eastwick lit the lamp above their workbench and pulled the door's bolt before the first client arrived.

Not that anyone would call them clients. In Greymarrow—a town of slate roofs and salt-rotted piers where the smugglers' boats came in with the tide and the empire's tax collectors came never—the people who climbed the narrow stair to Hazel's rooms were called visitors, or neighbors, or nothing at all. They brought small bundles wrapped in linen or oilcloth or, once memorably, a wool sock. Inside the bundles: bones. And inside the bones: the dead.

Hazel set the lamp so its light fell warm and yellow across the bench, not too bright. Brightness invited scrutiny, and scrutiny was death to a person like Hazel. They had learned invisibility the way other children learned prayers—repetition, patience, a little fear to keep it honest. Wear grey when others wore grey. Laugh at the second joke, not the first. Never be the most interesting thing in any room, because in Thornfell the interesting things had a way of being collected by men in imperial coats.

They were arranging their tools when the knock came: three soft raps, then two, then one. The old pattern. Hazel unbolted the door and found Maren Cole standing on the landing with rain dripping off her widow's hood and a bundle clutched to her chest like an infant.

"You're open," Maren said. It wasn't a question. It was relief.

"I'm open." Hazel stepped back to let her in. "Mind the third stair. It lies."

Maren crossed the room and sat in the chair Hazel indicated—the good chair, the one with the cushion, reserved for the grieving—and unwrapped her bundle with hands that shook only a little. Inside lay a fingerbone. A woman's index finger, the bone clean and pale, separated from the rest of her with the careful reverence of someone who had done it weeping.

"Mother's been gone nine days," Maren said. "Buried up at Kettle Row, proper ground, proper rites. But I keep—" She stopped. Swallowed. "I keep dreaming of water. And she was afraid of water her whole life. Never once crossed the harbor, even when the ferry was free on festival days. So either it's just my grief talking, or she left something unsaid."

Hazel took up the bone and turned it in the lamplight. To anyone else it would have been a relic, a keepsake, a morbid little token of mourning. To Hazel it was a letter sealed by the dead woman's own body, waiting for the one reader alive who could break it.

"Nine days is fresh," Hazel said. "She'll be loud. Some people find that frightening."

"My mother scared me for sixty years while she lived." Maren almost smiled. "I doubt death has changed her much."

Hazel laughed despite themselves—a short, surprised sound—and covered it by reaching for the carving knife. That was the trouble with this work. Every client brought a little warmth into the room, and every client left, and Hazel remained behind among the bones with all that borrowed affection cooling in their chest like tea forgotten on a sill.

They set the fingerbone against the leather pad and began to carve.

* * *

It was not magic, whatever the priests of Vael called it when they burned people for less. Or rather—it was magic, but of a humble kind, the sort that looked from the outside like craft. The dead left themselves in their bones the way a singer leaves a song in a bell. Strike the bell right and the song comes out. Hazel's gift was the striking: the angle of the knife, the pressure of the thumb, the small precise cuts that opened the bone's grain like pages. Anyone could learn the cuts. Almost no one could hear what came after.

Hazel had heard their first voice at seven years old, working a chicken bone at the kitchen table with their grandmother's paring knife. The hen had said, quite distinctly, *mind the fox*, and Hazel had screamed, and their mother had said *that's nonsense*, and their grandmother—who was from the high valleys, where the old ways kept better—had taken the knife away gently and said, "Then you'll want to learn to do it properly, little bird, so it doesn't happen to you sideways."

Grandmother Ashka had taught them everything: how to still the mind before listening, how to close a bone politely when the work was done, how to distinguish a memory from a message, a message from a haunting. And she had taught them the rule above all rules.

*"You help the dead, Hazel. You help the living who grieve. But you tell no one what you are. Not your friends. Not your lovers. Not the priest, not the magistrate, not the pretty boy who buys you wine. Names get written down, and written things get found, and found things get carried off to Vael in iron wagons."*

Hazel had obeyed for twenty years. It had cost them, some nights more than others. There was a man in the fish trade, Tam, who smiled at them across the market stalls with a persistence that would have been charming if Hazel could afford charm. Twice now they had let him walk them home through the fog. Twice they had said goodnight at the foot of the stairs and watched him go with something like grief. It was safer this way. It was lonelier than the drowned chapel at midnight, but it was safer, and Hazel had built their whole life out of that trade—safety bought with loneliness, coin by coin, year by year.

Now they bent over Maren Cole's mother and listened.

The first cuts sang low, the way old voices did. Then the bone opened, and the dead woman filled the room.

She came as sensation before she came as words: the smell of tarred rope, the ache of knees that had knelt on stone floors for decades, a fierce dry love like sun on whitewash. And under it all, water. Water everywhere in her, though she had never crossed the harbor. Water rising.

*Maren,* the dead woman said—or thought, or remembered saying, which in a bone amounts to the same thing—*Maren, my girl, if you're hearing this, then the carver's real, and everything I heard at the end was true.*

Hazel's knife paused.

Dead women spoke of many things: love, debts, recipes, grievances, the location of hidden silver, apologies thirty years overdue. Hazel had heard ten thousand confessions and could count on one hand the times a dead voice had spoken directly to the listener—to *them*—across the gap. The dead spoke to the ones they'd loved. The carver was only the ear.

But this voice leaned toward Hazel through the bone like a woman leaning over a fence.

*I went quiet about it while I lived,* the voice said. *Quiet is safe; my own mother taught me that, and hers before her, back to the burning years. But dying loosens the tongue, and there isn't time for quiet anymore, girl. Listen. Listen to me.*

"I'm listening," Hazel murmured. Their mouth had gone dry.

Maren leaned forward. "Did you hear something?"

"Hush," Hazel said, more sharply than they meant. "Please."

The dead woman gathered herself. Hazel felt her doing it—the strange effort a soul-in-bone made to arrange its fragments into order, the way a dreamer tries to hold a dream's logic against waking. What came through next came slow, and each word landed like a stone dropped down a well.

*There is a Door under Thornfell. The old ones knew it; that's why the high valleys kept their silence, why we never wrote our histories down, why we put our true names in songs instead of books. Books can be seized. Songs have to be sung. But the Door is real, and it sleeps, and it wakes when the last key walks the earth.*

*You are the key, carver. You don't know it yet. Maybe you never will, if we're lucky, and Thornfell goes on being poor and forgotten and ours. But I heard the other dead whispering at the edge of my going—they come to the dying, did you know that? They stand around the bed like crows on a fence. And they were saying the maps have started changing again. Saying the Vaelmen have new machines that eat names. Saying the Door will wake whether we want it or not, and there's a man coming across the mountains with fire in his briefcase and our whole country folded up inside his orders.*

*Hide, Hazel Eastwick. Be small. Be nothing. But if the day comes when hiding fails—*

The voice frayed. Nine days was fresh, but even fresh voices ran thin when they strained. Hazel carved faster, opening the grain wider, spending the bone's depth like a spendthrift, because this mattered, this mattered more than anything they had ever heard through bone—

*—if hiding fails, remember: three bones for the lock. The keepers hold them. The keepers are dead, all three, dead these hundred years and glad of it. Find the bones before he does. Before the Door seals. Because a country can die twice, child. Once in body. Once in memory. He means to take us both.*

And then, faintly, with the tenderness of a woman smoothing a blanket over a sleeping child:

*That's all. Tell my Maren I loved her crosser than I ever showed, and that the dreams of water weren't mine—they were theirs, the crow-dead, trying to reach me because I was closest to the ground. Tell her I'm sorry about the blue bowl. It wasn't broken when I blamed her.*

The bone went silent. Not empty—never empty—but finished, the way a book is finished, its pages read.

Hazel sat back. The lamp had burned lower without their noticing. Rain lashed the window. Their hands, they discovered, were trembling, and they pressed them flat against the bench until the trembling stopped.

"Hazel?" Maren's voice, small in the dark room. "Your face. What did she say? Is she at peace? Did I bury her wrong? Was it the ground—"

"She's at peace." Hazel closed the bone with three polite strokes, sealing the voice away, and wrapped it in fresh linen. Their voice came out steady, because twenty years of practice could make anything come out steady. "She loved you. She wants you to know she loved you crosser than she ever showed. And she says the blue bowl wasn't broken when she blamed you."

Maren made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and pressed her knuckles to her mouth. "The blue bowl. Saints' teeth, the *blue bowl*. She made me pay for it out of my festival money and I was eleven." Tears slid down her weathered face, unhidden. "And the water? I dreamed of water—"

"That wasn't her fear. That was—" Hazel hesitated. The rule. *Tell no one what you are.* But the rule had never accounted for this, for warnings passed from the crow-dead through a dying woman's bone straight into Hazel's hands. "That was the dead around her, trying to pass a message. She asked me to give you her apology and nothing else. Some things aren't for the living to carry."

It was a half-truth, and Maren, being grieving and grateful and tired, accepted it. She pressed coins into Hazel's palm—too many, as always—and clasped their hand between both of hers, and for one dangerous moment Hazel stood inside the warmth of another person's grip and wanted, with a violence that frightened them, to say *stay, talk, tell me about the blue bowl and your mother and your life, let me be a person to you and not a door.*

"Thank you," Maren whispered. "Whatever you did in there—I don't know how, and I won't ask. Thank you."

Then she was gone, down the lying third stair and out into the rain, and the room was Hazel's again, and the silence came back like a tide reclaiming sand.

* * *

They didn't sleep.

They told themselves it was the work—there were three other bones waiting on the shelf, a drowned fisherman's rib and a child's milk tooth and a soldier's knuckle, each owed its hour of listening—and it was true that the work waited. But Hazel sat instead with the dead woman's warning spread out in their mind like contraband on a table.

*A man coming across the mountains with fire in his briefcase.* Dead women did not lie. That was the one mercy of the gift: bones held truth the way amber held flies. Exaggeration, delusion, wishful thinking—all possible. Flat invention, no. Whatever Maren Cole's mother had heard from the crow-dead at the edge of her going, she had believed it utterly.

Hazel got up, moved the lamp to the window, and looked out over Greymarrow.

The town lay along its harbor like something spilled and drying. Fishing lights. The tavern glow on Rope Walk. Beyond the roofs, the mountain road climbed north toward the passes, invisible in the rain, and beyond the passes—Vael. The empire that had swallowed half the world map by map, treaty by treaty, name by name. Thornfell had survived a century on a single trick: being worth less to Vael than the cost of taking it. Poor. Remote. Full of smugglers and superstition and valleys that didn't appear on any survey worth reading.

But Hazel had noticed what the old woman had noticed, hadn't they? They simply hadn't let themselves know it. The survey teams last spring, measuring roads that needed no measuring. The new customs post at Miller's Ford, staffed by soldiers who wrote down names. Names, always names. The peddler from Lowmark who swore the village of Brindle had vanished—not destroyed, *vanished*—from every map and record, so that when he tried to swear he'd traded there, his own ledger disagreed with him.

Hazel had filed it all under *not my problem*, which was the drawer where they kept everything sharp.

Now the drawer wouldn't close.

*Three bones for the lock. The keepers hold them.* Hazel turned to the shelf where their finished work waited, and their eyes went unwillingly to the small locked box beneath it—the one holding their own leavings. Every carver kept their shavings, their failed pieces, the slivers of bone trimmed away over years of work. Grandmother Ashka had taught them to burn the leavings each solstice. Hazel had never managed it. Something in them balked at burning even the smallest piece of their own making.

*Why?* they thought, and the question had teeth. *Why did I never burn them?*

Below, on Rope Walk, a lantern moved through the rain. Hazel tracked it idly, then not idly at all, because it wasn't moving like a drunk or a watchman or a fisherman late home. It was moving steadily, purposefully, from the harbormaster's office toward the inn where travelers stayed—and travelers, in Greymarrow, in this season, were rare as honest tax collectors.

Even as they watched, a second lantern joined the first. Then a third. Men in long coats, walking in step. At this hour. Through this rain.

Hazel pinched out the lamp and stood in the dark with their heart going hard, telling themselves it meant nothing, patrols happened, storms drove strangers ashore—and hearing, underneath the rational voice, the crow-dead's whisper rising through nine days of grave-silence like water through floorboards:

*He is coming. Hide. Be nothing. And if hiding fails—*

On the shelf in the dark, the dead woman's fingerbone seemed almost to hum.

Hazel Eastwick, who had spent twenty years learning to be no one, stood alone in their cold room and understood, with terrible clarity, that by morning the being-no-one was going to stop working—and that they had no idea what they would be instead.
