## 2. A Date With Prophecy

The archivist's house smelled of dust and lamp oil and the sweet rot of a body quietly failing. Hazel noticed the last of these first—it came off him like heat off a stove, that particular sweetness they'd learned to recognize in the dying, in Greymarrow's back lanes where people paid for carvings they could not otherwise afford and did not always live to collect.

"Eastwick." Old Maren Corbishley said the name like he was tasting it, rolling it against his teeth. He sat propped in a chair by the window, wrapped in a blanket despite the mild evening, his hands folded on a wooden box as though warming them at a fire only he could feel. His eyes were yellow at the rims. "You came."

"You sent three messages." Hazel set down their satchel inside the door, out of habit—out of the long habit of never letting anyone see what they carried until the price was settled. "That's two more than most clients bother with."

"I wanted to be sure you'd come *before*." He didn't finish the sentence. Dying men rarely did; they'd all made the same pact with language, to leave the worst words unsaid as if silence could hold the door shut.

Hazel crossed the room slowly. The house was a hoard of paper—stacks of it on every surface, bundles tied with string, ledgers swollen with damp, scrolls in clay jars along one whole wall. An archivist's home. Maren had spent forty years cataloguing what the empire's clerks called *redundant records*: parish books, tithe rolls, genealogies, the small stubborn paperwork of a country that Vael's maps no longer acknowledged. Thornfell existed in Maren's house if it existed anywhere.

"You know my rates," Hazel said.

"For you? I know your rates better than your own mother would have." A dry laugh that turned into coughing. He pressed a handkerchief to his mouth and when it came away there was rust on it. "I'm not paying for a keepsake, girl—or boy—or whatever polite word you've decided on this season. I'm paying for work."

Hazel kept their face still. In Greymarrow you learned early that stillness was armor. "Work is work. What kind?"

Maren opened the box.

Inside, on a bed of wool gone grey with age, lay a fingerbone. Human. Old—the color of old ivory, honey-dark at the joint where the living tissue had once fed it. It was shorter than a fingerbone should be, or perhaps it only looked short because it was thick, dense, the bone of someone who had worked with their hands until their hands were the last part of them to stop.

"This belonged to Aldous Wren," Maren said. "Scribe of the Undercroft, dead these hundred years and more. I bought it from a relic-monger in Lowmarsh when I was younger than you are now, and I have never once dared ask it anything." He looked up, and for the first time Hazel saw real fear in him—not of death, but of something adjacent to it. "You'll carve it tonight. Tonight, do you understand me? Not tomorrow, not when the light is good. Tonight."

"Tonight costs double."

"Tonight is worth quadruple." He fumbled at his belt-pouch with hands that shook like leaves. "And there's a second fee. When you hear what's inside it—and you will hear something, Eastwick, I'd stake my catalogue on it—you tell me. All of it. No softening, no carving it pretty. I'm an archivist. I want the text, not the commentary."

Hazel looked at the bone for a long moment. A century-old fingerbone, uncarved, unasked. Most relics that reached them had been carved already by some loving hand—a sister's initial, a child's name—and the dead spoke through the carving the way sound speaks through a struck bell. But an uncarved bone was raw material. Silence waiting to be shaped. Whatever Aldous Wren had carried to his grave, it was still folded inside him like a letter never opened.

"All right," Hazel said. "Lamp closer. And water—I'll need clean water, and salt if you have it."

Maren had both. He always had everything; that was the trouble with archivists.

* * *

Hazel worked the way they always worked: alone inside their own skull even in a crowded room. The lamp was trimmed low because bright light flattened the bone's grain, and the grain mattered—every relic had its grain, its weather of calcium and time, and you read it the way a sailor reads sky. This bone's grain ran deep and true, without flaw. Aldous Wren had been healthy when he died. Healthy and, if the density of him meant anything, afraid of very little.

They clamped it gently in the vice of their left hand and took up the smallest blade, the one they used for fine work, for names and dates, for the delicate interior letters that held the loudest voices.

"What should I carve?" they asked, because protocol mattered. The dead answered questions better than commands.

"His last secret," Maren whispered from his chair. "Whatever he wouldn't say alive."

Hazel set the point against the bone and cut the first stroke of the oldest sigil they knew—the one for *memory*, a single vertical line with two branches, like a tree stripped to its meaning.

The room went quiet in the way rooms go quiet before snow.

It wasn't sound, exactly, what came through. It was pressure. The sense of a presence leaning close over their shoulder, patient, curious, faintly amused—as though Aldous Wren had been waiting a hundred years for someone to finally knock. Hazel breathed out slowly, let the blade find the second stroke, and the presence gathered itself the way a man gathers himself to speak in a large hall.

Then it began to recite.

Not speak. *Recite.* The cadence was liturgical, formal, the rhythm of a man who had memorized a text so thoroughly it lived in his body rather than his mind. Hazel's knife slowed. The voice was dry and precise and utterly certain, and it said:

*"In the year the empire counts as nothing, in the season of ash, the Door shall stand between the ninth and tenth bells of a winter night, and the key shall walk abroad wearing the name of Eastwick. Mark the date: the longest dark. Mark the child: neither son nor daughter, but the carver of what remains. Three bones the keepers keep, and the fourth bone is the hand itself. When the pale hunger comes to eat the names of Thornfell, the Eastwick child shall choose what the Door remembers—"*

Hazel stopped carving.

The voice stopped too. It hung there, unfinished, the way a bell hangs after the strike, vibrating in some register below hearing. The lamp flame stood perfectly straight, as though the air itself were holding its breath.

Their hands were steady—they were always steady, that was the gift's cruel joke, their hands never shook no matter what they heard—but beneath the steadiness their whole body had turned cold, a cold rising from the floorboards up through their boots into their chest.

Neither son nor daughter.

The Eastwick child.

*Mark the date.*

Hazel looked at the window. Beyond the glass, Greymarrow's rooftops stepped down toward the harbor, and above them the sky was the deep blue-black of midwinter, the stars sharp as nailheads. The longest dark. Tonight was the solstice. Tonight was the longest night of the year.

And somewhere past the ninth bell—Hazel's eyes found Maren's chipped clock on the mantel, its hands crawling toward eleven—the Door would stand open.

"There's more," the dead man said through the bone, and now the recitation had changed, had become urgent, personal, Aldous Wren speaking in his own voice at last across a century of silence. "*I have written it all down. Every word the Undercroft preserved. They will burn the books, but they cannot burn what a man carries in his marrow. Whoever hears this: the book is in the archive at Greymarrow, under the loose stone behind the third shelf, the stone with the antler mark. Take it to the Eastwick child when the child comes of age. Tell them the Door does not lead where Vael thinks it leads. Tell them the prophecy ends mid-sentence, and the ending has not yet been written.*"

Silence.

Hazel set down the knife with exaggerated care, the way you set down a live coal.

Maren was watching them from his chair with an expression of terrible patience. "Well?" he rasped. "Text, not commentary. You promised."

Hazel repeated it. All of it. Word for word, because that was the discipline, because the dead deserved exactness even when exactness was a knife between the ribs. Their voice sounded strange to them, flat, like someone else reading a list. When they reached *the Eastwick child*, Maren closed his eyes. When they reached *the longest dark*, his breath went ragged. By the end he was gripping the arms of his chair hard enough to whiten his knuckles.

"So it's true," he whispered. "Aldous told me once—he was the only voice in the Undercroft who ever mentioned it—that the prophecies weren't predictions. That they were *records*. Written backward. That whoever wrote them had already seen the end and was leaving receipts." He opened his eyes. "1823, Eastwick. That text was set down in 1823. Before your grandmother's grandmother drew breath. And it knew about you. It knew about *tonight*."

"The empire doesn't know about me." Hazel heard their own voice and hated how thin it came out. "No one knows about me. I carve trinkets for smugglers' widows and keep my head down and—"

"And you're sitting in my house on the solstice, having just raised a hundred-year-dead scribe who says otherwise." Maren laughed, and the laugh cost him; he folded forward, coughing, and when he straightened there was blood on his lips and a fierce, unholy joy in his yellowed eyes. "Do you understand what I've spent my life doing, child? Preserving scraps. Saving what I could from the Pale Engine's appetite, hiding parish books in flour barrels, copying genealogies onto cloth because paper gets burned. And all that time the answer was sitting in my box, waiting for a bone-carver born at exactly the wrong moment to pick it up." He pushed himself upright, swaying. "The loose stone. Third shelf. Go."

"Go where? Go *now*?"

"It's nearly the ninth bell." Maren's voice cracked like a whip. "The text says the Door stands between the ninth and tenth bells. Whether you believe it or don't, whether you want it or don't—you need to read that book before the night is older. And then you need to be gone from Greymarrow by morning, because if Aldous Wren's ghost knows your name, I promise you the empire's spies have gotten there faster."

Hazel stood frozen in the lamplight, and the carefully ordered architecture of their life—the invisibility, the routines, the small safe circle of clients and corpse-roads and careful silences—shivered around them like ice on a thawing pond.

"I'm nobody," they said, and it came out almost pleading. "I've spent twenty-six years being nobody. That's the whole trick of it. That's how I've survived."

Maren looked at them for a long moment, and his expression gentled into something that hurt worse than any accusation.

"Yes," he said softly. "And look what it's bought you. A rented room. A trade no honest person admits to. Friends who pay you and leave." He gestured at the walls of paper surrounding them both. "I am dying, Eastwick, and I will tell you the only thing I know for certain: a country can survive being conquered. It cannot survive being forgotten. And you—" he tapped the box with one shaking finger, "—you are the only person alive who can hear what it wants remembered. Nobody is a luxury you can no longer afford."

The clock chimed the ninth bell.

Hazel moved.

Later they could not reconstruct the decision—only the motion, the sudden terrible clarity of action. They went to the archive wall, found the third shelf, found the stone with the antler mark worn nearly smooth, and pulled. It came free with a grinding sigh, exhaling a breath of air a century old, and there in the cavity lay a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with wire, sealed with wax stamped by a hand long dead.

Inside: a book. Leather gone soft as skin, pages foxed and brittle, and on the first page, in faded iron-gall ink, a title in Aldous Wren's precise hand:

*Concerning the Hollow Door: Being a True Account of the Prophecies of Thornfell, Collected and Annotated, 1823.*

"Hazel." Maren's voice, from far away. "Hazel, listen to me. There's a man coming. An Inquisitor-General. My contacts wrote me a week ago—Corvane, they call him, the Pale Priest. He lands at Greymarrow within days with orders for Thornfell's records. For Thornfell's *people*. If he finds that book—"

"He won't."

"No. He won't. Because you'll be gone, and I'll be dead, and dead men keep secrets beautifully." He smiled, and it was the bravest, saddest thing Hazel had seen in years. "One last commission, carver. When this is over—if there is an *over*—carve my name on something. Anything. So that somewhere, someone can still hear me complaining about the quality of modern ink."

Hazel crossed back to him and, breaking every rule they'd built their life on, took the dying man's hand. His fingers were cool as the fingerbone in their pocket. For one wild moment they thought of offering—of pressing their thumb to his wrist and listening while he still had breath, of telling him what waited, of giving him that much. But the gift didn't work that way; it never had. The dead spoke through the carved. The living got silence, and courage, and whatever comfort memory could provide.

"Thank you," Hazel said. It was inadequate. It was all there was.

"Bells wait for no one," Maren said, and waved them toward the door with a hand that shook. "Go be somebody's prophecy. God help us all."

Hazel went out into the longest dark with a stolen book against their chest and a dead man's fingerbone in their pocket, and the streets of Greymarrow received them the way streets receive the guilty—silently, watchingly, indifferent. Behind them the ninth bell's echo died among the rooftops. Ahead, somewhere between the ninth bell and the tenth, a door that officially did not exist was standing open in a country that officially did not exist, waiting for a child whose name it had known for a hundred years.

At the mouth of Corpse Lane, where the smugglers' paths dropped down into the dark beneath the town, Hazel stopped and looked back once at the scatter of lamplit windows. Somewhere in one of them, an old man was settling into his chair to die alone, content, his life's work walking away in a stranger's satchel.

Somewhere beyond the harbor lights, Hazel could not have said why they were so sure of this, a ship was coming. On its deck stood a man whose job was erasure, whose faith was forgetting, and who would burn a mountain country to ash to reach a door he did not understand.

The tenth bell began to ring.

Hazel pulled their collar up, turned their back on the town, and went down into the dark, taking the dead man's road—the one only they could hear—toward whatever had been written for them in 1823.

Behind them, faint and fading, the last thing they heard from the world of the living was Maren Corbishley's window-lamp going out.

Ahead, in the bone against their heart, Aldous Wren had begun, very softly, to laugh.
