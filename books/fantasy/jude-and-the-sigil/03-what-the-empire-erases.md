## 3. What the Empire Erases

The ship that brought the Inquisitor-General to Greymarrow flew no colors.

Brannic Vael-ordered himself not to notice that, and noticed it anyway. Every vessel in the imperial fleet wore the sun-and-spear of the Ninth Throne somewhere visible—on the mainsail, on the prow, on the pennants snapping above the sterncastle. This ship was grey from keel to crown, the color of a thing that did not wish to be remembered having arrived. It slid into Greymarrow's harbor at dawn through a fog so thick the watchtowers had no warning, and by the time the fog lifted, the gangplank was down and soldiers in unmarked livery were already ashore, moving with the unhurried certainty of men who had never once been told *no*.

Brannic stood at his post on the customs quay, third rank from the left, hands clasped behind him in the posture of a man who had learned that stillness was safer than attention. He was a corporal now. He had been a sergeant once, with a sergeant's pay and a sergeant's authority and a sergeant's right to speak. He had spent all three of those things in a single afternoon in the town of Lowmark, eighteen months ago, when he had asked—politely, in writing, through proper channels—why the census records for Lowmark's eastern parishes were being burned rather than archived.

The answer had come back as a demotion, a transfer, and a lesson: *the empire does not burn records out of carelessness, Corporal. It burns them out of precision.*

He had thought about that sentence every day since. He was thinking about it now, watching the grey ship disgorge its cargo: crates of black lacquer, coils of copper wire, and a machine the size of a wagon bed, wrapped in oilcloth, carried down the plank by six men who walked as though it weighed nothing and feared it anyway.

"Corporal Vannic."

The name was almost his. That was deliberate too; he'd learned that much about the empire's clerks. They kept your name one letter wrong so you would correct them, and correcting an officer was insubordination, and then they had you.

"Vannic," he said. "Yes, sir."

The speaker was a lieutenant with a ledger under one arm and the pale, watered-down look of a man who had been promoted past the point where sleep could reach him. "You've been assigned to the Inquisitor-General's staff. Clerk duties. Records intake, transcription, certification." He looked Brannic up and down with the flat appraisal of a horse trader. "You have a reputation for thoroughness."

"Yes, sir."

"And for asking questions."

Brannic kept his face the way he kept his hands: still. "I've learned the difference between questions and objections, sir."

"Have you." The lieutenant almost smiled. "Good. The Inquisitor-General has no use for objections. Questions he'll answer himself, in his own time, or not at all. Come along."

They crossed the quay together, past the fish-wives who had gone quiet in the way that coastal people go quiet—not afraid yet, but counting. Greymarrow was a smuggler town; it had survived four centuries of kings and two decades of empire by being worth more intact than occupied. Brannic watched a woman selling smoked eels mark her prices higher with a piece of chalk even as she bowed her head to the marching column, and he thought: *this town is doing arithmetic.* The empire hated arithmetic it hadn't done itself.

The Inquisitor-General had established himself in the harbormaster's house, which was the largest building on the waterfront and had the additional advantage of windows facing the sea, so that no one inside could forget which direction orders came from. Soldiers were carrying furniture out—the harbormaster's own chairs, his own desk—and carrying other furniture in: plain black pieces, heavy, with no ornamentation, as if decoration itself were a form of evidence.

Corvane was standing at the window when Brannic was shown in.

He was not what Brannic expected, though Brannic could not have said what he had expected. Inquisitors-General came in two varieties in the army's imagination: firebrands and corpses. Corvane was neither. He was a man of perhaps fifty, silvering at the temples, dressed in a plain grey coat with a single chain of office at the collar—a small iron sigil, square, featureless. He had the build of someone who had once been a soldier and had let the soldier go the way you let a coat go out of fashion. His hands were folded behind his back. He was watching the fog burn off the harbor with an expression of mild, patient interest, like a scholar watching ink dry.

"Corporal," he said, without turning. "Vannic."

"Sir."

"Do you know what I have come to Greymarrow to do?"

"No, sir."

"Process," Corvane said. He turned then, and his eyes were the most ordinary eyes Brannic had ever seen on a powerful man—brown, slightly tired, the eyes of a provincial tax assessor. "That is the word on my commission. Not *purge*, not *cleanse*, not any of the dramatic vocabulary the provinces enjoy inventing about us. Process. Do you know why we use that word?"

"No, sir."

"Because processing implies a system." Corvane crossed to the desk—it was already his, the harbormaster's brass fittings stripped away—and opened a folio. "Fire is a confession. Fire says: this frightened me. We are not frightened, Corporal. Thornfell is a country of four hundred thousand souls, eleven towns, one capital, and approximately—" he touched the page, "—one million nine hundred thousand documents of record. Births, deaths, debts, deeds, marriages, trials, wills, guild rolls, parish rolls, tide tables, bone-carver licenses." A flicker, there, so fast Brannic might have imagined it. "Every document is a thread. Every thread, pulled, leads somewhere. My task is to determine which threads lead *out* of the pattern, and to remove them. The rest we leave. A country may keep its ledgers. It may not keep its exits."

"Exits, sir?"

Corvane looked up. For a moment the tax-assessor eyes were something else entirely, and Brannic felt the temperature of the room change the way it changes before snow.

"You were demoted at Lowmark," Corvane said. "For asking, in writing, why eastern parish records were being destroyed. I have read your letter. It was well written. Careful. You used the proper channels. You even proposed an alternative—micrographic storage, wasn't it? Very tidy." He closed the folio. "Do you know what happened to those records, Corporal? The ones you tried to save?"

"They were burned, sir."

"They were *processed*. Three hundred and forty-one names, removed from the record because each of those names appeared in correspondence with a party outside the empire's approved channels. Smugglers, mostly. Some foreign correspondents. One woman who wrote letters to her sister across the border in Kessrin." He said it without heat, without cruelty, the way a man reports rainfall. "Lowmark's eastern parishes do not exist anymore, Corporal. Not the people—the *parishes*. Ask anyone there where Marlowe Street was. Ask them their neighbor's name from six years ago. They'll tell you the street was always called something else, and the neighbor moved away, and isn't it strange, they can't recall to where." He smiled faintly. "That is processing done thoroughly. Your objection delayed it by nine days. Nine days, and three hundred and forty-one families lived nine extra days inside the record. Was that worth your sergeant's stripes?"

Brannic heard himself say: "I don't know, sir."

"Honesty." Corvane nodded, as if Brannic had passed some small test. "Most men would have said yes. Most men would have said *of course, sir, human dignity transcends rank*, and meant nothing by it. You don't know. Good. Uncertainty is the beginning of clerkship. A clerk who knows everything stops recording accurately." He slid the folio across the desk. "Your first assignment. The seizure lists. Transcribe, certify, index. Names of persons of interest in Greymarrow and the surrounding coast. Cross-reference against guild rolls. Bone-carvers especially—I want every licensed carver in Thornfell on a single page by week's end, with their current whereabouts."

"Bone-carvers, sir?"

"A hobby of mine." The smile again, thinner. "Old countries keep old crafts. Old crafts keep old memories. Go."

* * *

The work was worse than Brannic had feared, and he had feared it with a professional's imagination.

The seizure lists came in all day, carried by runners from the harbor offices, the customs house, the garrison, the temple of the Concordance which had stood in Greymarrow for longer than the empire had existed and which had, within six hours of Corvane's arrival, discovered a sudden enthusiasm for cooperation. Each list was a town distilled into ink: names, trades, addresses, associations, debts, denunciations. Half the denunciations were neighbors settling scores—the cobbler against the tanner over a boundary wall, a widow against the man who held her mortgage—and Brannic transcribed them all with the same neutral hand, because that was what clerks did, because neutrality was the grease that let the machine turn, because—

Because the alternative was Lowmark again, and he had already paid for Lowmark once.

By the third day he had stopped reading the names as people. That was the trick of it, he realized. That was how the machine actually worked—not the Pale Engine in its oilcloth wrappings, whatever that was, but this: the lists themselves, the sheer volume, the way a name became a line became a column became a page became a bale became a crate, until the person at the start of it was so far upstream you couldn't see them anymore. He caught himself doing it on the morning of the fourth day, certifying a list of forty-six residents of the Saltrow district for "memory audit," and he made himself read each name aloud in his head, slowly, the way you'd read a roll of the dead. It took him twenty minutes. The runner waited, bored. Brannic certified the list. Forty-six people, processed in twenty minutes, plus the time it took to feel bad about it, which the schedule did not include.

It was near dusk on the fifth day when he found the name.

He was cross-referencing the carver rolls, as ordered—Greymarrow's guild registry, copied in a clerk's crabbed hand, annotated with the seizure officers' additions. Most of the entries were routine. *Marrow & Sons, scrimshaw, licensed 1811, premises on Anchor Lane, cooperative.* *Widow Tull, reliquary work, licensed 1798, deceased 1821, license lapsed.* And then:

*Eastwick, H. Bone-carver, licensed 1819. Premises: Lantern Court, off Saltpan Row. No guild dues in arrears. No political record. No family registered. Note: subject known to take private commissions; clientele undocumented.*

And beside it, in fresh red ink, in a hand Brannic did not recognize but understood immediately, because it was the hand of a man who wrote orders:

*Priority. Retain for interview. Do not process locally.*

Brannic sat very still.

There was nothing remarkable about the entry. That was what struck him, afterward, when he tried to reconstruct the moment—there was nothing in it to explain what happened next. Eastwick, H. An initial, not even a full name. A carver of keepsakes in a smuggler town, the kind of artisan every coastal settlement had two or three of, the kind whose work ended up in sailors' pockets and widows' drawers: a knucklebone carved with a ship, a vertebra drilled and strung on a cord. He had seen a hundred such trinkets. He owned one himself, though he had never told anyone that—a finger-bone, carved with a mountain range, bought off a stall in Lowmark the week before the burning, back when he still believed souvenirs were innocent. He had kept it through the demotion, the transfer, the year of silence. He had never been able to say why.

*Retain for interview. Do not process locally.*

Why would the Inquisitor-General put his own red ink on a bone-carver?

Brannic looked at the entry for a long time. Around him the office went on: runners arriving, lists stacking, another clerk's pen scratching, the lamp being trimmed. Somewhere below, in the harbormaster's cellar, the wrapped machine hummed. He had been told it was a records press. He had heard it at night, though, and records presses did not hum like that—did not hum at all, in fact, and certainly did not make a sound like a room full of people breathing very quietly.

He dipped his pen. He transcribed the entry, exactly, completely, including the red-ink annotation, because omitting it would be discovered, and discovery would end not in demotion but in the cellar, and he was not brave enough for the cellar. He was honest enough with himself to know that. Five years in the empire's service had taught him precisely how brave he was: brave enough to write a letter, not brave enough to burn one.

But when he finished the transcription, he did one small thing. He hesitated over the certification line—the line where the clerk swore the copy was true and complete—and for the space of four breaths, he did not sign it.

Four breaths. Then he signed, because he had to, because the runner was waiting, because he was Corporal Vannic and Corporals signed things. But in those four breaths something had shifted in him, some load-bearing wall of certainty developing its first crack, and he understood with terrible clarity that the crack would not close. Cracks like that only widened. He had watched it happen at Lowmark, in himself, in the nine days between writing the letter and receiving the answer. Duty was a thing you could carry for years without examining, the way you could carry a stone in a pack without weighing it, and then one day you set the pack down and found the stone had grown teeth.

*Eastwick, H.*

He finished the day's work. He ate in the mess with the other clerks and said nothing. He lay in his bunk in the requisitioned barracks and listened to the machine humming beneath the floor, that sound like held breath, like a congregation, and he took out the finger-bone from Lowmark and turned it in his fingers. The little carved mountains caught the lamplight. Whoever had cut them had known stone and knew bone better; the ridges were fine as frost ferns. He had never wondered who made it. He wondered now, for the first time, whether the maker had a name, and whether the name was still written anywhere, and whether—when the lists reached Lantern Court, when the red ink found its subject—that maker would be retained for interview, or processed, or simply erased, street and shop and trade and all, until the whole town agreed it had never been.

The bone was warm in his hand. He could have sworn it was warm. Bones stayed at the temperature of the room; he knew that; everyone knew that. But it sat in his palm like a small sleeping animal, and when he held it to his ear—foolish, absurd, a child's gesture—he heard nothing, of course, nothing at all.

Except that the nothing had a texture. Like listening at a door.

He put the bone away. He slept badly. In the morning there were new lists, new names, new red ink, and the work went on, and he told himself the hesitation had been fatigue, and believed it for almost the whole day.

Then Corvane summoned him at dusk, and the Inquisitor-General was standing at the window with the carver rolls open on the desk behind him, and he said, mildly, without turning around:

"Corporal. Tell me what you know about the Eastwick carver."
