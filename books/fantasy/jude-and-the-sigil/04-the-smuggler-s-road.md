## 4. The Smuggler's Road

Hazel left Greymarrow before the lamps were lit.

They had learned long ago that the best time to vanish from a town was the hour when the living were busiest becoming themselves again—bakers sweating over ovens, dockhands arguing about tide and wages, children being shouted awake. In that hour, no one looked at a slight figure in a hooded coat carrying a satchel of bone-dust and grief. No one remembered them later. That had always been the point.

Tonight it was the point of survival.

The stolen book lay against their ribs, wrapped in oilcloth, heavy as a second heart. *The Eastwick child.* Their own name, or close enough to it, written in ink that had dried before their grandmother was born. Hazel did not let themselves think about it. Thinking about it made the road tilt under their feet. They thought instead about the practical things: the checkpoint at the Grey Bridge, the two days of hard walking to the pass, the fact that they had told no one where they were going, which meant no one would come looking, which meant—

Which meant they were alone. As designed. As always.

The dead woman's fingerbone sat warm in their left pocket. Maren Colle, drowned in the harbor thirty years past, her secret finally carved free of her by Hazel's knife: *the Door, the Door, they'll come for the Door.* She had been guiding them all night without meaning to—a faint pull at the edge of Hazel's gift, like a fisherman's line gone taut. The dead of Thornfell knew the corpse-roads. They had walked them dying.

"Show me," Hazel murmured to the bone, and felt the answer as a cool pressure behind their sternum, pointing north and slightly wrong—away from the high road, away from anywhere a cart could go.

Good. Carts meant soldiers.

* * *

The corpse-roads were not roads for the dead, whatever the smugglers' superstitions said. They were roads *of* the dead—the paths Thornfell's people had taken in flight during the Vaelish incursions, a century ago and more, worn into the mountainside by ten thousand terrified feet and then abandoned, unmapped, unremembered except by the bones scattered along them. An empire could erase a name from a ledger. It could not erase the way a body flinches downhill toward water, or the cairns grieving mothers stacked over shallow graves, each stone still humming faintly with the name it carried.

Hazel heard those names as they walked. That was the trouble with the corpse-roads: they were loud.

A child here, calling for a goat that had wandered off an hour before the soldiers came. A man there, repeating a debt he'd owed and never paid, as if the arithmetic might save him. An old woman singing a lullaby in a dialect so old that even Hazel, who collected such things the way other people collected coins, only caught every third word. They didn't know Hazel was listening. They never did. They simply went on being what death had made of them, and Hazel walked through the chorus with their jaw tight and their gift pulled in close around them like a cloak drawn too thin.

By midmorning they had climbed above the treeline, following Maren's bone along a shelf of rock that no living surveyor had ever recorded. Below and behind, Greymarrow shrank to a smudge of smoke on the grey sea. Somewhere beyond the eastern ridges, invisible but enormous, Thornfell proper went on existing—villages, passes, the capital whose name Hazel's own mouth kept misplacing lately, reaching for it and finding fog. The erasure was spreading. Hazel could feel it now that they knew to feel for it: a smoothness in the world, like a page rubbed blank.

They ate hard bread and harder cheese on a ledge overlooking a valley full of nothing. The wind smelled of snow coming early. Hazel wrapped the prophecy book out again despite themselves, read the same line for the dozenth time:

*And when the pale hand comes to eat the names, the Eastwick child shall stand at the Hollow Door, key and keeper both, and the choice shall be theirs alone.*

Eastwick. It wasn't even a common name. There had been Eastwicks in the hill villages once—Hazel's mother had claimed kinship with a family near Harrowmere, though she'd been vague about it in the way she'd been vague about everything. Had some archivist a century ago plucked the name out of the air? Or had someone in Hazel's line known, all along, what the blood was for, and said nothing?

"You could have warned us," Hazel told the book, and the book, being paper, declined to answer. But the fingerbone in their pocket pulsed once, cold then warm, and Hazel got the distinct impression of a dead woman rolling her eyes.

"All right," they muttered. "I'm moving."

They were moving when the bells started.

* * *

Greymarrow's alarm bells could be heard for miles on a clear day, and this day was painfully clear. Three strikes, pause, three strikes—the signal for a fugitive, not a fire. Hazel stood very still on the ledge and watched, absurdly, as if the town might explain itself across eight miles of open air. Then the wind shifted and brought the sound properly, and beneath the bells Hazel's gift caught something worse: the specific, organized silence of soldiers moving through streets, doors opening, voices being counted.

They hadn't run because of the bells. The bells confirmed they'd been right to run.

Maren's bone pulled hard north now, urgent, and Hazel ran.

The shelf of rock narrowed into a scramble, then a climb, then a descent through scree that shifted treacherously underfoot. Hazel fell twice and got up twice, palms bleeding, the satchel clutched to their chest. By late afternoon they could see the Grey Bridge—or rather, they could see why the corpse-roads mattered, because the bridge was the only crossing of the Greywater gorge for twenty miles, and the empire had built its checkpoint squatting on it like a spider on a thread. Watchtowers at both ends. A chain across the roadway. Lanterns already lit against the dusk.

Every traveler out of Greymarrow would funnel through that checkpoint. Every traveler would be asked for papers, for names, for reasons—and the names would be written down, and written-down names could be checked against seizure lists, and Hazel's name was on one.

"There has to be another way," Hazel whispered, and Maren's bone answered by pulling them off the trail entirely, down a goat track toward the gorge itself.

The Greywater ran deep and fast between walls of black rock. Where the trail ended, a rusted iron ladder descended the cliff face—smugglers' work, decades old, most of its rungs gone to orange flakes. At the bottom, half-submerged, the bones of an old footbridge jutted from the water: pilings, a few rotted beams, and nothing else. On the far side, forty feet of freezing current and slick stone.

Hazel stood at the top of the ladder and did the arithmetic of drowning. They couldn't swim well. The water would be snowmelt. The far bank was a scramble of wet boulders in failing light. And behind them, down the trail, they could hear dogs.

Not metaphorical dogs. Actual dogs, baying, and men's voices, and the crisp imperial cadence of orders given without heat. The patrol had turned off the main road onto the corpse-road. Someone had tracked them after all—not the empire's maps, then, but a person. A traitor with a good memory. One of the smugglers who'd carried Hazel's carved messages for years, perhaps. Hazel filed the betrayal away to hurt about later, when there was time.

They went down the ladder.

The iron bit through their gloves. Halfway down, a rung gave way and Hazel dropped the last six feet, landing hard on wet stone, the satchel slamming their hip. The river's roar filled the gorge. Above, the first lantern-light of the search party spilled over the cliff edge, and a voice called down—*"Light! Light below!"*—and then boots on the ladder.

There was no crossing. Hazel saw that now with perfect clarity. Forty feet of white water, night falling, soldiers descending. They pressed themselves into a crevice in the rock and put their hand in their pocket and closed it around Maren's bone, because it was the only thing they had ever held that didn't leave them.

*I'm sorry,* they thought at the dead woman. *You died for nothing. I'm going to die in a hole in a cliff, and the Door will seal, and the empire will win, and no one will ever—*

"Hold still," said a voice above them.

Hazel nearly screamed. A figure stood at the base of the ladder where the soldiers should have been—tall, broad-shouldered, wearing a travel-stained soldier's coat with the insignia torn off but the tailoring unmistakably Vaelish. He had a crossbow slung across his back and a face that might have been handsome if it had ever learned how to relax. He was looking down at Hazel with an expression of genuine bewilderment, as if he'd arrived at his own actions a step behind them.

"They're on the ladder," he said quickly, glancing up. "Four of them. I told them I saw your light go the other way, along the rim—they'll waste twenty minutes checking it. It won't be enough." He crouched, unhurried, and began unbuckling his boots. "Can you swim?"

"No." The truth came out flat with shock.

"Then hold on to me and don't fight the water. Fight the cold, not the river. The river wins either way." He stripped to his shirt, wrapped his coat and boots and crossbow in an oilskin, and tied the bundle to his back with a soldier's quick knots. "I'm Brannic."

"Why—" Hazel's voice cracked. "Why are you helping me?"

Brannic paused. Hazel watched him actually consider the question, watch him turn it over like a coin he suspected was clipped.

"I don't know," he said at last, and sounded disturbed by his own honesty. "Ask me again if we live."

He held out his hand. Behind him, above, the search party had discovered the empty ladder and begun to shout.

Every instinct Hazel owned—the instincts that had kept them alive and invisible for thirty years—screamed that this was a trap, that no stranger helped for free, that a soldier's coat meant a soldier's loyalty no matter what he'd done to the insignia. But the dogs were close, and the crevice was a coffin, and the man's hand was steady in the dark.

Hazel took it.

* * *

The river tried to kill them twice.

The first time was the entry: the cold hit like a blow to the chest, and Hazel's body seized, and they would have gulped water if Brannic hadn't already had an arm locked around them, hauling them into the current's throat. "Breathe when I breathe," he shouted over the roar, and then they were swimming—or he was swimming, and Hazel was cargo, clinging to the strap of his oilskin bundle while black water hammered them against rocks and the far bank came at them in fragments.

The second time was a whirlpool off the bridge's old pilings, a slow hungry spiral that caught Hazel's trailing leg. Brannic felt the drag, swore, and drove them sideways with a strength that seemed to come from somewhere outside himself. His boot found a submerged beam. He pushed off it. And then, all at once, the river let go, and they were dragging themselves up wet boulders on the northern bank, coughing, shaking, alive.

Hazel lay on their back on the stones and laughed—a thin, hysterical sound they didn't recognize as their own.

"Don't," Brannic said, wringing out his shirt. "Laughing means you're thinking, and if you're thinking you'll realize we're still in sight of the checkpoint."

That sobered Hazel fast. They got up. The far shore glowed with lanterns; figures moved along the bridge; a dog barked once, hopefully, at nothing. Here on the north side there was only darkness, scree, and the beginning of the high country.

Brannic retrieved his boots from the oilskin and dressed with his back to Hazel, which they noted—he was giving them privacy, or performing the idea of it. When he turned around, he studied them openly for the first time, and Hazel felt the look like a lamp being raised in a room they'd kept dark for years.

"You're the carver," he said. "From the lists. Hazel Eastwick."

The name in his mouth landed differently than it had in the prophecy book. In the book it had been fate. In his mouth it was a warrant.

"And you're deserting," Hazel said. "In imperial territory. With a crossbow and no insignia." They hugged their soaked arms around themselves, teeth chattering. "So we're both running. The difference is I know what's chasing me. Do you?"

Something crossed Brannic's face—there and gone, like a fish under ice.

"I'm chasing you," he said. "Officially. I was Corvane's clerk until yesterday. My orders are to find you and bring you in." He said it plainly, without apology, and Hazel respected the plainness even as terror crawled up their spine. "Except I've just swum a freezing river with you instead of reporting you, so officially is doing a lot of work in that sentence."

"You could still turn me in. Walk back across the bridge. Say you lost me and found me again."

"I could." He looked at the checkpoint lights, then at the black mass of the mountains ahead. "Corvane doesn't want you brought in for questioning, Eastwick. I've seen the requisition orders for what follows. If I walk you back across that bridge, Greymarrow stops existing. Not burned—*unwritten*. I watched the engine work on a village last month. I demoted myself asking questions about it." His jaw tightened. "I don't fully understand what you are. But I understand what he'll do to get you, and I find I can't be part of it. That's the whole of my reasoning. It isn't much."

It was more than Hazel had expected, and less than they needed. Trust was a currency they had never carried; it weighed too much and spent badly. But the river was behind them, the dogs were behind them, and the corpse-roads ahead were ones only the dead knew—and apparently, tonight, the dead were willing to share.

"The next shelter is a cave two miles up," Hazel said, deciding as they spoke. "There's a spring. We can't have a fire until we're over the ridge." They hesitated, then held out the hand that wasn't holding the satchel—an awkward, dripping offering. "Hazel. Though you knew that."

"Brannic Vael-something-or-other. The family name's not worth keeping." He shook it. His grip was careful, as if he'd noticed they were shivering and was afraid of breaking something. "Two miles. Can you make it?"

"I've made worse with company I liked less."

They climbed. The scree gave way to heather, the heather to the first stunted pines, and the checkpoint lights sank behind them until the darkness was complete and honest. Twice Hazel consulted Maren's bone, and twice it pulsed them back on course when the path forked along routes no map had ever admitted to. Once, passing a cairn, Hazel heard a dead shepherd say *mind the third stone, it rolls*, and said nothing, and stepped wide, and heard Brannic mutter a curse as the stone they'd avoided rolled away under his boot.

"How did you know that?" he demanded.

"Lucky guess."

"That wasn't a guess. You changed course before anything happened." He stopped on the trail, and in the starlight his face was sharp with something between suspicion and hunger. "What are you, Eastwick?"

"A bone-carver," Hazel said. "Ask me again when I trust you."

They walked on. The cave, when they reached it, was dry-floored and hidden behind a curtain of pine roots, exactly as the dead had promised. Hazel built no fire but shared out bread and cheese, and Brannic ate like a man who'd forgotten food was allowed to taste like anything, and for a while neither of them spoke.

It was Brannic who broke the quiet, staring out at the stars.

"When I grabbed your hand at the cliff," he said slowly, "I didn't decide to. I want that understood. I've been a soldier eleven years. I don't act without orders—it's the only thing I've ever been good at. And then I looked down at you and my hands just... moved." He shook his head. "It felt like hearing something. Like an order from somewhere there's no chain of command."

Hazel's blood went cold and strange. Slowly, they drew Maren's fingerbone from their pocket, where it had ridden warm all day, and held it up between them. It pulsed in the starlight—once, twice—like a small patient heart.

"She drowned in Greymarrow harbor thirty years ago," Hazel said quietly. "Her name was Maren Colle. She's been pulling me north since midnight." They met Brannic's eyes. "I think she pulled you too."

Brannic stared at the bone. Then at Hazel. Then, briefly, at his own hands, as if they belonged to someone else.

"What," he said, "exactly, does a bone-carver's blood open?"

Hazel looked at the dark shape of the mountains, where somewhere ahead lay a flooded chapel, a keeper, and the first piece of a lock older than the empire.

"According to a hundred-year-old book I stole from a dying man," they said, "a door."
