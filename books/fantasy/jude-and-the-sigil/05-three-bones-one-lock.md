## 5. Three Bones, One Lock

The book was warm.

That was the first thing Brannic noticed, and the thing that unsettled him most. Paper should not hold heat. It should be as dead as everything else the empire confiscated—cold, catalogued, filed away in a drawer until someone decided whether to burn it or forget it. But when he lifted the archivist's copy of *The Vellum Hours* from the bottom of his kit, wrapped in an oilcloth he'd told himself was for waterproofing, it felt like lifting a sleeping bird.

He glanced at the tent flap. Beyond it, the camp of the Third Processing Company sprawled down the mountainside in ordered rows: cook fires, picket lines, the long grey wagons with their sealed doors. Somewhere out there, Inquisitor-General Corvane was dining with Greymarrow's remaining magistrates, explaining to them, in his gentle way, that their town had never been important enough to remember.

Brannic sat on his cot, set a shuttered lantern on his knees, and unwrapped the book.

He had stolen it three nights ago from the evidence wagon. That was what the manifest called it—*evidence*, as though a dying man's library were a crime—and he had signed the intake form himself, his clerk's hand neat and obedient, and then he had written the volume down as *damaged beyond retention* and carried it out under his cloak while Corvane's acolytes burned crates of Thornfell's records in the square for warmth and doctrine in equal measure.

He'd told himself he only wanted to read it before it burned. A small theft. A clerk's curiosity. The kind of thing a man could do once and then stop doing.

Now he opened the cover and stopped pretending.

The book was a collection of prophecies, or what passed for prophecy among Thornfell's mountain congregations—verses copied by hand across two centuries, each entry dated, most of them gibberish or weather-lore or the sort of hopeful nonsense people carved into doorframes against bad luck. Brannic had been raised on imperial catechism, not mountain verse, but he had served eleven years in this country and buried nine friends in its soil, and he knew how the Thornfolk thought. They believed the dead kept accounts. They believed names had weight. They believed, in their stubborn bone-deep way, that a country could not be erased unless everyone agreed to let it go.

The empire had spent forty years proving them wrong.

Brannic turned pages by lantern light. He was looking for one entry—one the archivist had recited with his last breath while Hazel Eastwick's knife moved through the old man's keepsake bone, one verse that named a date and a child. He hadn't seen it himself; he'd only seen Hazel's face after, in the moment before they both ran. He'd seen a woman—or whatever precise word fit Hazel Eastwick, and Brannic suspected even Hazel might have to think about it—look at the world like a floor that had just opened.

*The Eastwick child.* On a list. In his own handwriting, because Corvane's clerks transcribed every seizure ledger into the master rolls, and Brannic's hand was the neatest.

He found the entry on page 141. Dated 1823. The ink brown with age, the script cramped, urgent—the hand of someone writing fast, or afraid.

> *When the maps forget and the bells fall dumb,
> the last carver of the hollowed country
> shall hear the Door knock from within.
> Three bones bear the lock: the Drowned One's rib,
> the Antler Keeper's finger, the Refuser's jaw.
> The fourth is no relic and lies unburied:
> it is the key itself, worked and reworked
> by living hands, and answers to no blood
> but a bone-carver's. Seek them in Greymarrow.
> Seek them when the frost comes early.*

Brannic read it twice. Then a third time, slower, the way he'd been trained to read supply manifests—not the words but the structure underneath them, what was being counted and what was being hidden.

Three bones. Three keepers. *Drowned. Antler. Refuser.* It read like riddle-language, but it wasn't; it was a catalogue. Whoever wrote this had known about specific objects held by specific dead people, and had written them down in the order you'd need them. And the fourth piece—

*It is the key itself, worked and reworked by living hands.*

Brannic set the book on his knees and looked at his own hands, scarred knuckles, ink-stained fingers, the hands of a man who made lists for a machine that ate countries.

Worked and reworked. Carved. Hazel Eastwick was a bone-carver. Every keepsake, every memento, every whispering relic they had ever cut and polished and sold to grieving families in Greymarrow—all of it, all those years of quiet invisible work, had been something else entirely. Not craft. Not commerce.

Tuning.

The word arrived in his mind whole and certain, though he couldn't have said where it came from. Hazel Eastwick had spent their life tuning themselves, note by note, carving after carving, until some instrument inside them was ready to open a door. The empire had spent forty years erasing Thornfell from maps and ledgers and memory—and the whole time, the counterweight had been sitting in a smuggler town with chapped hands and a rented room above a tannery, listening to the dead.

And Corvane didn't know. That was the extraordinary thing. Corvane had the seizure lists, the informant reports, the Pale Engine humming in its wagon-train like a cathedral organ made of teeth—and he did not know what Hazel Eastwick was. He knew the name because the name had appeared in a confiscated book. He wanted the carver because the prophecy mentioned a carver, the way a man wants a key because the lock is described in a document he doesn't fully understand. If Brannic handed him the full structure—if he annotated the verse the way a good clerk annotates anything, cross-referenced, decoded, laid bare—

Corvane would stop hunting a fugitive and start building a weapon.

Brannic sat very still in the lamplight and listened to himself breathe.

* * *

He had questioned an erasure exactly once before, two years ago, and he still bore the demotion like a scar. It had been a village then—Lowmere, up-valley, ninety-one souls. The orders came through: process the parish records, dissolve the community, redistribute the population. Standard procedure for a settlement standing on ground the empire wanted blank. Brannic had asked his captain a simple question at the briefing: *What happens to the people who remember Lowmere?*

The captain had looked at him with genuine pity. *The same thing that happens to Lowmere, Sergeant. Nothing. There is nothing to remember.*

He'd been broken to clerk for it. Clerk to the Inquisitor-General himself, which everyone said was either a punishment or a test, and which Brannic now understood was neither. It was a filter. Corvane surrounded himself with men who had already shown the exact shape of their doubt, so he would always know where the cracks ran.

Two years of watching. Two years of transcribing. Two years of telling himself that a soldier's duty was to the chain of command, that the empire's methods were harsh because the peace was precious, that the men fed into the Pale Engine's wagons were processed rather than destroyed, and that *processed* meant something other than what it meant.

He had almost made himself believe it. He'd been proud of that—the discipline of it, the way a man learns to sleep in any weather.

Then he'd stood at a checkpoint in the rain and watched a fugitive in a hood look at him with terror and recognition in the same instant, and instead of raising his voice, he'd pointed west and lied to his own patrol, and the lie had come out of his mouth smooth as water. He still didn't know why. He'd told himself it was impulse, misfired instinct, nothing.

But lying twice—that was not impulse. Lying twice was a decision wearing impulse's coat.

Brannic took a fresh sheet of paper, dipped his pen, and began to write.

Not the verse. Never the verse in full. He wrote, in his careful clerk's hand, a memorandum for the Inquisitor-General's morning review:

*Re: seized volume "The Vellum Hours," item 114-G. Text is fragmentary mountain devotional verse of no operational value. Contains folk references to burial customs (drowned interments, antler-field cairns) consistent with pre-imperial Thornfell practice. No geographic data recoverable. Recommend retention for cultural archive, pending incineration quota.*

He read it over. Every word true. Every sentence shaped like a stone wall built to hide a door. The drowned interments—true, there were flooded chapels in the low valleys where the old congregations had sunk their dead rather than surrender them. The antler-fields—true, the high hermit pastures where they stacked the shed antlers of a hundred years into grey labyrinths. He had put both into the memo deliberately, because a half-truth that could be verified made the whole document trustworthy, and a trustworthy document was never examined closely.

And nowhere in it: *three*. Nowhere: *key*. Nowhere the plain fact that the book described a mechanism, and that the mechanism required a living carver, and that the empire currently possessed exactly one lead to that carver and it was a twenty-year-old nonbinary bone-carver with a smuggler's road map in their head and the entire chorus of Thornfell's dead apparently on speaking terms with them.

He sanded the page, folded it, weighted it with his seal, and set it on the corner of the desk where the morning runner would collect it.

There. Done. His first betrayal, delivered in triplicate-quality prose before midnight.

It should have felt worse. He waited for it to feel worse—the way you wait for pain after cutting yourself, probing the wound to confirm you're still capable of feeling things. Instead what he felt was lighter. Appallingly lighter. As if he had been carrying the empire on his shoulders for eleven years and had just, without ceremony, set down one end of it.

*You're a traitor now,* he told the dark outside the lantern. *Say it plainly, the way you say everything else.*

I am a traitor to the Vaelic Empire, he thought, and the words tasted like cold clean water after eleven years of dust.

* * *

The tent flap moved at dawn.

Brannic was already dressed, already at the desk, already the perfect clerk with his ledgers squared and his pen moving—but Corvane never announced himself, never used the flap like a visitor. He simply appeared in openings the way cold air appears, and suddenly he was inside, folding his long grey frame onto Brannic's stool uninvited, smiling his small courteous smile.

"Working late, Sergeant." Not a question. Corvane's questions were all statements; his statements were all traps; it saved time.

"The master rolls required reconciliation, sir."

"They always do." Corvane picked up a letter-opener from the desk—a bone-handled thing, seized goods, Thornfell work—and turned it over without looking at it. "Tell me about bone-carving, Kavanagh. You've been in this country longest of my staff. What does the trade actually mean to them?"

Brannic kept his eyes on his ledger. "Keepsakes, sir. Grief-work. They carve a token from the deceased so the family has something to hold. Superstition holds that the dead speak through the tokens. Folk belief only. No mechanism we've ever confirmed."

"No mechanism." Corvane smiled again, wider, and set down the letter-opener with its edge aligned precisely to the desk's grain—an unconscious habit, Brannic had learned, the tic of a man who needed the world squared to his hand. "And yet our fugitive carver fled Greymarrow the very night a dying archivist whispered a verse from a banned book. Coincidence has never been that well-timed, in my experience. Someone told them something." He rose, smoothing his coat. "The checkpoints are doubled. The passes will close within the week. Whatever hole this Eastwick crawls into, we will pour the engine in after them." At the tent flap he paused, and his voice dropped into something almost gentle. "Your memo last night. Thorough work. I find your thoroughness restful, Kavanagh. Do continue."

He left. The lantern flame steadied.

Brannic sat unmoving for a long moment, heart tapping slow and hard, and understood two things at once. First: Corvane suspected nothing about the book's contents—he'd accepted the memo whole, because Brannic's hand was neat and his doubts were already catalogued and therefore safe.

Second: Corvane had just told him, casually, in the language of logistics, that he intended to feed the Pale Engine into every hole in these mountains until it found Hazel Eastwick. Doubling the checkpoints. Closing the passes. Pouring the engine in.

The western corpse-road—the one Brannic had pointed Hazel toward in the rain—ran straight through the Drowned Valley. Brannic stared at the map pinned above his desk, at the little blue vein of flooded chapel-country where, if the verse meant what it meant, the first keeper lay holding the first bone.

Hazel was heading for it. Or the dead were dragging them there. With Brannic's help, unwittingly given in the rain.

And every checkpoint between here and the valley would be doubled by week's end.

He pulled the map down, spread it flat, and began, with a clerk's precision, to memorize which garrisons reported to which officers—which checkpoints were run by men who followed orders blindly, and which by men like him, men who had once asked a question and never stopped hearing it. Because if he was going to be a traitor, he intended to be a useful one, and the first useful thing a traitor can do is know the walls better than the jailer.

Outside, the frost came down early off the peaks, silvering the tents, and somewhere west along the corpse-roads, a bone-carver was walking toward a drowned chapel with three graves and a lock waiting at the end of them.

Brannic blew out the lantern, packed the stolen book against his chest, and started planning how to desert a man he hadn't yet admitted he was going to follow.
