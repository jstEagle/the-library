## 6. The Drowned Chapel

The chapel had been drowning for sixty years, and it had learned to do it beautifully.

Hazel saw it first from the ridge above the Vale of Sennet: a stone roof breaking the green water like the back of a sleeping animal, its bell tower tilted at a drunkard's angle, one arched window rising just above the surface so that on still evenings—so the smugglers said—the sunset filled it and the whole ruin glowed like a lantern sunk in amber. The villagers of Lower Sennet called it Our Lady Beneath. They rowed out once a year to drop flowers through that window. They did not swim there. They did not fish there. And when children asked why, their parents said only: *the water remembers.*

Hazel knew better than most what water remembered.

They crouched in the bracken at the treeline, three days out of Greymarrow with Brannic's absence like a bruise they kept pressing—*he went ahead to watch the pass, he said; he'd meet them at the waystation below the vale*—and studied the flooded chapel while the light went long and copper. The dead woman's fingerbone hung at their throat on its cord, Maren's bone, the first voice that had warned them, and it had been warm all day. Warmer as they'd climbed. Warm now almost to the point of discomfort, like a hand closed around theirs.

*Maren*, Hazel thought at it, the way they'd learned to think at bones, shaping the question carefully. *Is she here? Is this where you meant?*

The answer came not in words but in pressure—a tightening, a *yes* that felt like fingers squeezing.

Below, the chapel window caught the last of the sun and held it.

Hazel exhaled slowly and began to climb down toward the water.

* * *

They made camp in the boathouse of an abandoned ferry landing, half-collapsed, smelling of rot and old pitch, and spent the evening doing what they always did before a working: preparing themselves the way other carvers prepared their tools. They ate. They checked the little kit—wax plugs for the ears, though they both knew wax was a courtesy, a lie they told their body; tallow and a stub of candle; the flat river-stone they used for grounding, cool and real against the palm. They laid out Maren's bone and spoke to her properly, aloud, because some part of listening was manners.

"I need you to tell me about her," Hazel said. "The keeper. Before I go down."

Maren's bone was quiet a long moment. Then the voice came—not sound, exactly, but meaning arriving in the marrow behind Hazel's ears, wearing Maren's accent from a village Hazel had never seen, thick and patient as bread.

*Her name was Odile Vasse. She kept the western door before your grandmother's grandmother was born. She was kind. That was her failing, in the end.*

"Kind how?"

*She never turned anyone away. When the empire came up the vale with fire, she opened the chapel and took in everyone who ran to her. Forty-one souls. The soldiers found them there and barred the doors and broke the millpond's levee above the valley.* A pause, the particular quality of silence that meant the speaker was gathering something heavy. *She could have saved herself. She was the keeper; the water would not have taken her. She stayed because the others were afraid, and she sang to them until the water came over the windows, and then she sang to them under the water, and she has not stopped singing for sixty years.*

Hazel sat very still. Candlelight moved on the boathouse wall.

"They're all still down there," they said. "All forty-one."

*They are all still down there,* Maren agreed. *And they have been waiting, child, for someone who could hear them. Not me—I am too new, too small a voice. But you.* The warmth in the bone flared. *You are what they have been waiting for. Do you understand yet what you are?*

"No," Hazel said honestly. "I understand that I'm tired, and scared, and that tomorrow I'm going to put my head underwater in a church full of the drowned."

*Good,* said Maren, and there was something in her tone that might have been approval or might have been pity. *Go in honest, at least. It's more than most of the living manage.*

* * *

Dawn came up grey and windless, the vale holding its breath.

Hazel stripped to their shift on the ferry stones, waded in to their waist, and stopped. The water was cold enough to make their ribs ache, cold enough to feel less like temperature than like opinion—the vale clearly disapproving of them. Above, the tilted bell tower leaned against a sky the color of unpolished pewter. Below their feet, somewhere down in the dark, forty-one people were singing.

They could already hear it. Faintly. The way you hear a neighbor's music through a wall—not the melody, just the fact of it, a sustained human hum beneath the water's silence. Sixty years of it. Their gift had been growing since Greymarrow, since the prophecy, since the archivist's keepsake bone had recited their own name back to them in a dead man's voice; every day the world of the dead pressed a little closer, as if some membrane between the living and the otherwise was thinning everywhere at once. Or as if the dead were leaning in.

Hazel took the grounding stone in one fist, Maren's bone in the other, and dove.

* * *

The chapel's interior was a cathedral of green shadow. Light fell in slanted columns from the high window, illuminating drifting motes like slow snow falling upward. Pews stood in rows, furred with silt, their wood gone soft and black. An altar. A rood screen draped in weed like a mourner's veil. And everywhere, everywhere, the hum.

Hazel surfaced inside the nave, treading water beside a pillar, and let the candle-lantern they'd brought in its little brass-and-glass case throw its small stubborn circle of light. Then they did the thing they had come to do. The thing no sane person would do.

They opened their gift all the way.

Listening to the dead was usually a matter of tuning—one relic, one voice, the rest of the chorus dimmed to murmur by discipline and distance. Here there was no distance. There were forty-one dead within thirty feet of them, drowned mid-terror sixty years ago, and Hazel pulled open every door in their mind at once.

The chorus hit them like a wave hitting a swimmer.

It was not sound. It was *drowning itself*, forty-one times over, layered and braided: the cold, the dark closing, the weight of doors that would not open, the moment the water reached the lips—forty-one versions of that moment, each one distinct, each one *forever*. A farmer's terror that his wife was on the far side of the chapel. A girl counting her siblings' hands in the dark and coming up one short. A soldier—deserter, hiding among the refugees—praying to a god he'd mocked. Old Odile Vasse herself at the center of it, arms spread in the aisle, singing, her voice the thread that bound the others, the rope they all clung to: *I have you, I have you, I have you, none of you drown alone—*

Hazel's mouth filled with water.

Not really—they were breathing air, their head above the surface—but their body believed otherwise, seized with the absolute conviction of the dead that this was the moment, the water was here, the lungs were opening—and they went under thrashing, struck the pillar, tasted blood from their lip, and surfaced again gasping with the whole chapel screaming through them—

*I have you I have you I have—*

*the doors the doors the DOORS—*

*mama mama mama the water is in my—*

*—counting hands, four hands, four hands, whose hand is—*

*—let me out LET ME OUT I didn't want to die in a CHURCH—*

—and underneath it all, steady as bedrock, Odile's song, and Hazel understood with a clarity that felt like falling that the song wasn't comfort. It was *containment*. Sixty years of singing to keep forty-one panics from becoming one endless scream. The keeper keeping, even now, even drowned, even mad with it.

Hazel clamped both hands over their ears, which was useless, which had always been useless—the dead did not speak through the ears—and screamed back, wordlessly, into the noise.

And the noise stopped.

Not faded. Stopped. Forty-one voices cut off at once, the way a room goes silent when a stranger walks in, and in the ringing quiet Hazel hung in the green water shaking, blood threading from their lip, and heard—small, close, directly before them—a single voice.

*Oh,* said the voice of Odile Vasse, wonderingly. *Oh, you can actually hear us. Not around us. Not past us.* A pause, and then something that was almost laughter, sixty years rusty. *You're listening TO us. No one has ever listened to us. They listen near us, they listen despite us—*

"I'm sorry," Hazel gasped. "I'm sorry, I didn't mean to—it's too much, all of you at once, I can't—"

*No,* Odile said gently. *You can't. Not alone. That's rather the point.*

Something touched Hazel's shoulder. Underwater, in a drowned church, something with the weight of a hand but not the substance of one, and the cold of it was not the water's cold. Around them, one by one, the voices returned—but changed. Quieter. Ordered. The farmer spoke of his wife and only his wife. The girl counted four hands and then, patiently, explained whose they were. The deserter confessed, simply, that he had been afraid his whole life and the water had been the first thing that hadn't lied to him about it.

They weren't screaming anymore. They were *introducing themselves.*

*We have watched the listeners come and go for sixty years,* Odile said. *Bone-readers, ghost-mongers, priests with their censuses of souls. All of them used us the way one uses a well—lowering a bucket, drawing up what they needed. You are the first who fell in.* Another pause. *We have decided we don't mind.*

"The bone," Hazel managed. Their teeth were chattering; whether from cold or the dead, they couldn't say. "Odile. I've come for your sigil-bone. The Door—" They swallowed. "There's a Door, and it needs three bones, and yours is one of them, and if I don't take it the empire will burn everything that's left of Thornfell and then erase the burning."

Silence, long enough that Hazel's heart climbed into their throat.

Then, all around them in the green dark, forty-one voices spoke at once—but together now, harmonized, a chord instead of a cacophony:

*We know.*

"You—you know?"

*Child,* Odile said, and her voice was very old and very tired and very kind, *we have been dead for sixty years in a country that is being deleted. Do you think we haven't noticed? We felt the Pale Engine eat Lower Sennet's name last spring. We felt it take the road. We are the most attentive audience in the world—we have nothing else to do but pay attention.* The presence drew closer; the lantern-light bent oddly, as if something dense and invisible moved through it. *The empire thinks erasure is silence. Erasure is not silence. Erasure is a room full of people no one will listen to. We know exactly what that is.*

Hazel's eyes stung, and it wasn't the water.

"Then give me the bone," they whispered. "Please. Help me stop it."

*We intend to,* said Odile. *But not the way you think. Come down here, carver. Come down and see where we've kept it. And while you swim, listen—really listen, the way you just did, all of us at once, but gently this time. Because we have been rehearsing something for sixty years, and you are the first conductor to ever walk in, and you should know—*

The chord swelled softly around Hazel, forty-one voices braided into one, and the words of it settled into their bones like sediment settling into silt:

*—you should know that we are not haunting you. We never were. Every dead soul whose bone you have ever carved, every whisper, every warning, every secret—they were not echoes. They were applications. We have been recruiting you since you were nine years old, Hazel Eastwick, and today we would like to show you what we've been building.*

* * *

The sigil-bone lay in Odile's chest, where her heart had been, and getting to it meant diving the length of the nave through the ordered dark while forty-one dead held a light for her—or him, or them; the dead had stopped gendering Hazel within the first minute, having more important taxonomy on their minds—that was not light at all, but memory: each drowned soul kindling faintly along the walls, marking the route the way candles mark a processional path. Down past the pews where the farmer showed Hazel, briefly, tenderly, the shape of his wife's laugh. Past the rood screen where the girl walked Hazel through her counting until the missing fifth hand resolved into a small brother who had slipped out a window an hour before the soldiers came and lived to be an old man in Vael, and the girl's relief at this was so enormous that Hazel had to stop swimming and hang in the water and weep into their mask of breath.

Odile waited at the altar. Of course she did. Her bones lay composed in the silt behind it, arranged with care by whoever had found her after the waters receded from the upper gallery—arms folded, face turned up toward the window, and set into the hollow of her sternum, grown there or placed there or simply *belonging* there, a finger-length of bone carved all over with script so fine it looked like frost.

Hazel took it with both hands.

The moment their skin touched it, the chapel *sang*—not the chorus of panic, not the rehearsed chord, but something third: a single clear note that passed up through Hazel's arms, spine, skull, and rang in the roots of their teeth, and in that note Hazel heard—not metaphorically, not in the marrow-way, but heard, with ears full of water—the true name of Thornfell. Three syllables. They lost two of them instantly, the way you lose a dream, but the shape of the whole remained, vast and specific, a country's name pronounced correctly for the first time in Hazel's hearing.

When they surfaced, gasping, in the slanted columns of light, the chapel was quiet in the way a room is quiet after music. Warmth spread from the sigil-bone in their fist, meeting the warmth of Maren's bone at their throat, and the two resonances twined like hands clasping.

*Well done,* said forty-one voices, gently, from everywhere.

Hazel treaded water and laughed—a cracked, terrified, astonished sound that echoed off the drowned vaulting.

"You knew," they said. "All of you. In Greymarrow, on the roads, every bone I ever carved—you've been *steering* me. The archivist, Maren, the whole—this whole time I thought I was helping you. I thought I was the one doing the favor."

*You were,* Odile said. *Both things are true. That is what no one tells the living: help runs in both directions across that line, if you leave the door open.* A pause, and when she continued, her voice had an edge of something almost like apology. *One more thing, carver, and then you should go, because the empire's boats crossed the north ridge an hour ago and they are rowing for this vale with instruments that taste for listeners. You must be gone before they arrive.*

Hazel went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the water. "How do you know that?"

*Because,* said the drowned keeper of the western door, *the dead downstream have been sending warnings up the current all morning. We are not the only congregation, Hazel Eastwick. We are merely the choir.* The warmth of the bones pulsed, urgent now. *Take the south channel. Go quickly. And when you reach your soldier—because he is waiting for you at the waystation, and yes, we know about him too—*

"Yes?" Hazel was already swimming for the broken window, the sigil-bone pressed to their chest.

*—ask him what he buried at Vasser's Ford,* said Odile Vasse, *and watch his face when you do. Even the living have bones, child. Even the living have graves they dug themselves.*

Hazel hauled themselves out through the tilted window into the grey morning light, dripping, bleeding from the lip, holding a piece of a Door in one fist—and behind them, as they climbed the bank, the flooded chapel of Our Lady Beneath began, very softly, to sing.
