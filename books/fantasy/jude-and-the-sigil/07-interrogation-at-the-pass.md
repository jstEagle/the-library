## 7. Interrogation at the Pass

The village was called Harrowgate, though by nightfall no one would remember that it had ever been called anything.

Brannic learned its name from the sign at the bridge—a leaning plank of black oak with HARROWGATE burned into it in letters gone silver with weather—and he made himself repeat it silently as they rode in, because he had already begun to suspect that soon it would be the only place the name existed. He counted forty-one rooftops below the pass road, terraced down the mountainside like steps for a giant, smoke rising straight from a dozen chimneys in the windless morning. It looked like a hundred villages he had marched through in nine years of service. It looked like nothing worth destroying.

That, he understood now, was precisely the point.

"You're quiet, Sergeant." Corvane rode beside him on a grey mare too fine for the road, her hooves striking sparks off the stones. The Inquisitor-General did not look at him when he spoke. He looked at the village the way an accountant looks at a column of figures—measuring, totaling, finding it sufficient. "Most men are quieter before their first processing. You've chosen an efficient way to manage it."

"I'm not quiet, my lord. I'm taking notes."

"Good answer." Something that was almost a smile touched Corvane's mouth without reaching anywhere near his eyes. "You'll find this work requires exactly that quality. Precision. The empire is not cruel, Sergeant Brannic. Cruelty is waste. We simply correct errors." He gestured with one gloved hand at the mountains, the terraces, the smoke. "Thornfell is an error."

Brannic wrote: *Village cooperative. No visible defenses.* His hand was steady. He was proud of that, in the way a man is proud of small virtues while larger ones rot beneath him.

The villagers came out to meet them because there was nowhere else to go. They lined the bridge in their good coats—their *only* coats—and the headman, a broad old man with a smith's shoulders and a clerk's frightened hands, presented the village ledger with both arms extended like a man offering up his own heart. Behind him stood perhaps sixty people, and among them Brannic saw children holding their mothers' skirts and one old woman who looked directly at him, at his imperial grey uniform, with an expression he would spend the rest of his life trying to forget. Not hatred. Not fear. *Recognition*, as if she had known him as a boy, as if she were disappointed in how he'd turned out.

He had grown up two valleys east of here. That was the thing he never said aloud. These were not strangers. These were the edges of his own childhood, worn smooth.

*You questioned an order once,* he told himself. *One order. And now you're the pen in this man's hand.*

"Excellent," Corvane said, dismounting. "Sergeant, establish the perimeter. Standard pattern. Then bring me the shelterer."

"The shelterer, my lord?"

Corvane turned, and for a moment his eyes held something almost like delight—the delight of a teacher whose student has asked the right question. "Yes. Every village between Greymarrow and the pass has seen them. A bone-carver, traveling with a deserter. Someone fed them. Someone warmed them. Someone *looked at them* and let them walk on." He smiled fully then, and Brannic wished he hadn't. "Someone remembers them, Sergeant. That is what we have come to correct."

* * *

They set up in the village square, which took no time at all, because the Pale Engine did most of the work of arranging things itself.

Brannic had seen the Engine once before, at the erasure of Lowmarsh, and he had told himself afterward that memory had exaggerated it. It had not. It was a wagon-sized assembly of white metal and glass, unmarked, unadorned, humming at a pitch just below hearing—or above it; Brannic could never decide whether he felt it in his ears or in his teeth—and around it, the air behaved strangely. Shadows leaned toward it. Voices near it arrived slightly late, like words spoken across a wide river. When it powered up, snowflakes fell upward for six feet around it and hung there, turning, patient as moths.

The technicians—there were three of them, pale men in paler coats who never spoke unless addressed—unfurled cables and began feeding the Engine strips of paper. The village ledger. Parish records. Deeds. A christening roll going back a hundred and forty years. Each strip went into a slot at the Engine's crown and did not come out again, and each time one went in, the hum deepened by a fraction, like a throat swallowing.

"What does it do with them?" asked Corporal Vess, beside him. Vess was twenty-three, freckled, and had been assigned to Brannic's detail only that morning; he watched the machine the way boys watch storms.

"It eats," Brannic said.

"Eats what?"

"Everything the paper says." He kept his voice flat, professional, the voice of a man describing supply logistics. "Names first. Then the things the names were attached to. You'll see."

And then, because he could not help himself, because some part of him was still the boy from the next valley over, he added: "Watch the old woman in the blue shawl. Watch what she does with her hands."

Corvane worked quickly. He always did. He sat at a table brought out of the inn—he had requisitioned the inn's best room for himself and complained, mildly, about the mattress—with the headman across from him and two soldiers flanking the man's chair. Brannic stood at the table's edge with his notebook open, recording testimony, and it was testimony at first: dates, numbers, the names of travelers passed through toward the high passes. The headman answered well. He gave them a tinker, a peddler, two pilgrims bound for the antler-fields. He did not give them Hazel Eastwick.

"Master Heddan." Corvane's voice was gentle, almost kind, and that gentleness was worse than any shout Brannic had heard in nine years of service. "You're lying to me."

"No, my lord. On my mother's grave, no—"

"Yes," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "On your mother's grave. Let us visit her, shall we?"

He nodded to the technicians. One of them consulted a list and drew a single sheet from a folio—a copy, Brannic knew, of the parish record they had already fed to the Engine—and laid it into the slot.

The hum changed. It was subtle, like a tuning fork struck somewhere behind the world. Around the square the hanging snow rotated a degree. And in the front row of villagers, the old woman in the blue shawl stopped moving her hands.

Brannic had been watching her hands because watching faces was unbearable. They were knotted, working hands, a spinner's hands, and all morning they had been worrying a frayed edge of wool in her lap, twist, twist, twist, the rhythm of seventy years of winters. Now they went still. Then they began to move again—but differently. Searching. Turning over, examining, as if she had woken holding an object she could not identify and could not put down.

"My lord," the headman said, and his voice cracked down the middle. "What did you—my mother is buried under the rowan tree behind the forge, she has been there thirty years, I planted the tree myself—"

"Do you remember her name?" Corvane asked.

"Of course I—" Master Heddan stopped. Brannic watched it happen. He watched a man's face become a landscape through which something vast and silent passed, flattening everything in its path. The headman's lips moved. Formed a shape. Failed. Formed another shape. His eyes went wet and blank and terribly, terribly ordinary, the eyes of a man trying to recall where he'd left his keys. "Her name was—it was—it begins with—"

"Maren," whispered the old woman in the blue shawl. She was staring at her own motionless hands. "Maren. Maren. Why do I know—I don't know why I know—"

"That will do." Corvane closed the folder. "Thank you, Master Heddan. Now. The carver. The one called Eastwick. You sheltered them in the week past. Where did they sleep? Which road did they take?"

And Master Heddan—who had been a wall, who had been a mountain of stubbornness for two hours, who had lied with such steadiness that Brannic had allowed himself one flicker of hope—looked at the empty space where his mother's name had been, and broke.

Not loudly. That was the horror of it. He broke quietly, the way ice breaks under snow, giving way all at once and without sound, and he told Corvane everything. The loft above the smithy. The soup his wife had carried up. The carver's bandaged hands and the stranger soldier with the imperial bearing and the deserter's eyes. The corpse-road north, the one that wasn't on any map, the one his grandfather had shown him and his father had sworn him to secrecy over.

Brannic wrote it all down. His handwriting did not shake until the third line, and then it shook for every line after.

* * *

It was Vess who found him afterward, standing alone at the bridge, looking at the sign.

HARROWGATE. Silver letters on black oak. Brannic had taken hold of the plank with both hands—not pulling, just holding, feeling the grain of it, proving to himself it was real wood from a real tree in a real place—and he did not know how long he had been standing there when the corporal's boots crunched up beside him.

"Sergeant? The Inquisitor-General wants the records inventory by sundown."

"I heard him the first time."

Vess hesitated. He was young enough that insubordination still looked like courage to him rather than suicide. "Can I ask you something, Sergeant? Off the books."

"No."

"The old woman. Spinner. Blue shawl." Vess was looking at the village, at the smoke, at the ordinary evening beginning in the windows—supper fires, a dog barking, someone singing a child down from a rooftop. "After the second feed, when they took the marriage rolls. She came up to me. Asked me if I'd seen her son." His voice had gone carefully neutral, the voice of a soldier reporting weather. "I said I didn't know her son. And she thanked me. Thanked me, Sergeant, polite as church, and went back to her spinning. Except—" He stopped. Started again. "Except she wasn't spinning. There was no wheel. Her hands were just doing the motions. And she'd forgotten why, but her hands hadn't."

Brannic let go of the sign.

"She has a son," he said.

"Heddan's her son. Was. The record said—"

"The record's in the Engine." Brannic heard his own voice come out wrong, low and scraped, like a blade drawn across stone. "She doesn't have a son anymore. She has a shape in her house that brings her soup, and tomorrow she'll ask it politely who it belongs to."

Vess said nothing. Above them the pass wound north into the darkening peaks, and somewhere along those heights, in a waystation or a smuggler's cave or a grave, Hazel Eastwick was walking with the dead, carrying three countries' worth of grief in a satchel, and Brannic had spent the entire day handing Corvane the map to find them.

Hadn't he?

He replayed the afternoon against the inside of his eyelids. The headman's testimony, transcribed word for word into his notebook. The corpse-road north. The waystation at Cold Saddle. And then the detail he had recorded faithfully, completely, and in one particular respect incorrectly: *the road forks below the shrine of Saint Verel; the eastern branch descends to the river; subject likely took the western branch.*

Likely. He had written *likely*. It was even plausible—the western branch was the faster route to the antler-fields, and everyone knew the carver sought the antler-fields. Everyone who read the prophecy, which meant Corvane, which meant the western branch would have watchers on it by dawn, crossbows and lanterns and the Pale Engine itself hauled up the switchbacks on a gun-carriage.

But the eastern branch descended to the river. And the river ran to the flooded chapel at Merrow's Deep, where the drowned lay in their hundreds under thirty feet of green water.

Brannic had never been inside a chapel that flooded. But he had grown up two valleys east of here, and every child in these mountains grew up on the story: *the keeper of Merrow's Deep sleeps in the water, and what she holds, she holds forever.*

Three keepers. Three bones. He didn't know how he knew it—except that he did know it, the way you know the shape of your own hand, the way the old woman's hands had known spinning after her mind forgot why. Somewhere in the archives he had processed last month, somewhere in the thousands of pages he had catalogued and copied and *not destroyed*—he had filed a duplicate set under a false seal in the sub-basement, an act so small at the time he had barely admitted it to himself—there was a book. An old book. And he had read it twice, and both times a line had caught in him like a fishhook:

*The Door requires three bones held by three dead keepers, and the fourth piece is living.*

He had told no one. He had written nothing down. And today, when Corvane demanded the full transcription of Heddan's testimony, Brannic had handed it over—every word except four.

*Sergeant,* Corvane had said at supper, not looking up from his maps. *Your assessment. Which road does our carver take?*

*Western, my lord. Almost certainly. It's the direct route to the fields.*

Almost certainly. Two words, and between them, a country's width of space for a carver and a river and a drowned woman's keeping.

"Sergeant?" Vess was still there. "You've gone strange. Stranger."

"Get back to the square, Corporal." Brannic picked up his notebook, weighed it in his hand—the weight of everything he'd written and everything he hadn't—and turned north, toward the light spilling from the inn where Corvane planned tomorrow's hunt. "Tell the Inquisitor-General I'm compiling the inventory."

He climbed the inn stairs slowly. In the room they'd given him—a cell, really, with a cot and a desk and a window overlooking the bridge—he unlocked his dispatch case and took out the duplicate archive key he had cut three weeks ago from a wax impression and told no one about, and he held it up to the lamplight, and he watched his own reflection bend in its teeth.

Nine years he had served. Nine years of orders, most of them bearable, some of them ugly, all of them *explainable*—and he had explained them, God help him, right up until the day a machine ate an old woman's son out of her head and left her thanking a stranger for his absence.

Loyalty, his father used to say, is a rope. It holds, or it hangs you. The trick is knowing which end you're tied to.

Below the window, the last light left the bridge, and the sign that said HARROWGATE became invisible in the dark, though the letters remained. Though the wood remained. Though somewhere in the village an old woman sat with empty hands, making the motions of a craft whose reason had been eaten, waiting for a son she would not recognize in the morning.

Tomorrow Corvane would march his watchers west. Brannic would file his inventory, polish his obedience, and lose one page of it—an accident, regrettable, the eastern fork gone missing between the desk and the archive.

And then he would desert. Not tonight. Tonight he would be a clerk, precise and loyal and dead-eyed. But the decision had been made somewhere between the bridge and the stair, quietly, the way ice gives way under snow, all at once and without sound.

He blew out the lamp and sat in the dark, listening to the Engine hum in the square below, swallowing paper, swallowing names.

*Eastern branch,* he thought, toward the dark where Hazel Eastwick walked with the drowned. *Take the eastern branch. Please. Take the river.*
