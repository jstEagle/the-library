## 8. Ash and Antler

The antler-fields began where the road gave up.

Hazel had been climbing for two days by then, following the corpse-road that wound out of the drowned valley and into the high country, the dead woman's fingerbone strung on a cord at their throat like a second pulse. The bone had gone quiet since the chapel—since the chorus of the drowning-dead had poured their testimony through Hazel's skull and left them hollowed, wrung out, new. Quiet, but not silent. It hummed now when Hazel walked in the right direction, a faint warmth against the sternum, the way a sleeping animal leans toward its keeper's hand.

North and up. That was all it would tell them. North and up, until the trees thinned to scrub and the scrub thinned to nothing but stone and wind and the pale gold grass that grew in the cracks like something patient.

And bones. Everywhere, bones.

The antler-fields of Thornfell were not fields of grain. They were a graveyard three centuries deep, where the old herds had come to die and where, after the wars, people had brought their dead to lie among them rather than surrender them to imperial charnel-houses. Elk and reindeer antlers lay half-buried in the grass by the thousand, bleached grey-white, branching like frozen lightning. And threaded among them, if you knew how to look—and Hazel always knew—the smaller shapes. Ribs. Skulls. Fingerbones curled like ferns before they unfurl.

A whole country's grief, composted into a hillside.

Hazel stood at the edge of it with the wind pulling at their coat and felt the field *notice* them.

It was not like the chapel. The drowning-dead had screamed; these dead did not scream. But as Hazel took the first step onto the slope, a pressure gathered around them, soft and immense, like standing in a crowd that has decided not to speak. A thousand voices holding their breath at once. The bone at Hazel's throat warmed.

"I know," Hazel murmured, to no one, to everyone. "I'm looking for one of you. Just one."

The pressure eased—not gone, but rearranged, the way a room rearranges itself around an expected guest. Somewhere upslope, beneath six feet of turf and three hundred years of weather, something turned over in its sleep and waited to be found.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen cairn, and it was there the hermits found them.

Hazel woke to stillness—which was wrong, because the wind on the high fields never stopped—and opened their eyes to four figures standing in a loose crescent around the fire's ashes. Grey-robed, hooded, faces shadowed. They had made no sound coming across open ground. Either they moved like snowfall, or Hazel had slept deeper than they'd meant to, and both possibilities sat cold in their chest.

One of them held a staff crowned with an antler so old it had gone the color of ivory.

"You're on our ground," the staff-bearer said. Her voice was a woman's, low and unhurried, worn smooth like river stone. "The Order keeps the fields. We keep what sleeps here."

"I'm not here to rob graves." Hazel sat up slowly, hands visible, the way you showed a strange dog you meant no harm. "I'm a carver."

"We know what you are." Another voice, younger, from the figure on the left. "Word came down the mountain two days past. A carver walking the corpse-roads alone, asking after keepers. The empire has a name for you."

"And what does the empire say I am?"

"Property," the abbess said simply. "Unclaimed. To be seized with the rest of Thornfell's records." She tilted her head, and the hood fell back enough to show her face: sixty years of weather carved deep, and eyes that were closed, and had clearly been closed for a very long time. Blind. She looked at Hazel anyway, straight on, with the terrible accuracy of the sightless. "But we are not the empire. We remember old Thornfell here. Every name. Every grave. Even the ones the maps have forgotten ever held anything at all."

Hazel's mouth was dry. "Then you know why I've come."

"The second keeper." The abbess said it without ceremony, the way you'd name a neighbor. "Sister Veyra. She kept the western sigil for forty years and asked, at the end, to be laid in the herd-ground with her face to the Door." Something moved behind her closed lids. "You want her bone."

"I need it."

"Need." The abbess tasted the word. "Come, then, Eastwick child. Walk with me. The others will mind your fire."

The phrase *Eastwick child* went through Hazel like a splinter finding its depth. They hadn't told anyone their name since Greymarrow. Hadn't spoken it aloud in weeks. But the prophecy book burned in the bottom of their pack, and apparently its words traveled faster than they did.

* * *

The abbess walked the antler-fields barefoot, which should have been impossible in the frost, and navigated by means Hazel couldn't see—her staff tapping once, twice, always finding the gap between the buried skulls, never disturbing so much as a blade of grass. Hazel followed a pace behind, and the field's held breath followed with them.

"You hear them," the abbess said. It wasn't a question. "The dead. All of them?"

"All who leave something carved. Bone mostly. Sometimes antler, sometimes shell. If a hand shaped it with meaning, I can read the shape."

"And the chapel." The abbess's staff paused over a depression in the turf—a grave, Hazel realized, one of thousands. "You went into the water for the first sigil. Word travels even up here." She turned her blind face. "How many voices?"

"Dozens. Maybe more. They'd been waiting under the flood for—" Hazel stopped. Swallowed. "For someone to listen. A long time."

"And you listened to all of them at once."

"Yes."

The abbess nodded slowly, as though Hazel had answered some question more important than the one she'd asked. "Most carvers can hold one voice. Two, if they're strong, and they pay for it with nosebleeds and nights without sleep. You held a congregation." She resumed walking. "Do you know why?"

"Because I'm the last key," Hazel said. "Because the prophecy says so."

"No." The word came flat and certain. "Prophecies are just gossip about the future, child, and gossip is usually wrong. I mean something older. The dead don't give their strength to just anyone who asks. They chose you. There is a difference between being born to a thing and being *given* it, and the difference is everything." She stopped at last, at a place where the antlers grew dense as a thicket, white branches rising from the earth in a tangle that might have been beautiful if you didn't know what else lay beneath. "Here. Dig where my staff points."

Hazel knelt. The ground was iron-hard with frost, and they dug with their knife and their hands, and the bone at their throat sang warmer with every inch. Three feet down, their fingers struck antler—then, nested inside the curve of it, wrapped in rotted linen and a prayer-knot of braided hair, a bone the length of a forearm, dense as riverstone, carved from crown to tip in script older than any alphabet Hazel knew.

The sigil-bone. The second piece of the lock.

It was warm. Not body-warm—*hearth*-warm, the warmth of a fire tended for centuries by hands that never stopped. And through the warmth, faint as a voice through a wall, Hazel heard her: Sister Veyra, keeper of the western sigil, forty years of vigil compressed into a single sustained note of attention. Not words yet. Just presence. Just *here, here, here*, the way a lighthouse says its one word to the sea.

"She's awake," Hazel breathed.

"She has been awake since the day the first map lost us." The abbess crouched beside Hazel with a grace that ignored her age entirely. "Now. Before you take her." She held out her open palm between them, an old gesture, older than the empire, older maybe than Thornfell. "There is a price on this ground. Not mine to set—hers. But I am her doorkeeper, and I ask the question she cannot. So answer me truly, Eastwick child."

The wind dropped. The whole field seemed to lean in.

"What would you trade for survival?"

Hazel opened their mouth—and the lie was already there, fully formed, smooth as a carved bead. *Nothing. My life only. Take my life if the price requires it.* The noble answer. The answer that cost nothing because no one could collect it, the currency of people who wanted to be seen as generous without ever opening the strongbox. Hazel had spent a lifetime learning exactly how to be unremarkable, and unremarkable people survived by knowing which truths to bury, and this truth—that they would trade almost anything except the thing that mattered—went into the ground of them so deep even they rarely found it.

"Nothing," Hazel said. "My life. Only my life."

The silence that followed lasted long enough for Hazel to count their own heartbeats. Eleven of them. Twelve.

The abbess smiled. It was not a cruel smile. That was the worst part.

"Liar," she said gently.

Heat flooded Hazel's face, shame like a physical weight pressing them down into the frost-hardened earth, into the neighborhood of the dead who had heard every word. "Abbess—"

"Don't apologize. Lying is how the living stay alive; I've buried enough of the honest ones to know." She rose, staff in hand, and looked at Hazel with those closed eyes that saw too much. "You would trade your life gladly, because your life is the cheapest thing you own. You've spent thirty years keeping everyone at arm's length so that losing you would cost the world nothing. No one to grieve you properly. No one whose grief you'd have to carry as a debt." She shook her head slowly. "But your gift? Your hearing? Ask yourself what you would do if taking the bone meant never hearing another voice again—dead or living. Ask yourself what you would do if it meant Brannic's voice going quiet to you forever."

Hazel flinched. "You know about him."

"I know the dead talk, and the dead have been watching you since Greymarrow." The abbess turned back toward the camp, gesturing for Hazel to follow, the sigil-bone still lying exposed in its grave between them. "So here is what happens now, child. I give you the bone anyway. Not because you passed the test—you failed it, honestly and completely—but because grace is not a reward. Grace is what falls on you when you deserve it least, and what you do with it afterward is the only test that counts." She paused. "But hear the warning, and carry it down the mountain with you:

"The Door will ask you the same question I asked. At the threshold, at the last moment, it will ask what you would trade. And it will know if you lie—it is made of the dead, and the dead cannot be lied to. Answer it true, or don't answer at all. A false bargain at the Hollow Door doesn't fail. It *inverts*. You get exactly what you said you wanted, in the exact shape you feared."

She began walking. Hazel scrambled up, frost burning their knees, and lifted the sigil-bone free of the earth—and the moment it cleared the soil, Sister Veyra's presence rose through it like heat off a summer stone, and the first words came, worn thin by centuries but unmistakable:

*"Carver. Little listener. They told me someone would come who hears the way I kept watch. Listen well: the third keeper walks. He refused his grave, and what walks refusing is dangerous to itself and worse to those who find it. And carver—" the voice gentled, and beneath the gentleness was pity, vast and ancient, "—when the Door asks you its question, and it will, do not look at what you'd lose. Look at what you'd be leaving it to."*

Hazel stood in the antler-field with the bone in both hands, shaking, the shame of the lie still hot in their throat and the grace of the gift heavier than any judgment could have been.

Behind them, the abbess's voice drifted back across the slope, almost lost in the wind:

"There's smoke on the southern pass, child. Engine-smoke. Whatever you're running from has learned to climb."

Hazel turned. Far to the south, below the tree line, a thread of grey rose against the morning sky—too straight, too steady, too deliberate for any hermit's fire.

The Pale Engine, eating its way uphill.

Hazel clutched the second sigil-bone to their chest, where it burned against the first, and started north at a run.
