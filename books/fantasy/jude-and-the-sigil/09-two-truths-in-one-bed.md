## 9. Two Truths in One Bed

* * *

**Hazel**

The storm found them an hour after dark, the kind of mountain storm that didn't fall so much as descend—white and howling and total, erasing the road, the sky, the difference between up and down. Hazel had been walking blind for twenty minutes, one hand on Brannic's pack strap because losing him in this would mean losing everything, when his arm shot out and caught them by the collar.

"There." He had to shout to be heard over the wind. "Light. Waystation."

It was barely that: a stone hut crouched against the slope, door swollen shut with frost, a chimney breathing smoke from some fire lit years ago by someone who'd never come back. Brannic put his shoulder to the door until it gave with a crack like a bone breaking, and they stumbled into a room that smelled of pine pitch, old wool, and mouse.

By the time Hazel got their boots off—the laces frozen stiff, their fingers too numb to work, so that Brannic ended up doing it without being asked, kneeling in front of them in the firelight like something out of a story no one told anymore—they were both shaking so hard the shivering had its own heartbeat.

The waystation was one room. One hearth. One cot, narrow as a coffin shelf, and a floor of packed earth cold enough to kill anyone who slept on it.

"I'll take the floor," Brannic said.

"You'll die on the floor."

"I've slept in worse."

"Where? A grave?" Hazel heard their own voice come out sharper than they meant, edged with something that wasn't anger. They were tired down to the marrow, and frightened, and the second sigil-bone rode against their sternum inside their shirt like a small cold animal, and the abbess's words from three days ago still rang in them: *You lied to me, carver. Grace doesn't care, but the Door will.* "We share it. Back to back. That's all."

He looked at them for a long moment. Firelight moved on his face, catching the new scar at his jaw where the checkpoint patrol's blade had kissed him, and Hazel thought—not for the first time—that he had the kind of face built for honesty, which made his lies more dangerous, not less.

"Back to back," he agreed.

They ate hardtack softened in snowmelt, and Hazel fed a little of their ration to the fire just to watch it flare, and said the dead woman's name under their breath—Maren, Maren of Greymarrow, who had died face-down in a smuggler's ditch and whose fingerbone still sang faintly in Hazel's pocket, a low note of patience. Old habit. The dead liked acknowledgment. So did the living, though Hazel had spent thirty-one years pretending otherwise.

Brannic watched them do it. He always watched, with an expression he probably thought was neutral and wasn't.

"Ask," Hazel said.

"Does it hurt them? When you listen?"

"No. It hurts me." They turned the bone over in their fingers. Maren's last secret hummed against their skin: *the Door, the Door, tell the Eastwick child about the Door.* Everyone's secrets were about the Door now. The whole country's dead had become one long sentence, and Hazel was the only ear left to hear it. "They're already finished hurting. I'm not."

"And you do it anyway."

"It's what I'm for."

The words came out flat and final, a door closing, and they heard it happen and hated themselves for it. But Brannic only nodded slowly, the way a man nods at a wound he recognizes.

They banked the fire and lay down on the cot, back to back as promised, the blanket pulled over both of them because there was only one and hypothermia made strange bedfellows of everyone. His spine was a wall of warmth against theirs. Hazel lay rigid, cataloguing the contact points—shoulder blades, the ridge of his hip, the accidental brush of his heel—and telling themselves the pounding in their own chest was fear.

Through the wall of him, faintly, they felt it when his breath finally slowed toward sleep.

They did not sleep. Above the wind, beneath the wind, threaded through the storm itself, the bones were talking. Both sigil-bones now, the drowned chapel's and the antler-fields', harmonizing in a register only they could hear, and underneath the harmony, like a bass line struck once and held, the drowned keeper's voice:

*Three bones, one lock, and the key is a hand that listens.*

And below even that, almost too deep to catch, something else—a phrase the prophecy book carried but Hazel had never let themselves read twice. The final clause. They knew where it sat on the page. They had skipped past it eleven times now, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth and flinches away.

*When the Door stands open, the key must be paid.*

Paid with what. They would not finish the thought. They rolled it back under like a stone.

Behind them, Brannic's breathing changed. Not sleep. The particular stillness of a man lying awake, listening to someone else lie awake.

"Hazel," he said quietly.

"Yes."

"The thing you're not saying. Is it going to get us killed?"

Hazel stared at the fire's dying coals. Outside, the storm screamed across the pass like a country crying out.

"Not tonight," they said. Which was true. And a half-truth. And the only kindness they had left to give.

* * *

**Brannic**

He woke before dawn to find Hazel gone from the cot and panic rising in him like floodwater before he saw them: sitting on the hearthstone, wrapped in the blanket, feeding sticks to embers. Alive. Just awake, the way soldiers were awake at this hour, or the way people were who had spent a lifetime waiting for the knock at the door.

He should have been asleep. Instead he'd dreamed of Corvane's voice, mild as milk: *"Sergeant Brannic. You'll keep the records. You'll write down what we unmake, so that someday no one can prove it was ever written."*

He'd been demoted for asking questions about Vaelmar Row. Nine houses, forty-three residents, a street that had appeared on every survey since the empire first drew Thornfell's borders—and then, in a single autumn, hadn't. He'd asked where the residents went. He'd been told there had never been any residents. He'd been told, gently, by men who genuinely believed it, that Sergeant Brannic was confused, that Vaelmar Row had never existed, and here were the corrected maps to prove it.

The horror hadn't been the erasure. The horror was watching his own memory soften around the hole, the way snow softens around a buried body, until he could walk past where the row had been and feel only a vague ache, like a tooth missing.

Only the question kept it alive. As long as he kept asking *where did they go*, the row stayed real somewhere, if only in the asking.

And then a clerk's assignment had dropped into his hands with a name inked on a seizure list—*EASTWICK, H., bone-carver, Greymarrow, hold for Inquisitorial review*—and he had looked at that name and felt the same ache, the missing-tooth ache, and understood that whatever the Eastwick child was, the empire needed them erased before they could be found. And he had walked off his post in the middle of the night with his commission folded in his pocket like a knife he hadn't decided to use yet.

Desertion. There was no softer word for it. Hanging offense, in theory. In practice, worse: the Pale Engine would eat his name within the year, and there would be no record that a man named Brannic had ever doubted anything.

"You're doing the thing," Hazel said, without turning around.

"What thing."

"Where your face goes somewhere else and you guard the door of it." Now they looked at him, eyes grey as slate in the firelight. "Soldiers do it. My father did it, after the war. You're doing it right now."

"My father was a quarryman," Brannic said, before he could stop himself.

"Was."

"He's alive. Probably. If the empire remembers him." He heard his own voice go rough. He hadn't planned to say any of this. The storm had boxed them in, the fire was warm, and Hazel Eastwick looked at him with the terrible patient attention of someone who listened to the dead for a living—as though nothing a person said could shock them, only interest them. "I deserted. Four days ago. You know that."

"I know you told me you were reassigned."

"That was—" He stopped. Breathed. "That was a half-truth. I'm told you deal in those."

Something flickered across Hazel's face. Wounded recognition. Fair enough.

"I told my commanding officer I was ill," he said. "Then I burned my transfer papers and walked north, and every mile I told myself I was going to turn back, and every mile I didn't. I don't know what I am now. That's the whole truth as of this morning." He fed a stick to the fire because his hands wanted occupation. "Your turn."

"My turn for what."

"Whatever you've been carrying since the abbess's field. You came down that mountain looking like a man who'd been shown his own funeral and couldn't decide whether to be flattered."

Hazel was quiet a long moment. Wind clawed at the door. When they spoke, their voice had changed—flatter, careful, the voice of someone reading from a document they hated.

"When I was nine, my mother took me to a bone-reader in Lowmarsh. She wanted to know if my gift was a blessing or a sickness. The reader held my hand over a lamb's knuckle and went white and wouldn't say. Wouldn't take payment either. Just told my mother to teach me to carve *small*. Keepsakes. Little things. Never anything with weight in it." Hazel turned their hands over, palms up, studying them like tools belonging to someone else. "She knew. Whatever she saw, she knew what a carver's hands can become. A weapon. Because that's what listening is, if you want it to be. Every dead person tells you where the bodies are buried. Sometimes they tell you where the living hid them. I could ruin anyone in Thornfell with an afternoon and a grave. I could—" They stopped. Started again, quieter. "Corvane wants me because I'm useful. Everyone who wants me wants me because I'm useful. And every night I ask whether the day comes when I stop choosing to be good, and simply am what I'm shaped like."

The fire popped. Brannic realized he'd been holding his breath.

"You gave water to a dying imperial scout at the pass," he said. "Left him your whole flask. I watched you do it. He'd have put a bolt through you an hour earlier."

"He was freezing."

"So are you. Right now, in ways I can't fix." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, close enough that the blanket between them warmed where their knees nearly touched. "I've served beside killers, Hazel. Real ones. They don't lose sleep wondering what they're shaped like. The fact that you ask is the answer."

"You can't know that."

"No. But I can bet my life on it, and I notice I already have."

Hazel looked at him then—really looked, the way they must look at a fresh bone, reading what lived inside it—and Brannic felt seen straight through, and discovered he didn't entirely mind.

* * *

**Hazel**

The storm held all day, and they held together in the one-room waystation, and by evening Hazel had run out of distance.

It happened stupidly. They were mending the torn shoulder seam of Brannic's coat—he'd insisted he could do it himself, and had produced stitches like a drunk spider's—when their fingers slipped, or maybe didn't slip, and found instead the heat of his neck above the collar. Neither of them moved. The fire ticked. Snow fell on the roof in soft tons.

"This is a mistake," Hazel said.

"Probably," Brannic said, and turned his head, and their mouths met.

It was not a sweeping thing. It was careful, and slightly desperate, and it tasted of hardtack and woodsmoke, and afterward Hazel pressed their forehead to his jaw and felt his pulse hammering there—hammering, honest, alive—in a way no carved bone had ever let them feel a heartbeat. The living, it turned out, had a music of their own. Louder. Worse. Better.

They shared the cot again that night, and this time not back to back. This time Hazel lay against his chest with his arm around them, listening to the great slow drum of him, and let themselves pretend, for exactly as long as the fire lasted, that there was a world after the Door where this was simply a thing they had.

"You're hiding something," Brannic murmured into their hair, near sleep. "Still."

"So are you."

"I know. I'm sorry."

"Me too."

Neither of them pushed. That was the fault line, Hazel thought, tracing it in the dark: two people telling the truth about everything except the one thing that mattered. He hadn't said why he really left his post—only that he had, only that doubt drove him, but Hazel had seen him read the stolen prophecy, had watched his finger stop on the final clause, had seen him close the book too quickly. And he had never asked what Hazel skipped past eleven times, though Hazel could feel the question sitting in him like a swallowed stone.

*When the Door stands open, the key must be paid.*

Somewhere south, beyond the storm, Corvane's engines would be chewing through another village's names. Somewhere east, the third keeper waited in the ruins of the capital—or walked, if the prophecy meant what Hazel feared it meant. And somewhere in the vault beneath the mountain, a door stood in the dark, wanting its key.

Hazel closed their eyes and listened—not to the dead this time, but to the man whose heart beat against their ear, steady as a drum calling soldiers home.

In the morning the storm broke, blue and brutal and clear, and far down the valley they saw smoke rising from the pass road in a thin, deliberate column. Not a campfire. A signal. Someone was telling someone else exactly where the road stood open.

Brannic shaded his eyes, and Hazel watched the color leave his face.

"That's an imperial column marker," he said. "Corvane's found the waystation trail. We have maybe two hours."
