## 10. Corvane's Offer

They took Hazel at the ford at Colder's Bridge, where the water ran thin and grey as old pewter and the corpse-road crossed the living one for forty paces—the only stretch of ground in the high passes where the dead had no say.

Hazel had known better. The drowned chapel woman had told them as much, her voice still pooled somewhere in the sigil-bone against Hazel's sternum: *never cross where the roads touch; that's where they watch.* But the second bone weighed in the satchel like a stone of its own making, and the antler-fields were behind them, and the storm coming down off the Spine would seal every pass but this one for a month. A month was longer than Corvane needed.

So: forty paces of ordinary road, and six soldiers rising out of the bracken on either side with the unhurried confidence of people who had done this many times and never once been interrupted by consequence.

Hazel did not run. Running confirmed value, and everything about surviving Thornfell—everything about surviving at all—had been built on being worth no one's trouble. They set down their satchel instead, slowly, and raised both hands, palms out, the universal grammar of *I am nothing.*

The sergeant looked at a paper, then at Hazel, then back at the paper, and his face did something complicated.

"Eastwick," he said. "Hazel Eastwick."

"Never heard the name."

"I expect not." He gestured with two fingers, almost courteously. "The General will want you comfortable. I'd take it kindly if you didn't make me carry you."

*The General.* The name moved through Hazel like cold water through cloth. Inquisitor-General Corvane, whose arrival in Greymarrow had emptied three streets and filled one grave in a single week, according to a dead smuggler whose skull Hazel had carved a year ago and who, being dead and therefore thorough, had kept count.

They bound Hazel's wrists with silk, which was somehow worse than rope. Silk meant orders from above. Rope meant men improvising.

* * *

The camp sat in the bowl of a valley that had been a village until recently. Hazel knew it had been a village because the bones said so—not dramatically, not screaming, just a low continuous murmur under the frozen mud, the way a hearth keeps murmuring after the fire dies. Wells that remembered hands. Cellars that remembered bread. A churchyard that remembered its own names and could find none of them to match the tents pitched among its stones.

At the center stood a pavilion of imperial white, absurdly clean against the ash-grey country, and before it, a machine.

Hazel had heard the Pale Engine described by frightened mouths all down the corpse-roads—a wagon-sized thing of pale metal and paler glass, humming at the edge of hearing, drinking names. Seeing it was different. It didn't hum so much as *subtract*, a silence shaped like a sound, and around its base the snow lay unmarked even where soldiers walked. Their footprints appeared a half-second late, as if the ground itself had to be reminded they existed.

A clerk fed it papers. It swallowed each sheet without tearing, and when the page came out the other side it was blank, and the clerk filed the blankness away in a case marked with numbers instead of letters.

"That's the archive," said a voice behind Hazel, "in the sense that an empty plate is a meal."

Corvane was smaller than Hazel had imagined. That was the first obscenity of him. You expected the man who unmade countries to be large; instead he was neat, grey-eyed, fiftyish, with ink stains on his fingers like any honest scrivener, and he looked at Hazel the way a carpenter looks at timber—not cruelly, not warmly, but with an assessment already complete.

"You're shorter than your reputation too," Hazel said.

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Approval, possibly. He waved off the guards. "Leave us. Bring tea for two." Then, to Hazel: "Do sit. My chairs are honest furniture. Nothing in this tent reports to anyone but me, and I report to no one at all, which is a freedom I recommend."

Hazel stayed standing. "I'd rather know what this is. If it's an execution, do it outside. Your machine makes this room feel like a held breath."

"The Pale Engine? It's nowhere near full power here. And it isn't aimed at you." Corvane poured the tea himself, which was another wrong note; emperors' hounds did not pour. "Sit or don't. I have taken the liberty of reading your file, such as it is. Would you like to hear what the empire knows about Hazel Eastwick?"

"No."

"Then I'll tell you what it doesn't know, which is briefer. It doesn't know anything. Bone-carver, Greymarrow district, no guild, no family record, no debts, no vices worth taxing. Twenty-six years of exemplary nothing." He sipped. "I admire it, frankly. Most people can't resist leaving a mark. You understood something most of my own colleagues never grasp—that invisibility is a discipline, and disciplines are rarer than talents."

"You've read my file and interrogated my neighbors for a compliment?"

"I've read much more than your file." He set down the cup and opened a ledger on the table between them. Hazel's stomach dropped before they saw what it was, because they recognized the binding. Dark leather, brass corners gone green, the archivist's copy—the book stolen from a dying man's rooms in Greymarrow, the book Brannic carried, the book that had ruined Hazel's life simply by containing their name.

It should have been in Brannic's coat. Unless Brannic—

"Before your imagination runs anywhere useful," Corvane said mildly, "your soldier friend is alive, at liberty, and currently believed by my staff to be tracking you toward the northern vaults. Whether he is actually doing that is between him and his conscience. I confiscated the book because he reads too slowly and I read fast." He turned pages with a lover's care. "1823. Anonymous authorship, which means either cowardice or prophecy, and in my experience the two are indistinguishable. Listen—I'll spare you the verse. Three keepers, three bones, a fourth key made of living craft. A Door beneath Thornfell that opens on—" he paused, delicately, "—well. The author says *the way out*. I have my own translation."

"And mine is the key."

"Your blood answers it. Or rather, your gift does. The key is a lock that must be *carved open*, and there is exactly one bone-carver left in Thornfell who hears the dead." He closed the book. "Which brings me to my offer, and I'll ask you to hear all of it, because I suspect you're the sort who wants the whole shape of a thing before deciding what it weighs."

Hazel sat. Not because they trusted him—because their legs had opinions, and because a seated prisoner listens longer than a standing one, and listening was how Hazel survived everything.

"Everyone believes the Hollow Door leads out," Corvane began. "Smugglers sell passage-rumors. Nationalists dream of armies returning through it. My predecessor's predecessor wanted it sealed; my predecessor wanted it mined into rubble. All of them assumed a door is for leaving." He laced his ink-stained fingers. "But doors are for *passing*. And I have spent eleven years studying what lies beneath this particular threshold, and I will tell you what I found, Hazel Eastwick, in the oldest stratum of records the empire hasn't yet digested: accounts. Ledgers. The founding treasury of Thornfell, sealed below the Door when the last kings chose memory over gold."

"Ledgers," Hazel repeated flatly.

"Ledgers, bullion, deeds, bonds on mines that are still producing—under new management, naturally. Enough wealth to buy a small country's survival twice over." His voice never rose; that was the horror of it, the reasonableness laid smooth as a planed board. "You think I came to burn Thornfell. I came to *appraise* it. Vael does not need Thornfell erased. Vael needs Thornfell *priced*. A vassal state pays tribute for four hundred years and everyone eats; a martyrdom feeds no one and costs a legion. I am offering you the difference between those futures, and all it requires is your hands."

"My hands open the Door."

"Your hands open the Door, we take the treasury, and I sign Thornfell's continuation with my own seal. Taxes, garrison, imperial schools—yes. Names, graves, songs—yours. The dead stay buried in their own language." He leaned forward. "You have been running through my country for weeks, carver, gathering bones out of grave-cold ground, risking checkpoints and worse, for a prophecy written by a dead romantic. Ask yourself what the Door has ever promised you except an exit you'd take alone. Now ask what I'm promising. Everyone. Alive. Staying."

And that was the poison of it: it *was* reasonable. Hazel had lain awake in waystations doing the arithmetic themselves—what is a country worth, what is a name worth, who counts the cost and who pays it. Corvane hadn't invented the question. He'd merely arrived at the answer with soldiers.

"You're forgetting something," Hazel said carefully.

"Name it."

"The keepers. Three dead people hold those bones, and the dead of Thornfell don't hand things to Vael."

"Ah." Corvane smiled, and it was the first genuinely unpleasant moment of the interview, because the smile was patient. "But the dead don't deal with me, do they. They deal with *you*." He slid a document across the table. Terms in fine script, a line at the bottom awaiting a signature. "You negotiate with your ghosts. I pay their price. Whatever they ask—masses said, bones returned to home soil, sins confessed in marble—you broker, I fund. That is what an Inquisitor-General is *for*, despite the folklore." He produced a pen. "Or."

"Or."

"Or my clerks finish processing this valley tonight, and tomorrow they process the next one, and the next, and in a year there will be no Thornfell to save at any price, because there will be no one left who remembers wanting to." He said it the way a man describes weather. "I don't prefer that outcome. It's simply the default. Defaults require no signature."

Hazel looked at the document. The empire's seal at the bottom was pressed deep enough to be felt with a fingertip, like a brand.

Somewhere under the frozen mud, the village murmured.

* * *

They gave Hazel a tent, a guard, and supper—actual meat, actual bread, the casual plenty of an army that ate well while it erased. Two soldiers sat outside the canvas flap. A third checked in hourly, young, freckled, nervous, with a chipped front tooth he tried to hide when he spoke.

"You're the bone-witch," he said, on his third visit, unable to help himself. "They say you talk to corpses."

"They say a lot."

"Is it true the dead know everything?"

Hazel looked up from the floor of the tent, where their eyes had been tracing the seams. "Only the parts they couldn't put down."

He laughed, then caught himself, glancing at the flanking guards, and left. His boots crunched away over ground that used to be someone's garden.

Hazel waited until the camp settled into its night rhythms—watch changes, horse-lines, the Engine's subsonic nothing humming at the edge of thought. Then they drew out the things they'd been permitted to keep, because Corvane was clever but he had never once in his tidy life been robbed by a dead man.

Among their tools, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a fingerbone. Not a sigil-bone. Something older and smaller: the knuckle of Tam Voss, smuggler, cutpurse, occasional hero, whom Hazel had carved in Greymarrow two winters past and who had died, per his own posthumous account, of a fall from a cliff he'd climbed drunk to prove the wine hadn't beaten him. Tam had been talkative in death. Tam had been, above all, a man who had spent thirty years talking his way past border guards, and who regarded authority as a kind of audience.

Hazel held the bone to their lips, the way one speaks into a shell to hear the sea.

"Tam."

The response came up through their palm, familiar as an old coat: dry, amused, slightly offended at the delay. *Thought you'd forgotten me.*

"Never. I need your voice."

*Whose ears?*

"Soldiers. Imperial. Young ones, tired ones, one with a chipped tooth." Hazel closed their eyes. "I need you to be a ghost with a grievance, Tam. Can you haunt on command?"

Silence, then a laugh with dirt and sea-salt in it. *Carver, haunting is the only work I ever loved.*

* * *

The trouble with Corvane's offer, Hazel thought, lying in the dark with Tam's bone warm against their jaw, was not that it was a lie. It might even be true—treasury, terms, tribute, survival. The trouble was that it was true the way a cage is true: perfectly constructed, entirely real, and built by someone else for their own purposes. Corvane would buy Thornfell the way he'd buy anything—at the lowest price the seller could be driven to accept—and the seller, in the end, would be Hazel, selling the dead.

Because that was the fine print no document contained. The Door answered to a carver's gift. Open it for Corvane and the opening was permanent; the empire's engine would walk through into whatever waited and begin its subtracting on the other side of history too. A vassal state with a name, perhaps—but a name issued by the empire, renewed annually, revocable. Survival as a leasehold.

The drowned woman had said: *the dead aren't haunting you, they're recruiting you.* And Hazel had begun, terribly, to understand the job description.

Outside, the sentry changed. Boots. The flap stirred.

"All quiet?" asked a voice—the sergeant's, from the ford.

"All quiet," said the younger one, the chipped-tooth boy. A pause. "Sarge—can I ask. The prisoner. Is it true what they're saying, that the General means to let them go if they cooperate?"

"It's true the General says so."

"But you don't believe it."

Another pause, longer. When the sergeant spoke again, his voice had dropped, and Hazel, listening the way they listened to everything, caught the fracture in it. "My mother's from Harrowmere. You won't find Harrowmere on any map now. I walked through it last spring and a man sold me eggs and didn't know his own street's name." A breath. "Get back to your post, Rell."

Hazel lay still in the dark, heart going hard.

*Rell,* said Tam's voice, faint and delighted, from the bone at Hazel's cheek. *Oh, that's a start.*

* * *

Dawn came up grey and thin. Corvane sent for Hazel at first light, and the tent smelled of ink and cold tea, and the document still lay on the table where it had spent the night, patient as a trap.

"You've considered," he said. It wasn't a question.

"I've considered."

"And?"

Hazel lifted their chin. "Ask your dead, General. Oh—forgive me. You can't." They let the silence sit a moment. "Here's my answer. The dead of Thornfell decline your terms."

Something moved behind Corvane's grey eyes—not anger; recalculation. "That's unfortunate."

"It is. Because you see, I did consider. All night. And here's what I concluded: a man who offers to buy a country doesn't intend to honor the receipt. Men like you buy things to own them, and ownership means the right to erase, and the day Thornfell's treasury sits in your vaults is the day its name becomes a line item you can delete whenever the ledger needs balancing." Hazel stepped closer, wrists still bound in silk, and watched the guards shift uncertainly. "You didn't come to save Thornfell. You came to make its destruction *affordable*."

For the first time, Corvane's face went still in a new way. "Take them back to—"

That was when the camp screamed.

It began at the horse-lines—a sound no animal makes, high and human and wrong, and the horses broke their pickets all at once. Then the sentries at the south perimeter started shouting about figures in the fog, and there were no figures in the fog, but every man on that wall swore otherwise, swore they could *hear* them, voices out of the mist calling names—sergeant, your mother's name is Maren, you told me yourself, why did you let them take it—and the fog was thick that morning, river-fog off the ford, and the Pale Engine at the camp's center stuttered, its blank pages jamming in the feeder, because it had never once been asked to digest a dead man's voice and Tam Voss was giving it everything he had.

Hazel had spent the night feeding him through the tent walls. Every guard's confession overheard. Every name murmured at watch-change. Every grief the camp carried unspoken—mothers, villages, streets already half-forgotten—and Tam, magnificent, drunken, generous Tam, had spent the hours arranging it all into a chorus, and now he threw it at the camp like a net.

"Sergeant!" Hazel shouted into the chaos, and the big man at the tent flap turned, wild-eyed, hand on his sword. "Maren of Harrowmere! Say it! Say it before the machine finishes eating it!"

"Maren," he whispered. "Maren." Louder, to his men, to the fog: "HER NAME WAS MAREN!"

And the fog took it up, or Tam did—*Maren, Maren, Maren*—and soldiers were dropping to their knees along the perimeter, and Rell the chipped-tooth boy stood in the open with tears freezing on his face, whispering a grandmother's name over and over like a prayer he'd just been handed back.

No one watched the prisoner.

Hazel ran. Silk bindings parted against the hidden edge in their boot heel—carved from a dead cooper's stave, honed on a dead wheelwright's stone; the dead armed their own. Through the fog, past the stuttering Engine, past Corvane's white pavilion where the Inquisitor-General stood in the entrance with his ink-stained fingers curled at his sides, watching Hazel go with an expression not of rage but of *interest*, the worst expression Hazel had ever seen.

"The northern vaults, carver!" Corvane called across the chaos, conversationally, horribly. "We'll continue our negotiation there. Do give my regards to your soldier!"

Hazel ran harder.

Behind them the fog thinned, and the voices faded, and Tam's presence in the bone guttered like a candle burned to its end—he'd given everything, and Hazel felt him go with a pang like a pulled tooth, felt the bone go quiet and ordinary in their pocket, a dead man's last expense paid gladly.

Ahead: the treeline, the corpse-roads, the north.

And stitched into the fading chorus, one final thread, whether Tam's or the country's own, Hazel never learned:

*Run, little carver. The Door's been waiting longer than either of you.*
