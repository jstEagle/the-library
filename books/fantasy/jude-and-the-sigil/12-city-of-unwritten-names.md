## 12. City of Unwritten Names

The road to Carrowgate did not exist, so Hazel walked it anyway.

That was the trick of it now—Hazel understood the trick at last. The empire had not burned the capital road or blockaded it or sunk it beneath a landslide. Vael had done something far more thorough: it had simply stopped agreeing that the road was there. Somewhere in the belly of the Pale Engine, the road's name had been fed into the machinery along with its milestones and its inns and the names of every family that had ever traveled it, and after that, travelers coming this way found their eyes sliding off the track like rain off oiled leather. They turned aside without knowing why. They told themselves they'd never meant to go to Carrowgate at all.

But Hazel carried bones in their pack, and bones remembered what maps were made to forget.

The dead woman's fingerbone—the first one, the singer from Greymarrow—had grown quieter as Hazel climbed into the high country, but not silent. It hummed against Hazel's sternum through three layers of wool, a low patient note like a struck bell fading toward its true pitch. *This way,* it said, in the wordless way of the dead. *This way, carver. We kept the path warm for you.*

By the third day the mountains opened into a valley that should have held a city.

It held a city still. That was the horror of it. Carrowgate rose out the morning mist exactly as Hazel's grandmother had described it: the seven bridges over the River Ossen, the bell tower with its green copper crown, the terraced houses climbing the hillsides like rows of birds on a wire. Smoke stood above the chimneys. Barges moved on the water. It was all there, whole and breathing and utterly, perfectly unnamed.

Hazel stood at the valley's mouth until their legs shook, because the silence was not an absence. It was a presence, dense as floodwater. A city that size should have roared—cart wheels and fish cries and church bells and ten thousand overlapping arguments. Instead there was sound, yes, but none of it meant anything. Voices spoke without words attaching to them. A bell rang with no hour in it. Hazel realized, standing there with frost forming in their eyelashes, that they could not remember the city's name either—not truly, not in the place where names live. They knew it the way one knows a face seen in a dream.

*Oh,* Hazel thought. *Oh, it's already inside me.*

They pressed their hand flat against the bone at their chest and breathed until the humming steadied them. Then they walked down into the country that was being deleted in real time, and went looking for a keeper who refused to die.

* * *

The first thing Hazel learned about erased Carrowgate was that its people had not been erased with it. Someone—Corvane, or the Engine itself, following some cold bureaucratic logic—had decided that bodies were useful even when identities were not. So the people remained, walking the streets that no map admitted, tending stalls and hauling barges and raising children, except that they answered to numbers.

Hazel heard it before they saw it: a market woman calling across the square, "Six-One-Nine! Six-One-Nine, come away from the water!" And a small boy turning, obedient and cheerful, answering to six-one-nine as naturally as if it were the name his mother had chosen for him at birth. Perhaps it was now. Perhaps it was the only name he'd ever known.

Hazel bought bread from a baker whose stall sign was blank wood, sanded smooth. When they fumbled for coins, the baker said, "Four-Forty-Seven thanks you," and smiled, and the smile was the worst part, because it was warm. It was a good smile from a person who had once surely been someone—who might have been called Marta or Ilse or Old Pem, who might have laughed at her own jokes and signed her loaves with a floury hand. Four-Forty-Seven wrapped the bread and asked no questions and remembered nothing to ask them with.

Hazel walked on and did not let themselves weep, because weeping drew attention, and because they suspected that once started they would not stop before dark.

The strangeness deepened street by street. In the older quarters—the ones the Engine had reached longest ago—the buildings themselves had begun to forget. Not crumble; forget. Walls stood sound but featureless, windows arranged with no logic any architect would own, doors opening onto walls while gaps in facades swung open where no door had ever hung. The city was losing its grammar. And threaded through it all ran streets that no one used at all: whole avenues swept clean, lamplit, immaculate, that pedestrians crossed without appearing to see. Hazel watched a man walk directly through a fountain's basin, boots dry, eyes fixed ahead, and understood that the fountain had been processed. It was no longer *there*, whatever it looked like. The people had agreed, without knowing they'd agreed, to step around their own history.

Only Hazel stepped *into* it—and only because the bones in their pack buzzed like a hive whenever they crossed one of those forgotten thresholds, singing *here, here, here was the Street of Cordwainers, here the House of the Ossen Ferry Guild, here a girl named Tamsin fell off this wall in the year the river froze.* The dead had not been processed. The dead had no numbers. The Pale Engine ate memory from the living, but the dead had already given theirs away—to whoever could listen.

Which meant, Hazel realized with a lurch, that they were the only archive left. Them, and whatever waited at the center of this quiet catastrophe.

* * *

The keeper found them before they found the keeper.

It happened at dusk, on one of the empty avenues, in a district so thoroughly unwritten that even the bones had gone reverent-quiet. Hazel had stopped to rest against a wall that bore, faint as breath on glass, the ghost of a painted sign—a fish, maybe, or a hand. And then a voice said, from ten feet away:

"You're the Eastwick child."

Hazel spun, hand going to the knife they'd never once needed against the living. A figure stood beneath a lamp that lit nothing—or rather, that lit everything *except* the figure, so that what remained was a shape cut out of the evening: tall, thin, draped in layers of coats gone gray with age, a scarf wound up past the chin. Eyes caught the lamplight and held it too long, like coins at the bottom of a well.

"You're supposed to be dead," Hazel said.

"I am." The voice was a ruin, cracked and rusted, a bell left out in weather for a century. "That is rather the entire point of me. You are difficult to find, Eastwick child. I have been walking toward you for eleven days. Or eleven years. The city moves its distances when it's frightened." The figure extended one gloved hand, palm up, an oddly courtly gesture. "You may call me what I was called. If you can."

"I can't," Hazel said honestly. "I've tried since the valley. There's no—it's like reaching for a stair that isn't there."

"Ah." Something moved behind those coin-bright eyes—grief, perhaps, worn smooth as sea glass from long handling. "Then you see it. You see what they do. Come. Walk with me, carver. The avenue is safe after full dark; the processed don't come out where the names used to be. They're afraid of the empty places." A dry sound that might once have been a laugh. "As well they should be. The empty places are where I keep myself."

They walked. And the keeper talked, in that ruined bell of a voice, and everything Hazel believed about the Hollow Door came apart like wet paper.

* * *

"I knew the prophecy would send someone eventually," the keeper said. Their boots made almost no sound on the clean stones; Hazel could not decide whether that was craft or erasure. "One hundred years, they gave it. The archivists of Thornfell wrote it down in 1823 and hid it in plain sight, bound as a merchant ledger, because the empire never reads ledgers—it burns them unread, which is its own kind of respect. Did you read the whole thing, Eastwick child? All of it? Every clause?"

"The clauses that mattered," Hazel said carefully.

"Mm." The keeper's stride did not change. "The clauses that mattered. Yes. That is how prophecy works, isn't it? It tells you everything and lets you choose what matters. Tell me, then—when you read that the Door must be sealed forever by the last key, what did you understand *sealed* to mean?"

"Closed," Hazel said. "Shut. Locked against Corvane."

"Closed." The keeper tasted the word. "And a closed door leads nowhere, so the Door must lead *somewhere*, and a somewhere that is not here must be elsewhere, and elsewhere, for a hunted people, means—" the ruined voice curled around it— "*out*. Escape. Safety. A future. That is what you understood."

"It's what the words say."

"The words say what the dead allowed them to say." The keeper stopped walking. They stood in the middle of the empty avenue, beneath a sky going bruise-colored over the bell tower, and turned to face Hazel fully, and for the first time Hazel saw that under the layered coats the body was wrong—too still, too light, a thing that walked because it refused to stop walking rather than because anything drove it forward. "We wrote that book, Eastwick child. The three keepers, with the last bone-singers of the old guilds. We wrote it in blood and marrow and we buried our truth inside a shape the empire would misread, because we knew—one day the Engine would come for Thornfell, and one day it would need a key, and the key would need to be warned."

"Warned of what?"

"That the Door does not open outward." Each word fell separately, like stones into a well. "There is no beyond. There is no other country waiting on the far side with soft beds and kind laws. The Hollow Door opens *backward*, carver. Into Thornfell itself. Into the Thornfell that was—every year of it, every street, every voice, stacked like rings in a tree. It is not an exit. It never was. It is a *memory with a hinge on it*."

The ground did not move, but Hazel felt it move, the particular vertigo of a floor giving way that they had first felt in the archivist's study and had never entirely recovered from. "Backward," they repeated stupidly. "Into the past."

"Into everything that was taken. Do you understand what that makes it?" The keeper's coin-eyes flared. "Corvane thinks he has found a treasury, or a weapon, or a door into history he can rewrite—I know what his kind wants, I watched his kind a hundred years ago wanting the same things. He believes the Door leads to a room where Thornfell's founding sits waiting to be edited. Let him believe it. But *you* needed to know the truth, because the choice at the threshold was always going to be yours, and you cannot choose rightly between two lies."

"What choice?"

The keeper began walking again, faster now, coat hems stirring in a wind Hazel couldn't feel. "Come. The vault quarter. I'll show you the Door's frame, what little shows above ground. And on the way I will tell you what my sisters paid, and what the third clause costs, and why I am the only keeper left walking—because I refused, Eastwick child. I refused to lie down and become a lock. And I have regretted it every day of my long unlife, and I have been glad of it every day too, because someone had to stay above ground to meet you." They glanced back, and the lamplight slid off their eyes like water off glass. "Someone had to tell you what the Door really is. Before he gets here."

"Corvane's coming here?" Hazel's hand found the fingerbone through the wool. It was ringing now, a clear alarmed note. "He knows about the vault?"

"He has always known about the vault. It is the finding of the key that stumped him." The keeper's laugh again, that terrible dry rustle. "But he has your name now, doesn't he. It appeared in a certain book. And the empire reads seized lists very carefully indeed."

* * *

They reached the vault quarter as the last light died.

It lay below the terraced houses, down stairs that descended farther than the hill could logically contain—erased space, folded inward, geography the Engine had chewed and spat back hollow. The dead in Hazel's pack had gone silent as held breath. At the bottom stood a plaza of black stone, and in its center, rising bare from the cobbles like a rib from shallow earth, stood the Door.

It was smaller than Hazel had imagined. Barely taller than a person. A frame of pale stone carved with spirals—the sigil-work, worn nearly smooth—and within the frame, nothing. Not darkness. Nothing. A absence shaped like a doorway, that the eye kept trying to fill with wall and kept failing.

Hazel's own bones answered it. The carved pieces in their pack, the work of their own hands across ten quiet years in Greymarrow, lifted and trembled like compass needles, all pointing home.

"There it is," the keeper said softly. "The back door of a country. Everything Vael has eaten is on the other side of that frame, Eastwick child. Every name. Every street. Your grandmother's kitchen. The bell tower when it still had a name to ring. The founding itself, if the founding is what you want." They turned, and in the starlight their face was finally visible: old, and ordinary, and wrecked with a century of walking. "A treasury. An escape. Or an archive that remembers better than any engine can destroy. It will be whichever you make it. That is the truth the prophecy hid, and now it is yours, and I am so tired, carver, and I have one more thing to ask of you—but not tonight. Tonight you should sleep, and eat, and grieve this city properly. Grief is fuel. You'll need every drop."

Above them, far up at the rim of the sunken plaza, lights appeared. Lanterns. Many lanterns, moving in disciplined order, descending the impossible stairs.

The keeper went very still. "That is too fast," they whispered. "That is days too fast."

The fingerbone against Hazel's chest sang one sharp, breaking note—and in it, for the first time, Hazel heard not the dead woman's voice but a living one, ragged with running, a voice they knew: Brannic's, gasping half a warning down whatever thread connected soldier to carver—

"*They leaked the route—they knew—don't go to the plaza—*"

Too late. The lanterns ringed the plaza's rim like a crown of small cold fires, and a voice rolled down into the dark, smooth and unhurried and terribly pleased:

"Eastwick child. And the keeper who wouldn't die. How thoughtful of you both to gather where I could collect you."

Corvane stepped into the lantern-light, and the Door at Hazel's back hummed like a struck bell.
