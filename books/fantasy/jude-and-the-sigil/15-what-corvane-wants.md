## 15. What Corvane Wants

The bone-vault smelled of dust and old prayers.

Inquisitor-General Aldous Corvane had been in a great many vaults in his career—treasury vaults in Vael's capital, relic vaults beneath conquered cathedrals, the sealed archive under the Ministry of Records where the empire kept the things it had decided never happened. This one was different. This one was *humble*. The walls were rough-hewn granite, unadorned, sweating cold. The ceiling was low enough that he had to be mindful of his hat. Someone had carved little marks into the doorframe, generations ago—heights, perhaps, children measured against the years—and no one had bothered to sand them away.

He found this offensive, in a quiet, administrative way.

"Leave me," he said.

The two soldiers at the door exchanged a glance that lasted half a second too long, and Corvane noted it for later. Noted everything for later. That was the whole of his method: attention, patience, and paperwork. People expected an Inquisitor-General to be a creature of fire and spectacle, all burning eyes and thunderous pronouncements. Corvane had learned early that terror was inefficient. Terror made people run, hide, lie with conviction. What made people cooperate was the sense that the man across the desk was simply *reasonable*, that resistance was more trouble than compliance, that the machine would grind on whether or not you fed yourself into it, so you might as well step aboard.

The soldiers left. Their boots faded down the stair. Somewhere above, the Pale Engine hummed its noteless note, the sound Corvane had stopped hearing years ago the way a man stops hearing his own heartbeat.

He turned to the Door.

It did not look like much. That was the first thing worth recording, he thought, and took out his notebook—a slim black volume, good paper, iron-gall ink, because some traditions deserved respect even when their owners did not. The Door stood in the center of the vault's far wall where the stone had been cut away in an arch taller than a man, and within the arch there was nothing. Not darkness. Darkness had texture, temperature, suggestion. This was absence with edges, a rectangle of *not*, and when Corvane looked at it directly for longer than a few seconds, he felt the peculiar sensation that he was not looking into it but being *omitted* from it—that the space where his reflection should have been had declined, politely, to include him.

He wrote: *Door intact. Threshold stable. Responds to proximity—see attached notes, entries 41 through 60.*

Then he closed the notebook, and allowed himself, for the first time in eleven days, to stop performing.

* * *

Alone, Corvane let out a long breath and rolled his shoulders, and the posture of the Inquisitor-General—the slight forward lean, the folded hands, the expression of patient disappointment—slid off him like a coat hung on a hook. What remained was a broad, gray-templed man of fifty-four with ink stains on his right middle finger and a bad knee that ached in mountain cold. He crossed the vault, wincing slightly, and sat down on an overturned crate some workman had left, because his knee preferred sitting and there was no one alive whose opinion of him mattered.

That was the luxury of this room. No one whose opinion mattered.

He considered the Door the way he considered all problems: as a question of authorship.

The official position—he had drafted it himself, three revisions, filed under seal—was that the Hollow Door constituted a strategic asset of the first order. A treasury, possibly. An escape route, certainly. Leverage over whatever survived of Thornfell once processing was complete. His superiors in the Ministry had signed off on the campaign with the enthusiasm men reserved for other people's expenses, and Corvane had let them believe it, because men who believed a thing were far easier to manage than men who knew it was a story.

But Corvane had read the prophecy. All of it, including the parts the archivist had thought were cipher and were merely bad Latin. And Corvane had spent thirty years studying how empires erased things, which meant thirty years studying what erasure actually *was*.

Here was the problem, and he laid it out in his mind with the satisfaction of a man laying cards on a table in perfect sequence:

Vael could burn Thornfell. It was doing so. Village by village, record by record, name by name—the Pale Engine ate them, and the maps redrew themselves, and the clerks in the capital filed Thornfell under *Administrative Fiction, Resolved*. In another ten years, no one born inside the empire would know the country had existed. In twenty, its own survivors would doubt it. He had seen it done to Kessrin. He had overseen it done to the Fenmark coast.

But erasure was a wound, and wounds healed. Memory was water; it found cracks. Some grandmother would tell a story. Some smuggler would keep a road-name alive. Some child would ask why the mountains on the old charts had no names, and the question itself would be a seed. Erasure, done honestly, required eternal vigilance—an empire could not afford to guard a grave forever.

Unless.

Unless you went back to before the grave was dug.

Corvane rose, knee protesting, and walked slowly toward the threshold until he stood close enough that the cold coming off it raised the hairs on his forearms. He did not touch it. He had watched a lieutenant touch it, early in the survey, and the lieutenant was now a clerk who answered to the number Nine-Twelve and smiled pleasantly when asked his name, because he no longer had one to give. The Door did not kill. It *edited*.

"I am not going to conquer your past," he told it conversationally. "I am going to *become* it."

That was the design. He had worked it through forty times, and each pass made it cleaner. The Door opened backward—not metaphorically, not mystically, but functionally—onto Thornfell as it had been. The founding years. The valley before the first wall, the first hearth, the first name spoken aloud and agreed upon. Every country began as a conversation: these are our bounds, these are our dead, this is what we call ourselves. And conversations could be joined.

He would walk through with a ledger, a pen, and thirty years of bureaucratic genius, and he would insert himself into that conversation. Not as a conqueror—conquerors were remembered and resented, and resentment was exactly the kind of crack memory loved. As a *founder*. A quiet man at the second fire, helpful with boundaries, good with records. The one who suggested the name. Whose hand appeared in the margins of the first documents. Who was, by the time the history thickened into fact, inseparable from it.

And then the present would correct itself. It always corrected itself; he had seen the Engine demonstrate the principle a hundred times—change the record, and the world tidied up around the change like water finding a new level. Thornfell would still fall, but now it would fall having *always been falling*, having been flawed from its conception, having been conceived—in part—by him. Its erasure would not be an event. It would be a conclusion. Retroactive. Eternal. No grandmother's tale, no smuggler's road, no child's question, because the country's own origin would testify against it.

Not conquest. *Authorship.* Anyone could burn a book. He intended to write one.

Corvane became aware that he was smiling, and examined the smile with mild clinical interest. It was not the smile of a fanatic. Fanatics frightened him, frankly—they were unreliable, they wanted things for reasons, and reasons introduced variables. His own smile, he judged, was the smile of a man reviewing a well-ordered ledger. Everything balanced. Everything would balance.

There was, of course, the matter of the key.

* * *

He returned to the crate and sat, and opened the notebook again, and reviewed what he knew, writing as he thought because thinking without writing was, in his experience, merely daydreaming with ambition.

The key was Hazel Eastwick. Bone-carver, nonbinary—the file used the phrase *of indeterminate registry*, which Corvane had found faintly amusing, as though the empire's difficulty were a failure of filing rather than of imagination. Age approximately twenty-six. Gift: heard the dead through carved bone. Clients throughout Greymarrow's lower town, all of whom had been interviewed, most of whom remembered nothing afterward, thanks to the Engine's local applications. Currently at large, though the net was closing; the smuggler routes had been purchased for less than the cost of the paper the contracts were written on. People sold each other so cheaply. It was the one constant of his career, and he had long ago stopped finding it tragic and started finding it *useful*.

Why did the key answer only to a carver? He wrote the question, then struck it and rephrased: *What is the mechanism?*

The prophecy called Eastwick "the last living key," which implied earlier keys, now dead—which implied the key was not a person but a *function*, and persons merely occupied it. And the function, as far as his researchers could determine, was listening. The Door was locked from the inside, in a sense; it had been closed by the founders, and the founders' voices were in the bones, and only someone who could hear bones could carry the founders' consent back to the lock. The Door did not open for power. It opened for *permission*.

Which was why Eastwick was alive. Which was why Eastwick would remain alive, comfortably housed, decently fed, treated with every courtesy—right up until the moment they put their blood on the frame and spoke the lock open. After that, the function would have no further use, and Corvane saw no reason to complicate the arithmetic with sentiment. He was not cruel by temperament. Cruelty was expenditure without return. But he had learned long ago not to flinch at necessary line items either, and he had stopped pretending the distinction cost him anything around the same year he'd realized no one was checking.

He paused over the page. There was a variable he disliked, and he made himself look at it squarely, because he disliked variables most when he avoided them.

Eastwick had been in the company of Brannic. Sergeant—formerly Captain—Brannic, demoted for insubordination at Fenmark, reassigned as Corvane's own clerk, and now, per the last three reports, a deserter traveling with the fugitive. Corvane had liked Brannic, in the limited way he liked any efficient instrument, and the betrayal had been instructive rather than painful: even good instruments bent if you applied them at the wrong angle. The man would be caught. Men like Brannic were always caught, because they could not stop doing the honorable thing long enough to do the smart one.

No. The variable he disliked was smaller than that, and older, and it sat in the vault with him now: the marks on the doorframe. Children's heights. Someone had loved this place in the ordinary way, the way people loved kitchens and doorsteps and the smell of rain on particular streets, and Corvane's entire design depended on the proposition that such love was *editable*—that it was data, and data could be rewritten at the source. Thirty years of erasures supported the proposition. And yet he sat in the cold with his bad knee aching, looking at the small knife-cut grooves where some father had said *stand straight, lad, there, that's a year*, and he felt—nothing. He checked. He performed the internal audit carefully, the way he audited everything, and confirmed that he felt nothing whatsoever about the marks, or the father, or the lad, and that this nothing was not suppression but simple absence, the way a room is absent of furniture.

Good, he concluded. That was the requirement of the work. You could not rewrite a people if you were one of them. Empires were not built by men who loved places; they were built by men who loved *ledgers*, and Corvane had loved his ledger since he was nine years old, since the first time he understood that a list, properly kept, outlived everyone on it.

He would outlive everyone on his.

* * *

A knock at the vault door. Two short, one long—the signal for report, not emergency. He restored the coat of himself, the lean and the folded hands, before saying, "Enter."

It was the young officer from the transfer detail, the one whose file said *Ostrell, L., exemplary, suggests further training*. The boy kept his eyes correctly lowered and his voice correctly flat.

"Inquisitor-General. The prisoner transport is confirmed for solstice morning. The subject Eastwick has been moved to holding cell four. Also—" a hesitation, correctly brief, "—the bone-vault instruments registered a resonance event fourteen minutes ago. Spontaneous. The engineers say the Door... hummed."

"Hummed," Corvane repeated.

"A tone. Very low. It stopped when the watch changed." The boy's throat moved. "Sir, the engineers wanted me to ask whether you consider it significant."

Corvane considered it. A Door that hummed when no carver was near it. A lock murmuring to itself in the dark—or, more precisely, being murmured *to*, from somewhere at a distance, by voices carried in no medium his instruments could name.

Bone, he thought. The prisoner had carved a great many bones in their time, and every one of those bones was still in the world, in pockets and reliquaries and grave-dirt across a country that was learning, hour by hour, how loudly it could speak.

"Log it," he said pleasantly. "Note the solstice correlation hypothesis. And Ostrell—double the watch on cell four tonight. Our guest has friends, and I suspect they are better organized than we assumed."

The boy saluted and withdrew. Corvane remained on his crate a moment longer, listening to the vault's silence, and beneath the silence—faint, deniable, almost certainly imagination—he thought he could hear the Door humming after all, low and patient, like a country clearing its throat to say its name.

He wrote down the time, closed the notebook, and went up the stairs to finish the paperwork.
