## 17. Breaking the Vault

**Brannic**

The voices had started three nights ago, and Brannic had told no one, because telling no one was the only way to keep them.

They came faintly, like speech through a stone wall—the dead Hazel had carved, reaching for him across the dark of the fortress at Cael Dun. He heard them best when he pressed his palm flat against cold rock, which he did now, crouched in the supply tunnel beneath the eastern wall, his wound pulling at every breath like a hook set behind his ribs.

*The transfer moves at dusk,* one voice said. A woman's, patient, worn smooth as riverstone. *The Door thins when the sun stands lowest. Corvane knows it. So must you.*

He knew. He'd read it in the stolen prophecy a dozen times since deserting—*when the year turns upon its hinge, the lock forgets itself*—and he'd spent six days becoming invisible inside the garrison that still believed him loyal, or believed him dead, or both. Six days carrying gravel sacks past guards who nodded to him. Six days memorizing the rotation of the bone-vault's outer ring.

Tonight the year turned on its hinge.

Above him, through a grate furred with frost, the courtyard glowed amber with torchlight. Solstice transfer: the Inquisitor-General did not trust his prize to ordinary cells on this one night of the year. Hazel would be moved from the deep cells to the vault chamber itself, bound and hooded, so that the key would be within arm's reach when Corvane chose to force the Door.

Which meant that for exactly the length of one corridor, Hazel Eastwick would be out in the open.

Brannic checked the crossbow at his hip, the knife at his back, and the folded scrap of smuggler's cloth in his breast pocket—a signal token he'd sent down-mountain nine days ago with no certainty anyone alive still ran those routes. *Vault. Solstice. Bring fire.* If the smugglers had sold him again, if Corvane waited behind every door, then this tunnel was simply the shape his death preferred.

The woman's voice stirred against his palm, almost amused. *You bleed too loud, soldier. They will hear your heart before they hear your feet.*

"Then I'll go faster," he whispered, and moved.

* * *

**Hazel**

They had stopped speaking to Hazel on the fourth day—not out of cruelty but out of policy. Corvane's clerks had determined that silence softened keys. No words, no names, no sound except the measured tread of boots and the rattle of the tray through the slot, and once, unbearably, a snatch of song from a guard too young to know better, some Thornfell lullaby half-remembered, sung wrong.

Hazel had wept at that, quietly, facing the wall. Not because the song was sad. Because it was wrong, and soon it would be wrong everywhere.

But the silence had another face. Deprived of living voices, Hazel's gift had spread through the fortress like water through chalk. There were bones everywhere in Cael Dun. A saint's knuckle under the chapel altar, older than the empire. Buttons carved from shinbone in a clerk's coat. The handle of the food tray itself, polished deer-antler, holding the ghost of a poacher who'd died laughing. And threaded through all of them, distant but unmistakable, the chorus Hazel had raised in their cell on that first desperate night—every client, every relic, every dead hand Hazel had ever held, gathered now at the edge of hearing and waiting.

*Solstice,* said the drowned woman from the flooded chapel, her voice bubbling up through the antler handle. *Today they move you. Today you are closest to the Door you will ever be while breathing.*

*I know,* Hazel thought back, the way they'd learned to speak into the quiet.

*And the soldier?*

Hazel hesitated. They had felt Brannic since the second day—a rough, stubborn presence at the far edge of the chorus, listening at walls, bleeding somewhere below the east range. Bleeding steadily. Not dying. Stubborn enough to refuse dying.

*Coming,* Hazel said. *I think he's coming.*

The dead made a sound that might have been laughter. It was not a comforting sound.

Boots in the corridor. Keys. The door opened and four guards entered with a hood and iron cuffs, and behind them, filling the doorway like a shadow given rank, the dry rustle of Corvane's grey coat.

"Inquisitor-General," Hazel said, because the silence order apparently didn't bind prisoners, only everyone else. Their voice came out cracked from disuse. "Lovely evening for a walk."

Corvane studied them the way he studied everything: as a document awaiting correction. "The year turns," he said. "You'll want to rest. What comes next requires nothing of you but blood."

"That's what I keep telling people about my carving," Hazel said. "Nobody listens."

The hood came down over their head, and the world went dark, and into that dark poured the chorus like a tide.

*Now,* said forty voices at once. *Now, now, now.*

* * *

**Brannic**

The transfer began at dusk, and Brannic watched it through the grate with his heart hammering so hard he feared it would crack his ribs where the bolt hadn't. Four guards, the prisoner hooded and shackled, and Corvane himself walking beside them like a man escorting a bride. They crossed the courtyard in a tight knot, torchlight sliding off their armor, and disappeared through the vault's bronze doors.

Brannic gave them ninety seconds. Then he climbed.

He'd prepared the route with a quartermaster's thoroughness: the grate, the coal chute, the gap behind the cistern where the old fortress masonry had settled away from the imperial repairs. His shoulder screamed going through the last of it, the bolt-wound tearing fresh, and he lay gasping in the snow of the inner yard with warmth spreading down his side and thought, with detached clarity, *you will leave a trail any child could follow.*

There was no help for it. He got up.

The bronze doors stood guarded by two soldiers, and here Brannic's plan met its single point of failure, because his plan had been *hope*, and hope is not a plan. But then the doors shuddered. From inside came a low groan of stressed metal, a sound like a ship in ice, and one of the guards turned toward it—and the other staggered, clawing at his own collar, choking on nothing, on air gone suddenly thick.

The dead were helping. Brannic could feel it now, unmistakably—a pressure in the courtyard, a congregation. He didn't waste the miracle. He crossed the snow in six strides and put the pommel of his knife into the first man's temple and his boot into the second's knee, and both went down without dying, and Brannic was ashamed of how grateful he was for that small mercy, and pushed through the doors.

Inside, the vault chamber opened upward into darkness, and at its center stood the Hollow Door.

He had imagined it a hundred times and been wrong every time. It was not grand. It was a door the size of a cottage door, set freestanding in a frame of black stone carved over every inch with names—thousands of names, spiraling, in a script that hurt to look at, that rearranged itself when he blinked. And it was *thin*. That was the word his mind kept returning to. The air around it shimmered like heat above a road, like something seen through water, and through the shimmer Brannic glimpsed—not beyond it, *through* it—green hills under an older sky, and a village burning, and the same village whole.

Four guards had Hazel against the wall. Corvane stood before the Door with a silver instrument in his hand, a lancet long as a writing pen, and he was saying something in a calm liturgical drone—"by right of authorship, by right of erasure"—and Hazel's hood had come askew, and their eyes found Brannic's across the chamber.

Hazel didn't shout. Didn't warn him off, didn't cry his name. They just looked at him, and then—slowly, deliberately—looked at the brazier beside the wall, and then back at him.

Fire. The smugglers' signal: *bring fire.*

Brannic brought fire. He tore the fuse-cord from his sleeve pocket—it had cost him three weeks' pay and a favor from a man in Greymarrow whose sister Hazel had once carved a keepsake for, and the man had asked no questions, which told Brannic everything about what Hazel was to this country—and he threw it into the brazier.

It went up with a crack like the world's spine breaking. White light, white noise, and in the chaos Brannic shot the nearest guard through the shoulder and closed with the second.

* * *

**Hazel**

The world was light and screaming, and through it Hazel heard the dead rise like a storm coming off the sea.

Not faintly now. Not through antler handles and altar stones. The solstice had thinned more than the Door; it had thinned everything, peeled back the membrane between Thornfell's living and its uncounted dead, and the chorus poured into the vault chamber with a force that buckled Hazel's knees even shackled and hooded. They felt every ghost take position. The drowning-dead flooding the lungs of the men who held them. The erased citizens of the capital standing in ranks along the walls, faces like smudged charcoal, pointing with fingers that had no substance at the guards' sword arms, turning blades aside by inches.

A guard swung at Brannic's head and his arm simply *slowed*, moving through syrup, and Brannic's knife took him across the thigh instead of the throat.

Hazel wrenched free of the last dazed soldier and pulled the hood off. Across the chamber, Corvane had not moved. He stood before the shimmering Door with the lancet poised, and he looked at the chaos around him with the expression of a man watching ink spill on a page he intended to rewrite anyway.

"The key," Corvane said, and his voice carried through the din with terrible ease, "comes to me, or the country loses another village. Your choice has always been fictional, Eastwick."

"He's stalling," Brannic shouted, appearing at Hazel's elbow, blood-sheeted, glorious, impossible. "The Door won't take forced blood—I've read the prophecy, it needs—"

"It needs a carver's blood freely given," Corvane said. "Yes. Which is why I have spent six pleasant days ensuring that the carver would rather give it than watch me feed the Pale Engine." He smiled. It was the worst thing Hazel had ever seen, that smile, because it was reasonable. "You misunderstand me entirely if you think I need to catch you tonight. Tonight I merely needed you present."

The bronze doors boomed. Boots—dozens. The garrison, responding to the fire, pouring into the outer corridor.

*Back way,* said the dead, all together, and the drowned woman's voice cut through sharpest: *Behind the frame, carver. The Door was built into a mountain shrine. The builders left themselves a door behind the door.*

Hazel grabbed Brannic's wrist. "Behind the Door—move!"

They ran together, straight at the shimmer, and Corvane lunged to intercept, and it was Brannic who turned to meet him, Brannic who took the lancet meant for Hazel across the meat of his forearm rather than let it touch them, Brannic who slammed the Inquisitor-General into the black stone frame hard enough to crack something in the man's chest—and then Hazel's hands found the hidden seam in the rear of the frame, old Thornfell masonry, a counterweight lever worn smooth by hands eight centuries dead, and pulled.

Stone ground apart behind the Door. Cold air breathed out, smelling of pine and snow.

*Go,* roared the chorus, and the vault's torches guttered as if a great inhale had taken the room.

* * *

**Brannic**

They came out onto a ledge above a moonlit gorge, and Brannic turned to hold the passage while Hazel wedged a dead branch into the mechanism, and that was when he saw the boy.

The young guard from the outer ring—the one who'd slipped him extra bread during his demotion week, who'd once covered for him on watch, who'd told him shyly that his mother came from a village near Thornfell's border and maybe that made them almost neighbors—stood in the breach with his sword up and his orders in his eyes and tears already freezing on his cheeks, because the dead had shown him things in the vault chamber too, and he knew what he served now, and he raised his sword anyway.

Duty, Brannic thought, is the cruelest loyalty there is.

"I can't let you close it," the boy said. His name was Pell. His name was Pell and Brannic had never learned it until this moment, until it was too late for the knowing to matter. "I'm sorry. I liked you, Sergeant."

"I know," Brannic said, and did not lower his blade, because lowering it would kill them both and strand a whole country in silence forever, and there was no version of this where his hands came clean.

Pell came at him crying. Brannic killed him quickly, the way you kill someone you'd have died for a month ago, and the quickness was the only mercy left to offer, and it wasn't enough, and it would never be enough.

Then Hazel's hand was on his arm—gentle, steady, the steadiest thing in the world—and the branch gave way behind them, and the passage collapsed into darkness with Corvane's scream of rage sealed on the far side of it.

* * *

**Hazel**

They descended the gorge trail by starlight, Brannic leaning on Hazel harder with every hundred yards, his blood leaving dark spots on the snow like a signature being written and written again.

"You're losing too much," Hazel said.

"I'm aware."

"There's a keeper's hollow two miles down. Old bones, warm spring. The dead there will help me pack the wound."

Brannic laughed—a wet, broken sound. "Your friends get more useful every day."

"They're not my friends." Hazel stopped on the trail. Below them, the valley of high Thornfell lay silvered and silent, real, remembered, *theirs* for one more night. Above, behind the collapsed rock, the Inquisitor-General was digging through rubble toward a Door that still hung open on the solstice thinning, and would hang open, Hazel realized, until the year's hinge finished turning—at dawn.

Two sigil-bones in Hazel's pack. One still walking loose in the ruined capital, mad and grieving. And a promise Hazel had made to a dead family's father, and a clause in a prophecy they still hadn't told Brannic about, and dawn coming whether they bled or rested.

"Brannic," Hazel said. "We have until sunrise. And I have to tell you what the last line of the prophecy says."

He looked at them, swaying, gray-faced, and nodded once.

The dead, all around them in the dark, went very quiet to listen.
