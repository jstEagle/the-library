## 21. Silence

The vault was quiet in a way that had nothing to do with sound.

Brannic felt it the moment Hazel's weight settled against his back—felt it in the way they went limp, not the boneless slack of unconsciousness but something stranger, like a rope cut from its anchor. Their breath was shallow. Their hands hung at his shoulders without gripping. And around them, where Brannic had learned to expect a certain hum—a pressure at the edge of things, the sense of being watched by ten thousand patient eyes—there was nothing.

The Door was closed. The dead were gone.

He adjusted his grip beneath Hazel's knees and started down.

The vault stairs had been carved into the mountain before Vael had a name, and they wound down through rock that still remembered being holy. Torch brackets lined the walls, empty now; he'd lit one on the way up and it had guttered out within minutes, as if the flame itself had decided there was nothing left worth illuminating. He navigated by feel and by the thin grey light bleeding down from the shattered seal above, where Corvane's soldiers had breached the outer corridor and where, if the smugglers' word held true, the remnants of the Pale Engine were even now being pulled apart with hammers and crowbars and bare, furious hands.

He could hear it as he descended—the distant ringing of metal on metal, the shouts of men and women unmaking the machine that had spent thirty years unmaking them. It sounded like a city tearing down its own gallows. It sounded like morning.

Hazel's head lolled against his shoulder. Their hair smelled of ash and cold stone and, faintly, of the tallow smoke from the vault candles. Brannic counted their breaths the way he'd once counted paces on night marches—one, two, three—because counting kept other thoughts at bay. Thoughts like *if they don't wake*. Thoughts like *I carried you into that vault*. Thoughts like the look on Hazel's face in the last instant before they turned to the Door and began to speak—not afraid, not then, but *full*, the way a cup is full right before it overflows.

They had poured themselves out. He had watched them do it.

*One*, he counted. *Two. Three.*

The stairs went on.

---

The world outside the mountain had changed overnight, and everyone in Greymarrow pretended they didn't know how.

Brannic saw it first in the faces. A week ago—and it had been barely a week, though it felt like the span of a long war—he had walked these streets as a nameless clerk in an imperial coat, invisible by design, and the people of Greymarrow had looked through him the way people look through weather. Now they looked at him. Not with fear. With something worse: recognition. An old woman selling smoked fish squinted at his face and said, "Kavanagh's boy? No—no, you're not from here." She frowned, searching. "But I know you."

"You don't," Brannic said gently. "You will, though."

She nodded slowly, satisfied, as if this were a perfectly ordinary thing to say—as if, in a country that had spent a century learning to forget itself, the sudden return of memory were simply a season arriving late. Behind her stall, chalked on slate in a hand that shook, was a list of names. Brannic stopped and read them. Fishers. Miners. Two children dead of fever in the winter of '41. Names no one had spoken aloud in decades because no one could hold them anymore, names that had slid off tongues like water off slate—and now here they were, recovered whole, as though they had been waiting patiently in the dark for someone to come back and say *remember*.

Thornfell remembered itself. Every map in every imperial office had redrawn itself in the night, borders snapping back like bones set straight. The garrison at the pass had woken to find their orders referring to a country that officially existed again, which had produced, by all accounts, a bureaucratic crisis of historic proportions and at least three resignations. The Pale Engine sat in the marketplace under guard—not imperial guard, but Greymarrow's own, dockworkers and fishwives with borrowed pikes—while engineers from the college argued over whether to dismantle it or preserve it as a warning.

Brannic voted, silently, for dismantling. He'd seen what it did up close. He would carry that up close for the rest of his life.

He carried Hazel up the hill to Maren Oduya's house, because Maren had said *bring them to me, I've kept the fire warm since before your empire had a flag*, and because Brannic had nowhere else and no one else and was honest enough, finally, to admit it.

Maren took one look at him—at Hazel across his back, at the blood soaked through his shirt from a wound he had genuinely forgotten he had—and said, "Down. Both of you. Don't argue."

He didn't argue. He was learning.

---

Hazel slept for two days.

Brannic knew because he counted those too, sitting in the chair beside their bed in Maren's back room, cleaning and rebandaging the bolt wound in his side with the methodical patience of a man who had once stitched his own arm shut in a snowfield and found it preferable to conversation. The room was small and low-ceilinged and smelled of rosemary and lamp oil. Through the window he could see the harbor, grey and glittering, and the masts of ships that flew no imperial colors.

On the second evening, Hazel's breathing changed.

Brannic was on his feet before he'd decided to stand. Hazel's eyelids fluttered. Their fingers twitched against the blanket—carver's fingers, ink-stained and scarred, fingers that had held more of the dead than any living person should—and then they went rigid, curling inward as though reaching for something that wasn't there.

"No," Hazel whispered. "No, wait—I can't hear—"

"Hazel." Brannic leaned close, keeping his voice low and level, the voice he'd used on green soldiers in their first battle. "Hazel. You're awake. You're safe. You're in Greymarrow."

Their eyes opened—dark, unfocused, swimming—and fixed on him with an expression he had never seen on their face before, not in the flooded chapel, not at the checkpoint, not when Corvane's crossbowmen had them cornered in the bone-vault with death an arm's length away on either side.

Terror. Pure, undiluted terror, and none of it aimed at him.

"It's gone," Hazel breathed. "It's all—it's *quiet*. Brannic, it's never been quiet. Not once. Not since I was six years old. There's always—" Their hand pressed flat against their own sternum, hard, as if checking for a heartbeat that belonged to someone else. "There's always *someone*."

"I know." He sat on the edge of the bed, careful of their space, careful of everything. "I know what you gave."

"Do you?" A cracked, half-hysterical note entered their voice. "Do you actually? Because I stood in front of that Door and I thought—I thought I understood the price, I thought I'd made my peace, but I didn't understand *this*. I didn't understand waking up. Everyone I've ever carved for, everyone who ever trusted me with their last words—they're just *gone* from me. Not dead. Dead I could bear. Just—" Their voice broke apart. "*Absent.*"

Brannic did the only thing he could think of. He reached out and took their hand—the one pressing at their chest—and held it between both of his, rough palm against soft palm, and waited.

It took a long time. Outside, the harbor bells rang the hour. Somewhere below, Maren moved through her kitchen, singing under her breath an old Thornfell song that had come back to her along with everything else, a song about a girl who followed a fox into the snow. Brannic had heard it as a child, in a different country, in a life that no longer existed, and hearing it again now made his throat tighten in a way he refused to examine.

Finally Hazel's breathing slowed. Their fingers uncurled in his grip.

"Say something," they whispered.

"Anything?"

"Anything. Please. I need to hear something that isn't inside my own skull."

So Brannic said the first true thing that came into his head: "Your hand is freezing and you should eat something before you argue with me about it."

A wet, startled laugh escaped them—half sob, half laugh, entirely involuntary. They stared at him. "That's what you lead with?"

"You said anything." He shrugged, and immediately regretted it, because the shrug pulled at the bolt wound and the pain must have shown on his face, because Hazel's eyes sharpened instantly, the carver's eye, the one that read bodies like texts.

"You're hurt."

"It's nothing."

"Brannic Kavanagh." His full name, spoken in that voice, landed like a hand on his chest. "You took a bolt for me. Show me."

"It's already bound."

"*Show me.*"

He showed them. They unwound the bandage with a professional's efficiency and a gentleness that undid him more thoroughly than any interrogation ever had, and they redid the binding properly, muttering about soldier field-dressings and the empire's criminal neglect of basic medicine, and their hands shook slightly the entire time, and neither of them mentioned it.

"There," they said at last, tying off the linen. "Now we're even."

"We're not remotely even."

"Then I'll owe you." They looked up at him, and the terror was still there underneath—he could see it, banked but burning—but layered over it now was something steadier. Something choosing. "Brannic. I can't hear anyone. The dead are silent. And I'm finding out—" They swallowed. "I'm finding out I don't know how to be a person without them. I built my whole self around listening. I don't know what's left when the listening stops."

Brannic considered this with the gravity it deserved. Then he said:

"Ask me something. Anything. And I'll answer honestly, out loud, with my actual voice, which is a thing I promise you is real and present and doesn't require any bone whatsoever. Ask me the worst question you have."

Hazel searched his face for a long moment. Looking for the lie, probably. Looking for the fault line they'd both spent so long pretending wasn't there.

"Why did you burn your commission?" they asked quietly. "Really. Not the version you told the abbess. Not the version for the smugglers. Why."

Brannic looked at the window, at the harbour, at the slate of names he couldn't see from here but knew was there.

"Because I stood in a village square and watched Sergeant Ellery forget his mother's face," he said. "Because I wrote the report afterward and called it 'standard memory-processing' and signed my name to it, and my hand didn't shake until later, and the shaking came too late to mean anything. Because when I saw your name on a seizure list, the first feeling I had was relief that it wasn't mine—and I hated myself for it, and I kept the list anyway, and keeping it was the first honest act of my adult life." He met their eyes. "Because I was loyal to an empire that ate countries, and the only way to stop being its instrument was to become something it couldn't hold. So I burned the paper. It didn't make me good. It just made me available."

Hazel was very still.

"Available," they repeated.

"For whatever came next." He cleared his throat, suddenly aware of how large his hands looked around theirs. "For whoever."

The silence that followed was different from the terrible silence Hazel had woken into. This one had texture. Weight. The kind of silence two people can sit inside together without drowning.

"The dead used to tell me everything," Hazel said at last, softly. "Every secret. Every regret. People paid me for it. I told myself I was helping them be heard." Their thumb moved once, slowly, across Brannic's knuckles. "But I never let them hear *me*. That was the trade. I listened to everyone and spoke to no one. And now the listening's gone, and I keep waiting to disappear along with it."

"You won't." Brannic heard his own voice come out rougher than intended. "I've spent a year watching you. Before the prophecy. Before any of it—you, in your shop, carving, humming to yourself when you thought no one was looking. The gift isn't why I—" He stopped. Started again, slower. "You were worth knowing before you were a key. You'll be worth knowing after."

"That's the most words you've ever said to me at one time."

"Don't get used to it."

But they were smiling—small and crooked and exhausted and real—and Brannic realized he was smiling too, and that his face had apparently decided to do this without consulting him, and that he did not mind.

---

Hazel slept again, properly this time, and woke hungry, and argued with Maren about salt pork, and lost, and by the third day they were standing at the window wrapped in a blanket, watching Greymarrow move below.

"They're pulling the engine apart," they said. "Can you hear it? All day. Like the town is chewing."

"I can hear it." Brannic stood beside them. His side ached. His hands, he noticed, had stopped shaking somewhere in the last day, and he hadn't noticed them stopping, and that seemed important somehow—that a body could heal without announcing it, that some things simply knitted while you weren't paying attention.

"Maren says the council wants a record made," Hazel went on. "Of everything. Every name, every story, written down and copied and hidden in cellars and taught to children, so it can never be eaten again." They glanced sideways at him. "They want me to carve the memorial bones. The founding ones. For the new archive."

"What did you tell them?"

"I told them bones hold nothing but love now. I told them I'd do it anyway." A pause. "I think I meant it."

Below them, children ran through the market square chasing a dog, shrieking, alive, unremarkable in every way that mattered. On the far side of the square, work crews swarmed over the Pale Engine like ants over a fallen beast. Someone had already painted a crude sign on its housing: *NEVER AGAIN, AND ALSO NEVER FORGET, AND ALSO LUNCH AT NOON.*

Hazel laughed at it—a real laugh, startled out of them—and the sound did something to Brannic's chest that he decided not to name yet.

"I keep reaching for them," Hazel admitted quietly, after a while. "The dead. When I'm frightened, or bored, or lying awake. My hand goes to my kit and I reach for a bone and—" Their jaw tightened. "It's like reaching for a stair that isn't there. I fall a little, every time."

"Then don't reach alone." Brannic said it simply, because he had spent his whole life complicating simple things and he was done. "When your hand goes out and finds nothing—put it somewhere else. Here." He lifted their hand and set it against his forearm, over the old scar from the snowfield stitching. "I'm not much. I talk too little and sleep badly and I'll probably always flinch at imperial uniforms. But I am *here*, Hazel Eastwick, entirely, and I intend to stay heard the ordinary way. Loudly. Repeatedly. Until you believe it."

Hazel looked at their hand on his arm. Then up at him. Their eyes were wet and furious about being wet.

"You realize," they said, voice wobbling dangerously, "that I'm going to hold you to that. Daily. Hourly, possibly. I have years of unsaid things backed up and no ghosts to say them to anymore."

"Good," said Brannic. "Start now. We've got time."

And Hazel opened their mouth—

—and stopped. Their gaze had drifted past him, out the window, down to the market square, where a knot of people had gathered around a cart newly arrived from the mountain road. Even at this distance Brannic could see what lay in the cart's bed, wrapped in oilcloth that the wind had half peeled back: a shape of dark iron, scorched and broken, unmistakably a piece of the Pale Engine's core.

And standing beside the cart, shaking hands with the dockworkers' captain, wearing travelling clothes and a scholar's satchel and an expression of brisk, cheerful purpose, was a woman Brannic had last seen giving testimony before an imperial tribunal—a woman whose name had been struck from every record in Vael.

She looked up. Directly at their window. Directly at Hazel.

And she smiled, and beckoned.
