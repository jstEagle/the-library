## 22. The Carver's New Trade

The morning came up over Greymarrow like a slow tide, gold spilling through the harbor fog and catching on every wet rooftop until the whole town looked dipped in honey. A year ago, Hazel would have known exactly how many gulls were circling the fish-market, how many sailors had died at sea and whose bones still ached for it, how many ghosts walked the corpse-roads with their unfinished business folded under their arms like parcels they could not put down.

Now Hazel knew only what their eyes told them: gulls, sails, fog thinning into blue. It was enough. It was more than enough. Some mornings it was so much that Hazel had to stand in the doorway of the shop with their hands pressed flat against the doorframe just to hold all of it.

The sign above the door was new—or rather, old made new again. *Eastwick & Kavanagh, Carvers.* Brannic had painted it himself in the spring, standing on a wobbly ladder, arguing with Hazel about the ampersand the entire time.

"It's pretentious," Hazel had said.

"It's accurate," Brannic had said. "You carve. I sand. I hold things. I'm very good at holding things."

"You're good at holding *me*."

"That too." He'd kissed them with paint on his hands and left a gray smudge on Hazel's jaw that three customers politely failed to mention for the rest of the day.

Hazel unlocked the shop now, breathing the familiar smell of it—oilsmoke and cedar shavings, tallow and beeswax, the faint mineral sweetness of bone waiting to be worked. The workbench sat by the window where the light was best. On its scarred surface lay the morning's commission: a keepsake raven, half-finished, carved from the knucklebone of a woman named Maren Ostrey who had died in her sleep at eighty-one, surrounded by grandchildren, wanting nothing from eternity except to be given to the granddaughter who used to feed the crows off the pier.

That was the trade now. That was all of it. No warnings from the dead, no secrets pried loose from marrow, no names whispered up through the grain like water through stone. Just love, carved small enough to fit in a pocket. Hazel had worried, in the first months of the silence, that the work would feel hollow—that bone without a voice inside it would be no different from wood or soapstone or any other honest material. But it wasn't hollow. It was *clean*. Every piece held exactly what Hazel put into it and nothing else, and when Maren Ostrey's granddaughter held the little raven in both hands, she would know her grandmother had been loved by someone skilled enough to make the love permanent.

That was a kind of hearing too, Hazel had decided. Just quieter. Just theirs.

They picked up the graving knife and set the first cut along the raven's wing.

Outside, Greymarrow went about its business, and its business included remembering itself now. That was the strangest part—not the maps, though the maps had redrawn themselves in cartographers' hands across three empires, Thornfell's borders reappearing like a bruise surfacing; not the records, though the Pale Engine's wreckage in the capital had become something between a monument and a warning, its memory-eating gears seized forever mid-turn; but the small things. The baker on Coil Street had remembered, one ordinary Tuesday, that his grandmother's grandmother had kept bees on this exact spot, and had started keeping bees again. The ferryman sang a work-song nobody had taught him, words in old Thornic that he couldn't translate but never got wrong. Children played a skipping game whose rhyme predated the empire, and when scholars came asking where they'd learned it, the children only shrugged, because everyone had always known it. Everyone had always known everything here. That was what it felt like now—the country settling back into its own shape the way a body settles into an old bed, all the erased weight of it returning at once.

Hazel's name had come back too. Not just *Eastwick*, which had never left, but the older name beneath it, the one the Door had spoken through their mouth in the vault while Corvane dissolved into the light like frost in sun: *Hazel of the Long Listening*. They didn't use it. It felt like wearing a crown to market. But once a month or so, someone would leave flowers on the vault steps in the capital, and once a letter had arrived addressed simply to *the carver who gave us back our names*, and Hazel had read it standing in the shop doorway with tears running freely into their collar, then put it in the drawer with the others and gone back to work, because the dead—and now the living—deserved a carver with steady hands.

The knife moved. Cedar-smooth strokes, the raven's wing taking feather-shape. Hazel had learned, in the year of silence, to hear the work differently—to feel the grain's resistance as a kind of conversation, wordless and patient. It was not the chorus. It would never be the chorus. But it filled the hands, and the hands filled the hours, and the hours were good ones.

The bell over the door rang just past midmorning.

"Open already?" said Tam Voss from the chandlery, letting himself in with the confidence of a man who had been coming here weekly since the shop opened. He set a wrapped parcel on the counter. "Tallow candles, as promised. And I need another one of those anchor charms before my daughter's wedding. Her fellow's a deepwater man and she wants him carrying something of her mother's."

"For luck?"

"For *her*," Tam said, wounded. "Luck's for gamblers."

Hazel laughed and took his order, quoted him a fair price, argued him up two coppers out of principle, and sent him off whistling. This was the other thing the silence had taught them: how much of their old life had been spent flinching. Flinching from clients who wanted too much, from neighbors who might notice the gift, from the whole bright loud world of the living that Hazel had treated like weather to be endured rather than a place to live. Now a chandler could come in and argue about prices and Hazel could enjoy it—actually *enjoy* it—with no ghost whispering that Tam Voss cheated on his taxes or nursed a grudge against his brother. People were allowed their mysteries now. Hazel found they liked people better for it.

Brannic came down from the loft around noon, where he'd been reorganizing the stockroom according to a system that made sense only to ex-soldiers and possibly not even to them. He was thinner than he'd been in the war years, softer at the middle in a way he pretended to resent, his hair going gray at the temples in streaks he refused to call handsome even though Hazel told him so often enough that it had stopped embarrassing either of them. The scar from the crossbow bolt showed white at his collar when he stretched.

"Lunch," he announced, setting down bread, hard cheese, and the pickled things he loved and Hazel tolerated.

"You're a tyrant," Hazel said, washing the bone dust from their hands. "I was at a delicate stage."

"The raven's been at a delicate stage since Tuesday."

"It's a *complex* bird."

"It's a bird," Brannic said, "that has watched you work for four days and developed opinions." He tore the bread, handed Hazel the larger half without looking, the way he did everything—carelessly, precisely, like a man who had unlearned ceremony but not attention. They ate at the bench with their shoulders touching, watching the harbor fog burn off.

"Do you ever miss it?" Brannic asked. He asked this maybe twice a year, always the same way—flat, unhurried, giving Hazel full room to lie if they needed to.

Hazel considered the question honestly, because he deserved honesty and because lying to Brannic had become, over one strange terrible luminous year, nearly impossible. His face was too readable. All those years of imperial discipline, and the man's heart sat right behind his eyes like a lamp behind glass.

"Sometimes," Hazel said. "In the evenings, mostly. The quiet gets... large. Like a room I'm not sure I furnished." They turned the bread crust between their fingers. "But then I think about the last thing I heard. All those names going into the Door. Ten thousand voices saying *remember us*, and then—" Hazel snapped their fingers softly. "Silence. My silence. The price paid in full." They looked up at him. "I don't regret it. I want that on the record, Kavanagh. Whatever the quiet does to me at three in the morning, I don't regret it."

"I know," Brannic said. "I keep the record." He tapped his own chest, where, Hazel knew, he kept a folded page torn from the prophecy book—the page with the final clause, the price, written in a dead archivist's careful hand. He'd offered to burn it a dozen times. Hazel had always said no. You didn't burn the receipt for the most expensive thing you'd ever bought. You kept it, and you looked at it sometimes, and you remembered what you'd purchased: a country, a name, a morning like this one with bread crumbs on the workbench and a man beside you who stayed.

The bell rang again in the afternoon, and this time it was a child.

She was perhaps seven, gap-toothed, windburned, with the fierce self-possession of children raised on working docks. She marched in past the counter, planted herself in front of the display of finished pieces—ravens and anchors and tiny carved doors, all of them holding nothing but love—and pointed at the smallest door with the imperiousness of a queen selecting tribute.

"How much for that one?"

"Three coppers," Hazel said. "It's for keeping things in. Secrets, mostly. Small ones."

The girl dug in her pocket, produced two coppers and a button, and negotiated with the ruthless skill of her class. Hazel accepted two coppers and the promise of the third by week's end, wrapped the little door in cloth, and would have sent her on her way—but the girl lingered, scuffing one boot against the floorboards, working up to something.

"My gran says you're the one," she said finally. "From the stories. She says you talked to the dead. She says bones *sang* for you." Her eyes had gone wide and hungry. "Is it true? Do bones really sing?"

Hazel set down the knife.

There it was. The question. It came maybe once a season, from children mostly, sometimes from drunk sailors, once from a visiting scholar who had wept openly in the shop and been gently given tea and sent home. And every time, Hazel felt the silence rise in them like water in a well—all that vast furnished room of quiet—and had to decide, again, what to do with it.

A year ago, they would have lied smoothly. *Just stories. Bones are bones.* A year ago, invisibility had been survival, and survival had been the whole of the art.

But Greymarrow remembered itself now. And so, increasingly, did Hazel.

"Bones are bones," Hazel said gravely, "if you treat them right. If you carve them with care, and give them away to people you love, they hold what you put in them. That's the whole magic there is, and it's plenty." They leaned on the counter. "As for singing—you'll have to ask me again when you're older."

The girl squinted at them, sensing—children always sensed—a door deliberately closed but not locked. "When's older?"

"When you've lost somebody," Hazel said, "and loved somebody, and done at least one thing you're sorry for. Then ask me again. I'll probably tell you the truth."

The girl considered these terms, found them acceptable, pocketed her little door, and left with the bell jangling behind her. Through the window Hazel watched her run down the lane toward the harbor, holding the cloth bundle against her chest like something alive.

Brannic appeared at Hazel's shoulder, having heard everything from the stockroom doorway with the studied nonchalance of a man pretending to inventory rope.

"'Ask me again when you're older,'" he repeated. "Very mysterious. Very wise. Also completely evasive."

"She's seven, Brannic. What was I going to say? *Yes, child, the dead spoke to me for thirty years, and then I fed every voice I ever loved into a door at the bottom of the world, and now I can't hear my own heartbeat echo, and some nights the quiet is so big I have to light every candle in the house—*"

"Yes," Brannic said simply. "That's what I'd have said."

Hazel looked at him. He looked back, entirely unrepentant, gray-streaked and scarred and steady as a keel.

"Well," Hazel said, "you're not seven."

"No," he agreed. "I'm worse. I'm a clerk." He kissed the top of their head, bone dust and all, and went back upstairs, and Hazel stood a moment longer in the warm dust-colored light, hand resting on the cool curve of the unfinished raven.

*Complex bird,* they thought. *Take your time.*

* * *

Evening came early in autumn, and by lamplight Hazel did the day's last task, the one that had become ritual without their ever deciding it: they took the prophecy book down from the shelf.

It sat where it had sat for a year, between the jar of carving oil and the little clay door Brannic had bought from a potter in the capital—*so you have a spare,* he'd said, *in case you ever miss the big one.* The book was the archivist's copy, stolen on the worst night of Hazel's old life, its leather cracked, its pages soft as moth wings. Scholars had begged to study it. Hazel let them copy it, page by page, under supervision, in this room, with tea. But the book itself stayed here. It had earned the right to retire.

Hazel turned to the end, as they always did, and read the last lines, as they always did, though they knew them by heart:

*...and the Eastwick child shall stand at the threshold, and give what cannot be spared, and the name shall be spoken, and the Door shall—*

And there it stopped. Mid-sentence. Mid-word, almost—the pen lifting clean off the vellum a hundred and eighty years ago, leaving a small ink-star where the next letter should have begun.

Every scholar who had copied the book had noted it. *Damaged manuscript,* they wrote. *Final leaf lost. Textual lacuna, date unknown.* One young archivist had spent a whole afternoon with a magnifying glass and left pale and shaken, insisting the break wasn't damage at all, that the ink hadn't faded but *stopped*, that the sentence ended the way a held breath ends.

Hazel had their own theory, which they shared with no one, not even Brannic. The prophecy had been written by a listener—one of the old bone-hearers, surely; the voice in the text was too much like the voice that used to live in Hazel's own hands. And listeners, Hazel knew better than anyone now, did not write endings. They wrote up to the edge of what the dead would tell them, and then they lifted the pen, because some things were not predictions. They were invitations. The last line of any true prophecy was always blank, and it was always meant to be filled in by whoever was brave enough, or foolish enough, or loving enough, to live it.

*...and the Door shall—*

Shall what? Close? Open? Remember?

Hazel ran a thumb along the ink-star, that small black pause a century and eight decades wide, and felt—just for a moment, just at the edge of the great quiet, the way you sometimes feel rain coming in an old wound—a faint stirring, like a word trying to remember itself.

Then it passed. Or waited. Hazel honestly could no longer tell the difference, and had stopped needing to.

They closed the book, put it back on the shelf between the oil and the little clay door, and blew out the lamp. Downstairs, Brannic was banking the stove and talking to the cat he swore he hadn't adopted, and beyond the walls Greymarrow settled into its evening—bells, gulls, the ferryman's song in a language everyone had always known—and Hazel stood in the dark a moment longer, listening to all of it with the only ears they had left.

The quiet was large. But it was furnished now. And somewhere in it, at the very edge, a sentence waited, patient as bone, for the day someone asked again.

*Ask me when you're older,* Hazel thought, smiling in the dark, and went downstairs to the light.
