## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The first rule of surviving Vespergate was to be useful. The second rule was to be unremarkable. Noor Nakamura had spent twenty years obeying both, and had gotten so good at it that some mornings they forgot there had ever been a third option.

This was not one of those mornings. This was a Tuesday, and Tuesdays meant the prince ate eggs.

Noor arrived at the palace kitchens before the bells finished striking five, as they always did, and stood at the prep table while Cook's apprentices pretended not to notice them, as they always did. There was a particular way the kitchen went quiet around a food-taster — not hostile, exactly, but careful, the way a room goes careful around a doctor or a debt collector. Nobody wanted to be the one whose sauce killed the prince. Nobody wanted to be the one whose sauce was *accused* of killing the prince, either. Noor had watched a pastry chef weep over a suspected almond once, and had privately thought the tears were excessive, but that was before they'd learned the man had seven children and a gambling problem and the palace had very little patience for either.

"Morning, Nakamura," said Cook, who was the only one who spoke to them by name. "Eggs, as ordered. The prince asked for the pepper sauce."

"The prince always asks for the pepper sauce."

"The prince always gets what the prince asks for, and you always stand there looking like you're about to tell him he can't have it."

Noor allowed themselves the ghost of a smile. "One day the pepper sauce will be poisoned, and everyone will be very sorry they laughed."

"Will you be sorry?"

"I'll be *vindicated*," Noor said, "which is nearly as good."

The ritual began, as it always did, with the hands. Noor washed in water that had been boiled and cooled, dried on a cloth that lived in their own pocket and nowhere else, and then stood with their palms open at their sides for the space of ten breaths — long enough for any residue of the previous night's work to settle, long enough for their pulse to slow into the particular patience the work required. Tasting was not chewing. Tasting was a conversation conducted at the threshold of the mouth, and like all good conversations it required you to listen more than you spoke.

They began with the bread. A corner torn from the heel, held on the tongue, counted to thirty. Flour, water, salt, the faint sour of the starter that Cook guarded like a state secret. No almond — bitter almonds were the classic, and the classic was what lazy assassins used, and Vespergate had not produced a lazy assassin in Noor's lifetime. No iron. No copper. No sweetness that didn't belong. They swallowed, or didn't; that was the taster's arithmetic, done a hundred times a morning without ever being written down. A little of this, none of that. A body was a ledger, and Noor's had been balanced with more care than the royal treasury.

The eggs next. Then the butter. Then the preserves, which were fig, and figs were treacherous because figs were sweet enough to hide most anything, so Noor held the fig preserve on their tongue for a full sixty count and let it tell them everything it knew. Sun. Orchard. A whisper of the wine the cook had deglazed the pan with. Nothing else.

Then the pepper sauce, which was where the morning went wrong.

Noor knew the pepper sauce the way they knew their own heartbeat. Charred red peppers, vinegar, a thread of honey, the particular smoky heat that came from the long peppers grown on the palace's southern wall. They had tasted it four hundred mornings running. They could have identified it blindfolded, upside down, mid-sneeze.

This morning, underneath it — so far underneath it that Noor almost missed it, almost wrote it off as a bad night's sleep or a tongue still sour from the charms they'd brewed after midnight — there was something else.

Metallic. But not iron the way iron usually arrived, all blunt and bloody. This was a cold, thin metal, the taste of a coin left out in frost, of a blade kept sheathed too long. It sat at the very back of the throat and *hurt*, in the way a high note hurts, in the way a memory hurts.

Noor did not spit. Spitting was a statement, and statements were how tasters died. Instead they swallowed, set down the spoon with the same mild care they used for everything, and said, "The sauce is fine."

Cook grunted. The apprentices went back to their dough. The morning resumed its ordinary shape, and Noor stood in the middle of it holding the taste of something that did not exist.

Because that was the thing. Noor knew metals. Their body was a ledger of them — iron and copper and lead and mercury and arsenic, which was not a metal but liked to pretend, and antimony, and a dozen compounds whose names the palace physicians had taught them and whose flavors their tongue had catalogued with the devotion of a scholar. They had tasted every poison the city could produce and most of the ones it couldn't. This was not any of them.

This was mountain-iron. And mountain-iron did not exist, because the mountain did not exist.

Well. That was not strictly true. The mountain existed; it loomed over Vespergate the way a creditor looms over a debtor, vast and silent and patient. But nothing *came down* from the mountain. Nothing had come down from the mountain in two hundred years, not ore, not travelers, not so much as a rumor. The border wards saw to that — the great wards, the ones the city paid for, though Noor had never been able to learn who collected the payment. The mountain was a wall on the map and a shape on the horizon and nothing else. Its iron belonged to the same category of things as dragons and honest tax collectors: spoken of in stories, encountered by fools.

And yet it was in the prince's pepper sauce.

* * *

The rest of the tasting proceeded with a steadiness Noor was quietly proud of. Bread from the second baker. The honeyed plums. The cold chicken, the warm milk, the little dish of candied ginger the prince pretended not to like and ate first. Each item held on the tongue, counted, catalogued, swallowed or set aside. The mountain-iron did not appear again — or rather, Noor told themselves, it had never appeared at all, and the first time had been a fluke of a tired tongue, and if they checked the sauce one more time it would be because thoroughness was a virtue and not because their hands had gone slightly cold.

They checked the sauce three more times.

The third time, the taste was there again, faint as a fingerprint on glass, and Noor understood two things with terrible clarity. First: someone or something had gotten mountain-iron into the palace kitchens, in a quantity too small to poison anyone, in a form too strange to be an accident. Second: Noor was going to have to figure out how, because the alternative was lying awake every night for the rest of their life wondering.

They delivered their verdict to the steward — "All clear" — in the same flat tone they'd used every morning for six years, and walked out of the kitchens with their heart hammering like an apprentice late for bread.

* * *

Noor's rooms were above a shuttered dye shop in the Tanners' Row, which was not the worst address in Vespergate but was certainly in the running. The rent was cheap because the dye shop had failed and the street smelled of its failure. Noor liked it for the same reasons other people disliked it: nobody visited, nobody asked questions, and the smell covered the smell of the other work.

The other work was charms.

Officially, magic in Vespergate was a death sentence. Not a fine, not a flogging — the rope, or worse, depending on the magistrate's mood and the size of the working. The wardens had a list, and the list had categories, and the categories ran from *harmful* down to *cosmetic*, and the bottom of the list was still the rope. Noor had watched it happen once, from a crowd, to a woman who had charmed a ribbon to change color for a festival. Noor had been nineteen. Noor had never forgotten.

And yet. People got sick. People got unlucky. People lost children and husbands and harvests and hope, and the palace's answer was wards and taxes and a god who had stopped answering letters two centuries ago, and so the night market had grown its own answer, small and quiet and illegal: charms. Luck-knots and fever-salves and little woven protections against bad dreams and worse landlords. Nothing that would make the wardens' list worth reading past the first line. Small magics, small prices, no spectacle.

Noor made them in the evenings, in a back room with the shutters closed, from recipes their sister had left behind in a notebook written in a child's cipher that Noor had never stopped being able to read.

Wren. The name arrived, as it always did, without permission, and Noor set it down the way they set down everything — carefully, and out of sight.

Tonight's commission was a luck-knot for the lamplighter, old Tam Voss, who had taken a cough that rattled like a dropped coin and who paid, when he paid at all, in gossip and gratitude. Noor braided the knot from three cords — red for blood and life, gray for the road, white for the return — and murmured over it the words that weren't really words, just intention given shape, the way a mother's hum is a lullaby only to the child who knows it. The knot warmed between their fingers. It would not save Tam's life. It might make the cough gentler, the nights easier, the road home from the lamp-posts less treacherous. It was not much. It was not nothing.

That was the whole creed, as far as Noor had ever been able to articulate it: *not much, not nothing.* Magic as mercy, never spectacle. A charm that worked a little was worth ten that dazzled. And never, ever be seen.

They wrapped the knot in waxed cloth, blew out the lamp, and went down the back stairs into the dark.

* * *

The night market lived in the space behind the fishmongers' stalls, which began emptying around nine and finished emptying around ten, at which point a different market assembled in its place with the speed and discretion of fog. Noor had been coming here for six years and had never once seen it being set up. It simply *was*, whenever they arrived: a dozen blankets, two dozen sellers, a hundred customers who moved like they'd all agreed in advance to be forgettable.

Tam was waiting at the lamplighter's corner, wrapped in a coat that had been fashionable when the old king was young, coughing into a rag with the practiced rhythm of a man who had made peace with his lungs and was now merely negotiating terms.

"Nakamura." His voice was gravel in a sack. "You came."

"You paid."

"Half paid. The rest when I'm better, which gives you excellent motivation to make this thing work." He took the waxed cloth, unwrapped it, and held the knot up to his lamp — the one lamp in Vespergate Noor trusted, because Tam cleaned its glass himself and had opinions about light the way other men had opinions about wine. The knot caught the glow and threw it back warm.

"It's not a cure," Noor said, the way they always said it. "It's a kindness. To your body. It'll ask the fever to sit down and be reasonable."

Tam laughed, which turned into coughing, which turned into laughing again, a loop Noor had seen before and would see, they suspected, until the loop stopped. "A kindness. Twenty years in this city and the only person selling kindness is an illegal one." He pressed coins into Noor's hand — too many, as always — and Noor pressed half of them back, as always, and the argument lasted the usual ninety seconds and ended the usual way, with Tam declaring that Noor was a terrible businesswoman and Noor agreeing that this was why the business stayed alive.

Then, at the edge of the blanket-light, a child.

She couldn't have been more than eight, in a coat that had been cut for someone twice her size, standing with the particular stillness of a child who has learned that wanting things out loud is expensive. Noor knew that stillness. Noor had *been* that stillness, once, on a different street, in a different decade, watching a charm-seller's blanket and calculating exactly how much hunger was worth the risk.

"What have you got?" Noor asked, gently.

The child said nothing. She looked at the luck-knots, the fever-salves, the little glass beads that were supposed to keep nightmares from settling in the corners of a room, and her eyes did the arithmetic of a person who has nothing.

Noor picked out the smallest charm on the blanket — a knotted thread, gray and white, meant to keep a body warm on cold nights, which was a lie the way all the best charms were lies: it didn't make heat, it just helped a body stop *losing* it — and held it out.

"That's two coppers," Noor said. "But I'm feeling generous, and you look like a girl who knows a secret, so I'll trade it for one."

The child's eyes narrowed with the deep suspicion of the very poor. "A secret about what?"

"Anything. Something I don't know. Something true."

The child considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she leaned in, and whispered, with the fierce confidence of absolute certainty: "The cats on Tanner's Row can talk. They just don't like you."

Noor kept their face grave. "That explains a great deal," they said, and gave her the charm, and the child took it and ran, and Noor sat there for a moment in the fish-smelling dark feeling something in their chest that was either warmth or warning.

They were still deciding which when they noticed the stranger.

He was across the lane, in the shadow between a shuttered chandlery and a wall that had given up on whitewash, and he was watching. Not browsing — the night market's customers browsed, drifted, pretended to be elsewhere. This man stood the way a nail stands in wood. Still. Aimed. He was big through the shoulders, with the ruined hands of a man who had once done fine work and now did none, and even at this distance, even in this light, there was something in the set of his face that Noor's body recognized before their mind caught up.

He was looking at the charms. Not at Noor — at the *charms*. At the knots and salves and threads, with an expression Noor had only ever seen on two kinds of people: the devout, and the ruined.

Noor packed up. Slowly. The way a person packs up who has nowhere in particular to be and nothing in particular to fear. Blanket folded, basket loaded, charms tucked away in their waxed cloth, every motion an ordinary motion performed by an ordinary seller of ordinary trinkets. The stranger did not move. He did not follow, either — Noor checked, the way they always checked, three turns and a mirror and the old trick of stopping to retie a bootlace in a dark doorway — and by the time Noor climbed the back stairs to their rooms above the dead dye shop, the lane behind them was empty and had probably been empty the whole time.

Probably.

They barred the door. They sat on the bed with the notebook in their lap — Wren's notebook, the child's cipher marching across the page in loops like birds — and they did not open it. They almost never opened it. Opening it was a door that didn't close again.

But the taste of the morning was still at the back of their throat. Cold and thin, a coin left in frost. Mountain-iron, in the prince's pepper sauce, in the heart of a city that had walled the mountain out for two hundred years. And a stranger with ruined hands watching charms in the dark like a man reading scripture.

Noor had survived twenty years by knowing which changes were weather and which were season. The itch at the back of the neck, the taste that shouldn't exist, the stillness in the shadows — any one of them alone was weather.

All three in a single day was a season turning.

They put the notebook away unopened, lay down in the dark, and did not sleep. Somewhere below the city — below the streets, below the sewers, below the old mine shafts that everyone agreed had been empty since their grandparents' grandparents — something was very quietly, very patiently, beginning to move.

And Noor Nakamura, useful and unremarkable, lay awake above Tanner's Row and listened to the cats not talking, and understood that the ledger of their careful life had just been opened to a new page.
