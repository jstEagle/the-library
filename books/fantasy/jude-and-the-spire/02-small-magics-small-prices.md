## 2. Small Magics, Small Prices

The night market in Vespergate began the way all dangerous things did: quietly, and one street at a time.

By the time Noor reached Lamplighters' Row, the sun had gone down behind the wall and the legal market had packed up its honest cabbages and honest fish, leaving the cobblestones to the other kind of trade. Lanterns bloomed along the eaves—real ones, oil-fed, because the lamplighters of this quarter took their work personally—and beneath them the stalls unfolded like moths from a coat: a woman selling secondhand dreams by the thimbleful, a boy with a tray of keys that opened locks nobody had built yet, an old man whose entire inventory was three jars of weather, labeled *fog*, *drizzle*, and *one very good morning*.

Noor's stall was smaller than most. A folding table, a strip of canvas, a candle stub burned down to a thumbnail. The goods were arranged with the deliberate carelessness of someone who had practiced looking unremarkable for twenty years: knotted cords, pressed-flower sachets, river stones with holes worn through them, a jar of honey that was only honey. Nothing glowed. Nothing hummed. If a warden walked past—and wardens did walk past, twice nightly on this stretch—the whole display could pass for a grandmother's attic emptied onto cloth.

Which was, in a sense, exactly what it was.

"Evening, Taster," said the dream-woman, whose name was Bess and who called everyone Taster whether they tasted things or not. "You look terrible."

"You're too kind."

"No, really. Like something followed you home and you let it sleep on your chest."

"That's just my face."

Bess laughed and turned to her next customer, and Noor set out the evening's small inventory with fingers that knew each knot without looking. That was the trick of it—the only trick Noor allowed. Every charm on the table was harmless. Genuinely, verifiably harmless, and not merely because harm would bring the wardens. Harm was against the code.

A luck-knot could nudge a coin flip, soften a stumble, keep rain off a wedding for exactly as long as the vows lasted. It could not make a man win at cards. A sleep-sachet smelled of lavender and chamomile and made the difference between lying awake and drifting; it could not put anyone under deep enough to rob them. The holed stones were for homesickness. You held one, thought hard about a place you missed, and for a moment the stone grew warm and smelled faintly of that place—bread from your mother's oven, salt from a harbor you'd left behind. That was all. A moment's warmth. Noor had spent two decades calibrating mercy down to the gram.

Because spectacle was death. Mercy, kept small enough, was invisible.

* * *

The lamplighter came at the third bell, the way he always came at the third bell, which was precisely when a man trying to hide how badly he felt should have been home in bed.

Tam Osgood had lit lamps in this quarter for thirty-one years. He had a ladder he loved more than his children, according to his children, who said it fondly. He was sixty-one years old and coughing in a way that Noor recognized the way a locksmith recognizes a broken key—not the sound of it but the shape underneath. Something worn through. Something nearly done.

"Evening, Noor." He lowered himself onto the stool across the table with the care of a man descending into cold water. Up close he smelled of lamp oil and fever-sweetness, that particular scent the body gives off when it has begun spending itself faster than it can earn.

"Evening, Tam." Noor slid the tea over before he asked. Ginger and honey, brewed strong. "How's the ladder?"

"The ladder's fine. The ladder doesn't snore." He drank, and the drinking cost him something; Noor watched him pay it and pretend not to. "I hear you've got new knots in."

"I've always got knots in."

"Bess says these are better than usual. Says she held one and her bad knee went quiet for a full minute."

"Bess is a merchant. She inflates everything. Her dreams especially."

Tam smiled, then stopped smiling, because smiling pulled at whatever lived under his ribs now. He looked at the table. He looked at the luck-knots, braided in three strands—red for blood, gold for coin, blue for the road home—a pattern old enough that no warden bothered memorizing it anymore.

"How much for the big one?" he asked.

"The red-and-gold one?"

"Mm."

"It depends what you want it to do."

"I want it to do what luck-knots do." He said it lightly, which meant he'd thought about it heavily. "I want my granddaughter's christening to go well next week. I want the rain to hold off. I want—" the cough came then, doubled him briefly at the waist, and Noor waited it out without touching him, because being touched while coughing was a pity Tam would not forgive, "—I want to climb my ladder eleven more times. Twelve if the weather's fair. There's twelve lamps on Coppersmith Street I've never once missed, and I don't propose to start."

The silence after that had weight. Down the row, the weather-seller was arguing gently with a customer about whether fog counted as a bargain at any price.

Noor picked up the red-and-gold knot and turned it over. Three strands, seven crossings, tied loose at the heart so it could be undone without cutting—which mattered, because luck-knots ended when they were cut, and some things deserved gentler endings.

"Twelve lamps," Noor said. "That's not much luck. That's barely any luck at all. That's practically just weather forecasting."

"That's all I'm asking."

"Then it'll hold. Rain off the christening, sure footing on the rungs, and a quiet knee for Bess if she ever borrows it." Noor pushed it across the table. "Four coppers."

They both knew four coppers was nothing. They both also knew that Tam Osgood would count out those four coppers one at a time, from a purse that had grown thin in ways that had nothing to do with money, and that letting him pay was part of the charm working at all. Magic given free curdled into charity, and charity sat in a man's stomach like a stone. Noor had learned that early, from a sister who'd been better at this than they'd ever been.

Wren would have charged him too. Wren would have charged him four coppers and then slipped eight back into his coat pocket while shaking his hand, and grinned about it afterward, and Noor's chest did the thing it always did at the memory—closed, like a fist around a coal.

Tam counted the coins. Each one clicked against the table like a small promise.

"There," he said. "Robbed blind in my own neighborhood. My wife will laugh herself sick."

"Tell her I threw in a discount for the snoring."

He tucked the knot inside his shirt, against the skin, where luck-knots liked to live, and stood up slowly, holding the table edge a moment longer than a man should need to. When he'd gone—ladder on his shoulder, moving slower than last month but still moving—Noor sat with the four coppers and felt the familiar arithmetic settle: another day invisible, another small mercy spent carefully, another evening closer to whatever it was Noor was actually saving toward. Because everything was saving toward something. Twenty years of tasting poison a drop at a time, twenty years of tying charms too small to hang a man for—none of it was a life, exactly. It was a preparation wearing a life's clothes.

Noor just hadn't let themselves finish the sentence about what for.

* * *

The child came near midnight.

Noor saw her before she meant to be seen—a scrap of shadow at the mouth of Tanner's Alley, thin as a folded letter. Most nights the hungry ones worked the market edges in twos and threes, quick hands and quicker feet, and everyone pretended not to notice because everyone had been fifteen once. This one wasn't stealing. This one was watching Noor's table the way starving people watch bread: without hope, on principle, so the disappointment wouldn't kill them first.

She crept closer in stages, each stage pretending to be interested in something else. The jar of weather. The honey. Then, finally, close enough to speak.

"What do the stones do?" Her voice had the rusted quality of disuse.

"They remember places." Noor held one out flat on their palm. "Hold it. Think of somewhere you'd rather be."

She hesitated—hungry children learn early that gifts have teeth—but hunger of the soul kind is bolder than hunger of the body kind, and she touched the stone.

Noor watched her face change. Whatever place she'd thought of, it must have been a good one, or a lost one, which amounts to the same thing. Her eyes went wet and furious about being wet.

"It's warm," she accused.

"Just for a moment."

"Just for a moment," she repeated, bitter as vinegar, and set the stone down with exaggerated care, the way you put down something you can't afford to want.

"Take it," Noor said.

"Don't have no coppers."

"I know. Take it anyway."

Her chin came up, all the pride a starved body could spare. "Nothing's free."

"This isn't free." Noor leaned forward. "It costs you remembering a good place. That's the price. It's steep. Are you sure you can afford it?"

Something flickered in her face—the very young, very old suspicion of a joke at her expense—but the stone was still warm on the table between them, still smelling, faintly, of wherever she'd sent it. She took it. She closed her fist around it like a bird she'd caught.

"You're the charm-seller," she said. "People say you're a mage."

"People say lots of things. People say the moon's made of cheese and that Alderman Voss pays his debts."

"Are you?"

Noor looked at her—at the hollows of her cheeks, at the fist clenched white around a river stone worth less than the copper it refused to cost—and gave the answer that had kept them alive for twenty years.

"I'm a taster," Noor said. "I taste the prince's food. That's all anybody needs me to be."

She frowned, unconvinced, and Noor understood the frown. Children could smell the truth the way dogs smelled fear; it was adults, trained and civilized, who swallowed lies whole. But the girl was too tired to press, and the stone was warm, and she backed away toward her alley with it, pausing once to look back—not at Noor, Noor noticed, but at the table, cataloguing, the way the hungry catalogue sources.

"Eat something," Noor told her.

"You first," she said, and vanished.

Noor sat back and exhaled slowly. *Magic as mercy, never spectacle.* Wren's rule, originally. Wren had written it out once in charcoal on the back of the shop door, back when there'd been a shop, back when there'd been a door: *Small magics, small prices. Never give them a reason to look.* And then the court had looked anyway, and the trial had lasted half a day, and the rule had survived its author by twenty years and counting.

Noor blew out the candle stub. Time to pack up, go home, bar the door, sleep badly, wake before dawn to taste a prince's breakfast. The ordinary machinery of survival, oiled and running.

Except.

Except that the feeling wouldn't shake. The feeling of being watched—not the general watchfulness of a market full of thieves and wardens, which Noor could sleep through, but something specific and patient, aimed. It had started at the edge of the third bell, maybe earlier. It was still there now, in the dark past the lantern-light, and it had weight the way a gaze has weight when it belongs to someone who knows what they're looking at.

Noor didn't turn around. Turning around was confirmation; turning around invited conversation; conversation invited questions. Instead Noor began packing the stall with the unhurried economy of long practice—cords coiled, stones jarred, honey stoppered—while listening with the whole surface of the skin.

There. Behind Bess's stall, in the alley-shadow where the lantern light gave up. Someone standing. Someone who had been standing there, Noor realized with a slow cold slide of the stomach, since before the child came. Since before Tam. Long enough to see a luck-knot sold. Long enough to see a stone given away free.

Long enough to know that neither of those things was possible.

Noor's hands stayed steady. Twenty years of steady hands; they'd hold even now. But under the steadiness the old fear woke and stretched, the fear with teeth marks in it, the fear that wore a courtroom's face. *Never give them a reason to look.*

Someone had looked.

Noor finished packing, closed the canvas over the table, lifted the candle stub and the coin purse, and walked—unhurried, yawning, a tired vendor going home—out of the pool of lantern light and down the row, counting steps, counting shadows, feeling that patient attention follow like a draft from a door left open somewhere behind the world.

At the corner of Coppersmith Street, Noor glanced back once, casually, a person checking the weather.

The alley was empty.

Of course it was empty. But on the cobbles where the stranger had stood, faint in the spilled lantern light, something remained: a scorch-mark, small and neat as a coin, still smoking faintly at the edges. Not a torch had dropped it. Not any lamp Noor knew.

Ward-smith's work, said a voice in Noor's memory, a younger voice, a voice that belonged to someone dead. *That's ward-sign, Noor. Somebody's been measuring.*

Noor stood in the dark a long moment, four coppers in one pocket and a sister's rule in the other, and understood that the arithmetic of invisibility had just changed.

Tomorrow, the prince's breakfast. Tomorrow, the careful, tasteless, survivable day. But tonight, walking home through a city that had begun, very softly, to watch them back, Noor permitted themselves one honest thought:

*Whoever you are—you saw mercy tonight. Pray you don't report it.*
