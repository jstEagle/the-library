## 3. The Night the Stars Went Out

Noor had a theory about fear: it was like salt in a stew. You couldn't take it out once it was in, but you could learn to cook with it until nobody noticed it was there.

They had been cooking with it for twenty years.

That evening, the fear-salt sat quietly at the bottom of them while they did what they always did after dark: counted their charms. It was a ritual as fixed as prayer and considerably more practical. The shop behind the shop—the real one, the one that didn't exist on any tax roll—had a back wall of pegs, and every peg held something small and harmless-looking. A knotted red thread to turn away a fever. A river-stone drilled through the middle, strung on leather, to keep nightmares from settling in a sleeper's chest like birds on a wire. A twist of wax and dried yarrow that made a door refuse to open for anyone meaning harm.

None of it was magic, if you asked the wardens. None of it was *anything*, if you asked the law. The law said magic meant death, and so Noor had spent two decades making certain that everything they sold could be explained away by a sufficiently tired magistrate. Knots were just knots. Stones were just stones. If you tied them right, though—if you tied them with the right breath, at the right hour, thinking the right stubborn thought—they held a little of you inside them, and a little of you was enough to do small kindnesses.

It had been enough for twenty years. Noor checked each charm anyway, the way a sailor checks rigging in calm weather, because calm weather is exactly when checking matters.

The luck-knot they'd made for Tam Wicker, the old lamplighter, hung on its own peg near the window. He was dying—slowly, the way lamp-oil burns down rather than all at once—and he'd come to the night market three evenings running before Noor finally gave him the knot and refused his coin. "You'll pay me," Noor had told him, "by outliving my expectations. I've set them insultingly low. You'll manage."

Tam had laughed, which turned into coughing, which turned into laughing again, and gone home with the knot around his neck where his lamp-key used to hang.

Noor touched it now, lightly, the way you touch something you're proud of without wanting to admit it. Still holding. Good. His lungs were bad but his luck was worse, and the knot would tip the balance where it could.

Outside, Vespergate was doing what Vespergate did at night: grumbling, glowing, going about its business under the border wards. From the little window above the workbench Noor could see the wards themselves, if they knew how to look—and Noor always knew how to look. They weren't visible, exactly. They were more like the memory of light, a faint tension in the air along the city's rim, the way a spiderweb announces itself only when the sun catches it sideways. The wards ran up the mountainside and over the walls and out into the dark hills beyond, a great slow heartbeat of borrowed power, keeping out everything the founders had been afraid of.

Everyone in Vespergate knew the wards kept out monsters. Almost no one asked what fed them. Noor hadn't asked either, not really, not for twenty years. Asking questions was how you ended up like Wren.

They didn't finish the thought. They never finished the thought. Twenty years of practice had worn a smooth channel around it, the way water wears around a stone.

* * *

The knock came an hour past midnight, which was wrong in three ways.

First, nobody knocked on Noor's door. Customers found them at the night market or not at all; the door was for neighbors, and the neighbors knew better than to need anything after midnight. Second, the knock was polite—two soft raps, apologetic almost—which meant it wasn't the wardens, who knocked like they were trying to arrest the door itself. Third, and worst, none of Noor's threshold charms had whispered to them first. The wax-and-yarrow twist on the lintel hummed faintly whenever anyone crossed the street below. It had been silent.

Noor stood very still in the dark of the workshop, one hand flat on the bench, listening the way they listened when tasting: whole body, held breath, attention poured out like oil.

The knock came again. Two raps. Then a voice, low and cracked and familiar.

"Noor. It's Tam. I know the hour. I'm sorry about the hour."

Noor let out a breath and unbarred the door, and there stood Tam Wicker in the blue dark, wrapped in a coat too heavy for the season, his face gray as tallow. The luck-knot glinted at his throat.

"You should be asleep," Noor said. "You're supposed to be sleeping twelve hours a day and complaining about it. That was the terms."

"I was asleep." Tam's eyes were strange—not frightened exactly, but wide in a way Noor had never seen on him, and Tam had been a lamplighter for forty years and had seen every kind of night a city could produce. "I woke up because my lamp went out."

"Your lamp goes out when you forget to fill it. Which is often. I've watched you fill it the way other men watch horse races."

"Not the street lamp." Tam's voice dropped. "All of them, Noor. Every lamp on Lantern Row went out at once. And then—" He stopped, swallowed. "Come and look at the sky."

Noor looked up instead, right there in the doorway, over Tam's shoulder.

And felt the world tilt.

The stars were gone.

Not clouded—there wasn't a cloud worth the name from here to the Spire. Not hidden. *Gone*, the whole field of them, every constellation Noor had learned as a child at Wren's elbow, every star the sailors steered by, every one the astronomers on the hill charged good coin to chart. The sky was a smooth black cloth pulled tight over the world, and through it nothing shone, nothing winked, nothing breathed.

"That's not possible," Noor said, and heard how thin their voice came out.

"I know," Tam said gently, as though Noor were the dying one. "But there it is."

And then Noor felt the second thing, the thing that mattered more, the thing that turned vertigo into cold: the wards were falling.

They didn't crash. That was the terrible part. A wall comes down with noise and dust and you can run from it. The wards came down the way a cut string falls—no drama, just a sudden absence of tension, a long slow sigh along the mountainside that Noor felt in their teeth more than heard. For twenty years that tension had been part of the background of their life, as constant and unnoticed as their own pulse. Now it simply wasn't there, and the silence of it rang louder than any alarm.

Every hair on Noor's arms stood up. Every instinct they owned lit at once like lamps on a dark street.

"Get inside," Noor said. "Lock your door. Bar it. Don't open it for anyone tonight, Tam, I mean it—not for me, not for the watch, not for anyone crying help. Tomorrow, maybe. Tonight, no."

"What's happening?" Tam asked. "What does this mean?"

"I don't know." And then, because lying to Tam felt like kicking a dog: "I know some things it might mean, and none of them are good, and the best thing you can do is be behind a locked door with your luck-knot on until sunrise. Can you do that?"

Tam looked at them for a long moment. Forty years of lighting lamps gives a man a certain talent for seeing in the dark, and Noor had the uncomfortable feeling of being seen straight through.

"You know more than you're saying," Tam said. It wasn't an accusation. It was almost fond. "You always did. All right. Locked door. But Noor—" He gripped their wrist, his hand dry and light as paper. "Whatever you're not saying. Be careful of it."

He shuffled off into the starless dark, and Noor barred the door behind him and stood with their forehead against the wood for a long moment, breathing.

* * *

By the time the bells started, half the city was already awake and screaming about it.

Vespergate had eleven bells and a strict opinion about when each should ring, and at half past midnight the great alarm bell in the Spire began tolling with a rhythm Noor had never heard outside of drills: three fast, one slow. Three fast, one slow. *The walls. The walls. Look to the walls.*

From the window, Noor watched the city come apart in orderly panic. Watchmen ran the streets with torches that seemed suddenly, desperately small. Families spilled onto their stoops in nightclothes, pointing up at the blank sky and then out toward the walls, where the garrison was lighting beacon-fires that threw wild shadows on the stone. Somewhere a child was crying in the flat, exhausted way of a child who has been crying long enough to have moved past comfort. Somewhere else, a priest was shouting scripture at nobody, his voice cracking on the high notes.

And through it all, threaded under it like a bass note, Noor felt the wards—or felt where the wards had been. There was a hollowness now along the city's rim, a shape of absence, the way a missing tooth occupies the tongue. Power had been flowing there, steady as a river, for longer than anyone living could remember. Now the riverbed lay empty, and Noor—who had never once in twenty years let themselves think directly about what filled that riverbed—found the thought rising anyway, unstoppable, the way a cork rises:

*Something stopped paying.*

The wards hadn't decayed. Decay took years; Noor had watched a badly-tied charm decay, had watched it go threadbare and leaky and sad. This was different. This was withdrawal. Something upstream had closed a tap, and the whole vast mechanism of Vespergate's safety was draining dry, and whatever was upstream—

The mountain. Everyone knew the mountain court existed beneath the Spire's foundations, older than the city, older than the crown, spoken of in the tone people used for debts and diseases. Everyone knew you didn't trouble it and it didn't trouble you.

Noor pressed both hands flat against the windowsill and made themselves breathe slowly, four counts in, six counts out, the way they did when a dish tasted almost-but-not-quite right and they needed their tongue to be honest.

Then the charms burned.

All of them. At once.

It happened between one heartbeat and the next. The fever-thread on its peg flared red and crumbled to ash. The nightmare-stone split with a sound like ice cracking. The wax-and-yarrow twist on the lintel went up in a puff of sweet smoke and was gone. On the workbench, the half-made charms—the ones waiting for tomorrow's market—curled and blackened like leaves in a flame, and Noor snatched their hands back and stood staring at twenty years of careful, patient, invisible work destroying itself in the space of a breath.

Not burning, though. That was the thing Noor understood a moment later, standing in the smell of scorched herbs and hot wax. Burning was destruction. This was *spending*. Every charm had emptied itself at once, poured itself out into—into what? Into the night? Into the hollow where the wards had been? Like candles throwing themselves at a darkness too big to notice them?

Or like soldiers, Noor thought, and didn't like the thought, and had it anyway. Like soldiers told to hold a line.

When the last wisp of smoke cleared, Noor's back wall of pegs stood bare except for one item: Tam's luck-knot, hanging untouched, still faintly warm, still holding. Everything else was ash.

And here was the thing that made Noor sit down slowly on the workbench stool, the thing that made their hands shake in a way they hadn't allowed since they were nineteen years old and learning what grief tasted like:

For twenty years, those charms had been hiding them.

Not the shop. Them. Every knot and stone and twist carried a little of Noor inside it—a little of the quiet, constant, low-burning magic that lived in their hands whether they wanted it to or not—and that little bit of them, spread across dozens of small objects, read to any trained sense as nothing at all. Background noise. Candle-smoke. A person of no consequence selling trinkets of no consequence in a city where consequence meant death. The charms had been a mask worn in pieces, and Noor had forgotten, somewhere across two decades, that they were wearing it.

Now the mask was ash, and Noor Nakamura sat alone in a bare room under a starless sky, and for the first time in twenty years they were *legible*. Anyone who knew how to look—any warden with a trained eye, any smith who'd ever worked a ward-line, anyone at all with the sense they were born with—could walk past this window and feel exactly what Noor was.

Illegal. Unregistered. A mage.

The word rose in them and Noor shoved it down, but it floated back up, buoyant, patient. Down again. Up again. Like a body in a flooded archive.

Somewhere out in the panicked city, the bells kept tolling. Three fast, one slow. The walls, the walls. But the walls were only stone, and the wards were only memory now, and the stars were gone, and Noor's protection was gone, and the fear-salt that they had spent twenty years learning to cook with rose up all at once and seasoned everything.

They did the only ritual left. They got out the broom and swept the ash off the workbench, carefully, into the fire-box, and sat with their palms open on their knees and named the facts, because naming facts was the oldest charm they knew and the only one that had never once burned out.

Fact: the stars went out at midnight.

Fact: the wards fell like cut strings.

Fact: my charms burned themselves out defending something, or fleeing something, and I don't know which, and either answer frightens me.

Fact: I am visible now.

Fact: Wren saw this coming, or something like it, and died for knowing, and left a letter I have not opened in twenty years because opening it means admitting she's dead, and I have managed not to admit that for two decades through sheer disciplined cowardice.

Noor's eyes went, without permission, to the floorboard under the third peg from the left. The letter was there, wrapped in oilcloth, sealed with plain wax, in Wren's slanted handwriting: *For Noor, when the sky changes.* She'd written it the week before the trial. Noor had read the superscription once, the day they pried up the board, and put it back, and told themselves *not yet*, and *not yet* had become a country they lived in, comfortable as any walled city.

The sky had changed.

Noor sat in the dark for a long time, listening to the bells, feeling the hollow where the wards had been, feeling the nakedness where the charms had been. Outside, footsteps passed in the street—watchmen, then a family dragging a cart of belongings toward the Spire as if the mountain could protect them, then someone running with no cart at all, just terror and legs.

And then, slower than the rest, one set of footsteps stopped.

Stopped directly below the window, in the blue dark, where a man stood looking up at the little shop with the bare pegs and the smell of burnt herbs drifting out of it. Noor couldn't see his face. They could see, faintly, the shape of him turning his head—slowly, deliberately, the way a man reads a page—and Noor felt it happen, felt themselves being *read*, felt the trained attention move over them like a hand over a scar.

The man stood there a moment longer. Then he said, quietly, to the window or to the night or to himself:

"Well. There you are."

And he walked on into the dark, leaving Noor alone with the ashes, the bells, and the floorboard under the third peg from the left.
