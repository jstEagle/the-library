## 4. What the Wards Were For

Sable Ives woke on the floor of his workshop with a wrench in one hand and a half-eaten turnip in the other, which was, by the standards of his last three years, a promising start. At least the turnip meant he'd eaten. At least the wrench meant he'd been working.

He lay still for a moment and took inventory of himself the way he used to take inventory of a ward: where are the cracks, what's leaking, what's holding. Head: cracked, leaking, but holding. Hands: steady enough. That was new. Usually there was a tremor until the second drink of the day, a fine shiver like a badly tuned resonance coil. This morning his hands were quiet as stones.

Then he remembered why.

The wards had sung all night. Not loudly — they never sang loudly anymore; that was rather the point of wards — but he'd felt them through the flagstones, through the soles of his boots, through the turnip itself if it came to that, a long low note like a bell being drawn backward into itself instead of struck forward out of itself. He had sat up in the dark listening to it with the particular horror of a man who knows exactly what he is hearing and wishes, passionately, that he did not.

Sable had built wards for twenty-two years. Then he had testified at a trial, and after the trial nobody wanted wards from a man who would do that, and so now he built nothing at all, unless you counted the small crooked structures of a drinking habit, which were load-bearing in their way but ugly as sin.

He got up. The room tilted politely and righted itself. On the workbench, under a drift of filings and dust, sat the thing he'd been avoiding looking at for three days: the gauge.

It was his own design, from back when designing things was allowed to him. A ward-gauge didn't measure power the way a barometer measures weather. It measured *intention* — the shape of the draw, whether a ward was feeding steadily or sputtering or being fed. Most gauges in Vespergate were decorative brass toys sold to merchants who liked the idea of safety more than the fact of it. His actually worked. That was the whole tragedy of Sable Ives in one sentence: everything about him worked, and none of it mattered.

The needle had not moved in three days. It hadn't dropped, either. It had *risen*, gently and without any of the flutter that accompanied decay, and then stopped dead, pointing at a reading Sable had seen only once before in his life, and never hoped to see again.

Withdrawal.

Not failure. Not rot. Withdrawal — the pattern a ward showed when whatever fed it was being pulled away on purpose, from the far end, by something patient.

*Something below,* said the part of his brain that had stayed sharp while everything else pickled itself. *Something underneath, reeling in the line.*

Sable put down the wrench. He looked at the turnip, decided against it, and reached instead for the bottle on the shelf above the cold hearth — then stopped, hand hovering, because the needle gave the smallest twitch when his shadow crossed the bench, and he thought: *no. Not this morning. This morning I want to remember what I find.*

That resolve lasted until noon, which was honestly better than most of his resolves.

* * *

By noon he was in the Lamplighter's Rest, which was not a tavern so much as a place where lamplighters went between rounds and everyone else was tolerated as furniture. He knew the owner, Bessam, from the old days — Bessam's father had held the contract for the palace approach lanterns, and Sable had warded every one of them against damp and spite, back when spite was the worst thing a light could meet.

"Water," Sable said.

Bessam stared at him across the bar with the expression of a man watching a dog walk on its hind legs. "Say that again slowly."

"Water. In a cup. Possibly two cups, sequentially." Sable sat down heavily on a stool that groaned in a familiar way. "I'm thinking today, Bessam. Thinking goes poorly with gin."

"Three years I've poured for you," Bessam said, pouring water anyway, "and this is the first time you've ordered water. Is the world ending?"

"Not yet," Sable said. "But somebody's started packing."

He drank the water. It tasted like nothing, which was its principal virtue. Around him the afternoon crowd murmured — and the murmur had a texture he recognized. Fear, but the polite kind, the kind Vespergate did best: talk of the stars going out delivered in the tone people used for bad harvests and price rises. *My mother saw it. Whole sky, dark as a cellar. Old Manet swears it's an omen. Old Manet also swears his pigeons vote.* Laughter here, thin there. Underneath, everyone's voice pitched slightly too high.

And under *that* — Sable closed his eyes and listened past it, the way he'd once listened past the noise of a construction site to the true voice of a lattice — the wards were still singing their wrong note. Drawn taut. Reeling in.

He'd felt it first three days ago, on the night the stars vanished. Everyone else had looked up. Sable had put his palm flat on the cobbles, because twenty-two years of habit dies hard even when drowned in gin, and what he'd felt coming up through the stone had frightened him sober for almost four hours.

A ward is a promise with a power source. Every promise in Vespergate drew from the same well — that was the genius of the original charter, the great unified lattice, the Spire's crown-work. And a well doesn't decide to stop giving. Wells fail by dwindling, by silting, by slow sad decay. What Sable had felt through the cobbles was not dwindling. It was *retrieval*. Deliberate, rhythmic, like fingers gathering thread off a table one careful pull at a time. Something at the bottom of the world was taking back what it had lent, and doing it gently, so no one would notice until it was finished.

Except someone had noticed. Someone always noticed. The problem was that the only someones who could understand what they'd noticed were ward-smiths, and Vespergate had exactly one of those left, and he was sitting in the Lamplighter's Rest drinking water and hating himself in a fresh and interesting direction.

Because here was the arithmetic that had kept him awake since the third night: the lattice drew from below. He had known that. Every smith of his generation had known it the way you know your own heartbeat — not something you think about, just something that is true. The mountain court supplied the power; the city supplied the tribute; the arrangement was older than anyone living and nobody asked questions about arrangements older than anyone living, because asking questions about such things was how families ended up standing in courtrooms watching their children be sentenced.

Sable set down the cup. His hands had started trembling after all. Good. Trembling meant feeling, and feeling meant he was about to do something stupid, and the stupid things were usually the correct ones.

"Bessam," he said. "Question. And answer it straight, because my reputation can't take much more strangeness."

"Your reputation," Bessam said, "is a barrel with the bottom knocked out. But ask."

"The night market. The one that moves around — behind the chandlers' row, the tannery, wherever the watch isn't. You hear talk. Who sells charms these days? Real ones. Small ones. Working ones."

Bessam's face did something complicated. "Charms are death, Ives. You know that better than—"

"I know what the law says. I'm asking what the market says."

A long pause. Bessam wiped the bar with a cloth that had outlived several empires' worth of spills. "There's one," he said finally, quietly. "Nobody knows the name. They call them the Taster, because — well. Because rumor says they taste for the palace by day and sell knots and blessings by night, and that the two trades keep each other honest, if you follow me."

Sable did not follow him, and then, horribly, he did. A taster. Poison-immune, poison-trained, someone whose entire craft was the precise detection of substances too small to see. Of course. Of course that was where you'd hide a mage in Vespergate — inside the one profession where exquisite sensitivity was a job requirement and a legal license. He felt something move in his chest that wasn't quite admiration and wasn't quite dread and was definitely going to cause trouble.

"Why do you want to know?" Bessam asked. "You're not going to report them. I've watched you be useless for three years, Ives. Useless, yes. Cruel, never."

"I need to see their work," Sable said. "And then I need to ask them a question about the wards."

"You could ask the Guild—"

"The Guild smiths are dead, retired, or me." He stood. The stool groaned its familiar groan. "And I'm the one who broke us."

* * *

Finding the night market took him until well past dusk, which annoyed him, because three years ago it would have taken ten minutes. Three years ago he'd have known which shuttered doorway breathed smoke that smelled faintly of rosemary and iron. Now he had to do it the peasant way: following whispers. A child selling chestnuts who looked at his coin pouch and pointed with her chin toward the tannery lane. A drayman who said nothing at all but walked a route so conspicuously aimless that Sable understood he was being led. The city remembered him a little, apparently. Or pitied him a lot. Either worked.

The market had set up that night in the shell of a burned granary, and it was beautiful the way weeds growing through pavement are beautiful — stubborn, illegal, alive. Two dozen stalls under paper lanterns. Sellers of secondhand coats, of songs, of rumors. And at the far end, in the corner with the best sightlines and the worst lighting, a small table and a person with ink-stained fingers and the stillness of someone who had spent a lifetime practicing not being noticed.

Sable knew the stillness. He'd taught it to himself once, standing in a witness box.

He drifted closer without drifting closer, the way you do, buying nothing, browsing a stall of lucky buttons he was fairly sure were just buttons. And he watched the charm-seller work.

They sold a luck-knot to a lamplighter — Sable recognized the man, hollow-cheeked, coughing, the gray look of someone the city had nearly finished with — and refused to take his coin, pressing it back into his hand and closing his fingers over it with a gentleness that made Sable's throat hurt. They sold nothing at all to a starving child but pressed bread into the girl's hands instead, quick and furtive, like a criminal act, which in Vespergate kindness very nearly was.

Magic as mercy. Never spectacle. Sable stood in the smoky dark of the burned granary and felt the last three years of his life rearrange themselves around a single thought, which was: *that's what wards are supposed to be. That's what I forgot.*

And then the charm-seller glanced up — just once, a flicker, the glance of a prey animal counting exits — and their eyes met Sable's across the market, and he watched them read him in about a second and a half: the smith's calluses gone soft, the gin bloat, the way he stood like a man leaning on an invisible lectern. Their face closed like a shop shutter.

They began packing up their table immediately.

"No," Sable said, crossing the space too fast, graceless. "Wait. Please. I'm not — I'm not here for the wardens. I'm the opposite of the wardens. I'm worse than useless, I'm *disgraced*, nobody listens to anything I say, which tonight is the safest thing about me—"

"Then you'll have no trouble being silent somewhere else." The voice was low, level, and tired in the bones. Up close he could see the ledger of their trade written on them: the steadiness of the hands, the faint chemical smell beneath the rosemary, the eyes that catalogued him the way his own eyes catalogued a lattice — for cracks, for leaks, for what was holding.

"My name is Sable Ives," he said. "I was a ward-smith. I testified at the trial of—" and here his voice did something humiliating, cracked clean down the middle, "—of Wren Nakamura."

The charm-seller went absolutely still. More still than still. The kind of stillness that has a scream folded up inside it.

"And I've spent three days listening to the wards sing a note I've heard once in my life," Sable went on, because if he stopped now he would never start again, "and it's withdrawal, do you understand, it's not decay, something under the mountain is pulling the power back through the charter like a fisherman hauling line, and when it's done pulling, the border wards don't fall — they're *recalled* — and everything outside Vespergate comes in, or everything inside goes out, or both, and nobody in this city has noticed because nobody alive knows how to listen except me, and I am a drunk, and I am a coward, and I am sorry, I have been sorry for twenty years, and I need to know if you're Wren Nakamura's sibling, because if you are, then I owe you a debt I don't know how to pay, and if you're not, then I've just confessed to conspiracy in front of a stranger and we should both run."

Silence. The paper lanterns breathed. Somewhere a seller laughed at his own joke, oblivious.

The charm-seller looked at him for a long, long moment. When they spoke, their voice was flat as a ledger line.

"You have the wrong person."

But their hand — their poison-steady, twenty-year-immune, unshakeable hand — had begun, very slightly, to shake.

Sable looked at that hand and knew, with the sick certainty of a man watching a needle settle, that he had found the right person after all — and that whatever door he had just knocked on, it opened onto the trial, the letter, the sister, the debt, and the dark below the mountain, all at once.

"I'll come back tomorrow," he said quietly. "Same hour. Bring the letter, if there's a letter."

The charm-seller's eyes widened — one heartbeat of pure, naked fear — and then the shutter came down again, and they turned their back on him and packed their table as if he were already gone.

Sable walked home through streets where the wards sang their tightening note, and for the first time in three years, he did not stop at the Lamplighter's Rest on the way.
