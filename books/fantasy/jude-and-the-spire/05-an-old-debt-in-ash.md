## 5. An Old Debt in Ash

The knock came at the wrong hour.

Noor had a taxonomy of knocks. The warden's knock was triple-tapped and official, delivered by someone who wanted you to know he could break the door instead. The neighbor's knock was apologetic, a single knuckle-rap from Mrs. Pellik asking to borrow salt she never returned. Customers knocked in little rhythms — two-and-one for luck-knots, four quick raps for the fever-charms that were absolutely just "dried herbs sewn in cloth, officer."

This knock was none of those. This knock was heavy, slow, and patient, like someone who had spent all day working up to it and intended to see it through.

Noor set down the luck-knot they'd been retying — third time that evening; their fingers kept slipping, which was itself an omen — and crossed the shop floor without lighting a lamp. Through the shutter slats: a big silhouette, shoulders like a doorframe gone soft at the edges, and the unmistakable amber glint of a bottle held loosely in one hand.

They knew that silhouette. Twenty years hadn't changed it much. Grief was loyal that way.

"Noor Nakamura." The voice was gravel steeped in wine. "Open the door. Or don't, and I'll stand here talking to the wood until the whole street comes to watch."

"The street already thinks I'm boring," Noor said through the slats. "I've worked very hard on that reputation."

"Then you'll want to open the door before I ruin it."

There were three sensible options: stay silent until he left, slip out the back into the alley, or lie. Noor had built a life out of lying — small lies, structural lies, load-bearing lies — but something in Sable Ives's voice suggested he'd come with truth as a crowbar, and crowbars don't care what you say to doors.

They opened it.

Sable filled the frame the way he always had, taller than memory made him, grey now at the temples where there had once been soot-black curls. He smelled of lamp-oil and cheap wine and, underneath both, of forge-smoke — a ghost smell, since no ward-smith had legally touched a forge in twenty years. His eyes went past Noor's shoulder into the dim shop, cataloguing it with a craftsman's eye: the shelves of harmless charms, the bundles of knotwork, the little brass bell that did nothing at all except ring when you touched it, which was its whole charm and its whole point.

"Charm-seller," Sable said. The word sat strangely in his mouth, like he was tasting whether it was poisoned.

"Retired charm-seller," Noor said. "I mostly sell soap now. People love soap. Soap has never once been declared illegal."

"Your knots are better than your soap." He stepped inside without being asked, because men like Sable always did, and turned a luck-knot over in his huge hands. His fingers, Noor noted despite themselves, still moved like a smith's — careful, reverent, reading the weave the way Noor read a dish. "Double-wound anchor, counter-turn on the third pass. Nobody sells soap with a counter-turn."

"You're drunk."

"I'm *thoroughly* drunk," Sable agreed cheerfully. "It's the only way I do anything these days. But I wasn't drunk last night, and last night I watched the wards die." He set the knot down with exaggerated gentleness, as if it might bruise. "Not fade. Not fray. *Withdraw.* Something pulled the power back through them like a hand pulling thread off a loom. And I thought — who do I know who ties a counter-turn? Who do I know who'd still be tying them, quiet as a mouse, in a soap shop?"

"I have no idea what you're—"

"Don't." It came out flat, and sober, and it stopped Noor's tongue mid-lie with an almost physical snap. Sable looked tired suddenly, all the performance draining out of him. "Please don't. I've done enough lying for one lifetime. I'm not asking you to confess anything. I'm telling you what I saw, and asking one question, and then I'll go." He drew a breath. "Is anyone coming for us, Noor? Is this the wards failing, or is this something *worse* than the wards failing?"

And there it was — not an accusation, not a debt called in. A question asked by a man standing in the dark of a world whose lights had gone out, wanting to know if he should be afraid.

It would have been so easy to give him nothing. Noor had given magistrates nothing. They'd given the Inquisitor of Wards nothing, beautifully, for three hours, once. Nothingness was their native language.

But Sable Ives had testified against their sister, and Noor had decided long ago that the one person they would never lie to about important things was themselves. Lying to him felt, tonight, like lying to the trial record. Like agreeing with it.

"The wards aren't failing," Noor heard themself say. "Failing is slow. Failing gives you time. What happened last night was fast, and it was deliberate, and it came from below." They kept their voice level, a taster's voice, giving nothing away in the flavor. "That's all I know. That's all I'll say."

Sable stared at them for a long moment. Then he laughed — one short, broken bark, no joy in it whatsoever.

"All I'll say," he repeated. "You sound just like her."

The words landed in Noor's chest like a dropped knife.

"Wren used to say that. *That's all I'll say.* Right up on the scaffold steps, when they asked her to name her teacher." His voice had gone rough. "She looked at me — I was in the second row, I'd been called as a witness — and she said it, and then she—" He stopped. Pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "Gods. I didn't come here to do this."

"Then leave."

"Noor—"

"*Leave.*"

He left. Slowly, the way he'd arrived, pausing at the threshold to say, quietly, "If whatever pulled the thread comes looking for weavers, you should know I remember how to build things too. If you ever need a wall between you and the dark, I still owe—" He caught himself. Shook his head. "Goodnight, Nakamura."

The door closed. Noor stood in the dark shop for a long time, listening to his uneven footsteps fade down the lane, feeling twenty years settle over them like ash after a fire.

*Just like her.*

They had spent two decades making sure no one said that.

* * *

The letter lived under the third floorboard from the hearth, the one with the knot burned into its grain — a counter-turn, naturally, because even hidden things deserved good craftsmanship. Noor had not opened the board in six years. There was never a reason. There was never going to be a reason. That had been the entire architecture of the plan: survive, stay small, stay alive, and let the past stay buried in its nice oak coffin.

But Sable Ives had come tonight smelling of smoke and guilt, saying *just like her*, and the floorboard might as well have been knocking.

Noor knelt at the hearth with a pry-knife and took up the board, and lifted out the tin box, and opened it, and there it all was — the inventory of a dead family. Mother's thimble. Father's ledger-book, water-stained. A child's drawing of a spire, crayoned by someone who clearly believed spires were purple. And beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth tied with a luck-knot in Wren's own hand, the letter.

Wren had written it the night before her arrest. Noor knew that because Wren had told them, laughing, pressing it into their fifteen-year-old hands: *If they come for me, don't open this until you're ready. You'll know when you're ready.*

Noor had never been ready. Sixteen years old, then seventeen, then twenty-five, then thirty, then a royal food-taster with poison in the blood and a name nobody bothered to learn — and never once ready.

Tonight they untied the knot.

*Little star,* the letter began, and Noor had to stop, and press the paper flat against their knee, and breathe the way you breathe when a dish is dangerous — shallow and controlled, letting the worst of it pass.

*Little star. If you're reading this, then either I'm dead or you finally got brave, and knowing you it's the second thing, which means it probably also took you twenty years.*

A wet laugh came out of Noor's chest, surprising them entirely.

*I need you to know what actually happened, because they're going to lie about it at my trial, and you're going to be in the crowd hating everyone, and you should hate the right people.*

*Father gambled. Not cards — worse. He gambled with the mountain court. There's an old bargain under Vespergate, older than the walls, older than the crown. The city's protection isn't built. It's bought. Every year the court beneath the mountain takes payment, and every year the payment gets heavier. Father owed them. I don't know how much or why; he wouldn't say. But they came for the debt, and the coin they accept isn't coin.*

*It's souls. They buy souls to feed the border wards. That's what the wards are, Noor. That's what keeps the dark outside the walls. Us.*

*They offered Father a trade. My soul for the family's freedom. And he signed.*

*So listen to me, and listen well, because this is the only thing I will ever ask of you: do NOT forgive him, and do NOT follow me. Live small. Live safe. Eat everything suspicious and trust no one's cooking. And if someday — someday far away, when you're old and gray and it doesn't hurt anymore — if you ever find a way to get me back, then fine. Come get your sister. She'd like that. She always said you were the stubborn one.*

*Until then: be boring, little star. Be so boring they forget you exist.*

*I love you more than the whole gray sky full of nothing.*

*— Wren*

Noor read it twice. Three times. The fourth time they weren't really reading; the letters had blurred into candlelight and their own breath was doing something unsteady and embarrassing that no food-taster could afford.

Their father. Their quiet, kind, ledger-keeping father, who had died apologizing to a chair. He hadn't lost the family fortune gambling. He had sold his daughter to keep the rest of them fed inside the walls, and then he had sat across from Noor at breakfast every morning for eleven years with that signature drying in some vault beneath the mountain, and he had never said a word.

And Wren — Wren wasn't gone. Wren wasn't scattered ash and a plaque on the Reconciliation Wall. Wren was *somewhere*. Down in the dark, in a court that lit itself with captive souls, doing whatever was done to a soul that had been purchased and spent. Working, maybe. Burning, maybe. Dimming.

Twenty years Noor had prepared for a rescue they'd refused to believe was possible. Twenty years of sipping venoms, of building immunities, of learning every poison the palace physicians could name and several they couldn't — and they had told themselves it was survival instinct, professional caution, the reasonable paranoia of a man whose job was dying professionally. They had never once admitted, even alone, even in the dark, what the preparation was actually *for*.

Well. Now the letter had said it for them.

*Come get your sister.*

Noor folded the letter along Wren's original creases, retied the luck-knot — double-wound, counter-turn on the third pass, because even grief deserved good craftsmanship — and sat back on their heels in the cold hearthlight.

The stars were still gone. Somewhere under the city, wards hung dead in their channels like cut nerves. Somewhere above, Prince Aurel lay sleepless in a palace Noor was about to need access to. And somewhere below, a ledger waited with a line in it that read *Wren Nakamura*, and Noor intended to cross that line out with whatever instrument the job required.

They blew out the candle.

"Be boring," they murmured to the empty room, and almost laughed. "Sorry, Wren. That part of the letter's void."

Outside, far off toward the mountain, something in the deep dark seemed — very faintly, very patiently — to be breathing.
