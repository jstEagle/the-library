## 6. The Prince Who Cannot Sleep

Prince Aurel Vespergate had not slept in six days, and he had decided, somewhere around the fourth, that he was handling it magnificently.

The court disagreed. He could tell by the way they spoke to him—gently, carefully, the way one speaks to a man standing on a ledge. His chamberlain had begun leaving chamomile outside his door with the frequency of a woman feeding a dangerous animal. His ministers had taken to speaking in softer voices during council, as if volume might be what was keeping him awake.

It was not volume. It was the sky.

Or rather, the absence of it.

Six nights ago, Aurel had been walking the eastern battlements—he did his best thinking on the battlements, where no one could find him and ask him things—and he had been looking at a particular star. Not an important star. A small blue-white one, low over the mountains, the kind of star a boy learns by accident because it hangs above the window where his mother used to read to him. He had been thinking, idly, that he should learn its proper name someday, instead of just calling it *hers*, when it went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded over. *Went out*, like a candle pinched between wet fingers. And then the next one, and the next, all across the vault of heaven, a great soft blackness rolling westward like spilled ink, until there was nothing above Vespergate at all. No stars. No moon. Just a sky like the inside of a closed lid.

He had shouted for the watch. He had run down three flights of stairs before remembering he was the crown prince and princes do not run, and then he had run anyway, because some part of him understood that the rules were already breaking.

That had been six days ago. The city had gone mad, recovered, and gone mad again in new and inventive ways. The temples were full. The grain markets had doubled their prices by noon of the first day and tripled them by the second. Three different prophets had been arrested for inciting panic, and two more for being insufficiently panicked, which Aurel felt was the government working at its finest.

And through all of it, through councils and petitions and his father's long silences from behind the mountain doors, one thread kept pulling at Aurel's attention, small and persistent as a splinter:

His food taster flinched.

* * *

Noor Nakamura had been tasting for the palace for four years, which was, in the strange arithmetic of that profession, a very long life. They were quiet, precise, unremarkable in the way of furniture or good ink. They arrived before meals, tasted each dish with a ritual so consistent it could have marked time, nodded once—the same nod every day, a nod Aurel had come to think of as *the world continues*—and withdrew.

Aurel had never spoken to them beyond a handful of necessary words. This was not rudeness; it was policy. You did not befriend the person whose job was to die for you. Befriending them made the dying harder for everyone involved, and Aurel's life had enough hard things in it without adding sentiment to breakfast.

But he watched people. That was the one skill his father had never managed to breed out of him, though he'd tried for years, mistaking observation for weakness. And so Aurel noticed, on the second sleepless morning, that Noor's hand had paused half a heartbeat over the honeyed pears before dipping the tasting spoon. On the third morning, they checked the salt cellar twice. The salt cellar. In four years, Noor Nakamura had never checked anything twice; checking twice implied doubt, and doubt implied fear, and Noor's whole existence seemed engineered to produce neither.

On the fifth morning, they flinched at nothing at all—a cook's ladle clattering against a pot, a sound that happened forty times a day in any kitchen—and the flinch traveled up their arm like something alive before they killed it.

Everyone else in the palace had an excuse for behaving strangely. The stars had gone out; the whole city was entitled to its nerves. But Noor had been strange *before*, or rather, Noor had been perfectly calm while everyone else fell apart, and now the calm itself was coming apart, seam by seam, and that interested Aurel more than the stars did.

Because here was the thing about the stars going out: everyone in Vespergate had lost exactly the same thing. The shepherd and the prince had both looked up into the same nothing. It was almost democratic, that loss. And yet his taster was grieving something else entirely. Aurel could see it—the specific grief of someone who has lost something the rest of the crowd doesn't even know exists.

He knew that grief intimately. He carried his own version of it under his ribs, where his mother used to be.

So on the sixth morning, Aurel did something he had been told since childhood never, ever to do.

He interfered with breakfast.

* * *

The plum had come up from the southern orchards with the autumn carts, dark-skinned and heavy, and it sat in the fruit bowl looking innocent in the way that only genuinely dangerous things can manage. Aurel had known about the plum since the previous evening, because his father's steward had told him, in the flat administrative voice reserved for matters of succession: a poisoner's attempt against the king, intercepted, the toxin isolated, a single contaminated plum left over as evidence. Keep it away from His Majesty. Dispose of it properly.

Dispose of it properly had apparently meant *put it in a bowl*, because bureaucracy is eternal and finds a way.

Aurel took the plum out of the bowl himself, early, before the kitchen staff came up. He held it in his palm for a long moment. It weighed about as much as a secret.

Here is what nobody tells you about being the prince of a walled city whose stars have just vanished: you are surrounded by people whose job is to lie to you. Ministers lie about the treasury. Priests lie about the omens. His father lied about everything, with such completeness that Aurel sometimes wondered if the man remembered what truth tasted like. Even kindness, in the palace, came pre-wrapped in strategy.

Aurel had spent twenty-two years learning to detect those lies, and he had gotten very good at it, and it had made him lonelier than any tower ever could. Because the terrible thing about hearing lies well is that you stop being able to hear anything else. Every conversation becomes a puzzle. Every friend becomes a suspect. You begin to feel like the only honest person in a city of liars, and then, late at night on the battlements, you admit the worse truth: that you lie too, constantly, beautifully, just to survive the lying.

But his taster—that was different. Noor Nakamura's entire profession was honesty. The one person in Vespergate contractually forbidden to deceive him. Their tongue was the only instrument in the palace that reported the world as it actually was.

Which meant that if Aurel wanted one true answer in this city of lies, he would have to trick the honestest person he knew.

He felt appropriately filthy about it.

* * *

Breakfast was served at the eighth bell, as always. Aurel sat at the small table by the window—not the grand dining hall, never that, his father preferred to take meals alone behind closed doors, another inheritance Aurel resented—and watched the dishes arrive. Eggs in cream. Bread with butter and bitter orange preserve. Smoked fish. The plum, returned to the bowl, sitting among its innocent cousins like a spy at a wedding.

Noor came in at the seventh-and-a-halfth bell, as always. Grey clothes. Quiet feet. The tasting spoon worn smooth at the handle from years of the same grip. They bowed the exact correct amount—not too deep, which implies ambition, not too shallow, which implies contempt—and began.

Eggs. Nod. Bread. Nod. Fish. Nod.

Then the fruit.

Aurel watched with the focus of a man reading a confession. Noor's hand went into the bowl, and Aurel saw it happen: the smallest possible hesitation, a hesitation measured in nerve-endings rather than seconds. Fingers brushing skin. Skin that was wrong. Not visibly wrong—no bloom, no rot—but wrong the way a room is wrong when someone has died in it recently.

Noor lifted the plum. Turned it once, unhurried, a connoisseur's idle turn. Set it back down.

And then—they picked a pear instead. Tasted it. Nodded.

They skipped the plum.

Now, a reasonable person might say: perhaps they simply weren't hungry for plum. Perhaps the pear looked better. People skip fruit all the time; it is hardly treason.

But Aurel had spent six days doing nothing but watching, and he knew what he had seen. Noor Nakamura had identified a poisoned plum by touch alone, in under a second, without tasting it, and had elected to say nothing about it. Which raised a question so interesting it made Aurel forget, briefly, to be exhausted:

How?

There were exactly two explanations. Either Noor Nakamura had senses no ordinary taster possessed—which was impossible, or nearly—or Noor Nakamura had known about the plum before entering the room. Had known what poison, on which fruit, in whose household. And either way, the quiet, careful, unremarkable person standing ten feet away pouring nothing and saying nothing was the most remarkable secret in Vespergate.

Aurel reached for the plum.

"No," Noor said.

It was the first word Aurel had ever heard them speak that wasn't a report. It came out fast, stripped of all their careful blandness, raw as a nerve. Then Noor caught themselves, smoothed their face back into furniture, and said, in the tone of a man remarking on weather: "Your Highness may wish to choose a different fruit this morning."

"May I." Aurel turned the plum slowly in his fingers. "Why?"

"The skin is bruised."

"It isn't."

"The flesh beneath the skin, then."

"You haven't cut it open."

"I'm very good at plums, Highness."

Aurel smiled, and it was not a kind smile, because he was tired and lonely and holding a poisoner's work in his bare hand. "So am I," he said. "I've been practicing since yesterday evening."

He watched Noor understand. Watched it move through them like weather—the stillness first, then the calculation, running through exits and denials and finding, as Aurel had intended, that the prince was between them and the door and the prince had all day. Six days without sleep had at least bought him that: nowhere to be, nothing to lose except answers.

"Sit down, Master Nakamura," Aurel said, more gently than he'd planned. "Please. I'm not going to eat it. I promise you that much honesty, since I'm about to ask for yours."

Noor did not sit. But they didn't leave, either, and in the economy of Noor Nakamura, Aurel suspected that was practically a declaration.

"That plum came from my father's kitchens," Aurel said. "It was meant for him, or possibly for me—we're not sure which, and frankly the assassin may have improved both our moods by failing. The steward set it aside as evidence. Evidence which, in the infinite wisdom of the palace, was stored in a fruit bowl. I removed it last night and put it back this morning, and I have spent the night watching you taste your way past it like it was a stone in the road." He set the plum down on the table between them, a dark little planet. "You touched it for less than a second. You knew. So I'm going to ask you a question, Master Nakamura, and I would very much like a true answer, because I have had six days of false ones and I'm beginning to hear them in my own voice."

Noor stood very still. Outside the window, the starless sky hung over the city like a held breath.

"What did the plum tell you?" Aurel asked. "And don't say *bruise*. I've eaten plums from that orchard my whole life. I know how they bruise. That plum told you something no tongue should be able to hear."

Silence. Long enough that Aurel heard the kitchen below, the clatter of the morning, the ordinary machinery of a city pretending to be fine.

"Noor Nakamura," Aurel said quietly, "who are you?"

And there—there it was. The flinch. Not at the ladle, not at the noise, but at the question itself, the flinch of a person whose whole architecture of survival has just been named aloud by the last person they wanted naming it. Aurel recognized it instantly, with a jolt of something close to kinship. He wore the same expression every time someone called him *the king's son* instead of his name. It was the look of a person being addressed as a role when they had fought their whole life to remain a person.

"I'm your food taster, Highness," Noor said.

"Yes," Aurel agreed. "And I am my father's heir, and neither of those sentences contains a single true word."

He pushed a chair out from the table with his foot. An invitation. Noor looked at it the way a soldier looks at a bridge that may or may not be mined.

"I'll tell you what I see," Aurel offered. "Fair trade. You've watched me for four years—you've tasted my food, you know me better than anyone in this palace admits to knowing anyone. So let me show you I can pay for answers." He ticked them off on his fingers, tiredly, honestly. "You are left-handed and hide it. You count exits when you enter a room—I've seen your eyes do it, always two flicks, door and window. You wear grey because grey is invisible in this palace and you have worked very hard to be invisible. And since the stars went out, you've been afraid of something the rest of us haven't thought of yet, which frightens me more than the empty sky, because I like to think I've considered most possibilities and you clearly have a category I lack."

Noor's face did something extraordinary, which was nothing at all—but it was the *effort* of nothing, the visible labor of it, and that effort told Aurel everything the face refused to.

"Highness," Noor said finally, and their voice was level again, repaired, "if I answered your questions truly, you would have to arrest me."

"Almost certainly," Aurel said. "My father would insist on it. Which is why—" and here he surprised himself, the words coming out before he had decided to say them, six sleepless days stripping away his caution like old paint— "which is why I'm not asking as the prince."

Noor blinked. Once.

"I'm asking as the other person in this palace who hasn't slept since the sky broke," Aurel said. "As the person who watched a star go out that I wasn't finished needing. As the only son of a king who locks his doors, in a city that just learned its oldest protection might not exist anymore. I am asking as the loneliest man in Vespergate, Master Nakamura, and I suspect—" he gestured, gently, at the whole grey, careful, flinching shape of them— "that you may be the second loneliest, and that we have simply been standing in the same rooms for four years, too well-trained to notice."

The silence that followed was different from the others. Less like a wall. More like a door, unlocked but not yet opened.

Noor looked at the plum on the table. At the window. At the chair. And when they spoke, their voice was quiet enough that Aurel leaned forward to catch it, and what they said was not an answer, and not a denial, and not the truth, quite—

but it was not a lie, either, and after six days of lies, Aurel had learned to treasure even the borderlands.

"I need to think," Noor said. "About what to tell you. About how much."

"Take until tomorrow's breakfast," Aurel said. "Same table. I'll bring a different fruit."

He expected them to bow and go. Instead Noor hesitated at the door, hand on the frame, back still turned, and said—so softly it might have been meant for no one:

"The plum was laced with kingsbane. Half a drop, in the stem end. Whoever sent it wanted him dead slowly, over days, so it would look like grief for the stars." A pause. "You should have your guards check whoever arranged the southern orchard delivery. Someone signed for it who shouldn't have."

Then they were gone, quiet as grey cloth, leaving Aurel alone with a poisoned plum, a full breakfast going cold, and the sudden vertiginous certainty that the most dangerous secret in Vespergate had just been handed to him voluntarily—wrapped, like everything in this palace, in just enough truth to make the rest irresistible.

Aurel looked down at the plum in his palm, then up at the empty, breathing dark where the stars should have been.

"Well," he said to the sky, to the plum, to the empty room. "Now I'll never sleep."

He was, of course, entirely wrong about why.
