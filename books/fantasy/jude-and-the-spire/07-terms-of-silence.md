## 7. Terms of Silence

Noor had been summoned to the prince's private study at an hour when honest people were asleep and dishonest ones were counting their money, and they had decided—somewhere between the fourth staircase and the fifth—that this was either the end of their life or the beginning of a great deal of trouble.

The two were not mutually exclusive.

The study sat in the east wing, above the waterline of the old cisterns, which meant it had been built by someone who expected the palace to flood and wanted to keep his books dry anyway. Noor approved of that instinct. It was a sensible sort of paranoia, and sensible paranoia was, in their experience, the only reliable kind.

Prince Aurel stood at the window with his back turned, which was either a gesture of trust or a way of letting Noor look at the back of his head without having to watch them do it. He was not wearing his circlet. He looked, without it, less like a prince and more like a young man who hadn't slept in three days—which, to be fair, he wasn't.

"You flinched," he said.

"Your Highness?"

"At breakfast. Yesterday. The plum course." He still hadn't turned around. "You flinched before you tasted it."

Ah. The plum. Noor had spent the better part of a day trying not to think about the plum, which was exactly as effective as trying not to think about a lit fuse.

"I stepped on a cat," Noor said.

"On the terrace."

"The cat was on the terrace."

"There are no cats on the terrace. I've had the dogs trained to see to that." Aurel turned then, and there was nothing princely in his face at all—just tiredness, and underneath it something sharper. "I put hemlock extract in that plum myself. A dose sized for me. You tasted it, identified it, and told me the dish was clean."

The candles guttered in their stands. Somewhere below, in the dark of the city, the wards that had held Vespergate's borders for three hundred years were lying slack as cut strings, and the sky where the stars used to be was a smooth black nothing, like a table wiped clean before a meal no one wanted to eat.

Noor considered their options.

They were, on balance, excellent at this. Twenty years of tasting other people's food taught a person to weigh a lie the way a jeweler weighs a stone—by its color, its cut, its flaws. A lie needed to be small enough to swallow whole. It needed to be boring. *I misjudged* was too weak; *the poison was masked* invited questions about masking agents. The best lie was one the listener wanted to believe, and what did a sleepless prince want to believe?

That his taster was merely human. That someone, somewhere, was still doing their job properly.

"Your Highness," Noor said slowly, "I have eaten poison every working day for twenty years. My tongue is a ledger. Sometimes a ledger makes an error, and the error is that I assumed a dish from your own kitchens could not be poisoned by your own hand, because what kind of prince poisons himself?"

"One who suspects his taster," said Aurel.

"Of what?"

He crossed the room and sat behind his desk—not behind it, exactly; more like he braced himself against it, the way a sailor braces against weather—and looked at Noor with an expression they couldn't immediately catalogue. That bothered them. They prided themselves on cataloguing expressions the way other people collected buttons.

"Of knowing things," he said. "The stars went out four nights ago. The border wards fell the same hour—I have the reports; the garrisons saw the light die along the wall like snuffed candles. Every charm-seller in the night market has gone quiet or gone missing. And my food-taster, who once detected two grains of arsenic in a soup meant for forty people—" he paused, delicately, "—flinches at nothing and everything, sleeps badly, and looks at the sky like it owes him money."

"Him or them," said Noor. "Depending on the day."

Aurel blinked. For the first time since Noor had known him—which was most mornings, over eggs—the prince seemed genuinely thrown. "I beg your pardon?"

"It's a long story involving my mother's naming conventions and a great deal of argument. The point is, Your Highness, that I am a food-taster with indigestion, and the sky is not my department."

"No," Aurel agreed softly. "But I think it might be somebody's."

* * *

Here is what Noor knew about Prince Aurel, gathered the way they gathered everything: patiently, sideways, over years of standing slightly behind his left shoulder with a silver spoon.

He was twenty-six. He had been engaged twice and neither engagement had survived his habit of asking precise questions at dinner parties. He read correspondence his father should have read and answered letters his father never would. He had once dismissed a minister for falsifying grain tallies during a shortage—a scandal that had made him beloved in the lower city and deeply unpopular everywhere else, which was perhaps why he took his breakfast with only a taster for company instead of a full guard of flattering relatives.

And he could not sleep. Not since the stars went out. The kitchen staff whispered about it—whispered everything, always, which was how Noor heard most things worth hearing. The prince paced his rooms until dawn. The prince had stopped eating anything he hadn't watched prepared. The prince stood at his window like a man waiting for a ship.

Noor had assumed it was fear. Everyone in Vespergate was afraid; fear was the new civic religion, practiced nightly, in crowds, with candles.

It was not fear. Watching him now, Noor revised their assessment. It was grief. Which was stranger, and more dangerous, because you could reason with a frightened man, but a grieving one would follow any thread that promised to make sense of the loss.

"Let me tell you a story," Aurel said. "Once upon a time, a prince found a plum in his own orchard and put poison in it, because his taster—who has served him faithfully through two assassination attempts and one regrettable incident with oysters—has been acting like a woman carrying a secret too heavy to set down. And the taster tasted the plum, and named the poison under her breath so quietly even I almost missed it, and then lied to my face with the calm of a lake in summer." He leaned forward. "I'm going to ask you once, plainly, because I'm tired and it's late and the sky is empty. What do you know about the wards?"

And here is the thing about Noor Nakamura, the thing they themselves would have told you if you'd caught them in an honest hour, which they generally arranged never to be: they were very good at lying, and worse every year at wanting to.

Because the truth had a shape now. It had been growing a shape all week, ever since Sable Ives had stood in their shop smelling of gin and guilt and said the words *withdrawal* and *pulling power* and *below*, and ever since Noor had opened Wren's letter at three in the morning with hands that shook and read, in their sister's careful script, the sentence that had rearranged the inside of their skull:

*They are buying us, little star. The court beneath the mountain is buying souls, and mine was among the first.*

The truth had a shape, and it was heavy, and Noor had carried heavier—but they had never carried it in front of a prince.

"I know the wards didn't fail," Noor said.

It came out before they could stop it, which was itself alarming, because Noor did not say things before they could stop them. Ever. Their entire survival rested on the principle that words left the mouth only after passing three inspections and paying a toll.

Aurel went very still. "Go on."

"That's—that's all I know. That they didn't decay. Decay looks like fraying. This looked like—" Noor searched for the word Sable had used, and hated how right it was. "Withdrawal. Like something pulled the power back. Like the wards weren't ours to begin with."

The silence stretched long enough that Noor could hear the candles burning, which is a sound only insomniacs and liars learn to recognize.

"My father," Aurel said at last, "signed something in the archive vaults the year I was born. I know because I asked him about it once, when I was nine, and he hit me." His voice stayed perfectly level, which told Noor more than shouting would have. "I have been trying to get into those vaults for six years. The archivists stonewall me. The keys require two signatures—mine and the Lord Treasurer's, and the Lord Treasurer has developed a sudden passion for hunting trips whenever I raise the subject."

"Your Highness, I'm a food-taster."

"Your Highness, I'm a liar," Noor corrected, and then, hearing it, winced. "I mean—"

"No," Aurel said, and something almost like a smile crossed his face, tired and crooked. "I don't think you are. I think that's the trouble. A liar would have told me the plum was clean and slept well afterward. You've looked guilty for four days. Guilt is the one thing you can't mask—it's why we use tasters and not poison-sages. A sage can hide the symptoms. Nobody can hide the conscience."

*You would be surprised,* Noor thought, *what twenty years can teach a person to hide.* But they said nothing, because saying nothing was the oldest trick in their book, and the book was otherwise empty tonight.

"Here is my offer," said the prince. He straightened up off the desk, and for a moment he looked every inch his father's son, which was not entirely comforting given what Noor suspected about the father. "My protection—real protection, written protection, the kind that outlasts a bad mood—for you and anyone under your roof. In exchange: honesty. Whatever you know about the wards, whoever you've been talking to, whatever it is you flinch at—you bring it to me. And I give you access to the royal archive."

Noor's heart performed a maneuver that would have concerned a physician.

"The archive," they repeated.

"All of it. Including the vaults under the waterline. I can't get the Treasurer's key, but I can get you past a distracted clerk and a locked door, if you're the sort of person who can pick a lock." A pause. "Are you the sort of person who can pick a lock?"

"Your Highness, I am the sort of person who knows which locks are worth picking."

Something flickered across the prince's face—recalculation, Noor thought, the rapid mental arithmetic of a man discovering that his food-taster had depths, and deciding whether to be alarmed or delighted. To his credit, he appeared to settle on delighted.

"Why?" Noor asked. It wasn't caution, exactly. It was genuine curiosity, which was nearly the same thing and considerably more dangerous. "Why offer a taster the archive? Why not interrogate me? You could have me taken to the wardens tonight. One word—mage—and I'd be ash on the pyre by noon. It would be tidy. It would be legal. It would play well with the council."

"Yes," Aurel agreed. "It would." He walked back to the window, and Noor watched him look out at the starless black and understood, suddenly and completely, that they were looking at the loneliest person in Vespergate, which was a title Noor had privately held for two decades and did not relish surrendering. "Everyone lies to me," he said. "Ministers lie upward. Servants lie downward. My father lied sideways until the day he died. I have spent my whole life being handed beautifully plated dishes that taste of nothing." He turned. "You're the only person in this palace whose job is to tell me the truth about what's in front of me. I find I'd rather have your lies than their truths. But I'd rather have your actual truth than both."

Well. There was no good answer to that. Noor considered several and discarded them all.

* * *

Later—they would never be sure how much later, hours perhaps, the candles burned down to stubs—Noor walked home through streets that smelled of woodsmoke and fear, and conducted the argument they had been losing since the study.

Take the deal, said the part of them that had spent twenty years surviving. Protection. Written protection. From a prince who, whatever his flaws, had fired a minister for starving the poor. Take the archive. Wren's name was in some ledger somewhere—Sable had sworn it, and Sable was a drunk, but drunks remember what ruined them. The archive was a door, and doors were for walking through.

Don't take the deal, said the other part, the older part, the part that sounded disconcertingly like Wren. He's a prince. Princes collect people the way magpies collect silver. And when he learns what you actually are—

"He won't learn," Noor said aloud, to an empty street, and a cat on a fence gave them the particular look cats reserve for humans talking to themselves.

Because here was the compromise, the first of what Noor's smarter instincts warned would be many: they would take the deal. Of course they would take the deal. But honesty was a currency and Noor had never been rich enough to spend it all at once. So they would give him truth in installments—the wards, the withdrawal, the direction of the pull—all true. All verifiable. And they would withhold the rest the way a banker withholds collateral: not out of malice, but because the moment Aurel learned that his taster was an illegal mage who kept charms in a false-bottomed tea chest and had been quietly breaking the law of the land since before he was crowned, the deal would stop being a deal and become a leash.

Unless, whispered the third voice, the one Noor tried never to listen to, unless he already knows, and the whole offer is a net with honey on it.

Noor reached their street. The lamplighter's daughter had left a candle burning in the shop window—Mira was fourteen and believed, with the fierce unexamined faith of the young, that a lit window mattered—and Noor paused in the doorway and let the small warm light argue on her behalf for a moment.

The truth was that Noor had lain to a prince tonight and felt, for the first time in twenty years, bad about it. Not afraid—afraid was furniture, afraid was load-bearing, Noor had built their whole house out of afraid. Bad. There was a difference. Fear said *you'll be caught*. Badness said *he looked at you like a human being and you handed him a plated dish that tasted of nothing*.

Noor unlocked the shop, lit no lamps, and stood in the dark among the harmless charms—the luck-knots and the fever-wards and the little braided promises that everything would hold—and admitted it to the room:

They were going to betray him. Gently. Slowly. In installments. And they were going to hate themselves a little more each time, and they were going to do it anyway, because somewhere beneath the mountain, in a ledger with a water-stained cover, their sister's soul was listed as paid in full, and Noor Nakamura intended to read every page of that ledger even if it cost them the prince's trust, the city's peace, and quite possibly their neck.

"First compromise of many," they told the dark, and the dark, which had lately swallowed the stars, declined to disagree.

They slept two hours. At dawn, a note arrived at the kitchen door in the prince's own handwriting, sealed with the royal crest and addressed, remarkably, to *N. Nakamura, Taster, and Person of Interest*—which was either a joke or a threat, and with Aurel it was usually both:

*The archive clerk takes his lunch at noon and his ale after. The door sticks; lift, don't push. Bring your lock-picking opinions and your actual truth. —A.*

Noor read it twice, burned it in the stove, and went to fetch the tea chest.

Under the false bottom, wrapped in oilcloth beside Wren's letter, lay the tools of a trade Noor had never officially practiced. They weighed them in their hand—small, cold, familiar—and thought of flooded stacks, of ledgers, of a sister's name waiting in ink.

"Lift, don't push," Noor murmured. "How does a prince know his own doors stick?"

Then they put the picks in their sleeve, because some questions you answer yourself, down in the dark, with your own two hands.
