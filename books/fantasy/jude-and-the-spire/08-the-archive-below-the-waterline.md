## 8. The Archive Below the Waterline

The royal archive lived below the waterline, which Noor had always assumed was a metaphor until the morning they were handed a lantern and a pair of waxed boots and told to mind the third stair.

"Third stair's rotten," said the archivist, a small woman named Pell whose spectacles were fogged with the damp. "Has been for eleven years. Nobody goes down there anymore. That's rather the point."

"That's rather the point," Noor agreed, and meant it in an entirely different way.

Pell had been paid — generously, in coin that Aurel had produced from a drawer in his own chambers without so much as a ledger entry, which told Noor something about how princes kept their secrets — to forget the visit entirely. She had accepted the coin, polished her spectacles on her sleeve, and said, "I'll forget everything except the boots. Those are archive boots now. You'll bring them back or I'll haunt you."

"Fair terms," Noor said.

It was, they reflected as they descended past the second landing, the most honest negotiation they'd been part of all week.

* * *

The water began at the ankles and did not apologize for it. Black, still, faintly warm — fed by some underground vein that had been seeping into the lowest stacks for decades while the court above pretended the archive was a place where things were merely *stored*, not drowned. The lantern light lay across its surface like oil. Shelves rose out of it in ranks, row after row of them, each one labeled with brass plates gone green: *Tithes of the Eastern Marches. Grain Ledgers, Old Rite. Correspondence of the Wardens' Office, Restricted.*

Restricted. Of course. The wardens' records sat deepest in the flood.

Noor stood in the doorway a moment longer than necessary, letting their eyes adjust, letting the cold climb through the waxed boots and settle into their shins. This was a habit from the tasting bench: never move first. Let the room tell you what it wanted. A kitchen announced itself in smells; an archive announced itself in silence, and this one was silent the way a held breath is silent.

Somewhere above, muffled by stone, the city went on being terrified. Three days since the stars went out. Three days of bread lines and rumor and the temple bells ringing at wrong hours because half the bell-ringers had fled. And somewhere beneath all that panic, Prince Aurel had unlocked a door for them with his own seal and said, in that flat, sleepless voice of his:

*"You'll find what my father didn't want found. I'd do it myself, but everyone watches me. Nobody watches you."*

Nobody watches you. It was supposed to be a compliment. It landed like a diagnosis.

Noor lifted the lantern and waded in.

* * *

The trick to searching flooded stacks, Noor discovered within the hour, was grief management. Every few steps the water gave up a book — swollen, furred with mold, pages fused into a single gray brick. Each one had been someone's careful work. Someone had ruled those lines and pressed those covers, and the city had let the water rise over them because noticing would have cost money.

"Don't get sentimental," Noor told themselves aloud, because the alternative was standing ankle-deep in drowned history feeling feelings, and they had a job.

The job, specifically: the original charter of Vespergate's border wards. Not the copy displayed in the Council Hall with its gilt frame and its flattering inscription — Noor had read that one a hundred times on delivery errands, had practically memorized its self-congratulation. *"Built by the hands of the founding houses, sustained by the vigilance of their heirs."* Built by hands. Sustained by vigilance.

Sable Ives, drunk in a doorway two nights ago, had laughed at that inscription the way a man laughs when the laugh is holding back something worse.

*"Built," he'd said. "That's a good word. You know what a ward costs to build, properly? You know what it costs to keep? Nothing built by hands lasts two hundred years without feeding. Ask yourself what it ate."*

So Noor was asking. They worked inward from the oldest shelves, following dates backward through the dark, cataloguing titles in a waterproof slate they'd brought against their better judgment. *Ward Assessments, Year 141. Renewal Fees, Northern Circuit.* Renewal fees. Wards weren't renewed like vows. They were renewed like rents.

The water deepened. By the fourth row it was at their knees, and the shelves here leaned together overhead like conspirators, and the air had the taste — Noor's tongue, that cursed reliable instrument, identified it before their mind caught up — the air had the taste of old iron and old ink and something underneath both, faint and sweetish, that they had last tasted on a breakfast plate six days ago.

Mountain-iron. Here, under the palace, under the water.

"Well," Noor said to the flooded dark. "That's not reassuring."

* * *

They found the charter in a lead-lined case on a shelf marked, with a bureaucratic poetry Noor almost admired, *Miscellaneous, Pre-Founding.*

Lead lining. Somebody had wanted this box forgotten thoroughly enough to spend real money on it. The lock was salt-welded shut; Noor broke it with a pry bar from the maintenance rack by the stairs, wincing at the sound, then listening, then remembering that nobody came down here except water.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth that had actually done its job, lay three documents.

The first was the charter itself — the true one. Smaller than its gilded descendant. Plain vellum, plain hand, the kind of script written fast by someone who expected to be believed without decoration. Noor unrolled it across the top of a dry ledger-case and read it twice, because the first time their eyes refused to cooperate.

*Let it be set down plainly, so no heir may claim ignorance:* the opening line began, and Noor felt the floor tilt even though the floor was water and couldn't tilt.

The wards of Vespergate were not built. They were **bought**. Purchased from a power beneath the mountain — the contract named no name, only *the Party Below, being holder of the Old Fire* — and the price was set not in gold. Gold, the charter noted with what Noor could only describe as relief, had been *offered and declined.*

The price was souls. One soul per season, delivered by the court above, freely given by contract, in perpetuity. In exchange: the borders held, the walls stood, the stars — Noor stopped. Read it again. *And the sky-shares shall remain the Party Above's, save such portions as are ceded in years of plenty.*

Portions of the sky. Ceded in years of plenty.

Three days ago, every star over Vespergate had gone out at once.

"No," Noor said, to the charter, to the water, to the long dead clerk who had written *let it be set down plainly* and been ignored for two centuries. "No, that's — the stars aren't *property*, they're —"

But they were, apparently. Or could be made to be, if the buyer was patient and the sellers were comfortable. Noor thought of the founding houses, all those stern portraits in the Council Hall, men and women with good jaws and better posture, and tried to imagine the meeting where they'd signed this. Tried to imagine someone saying *and we'll pay in people* and someone else saying *well, whose,* and no one walking out.

Someone should have walked out. Someone should always walk out.

* * *

Noor sat down on a shelf edge, boots dangling in the black water, and made themselves breathe the way they breathed after swallowing something bitter: slowly, deliberately, waiting to learn what kind of sick this was.

The second document was the payment schedule. It confirmed the arithmetic with horrible tidiness — four souls a year, one per season, "of sound condition, aged between twelve and forty, consigned by consent of kin." Consented. Everyone in these papers had consented to everything, right up until the moment the papers were done with them.

The third document was the oldest ledger.

It was small. That was the thing Noor hadn't expected — after the scale of it, the machine of it, two hundred years of seasons and souls, the actual record was a slim book with a cracked spine, the entries cramped and practical, the way a grocer tallies flour. *Springtide, Yr 3: one (1), female, 19, consigned by Nakamura, settled in full.*

Nakamura.

The water sounded very loud suddenly. Noor's own heartbeat sounded louder.

Their family name. Their father's name — consigning. Settling accounts. *In full,* as though a daughter were a debt that could be cleared, a fee rendered, a renewal paid on time and in good order.

Noor turned pages with fingers that did not shake, because twenty years on the tasting bench had taught every part of their body to hold still under any pressure whatsoever, and read the early entries one by one. Names, ages, consignors. Some surnames they knew — old families, honored names, streets named after them. Whole dynasties had bought their first seasons of safety with somebody's child, and the children had gone down the mountain, and the wards had held, and everyone above had called it vigilance.

Then, near the middle of the first gathering of pages, in a hand slightly steadier than the rest:

*Wintertide, Yr 4: one (1), female, 17, consigned by Nakamura, F., settled in full. Note: consignor disputed the valuation. Overruled.*

Wren.

Not Wren's execution. Noor had known about the execution, had stood in the crowd at fourteen with their father's hand clamped on their shoulder, had watched the wardens do it legally, publicly, with all the proper forms — illegal magic, the charge said, though Wren had only ever done what Noor did now, small mercies, luck-knots, charms priced at whatever the buyer could spare. Noor had spent twenty years believing that was the whole story. The trial. The pyre. Their father's silence afterward, which Noor had read as shame and grief and had forgiven him for, mostly, before he died.

But this entry predated the trial by a full year.

Wren had been sold first. Consigned by their father, disputed, overruled, *settled in full* — and then, a year later, she'd been executed for magic. For the crime Noor committed nightly in the night market. And Noor had never once wondered why the timing was strange, why the wardens who ignored a hundred charm-sellers had come for Wren specifically, why their father had stopped eating anything Noor cooked—

He'd known what he'd done. He'd watched the wardens take her back, or punish her for running, or simply close the loose end, and he'd known, and he'd sat across the table from his remaining child every day and swallowed food he couldn't taste.

Noor put a hand flat on the cold vellum and stayed like that for a long time. The lantern hissed. The water breathed against the shelf legs with a patience that felt ancient.

When they finally spoke, their voice came out steady, which somehow made it worse.

"You sold her to keep us safe," they said, to a dead man, in a drowned room. "And it wasn't even enough, so they took the excuse instead. And I spent twenty years being useful so no one would ever look at me twice, and the whole time the bill was already paid. With her."

Horror, they discovered, has a final stage. Past the shaking and past the sickness there is a flat clear place where the horror stops churning and simply becomes information. The city's protection was purchased. The purchase price was people. Wren was not lost to injustice; she was lost to *accounting*. And the wards that fell three nights ago — the wards that Sable said were being pulled back, withdrawn, *collected* —

The mountain wasn't failing. The mountain was calling in the loan.

Everything fit. That was the terrible gift of clarity: it fit, and it would go on fitting, and no amount of wishing would make the pieces separate again.

* * *

Noor copied everything. Charter, schedule, the first fifty entries of the ledger, copying by lantern light with numb fingers and the slate balanced on their knee, because documents drown and princes change their minds but a taster's memory is trained to hold poison precisely, dose by dose, indefinitely. They recited the entries back to themselves twice, sealed them behind the mental door where they kept every toxin and antidote of their career, and then — this was the part that surprised them — copied Wren's entry a third time onto a scrap of paper, folded it small, and put it inside their shirt, against the skin.

Not evidence. Evidence could be seized. This was closer to a relic.

They were repacking the lead case, debating whether to steal the originals outright (yes, obviously yes, and yet — Pell's boots, Pell's haunting, and the fact that a missing charter announced itself far sooner than a damaged one) when the lantern flame leaned sideways.

Noor froze. There was no draft below the waterline. There was no wind, no door open, nothing that moved air — and yet the flame bent steadily toward the deep rows, toward the oldest shelves, the way a plant leans toward light.

Or the way a compass leans north.

They followed it. Ten rows. Fifteen. The water rising past their thighs now, soaking the hem of their coat, and the shelves here were different — older construction, black wood, and the brass plates worn nearly blank except one, on the last shelf of all, which read only:

*Payments Received.*

Empty. The entire shelf was empty. Except that the dust on it was disturbed — recently, cleanly, in the shape of a book exactly the size of the ledger in Noor's satchel. And beside that shape, pressed into the dust in a hand too fine to be human, too deliberate to be accidental, someone had drawn a single character Noor recognized from Wren's old charm-work: the mark she used for *return.*

The flame stood upright again. The water stilled.

Noor stood in the flooded dark with their heart going like a drum and understood two things with total certainty. First: something down here — or something that had passed through here, recently, on its own errand — knew they had taken the ledger, and was not stopping them.

Second: they were not the only person in Vespergate hunting the truth about the vanished stars. Someone else had reached this shelf first. Someone who left messages in dead women's handwriting.

Behind them, far up the stairwell, faint through stone and water, a bell rang the wrong hour again — and under it, barely, a voice called down through the archive door, a woman's voice, crisp and frightened and unmistakably a stranger's:

"Hello? Is someone down there? I've been watching this door for two days, and either you're the charm-seller or we're both about to be very disappointed."
