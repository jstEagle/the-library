## 9. Astronomer Without a Sky

The first thing Odile Ferrant had learned about astronomy, at nine years old, standing on her father's shoulders to peer through a brass tube older than the city itself: the sky did not care whether you were watching.

She had spent thirty-one years proving it wrong. The sky cared. The sky *kept records*. Every wobble of every star was a sentence in an argument the heavens had been having with themselves since before Vespergate had walls, and Odile's life's work — her charts, her ephemerides, her forty-one volumes of careful, obsessive notation — was simply transcription. She took dictation from the universe and tried not to misspell anything.

And then, eleven nights ago, the universe had stopped talking mid-sentence.

Odile sat now in the great observatory above the Palace of Records, in the dark, because there was nothing else to do in the dark. The dome stood open overhead like a mouth that had forgotten what it was for. Where the sky should have been there was only blackness — not cloud-black, not night-black, but a black with texture to it, the way velvet has texture, the way a closed eye has texture. She had measured it. Of course she had measured it. She had measured everything about it, because measurement was the only prayer she knew.

The darkness began at precisely 4,200 fathoms.

She knew this the way other people knew their own heartbeat. On the first night she had assumed cloud cover. On the second, ash from some distant fire. On the third, when the border wards fell and half the city took to the streets screaming about omens, she had climbed to the roof of the observatory with her oldest instruments and done the thing that made her colleagues — both of them, before they'd stopped coming — call her *the Ferrant Method*: she refused to guess when she could measure.

A candle flame at the bottom of a deep well still illuminates the walls near it. Light behaves the same even when its source is gone. So Odile had rigged lanterns on long poles, hoisted them up past the parapet on ropes and pulleys, and read the way the light fell against whatever hung above the city.

It reflected back. That was the horror of it. Not perfectly — a true void swallows light without comment — but dimly, grudgingly, the way a wall reflects. Something was *up there*, and it had a surface, and the surface was four thousand two hundred fathoms up, and it covered everything from the Spire to the western sea as far as her instruments could tell.

Tonight she was checking whether it had moved.

* * *

"Still there," she said aloud, to no one, because saying things aloud helped her think, and also because the silence up here had developed a quality she could only describe as *attentive*. "Four thousand one hundred ninety-six fathoms. It's come down four."

She wrote the number in the log with a hand that only shook slightly. Four fathoms over twenty-four hours. Negligible, if you were measuring tides. Apocalyptic, if you were measuring the sky.

Her assistant's chair sat empty by the far wall. Pell had stopped coming on the fifth night, leaving a note that said his mother was ill, which might even have been true, though Odile suspected the truer illness was that Pell was twenty-three and wanted very much to go on being twenty-three, and staring into a starless dome made a person feel briefly mortal in a way no lecture ever had. She didn't blame him. Some mornings she envied him so fiercely it felt like a moral failing.

That left her alone with the instruments, the log, and the growing, unspeakable suspicion that she was not observing a phenomenon.

She was observing a *creature*.

The word had arrived three nights ago and refused to leave. It had come, as most of her worst ideas did, out of the data. She had been plotting the surface-height readings across the whole span of the city — she'd bribed six lamplighters and one extremely confused harbor pilot to take elevation measurements of their own at points across Vespergate, using lanterns and plumb lines and a system of notation she'd invented on a napkin — and the numbers had refused to lie flat.

They rose and fell. All of them. Together. In synchrony.

Not randomly. Not like weather. Like — she had lain awake until dawn searching for the honest word, discarding *oscillation*, discarding *pulsation*, discarding several terms that sounded properly academic and were therefore lies —

Like breathing.

Slow. Enormous. A cycle of roughly nine hours between breaths, if you could call it that, the whole underside of heaven swelling by a dozen fathoms and settling again, patient as something that had never once needed to hurry.

Odile had sat with that chart for a full day before writing the conclusion in her log, and even then she'd written it small, tucked in the margin, as if the ink itself might be embarrassed:

*Hypothesis: the occlusion is animate. Hypothesis rejected twice; cannot reject a third time. The sky is breathing.*

And here was the part she had told absolutely no one, because it was the part that would get her locked away or worse: the breathing had changed last night.

For twenty years — she'd gone back through her father's old logs to check, and his father's before that — the sky had held steady. Stars wheeled, seasons turned, the occlusion height stayed at 4,200 fathoms within instrument error. Whatever was up there had been asleep, or indifferent, or bound.

Then the stars went out, the wards fell, and the thing above began, slowly, to inhale.

Something below had woken it. And Odile Ferrant, who had spent her whole life believing the sky did not care whether you watched, had begun to suspect that it cared very much indeed, and that it had noticed her noticing.

* * *

She went down the mountain of stairs into the palace proper at dawn, carrying her log under her arm like a shield.

The Palace of Records had always struck her as a building designed by someone who loved knowledge but hated people: corridors wide enough for processions and narrow enough in spirit that you could walk them for an hour without meeting a friendly face. Today the faces she did meet were all wearing the same expression — the tight, sideways look of people waiting for the next catastrophe. Since the wards fell, the city guard had doubled its patrols, bread had tripled in price, and three separate street preachers had declared the end of the world, each citing different evidence and each, Odile noted professionally, offering no falsifiable predictions whatsoever.

She went first to the Lord Chamberlain's office, because protocol was protocol, and was kept waiting two hours in an antechamber decorated with portraits of men who had funded the observatory and looked, in their painted eyes, faintly regretful about it.

When she was finally admitted, the Chamberlain — a soft, damp man named Corvus who had gotten his post by surviving longer than everyone else in the room — listened to exactly four sentences of her report before holding up a hand.

"Ferrant. Forgive me. You're telling me the sky is..." he consulted a scrap of paper where he'd been taking notes, "...*breathing*?"

"Atmospherically speaking, the occlusive layer exhibits periodic vertical displacement consistent with—"

"You're telling me the sky is breathing," Corvus said again, flatly.

"I'm telling you it's *moving toward us*, my lord, at a measurable rate, and I have reason to believe the movement is connected to the failure of the wards, and I am requesting funding for a survey expedition to the eastern highlands where I can get instruments above the—"

"The treasury is rather occupied at present." He said it the way one mentions weather. "There are riots in the Tannery Quarter. There are priests saying the gods have closed their eyes. His Highness has not slept in — well. There are matters requiring attention more urgent than..."

He gestured vaguely upward, at the ceiling, in the direction of everything.

"*Than the sky,*" Odile finished.

"If it makes you feel better, the Royal Society declined to fund Master Hollen's earthquake machine last month, and that was at least *underground.*"

"It did not make me feel better."

"I didn't think it would." Corvus sighed and leaned forward, and for one moment something almost human moved behind his damp bureaucratic face. "Off the record, Ferrant. What do you actually think is happening?"

Odile considered lying, found she was bad at it and too tired to try, and said: "I think something ate the stars. I think it is connected to whatever broke the wards. And I think it is getting closer, and nobody with money wants to hear it."

Corvus nodded slowly, as a man nods at a physician's diagnosis he intends to ignore. "Send me your reports," he said. "I'll file them."

Which, translated from Chamberlain, meant: *I will put your papers in a drawer where terrifying truths go to die.*

* * *

She should have gone home after that. She should have eaten something, slept, done any of the sensible maintenance a body requires. Instead Odile walked down through the city in the gray morning with her log under her arm, taking the long way through the Lamplighters' Ward, because her feet had apparently decided something her head hadn't caught up to yet.

The ward had changed since the stars went out. Lamplighters worked double shifts now — the city had decided, with the instinctive wisdom of frightened crowds everywhere, that if heaven would provide no lights, it would make its own — and the little shrine at the corner of Wick Street, where the guild kept a perpetual flame dedicated to the Lady of Lanterns, had accumulated offerings: candles, oil flasks, a worrying quantity of cheese.

It was at the shrine that Odile heard the rumor.

She hadn't been listening for it. She'd stopped to buy a meat pie from a cart — the vendor charged her double and apologized for charging her double, which was somehow worse — and while she waited, two lamplighter women were talking in low voices by the shrine, and Odile had a scholar's incurable habit of overhearing.

"—swears by it. Her boy had the fever-feats, burning like a coal, and she took him to old Maren's luck-knots and the fever broke by morning."

"Maren's been gone two years."

"Not Maren. The *other* one. Down by the tallow-chandler's. Little stall, no sign, you have to know to ask. They say the charms are quiet ones — luck, sleep, mending — nothing that'd bring the wardens. But Tam swears the knot she tied for his cough worked better than the physicker's syrup, and Tam wouldn't know honesty if it bit him."

"And the wardens haven't—"

"Wardens have bigger worries lately, haven't they. Whole sky's gone out. Who's counting knots?"

The women moved off. Odile stood holding her overpriced meat pie and feeling the strange electric shiver that she had learned, over thirty-one years, to trust above all other instruments.

Data. That was data walking around on two legs.

A charm-seller. Operating quietly, in the cracks, since before the crisis — which meant someone in this city was practicing what the law called *illicit craft* and what Odile's professional vocabulary insisted on calling *unlicensed empirical work*. And more to the point: if the wards had failed, if the great purchased protections of Vespergate had snapped like cut strings, then the only people who might understand *how* those strings had been attached were people who worked with the same material. Small magic, quiet magic — but magic, real magic, the kind that touched whatever current ran underneath the world.

The court astronomers couldn't help her. The court had stopped listening. The Royal Society would want six months and a committee.

But a woman who sold luck-knots out of a shadow by the tallow-chandler's might know why the sky was breathing.

Odile ate the pie — it was terrible, and she barely noticed — and turned toward the Tanners' Lane, and then stopped herself, and stood very still in the middle of the street while porters flowed around her like water around a stone.

Because here was the trouble with being a scientist: you could not un-know your own methods. And her methods said that walking up to a hidden illegal mage and asking direct questions was the observational equivalent of poking a sleeping bear with a measuring rod. If the charm-seller was cautious — and anyone surviving twenty years as an illegal mage in Vespergate would be *very* cautious — then a court astronomer, recognizable by her guild badge and her ink-stained fingers and her general air of official business, would be exactly the sort of visitor to send them running.

No. She would watch first. Observation before intervention; it was the first rule of her method and, increasingly, the first rule of staying alive.

She spent the rest of the day establishing herself as a fixture. This was easier than it sounds, because Odile Ferrant had been a fixture her entire life — tall, angular, prone to standing in one place for hours with an air of mild disapproval at the universe. She bought a cup of cider she didn't want from a stall with a sightline to the chandler's alley. She sketched rooftops in a notebook, which gave her a plausible reason to stand and stare, and which was not entirely a fiction, because she *was* sketching rooftops, because she was also still tracking the occlusion height and the best vantage points happened to be right where she needed to be. Science rewarded the idle observer. This was the only field in which loitering counted as work.

The alley yielded nothing for four hours. Then, as dusk came down — real dusk now, indistinguishable from the false sky, which made Odile's chest tighten every time she noticed it — a figure slipped into the chandler's alley carrying a basket, and a minute later a second figure followed, and Odile marked the door, and the time, and the direction of the wind, and felt the old pure joy of a pattern emerging from noise.

And then the joy curdled.

Because the figure with the basket came back out within minutes, moving fast, glancing over their shoulder — and Odile, watching with an astronomer's trained eye for anomalous motion, saw why.

At the far end of the lane, where the lamplight didn't reach quite as well as it used to, someone else was watching the same door.

Not a neighbor. Neighbors leaned. This figure stood the way guards stood — weight balanced, still, *patient* — half in the shadow of the cooper's awning, and though Odile could see no badge, no uniform, everything about the posture said *warden*, or something wearing a warden's patience. The figure watched the door for a long time. Then it watched the street. Then, with a smooth unhurried turn, its gaze traveled down the row of faces along the lane—

—and passed over Odile, paused, and came back.

One second. Two. Odile kept sketching. Her pencil did not shake, because her hands had spent decades on delicate instrument work and had sworn an oath of steadiness, but inside her ribs something small and mammalian was screaming *run.*

The figure looked away. Melted back into the dark beyond the lamplight, going she-didn't-know-where.

Odile closed her notebook with great care. She walked home by a route she had never taken before, doubling back twice, telling herself this was paranoia and knowing perfectly well it wasn't, because she had spent the last eleven nights measuring a darkness that breathed, and she had learned its most important lesson already:

Things that breathe are things that notice.

* * *

That night she did not go up to the observatory. She sat in her rooms above the archive annexe with every candle lit — a extravagance she'd have lectured Pell about, once — and laid out her work across the floor like a spread of tarot cards, except hers were graphs.

The occlusion heights. Eleven days of them, plotted against time, against the tide tables, against the ward-failure timeline Sable Ives had published in the one pamphlet of his she'd ever bothered keeping. (Disgraced ward-smith. Drunk, according to reputation. Also, according to the same reputation, the finest intuitive grasp of ward-structure in a generation, which was the sort of combination Odile had learned to take seriously; genius and ruin frequently shared lodgings.)

The curves lay across her floorboards, and they all told the same story, and she finally let herself say it out loud to the empty room, because some conclusions need air to become real:

"They didn't fail. They were *called home.*"

The wards hadn't decayed. They had been withdrawn — drawn down and inward, toward the mountain, the way a fisherman draws in a line. Which meant the wards had never belonged to Vespergate at all. Which meant the protection the city had paid for, in coin and taxes and whatever else, had been a lease, not a deed. And the lessor, whatever lived beneath the mountain, had decided to collect.

And the sky — the breathing, descending, attentive dark — was either the lessor's other property, or its other self, or its *mouth.*

Odile sat on the floor among her charts until the candles burned low, and felt wonder and dread settle into their final proportions, which was: mostly dread, with wonder reduced to a fine sharp residue, like salt after a flood.

Tomorrow, she decided. Tomorrow she would find the charm-seller — carefully, indirectly, through the lamplighters, through the shrine, through the network of desperate hopeful people that rumor had already mapped for her. Tomorrow she would knock on the right door and say the right careful words, scientist to empiricist, and hope that curiosity was a language they shared.

Because the charts were clear, and the charts were merciless: the dark above Vespergate had descended another four fathoms today.

At the current rate, she calculated, tapping the pencil against her knee, the sky would reach the rooftops in something under sixty years.

At the rate it was *accelerating* —

She didn't finish the calculation. She got up instead, blew out the candles one by one, and stood a moment in the dark, testing how it felt.

It felt like being noticed.

"Tomorrow," she told the darkness, in her best lecture-hall voice, because if it was going to pay attention to her, it might as well learn she was not easily impressed. And she went to bed with her boots by the door, and her charts rolled and ready, and the name of a tallow-chandler's alley written on her wrist in ink, where she would be sure to find it in the morning.

Somewhere below the city, something turned over in its sleep. Or perhaps it wasn't sleeping at all.
