## 10. Three Thieves Make a Plan

The observatory had been beautiful once. Noor could tell because the parts that remained were still trying.

They climbed the hill at dusk, past the rusted gate with its official seal half-eaten by salt air, up a path gone soft with moss and neglect. The dome sat on its hill like a dropped egg, one side caved in where a storm—or a budget cut, or both—had finished what indifference started. Through the gap in the copper skin, Noor could see the great brass arm of the telescope jutting out at a drunken angle, pointing at nothing, which was lately all there was to point at.

Sable Ives was already inside, of course. He'd arrived early because he didn't trust anyone else alone with the site, and he'd brought a lantern because he didn't trust the dark either, and he'd arranged three crates in a rough triangle around the old observation floor because he didn't trust anyone to sit anywhere sensible without instruction.

"You're late," he said, when Noor ducked through the broken door.

"I'm early," Noor said. "You're just earlier."

"That's not—" Sable pinched the bridge of his nose. "Fine. Yes. I couldn't sleep. The wards twitch every time I close my eyes. Like a dog dreaming." He gestured at the crates. "Sit. Don't touch the brass. Some of it's older than the city and it bites."

Noor sat. They had brought bread, cheese, and a jar of pickled things, because a meeting of conspirators without food was just an argument waiting for an audience, and arguments went better fed. They set it out on the middle crate like a peace offering, which, given the history between them, it rather was.

Sable looked at the food. Looked at Noor. "Is it poisoned?"

"No."

"Can you *prove* it's not poisoned?"

"Sable. I am the only person in Vespergate who can prove anything isn't poisoned. It's my entire profession."

"Right." He took the cheese anyway, warily, like a man accepting terms.

* * *

Odile Ferrant arrived as the light died completely, which Noor suspected was deliberate. She came through the gap in the dome instead of the door, dropping lightly from the collapsed scaffolding with a leather tube under one arm and star-charts under her other arm and, when she landed, a third set of charts somehow produced from inside her coat, because apparently Odile traveled the way other people shed lint.

"You found the place," she said, pleased.

"It wasn't hard," Noor said. "It's the only building on the hill."

"Yes, but you *came*. People say they'll come and then they don't. I've been stood up by four separate colleagues this month." She said it cheerfully, the way she seemed to say everything, even things that were clearly devastating her. "Mostly because they think I'm mad. Which is fair. I have been charting darkness."

She unrolled her charts across the crates, over the bread, briefly over Sable, who objected and was ignored.

"Here." Her finger traced lines Noor couldn't follow. "Before the stars went out, I kept records of their positions. Twenty years of positions. Boring work. Beautiful work. And now—" she flipped to a fresh sheet, covered in dense annotations, "—now I chart the dark instead. And the dark has *shape*, friends. It isn't empty. Where the stars should be, there's something. A pattern. And the pattern moves."

"Moves how?" Sable asked.

"Breathes," Odile said simply. "In and out. Slow. Very slow. Like something enormous sleeping under a blanket, and the blanket is the sky."

Noor felt the words land somewhere below their ribs. They thought of the mountain above Vespergate, the one everyone politely called the Spire's shadow and nobody called anything else, because the mountain court did not appreciate being discussed. They thought of the wards falling like cut strings. Of Wren's letter, open on their table at home, its ink pressed deep enough to read with fingertips: *They sold me, Noor. Before they killed me, they sold what was left.*

"The mountain," Noor said. "It's under the mountain."

"Everything points under the mountain," Odile agreed. "The breathing pattern centers there. The vanished stars—if they're somewhere, they're somewhere *down*. Light doesn't die, you know. It gets moved."

"And the wards?" Noor turned to Sable. "You said withdrawal. Say it again, properly, because I want to hear it sober."

"I was sober," Sable protested, and then, catching both their looks, "I was mostly sober. Fine." He wiped his hands, took a stub of charcoal from his pocket, and drew on the back of one of Odile's charts—she made a noise of protest and he ignored her, which Noor noted with some satisfaction was apparently contagious. "A ward isn't a wall. People think walls. It's not. It's more like... a lung. It breathes power in and holds it, and what it holds, it spends. The border wards around Vespergate were built—I built them, or my order did, before I was—" he waved a hand at himself, at the stains and the disgrace, "—before. And we built them to run on a reservoir. A big one. Deep."

"Deep," Noor repeated.

"Underground-deep." He met Noor's eyes. "I always assumed the reservoir was water. Or earth-power, the old kind. But if someone down there has been *drawing*—if the mountain court has been pulling our ward-power upward, or inward, feeding it into something—" he tapped the charcoal against the chart, leaving little gray stars, "—then the wards aren't failing. They're being *drunk*. We're watching the last of the wine leave the bottle."

Silence settled over the observatory. Somewhere outside, the wind worried at the broken dome, making the whole structure hum a low, mournful note, like a bottle blown across.

"So," Noor said, because someone had to. "We go down and take it back."

"Take *what* back?" Sable asked. "The power? The souls? The stars?"

"All of it. Everything they stole." Noor heard their own voice come out flat and certain and thought: *Wren would laugh at this voice. Wren would say, look at you, planning crimes.* "My sister is down there. Whatever's left of her. And I intend to walk in and steal her back."

Odile clapped her hands once, delighted. Sable put his head in his hands.

"This is theft," he said, muffled. "From the mountain court. From beings that have been collecting souls since before the city had a name. You're proposing burglary."

"I'm proposing burglary," Noor agreed. "Do you have a better word?"

"Lunacy. Suicide. Tuesday, possibly."

"Do you have a better *plan*?"

He lowered his hands. And here was the thing about Sable Ives that no one in the guild halls remembered anymore, buried under the drink and the disgrace: when you actually asked him a question about wards, the shabbiness fell away and something precise and bright looked out through his eyes. "Actually," he said, "yes. Maybe. Possibly a better plan, if the mine shafts are what I think they are."

* * *

What followed was two hours of the strangest cartography Noor had ever participated in: a disgraced ward-smith drawing tunnels from memory, a starless astronomer correcting him with measurements taken from the way sound echoed off hillsides, and a food-taster contributing, in their own estimation, almost nothing except the observation that the pickled things were going fast and perhaps they should slow down.

"The old veins," Sable said, and the map beneath his charcoal had grown a root system, branching and descending toward the mountain's base. "Before Vespergate was a city it was a mining camp. Silver, then iron, then whatever the mountain court wanted. The shafts go down for miles. And here's the part your history books leave out—" he tapped a junction near the mountain's root, "—the first wards weren't built *around* the city. They were built *in the mines*. My order inherited them. Sealed most of the shafts. But sealed isn't destroyed. Dead wards don't die, they just... sleep. And sleeping wards remember the shape of the road."

"You're saying we can walk in through the wards' own bones," Odile said.

"I'm saying we might be able to, if the seals have rotted as badly as everything else I've built." He grimaced. "Which they will have. I rot things. It's my gift."

"You testified against my sister," Noor said.

The words came out before Noor decided to say them, the way a splinter works itself loose. Sable went very still. Even Odile stopped rearranging her charts.

"I know," Sable said quietly.

"No, I mean—you knew her. You knew Wren. You said so, that night at my stall. You said her charms were good." Noor's hands were steady in their lap; twenty years of practice kept them steady. "So when you stood up in that courtroom and told them what she was, you knew exactly what you were telling them."

"Yes."

"And you're here anyway. Offering to help me steal her soul back from the people you handed her to."

Sable looked at the map for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was rough. "There isn't a version of sorry that fits in a sentence, taster. I've been trying to write one for twenty years and it keeps coming out as drink. So instead I'm offering you this: every tunnel I know, every ward I ever touched, every trick left in these rotten hands. Use me. Spend me. If we get her back, maybe the ledger tilts a hair's width in my direction. That's all I'm asking. Not forgiveness. Arithmetic."

Noor studied him. Drunk-scented, gray-stubbled, hands stained with charcoal and worse memories. A man who had chosen the wrong side once and had been choosing it again, daily, in small cowardly ways, ever since—and who had finally, tonight, picked a side that cost something.

"Arithmetic," Noor said. "Fine. But understand me, Sable. If your arithmetic ever balances against Wren, I will know. Tasters know."

"Tasters know," he agreed solemnly, and something in the room loosened, though Noor wouldn't have called it trust yet. Trust was a charm that took longer to knot than this.

* * *

It was Odile, naturally, who named the flaw.

She had been quiet for several minutes, which for Odile constituted a geological event, and when Noor glanced over she was frowning at the map with the expression of a woman whose equations refused to balance.

"The problem," she said, "is the door."

"There's no door," Sable said. "That's the point. Shafts. Ropes. Bad air."

"Not that door. The *other* door. Think." She counted on her fingers. "We descend through dead wards. Fine. We surface inside the mountain court. Also fine, assuming the mountain court hasn't posted guards in its own basement, which, granted, is optimistic. And then what? We stroll up to the ledger of souls and ask nicely? These are beings who trade in contracts. Contracts have *terms*. Terms have *authority*. We are three nobodies with a stolen map and a bag of pickles." She paused. "The pickles are excellent, by the way. But they are not authority."

"She's right," Noor said reluctantly.

"I usually am, it's terribly lonely."

"What we need," Noor said slowly, feeling the idea assemble itself the way a poison assembles its symptoms—one element, then another, then the terrible click of the whole, "is someone the mountain court has to take seriously. Someone with standing. With a seal. Someone whose presence at a negotiation makes the Ledger Keeper pause."

Sable snorted. "And where do we find a person like that? The guildmasters won't spit on us. The council's bought down to its bootlaces. Half the nobility probably signed the original bargain themselves—"

"Prince Aurel," Noor said.

The word dropped into the room and rang.

"No," said Sable.

"He has the royal seal," Noor said. "The full seal, not the council's stamp. His father's authority, such as it was, such as it *is*. If the mountain court honors contracts—and everything says it does, contracts are its entire religion—then a prince's seal at the threshold changes what we are. We stop being burglars. We become a delegation. A theft with paperwork."

"He's the *prince*," Sable said. "He eats off plates you lick first. He lives behind forty wards and a hundred guards. What possible reason—"

"He cannot sleep," Noor said.

They both looked at Noor.

"Since the stars went out. I taste his breakfast every morning, and I watch him, because watching is also my profession. He hasn't slept properly in weeks. He wanders. He asks questions he shouldn't. Last week he asked me whether I believed the wards were dying, and then pretended he hadn't asked, and then watched me all through breakfast to see if I'd lie." Noor turned their cup in their fingers. "He knows something's wrong. Princes always know something's wrong; it's the one thing they're bred for. And he is, I think, the loneliest person in Vespergate, which is a competitive category."

"You want to recruit the crown prince of Vespergate into a burglary," Sable said, "based on your observations at *breakfast*."

"I want to *ask* him," Noor corrected. "Asking costs nothing. Well." They considered. "Asking a prince costs something. Princes keep score. But I've watched him keep score, too, and his ledger is stranger than you'd think. There are entries in it that look almost like guilt."

Odile had begun, quietly, to smile—the wide, slightly unhinged smile of a woman watching a hypothesis walk upright. "Three thieves," she said. "And we need a fourth. A thief with a seal."

"A prince," Sable said, "is not a thief."

"Every prince is a thief," Odile said serenely. "They just inherit the stealing. It saves time."

Noor almost laughed—a real laugh, startled out of them, the first in longer than they cared to count. It surprised all three of them. Sable stared. Odile beamed as though she'd personally manufactured it.

Outside, the wind changed note, and for a moment the humming of the broken dome sounded almost like breathing. In. Out. Something enormous, asleep under a blanket, far below.

Noor looked at the map—at the thin charcoal thread running down through the dead veins of the mountain, toward a sister made of fragments and a ledger written in souls—and felt the fragile, terrifying architecture of the thing they were building: three liars and a plan, held together with pickles and arithmetic.

"Well," they said, standing, brushing crumbs from their knees. "Someone has to ask the prince. And I know exactly what he's having for breakfast tomorrow."

* * *

The palace kitchens received Noor at dawn as they always did, with the nod reserved for furniture that could detect arsenic. On the tray: honeyed rolls, smoked fish, a small dish of stewed plums, and a pot of black tea.

Noor carried it up the east stair, past the guards who never saw them, into the prince's morning room where Prince Aurel sat by the window looking at a sky that offered him nothing back.

Noor set down the tray. Lifted each cover. Tasted each dish with the small silver spoon, unhurried, professional, invisible.

Then, instead of withdrawing to the corner as twenty years of habit demanded, Noor remained standing beside the table. And waited.

Aurel looked up from the window. His eyes were shadowed underneath like bruised fruit. He glanced at the untouched tea, at the taster who had not stepped back, and something in his sleepless face shifted—recognition, perhaps, or dread, or the particular relief of a man who has been waiting a long time for someone to finally say the thing.

"You're standing there," the prince said slowly, "as if you have something to tell me."

"Your Highness," Noor said, "I need to borrow your seal. And I suspect you need to hear why."
