## 11. Blackmail Is a Kind of Honesty

Aurel had learned to read people the way his father had taught him to read treaties: slowly, looking for the clause that would cost him.

His taster was a treaty with legs.

Three days since the stars went out, and Noor Nakamura still moved through the morning ritual like nothing had changed—arriving at the same hour, bowing the same shallow bow, tasting each dish with the same maddening serenity. But Aurel had spent three sleepless nights watching them from behind the screen of his own exhaustion, and he had noticed things.

The way their fingers paused a half-second too long over the honey jar. The way they'd stopped humming—the tiny tuneless sound they made when they thought no one could hear, some market melody they'd probably absorbed without meaning to. The way their eyes went, once, twice, three times, to the window that faced the mountain.

Tasters were trained not to look out windows. Windows were where assassins kept crossbows.

"You've stopped sleeping," Aurel said, on the fourth morning, into the silence between the poached eggs and the spiced pear.

"I've never slept," Noor said. "I stand very still in a dark room and call it a courtesy to my bed."

"Your hands shake when you think I'm not looking."

"My hands are cold. It's autumn."

"It's been summer for two weeks." Aurel set down his fork. "And yesterday you tasted the almond cake and didn't tell me it had been made with bitter extract cut with sweet oil—a trick to disguise arsenic that hasn't been used in this palace since before I was born, because everyone who knows the trick is dead or retired. You told me the cake was fine. It wasn't fine. Someone in my kitchens is either trying to kill me or testing you, and you decided I didn't need to know which."

For the first time in six years of service, Noor Nakamura looked genuinely surprised. It lasted about as long as a soap bubble.

"The cake was fine," they said. "I neutralized it."

"You *what*?"

"There's a clay in the pantry—fuller's earth, the potters use it. A pinch in wine, taken quickly, binds most alkaloids before they reach the blood. I've been dosing myself for years." They said it the way another person might say *I take my tea with honey*. "You were never in danger. From me."

"*From you*," Aurel repeated. He stood up. His chair scraped. Somewhere down the corridor, a guard pretended very hard to be deaf. "You have been quietly eating poison out of my breakfast for years and your confession is that I was never in danger from *you*?"

"I'm summarizing."

"You're—" Aurel pressed both hands flat against the table and breathed. This was, he reflected, what came of being raised by a father who considered honesty a form of indigestion: he had become a man who could be ambushed by a taster's side effects. "How many poisons are you immune to?"

"All the ones that matter."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer I give for free."

Something in the phrasing snagged him. Free. As opposed to—

"Noor," he said carefully. "What do you know?"

* * *

They ended up in his private study, because Noor refused to say anything else in a room with servants, guards, or—Aurel suspected—more than one exit. The study had one door, two windows, and a portrait of his father that Aurel kept meaning to have removed and never did, mostly because the old king's painted eyes were useful for practicing speeches he'd never dare deliver aloud.

Noor stood by the cold fireplace with their arms crossed, looking like someone who had rehearsed a speech and then decided, at the last moment, to improvise something worse.

"Before I say anything," they said, "I want you to understand that I am not doing this because I enjoy it."

"Nobody enjoys blackmail. That's rather the point of it."

"Some people enjoy it," Noor said. "I've met them. They're the reason I'm about to do this." They took a breath. "Three nights ago, I broke into the royal archive."

Aurel waited for the rest of the sentence. It didn't come.

"You broke into the archive," he said. "My archive. The flooded one under the waterline that my father sealed and that I have been forbidden to reopen since I was nine years old, on pain of—"

"On pain of whatever your father threatened nine-year-olds with. Yes. That one." Noor's chin lifted a fraction. "I found the original ward charter. The border wards around Vespergate—the ones that fell three nights ago—they weren't built. They were bought. Purchased from the court beneath the mountain, sixty years ago, by your grandfather. And the price of renewal, every year since, has been paid in souls."

The room got very quiet. Somewhere below them, a clock ticked with the smugness of clocks everywhere.

"That's a lie," Aurel said, but even as he said it, he heard how it sounded—not like refutation, like flinching. "That's treasonous nonsense. The wards are maintained by the Wardens' Guild, they're fed by—"

"By what? Did anyone ever tell you what feeds them?" Noor's voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "Because I've read the ledgers now, Your Highness. All of them. And there is a name in the oldest book that I have been trying not to read for twenty years, and it's mine. My sister's. Wren Nakamura. Sold to the mountain court the year I turned eight, to pay for a debt our family owed, and I have spent every day since then learning how to survive long enough to find out why."

Aurel sat down. Not gracefully. He more or less missed the chair and corrected on the way.

He thought about arguing. He thought about calling the guard—he could see himself doing it, the whole performance, the righteous prince and the criminal taster, and it would work, it would all work, and it would change absolutely nothing, because Noor had just described his father's reign in a single sentence and every instinct Aurel owned was screaming that it fit. The sealed archive. The annual tribute caravans that officially carried "ceremonial goods" to the mountain shrines. The way his father had gone pale, actually pale, the one time seven-year-old Aurel had asked what was inside the tribute chests.

"What do you want?" he asked instead.

"Access," Noor said. "Protection. Time. In exchange—" and here they hesitated, and Aurel watched them decide to stop lying, watched it happen like watching someone put down a heavy load after carrying it so long they'd forgotten its weight, "—in exchange, I'll tell you the truth. All of it. Whatever you ask."

"That's not blackmail."

"No. This is the blackmail part." Noor reached into their coat and produced a folded page—actual paper, actual ink, actual archive seal, and Aurel felt his stomach drop through the floor and keep going. "This is a copy of the renewal ledger from the year your mother died. Her name is on it, Your Highness. Queen Maren. She wasn't killed by the fever. She was sold."

* * *

Later—and Aurel would never be able to reconstruct how much later; the light through the windows moved from midmorning to gold to nothing while neither of them touched it—he found himself standing in front of his father's portrait with the ledger page in his hand.

He had read it eleven times. The handwriting was his father's. He knew it the way you know a scar: the cramped leftward slant, the way the old king wrote his *r*'s like little knives. *One soul, crown-weight, against arrears.* Crown-weight. His mother had been entered into a ledger as a unit of currency, and his father had written her in himself, in his own hand, and then come home and told Aurel she had died of fever, and held his hand at the funeral, and wept.

Aurel believed the weeping. That was the worst of it. He believed his father had wept, and paid anyway, because the alternative was letting the wards fall and the city die, and his father had always been the sort of man who would carry any weight if someone called it duty.

It didn't make it better. It made it worse. Monsters could be hated cleanly.

"She sang to me," Aurel said, to the portrait, because Noor was still in the room and he had apparently passed through shame and out the other side. "Every night. There was a song about a fisherman who bargains with the sea. I always thought it was strange that she liked that song. Bargaining songs." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "She knew. Didn't she. She must have known what she was."

"Probably," Noor said quietly. "The ledger shows consent clauses. Most of the early entries signed willingly. It was cheaper than conquest, for the mountain. People will sell almost anything if you let them believe it saves someone else."

Aurel turned around. His taster stood by the fireplace, arms still crossed, but the armor of it had cracked somewhere along the way; they looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. Twenty years of standing very still in dark rooms, he thought. Calling it a courtesy to the bed.

"You realize," he said, "that you've just handed me enough to burn the monarchy down. My father's legacy, the Wardens' Guild, half the council—you could have sold this to the rebels for a fortune. You could have sold it to the mountain court itself, probably. Instead you're offering me a partnership and asking for access to the same archive you already robbed." He shook his head. "Why? What do you actually want, Noor?"

"Wren."

"One soul. Out of hundreds in those ledgers."

"One soul that's mine." Something flashed in their face—grief, fury, the specific stubbornness of someone who had survived by refusing to want things out loud and had finally, catastrophically, wanted one anyway. "The rest can burn the city down around us for all I care. But if you're asking whether I feel guilty about caring about one name more than the others—no. I don't. Guilt is for people with spare time."

"And what are you going to do? Walk up to the mountain and knock?"

"I was going to go through the old mine shafts with a disgraced ward-smith and an astronomer who's been charting the darkness, actually, but thank you for your concern."

Aurel stared at them. "You have a *plan*."

"A rough plan."

"With a drunk and a stargazer."

"Sable Ives is the best ward-smith this city ever exiled, and Odile Ferrant is the only person alive who understands what happened to the sky. And they're coming because they have their own reasons, which are none of your business yet." Noor unfolded their arms at last. "Which brings us to the terms."

"Terms. Of course. Blackmail always has terms."

"First: I get full access to the palace seal archives and the Warden correspondence. Second: if I'm caught, you never heard of me, and I mean that literally—I need you to disown me so thoroughly that even your enemies believe it. Third—" Noor paused, and for a moment looked almost embarrassed, which on a face built entirely out of composure was nearly alarming. "Third, I need to know what you intend to do with what I've shown you. Because if your answer is 'seal it back up and protect the crown,' then I walk out of here and you never see me again, and I release copies of that ledger to every printing house in Vespergate within the week."

"Those are bold terms for someone whose entire legal existence depends on my signature."

"I've noticed," Noor said, "that depending on your signature is safer than it sounds. You sign everything. You've never once refused to sign something. It's your one flaw as a blackmailer's target."

Aurel opened his mouth to object and closed it again, because he had reviewed the year's documents that very week and the objection had no leg to stand on.

"Here are *my* terms," he said.

Noor's eyebrows rose a cautious quarter-inch.

"One: you stop lying to me. Starting now, starting today. If you're planning something dangerous, I hear it from you first, not from a warden's report. Two: the ledger stays sealed until I've finished what I'm going to do with it—which is not sealing it, before you threaten me again, so you can put the dramatic pause away. Three." He stopped. He had been composing terms in his head all afternoon, sensible princely terms, and now that he reached the third one he found it wasn't a term at all, it was just a thing he needed to say, and saying it felt like stepping off a ledge.

"Three?" Noor prompted.

"I'm coming with you."

Silence. The clock ticked. Down in the courtyard, someone dropped a crate of oranges, and the swearing carried all the way up through the window.

"No," Noor said.

"The mountain took my mother. I have spent my whole life being the heir to a bargain I didn't know existed, and I am *done*—" his voice cracked and he hated it and kept going anyway, "—done being protected from the truth by men who paid for my safety with other people's souls. You want the palace seal? You want access, protection, a prince who owes you? Fine. But I don't buy my way in. I walk in beside you, or the deal changes shape."

"You're the crown prince of Vespergate. If you vanish, the entire city tears itself apart looking for you. Every warden, every minister, every spy—"

"Then we move fast."

"This isn't a trade delegation, it's a theft—"

"From *thieves*, Noor." He heard his own voice go quiet and hard, and realized distantly that he meant every word. "We are stealing back stolen property. I've read the charter now too. Do you know what it says about reclamation? Any soul may be reclaimed by one willing to stake a soul of equal weight. Equal weight. Crown-weight." He tapped the ledger page. "There is exactly one soul in this city registered at crown-weight besides hers, and I'm wearing it."

Noor looked at him for a long moment. He watched them run the calculation—risk, exposure, the sheer unwieldiness of adding royalty to a smuggling operation—and he watched them lose to something that wasn't calculation at all.

"You'll slow us down," they said finally.

"Undoubtedly."

"You complain constantly. I've seen you send back soup over the *wrong garnish*."

"The garnish matters, Noor. Soup is architecture."

"You cannot fight."

"I can negotiate. Watch me negotiate right now: I'm winning."

"You are not—" Noor pinched the bridge of their nose, and Aurel had the sudden, absurd pleasure of watching six years of professional serenity collapse into pure exasperation. "Fine. FINE. You come. But you follow instructions, you wear a disguise, and if anyone asks, you're my cousin Jude, a clerk, and you have never in your life eaten anything interesting."

"Jude," Aurel repeated. "You're naming me after yourself."

"It's a boring name. Boring names save lives." Noor headed for the door, then paused with a hand on the frame, not quite turning around. "For what it's worth—your mother's entry in the ledger. There's a note in the margin, in her hand. I didn't show you because it wasn't leverage. It's just... a sentence. About you."

Aurel's throat closed. "What does it say?"

"'Tell him the sea song was true. The fisherman gets everything back.'"

Noor slipped out before he could answer, which was probably wise, because Aurel was fairly sure his face was doing something unprincely, and because dawn was coming, and somewhere across the city a charm-seller was presumably already deciding which parts of their old life to burn.

He looked at the ledger page one last time. Then he pulled open his desk drawer, took out the royal seal, and began, very quietly, to practice forging his own signature badly enough to pass as a clerk named Jude.
