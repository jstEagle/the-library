## 12. The Last Charm Shop

The shop had no name on the door, which was the first rule of having a shop in Vespergate. The second rule was that it should look like nothing worth entering: a narrow slot between a chandler's and a cobbler's, its window fogged with grime, its shelves stocked with things a warden's eye would slide past without catching—dried lavender, spools of thread, bundles of kindling, jars of honey.

Noor had spent eleven years making sure it looked like nothing. It had taken most of those years to learn how much work *nothing* actually was.

Now they stood in the middle of it with a lamp turned low and a list written in their own careful hand, and the list said, in effect: burn everything.

*Luck-knots, nine.* Burn.

*Warding threads, two dozen, various strengths.* Burn.

*Sleeper's bells, four.* Burn — though it pained them; old Marta down the lane swore by hers for the nightmares that came after her husband drowned.

*Bitterleaf tincture, three bottles.* That one wasn't magic at all, just medicine, but medicine in a mage's cellar was evidence all the same. Burn, or drink, which amounted to the same thing by morning.

Noor set the list down and pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes until they saw soft orange ghosts. Twenty years of being useful and unremarkable, and here was the sum of it: a room full of small mercies, each one a hanging offense if anyone looked too closely.

And someone was going to look. Sable had been clear about that, in his flat smith's way, the night they'd planned in the ruined observatory. *Wardens sweep before any operation this size,* he'd said, turning his cup instead of drinking it. *They'll go through every registered residence within a mile of the palace first. Then every unregistered one. They'll find your shop, Noor. It's only arithmetic.*

Arithmetic. Noor had survived on arithmetic for twenty years. So: the charms could not be found, because found charms meant a name, and a name meant the gallows before they ever reached the mountain, and Wren's soul would sit in its lantern another twenty years while Noor's body swung politely outside the walls for the crows to consider.

There was really only one way to do this.

They fetched the iron kettle from behind the counter—the one they used for rendering wax—and set it in the yard behind the shop, where the neighbors' washing lines couldn't see. Then they began to carry out their life's work, armload by armload.

It went into the kettle in layers. Thread first, because thread caught fast and burned hot. Then the knots, which fought a little, as if they knew. Noor had tied every one of them with the same words under their breath—not a spell exactly, more a habit of the hands, something Wren used to hum while she worked. *A knot for luck, a knot for grace, a knot to hold what time can't erase.* She'd learned it from their mother, who'd learned it from nobody, who'd simply made it up one rainy afternoon because it sounded nice. That was the whole inheritance. A nonsense rhyme and a pair of quick fingers.

The fire took the rhyme with everything else.

Noor watched the smoke rise thin and gray over the wall and thought about the lamplighter, Tam Broadstairs, who had bought a luck-knot eight months ago with coins counted out of a boot because his hands shook too badly for a purse. He'd known what he was buying. Most people didn't. He'd held it up to the light of his own lamp and said, *My girl thinks I'm a fool. But I've walked these streets thirty years and never once been robbed, and I don't think that's arithmetic.* He'd died in the winter anyway—lungs, not luck—but his daughter still came by sometimes, and Noor still charged her nothing, and neither of them called it grief.

Which reminded Noor that they were expected somewhere, and that lying to Tam's daughter was going to be harder than lying to a prince.

* * *

Perrin Broadstairs arrived at dusk, as arranged, carrying a basket that smelled of bread and hiding a shovel.

"You're sure about this," she said. It wasn't quite a question. Perrin was seventeen and had her father's habit of stating things firmly enough that questions became unnecessary. She was tall and freckled and unimpressed by most things, including, currently, Noor.

"I'm sure."

"Because I can think of six better ways to get rid of charms than setting them on fire in your own yard where anyone with eyes can see the smoke."

"Name one."

"Bury them. Sell them fast and cheap. Give them away." She ticked these off on floury fingers. "Float them down the canal on a raft, which I admit is dramatic but at least it's *elsewhere*."

"And when the wardens dig," Noor said, "or question everyone who bought one, or drag the canal—which they will, they're thorough, it's the one thing they're good at—every single charm leads back here. Ash leads nowhere. Ash is just ash. Half the yards in Vespergate have ash in them."

Perrin considered this with the grave attention she gave to everything, then nodded once, sharply, like a judge accepting testimony. "All right. But you're doing it wrong." She set down the basket, rolled up her sleeves, and took the poker out of Noor's hand. "You've got the knots layered under the thread. Thread burns to nothing; knots hold their shape. You want the slow stuff on top so the heat works down. My da taught me—" She stopped. Her jaw did something complicated. "He taught me how fires work. Lamps, mostly. Same principle."

So they burned the shop together, and Perrin was right about the layering, and Noor let her be right because it seemed important to her, and because there was a particular kindness in letting someone teach you a thing their dead father taught them. The smoke went up thick and sweet-smelling, lavender and beeswax and char, and Noor stood in it and felt each charm go like a small door closing.

The sleeper's bells went last. Noor had made those for people who wept in their sleep, and there had been more demand for them lately than ever before, whole city weeping into its pillow since the stars went out, and now even that small mercy was fuel.

"There," Perrin said finally, when the kettle held nothing but gray flakes and the yard smelled like the end of a fair. "That's that." She wiped her face and left a smear of ash across one cheekbone, which she would discover later and be annoyed about. "What now?"

"Now," Noor said, "we make it look like nothing happened."

This was Perrin's real contribution, and she'd brought tools for it: a sack of cold hearth-ash from her own house, ordinary and blameless, to scatter over the yard and blur the edges. A bucket of dishwater to kill the smell. And, produced from the bottom of the basket with some ceremony, half a loaf of bread and a pot of dripping, because in Perrin's opinion no job was properly done without food afterward, and Noor had long ago stopped arguing.

They worked by feel as the light failed. Cold ash over hot ash. Dishwater slopped generously over the stones. Perrin dragged the kettle to the corner where the chandler stored his empty barrels, on the theory that a kettle near barrels looked like a kettle that belonged there, and honestly Noor couldn't fault the logic.

"The window," Perrin said, straightening, hands on hips, surveying the yard like a general. "You'll want to make the shop itself look abandoned. Dust everywhere. Furniture under sheets. If wardens come, you want them bored in under a minute."

"I know how to be boring, Perrin."

"I know," she said, and then, with devastating gentleness: "I mean you're good at it. That's the sad part."

Noor laughed despite themselves—a short, surprised sound, the kind they usually kept locked up. Twenty years of practice at being unremarkable, summarized by a seventeen-year-old with ash on her face. It was fair. It was also, they were beginning to understand, over. Whatever happened next—in the mountain, under the mountain, inside the dark where the stars hung caged like moths in jars—Noor Nakamura was done being invisible. You didn't walk into a court that ate light and come back unnoticed. Either they'd succeed loudly or fail permanently, and both options required, for the first time in their adult life, showing their whole hand.

The thought should have terrified them. It did terrify them. But underneath the terror was something else, something lighter, like a splint coming off a healed bone.

"Perrin," they said, "if I'm not back in ten days—"

"I know. Da told me the same thing before his last route in the flood year." She was already shaking her head. "I'm not taking a letter. Letters are for funerals. Tell me yourself when you come back, and it had better be a good story, because I've been covering for you with Mrs. Ansel for two years and I intend to collect."

"Collect what?"

"Charm lessons." She said it flatly, daring them to object. "When it's legal again. Which you seem to think it might be, after whatever you're all doing down there. Odile Ferrant doesn't sneak around ruined observatories over nothing." She paused. "Also I want to know why the prince's taster knows an astronomer and a disgraced ward-smith, but that can wait for the story too."

Noor looked at her—this sharp, steady girl who had guessed more than half of it and asked for nothing but the future—and felt the loneliness that had fit them like a coat for twenty years suddenly hang wrong on the shoulders.

"It's a deal," they said. "Lessons. The whole ugly story. Everything."

"Everything?" Perrin's eyebrows went up. "Even the part where you're—"

"Don't say it out loud in a yard," Noor said quickly, "even a clean one."

"See, that's exactly the sort of thing I'll need lessons for." But she grinned, and it was Tam's grin, the lamplighter's grin, warm as a lit street, and Noor thought: *this is what we're buying. Not Wren alone. This. All of this, able to stand in the open.*

* * *

Back inside, the shop looked like a wound being dressed. Sheets over the shelves. Dust scattered artfully—Perrin had opinions about dust, too, namely that real neglect clumped and theatrical neglect didn't, and she'd demonstrated on the counter until Noor admitted defeat. The sign of the trade, such as it was—nothing, a blank board—stayed up. Nothing was the best disguise. Let the wardens find an empty room where a dull person had once sold dried lavender.

One thing remained.

It sat in Noor's pocket where it had sat for twenty years, through trials and funerals and ten thousand careful meals: a luck-knot, small and worn soft, tied in red thread gone pink with age. Wren's knot. The first one she'd ever tied well, the one she'd pressed into Noor's hand the morning of her arrest, laughing, saying *you'll need this more than me, little brother, you worry like an old woman*—and then she hadn't laughed at the trial, and Sable Ives had testified, and the knot had become the only thing left in the world with her hands' work in it.

Every other charm in the shop had gone into the kettle. This one would go into the mountain, into the vault where its maker's soul hung dim in a lantern, and Noor would put it back into her hands if it was the last thing they did. It probably would be the last thing they did. The Ledger Keeper's terms were clear enough: reclaim by dawn of the third day, or forfeit your own soul. And beneath the terms, unsaid but understood by everyone planning this mad errand, lay the simpler truth—that walking into the deep vault alive required crossing the soul-barrier, and crossing the soul-barrier alive required the poison Noor had been sipping for twenty years, taken now in full, all at once, burning out forever the immunity that had kept them breathing through a thousand poisoned meals.

Their body was a ledger of poisons. Soon it would be a ledger with every page torn out.

Noor closed their fist around the knot and blew out the lamp.

In the doorway, Perrin waited with the basket under her arm. "Walk with me as far as the corner," she said. "Anyone sees us, we're neighbors and I'm complaining about the price of candles. It's very convincing. I've practiced."

They walked together through streets gone strange since the stars went out—lanterns lit at noon now, wards flickering on their posts like dying beetles, people moving fast and not looking up because there was nothing up there to look at anymore. At the corner, where the ways parted, Perrin stopped.

"For what it's worth," she said, not looking at them, studying the candle-shop window with tremendous interest, "my da always said you were the best customer he ever had. Never haggled. Always asked after my mother. He said some people are just *good*, and you can tell because they're embarrassed when you notice." She finally glanced over, fierce and young. "Come back and be embarrassed at me for years. That's an order."

"Yes, ma'am," Noor said, and meant it as a promise, and Perrin nodded once—judge again, accepting terms—and marched off into the lantern-light with her empty basket, leaving Noor alone at the crossroads with ash on their sleeves and a pocketful of red thread.

* * *

The safehouse was a disused wine cellar under a shuttered dyer's, chosen by Sable because it had two exits and by Odile because the damp made her instruments behave oddly, which she claimed was useful data. Aurel had not yet arrived. He was, presumably, extracting himself from the palace without triggering the very wardens he technically commanded, a feat Noor did not envy him.

Sable looked up when they came in. He was cleaning his tools, which he did constantly, the way other men prayed. His eyes went to Noor's sleeves, to the smell of smoke that clung to them, and he understood immediately—he'd burned his own forge-work two nights ago, they'd heard, every ward-thread he'd ever woven, in a barrel behind a tavern, standing watch over it until dawn.

"Gone?" he asked.

"Gone. Ash and dishwater. The Broadstairs girl helped." Noor sat down on a crate. Their hands, for the first time in years, had absolutely nothing to do, and it was the strangest sensation of their life. "She wants lessons. When it's legal."

Sable's mouth twitched—the closest thing to a smile he owned. "Then we'd better win."

Odile looked up from her charts, quill poised. "Statistically speaking—"

"Don't," Noor and Sable said together.

Footsteps on the stair then, unhurried, and a knock in a pattern they'd agreed on, and Prince Aurel came down into the lamplight wearing a dyer's drab cloak and the expression of a man who had recently lied to his entire household and enjoyed it less than he'd expected. He carried a satchel heavy with papers—the royal seal wrapped in oilcloth, the thing that would open the mine shafts—and something else, smaller, which he set on the crate between them without explanation.

A plum. Fresh, dark-skinned, perfect.

Noor stared at it. So did Sable. Odile's quill stopped mid-stroke.

"Eat it or don't," Aurel said quietly. "But know that I checked it myself, against everything my father's kitchens ever taught me, and it's clean. First honest food I've handled in weeks." His eyes met Noor's, and there was no test in them anymore, no trap—only the same tired, stubborn loneliness Noor saw in their own mirror. "Consider it a last meal in the old city. We leave for the shafts at midnight."

Noor picked up the plum. It weighed almost nothing.

Twenty years of tasting other people's food, and here at the end was one piece of fruit offered freely, by a friend, with nothing hidden in it—and their immunity, the great armor of their life, already spoken for, already promised to the dark. They bit into it, and it was the sweetest thing they had ever tasted, and they understood, with the simple clarity of someone standing at the edge of a high place, that they were never going to taste anything safely again.

Midnight was coming. The mountain was waiting. Noor swallowed, and reached for Wren's luck-knot, and began to tie it into their hair where the red thread would show.

Some things, after all, were done with being invisible.
