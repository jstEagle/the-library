## 13. Down the Old Veins

The entrance to the old mine shafts was hidden behind a wall of bramble and a century of official forgetting, exactly the kind of place Sable Ives had spent his whole career pretending didn't exist.

"Charming," Odile Ferrant said, peering into the black mouth of the shaft. She held her lantern high. "A hole in the ground that smells like regret and bat. I've had lodgings like this."

"You *have* lodgings like this," Aurel said. The prince was dressed in borrowed wool two shades too fine for a mine crawl, and he kept touching the hilt of a knife he clearly didn't know how to use. "The observatory. I've seen it."

"The observatory has *dignity*."

Sable grunted and tested the first timber brace with his palm. Old ward-smith habit: you never trusted wood you hadn't insulted personally. The beam groaned, considered its options, and held. He marked it with a scrape of chalk anyway. Twenty years since he'd worked a living ward, but his hands still wanted to mark things.

"Noor," he said. "You're sure about this route?"

Noor Nakamura crouched at the shaft mouth, running their fingers along the stone in a way that looked idle to anyone who didn't know better. Sable knew better. He'd built wards beside mages before the trials; he recognized the small, guilty economy of a working spell. Noor wasn't touching the rock. They were listening to it.

"There's a vein here," Noor said. "Dead, mostly. But it remembers being a road." They glanced up at him, and the lantern light caught the flat, careful expression Sable had come to think of as the taster's face—the one they wore the way other people wore armor. "Your testimony said the mines were collapsed."

"My testimony," Sable said, "said a lot of things."

The silence after that had edges. Odile looked at the sky, which was to say she looked at the dark ceiling of the world where stars should have been, and Aurel suddenly found his bootlaces fascinating.

"We should move," Noor said, and went down into the earth without waiting for agreement.

* * *

The first hour was easy, which should have warned them.

The old Vespergate mines ran under the city like veins under skin—silver once, then iron, then nothing worth the digging—and the court beneath the mountain had used them for centuries as the quiet road between what the city saw and what it paid for. Sable had helped seal them, back when he still believed sealing things was the same as ending them. He'd written the collapse wards himself. He'd been proud of them.

Now he walked through his own dead work and felt it twitching under his boots like nerves in a severed limb.

"It's here," Odile whispered, half an hour in, when the shaft widened into a gallery propped on blackened timbers. She pressed her lantern against the wall, and there it was: a faint tracery of lines in the stone, silver-gray, pulsing. Slowly. Rhythmically. Like something breathing in its sleep.

"That's my pattern," Sable said. His voice came out strange. He cleared his throat. "Third revision. The one after the inquiry. I—" He stopped. There was no way to finish that sentence that didn't end with *I built the locks on the cage*, so he didn't.

"They're dead," Noor said. Not unkindly. Factually, the way they said everything.

"Dead things don't pulse."

"These do." Noor laid two fingers on the wall, light as a taster testing a spoon, and the tracery flared—a brief, sick shimmer—then dimmed again. Whatever lived at the far end of those lines had felt the touch and decided it wasn't worth waking for. Yet.

"Wonderful," Aurel said. "The walls are asleep. Let's all agree not to wake the walls."

They walked on. The air grew warmer and wetter, tasting of iron and something sweeter underneath, something that made Sable's teeth ache in a way he remembered from his apprenticeship and wished he didn't: soul-fire, leaking up from below like heat off a forge. Somewhere far beneath them, the mountain court was burning what it had bought.

He caught himself counting the pulses in the walls. Sixty to the minute. Almost a heartbeat. He made himself stop counting, which lasted four minutes.

It was somewhere in the fifth hour, in a gallery so narrow they had to go single file with their shoulders brushing the walls, that Sable finally ran out of ways to not say it.

* * *

They'd stopped to rest at a junction where three shafts met, marked by an ancient surveyor's post worn smooth by generations of hands. Noor sat with their back to the stone, eyes closed—not sleeping, Sable suspected, but rationing themselves. Aurel was asleep genuinely and completely, the way only exhausted princes can sleep, slumped against Odile, who was sketching the junction by lantern light and muttering calculations at it.

Sable sat down across from Noor. In the dark, with the walls pulsing their slow sixty-to-the-minute, it seemed suddenly important that someone be awake and honest, because he wasn't sure either of them would get another chance.

"I testified against your sister," he said.

Noor opened their eyes.

He'd planned, over twenty years, about forty versions of this conversation. In most of them he was drunker. In several of them Noor hit him, which he'd privately thought he'd deserve. None of the versions had him saying it plainly, in the dark, with his own dead wards breathing around him like a confession written on the walls.

"At the trial," he went on, because stopping now would be worse. "Wren Nakamura. Charged with unsanctioned magic. They needed a ward-smith to confirm the residue patterns matched forbidden craft. I was twenty-six years old and I'd just lost my commission for—" he laughed, short and joyless, "—for honesty, actually, if you can believe that. I'd told the Guild their new border-ward design was fraudulent. So they gave me a choice. Testify for the Crown and be restored, or testify honestly and be finished."

"And you testified for the Crown."

"The patterns *were* hers. That part was true—I confirmed true patterns and let them build the gallows out of them. What I didn't say, what I could have said, was that the residue wasn't harmful. It was a luck-charm. A *hearth-blessing*. The kind of thing every grandmother in the lower city does with a knot and a prayer and nobody calls it magic." His hands had curled into fists somewhere without his permission. "I told the truth the way a knife tells the truth. All edge, no context. And they thanked me, and restored my commission, and I drank to feeling clean for about a year before the drink stopped working."

Odile had stopped sketching. She was very still, lantern low, watching the space between the two of them the way astronomers watch eclipses.

Noor hadn't moved. Their face in the dimness was the taster's face again, smooth and closed, and Sable understood—with a clarity that felt like falling—that they had known. Had probably always known. Had tasted his name in the trial records the way they tasted poison, and filed it away, and kept selling him directions to the night market anyway because he was useful.

"You're telling me this now," Noor said quietly, "because we're underground and I can't leave."

"No." It came out too fast. He made himself slow down. "No. I'm telling you now because in six hours we might all be dead, and I couldn't stand for the last true thing between us to be a lie I told twenty years ago."

The silence stretched. Above them, or below them—it was hard to tell, down here—a deep sound rolled through the rock, distant and vast, like a door closing in a house the size of a country.

"When they executed her," Noor said at last, "I was fifteen. I stood in the crowd because they wouldn't let family refuse. And afterward I went home and I ate everything in the pantry, one bite at a time, checking each one, because it was the only thing I knew how to do." A pause. "I taught myself poisons so that no one could ever take another person from me that way again. Do you understand? Everything I am started in that square. And your name is in the record next to hers."

"Yes."

"I know," Noor said. "I've known since I was sixteen. I read the transcripts until I could recite them."

Sable felt that land in his chest like a dropped anchor. "And you still—"

"I still sold you a charm for your cough every winter. Because you paid fair and you never asked questions and because—" Noor exhaled slowly, and some of the taster's face cracked, just at the edge, just enough to see the grief underneath, old and load-bearing. "Because hating you was easier than deciding what to do about you, and I'm very good at not deciding things. Ask anyone."

From the shadows, Odile said softly: "That's the most honest sentence anyone's spoken to me in eleven years, and I work with telescopes."

"Shut up, Odile," Sable and Noor said together, and something in the saying-it-together almost, *almost*, cracked a smile on both of them.

Noor stood. Picked up their pack. Rolled their shoulders like a person taking up a weight they intended to carry the rest of the way, whatever the rest of the way cost.

"I don't forgive you," they said.

"I know."

"But Wren is down there, and you know these tunnels better than any of us, and I need your hands more than I need your punishment. So." They turned toward the descending shaft, toward the sweet iron smell of the deep places. "Keep walking, Sable. That's not forgiveness. It's arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," Sable repeated. His voice cracked on the second syllable. He decided to pretend it hadn't. "Right. I can live with arithmetic."

He got up and followed. Behind him, Odile woke the prince with the traditional method of removing his support, and Aurel surfaced sputtering with a question about how long he'd been asleep and why everyone was looking at him strangely, and the little company re-formed itself in the dark and went on down.

But Sable noticed—because he was, despite everything, a man who noticed structures—that Noor walked close to him after that. Closer than the narrow tunnel required. Not touching. Just near. The way you stand near a wall that's failed before, because you want to feel it when it goes.

He'd take it. Arithmetic was more than he'd earned, and he'd carry it the whole way down.

* * *

The third watch of the descent was where the wards began to fight back.

It started as a hum in Sable's fillings—he still had two, from a life he barely remembered—and became a pressure, and then a whisper. His own patterns, the third revision, the ones he'd sealed the veins with, were still threaded through the rock like stitches in a wound, and something below was pulling on them. Testing knots.

"Sable." Noor's hand closed on his arm. "The walls."

He already felt it. The pulse had changed. Sixty to the minute had become fifty-eight, fifty-five, the sleep-breathing quickening toward something with intent behind it. In the lantern light the traceries on the stone had begun to glow, faint and silver, lighting up one after another down the gallery ahead of them like windows going on in a house preparing for guests.

Or guards.

"It knows we're here," Odile breathed. "Or it knows *something* is. It's waking the old lines to look."

"It's using my work to do it," Sable said grimly. He knelt and pressed his palm flat against the nearest glowing seam. The light crawled up his wrist, cold and probing, reading him. "Of course it is. I built the lock. It has my signature on file." He looked back at the others, and heard himself say the thing he'd been avoiding saying since the shaft mouth: "There's one way past this section. The wards need a smith's countersign, and I'm the only smith here. But a countersign means opening myself to the network. Every dead line in these veins will feel me. Including the ones connected to *them*."

"To the mountain court," Aurel said.

"To the thing that bought the sky," Sable said. "Yes."

Silence, except for the quickening pulse. Fifty-two beats now. Forty-nine.

"Do it," Noor said.

"You don't know what it'll—"

"Noor," Odile interrupted, with the patience of a woman explaining astronomy to a particularly confident duck, "we are four people in a hole, walking toward a court that buys souls, pursued by nothing yet but certainly soon, carrying one prince, one disgraced drunk, one illegal mage, and me. At this point 'it might go wrong' is not an argument. It's a weather report. Open the doors, smith."

Sable laughed—one real laugh, surprised out of him, the first in longer than he could count—and turned back to the wall. He set both hands against the glowing seam. Closed his eyes. Reached for the old language, the one written in tension and geometry and trust, the one he'd sworn off along with sobriety and self-respect.

The wards knew him instantly. Of course they did. He'd left his name all over them.

*Countersign,* they asked, in the wordless grammar of his craft.

*Sable Ives,* he answered. *Formerly of the Royal Guild, currently of nowhere, opening by request of—* he hesitated, and then, because the day had apparently decided to make him honest whether he liked it or not— *by request of Wren Nakamura's family.*

The seam blazed white.

Every line in the gallery lit at once—not the cold probing glow of a thing searching, but something else, something older, and for one heartbeat the whole tunnel rang like a struck bell, and Sable felt, impossibly, a warmth move through the dead network. Faint. Flickering. Like a hearth-blessing remembered by stone that had been told for twenty years to forget it.

Then the passage ahead unsealed with a groan of shifting rock, and the light settled into a steady, even glow, lighting their way down.

Nobody spoke for a moment.

"Well," said Aurel finally. "Either that worked beautifully, or we've just announced ourselves to an entire subterranean civilization."

"Both, probably," Sable said hoarsely.

Noor was staring at the lit corridor, and their face—for once, for one unguarded second—wasn't the taster's face at all. It was fifteen years old and standing in a crowd.

"She's down there," Noor said. Not to anyone. To the light.

And far below them, deeper than any surveyor had ever mapped, something vast turned its attention upward through the veins of the mountain, and the walls began, very softly, to sing.
