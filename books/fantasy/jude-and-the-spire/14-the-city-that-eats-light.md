## 14. The City That Eats Light

Odile Ferrant had spent eleven years learning to read darkness. She had charts of it—forty-one of them, inked in her own hand, each one a portrait of the nothing where the stars used to be. She had learned its textures the way a blind woman learns a room: the flat black of clouded night, the velvet black of deep sky, the hungry black that sat over Vespergate's walls since the wards fell and seemed, if you watched it long enough through her cracked telescope, to *lean*.

So she was, she told herself as they climbed the last stretch of mine shaft, uniquely prepared for whatever waited below.

She was not prepared.

The shaft ended at a lip of broken stone, and beyond it the mountain opened like a mouth, and inside the mouth was a city.

"Careful," Sable whispered behind her, which was absurd, because none of them could have spoken above a whisper anyway. Sound behaved strangely in a space that large—it went out of them and never came back, swallowed whole.

Odile gripped the edge of the rock and looked down into the cavern city of the mountain court, and every instrument of her mind, every careful grid she'd built over eleven years of grief, simply laid itself down and stopped working.

It was lit.

That was the first thing. Not torchlit, not lamplit, not the sullen orange of forge-fire. The whole vast bowl of the cavern—terraces cut into living rock, bridges arcing over an underground river, spires of pale stone rising toward a ceiling so high it might have been sky—glowed with a light she had no name for. It was silver, but not moon-silver. It was gold, but not sun-gold. It shifted. It breathed. Whole districts of the city pulsed gently, dimming and brightening on rhythms that were almost—but not quite—the same, so that the light moved across the city in slow tides, like weather made of glow.

"It's beautiful," Noor said beside her, very quietly.

And then Odile looked closer, the way she looked at everything, because looking closely was the only prayer she knew, and the beauty curdled in her chest like milk.

The lights were caged.

* * *

They descended by way of a stair cut into the cavern wall, wide enough for processions, worn smooth by centuries of feet. Nobody challenged them. That frightened Odile more than wardens would have. They wore the rough grey cloaks of tribute-bearers—Sable had produced them from somewhere, along with a story about a tribute caravan delayed by the collapsed border, and if his story was a lie it was at least a well-tailored one—and the people of the city glanced at them and looked away, the way people look at servants everywhere: without seeing them at all.

But Odile saw.

She saw the lanterns first on a terrace overlooking a market square. They hung from iron brackets in rows of twelve, and each one was a sphere of glass or something like glass, and inside each sphere burned a point of light. Not flame. Points of light that did not flicker, that held their shape with the terrible steadiness of things that had held their shape for a very long time against their will.

She stopped walking.

Noor bumped into her back. "Odile?"

"That one." She pointed, and her hand was not steady, and she hated that her hand was not steady. "Third bracket from the left. The blue-white one."

Noor squinted. "It's a lamp."

"It is not a lamp." Her voice came out strange, flattened, the voice she used when a calculation came out wrong three times in a row and she knew, she *knew*, the error was in the world and not in her arithmetic. "I have spent eleven years charting the positions of two hundred and six stars. I know their colors the way you know your sister's handwriting. That is Alcor."

Silence. The market murmured below them—a real market, vendors calling out prices for mushrooms grown in the dark and fish from the underground river, children chasing each other between stalls, an ordinary Tuesday in an impossible place—and above the stalls hung twelve small prisoners.

"The star?" Aurel said. He had gone very pale, which under soul-light made him look carved from tallow. "You're telling me that's an actual—"

"I'm telling you that on the night the sky went dark, I recorded the exact moment each star vanished. I thought it was an eclipse. I thought—" She laughed, one dry note with no humor in it at all. "Eleven years ago I wrote a paper proposing that the stars were being occulted by a body of perfectly non-reflective material passing between us and the celestial sphere. My colleagues called it the work of a grieving mind. My colleagues were right about the grieving and wrong about everything else." She turned back to the lanterns. "They weren't occulted. They were *collected*."

Down in the square, a vendor reached up with a long pole and turned one of the lanterns, adjusting its angle over her stall, the way any shopkeeper adjusts any lamp, and Odile's stomach turned over.

"They're dimmer," she said. "All of them. Alcor should be brighter than that. Mizar's companion has been dimming for centuries in the old records—I assumed it was atmospheric extinction, bad data, but—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eye. "They're being *used*. Whatever this city runs on, it runs on them."

"And the rest of the light?" Noor asked softly. "The terraces. The tides of it. That's not stars."

"No." Odile looked up at the great slow breathing glow that filled the cavern, silver and gold and one district that burned a deep, unsettling rose. "That's something else. Something bigger." She didn't say what she suspected. She wasn't ready to say it, even to herself. Some hypotheses you carry like a stone in your boot, feeling it with every step, refusing to take it out and look at it until you absolutely must.

* * *

They crossed the city in ones and twos, as Sable directed—never all four together, never lingering. Odile walked with Noor, because Noor was the steadiest of them and also, Odile had come to realize, the most frightened, and those two qualities apparently lived in the same person quite comfortably.

"You're cataloguing," Noor said, after a while.

"I'm always cataloguing."

"No, I mean I can see it. Your eyes keep doing a little jump every time we pass a lantern. Left to right, left to right. You're counting."

Odile huffed. "Am I so transparent?"

"To me? Yes. You have exactly one face and it says 'currently observing.'"

"That's slander. I have at least three faces. Currently observing, currently hungry, and currently deciding whether to say the thing everyone will hate me for saying."

"And which one is on duty now?"

Odile didn't answer immediately. They had paused beneath an aqueduct, and above them, strung along its spine like beads on a wire, hung forty lanterns. Forty points of patient, captive light. She counted them anyway, though she already knew.

"Two hundred and six," she said quietly. "There are two hundred and six classical stars in the navigational canon. I've found sixty-one lanterns so far. If the rest are here—if they're distributed through the city the way lamps would be distributed—then someone went up into the sky and took down the entire heavens to light their hallways." She wrapped her arms around herself. "Do you understand what that means? Those aren't fires. A fire doesn't care where it burns. Stars are *places*, Noor. Each one is a sun, or the corpse of one, or the cradle of one. Someone looked at the oldest, largest things in existence and thought, *those would look nice over my dinner table.*"

Noor was quiet for a moment. Below them, the rose-lit district pulsed once, slowly, like something exhaling.

"When Wren died," Noor said, "I stopped noticing beautiful things. For years. It felt like a betrayal to enjoy anything." They nodded up at the aqueduct. "I think I understand what you're feeling. It's not just anger. It's that the beauty is being used as an argument. As if the mountain is saying, *look how lovely it all is, surely nothing done this beautifully can be wrong.*"

"Yes." Odile blinked at them. "Yes, exactly that. Has anyone ever told you that you're wasted on food?"

"Frequently. Usually right before I taste it for them." But there was a ghost of warmth in it, and Odile clung to that, because the alternative was to stand under forty stolen suns and weep, and she had a job to do.

Her job, she reminded herself. She hadn't joined this mad expedition to rescue souls—though she'd come to care about Wren Nakamura with a fierceness that surprised her, for a woman she'd never met. She'd joined it because she was the astronomer without a sky, and if there was a sky to be had back, however many miles underground, then by every god ever misnamed, she was going to be the one who mapped the way to it.

* * *

The observatory, when they finally found it—or rather, when Odile finally admitted that the enormous domed structure at the cavern's northern apex was, in fact, an observatory—nearly finished her.

They had split from the others an hour before; Sable and Aurel had gone to find the tribute-registers, hunting for Wren's name, while Noor and Odile worked their way north on the theory that whoever kept stars in cages would want to study them. The dome rose from a plateau of rock like a stone skull, and its aperture stood open, and Odile climbed the outer stair with her heart hammering in a way she pretended was scientific excitement.

Inside: instruments. Real ones. A great refractor on an equatorial mount, brass green with age but lovingly maintained. Armillary spheres. Star charts covering the walls—not the cavern's walls, but the *sky's*, the true sky, the missing sky, rendered in exquisite detail and annotated in a cramped, precise hand.

And in the center of the floor, arranged in a spiral, sat ninety-one empty lantern-cages, their doors hanging open, their interiors scorched faintly silver.

"Oh," Odile said. It was all she could manage.

"Odile?" Noor, at the door, keeping watch. "What is it?"

She crossed the floor slowly, reading the annotations on the wall charts. Dates. Measurements. Brightness estimates, tracked over decades, over *centuries*—the oldest charts were on vellum, the newest on paper, and the same cramped hand appeared in both, which meant either a very long-lived astronomer or a dynasty of them, generation after generation, standing in this room measuring the slow starvation of stolen stars.

"They're studying them," she said. "They took the sky apart and then they studied it, like—like vivisectionists. Like collectors pinning butterflies and then writing papers about how the wings fade." Her finger traced a column of figures. "Look. Brightness decline, four percent per century, consistent across all specimens. They *know* the stars are dying in here. They've known for three hundred years. And they kept taking more."

"Maybe they don't know how to stop," Noor offered. "Maybe the bargain needs feeding."

"Then they should have starved." The words came out of her hot and fast, and she let them. "Forgive me, but I am so tired of enormous cruelty being excused as necessity. Necessity is what you call it when you're the one holding the net."

She turned in the center of the room, and the great telescope loomed over her, aimed through the open aperture at the cavern ceiling—at the false dark where a sky should have been—and she understood, suddenly, what this room was *for*. Not astronomy. Astronomy was looking outward, upward, toward what you could never reach. This was inventory. This was a counting-house with a telescope in it.

And yet.

Her hands found the eyepiece before she decided to touch it. Old habit. Old love. She put her eye to the glass, knowing there was nothing up there but rock, and—

Something moved across the field of view.

She jerked back, then bent again, heart slamming. The aperture framed a slice of the distant ceiling, and across that slice, at the very limit of the glass's reach, something vast and slow had passed. Darker than the dark. A shape with edges, the way a whale has edges beneath black water.

"Odile." Noor's voice, sharp now. "Your face. What did you see?"

"I don't—" She straightened, and her legs felt odd, and she realized she'd been holding her breath. "There's something on the ceiling. Something big. Moving." She looked at the wall charts again, at the annotations she'd taken for observations of the captured stars, and now noticed the other notes, the ones in the margins, the ones written smaller, tighter, the handwriting of someone afraid: *it circled again tonight. it is getting closer to the lantern districts. we do not know what it eats.*

"Noor," she said slowly, "the darkness over Vespergate. The one I've been charting for eleven years. The one that leans."

"What about it?"

"I think it's not a void. I think it's not the absence of the stars." She swallowed. "I think it's the thing that ate them. And I think it followed them home."

Above them, far off across the cavern, the rose-lit district pulsed—once, twice—and this time the pulse stuttered, like a heartbeat catching, and every lantern in the observatory dimmed in sympathy, and somewhere out in the breathing dark of the mountain court, something enormous turned over in its sleep.

Or woke up.

Noor was already moving for the door. "We need to find the others. Now."

"One moment." Odile pulled her battered notebook from her coat, opened it to a fresh page, and wrote, in her own cramped and precise hand, the first honest observation anyone had made in this room in three hundred years:

*Day one. The sky is underground. So is the wolf.*

Then she ran after Noor, down the stair, into the beautiful, terrible light.
