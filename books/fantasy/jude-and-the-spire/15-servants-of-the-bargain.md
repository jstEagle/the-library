## 15. Servants of the Bargain

The robes were Odile's idea.

"They'll never look at your faces," she said, shaking out the garments she'd found folded in a dead astronomer's locker at the observatory—long coats the color of wet slate, hoods that shadowed everything but the mouth, and collars stitched with a symbol none of them recognized: a circle bisected by a descending line. "Tribute-bearers are furniture here. Furniture with legs. Nobody interrogates a chair."

"Speak for yourself," Sable muttered, wrestling his broad shoulders into a coat cut for someone considerably narrower. A seam gave up with a small tragic sound. "I've been interrogated by actual chairs. The warden-cells in Vespergate have opinions."

Noor adjusted their hood and said nothing. The coat fit them well enough, which felt like an omen of some kind, though whether good or ill they couldn't say. Twenty years of being unremarkable had taught them that clothes which fit were either luck or a trap.

Aurel, meanwhile, looked *wrong* in the disguise—not because it didn't suit him, but because it suited him too well. Stripped of the silver circlet and the palace-cut jackets, he was just a tall young man with tired eyes and ink stains on his fingers, and Noor kept catching themselves forgetting he was the prince. He noticed them noticing.

"I had a phase," he offered, as they gathered at the mouth of the last mine shaft before the cavern city proper, "at fourteen, when I tried to run away and live as a commoner. I made it four hours. I bought a meat pie and paid with a gold sovereign and the pie-seller wept."

"What happened?" Odile asked.

"He insisted I take the whole tray. I ate eleven pies. It was the worst day of my life until approximately three weeks ago." He rolled his shoulders, settling the slate-colored cloth like a man putting on someone else's skin. "I'm better at it now. Being nobody. Practice."

There was something in his voice Noor chose not to examine too closely—the particular flatness of a person who has recently learned his whole inheritance is built on graves. They'd heard that flatness before. In their own voice, mostly.

* * *

The shaft opened onto the city like a throat.

Noor had imagined the mountain court from Wren's old stories—a place of echoing halls and cold thrones—but nothing in those stories prepared them for the light. The cavern was vast enough to hold weather; mist drifted near its ceiling where no ceiling should be, and the glow came from everywhere and nowhere, a warm amber radiance that seemed to seep from the stone itself. Streets wound down the cavern walls in terraces, lit by lanterns of every size and shape, and the lanterns—

The lanterns were wrong. Noor's eyes kept sliding off them the way a tongue finds a broken tooth. Each one burned with a color that didn't sit still: gold going green going violet going gold, flickering at rhythms too irregular for flame and too alive for glass.

Odile stopped dead on the terrace road and made a small wounded noise.

"That's—" She gripped Noor's sleeve through the slate robe. "That's *Procyon*. That's—no. No, that can't be, the color signature—" Her breath went ragged. "They're not lanterns. Noor. *Noor.* Those are stars."

Noor had known, in the abstract way one knows anything these days. But knowing and seeing were different animals, and seeing was worse. A star the size of a cathedral hung caged in iron filigree above a wine-shop door, dimming obediently whenever the shopkeeper pulled a cord, brightening when he let it go. It flickered, just slightly, in a pattern that might have been struggling.

"Twenty years," Aurel said quietly. "My whole life, the sky's been getting emptier, and everyone said it was weather. It was *furniture*."

"Don't stare," Sable said, low and urgent, herding them forward with his bulk. "Furniture doesn't gawk at furniture. Eyes forward. Walk like you're bored. Boredom is the great camouflage—I once stood in a guard post for two hours holding a mop and nobody questioned me, because who questions a bored man with a mop?"

So they walked, four bored pieces of furniture in slate robes, down into the amber heart of the mountain court.

* * *

The tribute-bearers' quarter revealed itself to be exactly what Odile promised: a place where no one looked. A long stone hall near the base of the central spire—*the Spire*, Noor thought, and something in their chest twisted, because of course, of course the book of their own life would have a spire in it—lined with narrow bunks and long tables, filled with dozens of figures in identical slate robes. Most were human. Some were not. One had too many joints in its fingers. Another cast two shadows under the amber light and neither of them matched its movements quite on time.

Nobody remarked on any of this. That was somehow the most frightening part.

They learned the rhythm of the place by imitation, which was how Noor had learned everything worth knowing. Meals appeared at fixed hours—strange food, mushroom bread and pale broth and fruit Noor didn't recognize, and Noor tasted everything first with the tiny automatic ritual of twenty years, tongue against lip, waiting. Nothing bit back. The food here wasn't poisoned; it was merely sad, which Noor supposed was its own kind of mercy.

The work was simple. Tribute-bearers carried things. Casks of dark wine up the terraces. Bundles of iron chain down. Once, a covered litter whose occupant breathed in a way Noor chose very firmly not to think about. The other bearers spoke little, and when they spoke it was in a dialect of Vespergate's trade-tongue worn smooth and strange, like river stones. From them, in fragments, over two days, the shape of the city assembled itself.

"It's a court," Aurel murmured on the second night, lying on his bunk with his hands laced behind his head, staring at the cavern ceiling as if he could read it. "Not a kingdom. Listen to them—the ministers, the heralds, the *contracts*. Everything here is contractual. My father's court pretends to be a family. This one doesn't pretend."

"You've been listening to ministers already?" Odile whispered from the next bunk. "We've been here two days."

"I carried wine past a conference room six times. You'd be amazed what people say in front of furniture." He turned his head toward Noor's bunk. His eyes in the amber light were almost kind. "You'd know. You did it for years."

Noor didn't answer. Sleep came slowly, and when it came, it smelled like the charm shop, like cedar and ash, like a roof drumming with rain.

* * *

It was the third bearer—the one with too many finger-joints, whose name was Hesh and whose voice was dry as old paper—who finally explained the law.

They were hauling chain down the seventh terrace, Hesh walking beside Noor with the peculiar rolling gait of someone whose knees bent in more directions than knees should. Below them, the Spire rose out of the cavern floor like a needle of black glass, and around its base, in a plaza of polished stone, a crowd had gathered. A ceremony of some kind. Drums, deep and slow, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

"Tribute day," Hesh said, catching Noor looking. "You're lucky. Most bearers wait a season to see a Reclamation."

"A Reclamation," Noor repeated carefully.

Hesh's many-jointed fingers counted off the words like coins. "Every soul sold to the Court is entered in the Ledger. And every entry carries a right, written into the original Bargain—older than the ministers, older than the Spire. Any soul in the Ledger may be reclaimed." The drums boomed below. "By one willing to stake a soul of equal weight."

The words went into Noor like a key into a lock they hadn't known was there.

"Equal weight," Noor said. Their voice sounded far away, like someone else speaking through a wall.

"Hm? Oh—it's not cruelty, whatever the surface folk say. It's arithmetic. The Court runs on soul-fire; every lantern, every ward, every warm stone of this city drinks from the Ledger. Take a soul out, you must promise one of the same measure to replace it. Weight, not blood. Not love." Hesh shrugged, a gesture that traveled oddly through their extra joints. "The Ledger doesn't care whom you love. Only what you weigh."

Below, the drums stopped. A figure in white stood at the base of the Spire—an old woman, straight-backed, alone—and her voice carried up the terraces with unnatural clarity, the way sound carries in cathedrals:

"I stake myself. Weigh me against my husband, and if I am lighter, let the Bargain take the difference from my line."

The crowd murmured. Somewhere a bell rang, or something like a bell. And then—Noor saw it clearly, and would see it for the rest of their life—a lantern at the Spire's summit flared bright, brighter, blinding, and a second lantern beside it guttered and went out, and the old woman in white swayed as if a great wind had passed through her, and steadied, and wept without any change in her posture at all.

Reclaimed. Paid for. Done.

The crowd dispersed with the mild efficiency of people who had seen it before. The bearers picked up their chains.

Noor did not pick up their chains.

"Noor." Sable's hand landed on their shoulder, heavy through the slate cloth. "Pick up the chain."

"Wren is in the Ledger." The words came out level. Noor was distantly proud of that. "Wren has been in the Ledger for twenty years. And all this time, all this time, there was a *door*, and the door only needed—"

"Stop." Sable's grip tightened. "I know that tone. I heard that tone in a courtroom twenty years ago, right before you did something the whole gallery remembered for a decade. Pick up the chain, walk back to the bunk hall, and we *talk about it*—"

"There's nothing to talk about." Noor turned. Under the hood, Sable's face was gray with dread, and behind him Odile had gone still as a held breath, and Aurel was pushing through the bearers toward them with his jaw set in a way Noor recognized from the breakfast hall, from the plum, from every moment the prince decided something and could not be talked out of it. "Someone has to stake a soul of equal weight. Wren was—Wren weighed *everything*. Wren was the loudest, brightest, most impossible person in any room, and the Ledger knows it, and so the price will be—"

"Noor." Aurel arrived, breathless. "Whatever you're about to do—"

"—the price will be enormous. I know." And here was the strange thing, the thing Noor would turn over in their mind for years afterward, examining it like a charm that wouldn't work: there was no decision. There was no moment of choice, no scale tipping, no angel and devil arguing on the shoulder. There was only Wren's name in a ledger, and a door, and their own hand reaching for the handle the way a hand reaches for a falling child—before thought, before fear, before anything except the oldest fact in Noor's body, older than the immunity, older than the tasting, older than the silence: *my sister, my sister, my sister.*

"I stake myself," Noor said.

Not loudly. Not dramatically. Just clearly, in the trade-tongue of the bearers, in front of Hesh and forty other tribute-bearers and the open air of the plaza where ceremonies happened—and the words left them the way a bird leaves a hand, immediately beyond recall.

The effect was instant and terrible. Every jointed head in the bearing-line turned. The two-shadowed bearer stopped casting both shadows for a heartbeat, as if even its shadows were listening. Somewhere in the crowd below, a bell—or something like a bell—rang once, softly, the way a ledger page turns.

"Oh, you *idiot*," Sable breathed. "Oh, you magnificent, catastrophic—"

"The claim must be entered," said a voice.

It came from everywhere. From the stone, maybe, or the amber light, or the drums that had begun again, slow and patient, like a heart deciding whether to keep beating. The bearers sank to their knees—all of them, Hesh included, folding down with the smooth obedience of long practice—and Noor, who had spent twenty years perfecting the art of being beneath notice, found themselves suddenly, catastrophically *noticed*, standing upright in a field of bowed backs.

At the edge of the plaza, where the Spire's black glass met the polished stone, the light gathered. It drew itself upward into a shape—tall, robed in something that was not fabric but writing, thousands of lines of script flowing across its form like water finding its level. Where a face should have been there was a page, blank, waiting.

The Ledger Keeper.

Behind Noor, Aurel caught their arm. Odile made a sound like a teakettle taken off heat too late. Sable said, very quietly, in the tone of a man watching a second courtroom begin: "Don't sign anything."

But Noor's heart was doing something strange and calm, the way it did in the seconds after swallowing poison, when the die was cast and all that remained was to be steady while the world rearranged itself.

"I stake myself," Noor said again, to the page-faced thing, "for the soul of Wren Nakamura. Entered twenty years ago. Weigh us."

The blank face tilted, considering. Somewhere in its flowing script, Noor thought they saw a name move past—just a flicker, a familiar curve of letters—and their breath caught, because it had been twenty years since anyone wrote Wren's name anywhere, and here it was, alive, inked into the bones of a monster.

"Then come," said the Ledger Keeper, in a voice like a pen scratching across vellum, "and be *weighed*."

It turned, and the doors of the Spire opened for it—black glass parting like a curtain of night—and Noor stepped forward before anyone could stop them, which, they understood dimly, was the entire problem, and also the entire point.

Behind them, four voices started arguing at once.
