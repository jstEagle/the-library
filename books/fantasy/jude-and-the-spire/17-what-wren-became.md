## 17. What Wren Became

The lantern-vaults smelled of cold metal and something sweeter underneath, like honey left too long in a copper pot. Noor had catalogued a thousand smells in twenty years of tasting—the almond whisper of bittercyan, the green bite of foxglove, the mushroom-earth of the sleeping draughts—and this one sat wrong against all of them. Sweetness that had no business being sweet. Light that had no business being light.

They walked between the racks.

That was the only word for it: *racks*. Rows upon rows of iron frames rising toward a ceiling lost in shadow, and in each frame, a cage of black glass, and in each cage, a flame that was not fire. Soul-fire burned differently than anything born of wick or oil. It did not flicker. It *breathed*. In and out, slow as a sleeper, and every breath threw a little wash of color across the vault floor—gold, blue, a bruised violet that made Noor's teeth ache to look at.

*These are people.* The thought arrived without drama, the way all the worst thoughts did. These are people, and I am walking through their bedrooms.

A attendant-servant—Noor had stopped trying to classify what exactly the pale robed figures were sometime around the second hour—glided ahead, gesturing at the racks with a hand like folded paper. "Tribute-bearers inspect the western rows," it said. Its voice was pleasant the way a locked door is pleasant. "Do not touch the glass. Do not speak to the flames. The flames do not answer."

"The flames don't answer," Noor repeated, because repetition was how they survived everything. Say the rule twice, mean it never.

"Flames that answer have been *handled*. You will know them by the quiet."

And it glided on, leaving Noor alone in a cathedral of stolen light with instructions not to talk to anybody.

Noor pressed a palm flat against their own sternum, feeling for the heartbeat beneath, steady and stubborn. Twenty years of mornings spent putting small objects in their mouth and waiting to see if they would kill him, and here at last was the thing all those mornings had been for. Somewhere in these rows. Somewhere behind one of these panes of glass.

Wren.

They had imagined this moment so many times that the reality of it felt almost rude. In the imaginings, Wren turned around. In the imaginings there was always recognition—a gasp, a name, arms. Twenty years of rehearsing a reunion does something strange to a person; you begin to believe the rehearsal is a promise.

Noor began at the first rack and read.

Each cage bore a small brass plate, and each plate a name—not the soul's true name, Noor realized after a few rows, but a *lot number*, a date, a weight. *Acquired: spring thaw. Weight: moderate. Temperament: restless.* As if souls were tea. As if people were measured in spoonfuls.

Row by row. Rack by rack. Noor kept their face arranged in the mild blankness of an inspector, the expression they'd worn through a hundred poisoned breakfasts, and underneath it they were saying the name over and over like a prayer said backwards. *Wren. Wren. Wren.*

The vault went on much further than it should have. That was the other thing about the mountain court—its insides didn't agree with its outsides. Corridors doubled back on themselves politely, like servants pretending not to watch you get lost. Twice Noor passed the same cage with the restless temperament and the moderate weight, and both times its little flame leaned toward them as they passed, and both times Noor's chest did something complicated and stupid, and both times they kept walking.

On the third pass of the restless soul, Noor stopped.

It wasn't Wren. They knew that immediately—knew it the way a taster knows bitterness from poison, instantly and below thought. But the flame had leaned. And Noor was so tired, and so far from home, and so afraid that leaning toward a stranger might be the closest thing to family they got in this place.

"I can't take you," they said softly. "I'm sorry. I'm here for someone else."

The flame pulsed once, gold going briefly white, and Noor had to press the back of their hand to their mouth and count to ten before moving on.

*Do not speak to the flames.*

Too late. Too late by about twenty years.

* * *

They found her in the eastern rows, where the cages were older and the brass plates had gone green at the edges, in a rack near a wall that wept a thin constant trickle of mineral water. The mountain's own moisture, seeping through stone that had forgotten it was ever sky.

The lot plate read: *Acquired: the year of the border frost. Weight: slight. Temperament: singing.*

Noor's knees nearly went.

Because that was right. That was exactly, unbearably right. Wren had been slight—small-boned, quick, always half a step ahead of trouble. And she had sung. Constantly, tunelessly, gloriously. Songs she invented on the spot about bread prices and neighbor disputes and the specific misery of darning socks. Their father used to say Wren would sing at her own funeral, and he'd meant it fondly, and then—

Then the trial. Then the sentence. Then the year of the border frost, which Noor now understood had not been a weather report but a *transaction date*.

"Wren," Noor whispered.

The flame behind the glass was dimmer than the others. That was the first wound. All around it, the younger souls burned bright and breathing, but this one guttered low, a candle in a draft, its light coming and coming and coming in a rhythm that stuttered. Fragmented. Like a song with bars missing. Like a person who has been asked the same question ten thousand times until the answer wore smooth and hollow.

"Wren, it's me."

Nothing.

Noor crouched down to bring their eyes level with the glass. Up close, the soul-fire resolved into something almost like a figure—a suggestion of shoulders, a tilt of head, the ghost of a posture Noor had known across a shared bedroom for fifteen years. But the edges of it frayed. Pieces of it drifted loose and reattached wrong, a shoulder drifting up where a hand should be, light pooling and scattering like sediment stirred in water.

"It's Noor," they said. Steadier now. Practiced steadiness, the kind that came free with every dish they hadn't died of. "You used to call me—" 

*Jude.* The childhood name. The shop-roof name, the rain-on-tin name, the name that existed nowhere in any record of Vespergate except inside two people's heads, one of whom had been dead for twenty years.

The flame did not lean. The flame did not flicker in recognition. The flame breathed its broken rhythm and threw its dim, bruised light on Noor's face, and behind Noor's face nothing moved at all.

"You used to call me Jude," Noor finished anyway, voice cracking on the last syllable like old rope. "And I called you Ren. Because you hated Wrennie. You bit the last auntie who called you Wrennie. You drew blood. Mama said you'd go far in life."

The soul burned on, indifferent as a lamp.

Noor stayed crouched there a long time. Long enough for their knees to ache, long enough for the mineral trickle on the wall to carve some new microscopic path through the stone, long enough that somewhere deeper in the vaults a bell rang the change of shift and neither of them noticed.

Here is what no one tells you about hope: it is not killed by failure. Failure is easy; failure is clean. Hope is killed by *almost*. By the flame that leans when it isn't your sister. By the posture that's almost right. By a plate reading *temperament: singing* in a dead clerk's handwriting while the actual singing—that endless, ridiculous, alive noise of her—has clearly been worn away like lettering on a well-used coin.

"They told me I could reclaim you," Noor said quietly. "There's a bargain. A stake. I've already—" They laughed, a small ugly sound. "I volunteered before I knew the terms. Which you'd have laughed at. You always said I read contracts twice and still signed the bad ones. Remember the goose? We signed the goose contract. Papa was furious."

The flame guttered. Once. Maybe a draught from the wall-seep. Maybe nothing.

Noor pressed their forehead gently against the glass. Cold, of course. Everything here was cold except the light, and even the light gave no heat—it just *showed* you warmth, the way a portrait shows you a face.

"I'm going to get you out," they said. "I don't care what it costs. I've been paying toward this my whole life, you just didn't get the invoices."

And then—because the servant had said the flames do not answer, and because Noor Nakamura had built a whole life on doing precisely what they were told not to—they began to hum.

It was an old tune. Shop-roof music, rain-rhythm under it, the melody Wren had invented one stormy autumn and refused ever to write down, insisting that songs written down got *tired*. Noor's humming was poor. Twenty years of tasting had given them a fine tongue and no voice whatsoever. They hummed it badly, off-key, missing bars, filling the gaps with nonsense the way Wren used to fill them with words about bread.

The flame shuddered.

Not the draught-shudder. Something else. Something that started deep in the fragmented core of it and rippled outward, and one drifting piece of light—one small, stray, shoulder-shaped piece—drifted *down* instead of up, and hung there, trembling, at the level of a listening ear.

Noor's heart slammed against their ribs hard enough to hurt.

"Ren?"

The piece of light pulsed. Faint. Arrhythmic. Not recognition—no, be honest, Noor, it isn't recognition, it's a body remembering a rhythm the way a severed hand sometimes remembers itching. Muscle-memory of a soul. Echo without an owner.

But it was *something*. First movement in twenty years that wasn't the contract's rhythm. First sign that somewhere in the worn-smooth hollow of what the mountain had bought, a groove remained that matched a shape Noor knew.

"I'm coming back," Noor said. Their voice came out wrecked and they let it. "Tomorrow. Next day. However the bargain runs. I'm coming back with a way to tear the whole rotten page open, and when I do, you're going to walk out of this glass and complain about my humming, and I am going to let you."

Behind them, soft as folded paper, a voice said:

"Tribute-bearers do not sing to inventory."

Noor rose slowly and turned. The Ledger Keeper stood at the end of the row—or rather, the shape of stitched contracts that served as the Ledger Keeper stood there, pages rustling faintly in a windless room, and though it had no face that Noor could find, Noor had the distinct, skin-crawling certainty that it was looking at the cage. At the dim flame. At the fragment of light still trembling at a listening angle.

"Interesting," the Ledger Keeper said, in a voice like a seal pressing into wax. "This lot has not moved in nineteen years."

Noor put their inspector's face back on, the practiced calm, the mild blankness—but underneath it, a new fear was already taking root, cold and precise: *it noticed. Whatever I just woke, the mountain noticed it too.*

"She's restless," Noor said. "Temperament's listed right on the plate."

"Mm," said the Ledger Keeper, which was somehow the most frightening sound Noor had heard in twenty years of listening for poisons. It drifted closer, pages whispering. "Come. There is paperwork regarding your stake. And taster—you should know that dawn is closer than it appears underground."

It turned, and Noor followed, and at the corner of the row Noor allowed themselves one look back—at the dim flame, the listening fragment, the sister-shaped light that had not recognized them but had, for three heartbeats, remembered a song.

*I'll teach it to you again,* Noor promised silently. *Every bar. Even the ones about bread.*

Somewhere above them, far beyond the mountain's stone, the second day was ending.
