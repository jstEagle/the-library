## 18. Twenty Years of Immunity

The lantern-vaults were quiet in the way that graves are quiet — not silent, but *attending*. Noor sat on a crate of salt-iron beside the dim soul that had been their sister for twenty years and told her a story about rain.

Wren didn't look up. Her hands moved through the motions of tending an empty lantern, fingers passing through the glass as though it were still there, still lit, still hers to keep. Whatever work she had done down here had worn grooves into her so deep that even freed of the task, the task remained.

"Rain," Noor said again, patiently. "You used to hate it. You said it made the shop smell like wet wool and regret."

Nothing. The soul-fire behind Wren's eyes guttered at some private wind.

Noor's mouth kept smiling because that was what you did with dying things and small children and sisters who couldn't hear you. Inside, something old and patient began counting.

*Twenty years,* it said. *I spent twenty years getting ready for this. So start spending.*

* * *

The first poison had been hemlock water, taken on the night of Wren's burning, in the back room of a shop that smelled like wet wool and regret.

Noor remembered it with terrible clarity: nineteen years old, sitting cross-legged on the floor among Wren's confiscated things — the ones the wardens hadn't bothered to take because they looked like nothing. A luck-knot half-finished. A jar of buttons. A recipe book written in their mother's hand, which was really a book about herbs, which was really — everyone had agreed at trial — evidence.

Wren had been executed for selling charms. Small ones. Harmless ones. A knot for luck. A sachet against nightmares. The kind of magic that wouldn't hurt a fly, sold to people who needed it anyway, because Vespergate was the kind of city where a lamplighter might pay his last coin for the hope of one more year of light.

And the court beneath the mountain wanted souls instead, but nobody knew that yet. All anyone knew was that Wren Nakamura had stood in the square and been very small and very brave and had not cried, and Noor had watched from the crowd with their teeth shut so hard they ached for a week.

That night, Noor mixed hemlock water — weak, insultingly weak, a dose that would make a healthy man queasy — and drank it looking at their sister's unfinished luck-knot.

It tasted like green and endings. They vomited into the herb sink. Then they wrote the date in the margin of the recipe book, next to their mother's handwriting, and made a note: *Day one. Again tomorrow.*

They did it again the next day. And the day after. And for seven thousand mornings after that.

* * *

"You're doing it again," Sable Ives had told them once, years later, drunk in the doorway of the night market stall. "That thing with your face."

"What thing?"

"The ledger thing. Like you're adding up a bill nobody else can see." He'd waved his cup at them, sloshing. "You know most people survive by accident? You've turned surviving into a trade."

"It is a trade," Noor had said. "I'm a food-taster."

"That's a job. What you do is a religion." He'd squinted at them with red-rimmed eyes. "What are you preparing *for*, Nakamura?"

At the time, Noor had said: *For Tuesday.* And he'd laughed, because Sable laughed easily when he drank, and neither of them had known that the true answer was: *For the day I walk into the dark and ask for my sister back.*

* * *

In the vault, Noor shifted on the crate and kept talking, because talking was a kind of tending too.

"There was a woman in the market," they told Wren's dim shape, "who bought a charm for her son's cough. It was just willow bark and a good word, but she paid me double because I tied it with blue string. She thought the string was the magic. Maybe she was right."

Wren's hands kept moving through the ghost-lantern. But her head tilted. One degree. Perhaps two.

Noor's heart did something reckless and hopeful, and they disciplined it the way you discipline a first-year apprentice: firmly, without cruelty. *Not yet. Keep pouring.*

* * *

Here is how you build an immunity, if you are fool enough and stubborn enough and grieving enough to try.

You begin with the gentle poisons — the ones that only bruise. Bitterleaf, which turns your tongue numb for a day. Sleepshade, which gives you dreams with teeth. You take a dose so small it's almost an apology, and then you wait, and then you take a slightly larger one, and you write everything down: date, dose, symptoms, recovery time.

You learn that your body keeps books too. That it remembers each insult and, grudgingly, files it under *known*. That immunity is not armor. It is a library, built one painful volume at a time.

By the second year, Noor could drink bitterleaf tea straight from the pot and feel only a pleasant numbness, like a kiss from someone they didn't trust. By the fourth, they had survived foxglove twice and spent three days seeing halos around every light in Vespergate, including lights that weren't there.

By the fifth, they were ready for the hard shelves of the library. And that was the year they got the position at the palace — not because they applied, but because a food-taster died. Poisoned, ironically, by something mundane: bad shellfish. The palace needed a replacement who could start immediately and ask no questions, and there was Noor, already immune to eleven of the fourteen classic poisons, already practiced at eating calmly while their stomach staged a riot.

The interview had consisted of a single question from a bored steward: "Can you detect arsenic?"

"No," Noor had said truthfully, "but I can survive it."

They were hired before the steward finished his wine.

* * *

There was no single morning when the preparation became deliberate rather than desperate. It happened the way a river becomes a canyon — gradually, and then all at once, and then it was simply the shape of your life.

But there was one evening Noor returned to, often, in memory. The third anniversary of Wren's burning. They had gone to the market square out of some masochistic instinct and found it full of ordinary life — fishwives arguing, children playing knucklebones against the plinth where the scaffold once stood. Nobody remembered. The city had digested their sister and moved on.

Noor had stood there in the crowd, invisible, useful, unremarkable — all the things they had trained themselves to be — and felt the full horror of their own competence.

*I have gotten so good at living without her,* they'd thought, *that I could do it forever.*

And underneath that thought, quieter, in a voice that sounded uncomfortably like Wren's: *Then get good at something else.*

So they had gone home and opened the recipe book to the page their mother had annotated in the margins — the poisons section, naturally; their mother had been a practical woman — and begun planning in earnest. Not just survival anymore. Preparation. Because somewhere beneath the mountain, if the old stories were true, there was a court that dealt in souls, and if a person were ever mad enough to go knocking on its door, that person would need a body that could survive the knock.

You cannot fight the dark, their mother's marginal notes implied, between instructions for feverfew tincture. But you can be very difficult to kill.

* * *

The worst year was the ninth.

Noor had graduated to the venoms — the ones that didn't forgive dosing errors — and had miscalculated. Adder-scale extract, meant to be taken at three drops. They had miscounted. Or their hand had shaken. History, Noor felt, was entitled to its ambiguity.

What was not ambiguous was waking on the floor of the shop four hours later, unable to move anything but their eyes, watching a spider descend from the ceiling beam on a thread directly toward their face with what seemed, in the circumstances, like personal malice.

They lay there for six hours. The spider lost interest. Eventually feeling crawled back into their limbs like tenants returning after a fire, and Noor lay staring at the ceiling and did the arithmetic they always did afterward:

*Seven thousand doses planned. Two hundred and forty taken. Sixty-one to go before the body is ready for the last one.*

Because that was the secret arithmetic, the one they never wrote down, never spoke aloud, barely admitted to themselves: every dose had a destination. There was one poison — a slow venom, sipped in fragments over years until the whole of it lived coiled in the blood — that would let a living body pass through a soul-barrier. Their mother's notes called it *the ferryman's toll*. It took decades to accumulate safely. It would burn out every immunity they had built, all of it, permanently, the moment they took the full dose.

One crossing. One toll. One chance.

Noor had lain on the floor with their heartbeat stuttering and understood, finally and completely, that they were not building a life. They were building a key. And a key, by definition, exists to be spent.

They got up. They washed the cup. They wrote in the margin: *Day 3,287. Dose high by error. Survived. Note: count twice.*

* * *

"You're staring," said a voice in the present, and Noor nearly came off the crate.

Aurel stood at the end of the vault row, dressed in the grey of a tribute-bearer and wearing the particular expression of a prince who has just discovered that courtiers can smell fear the way sharks smell blood, and that he had bled a little today.

"I'm working," Noor said.

"On your sister."

"On my sister."

He came closer, moving carefully among the crates, and glanced at Wren's dim, industrious ghost-shape. Something crossed his face — not pity; Noor had learned to tell the difference, and pity would have made them close like a shop at curfew. It was recognition. His mother was somewhere in this mountain too. He knew exactly what it was to talk to someone who couldn't answer.

"How long?" he asked quietly. "Preparing, I mean. For all this."

Noor considered lying. Lying was a skill they maintained the way other people maintained instruments — daily scales, occasional performances. But the vault was quiet and honest in its grave-way, and Wren's hands passed through her empty lantern, and Noor found the lie wouldn't come up their throat.

"Since the night they burned her," they said. "Nineteen years old. Hemlock water, one sip, over the sink."

Aurel absorbed this. "Twenty years," he said. "You spent twenty years becoming a person who could stand here."

"And it cost—" Noor stopped. Recalculated. Told the truth, which was rarer than lying and heavier. "Everything I might have been instead. Every friendship I kept shallow because grief makes a poor conversation partner. Every year I ate dinner alone on purpose, because a man who eats alone asks no questions about why he never gets ill." They shrugged. "Survival isn't free, Your Highness. It's just that nobody itemizes the bill."

"No," Aurel said. "They do. My father did. He just sent the invoice to other people." He looked at Wren again. "Will she come back? Whole?"

"I don't know." Noor watched the dim soul tend her dim lantern. "She's worn down. Like a coin that's been through ten thousand hands. Still itself, but thin." They flexed their own hands, felt the library of poisons sleeping in their blood, sixty-one volumes thick, waiting for the final withdrawal. "I have something that might carry her out. If I use it, I can't ever build it again. Twenty years of work, spent in one night."

"That's a terrible bargain."

"Yes," Noor said warmly. "It's my favorite kind. The kind where you know the price going in."

Something flickered at the far end of the vault — lantern-light where no lantern burned, or perhaps the attention of something that kept accounts. Down the long row, one of the caged stars shivered in its glass, and the shadows of the shelving leaned, briefly, like reeds in current.

Aurel noticed it too. His jaw tightened. "The ministers are asking questions about the new tribute-bearers," he said low. "I bought us tonight. Possibly not tomorrow."

"Then tomorrow we move," Noor said, and reached out, and — gently, the way you touch a bird that may not be real — brushed Wren's cold wrist. "Hey. Rain. Wet wool and regret. Remember?"

And Wren's head tilted further. Her hands went still inside the ghost of the lantern. Somewhere very deep in the dimness, a coal that had been grey for twenty years glowed — faintly, uncertainly, the color of a shop roof in a storm.

"Wren," Noor whispered. "It's me. I'm taking you home."

Behind them, unhurried, footsteps began coming down the vault aisle. Too many feet, and none of them trying to be quiet.
