## 19. The Prince Plays Courtier

Aurel had spent his whole life learning one lesson above all others: a crown is mostly costume. The weight people imagine is gold and iron; in truth it is posture. You stand a certain way, you speak at a certain pace, you let silence do your arguing for you, and eventually everyone forgets to ask whether there's anything underneath.

He had never expected to be grateful for the lesson here, of all places — three hundred feet under a mountain, in a cavern city that smelled like hot copper and old candle smoke, wearing borrowed robes the color of dried wine, smiling at creatures who would eat him if they knew what he was.

But gratitude was easier than terror. He'd take it.

"Prince Aurel of the Spire," said the minister gliding beside him, her voice like a spoon stirred slowly through honey. She was called Vessamin, and she was either a very tall woman wearing too much jewelry or a very convincing arrangement of jewelry wearing a woman; Aurel had decided not to investigate which. "The court is honored. It has been — some time — since the mountain received tribute from above."

"I'm sure my father sent his regards," Aurel said pleasantly.

Something moved behind Vessamin's face. Not quite a flinch. More like a page turning.

"His Majesty's regards are always received," she said.

*Always received.* Aurel filed the sentence away the way Noor had taught him to file things — carefully, in the dark, where no one could see him doing it.

Because that was the other thing he'd learned in the last few days: he had been trained for this by the best liar he knew, and his best liar wasn't even in the room. Noor worked the lantern-vaults somewhere below, small and unremarkable and utterly unforgettable once you knew how to look, and before they'd parted they had given him exactly three pieces of advice, delivered in the flat tone of someone reading a shopping list:

One. Let them talk first. Important people hate silences more than they hate strangers.

Two. Ask about costs. Everyone loves complaining about costs. Nobody suspects the question.

Three. If you feel clever, stop talking. Cleverness leaks.

Aurel had repeated these to himself like prayers, and so far they were working better than any prayer ever had.

* * *

The banquet hall of the mountain court was a long throat of a room lit by soul-lanterns, and Aurel made himself look at them properly this time, because not looking felt like a betrayal he couldn't afford. Each lantern held a light that was not quite fire and not quite starlight and not quite anything honest. Some of them flickered in rhythms that looked almost like breathing. Once — just once, at the far end of the hall — he could have sworn one of them turned toward him as he passed, the way a cat turns toward a door opening in another room.

He smiled at Vessamin and asked about the vintage.

It was, absurdly, excellent wine. The mountain court grew pale grapes in caverns lit by captive souls, and the wine tasted like sunlight that had been stolen rather than saved. He complimented it with genuine feeling, because lying is easiest when ninety percent of the sentence is true.

"You have good vintages every year?" he asked.

"The same year, over and over," Vessamin said, with something almost like fondness. "We keep one summer. We keep it very well."

"And the cost of keeping anything well—"

"Ah." Her jeweled fingers drummed the table. "You sound like the Ledger Keeper's clerks. Everything below has a price, little prince. Above, I'm told, you pretend otherwise. You call your prices taxes and tithes and duty, and you pay them in coin and grain." She leaned close, and her perfume smelled like struck matches. "Here we are honest. We pay in what we actually value."

"And what does the mountain value?"

"Weight," she said simply. "Soul-weight. It is the only currency that doesn't rust."

Aurel laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him, because it was such an obscene thing to say so plainly and because he'd been raised on the pretending she'd just described. His whole childhood had been prices dressed as pageantry: the grain tithe, the ward-levy, the mysterious annual disbursement his father signed each midwinter with the seal that weighed four pounds and opened doors that should never open.

*Ask about costs,* Noor had said.

So he asked about costs all evening. He asked the wine-steward what a vintage like this required. He asked a minor duke of some subterranean province why his household's lanterns burned brighter than his neighbors'. He asked an ancient, sweet-faced creature introduced only as Aunt Corvel what the mountain did on the anniversary of its founding, and Aunt Corvel patted his hand and said, "We count, dear. Every founding day, we count what we're owed. And every founding day, for longer than your city has had a name, someone from above comes down and pays."

"Someone from above," Aurel repeated.

"Mm. Your line especially. Reliable payers, your line." Aunt Corvel smiled with too many teeth arranged too kindly. "Kings who keep their debts. There's a rarity."

The floor did not tilt. Aurel noted that it didn't, distantly, the way you note that a wound hasn't started bleeding yet.

"My father," he said, "renews the bargain annually?"

"Oh, the bargain isn't renewed, dear." Aunt Corvel selected a grape. "Renewal implies it lapsed. It's maintained. Annually. Like a roof. Like a heart. You don't renew a heart; you keep it beating." She ate the grape. "Your grandfather began the payments when the border wards went up. Someone had to hold back the dark, and the dark holds nothing back for free. Your father has kept the account current every single year of his reign. Never missed a midwinter. A dutiful man." She sighed. "Dutiful men make the best debtors."

There it was. The whole shape of it, laid out between grapes and candle-soul-light, and Aurel sat there in borrowed robes with his spine straight and his smile intact while something inside him quietly came off its hinges.

He thought: *the wards fell the night the stars went out.*

He thought: *the payments must have stopped.*

He thought: *or been refused.*

And beneath those thoughts, older and colder, he thought about the ledger Noor had found in the flooded archive — the oldest ledger, the one with names in it going back past memory. Wren's name. His mother's name. And now this: not one terrible bargain made once by a desperate ancestor, but a *subscription*. An annuity of souls, paid faithfully, midwinter after midwinter, by the man whose knee he'd sat on, whose seal he'd been training to lift since he was six years old.

Every year of his childhood — every feast, every frost fair, every midwinter bonfire on the spire — had been paid for out of the same account.

He felt clever. He stopped talking.

* * *

Later — much later, when the banquet had thinned into smaller, darker conversations in alcoves hung with velvet the color of bruises — Aurel found himself seated across from a minister named Halvene, who managed the tribute rolls and drank like a man trying to drown a memory that swam.

Halvene was miserable. This made him useful. In Aurel's experience, the second-most-honest creature in any court is the one who hates their job.

"Tell me about the tribute rolls," Aurel said, refilling his cup uninvited. "I confess I know nothing of how souls are... assessed. Weighed. Whatever the term is."

"Weighed," Halvene said into his cup. "Everything weighs. A lamplighter weighs less than a judge. A child—" He stopped. Drank. Started again. "A child weighs more than anyone expects. All that unlived life. Heavy stuff, unlived life."

Aurel kept his hands steady on the bottle. Somewhere below them, in the vaults, Noor was looking for a sister who had been twenty years old and full of exactly that kind of weight.

"And the rolls record the payments from above? From the Spire?"

"Record, verify, archive." Halvene gestured vaguely at the air, as if the rolls were a smell. "Midwinter tribute, one hundred and forty-one consecutive years. I've signed the receiving entries myself these last twelve. Your father's seal on each one. Good strong wax. Red, with the spire-and-star." He snorted, an ugly little sound. "Star. As if we hadn't bought most of those."

Aurel's mouth was dry. He reached for the wine and then didn't drink it, because his hands had begun to feel unreliable, and a prince who spills wine on a minister's documents draws attention.

"Halvene," he said gently, "may I ask an impertinent question?"

"You may ask. Whether it's answered depends on the wine."

"This year's payment. Midwinter came and went above, and yet the stars went out, the wards fell, the mountain is..." he chose the word carefully, "...expecting guests. Did the payment fail?"

Halvene looked at him for a long moment. Up close, his misery had texture — not guilt exactly, but the exhausted resentment of a clerk who has done arithmetic he wishes he'd never learned.

"The payment didn't fail," Halvene said. "It was *declined*."

"Declined."

"There was a dispute. Over the terms." Halvene lowered his voice, though in that hall voices carried strangely, bending around pillars like water around stones. "Your father sent the usual tribute — the usual *weight*, drawn from the usual sources. And the Ledger Keeper returned it. Unopened. First time in one hundred and forty-one years. Returned with a notation."

"What notation?"

"'Insufficient.'" Halvene laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "Insufficient! After a century and a half of faithful payment. But the Ledger Keeper's arithmetic is not ours. Something changed in the accounting. Something above or below decided the old weights no longer balance. And when a debt is disputed—" he spread his hands, "—collection begins."

Aurel sat very still.

Collection. Wardens in the mine shafts. Dead wards twitching like nerves. A hunt that had already brushed past them once in the dark.

"And the dispute," he said. "Who raised it?"

"That, little prince, is above my station." Halvene drained his cup and set it down with the finality of a man closing a ledger. "But if you want my advice — unsolicited, worthless, free — don't be in this mountain when the collection concludes. Debts like these don't resolve. They *transfer*. Someone always ends up holding the paper."

* * *

He left the banquet hall alone, which was itself a small miracle of timing and a borrowed robe with the right hood. The corridors of the mountain court were veins of warm stone, and the deeper he walked toward the guest chambers assigned to the "tribute delegation," the quieter everything became, until the only sound was the soft irregular flicker of the wall-lanterns and his own heartbeat, which had gotten loud enough to be embarrassing.

In the shadow of an archway, he stopped, put his hand against the stone, and allowed himself — briefly, privately, with no audience but a caged light — to feel it.

Betrayal is a strange thing to feel on behalf of strangers. His mother's soul, sold. Wren Nakamura's soul, sold. Lamplighters, children, judges — sold. And not once, not in some ancient desperate hour, but *annually*, with good strong red wax, with the spire-and-star pressed neat and proud into each seal. His inheritance wasn't a kingdom. It was a payment plan.

And the worst of it — the part that made him press his forehead against the warm stone and breathe through his teeth — was that he couldn't even summon surprise. Because hadn't he always known? Hadn't he known the way you know a house has rot behind the plaster, from the way the floors sounded? His father's eyes at midwinter ceremonies, always drifting toward the mountain horizon. The sealed disbursement no treasurer would discuss. The way his mother's name had been quietly removed from the memorial rolls when he was nine, and no one would tell him why, and his father had said, "Some accounts are closed, Aurel," and poured himself a drink with a hand that was not entirely steady.

*Some accounts are closed.*

They weren't closed. They were *current*. Maintained. Like a roof. Like a heart.

A lantern near his shoulder guttered, and in its stutter he saw — or dreamed he saw — a small light leaning toward him, attentive, listening. He thought suddenly and absurdly of Noor's third rule. *If you feel clever, stop talking.* He wasn't talking. He hoped thinking didn't leak.

He pushed off the wall and walked on, faster now, and the feeling that settled into his chest as he went was not grief, exactly, and not rage, exactly. It was colder and more useful than either. It was the specific clarity of a man realizing that the thing he was loyal to had never existed — that he had been heir not to a crown but to a creditor's signature — and that therefore nothing he did next could be treason.

You cannot betray a lie by telling the truth. Someone wise had probably said that. If no one had, he'd say it himself, loudly, in front of the council, at the worst possible moment, once he got home.

Assuming there was a home. Assuming there was a *him*.

* * *

The guest chamber was a carved hollow with a bed like a stone bier and a washbasin of polished obsidian. Noor wasn't there — Noor was deep in the vaults by now, doing whatever quiet impossible thing quiet impossible people do — but they had left a sign, as agreed: a thread tied around the basin's rim, knotted in a particular way. Untouched meant *not back yet*. Moved meant *back, and we talk*.

The thread was untouched.

Aurel sat on the edge of the stone bed and took out the thing he had taken, earlier that evening, without quite deciding to take it. He'd lifted it from Halvene's document case during the fourth course, using a sleight of hand learned from a card sharp in the palace guard who had believed he was teaching a future king a harmless vice. It was a single page. A receiving entry. Twelve years old, in Halvene's cramped clerk-hand, verified and countersigned.

**Midwinter tribute, received in full. Weight: 1. One soul, female, adult. Designation per donor's instruction: [name struck through]. Note: donor requests no memorial. Payment accepted against the standing account of House Aurelian.**

Against the standing account. His family name, used as a *currency designation*. And the struck-through name — he didn't need to read it. He had spent nine years wondering why his mother's name vanished from the rolls, and here it was, struck through by his own father's instruction, *donor requests no memorial*.

Donor.

His father had *donated* her. Had stood at the midwinter bonfire that year, Aurel remembered, and smiled, and held Aurel up on his shoulders so he could see the fireworks, and the fireworks had been paid for, along with everything else, out of an account that had just been replenished.

Aurel set the page down on the obsidian basin, very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off. Then he looked at himself in the black mirror. A young man in a borrowed hood, eyes too bright, jaw set in exactly the posture his tutors had praised as regal.

"Right," he said to the mirror, quietly. "So that's what I am. The heir to *that*."

The mirror declined to argue.

He folded the page into the lining of his robe, next to his heart, which seemed appropriate and also slightly dramatic, and he was still deciding whether he was being dramatic when the thread around the basin twitched.

Noor's knot, sliding loose.

Which meant Noor was back. Which meant, by the terms of their agreement, *we talk*. But when Aurel slipped out into the corridor to find them, Noor was already there — coming fast down the vein of warm stone, hood back, face bloodless, walking with the terrible controlled speed of someone carrying news that can't be set down safely.

Behind them, far below, a bell began to ring. Not the mountain's slow ceremonial bells. A quick one. An urgent one.

Noor grabbed his sleeve. Their hands were shaking, and their voice, when it came, was flat in the way Aurel had learned meant the opposite of calm.

"Aurel. Where are the wardens' cells."

"Why?"

"Because Sable went to scout the tribunal wing four hours ago," Noor said, "and he hasn't come back."
