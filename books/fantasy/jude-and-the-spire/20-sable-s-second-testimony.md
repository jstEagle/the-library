## 20. Sable's Second Testimony

The cell smelled of wet stone and old iron, which Sable Ives considered a professional insult. He had built wards for thirty years—had built *this* one, in fact, back when the mountain court still pretended its warden-cells were for holding prisoners rather than inventory—and he knew shoddy work when he was sitting in it.

The containment lattice on the far wall flickered every fourth pulse. Someone had spliced a replacement thread into the original weave with all the delicacy of a man darning a sock with rope. The anchor stones were set half a finger too shallow. And the ventilation shaft—the ventilation shaft was *unwarded*, which meant whoever maintained this place had decided that air was beneath suspicion.

Sable had been arrested by smarter people. That was almost comforting.

He sat on the bench—they had left him the bench, a courtesy he intended to repay with interest—and counted pulses. The lattice breathed in a slow rhythm, bright-dim-bright-dim, like something sleeping. Like something *caged*, his mind supplied unhelpfully, because everything down here was caged; that was rather the point of the place.

His wrists still ached from the manacles they'd used in the corridor above. Warden-iron, spelled to bite. He'd felt the ward-signature as they clamped them on and had thought, with the peculiar clarity of a man watching his own disaster from a great height: *I know this hand. This is my hand. I taught the guild that binding.*

They had caught him at the mouth of the third mine shaft, alone, standing watch while Noor and Odile went deeper with Aurel's seal and Wren's dim light between them. He'd had time to call out a warning. He'd had time to run. Instead he had turned around, planted his feet like a man defending a bridge that no longer existed, and thrown up the best improvised barrier of his life.

It had held for eleven seconds.

Eleven seconds was enough for two shadows to slip past in the dark behind the wardens' torches. Sable chose to believe that. He needed to believe it the way other men needed wine, and for similar reasons.

* * *

The door opened on the second day—or what he assumed was the second day; the cells kept their own hours, and the hours were liars.

Two wardens came in, and between them, unhurried as a man entering his own parlor, came the Minister of Seals.

Sable knew him by reputation only. Minister Corvane. A narrow man with a narrow face and hands folded in front of him like a prayer he didn't mean. His robes were grey—not mourning-grey, but the grey of documents, of ledgers, of things written down and made permanent.

"Sable Ives," Corvane said. "Once Chief Ward-Smith of Vespergate."

"Once," Sable agreed. "You've read my file. Very thorough. Did it mention I make an excellent plum cordial? Because if you're going to keep me here, I have opinions about your cellar."

One of the wardens shifted. Corvane did not. He simply looked at Sable with the patience of a ledger waiting to be balanced.

"You testified once before," Corvane said. "At the trial of Wren Nakamura. Charm-seller. Illegal mage. You gave testimony regarding her craftwork that the tribunal found decisive."

The words landed the way Sable had known they would land, someday, somewhere—in the soft place below the ribs where he kept everything he drank not to feel.

"I did," he said quietly.

"And you were stripped of rank for unrelated failures shortly after." Corvane tilted his head. "Or perhaps related ones. Guilt is corrosive to craftsmanship. I've seen it."

"You've seen a lot from behind that desk, I expect."

"I see you now." Corvane drew a folded paper from his sleeve and laid it on the table between them without opening it. "Here is what happens next. The four of you are thieves and saboteurs. The court will want examples. But—" and here the minister smiled, a small dry movement, "—the court also values precedent. And the strongest precedent we possess is *you*. The disgraced smith who told the truth once and was ruined for it. Tell the truth again, Sable Ives. Testify against your companions. Name them, name their plan, name where they've gone and what they intend. Do that, and walk out of this mountain a free man. Reinstated, even. There are wards falling apart all over Vespergate, and so few who remember how to mend them honestly."

"Honestly," Sable repeated. "That's a word you use?"

"I use all words. They're tools." Corvane tapped the paper once. "You testified against a charm-seller once already. One more name will cost you nothing. You've had practice."

And there it was. Not the threat—the *craft* of it. Corvane wasn't threatening him. He was doing something worse: he was being reasonable. Offering the arithmetic that Sable himself had used twenty years ago, when a tribunal asked him whether the charms in Wren Nakamura's shop were harmless knots and knotted thread or instruments of forbidden craft, and Sable—Ivy-green, newly promoted, terrified of losing the only thing he'd ever been good at—had examined her luck-knots with a smith's precision and said:

*The weaving incorporates sympathetic resonance. Under examination, these are functional workings.*

Which was true. Which was also like calling a lullaby a weapon because it altered the listener's heartbeat.

They had taken her away in a cart, and Sable had gone home and gotten drunk for the first time in his life, and then gotten drunk in a more organized fashion for the next twenty years, because organized drinking at least felt like a project.

"No," Sable said.

Corvane raised an eyebrow. "You haven't heard the terms fully—"

"Oh, I heard them. Free man, reinstated, plum cordial probably included. Very generous." Sable leaned forward. "Let me tell you about testimony, Minister, since you're such a student of it. The first time, I told the truth as the tribunal wanted it shaped, and I told myself it was truth, and it wrecked me so thoroughly that I spent two decades marinating in cheap spirits trying to drown a woman's face. So either I'm a coward who folds under pressure, in which case your offer is wasted, or I'm a man who learned exactly what testimony costs when it's a lie wearing truth's coat." He spread his hands. "I can't testify against them. Not won't—*can't*. It's beyond my craft."

Something flickered across Corvane's narrow face. Respect, possibly. Wardens respected good masonry even when it imprisoned them.

"We will speak again," Corvane said, retrieving the paper. "In a day. Then half a day. Then hourly. Grief is patient work, Master Ives, but I am more patient than grief."

He left. The wardens left. The lattice pulsed its sleeping rhythm, bright-dim-bright-dim.

Sable sat back down on the bench and allowed himself, for exactly ten heartbeats, to be terrified.

Then he got to work.

* * *

The first thing he did was nothing. Nothing was important. Nothing established rhythm. When the meal slot opened—a hatch grinding aside to admit a tray of bread and water—he ate slowly, visibly, a man settling in for a long stay. When a warden passed his barred door on rounds, Sable was always doing something legible: pacing, muttering, staring at the ceiling like a man counting his regrets.

There were six cells in this block. He'd mapped them by sound within the first hour, because old habits die hard and dead habits die harder. Cell one: empty. Cell two: empty. Cell three: occupied—slow breathing, a woman's, steady as a metronome. Cell four: occupied, breathing quick and shallow, someone young and frightened. Cell five: himself. Cell six: occupied by something whose breathing he could not hear at all, which bothered him profoundly, because everything breathed.

On the second day—or the day-shaped interval after the first day-shaped interval—he began talking to the walls.

Not mad-talking. Well. Some mad-talking. But mostly the rambling of a drunk old smith reviewing his life, which was a genre the wardens would have catalogued and dismissed within minutes. He talked about wards. About how the great border-wards of Vespergate weren't built at all but *bought*, though he kept that part vague, slurred, the ravings of a bitter man. About how a lattice draws its power from whatever it's anchored to, and how the anchors of these particular cells were—

He paused there. Let the silence hang.

"—well," he muttered, "doesn't matter. Nobody listens to a drunk."

But the lattice had heard him. Lattices always hear. Every syllable spoken near a working feeds faintly into its harmonic field, the way a singer's voice makes a wineglass tremble. Sable had spent three days feeding the cell-block lattice a very particular diet of syllables: names of anchor-stones, old guild cant for resonant frequencies, fragments of the very weave-patterns he'd invented decades ago and taught to the smiths who maintained this place.

He was, in essence, teaching the lock his own handwriting.

By the fourth interval, the lattice's pulse had changed. Bright-dim-*bright*-dim. Off-rhythm. Sick. A lattice fed conflicting harmonies develops a stutter, like a heart with a murmur, and a stuttering lattice cannot hold a clean charge across its threads.

Cell three first. The woman. Her lattice guttered and died with a sound like a sigh, and in the sudden ordinary darkness she gasped—the first full breath she'd taken since arriving, because contained air always feels thin.

"Don't thank me yet," Sable whispered through the wall. "Door's on a separate mechanism. Physical bolt, top hinge side, there should be a release lever—yes, I can hear you finding it. Good. Now listen. The corridor turns left toward the tribute-halls and right toward the deep stairs. You want right, but not yet. First—"

"First?" Her voice was hoarse, disused.

"First you're going to open cell four, because that child in there has been crying since yesterday and it's ruining my concentration."

A wet, startled laugh. Then footsteps, a scrape of iron, a small choked sob of relief.

Cell six took longer. Sable pressed his palm flat against the shared wall and felt—there. A vibration too low for ears, a hum like a struck bell remembered rather than heard. Something that didn't breathe because it wasn't flesh. Something the mountain had put in a cell because the mountain puts everything in cells.

"That'll be a soul," he murmured. "Stolen. Stored. Waiting on a shelf like a jar of preserves." His throat tightened. Twenty years ago he had helped fill shelves like this one, testifying that the woman who made luck-knots deserved the cart. "Right. Hold on, friend. Smith's coming."

The sixth lattice didn't stutter—it *shattered*, all at once, with a crack that rang down the corridor like a dropped chandelier. Light poured out from under the door. Not torchlight. Starlight, or the memory of starlight, the color of the sky before dawn, and Sable heard the wardens' boots stop mid-stride two corridors away, heard voices rise in confusion, heard the alarm-bell begin its stupid frantic argument with itself.

Because here was the beautiful, terrible arithmetic of it: the mountain ran on stolen souls. Its alarms, its locks, its lanterns—all of it anchored, ultimately, to the same captive light. And when a soul tore free of a warden-cell, it didn't slip out quietly. It rang. Every working in earshot felt it, flinched, and shouted.

Which meant that somewhere below, in the lantern-vaults and the contract rooms, Noor and Odile and the prince were hearing the same bell Sable had just rung.

*Go,* he willed them, pressing his forehead to the cold wall. *Whatever you're doing—go faster.*

* * *

The wardens found him sitting on his bench, hands folded, looking profoundly innocent in the way only guilty men can manage.

"Master Ives." The senior warden's voice was tight. "There's been a breach."

"Has there? I wondered about the noise. I assumed the plumbing."

"Three cells are open. Two prisoners fled. And the sixth—" The warden hesitated, and Sable saw genuine fear in the man's eyes, the fear of a practical person confronted with a thing outside his categories. "The sixth cell held a stored soul. It's gone. The lattice didn't fail. It was *undone*. Undone from inside the block, which is impossible, because no one in this block has—"

"Craft?" Sable offered helpfully.

The warden stared at him. Then, slowly, horribly, understanding arrived. "You. You were chief ward-smith. You built half this block—"

"Built it, yes. Terrible shame about the maintenance budget, though. Half-finger shallow on the anchor stones? Whoever did that splice on the north lattice ought to be ashamed. I taught better than that. Well." He sighed. "I suppose I didn't teach it *thoroughly*."

They dragged him up out of the cell-block, and he let them, because his legs were glad of the exercise and his heart was doing something it hadn't done in twenty years: beating in clean, even time.

They threw him into a different cell—one higher up, nearer the tribute halls, with a proper door and a proper lattice, because apparently he'd earned professional respect. Through the small barred window he could see a slice of cavern city, and the cavern city was in chaos. Lanterns swinging. Wardens running in ranks. And above it all, high in the vaulted dark where the caged stars hung in their iron nets, one net was swinging empty, its light loose and rising, rising, a spark climbing toward a shaft that led, if Sable's memory of the mine-maps served, all the way up to the night sky of Vespergate.

Somewhere down there, Odile Ferrant was doing her slow, careful, astronomer's work. Somewhere down there, Noor was walking toward a poison and a bargain. Somewhere down there, a prince was learning what his inheritance actually cost.

And Sable Ives sat in a cell he could probably escape, and did not try.

That was the trick of it, he realized, watching the freed star dwindle upward like a wish returning to its owner. Corvane had offered him freedom for testimony, and Sable had refused—but refusing wasn't the redemption. Anyone could refuse in a cell. Redemption was the sixth lattice. Redemption was a stranger's sob turning into laughter, a stolen soul ringing like a bell across the dark, a debt paid in the only currency he had left: his craft, spent recklessly, on other people's behalf.

Wren Nakamura had woven luck-knots for strangers and charged them nothing, and he had called it evidence.

"Watch me now," he told the empty air, and grinned like the drunk everyone believed he was.

The door opened sooner than expected. Not the wardens. A clerk—young, sweating, holding a message slate—and behind the clerk, framed in the corridor's torchlight, stood Minister Corvane, and Corvane was not smiling anymore.

"The soul you freed," Corvane said quietly, "was entered in the ledger under reclaim-pending. A claimant signed for it. Three days hence, or forfeit."

Sable's stomach dropped through the floor, through the mountain, all the way down. *Noor.* Noor had staked their own soul, and Sable had just torn the prize off the board.

"So you see, Master Ives," Corvane said, and now the smile returned, thin as a knife's edge, "your little act of conscience may have killed your friend. Unless—" he lifted the folded paper again, still unopened, patient as stone, "—you'd like to reconsider your testimony. I'm told the Ledger Keeper honors amendments."

Down the corridor, faint but unmistakable, the alarm bells changed their rhythm—from panic to pursuit.

The hunt had begun.
