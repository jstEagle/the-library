## 21. The Vault of Caged Stars

Odile Ferrant had spent eleven years charting a sky that wasn't there.

She had done it the way all good science is done: stubbornly, obsessively, and against the express wishes of everyone in authority. When the stars went out, she had not wept. She had sharpened her pencils. She had measured the darkness every night from the observatory roof until the wardens burned her charts, and then she had measured it again from memory, and then—when memory proved an unreliable instrument—she had gone looking for the darkness itself.

And here it was. Here it had been all along. Hanging in vaults beneath a mountain, wearing iron cages.

"Oh," Odile said softly, to no one, because Sable and Aurel were two corridors back keeping watch and Noor was somewhere in the lantern-vaults below, and because there are moments when language fails and only the oldest human sound survives: a small, wondering *oh*.

The Vault of Caged Stars was not what the old texts promised. The texts—the ones she'd memorized as a girl, the ones that got her laughed out of three academic societies—described starlight as cold and distant and clean. This light was none of those things. It was warm. It pooled on the cavern floor like spilled honey and crept up the walls in slow golden tides. Each cage held a knot of radiance the size of a fist or a heart or a head, and each one pulsed, and the pulses were not synchronized, and that—that above everything—was what made her eyes sting.

They were breathing at different rates.

"Of course you are," she whispered. "You're alive."

She had known it, in the way one knows a thing abstractly and then meets it in person and realizes one never knew anything at all. She had told Noor, weeks ago in the ruined observatory, that the darkness moved like something breathing. She had meant it as a finding. A datum. She had not understood that she was describing a lung.

The cages themselves were beautiful, which was somehow worse than if they'd been crude. Wrought mountain-iron worked into filigree, each bar curved to fit its prisoner's shape, each lock a small brass mechanism etched with contract-script. Someone had loved making these. Someone had taken pride. Odile had spent enough years among astronomers and courtiers to know that cruelty almost always arrived dressed as craftsmanship.

She set down her satchel and took out her instruments: a lens ground from river-glass, a notebook, six pencils (she had learned about warden confiscation the hard way), and the thing Sable had made for her—a lockpick shaped like nothing so much as a very small, very determined bird.

"Sable Ives," she murmured, turning it over, "you absolute disgrace to your guild, this is elegant work."

It was also, he'd admitted, the first honest thing he'd smithed in nine years. Everything else since his disgrace had been hinges for smugglers and grates for liars. But a key for freeing stars—that, he said, he could sign his name to. He hadn't signed it, obviously. But she could tell he'd wanted to.

Odile chose her first cage carefully. Not the largest star—that would be greedy, and greed makes noise—but a middling one near the vault's edge, where the shadows between cages ran deepest. Its pulse was slow and labored, like a sleeper breathing through illness. Up close, she could see the problem: the contract-script around the lock was feeding on the light. Every pulse dimmed fractionally as the bars drank. The mountain court wasn't merely storing its stars. It was digesting them.

"Well," Odile said. "We can't have that."

She fitted the bird-pick to the lock and began to work, and the work was slow, and the slowness was the whole point. Sable had been explicit about this in the way only a guilty man can be explicit: *each star you free takes weight off the wards, but the locks feel it. One wrong turn and every cage in the vault will scream at once.* So she worked by touch and patience, feeling for the tumblers' rhythm, learning each lock's particular stubbornness the way she'd once learned the particular stubbornness of clouds.

The first tumbler gave after twenty minutes. The second fought her for nearly an hour, and she talked to it the entire time—quietly, absently, the way she talked to her telescopes.

"You're a difficult one. Yes. I respect that. My mother was a difficult one too. She once refused to speak to my father for a full month over a mislabeled star chart, and honestly? Correct behavior. There—" the third tumbler shifted, "—there we go. You see? Firmness and courtesy. That's how it's done."

The fourth tumbler was the last, and when it clicked free the whole mechanism exhaled—a breath of cold air smelling of rain and distance—and the cage opened like a flower deciding, after long consideration, to be one.

The star rose.

Not fast. Not dramatically. It lifted out of its cradle of iron the way steam lifts off morning tea, unhurried, gathering itself, and as it rose the light around it changed quality—sharpened, sweetened, became briefly and unmistakably *itself*—and Odile felt tears run down her face without any decision on her part to produce them.

It drifted toward the ceiling, found a seam in the rock where the mine shafts opened upward into dark, and paused there. Turning. As if considering her.

Astronomers do not believe stars notice us. Odile Ferrant had built a career on the principle that the heavens are indifferent, that wonder must survive indifference or not at all. She stood beneath that small caged sun with tear tracks drying on her cheeks and revised her entire cosmology in the space of four seconds.

"Go on," she whispered. "Up. Through the shafts. There's a whole sky waiting to remember you."

It went.

* * *

The second star took longer, because after the first one left, the remaining cages grew agitated. Their pulses quickened. The contract-script brightened along every bar, and somewhere far above, faint but distinct, Odile heard a bell begin to ring—not an alarm, not yet, but the deep patient tone of a ledger being consulted.

"They're checking the count," she muttered, hands steady on the second lock. "Fine. Count away. You'll be short by one and blaming arithmetic by dawn."

She freed the second star ninety minutes later. Then the third. Between unlockings she recorded everything—pulse rates before and after, the exact script-patterns on each lock, the direction each freed star chose when it rose. Her notebook filled with figures that would have gotten her committed to any asylum in Vespergate and would, someday, if anyone survived to read them, found a new astronomy entire.

The patterns told a story she didn't like. The deeper stars—caged longest, drunk from longest—pulsed slower. Some barely breathed at all. And the script on their cages had grown fat, luxuriant, thick with decades of feeding. Those locks wouldn't yield to patience alone. They'd need something more.

But the shallow ones, the newer captures—those she could reach. She worked her way along the vault edge, star by star, hour by hour, and each liberation did exactly what Sable had promised: somewhere in the mountain's bones, a strain eased. She could feel it in the floor. The constant low tremor of the wards—the vibration she'd been charting from the surface for years without knowing its source—weakened by a hair each time. Like a great animal shifting weight off a wounded leg.

By her ninth star she had begun humming. By her twelfth she was narrating.

"Right, this one's shy. They get shy, you know. Eleven years of watching the empty sky and I promise you the stars have personalities, everyone said I was mad, Professor Halloway said I was 'a charming embarrassment to the discipline,' which, incidentally, is what I want engraved on my tombstone—"

"That is genuinely what your tombstone should say," said a voice behind her.

Odile did not shriek. She would maintain until her dying day that she did not shriek; she made a controlled scientific vocalization of surprise. She spun, pencil raised like a duelist, and found Noor standing in the shadow of a cage-pillar, arms crossed, expression caught somewhere between alarm and amusement.

"How long have you been standing there?"

"Long enough to hear you tell a lock about your mother." Noor stepped closer, and Odile saw the lantern-vault grime on their sleeves, the exhaustion worn like a coat. "Is it going well?"

"I've freed twelve. Thirteen shortly, if this one stops sulking." She turned back to the lock, partly for the work and partly because her face was doing things she preferred not to have witnessed. "There are hundreds, Noor. Hundreds. I counted the pillars. At my pace we're talking weeks."

"We don't have weeks."

"No," Odile agreed. "We have, by my estimate of the ledger-bells, rather less than that. Which is why I'm thinking about the deep ones." She nodded toward the vault's center, where the oldest cages hung in ranks, their lights guttering, their script swollen fat. "The big old stars. If I could crack even a handful of those, the wards would buckle outright. But the locks are..." She hunted for the word. "*Well-fed.*"

Noor came to stand beside her, looking up at the nearest ancient cage. Their face did something complicated.

"Wren works down there," they said quietly. "In the lower vaults. Among these."

Odile's pencil stopped moving.

"The fragmented ones," Noor went on, in the flat careful voice of someone carrying something breakable. "The souls that were worked until they scattered. The court calls them dim stock. Cheap labor." A pause. "Wren doesn't know me. Yet. I'm working on it. But Odile—" and here the flatness cracked, just a hairline, "—these aren't all stars, are they. Some of what's in these cages started out as people."

Odile had suspected it since her sixth unlocking, when one small light had circled her twice before departing, in a way that felt less like astronomy and more like gratitude. She looked up at the ancient cage, at the guttering ember inside it, and let herself say the true thing out loud, because someone in this expedition had to be precise even when precision was cruel.

"Some of them started as people. Some of them started as stars. Down here, after enough years in the script, I'm honestly not sure the distinction holds anymore." She touched the warm iron gently, the way one touches a hospital bed rail. "Light is light. It wants out either way."

"Then keep wanting it out," Noor said. "I'll go back down. Wren remembered something today—almost. A shape. Maybe a name." They turned to go, then stopped. "Odile. When this is over. When the sky's back. Will you still be able to... do your work? With real stars to chart?"

Odile considered the question with the seriousness it deserved.

"Noor Nakamura, I have spent eleven years studying absence. Do you know what happens to an astronomer who finally gets her sky back?" She smiled, and meant it. "I become unbearable. Insufferably happy. Publishing constantly. It will be terrible for everyone."

Noor huffed—almost a laugh, the first Odile had heard from them since the descent—and slipped away down the corridor toward the labor-vaults, toward the sister who didn't know them yet.

* * *

Star fifteen resisted.

Star sixteen yielded, then seventeen, and by nineteen Odile had established a rhythm so smooth she could almost forget where she was: feel the tumblers, talk to the lock, wait out the sulk, open, release, record, move on. The freed lights were finding the shaft seams faster now, as if word traveled among them. Twice she saw distant glimmers rising through cracks she hadn't known existed, stars other than hers—or perhaps hers, doubling back to show others the way. The vault's ceiling was beginning to look, faintly, wonderfully, like the night sky of her childhood.

That was the mistake. She knew it was the mistake even as she made it, and she made it anyway, because she was human and eleven years is a long time to go without a sky.

She stopped working, stood in the middle of the aisle between cage-pillars, and simply looked up.

Golden points of ascending light. Slow drifts of radiance pooling and parting. A counterfeit constellation wheeling overhead in the dark, assembled from everything the mountain had stolen, going home piece by piece.

"It's beautiful," she said aloud. "Do you understand that I mean it as an accusation?"

The vault answered her.

Not with words. With attention. Odile felt it arrive the way one feels a change in pressure before weather—the contract-script on every remaining cage brightening at once, hundreds of locks turning their etched faces toward her like flowers toward an unwelcome sun. The ledger-bell above rang again, closer now, and under it came footsteps. Measured. Many. The particular synchronized tread of wardens who had stopped patrolling and started hunting.

Her nineteenth star chose that moment to come free—it had been nearly finished, she'd been working it in absent-minded intervals while staring at the sky—and its rising light fell across her like a spotlight, gilding her from notebook to boots, announcing her position to the entire cavern city with the cheerful indiscretion of a lighthouse.

"Oh, you couldn't have waited," Odile hissed at it. "You couldn't have risen *discreetly*. Twenty years of stellar mechanics and not one of you has mastered subtlety—"

The footsteps rounded the far pillar-row.

She did the mathematics in two seconds flat, the same rapid triage she applied to collapsing telescopes and failing careers: one astronomer, unarmed, carrying six pencils and a bird-shaped lockpick, versus—she counted silhouettes—eight wardens in mountain-iron, moving in the wide formation of people who expected their prey to be alone.

She was not supposed to be alone. Sable was supposed to be two corridors north. Aurel was supposed to be charming the ministers into complacency. Noor was supposed to be reachable.

None of them were here. The vault was hers, and her nineteen liberated stars were still streaming upward through the ceiling seams like champagne through a cracked bottle, and every single warden in the room could see exactly where the leak was coming from.

The lead warden spoke, and his voice had the flat polished quality of contract-script speaking through a human throat. "The Ledger Keeper notes irregularity in the count. The Ledger Keeper requests the irregularity present itself."

Odile Ferrant had been thrown out of three academic societies, one royal observatory, and a single memorable dinner party. She knew precisely how one behaved when cornered by an institution.

"Certainly," she said, lifting her chin, stepping backward—casually, conversationally—until her shoulder blades found the pillar behind her, and her hand found the cage beside it, and her fingers found the half-worked lock of star number twenty. "Though I should warn you. I'm terribly slow at presenting myself. It's a flaw of mine. Patience has never been—"

Behind her, beneath her fingers, tumbler one gave way.

"—one of my virtues."

The wardens began to walk forward, eight sets of mountain-iron feet striking stone in perfect time, and Odile smiled at them the smile she'd once given Professor Halloway across a faculty table, and kept picking.
