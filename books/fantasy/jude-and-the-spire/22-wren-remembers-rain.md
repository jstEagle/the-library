## 22. Wren Remembers Rain

The lantern-vaults were quieter at what Noor's body insisted was night, though down here night was only a rumor the mountain had never heard confirmed.

Noor had learned the vault's rhythms in three days of sitting on a cold stone bench beside a woman who was and was not their sister. The soul-fire dimmed on a slow cycle—someone, somewhere, rationing it—and in that dimness the fragments of Wren grew restless, drifting from her like moths unsure of their lamp. In the bright hours she was worse: brighter, busier, stitched into her work with a hundred other dim souls, all of them folding light into lantern-glass with hands that never tired because they barely belonged to them anymore.

But in the dark hours, Wren sat. And sometimes, if Noor was patient, she let herself be talked to.

Noor had brought stories the way other people brought bread to prisoners—wrapped up warm, carried carefully, offered without expectation that they'd be eaten.

"—so there I was," Noor said, hands moving in the half-dark, "nineteen years old, standing in the palace kitchens with a spoonful of stewed quince in my mouth, and I *knew*. Almond water. Someone had cut the bitter-almond oil into the prince's preserves, and everyone in that kitchen was looking at me, and I had maybe forty heartbeats to decide whether to spit or swallow."

Wren's hands lay folded in her lap. They were her hands. That was the cruelest part—the mountain hadn't changed the shape of anything, only emptied it out and left the shape standing, like a shop after everything inside had been sold.

"And I swallowed," Noor went on. "Of course I swallowed. Spitting is a confession, and I've never confessed to anything in my life. Forty heartbeats later my tongue goes numb, my ears start ringing, and the head cook is asking me very loudly whether the quince meets standards, and I say—" Noor pitched their voice into the gruff bark they'd practiced for twenty years, "—'Adequate,' and then I go behind the flour barrels and vomit until my eyes water."

Something moved at the corner of Wren's mouth. It might have been a twitch. Noor chose to believe it wasn't.

"The thing nobody tells you about being a taster," Noor said, settling back against the cold wall, "is that you stop tasting. Everything. Twenty years of small poisons, and now honey tastes like wax and wine tastes like rain off a hot street and my mother's spiced pears—I'd give almost anything for those, and I couldn't tell you anymore if I'd know them." A pause. "Don't take the job, is what I'm saying. Terrible career advice, coming from me."

Silence. The dim souls down the row folded their light, fold after patient fold, like a tide that never reached shore.

Noor kept talking anyway. They had discovered this about themselves in the last three days, and it surprised no one less than it should have: grief had made them a chatterbox. Twenty years of saying nothing, hoarding words like coins, and now here they were, spending the whole purse on a sister who couldn't make change.

* * *

They told her about the lamplighter, Tammas, who had bought a luck-knot with his last coin and lived eleven days longer than his lungs had any right to allow him, long enough to teach his daughter the trade. They told her about the child who stole a charm and got caught, and how Noor had pretended to chase her out of the market while actually pressing a warming-stone into her coat pocket. They told her about Sable Ives, drunk in the Ash Quarter, and stopped halfway through that story because the shape of it still stuck in their throat.

They told her about the stars going out.

"You'd have hated it," Noor said. "Or loved it. You always did both at once, with things like that. You used to climb onto the shop roof when the sky did something interesting and yell down at me to come look, and I'd say the sky does something interesting every single night, Wren, and you'd say yes, *exactly*, isn't that marvelous."

Wren's breathing didn't change. But her head tilted, very slightly, toward Noor's voice. Three days ago it hadn't done that.

Encouraged, Noor leaned closer. The soul-fire in her chest was a gray ember, guttering, but it guttered *toward* them, the way a flame leans when a door opens somewhere in the house.

"There was a storm," Noor said slowly, feeling for it the way they felt for poison under their tongue—one careful touch at a time. "Do you remember? You were—fourteen? Fifteen? We were supposed to close early because of the weather, and you wouldn't. You said storms were when people needed charms most. And the rain came down so hard on the roof tiles it sounded like—"

Like applause. Like a thousand small hands. Like—

"—like the whole city clapping for us," Noor finished, barely above a whisper. "And we turned all the lamps off and sat in the dark listening to it, and you said, 'Listen, Jude. Nobody else gets this exact rain. It's ours.'"

The name came out before Noor could stop it.

Jude. Not Noor. Not the careful, flavorless, unremarkable person they had built around themselves like a second set of walls. *Jude*—the name Wren had called across the shop, up the stairs, over rooftops; the name that had died with her, or been buried alive with her, because no one else living knew it. Noor hadn't said it aloud in twenty years. Saying it felt like taking off armor in front of an open door.

Wren's folded hands came apart.

Her fingers moved—not the smooth mechanical motion of lantern-work, but something jerkier, hungrier, a hand remembering itself one tendon at a time. Her brow furrowed. The gray ember in her chest flickered, steadied, flickered again, and for one terrible moment Noor thought the fragment was going to gutter out entirely, that they had spent too much of her at once, that memory was a currency here too and they'd just overdrawn the account.

"Wren?" Noor's voice cracked down the middle. "Hey. Hey, look at me. I'm sorry. I shouldn't have—"

"Rain," Wren said.

It was barely a voice. More air than sound, more rust than speech. But it was *aimed*, and it landed.

"On the roof," Noor managed.

Wren's head turned. All the way, this time, deliberate as a compass needle finding north. Her eyes found Noor's face in the dimness, and behind the fog in them something surfaced—something small and specific and unbearably familiar. The look she used to get when she'd figured out a charm three steps ahead of explaining it. The look of a girl about to do something reckless and wonderful.

"Jude," Wren whispered.

The world went very quiet and very large.

Down the row, the other dim souls went on folding their light, indifferent. Somewhere far off, water dripped in the deep galleries where the mountain kept its oldest contracts. And between those two silences, a fragmented soul looked at her sibling and said their name—the true one, the buried one, the one no ledger had ever recorded because some things you don't sell even by accident—and Noor Nakamura, royal food-taster, illegal mage, professional keeper-of-a-straight-face, put their face in their hands and wept like a child.

* * *

Afterward—they couldn't have said how long after—Wren's hand had found theirs. Her grip was weak and wrong-temperature, cool as river stones, but it closed around Noor's fingers with unmistakable intent.

"Sit," she rasped. "Tell again."

"Which part?"

"All of it." A frown, effortful. "I have... holes. Big ones. Small ones worse." She tapped her temple with one finger, and there—there it was, buried under twenty years of mountain dust—a flash of wry humor, dry as the shop's best cinnamon. "You'll have to talk fast. I keep leaking."

Noor laughed, wet and startled. "That sounds like you. The real you."

"I hope so." Wren's gaze drifted toward the distant glow of the work-fires, and the humor dimmed. "There are pieces of me doing things right now, aren't there. I can feel them. Hands I'm not using. Work I don't remember starting."

"Yes." Noor squeezed her fingers, gentle. "We're getting all of you back. Every piece."

"How?"

"That's my problem, not yours."

"It was always your problem," Wren murmured. "You took every problem in the household and declared it yours by law. You were nine. You drew up documents."

"You remember the documents?"

"I remember being *furious* that your documents were better than mine."

And just like that they were sisters again, or the ghost of sisters, trading old grievances across a gap that twenty years and a stolen soul had cut between them—and Noor would have sat there forever, mining the dark hours for every scrap, except that the ember-cycle shifted and the vault began its slow brightening, and with the light came the wardens' footsteps, distant but regular, counting their flock.

Noor stood. Their knees protested; they'd been sitting on stone for hours and their body, lately poisoned and lately heroic, filed complaints about everything.

"I have to go," they said. "But I'll come back. Dark hours, every time."

Wren's hand caught their sleeve. "Jude." Her voice had gone urgent, thin as wire. "The others—the ones who work beside me—some of them *remember*. Fragments. And they're afraid." Her grip tightened. "There's a rumor in the folds. Something about the bargain. Something broken."

Noor went still. "Broken how?"

"I don't know. Pieces. A page torn somewhere, a debt unpaid—" Wren shook her head, frustrated at her own gaps. "But the wardens are angry. Angrier than usual. And tonight they're not counting us." Her eyes lifted to Noor's, and in them the ember burned suddenly, fiercely bright. "Tonight they're hunting."

Far off in the galleries, a bell began to ring—the same bell that had sounded when the four of them first came down the mine shafts, but faster now, uglier, a rhythm with teeth in it.

Noor looked at their sister, whole enough to worry, whole enough to warn them.

"Hold on," they said. "I'm getting you out. Even if I have to tear this mountain apart with my bare hands and eat every ward in it."

Wren smiled—the crooked, reckless, marvelous smile from the rooftop, from the rain, from the life the mountain thought it had erased.

"There's my Jude," she whispered. "Go. Before they find you here."

Noor ran, the warning ringing louder than the bell, toward whatever had broken in the dark.
