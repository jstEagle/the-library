## 23. The Hunt Begins

The bell that woke the mountain city was not a bell.

Noor had spent three days learning the sounds of the cavern court—the drip of water through the vaulted dark, the shuffle of soul-servants going to their endless work, the low hum of the lantern-vaults like a hive thinking. They had catalogued every sound the way they once catalogued flavors: this one safe, this one dangerous, this one merely strange.

This sound belonged in none of those columns.

It came from everywhere at once, a vibration in the stone itself, as if the mountain had cleared its throat and found it wanted clearing further. The soul-fire in the street-lamps guttered from gold to a sickly white. Down the boulevard of the tribute-bearers' quarters, doors began opening one by one, and faces Noor had spent three days not memorizing—because memorizing was what got you killed—poked out and looked up.

Noor looked up too.

High above the cavern city, where the rock arched away into darkness, something was written in the air. Letters of pale fire, enormous, unhurried, as though whoever wrote them had all the time in the world and intended for everyone else to have none:

**THE BARGAIN IS BREACHED.**

*Well,* Noor thought, with the particular calm that twenty years of tasting other people's breakfasts had taught them—the calm that arrived precisely when screaming would be most useful. *That's not good.*

The letters burned on. Beneath them, a second line assembled itself out of sparks, each character arriving with the finality of a stamp pressed into wax:

**SOULS HAVE BEEN FREED BEFORE THEIR TERM. THE DEBT IS CALLED DUE. ALL CONTRACTED PARTIES WILL PRESENT THEMSELVES TO THE LEDGER BY THIRD BELL.**

And then, smaller—though "smaller" here still meant tall enough to read across a city square—as if the writer were capable of something almost like pettiness:

**THERE ARE NO EXCEPTIONS. THERE NEVER WERE.**

Noor stood very still in the doorway of their borrowed room and did the arithmetic they had been dreading since Odile first put her hands on a star-cage and smiled the smile of a woman about to do something magnificent and unforgivable.

Three stars freed. Three souls loosed from their contracts early. Which meant the Ledger Keeper knew. Which meant the Ledger Keeper knew *when*, which meant—

A knock at the door. Two knocks, then one, then two again. Sable's signal, though Sable had invented it drunk and it had never once been practiced sober, so it always sounded slightly apologetic.

Noor opened it. Sable stood in the corridor looking like a man who had slept in his clothes, which he had, and who regretted everything, which was simply his face. Behind him came Aurel, dressed in borrowed servant gray that fit him badly because he insisted on standing like a prince even when dressed as a scullion. Odile was absent.

"Tell me you've seen her," Sable said.

"I haven't." Noor stepped back to let them in, already pulling the door shut behind them. "When did she last check in?"

"Second bell yesterday," Aurel said. "She said she'd found three more cages that could be opened without waking the vault-wardens, and she said—" he paused, and there was something pained in his expression, the look of a man quoting scripture he'd personally disproven, "—she said, and I quote, 'the hard part is done.'"

"The hard part is never done," Noor said. "The hard part is the part after the part she thought was done."

"That is genuinely the wisest thing anyone has said in this mountain, and I include the Ledger Keeper."

"Sable. How long since second bell yesterday?"

Sable did the math with visible effort. "Nine hours? Ten?"

"And third bell today is—"

"Noonish. If the mountain keeps honest hours, which it doesn't, but the fire-letters will keep *its* hours, so call it noon."

Noor looked at the two of them, and felt the old familiar sensation rise in their chest—not fear exactly, but its well-dressed cousin, the one that wore competence as a coat. Panic, disciplined. Marched into formation. Given a job.

"Then we have perhaps four hours," they said, "to find Odile, find Wren, and get out of a city full of wardens who now have permission—and, I suspect, enthusiasm—to tear us apart."

* * *

The city changed around them as they walked.

It wasn't dramatic. That was the terrible part. Noor had expected alarm—bells, running, wardens kicking down doors. Instead the mountain court simply... tightened. The soul-servants in the streets moved a little faster, heads a little lower. The lamp-flames stayed that wrong white color, and under it everyone's skin looked faintly blue, like the whole city had been dipped in cold water. Wardens appeared at intersections where no wardens had stood an hour before—tall figures in gray lacquer, their faces smooth as unglazed pottery, each one carrying a hooked staff that hummed at a pitch Noor felt in their teeth.

"They're checking hands," Sable murmured, as the four of them—three, counting carefully, because Odile was still missing—joined a slow river of servants moving toward the central plaza. Everyone was being funneled the same direction. Toward the Ledger.

"Checking hands how?" Aurel asked.

"You'll see. Don't flinch. Flinching is a confession down here."

At the mouth of the plaza, a warden stood beside a basin of silver liquid that reflected nothing. Each servant passing dipped in both hands, held them there a moment, withdrew. Most passed through without comment. One woman—a baker by the flour still ghosting her sleeves—drew her hands out glowing faintly green, and two more wardens detached from the crowd's edge and walked her away, gently, the way you'd walk a child out of a room where something sad had happened. She didn't resist. That frightened Noor more than any struggle would have.

"It reads residue," Sable said quietly. "Star-fire clings. Odile's been elbow-deep in caged constellations for three days; if she walks past that basin, she lights up like a festival."

"Then we find her before she reaches a basin," Noor said. "Or we find a way to wash her hands."

"With what? Mountain soap?"

"With whatever's in the alchemists' row, yes, actually. Vinegar cuts it—I watched her scrub after the first cage and she used vinegar and salt. She complained about the smell for an hour, which for Odile constitutes a sonnet."

Aurel made a small sound. When Noor glanced at him, he was watching the baker being led away, his jaw set in a way Noor recognized from the palace—from mornings when the prince tasted his own helplessness along with his breakfast.

"My father sold these people," Aurel said. Not loudly. Just stating it, the way you'd note the weather. "Every person in this line. And they line up. They line up because the alternative has never once occurred to them."

"The alternative occurs," Noor said. "It just costs more than they can pay. I know something about that arithmetic."

They crossed the plaza's edge in the shadow of a colonnade, avoiding the basin by taking a service passage Sable half-remembered from a map he'd studied while pretending to be less drunk than he was. It let them out near the lantern-vaults' upper galleries, where the light of the captive stars pooled gold and warm and utterly at odds with the white-lit streets below.

And there, sitting on the gallery steps with her elbows on her knees and vinegar-stained fingers laced together, was Odile Ferrant—arguing with a warden.

Not fighting. *Arguing.* She had produced, from somewhere, a scroll of her own star-charts, and she was gesturing at it with the fervor of a lecturer whose student has asked a promising question, and the warden—smooth-faced, humming, terrible—was standing there listening, or doing whatever wardens did instead of listening.

"Oh no," Sable breathed. "Oh, she's *lecturing* it."

"She does that," Noor said, already moving. "Stay here. Both of you. If I come back running, run in the other direction and don't be noble about it."

"No promises," said Aurel, who had clearly already decided to be noble about it, because princes were constitutionally incapable of following simple survival instructions. Noor decided to deal with that later, assuming there was a later, which the morning's correspondence suggested was uncertain.

* * *

Up close, the warden was worse than up-far. Its face wasn't blank so much as *uncommitted*—features present but refusing meaning, like a portrait painted by someone who'd only ever had faces described to them. Its staff hummed. Odile stood a hand-span outside the staff's reach, which Noor suspected was deliberate, because Odile measured everything, including mortal danger.

"—and furthermore," Odile was saying, tapping her chart, "if your Ledger tracked stellar mass correctly—which it demonstrably doesn't, see column four—your own breach-clause wouldn't have triggered until the fourth freeing. You're punishing yourselves for your own arithmetic errors. Honestly, it's the sloppiest contract drafting I've ever seen, and I reviewed a paper once by a man who dated his observations *before he was born*."

The warden's head turned toward Noor. The humming changed pitch.

"Friend of mine," Noor said quickly, stepping between them with the bright, bland smile of a food-taster assuring a nervous cook that no, the soup is fine, the soup is always fine. "Forgive her. She's a tribute-bearer's clerk. Numbers are her whole personality."

"I contain multitudes," Odile said.

"You contain vinegar residue on both hands," Noor said, sweetly, taking her arm, "and you are coming with me to wash them before someone official notices. Excuse us."

The warden didn't move to stop them. That was somehow worse than if it had. As Noor drew Odile away, they felt its attention on their back like a hand resting lightly between the shoulder blades—not gripping, just *noting*.

Around the corner, out of the staff's hearing, Odile finally stopped performing composure and sagged against the wall.

"Two more cages open," she whispered. "I couldn't stop. Once I understood the mechanism, leaving them locked felt like—" Her hands shook. Noor had seen soldiers' hands do that. Astronomers', apparently, too. "I freed a shepherd, Noor. He'd been in there sixty years. He looked at me like I was the dawn."

"You were," Noor said. "And now the dawn has a price tag, and we're all holding the invoice. Third bell. Four hours. Can you get yourself clean?"

"Vinegar and salt, twice, nails included. There's an alchemist's stall in the tinker-row, third arch, the man owes me for fixing his astrolabe." She paused. "Where's Wren?"

That was the question Noor had been carrying since the sky caught fire with letters. Wren worked the lower vaults. Wren, dim and fragmented, who yesterday had remembered rain and whispered Noor's childhood name like a key turning in a lock that hadn't moved in twenty years. The lower vaults were the first place the wardens would sweep, because the lower vaults were where the breach had happened, and because broken things were always blamed first.

"The lower vaults," Noor said. "With me. You two—"

"We're coming," Aurel said, materializing with Sable from the colonnade shadow, having obeyed instructions in the manner of royalty everywhere, which is to say not at all. "Don't waste breath arguing. I'm told I'm charming under pressure and Sable knows the shafts."

"I know the shafts *badly*," Sable corrected. "Which is different, but usefully."

* * *

The lower vaults were already crawling.

Wardens moved through the lantern-rows in pairs, and between them drifted lesser figures—clerks of the Ledger, Noor guessed, stitched shapes like their master but smaller, patchwork things trailing ribbon and vellum, checking each cage, ticking each soul off some invisible list. The soul-fire in the cages had gone anxious, flickering, the way candlelight behaves when people are arguing nearby.

Noor found Wren's station empty.

For three seconds, three seconds that lasted approximately a decade, Noor stood in front of the vacant workbench—Wren's polishing cloths folded neatly, Wren's little tin cup of oil still full—and let the panic off its leash entirely. Just for those three seconds. Then they took it back, collared it, and put it to work, because that was the family trade, wasn't it? Turning terror into tasks. Their father had built poisons into medicine; Noor built dread into lists.

*Think. Where does a half-remembered soul go when the wardens come?*

Not hiding—Wren didn't hide; hiding was a living person's instinct and Wren's instincts had been filed down by sixty years of captivity. Running—no. Wren would go somewhere *familiar*. Somewhere that felt like safety in the shape of the old world. And yesterday, in the story of the rain on the shop roof, Wren had said the word—had whispered the name—and afterward had asked, in that papery fragment-voice, whether the shop still smelled of cedar.

The storerooms above the lantern-vaults were stacked with cedar crates. Tribute packaging. Cedar everywhere.

Noor went up the gallery stairs two at a time.

They found Wren in the third storeroom, sitting on a crate in the dark with their arms wrapped around themselves, humming—the shop-roof song, barely, a thread of it, worn thin as cloth rubbed between fingers for years.

"Wren."

The humming stopped. In the gloom, Wren's face turned—dim, translucent at the edges, a person rendered in watercolor—but the eyes found Noor in the dark without trouble, because some things survived even the Ledger's bookkeeping.

"You came," Wren said. "When the loud bells started. I remembered—you always came when the bells started, when I hid from the thunder. Under the table. With the cedar."

"Yes," Noor said, and their voice cracked on it, and they let it, because some cracks were load-bearing. "Yes, I always came. I'm coming now. We're leaving now."

"The letters said breached." Wren's hands twisted in their lap. "Was it you? Did you breach?"

"No." A pause. Honesty had become expensive lately and Noor kept paying anyway. "A friend. An astronomer. She freed three souls early, and the whole bargain's been called due, and if we don't reach the deep vault before third bell and void your contract properly, the Ledger takes mine instead. So we're going to go and void it. Together. Like everything else from now on."

Wren considered this with the slow gravity of someone relearning how consideration worked. Then: "You got taller."

"I did not."

"You got *stubborn*-er, then. That's taller for you."

Despite everything—despite the wardens and the letters and four hours left on the clock—Noor laughed. It came out ragged and real. Twenty years of careful, flavorless invisibility, and their sister's first act of recovered memory was an insult. It was the best thing that had happened all week.

Footsteps below. Gray lacquer, pairs, climbing.

Noor took Wren's hand. It felt like holding a handful of cool evening air that had decided, stubbornly, to be a person.

"Time to go home," Noor said. "The long way."

* * *

They nearly made it.

The service stair behind the storerooms descended toward the mine-shaft junction where Sable's bad maps lived, and for eleven blessed minutes the five of them moved through the mountain's bones unmolested—Odile ahead with her freshly scoured hands smelling of vinegar, Sable navigating by a combination of memory and vibes, Aurel carrying Wren when the stairs grew steep because Wren's form tired fast and because Aurel carried people the way he apparently did everything, with unnecessary grace and total commitment.

At the junction, where the dead wards twitched in the walls like nerves in an amputated limb, the humming started.

Every staff-hum at once, from every direction, converging.

"They've sealed the shafts," Sable said, going pale in the ward-light. He pressed his palm flat against the tunnel wall, feeling. "The old wards—they're not dead anymore. Something's driving current through them. The Ledger's turned the whole vein-system into a net, and we're—" He swallowed. "We're the fish."

Above them, far up the shaft, torchlight bloomed. Below, the same. The humming thickened, harmonized, became almost musical, which was the worst development yet because it meant coordination.

Noor stood in the dark with their sister's hand in theirs, their friends at their shoulders, and the arithmetic of the morning rearranged itself into brutal new columns. Four hours to third bell. Shafts sealed. Wardens converging. One route left that anyone knew of, and it ran straight through the heart of the mountain court, past the ministers' halls, through territory where a disguised prince might just possibly be worth more than a thousand charms.

"Aurel," Noor said slowly, "how much of that courtier training of yours survives under pressure?"

The prince looked at the torches closing in above and below, and then at Noor, and smiled the smile of a man about to do something self-loathing and magnificent.

"Enough," he said, "to buy us an hour. Maybe two. Follow my lead—and whatever they offer me, don't let me shake on it."
