## 24. Through the Minister's Teeth

Aurel had always thought that if he ever became a liar, it would feel like falling.

It didn't. It felt like putting on a coat he'd been measured for since birth—his father's coat, heavy in the shoulders, cut from the same wool as every promise the crown had ever made and broken. He straightened the collar, looked at himself in the tarnished mirror of his borrowed quarters in the mountain court, and practiced smiling until the smile stopped feeling like someone else's teeth in his mouth.

*You are the heir to Vespergate,* he told the reflection. *You have come to negotiate. You are your father's son.*

The last part was the lie, and it was the load-bearing one.

Behind him, on the narrow cot, Noor sat very still with Wren's luck-knot wound around their fingers like a rosary. They had been arguing for an hour about whether this plan was madness, and had only stopped arguing because there was no time left to lose.

"They'll want terms in writing," Noor said. "Ministers always do. Ink is how they prove they were never the ones who decided anything."

"Then I'll give them ink." Aurel tugged his cuffs straight. "Ink is cheap. It's the truth that costs."

"And when they come to collect on your signature? When your reign begins and they arrive at the gates holding a contract with your name on it?"

"Then I'll be king," Aurel said, "and kings break contracts. I've watched one do it annually for twenty years." He heard how it came out—the flint edge under the words—and softened it, because Noor hadn't chosen his father either. "Besides. There won't be anything left to collect. That's rather the point."

Noor studied him with those ledger-keeper's eyes, the ones that weighed everything and revealed nothing, though Aurel was learning to read the margins now. What lived in them tonight was not trust, exactly. It was something more practical and more painful: the look of someone deciding to need you, and hating the needing.

"If this fails," Noor said, "they'll take you apart slowly enough that you'll wish you'd been born anyone else."

"I've wished that since I was six." He picked up the circlet—a light thing, ceremonial, the only crown the mountain court would recognize—and settled it on his head. "Wish me luck."

"Luck's a charm," Noor said. "I don't sell to royalty."

It was almost humor. From Noor, it counted as a blessing.

* * *

The ministers received him in a chamber carved from the living rock of the mountain, which was the sort of detail Aurel might once have found impressive. Now he saw it for what it was: architecture as appetite. The walls curved inward like a throat. The lamps burned with that pale, honeyed soul-fire that made everyone's face look kind and made no one's eyes warm. Somewhere below them all, caged stars turned in their vaults, and Aurel felt them the way you feel a headache starting—behind the eyes, before the pain has a name.

There were seven ministers. Of course there were seven; the mountain loved its sevens. They sat in a crescent behind a table of black stone, and they wore robes of grey so fine it seemed woven from weather, and they had faces like well-fed priests, which was to say faces that had never once been hungry and could therefore afford to discuss hunger at leisure.

The eldest sat at the center. She was called Minister Vael, and her voice had the particular smoothness of someone who has delivered bad news so many times it has worn grooves in her tongue, channels down which the words flow without effort or conscience.

"Prince Aurel." She gestured to a chair that had clearly been placed lower than theirs. A small cruelty, or perhaps just furniture. He declined the chair and remained standing, which made the youngest minister shift uncomfortably. Good. Discomfort was currency here too. "We did not expect the heir of Vespergate to grace us. Your father sends his regrets, we assume."

"My father," Aurel said, "sends nothing. My father is dead."

A ripple went through the crescent—not shock, he noted, but recalibration. Seven sets of hands moving invisible abacuses.

"So we understand," Vael said. "Condolences. Though of course, the death of a signatory does not dissolve a contract. The bargain binds the throne, not the man. The annual tribute will be expected, and given the disruption above—" she allowed herself the ghost of sympathy, "—the court anticipates some difficulty in collection. We are prepared to be reasonable about timing. Not amount."

And there it was. The reason he had walked down a dead king's mine shafts into a city that ate light. The ministers had already smelled the weakness in Vespergate—wards fallen, stars gone, panic in the streets—and like any good creditor they had arrived early to the funeral of his kingdom's solvency, ready to repossess.

His father would have knelt. His father had knelt, every year, in letters Aurel had found in the archive with Noor's help: *the tribute shall be rendered as agreed, in souls of sound quality and sufficient weight.* Twenty years of kneeling in ink. Aurel had read each letter twice, once as a prince and once as a son, and both times he had wanted to burn the paper and his own skin with it.

He did not kneel. He smiled instead—the new smile, the one that fit—and said:

"The tribute will not be paid."

Silence, of the kind that has weight and temperature. One of the younger ministers actually laughed, a single startled note, then smothered it.

Vael's expression did not change, which meant it changed enormously somewhere underneath. "Prince Aurel. May I remind you what your borders look like without our wards?"

"You may not," Aurel said pleasantly, "because you know perfectly well that your wards fell four nights ago, and my borders have been open as a barn door ever since. Whatever power fed them, you've been drinking it back down here like wine before a toast." He let that land, watched the youngest minister's hands go still. "I've read the charter, Minister. I know what the wards ran on. I know what my father paid. So let us not spend the evening pretending this is a conversation about protection. This is a conversation about supply."

Something moved through the crescent again, and this time he could name it: respect, the ugly kind, the kind creditors feel for debtors who learn arithmetic.

"You are blunt," Vael said.

"I'm out of time. My city is frightened, my astronomers can't find the sky, and my food-taster keeps looking at me as if I'm about to do something interesting." A small true thing, offered as camouflage. "So I'll be blunt. You want souls. I am the last person in Vespergate with the authority to promise them and the first person with the sense to stop promising them. Unless."

"Unless," Vael echoed. Ministers, he was learning, were creatures of echo. They repeated your word back to you while they decided how much it would cost to take it away.

"Unless the terms change," Aurel said. "Here is my offer."

* * *

Here is the thing about lying to monsters, Aurel discovered as he spoke: it is easier than lying to people you love, because monsters only ever hear what they already believe. And what the mountain court believed—what it had been built on, bedded into the stone like roots—was that crowns would always pay. That power recognized power. That any prince, shown the price of refusal, would choose the same arithmetic his father chose.

So he gave them arithmetic.

"A single payment," he said. "One final settlement, ending the annual tribute forever. In exchange for full and permanent warding of Vespergate—wards that hold without renewal, without collection, without your collectors ever crossing my border again."

Vael's fingers had stopped moving. "And the substance of this settlement?"

"Not souls of my people." He let the pause stretch just long enough. "Mine. The tribute of my reign. Every soul the crown owes, drawn from the royal line itself—from my own succession, my own blood, for as long as my house sits the throne of Vespergate."

He watched it take hold. Watched seven faces perform the same calculation at seven different speeds: *an heir staking his dynasty against a city's debts. Greedy, arrogant, exactly like his father after all. Exactly like the men we have always dealt with.*

That was the knife of it. That was the part he would scrub at later, in the dark, and never get clean: they believed him instantly, gladly, because he was telling them the story they already knew about people like him. He was not deceiving them despite being a prince. He was deceiving them *with* it.

"The court will require definition," Vael said at last, and her voice had gone soft and wet with satisfaction. "Souls of the royal line. How many? Over what term?"

"One soul per decade of my reign," Aurel said. "Beginning with mine, at my death, whenever death finds me. Then my heirs', in order of succession, eldest first. In perpetuity, for as long as the crown endures." He shrugged, careless as a man discussing grain tariffs. "Kings die anyway, Minister. At least this way the dying purchases something."

The youngest minister made a small sound, quickly swallowed. Aurel catalogued that face—young, appalled, ambitious enough to be here anyway—and filed it away. Everyone in this room had a seam. Even mountains.

"It is generous," Vael said slowly.

"It is *final*," Aurel corrected. "That's the generosity. No more collectors in my streets. No more letters. No more—" he caught himself an inch from saying *my mother* and rerouted with a smile, "—no more ambiguity between our thrones. Sign it, seal it, and Vespergate sleeps behind honest walls tonight."

They conferred among themselves in low voices, leaning together like crows over a carcass, and Aurel stood with his hands folded and his heart going like a smith's hammer and thought, with a clarity that felt like frost forming: *I am good at this. That's the worst discovery of my life. Fathering taught me nothing but this, and I am good at it.*

Somewhere above them, past miles of stone, the sky was empty where the stars should be, because his family had sold them by the handful to keep its secrets lit. And here he stood, spending the only coin he had left that wasn't stolen—his own future, his own bloodline's future—on a lie, wearing the lie like armor, and finding that it fit his shoulders exactly the way his father's coat fit.

He hated it. He held onto the hate like a handrail. As long as he hated it, some small cold corner of him stayed his.

* * *

The contract was brought out within the hour, which told him everything about how eager the mountain was—creditors always move fastest when they smell a lifetime's interest compounding. It was written on vellum the color of old milk, in ink that shimmered faintly, and Aurel did not let himself think about what made it shimmer. Noor had warned him: *don't touch the ink barehanded, don't read the marginal clauses aloud, and whatever happens, don't thank them. Gratitude is consent here. They count it.*

He read every clause. Slowly. Out loud, silently, and then aloud where required. Let them watch him be careful; caution read as fear, and fear read as sincerity. The contract bound "the Crown of Vespergate, in perpetuity, to render unto the Court Beneath the Mountain one soul per decade of reign, drawn from the royal bloodline, beginning with the reigning sovereign's own"—and it named *him*, Aurel, son of Aldric, as principal signatory, which was precisely what Noor had said to insist on. A contract signed by the heir could be voided by the heir. A contract signed "by the crown" would have outlived them all.

"There is a clause here," Aurel said, tapping the ninth section, "about 'collection rights within the sovereign's territories.' Strike it."

"That clause is standard," Vael said.

"It was standard when it let your wardens walk into my capital and take a lamplighter's grandmother because her neighbor sold her soul to buy medicine. It was standard when it took a charm-seller named Wren Nakamura twenty years ago." He kept his voice level, bored even, a man negotiating punctuation. "Strike it, or there is no bargain, and you can explain to your Ledger Keeper why the court preferred nothing to something."

He had not planned to use Wren's name. It had simply been there, in his mouth, loaded, and he understood as he fired it that this was what Noor had done to him—infected him with other people's dead, until he carried strangers' griefs like state papers in his breast pocket.

Vael looked at him for a long moment. Something flickered behind her face—recalculation, or memory, or merely the reflex of a creature that has been contradicted rarely and resents the novelty.

"Struck," she said.

They struck it. The youngest minister did the striking, and his pen shook slightly, and Aurel met his eyes across the table and gave him the smallest nod, one conspirator to another—or perhaps just one prisoner to another; in this room the difference was academic.

Then he signed.

The quill was white bone. The ink was cold going on, colder than it should be, and for one vertiginous heartbeat Aurel felt the page *notice* him—felt something vast and patient turn its attention through the vellum and take his measure, the way Noor must feel a poison bloom on the tongue. It knew his name. It knew his weight. Somewhere in the deep vaults below, a space was quietly reserved beside his mother's contract, labeled and waiting, decades distant but *reserved*.

He finished the signature with a flourish he did not feel. His hand did not shake. He was distantly proud of that, and infinitely disgusted by it, and he wore both feelings at once like a doublet lined with nettles.

"Witnessed and sealed," Vael said, and the seven ministers rose, and for the first time they bowed—to *him*, shallow and correct, the bow owed to a paying customer. "The court honors its bargains, Prince Aurel. Your city's wards will be restored by dawn. You may tell your people the crown has secured their safety."

"Oh, I'll tell them something," Aurel said. "Thank you for your hospitality."

*Don't thank them,* Noor had said.

The word was out before he could catch it, hanging in the soul-lit air like a dropped glass. For a moment nobody moved. Then Vael smiled—genuinely, terribly—and said, "Graciousness becomes you, Highness. It runs in the family."

He walked out of the chamber on legs that behaved beautifully, through corridors of breathing stone, past lanterns that burned with a light that had once been somebody's Tuesday afternoon. Only when he reached the junction where Sable waited in the shadow of a buttress—drunk-looking even sober, wired tight as a snapped bowstring—did his composure crack, one hairline fracture running ear to ear.

"Well?" Sable whispered.

"Signed, sealed, struck the collection clause, and I thanked them." Aurel pressed his back to the cold rock and laughed, quietly, horribly. "I thanked them, Sable. I'm going to be sick."

"Don't you dare. Do you know what I gave up to smell like this?" Sable peered around the corner, then back. "The wards restore by dawn?"

"By dawn. Which means by dawn, every warden in this mountain believes Vespergate is paid up, protected, and *pacified*. Nobody watches the tribute roads. Nobody counts the doors." He pushed off the wall. "We have until dawn, and probably less, before someone upstairs remembers to wonder why the heir of Vespergate is still wandering the guest corridors instead of riding home to his new peace."

Sable grinned at him, gap-toothed and unlovely and, for the first time since the descent, hopeful. "You lying, scheming, magnificent little—"

"Don't." Aurel held up a hand. "Please. Compliments make it worse."

Because here was the arithmetic he couldn't put down, the sum that followed him up the corridor: it had worked. It had worked *because* of what he was. His name, his blood, his father's reputation for selling anything at the right price—all of it had been the instrument, and the instrument had played true, and somewhere far above, a girl with rain-song in her memory was sitting in a vault waiting for them, and the only way to reach her had been to become, for one hour, exactly the kind of man who had put her there.

He climbed the stairs toward Noor and Odile and the plan's next hinge, wearing the disgust like armor, and it fit him perfectly, and he hated that most of all.

At the top of the stair, Noor was waiting, and read his face in half a second the way they read a dish.

"It worked," they said.

"It worked."

Noor nodded slowly. "Then we move tonight. Because Odile freed two stars an hour ago," they added, conversationally, terribly, "and the third one screamed loud enough to wake the Ledger Keeper."

Far below them, through the bones of the mountain, something vast began to stir—and every lamp in the cavern city guttered at once, as if the whole court had drawn a single breath and held it.
