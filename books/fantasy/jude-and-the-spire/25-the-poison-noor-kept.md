## 25. The Poison Noor Kept

The lantern-vaults had a way of making time feel like syrup—slow, golden, and slightly suffocating. Noor felt it most in the hours after leaving Wren, when their sister's fragmented voice still echoed in their skull like a bell rung underwater. *Rain,* Wren had whispered. *It sounds like rain.* And then her attention had drifted again, dimming back into the work of tending soul-fire, and Noor had walked away with their heart in pieces small enough to fit through the bars of a lantern cage.

They found the others in the alcove behind the tribute-bearers' quarters—a cramped space that smelled of cold stone and someone else's unwashed ambition. Sable was asleep sitting up, which Noor suspected was less a skill and more a habit earned through twenty years of drinking himself horizontal wherever he happened to be. Odile sat cross-legged with her charts spread around her like a fortune-teller's cards, muttering measurements to a sky she couldn't see. Aurel stood at the entrance with his arms folded, wearing the expression of a man who had recently learned his entire inheritance was a lie and was still deciding how offended to be.

Noor closed the curtain of sacking behind them.

"We need to talk," they said. "And I'd rather you were all sitting down for it. Sable, wake up. If you fall over mid-conversation I'm not catching you."

Sable cracked one eye. "I've been threatened by wardens all day. Your threats are a holiday."

"Sit down anyway."

Odile gathered her charts without being asked. She had developed, over the past two days underground, an instinct for when Noor's voice went flat—the particular flatness of a person who has rehearsed something until the rehearsal wore grooves in them. "What did you find?" she asked quietly. "In the deep vaults. You came back different."

"I found out what I've actually been doing for twenty years." Noor lowered themselves onto an overturned crate, knees loose, hands open on their thighs—an old taster's posture, designed to keep the body from tensing before bad news arrived. "You all know I built my immunity deliberately. Poison by poison, year by year, so I could survive the court. That's the story. The tidy one."

Aurel's eyes narrowed. He had spent his whole life around people whose stories had tidy versions, and he had learned to distrust the tidiness on principle. "And the untidy version?"

"The immunity was never only for the court." Noor reached beneath their coat, into the inner lining where a flat leather case had lived against their ribs since before any of them had met. It came out heavier than its size suggested. Everything true did. "There's a venom called hollowroot. Grows in the flooded caves under the Spire, where the old mine shafts meet the waterline. The mountain court's own servants used to take it—back when the court still bothered to hide what it was. A living body can't cross the soul-barrier. The barrier reads breath and heartbeat as a locked door. But hollowroot doesn't kill you if you take it slowly, in sips, over years. It teaches your body a new language. It makes you *less alive* in the specific ways the barrier cares about."

Odile had gone very still. Not frightened-still. Astronomer-still—the stillness of someone watching something impossible move across a chart. "You've been taking it."

"Since I was nineteen. A drop at a time, hidden in my evening tea. The same discipline as the poisons, just pointed somewhere else." Noor turned the case over in their hands. Inside, nested in waxed linen, sat six small vials of liquid so dark it seemed to drink the vault-light rather than reflect it. "Everyone assumed I was preparing to survive the palace. I was preparing for this. For the ledger, the barrier, the vault where they keep the contracts. For Wren." Their voice caught on the name and they let it, because hiding it would cost more than showing it. "I have spent twenty years getting ready to walk through a door no living person can walk through."

Sable had stopped pretending to be asleep. He was staring at the vials with the face of a man doing arithmetic he didn't want finished. "That's not how hollowroot works," he said hoarsely. "I've heard of it. Everyone who worked wards heard rumors of it. The slow dose keeps you functional, yes—but the full dose, all at once—"

"—burns the tolerance clean out of me," Noor said. "All of it. Hollowroot and every other resistance I've built, gone in a single night. My body will forget every lesson it ever learned. Every poison I can shrug off today becomes lethal tomorrow. One badly cooked mushroom. One careless cook. One drop of anything, in anything, ever again." They smiled, and it was the smile they used at the prince's breakfast table, steady and warm and worth absolutely nothing. "I'd go from the safest mouth in Vespergate to the most fragile one."

The silence that followed had texture. It pressed against the walls of the alcove.

"That's—" Aurel began, and stopped, and started again, which Noor had never seen him do. Princes were trained never to start sentences twice. "You're telling me you intend to drink those. All of them. Tonight? Before dawn?"

"Before the third dawn. We have time. Not much, but some." Noor held up a hand before anyone could speak. "Before you say anything—and I can see all four of you loading something to say—I want you to understand the arithmetic. The Ledger Keeper holds my signature now. If I don't bring back proof of consent from the contract room, or if I fail, my soul forfeits. But a dead body can't sign anything, and a living body can't reach the room. Hollowroot is the only key that fits both locks. I didn't choose this path last week. I chose it twenty years ago, standing over Wren's empty grave, deciding that someday I would walk into the dark and get her back."

"Twenty years," Sable repeated. His voice had gone strange. "You were nineteen. I testified when you were—" He stopped. His hands, which usually shook with wine, went still instead, which was somehow worse. "You started building a coffin for yourself the same year I helped put your sister in hers."

"You built a door," Noor corrected quietly. "Whether you meant to or not. I decided to build the key." They looked at each of them in turn. "I'm not telling you this to ask permission. I'm telling you because you'll be guarding the barrier while I'm inside, and you deserve to know exactly what you're guarding, and why."

Odile unfolded from her cross-legged position slowly, the way she moved when she was thinking faster than her body could follow. "The mathematics of it are elegant," she said, almost to herself. "Tolerance is a debt paid in installments. You've paid twenty years of them. The full dose calls in the loan—all at once, with interest." She looked up, and her eyes were bright and terrible with understanding. "But Noor. After tonight, you'll be *ordinary*. Ordinary bodies die from ordinary things. You'll taste the prince's breakfast and a single almond could stop your heart."

"Yes."

"And you did all this—" Aurel's voice had dropped into something raw, "—knowing that surviving the mountain meant becoming breakable forever?"

"I knew that surviving anything means becoming breakable eventually." Noor tucked the case away, back against their ribs, where it had warmed to the shape of them. "I spent two decades being unkillable and completely alone. I've had three days of being terrified alongside people who argue with me about it, and I'll tell you honestly: I prefer this. Even the arguing. Especially the arguing."

"Glad to be of service," Sable muttered, but his eyes were wet, and he turned his face toward the wall so nobody would count it against him.

* * *

They did not, of course, simply accept it. Noor had known they wouldn't—that was why they'd rehearsed. What they hadn't anticipated was how the objections would arrive: not as a wall but as weather, drifting in from every direction at once.

Sable took the practical route first. "Let me test it," he said, cornering Noor by the water cistern an hour later. "One vial. On me. I'll tell you exactly what it does, how fast, how bad—"

"It would kill you before you finished describing the symptoms. Twenty years of installments, Sable. You'd be trying to pay a mortgage with pocket change." Noor kept filling their cup. "Besides, if it killed you, who would carry me when my legs stop working? Because they will. I've read the accounts. The full dose drops a body like a felled tree. Someone has to drag me back up those shafts."

"I'm sixty years old and built like a soaked sack of turnips."

"You're also the strongest person I know, and we both know I'm including your drinking arm."

He laughed despite himself—a wet, cracked sound—and then pressed his forehead briefly against the cold stone beside Noor's head, the closest thing to an embrace either of them trusted themselves to manage. "Twenty years," he whispered. "You were a child. You should have been angry at the world. Instead you were doing chemistry homework for your own funeral."

"Some of us grieve productively."

"Some of us grieve terrifyingly, is what you mean."

Odile took the scholarly route, which Noor had expected even more. She appeared at midnight with her charts and a candle stub and laid them out on the floor between them like evidence at a trial. "Show me the dosage," she demanded. "Not the amount—the *timing*. When did you take your last sip?"

"Three days ago. Night the stars went out. I remember thinking it might matter."

"Then your blood concentration is already near threshold. The full dose won't be six times your maintenance level—it'll be barely more than double." Her fingers moved across her figures, quick and hungry. "Do you understand what that means? The margin is thinner than you think. If you mismeasured even once in twenty years—if a single evening's tea was stronger than the rest—you could be closer to the edge than your notes suggest."

"I weighed everything," Noor said. "On scales I calibrated myself. I'm a food taster, Odile. Precision is the one prayer I've ever been fluent in."

She sat back on her heels, studying them with that terrible tenderness scientists reserve for phenomena they cannot save. "You know the worst part?" she said softly. "If anyone else had done this—anyone—I'd call it madness. But you've checked every variable I can name and half the ones I can't. You've left nothing to chance except the chance itself." She blew out the candle. "That's the part I can't chart. Be careful with the part I can't chart."

Aurel took the royal route last, which surprised Noor least of all, though the shape of it surprised them completely.

He came to them alone, near the false dawn, when the cavern city's soul-lanterns burned lowest and everyone else had finally run out of arguments to lose. He didn't sit. He stood over Noor like a man delivering a verdict he hated.

"I forbid it," he said.

Noor raised an eyebrow. "You're welcome to try. Do you have wardens down here? Because I seem to recall we stole you."

"I am the crown of Vespergate, and I forbid—"

"Aurel." Noor's voice was gentle, and gentleness, they had learned, disarmed princes faster than defiance ever had. "Your father signed Wren's contract. Your grandfather probably signed others. Your crown bought twenty years of my silence and my sister's soul along with it. If you want to spend your authority anywhere down here, spend it finding another way through that barrier. The moment you do, I'll happily stand aside and let someone else drink the poison."

The prince opened his mouth. Closed it. In the low light, for a moment, he looked very young—not the polished courtier, not the sleepless king-in-waiting, but a boy who had inherited debts he couldn't read in a script he'd never been taught. "I could order you," he said finally, without conviction.

"You could," Noor agreed. "And I'd obey, right up until the second you fell asleep, which we both know happens roughly never. Then I'd pour the tea myself." They patted the crate beside them. "Sit down. Arguing with you is more fun when you're at eye level."

To their mild astonishment, he sat.

For a while neither spoke. The soul-lanterns breathed their captive light across the stone, and somewhere far off, water dripped in the rhythm of a clock counting down hours none of them wanted to name.

"My mother used to sing," Aurel said at last, to the darkness rather than to Noor. "Badly. Court musicians wept. She sang anyway, every morning, through the whole palace." His jaw tightened. "When the stars went out, I realized I'd forgotten the tune. Twenty years, and it slipped out of me somewhere between lessons and councils and learning to wear a crown like armor." He looked at Noor then, and there was nothing princely in his face at all—only the naked envy of a man watching someone else refuse to forget. "You kept the tune. Twenty years of poison, and you never let the song slip."

"Somebody had to hold it," Noor said. "Wren was busy."

The laugh that escaped him was startled and rough-edged, and it broke whatever remained of the argument. He reached out—hesitated—and then, with the gravity of a man signing a treaty, rested his hand briefly on Noor's shoulder. "Come back," he said. "Whatever it costs. Come back and teach me the song."

Noor covered his hand with their own, once, and let go. "Bring the others to the barrier at moonrise," they said. "And Aurel—whatever you hear from me on the other side, however long it takes—don't come in after me. There's no second key. I made sure there was only ever one."

"Why would you make sure of that?"

"Because if there were two, one of you kind, stubborn, impossible people would use it."

* * *

They drank it alone, because some doors you have to open by yourself.

Noor chose the little antechamber off the barrier corridor, where the stone was smooth and the soul-light came through a crack in the wall like dawn through shutters. They set out the six vials in a row and allowed themselves, for one indulgent moment, to admire the craftsmanship of their own obsession—twenty years of evenings distilled into six small decisions.

Their hands did not shake. That was the strangest part. Twenty years of tasting death in thimblefuls had taught their body a courtesy: it did not tremble before poison; it waited to be introduced.

"One last toast," they murmured to the empty room, and lifted the first vial to the crack of light. "To useful and unremarkable. It kept me alive." They tipped it back. "But it was never going to keep me *human*."

The hollowroot tasted of cold iron and winter wells and, faintly, impossibly, of rain on a shop roof.

By the third vial, the world had begun to thin at its edges, going soft as wet ink. By the fifth, Noor could hear their own heartbeat slowing—not failing, just *lowering its voice*, politely, the way one does entering a library. They lay back against the stone and thought about Wren's face when she'd whispered *rain*, and about four impossible people waiting beyond a barrier that was already beginning to mistake Noor for mist.

The sixth vial was empty before they noticed lifting it.

The last thing Noor saw, before the world folded down to candlelight and ink, was the crack of light in the wall—and the shadow of a figure standing in it, refusing, despite everything, to leave.

"Noor." Odile's voice, distant, urgent, cracking. "Your pulse—it's—"

Noor tried to say *the barrier will hold me safe*, tried to say *don't come in*, tried to say the twenty-year-old sentence they had practiced ten thousand evenings—

And stepped through the door.
