## 26. Arguments at the Threshold

Odile Ferrant had spent her whole life arguing with the sky. The sky never answered, which she had always considered a professional courtesy. People, it turned out, were far less polite.

The safehouse was a collapsed cistern two levels below the lantern-vaults, chosen by Sable because its dead wards made it invisible to the mountain's sweepers and by no one else because it was damp, cold, and smelled of old mineral water and older regret. They had been there six hours. In that time Odile had charted the tremor-cycle of the mountain's breathing on three slate tablets, eaten half a brick of travel bread, and listened to her companions have the same argument four times.

They were now beginning the fifth.

"You are not drinking that," Sable said, for the fourth time, in the tone of a man who had said it four times and intended to keep saying it until the words wore a groove in the air deep enough to live in.

"I am," Noor said, for the fourth time, in the tone of a person stating that water was wet and the mountain was dark and other well-established facts.

"It will burn out your immunity. All of it. Twenty years of—"

"Yes."

"—and you'll be *ordinary*—"

"Thank the stars I'm about to free," Noor said, and even Odile, who did not consider herself a humorous person but was frequently told she was a funny one against her will, felt her mouth twitch.

It was not funny, though. That was the problem. Nothing about this was funny, and Odile had discovered somewhere between the star-vaults and the warden sirens that when nothing is funny, people reach for humor anyway the way drowning men reach for driftwood, and then they hold onto it too tightly and it becomes something sharp.

She looked at the vial.

It sat on an upturned crate between them like a small black verdict. The poison Noor had been sipping for twenty years, drop by patient drop, building a tolerance the way a mason builds a wall—one stone at a time, in secret, for a purpose they would not name until tonight. Taken in full dose, Sable explained—and he had explained it twice more since they'd arrived, as if repetition might change the chemistry—it would saturate the body so completely that the soul-barrier at the deep vault would slide past it without gripping. A living body could walk through. A living body could walk back out.

And the immunity would be gone. Not paused. Gone. Every poison Noor's blood had ever learned to defeat would be a stranger again, and strangers, in Vespergate, killed tasters.

Odile had done the arithmetic. She always did the arithmetic. It was how she loved things: by counting them.

Twenty years of drops. Perhaps ten thousand sips. A discipline so long and so quiet that Noor had built it inside their own body without telling a single soul, and now they meant to spend all of it in one night, on a sister who didn't remember their face.

"We could find another way," Aurel said from the shadows near the cistern's far arch, where he had been standing for an hour doing his brooding-in-silhouette routine, which Odile suspected he practiced. "There has to be another way through the barrier. A key. A bribe. I'm a prince; bribes are practically my native language."

"The barrier doesn't take bribes," Sable said. "I helped design its grandmother, back when I still had a reputation and both my hands steady. It takes weight. Soul-weight or chemical weight. There's no third door." He rubbed his face with both hands. His beard had gone fully grey sometime during the last two days; Odile had watched it happen in increments, like a very sad time-lapse. "Believe me. If there were a third door, I'd have built it myself out of spite."

"Then we go over the wall," Aurel said.

"There is no wall. It's a *law*, not a fence—"

"Then we break the law. I'm good at that now. It's been a very educational week."

"Aurel." Noor's voice was gentle in a way that Odile had learned to recognize as dangerous—the gentleness of a person who has already decided and is merely being kind about it. "Sit down."

"I don't want to sit down. Sitting down feels like agreeing."

"That's because you usually agree while sitting down. You're very agreeable seated. It's your standing posture that causes diplomatic incidents."

Despite everything, the prince laughed—a short, cracked sound, like a hinge that needed oiling—and sat down on a broken pipe next to Odile. Up close she could see what the mountain court had done to him in three days: he'd arrived polished and sleepless, and now he was simply sleepless, the polish scoured off, a young man in borrowed minister's robes with ash on his cuffs. She found she preferred him this way. Polished things told you nothing. Scratched things told you everything.

* * *

The argument might have ended there, if Sable had been a man who let arguments end.

He wasn't. Odile had known him for three days and she could already tell: Sable Ives treated a finished argument the way other men treated an unfinished drink, with visible distress and a compulsion to order another round.

"No," he said again, standing up, pacing. The cistern floor was slick and he paced it anyway, with the confidence of a man whose relationship with falling was long and complicated. "No. I've been thinking. While you were all sleeping—which none of you did, I heard you, you breathe like conspirators—"

"We *are* conspirators," Odile pointed out.

"—while you were all *not* sleeping, I've been thinking, and there's an alternative." He stopped pacing. He stood very straight, the way he must have stood once, in a courtroom, giving testimony. Odile's stomach went cold, because she recognized the posture. People only stood like that when they were about to say something they'd rehearsed.

"Sable," Noor said quietly.

"Hear me out."

"No."

"*Hear me out.*" His voice cracked on it. "The barrier takes weight. Right? Equal weight. That's the law of the thing—that's what the Ledger Keeper said, that's what the charter says, everything down here runs on balance, on payment, on *weight*. So it doesn't have to be your twenty years. It just has to be a life offered freely. Freely, Noor. That's the term. And mine—" he spread his arms, and the gesture took in the damp, the dark, the dead wards, the whole ruined architecture of his last fifteen years— "mine is *right there*. Unspent. Nobody's using it. I've been keeping it in storage like a widow keeps a wedding dress."

Nobody moved.

Somewhere far above, water dripped from the vaults with the patience of a clock.

"You testified against Wren," Noor said. Their voice was perfectly level. Odile, who had spent her career reading light where other people read faces, saw the levelness for what it was: a held breath. "You stood up in front of the wardens' tribunal and you lied, or you half-lied, or you told the truth badly—I've never decided which is worst—and my sister died for it. And your offer is to die for me instead. As if that settles anything. As if death is a currency that converts."

"I know what I did."

"Do you?" Noor rose. They were smaller than Sable—they were smaller than everyone; Odile sometimes thought the whole conspiracy was organized around protecting one small, stubborn person—but the room rearranged itself around them anyway. "Because dying is easy, Sable. Dying is *one afternoon*. Living with it is the hard part, and you've barely started. Fifteen years drunk isn't penance. It's anesthesia. And I am not going to be the thing that finally puts you under. I won't carry that. I've carried enough."

"I'm offering—"

"I know exactly what you're offering. You're offering to make yourself feel clean. That's not a gift, Sable. That's a bill you're trying to hand to someone else."

Sable's arms came down slowly. For a moment he looked, Odile thought, like a building after a demolition—not collapsed, exactly, but emptied, all the rooms suddenly open to the sky.

Then, being Sable, he tried again. "Odile," he said, turning to her, desperate, recruiting. "Tell them. Tell them the math. One life offered versus twenty years of preparation—if anyone respects preparation—"

"Don't drag me into your suicide auction," Odile said.

"It's not an *auction*—"

"It's absolutely an auction. It's the worst-run auction I've ever attended. Three bidders, one item, and nobody willing to hear the reserve price." She stood, brushing damp off her robes, because if she was going to referee this she was going to do it on her feet, like a lecturer. "Since you've invoked the math, let's do the math. Sable: if you take the dose, unprepared, unsensitized, you die in the barrier. Not metaphorically. Your body stops. The barrier takes your weight and gives back nothing, because Wren's contract isn't yours to void—you're not blood. You'd be a coin dropped down a well. Noor: if Noor takes the dose, prepared, the body passes through, the contract gets voided by the only consent that counts, and one sister walks out of this mountain instead of zero. Aurel: if *you* take the dose, the crown loses its heir, the city loses the only person positioned to dismantle the tribute system from the inside, and your mother's soul stays caged forever because, again, not blood. Have I missed anyone?"

Silence.

"Good," said Odile. "I hadn't missed anyone. I never miss anyone. It's my single most annoying quality and I've made peace with it."

* * *

But the argument didn't end, because arguments between people who love each other never end at the point of logic. Logic is just the lobby. The real rooms are elsewhere.

Aurel got up again. Of course he did.

"My mother," he said. "Her soul is down here. Somewhere in these vaults, there's a woman who sang me to sleep, and she's been a lamp for nineteen years." His voice did something complicated in the middle of the sentence and he pushed through it. "If Noor can walk through that barrier, then someone should walk through with them. Someone should stand beside them when they find the Ledger Keeper's true form. Someone should—"

"Noor should," Noor said. "That's the terms. One staker. One claimant. You read the charter, Aurel. The bargain doesn't allow witnesses."

"The bargain doesn't allow *anything*. We've been breaking it since we climbed down the mine shafts—"

"And every time we broke it, someone paid. Sable's in a warden cell's future. Odile's stars are scattered across three vaults. I'm not adding your soul to the invoice." Noor crossed to him, and Odile watched them do something she had no framework for: they put a hand on the prince's shoulder, briefly, the way you'd steady a piece of equipment you couldn't afford to lose. "Your job is topside. It was always topside. The seal, the council, the truth. You can't buy dawn from down here."

"I hate it when you're reasonable," Aurel muttered. "It makes me feel managed."

"You are managed. You're the most managed person in Vespergate. Somebody has to do it and apparently it's me."

The prince laughed again, that cracked-hinge sound, and Odile realized with a small internal start that she was watching something being built. Right there, in a dripping cistern, between a taster and a prince and a disgraced smith and herself: not a plan—plans they had, plans they had too many of—but the thing underneath plans. The load-bearing walls.

Which was why what happened next hurt so much.

* * *

It was Odile herself who broke the truce. She'd like it noted, someday, in whatever record survived, that she knew exactly what she was doing.

"Show me the vial," she said.

Noor handed it over without hesitation, which was either trust or exhaustion, and with Noor it had always been impossible to tell the difference. Odile held it up to the light of their single surviving charm-lamp and did what she did best: she observed.

The liquid inside was nearly black, with a slow iridescence, like oil on rainwater. When she tilted it, it clung to the glass a half-second longer than water should. She uncorked it—carefully, with a technique she'd invented on the second day of knowing these people, holding things at arm's length as if they might be stars—and smelled it.

Bitter. Beneath the bitterness, faintly, impossibly: *plums.*

"You flavored it," she said.

Noor shrugged. "Twenty years is a long time to drink something vile."

"You *flavored your own poison* so the medicine would go down easier." Odile stared at them. "You absolute—of course you did. Of course. You've been preparing for this since before any of us knew there was a this. You've been preparing since the trial, haven't you. Since Wren died. Every dish you tasted, every drop you sipped, every year you stayed small and useful and invisible—it was all *for this night*. You built a key out of your own body."

"Yes," Noor said simply.

"And now you want to melt the key in the lock."

"Now I want to open the door."

Odile set the vial down on the crate with exaggerated care, because her hands wanted to do something less careful, and turned to face all three of them.

"Here is my problem," she said. "Professionally. I study things that are gone. Stars are gone before you see them—their light is a letter from the dead. I have spent thirty years loving objects that no longer exist. And I am looking at the only person in this mountain who knows where my stars are kept, and you want to walk into the dark and possibly not come back, and I—" She stopped. Recalibrated. Started again, quieter. "I have lost a sky. I am not losing a friend to the same darkness. It would be methodologically obscene."

"Odile—"

"Let me finish. I have one more observation." She picked up the vial again and held it out—not to Noor, but flat on her palm, between all of them, like evidence at a trial. "This took twenty years. Twenty years of quiet, unglamorous, invisible work, done drop by drop, with no guarantee it would ever matter. Do you know what that is? That's the most beautiful piece of scholarship I've ever seen. You are the finest experiment of my lifetime, Noor Nakamura, and I refuse to sign off on destroying the results before publication."

"That's not—" Sable began.

"It's a metaphor. Let me have it. I've earned one metaphor."

But Noor was looking at her with an expression Odile couldn't chart, and then at the vial, and then at Sable, and Aurel, and something in their face was doing what Odile's stars did: burning steadily, at a fixed distance, impossible to reach and impossible to look away from.

"I spent twenty years getting ready," Noor said.

The words came out quiet, but they landed in the cistern like stones in a well.

"Not for glory. Not to be brave. I'm not brave—you've all seen me flinch at soup. I got ready because I couldn't save her, and I couldn't bring her back, and I couldn't even *grieve* properly, because grieving in public gets mages hanged. So I grieved in chemistry. Drop by drop. For twenty years I carried a debt I couldn't pay, and I built the payment into my own blood, and I told myself: someday. Someday it will mean something." They closed their hand around the vial. "Tonight it means something. Tonight it means *her*. If you take this from me—if any of you take this from me—you're not saving my life. You're stealing the only meaning I've ever made. And I have given up enough invisible things. I will not give up this one."

The drip of water counted five seconds. Ten.

"So," Noor added, with the small dry tilt that was, Odile had learned, the closest thing they had to a smile. "Anyone who wants to fight me about it should know I've been poisoned on purpose for two decades and I have *excellent* reflexes."

* * *

Afterward—or in the middle of afterward; Odile wasn't sure the argument ever truly ended, it just changed rooms—she found herself sitting beside Sable on the broken pipe, sharing the travel bread neither of them wanted.

"She's right," he said miserably. "About the anesthesia. About all of it."

"I know. I ran the numbers."

"That's not what I meant." He tore the bread in half and gave her the bigger piece, which she noted, filed, and appreciated. "When I testified, I told myself it was true. What I said about Wren. I half-believed it. And the worst part—the part I can't drink away anymore—is that believing it was *easier*. Truth was harder, so I chose the lie, and a woman died, and I chose comfort over and over for fifteen years and called it survival." He looked across the cistern to where Noor sat with the vial, turning it over in their fingers, talking low with Aurel about timings and alibis. "And now they're choosing the hard thing. First try. Like it's nothing."

"It's not nothing," Odile said. "Look at their hands."

He looked. So did she.

Noor's hands were not quite steady. Anyone else wouldn't have seen it—the taster's discipline was magnificent, a cathedral of control—but Odile had spent her life measuring tiny motions across impossible distances, and she could see the fine tremor, the held breath living in the knuckles. Noor was terrified. Noor had been terrified for twenty years, probably, and had simply declined to let terror vote.

"They're going to do it," Sable said. "At dawn. Aren't they."

"At dawn," Odile agreed. "The barrier's breathing cycle bottoms out. I charted it." She hesitated, then reached into her robe and produced the thing she had been carrying for two days without quite admitting why: a scrap of starlight, freed from a shattered lantern in the vaults, folded into a twist of black cloth where it glowed like a coal wrapped in night. She'd caught it without thinking, the way astronomers catch everything—reflexively, hungrily, helplessly.

"What's that?"

"A letter from the living, for once." She pressed it into Sable's hand. "For after. When they come back through the barrier, if the dark is bad—if the crossing takes something out of them—light this. Starlight remembers people. It's the one thing I know for certain that I shouldn't, and I've never tested it, and I'm asking you to test it at the worst possible moment, because that's how all my best science happens."

Sable closed his fist around it, and the glow leaked between his fingers and lit his face from below, and for one moment he looked less like a ruin and more like a smith again—someone who made things that held.

Across the cistern, Noor corked the vial one final time and tucked it into their coat, over the heart, where Wren's luck-knot already lived. Two talismans. One for luck, one for leaving.

Dawn was four hours away, and the mountain above them breathed its slow, hungry breath, and somewhere in the deep vaults a Ledger Keeper waited with a page that bore a father's signature, and Odile Ferrant, astronomer without a sky, sat in the dark with two men she'd known for three days and loved with the unreasonable speed of people who have almost died together, and did the only mathematics left to her.

She counted the hours. She counted the heartbeats. She resolved to count them all the way to the threshold, and to keep counting until Noor walked back out of the dark—

—or until there was nothing left to count but starlight.

Above them, faintly, through forty feet of stone, the mountain exhaled.
