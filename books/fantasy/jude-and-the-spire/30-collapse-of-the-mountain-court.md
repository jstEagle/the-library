## 30. Collapse of the Mountain Court

Odile Ferrant had spent her whole life believing that the sky was the largest thing there was.

She had been wrong.

The mountain court was coming apart like a book whose spine had been cut, and the size of it—the sheer *scale* of the unraveling—made her feel like a woman standing inside a clock as it decided, gear by gear, to stop being a clock at all.

It began with light.

She was in the lantern-vault when the first contract tore. She felt it before she heard it: a change in the pressure of the dark, the way a sailor feels weather in his teeth. Then somewhere far above—farther than any distance she could name, because distance down here was measured in bargains and not in feet—a sound rolled through the cavern city like a bell being unmade.

And every caged star in the vault flared at once.

"Oh," Odile said, to no one, to everyone, to the vault itself. "Oh, you *beautiful* fools."

Because she understood it immediately, the way she understood eclipses—not slowly, not with effort, but all at once, whole. Noor had voided the charter. And the contracts were chained—one soul sold to buy another ward, that ward's power pledged against another soul, and on and on, twenty years deep, a hundred years deep, an architecture of debt so vast that pulling out the first stone meant the whole cathedral of it would come down.

Chain-wise, Sable had called it once, over cheap wine in the ruined observatory, gesturing with a stub of charcoal. *You don't steal back one page from a ledger. You make the ledger eat itself.*

He'd been right. The ledger was eating itself, and it was screaming.

The nearest star-lantern shuddered in its cage of black iron. Odile had spent hours on these cages already—three stars freed, each unlocking taking longer than the last, each star dimmer, more confused, more *bruised*. But this one didn't wait for her picks. It pushed. From the inside. The iron groaned, split along a seam she hadn't even known was there, and light poured out like water finding its old riverbed.

The star did not speak. Stars, as far as Odile could tell, did not speak. But it *knew*, somehow, which way was up, and it went—streaming toward the ceiling of the cavern, up into the throat of the old mine shaft, dragging a tail of brightness behind it like a comet remembering what comets were for.

Then the next cage broke. Then the next.

"Go," Odile told herself firmly, because part of her wanted very badly to stay and watch every single one go free, and the other part of her remembered that caverns full of collapsing contracts tended to drop rocks on astronomers. "Catalogue later. *Run* now."

She ran.

* * *

Sable Ives felt the mountain die the way he felt wards die—which is to say, in his hands.

They were aching. They had been aching since the descent, ever since the dead wards in the mine shafts had twitched under his palms like old nerves remembering pain. But now the ache turned into something else, a high singing frequency that lived in his knuckles and his teeth, and he stopped halfway across the tribute bridge and gripped the railing and laughed, actually laughed, a wild unlovely bark of a laugh that echoed off the cavern walls.

"What?" Aurel asked, half a step behind him, hand already on his sword. The prince had learned fast; three days underground and he moved like a man who'd grown up in alleys instead of palaces. "What's funny?"

"The wards." Sable held up his hands. They were shaking. He let them. "Feel that? That's not them failing. That's them *quitting*. Every ward in this mountain was built on the same stolen foundation, and Noor just pulled the foundation out, and now—" he spread his fingers, watching them tremble with something that wasn't fear anymore, hadn't been fear for a whole minute now, "—now they're all resigning at once. Twenty years I built things on lies. Twenty years, and the cure is one taster with a grudge and a pen."

Above them, the great chains that held the lantern-constellations began to sway with nothing pushing them.

Aurel looked up. So did Sable. And for a moment neither of them spoke, because the cavern city's false sky—the ceiling hung with caged stars in patterns some long-dead courtier had probably thought were *tasteful*—was beginning to empty. One by one, then ten by ten, lights were tearing loose and streaming upward into the mine mouths, and the shadows they left behind were enormous.

"It looks," Aurel said quietly, "like the night my mother died. The sky going out piece by piece."

"It's the opposite of that," Sable said. "Watch. It's going back *in*."

Something crashed behind them—far away, deep in the ministerial quarter—and then a sound Sable knew from bad dreams: the particular groan of masonry built without honesty deciding to be honest about it. A tower of pale stone folded inward like a man sitting down.

"We need Wren," Sable said. "We need Noor. We need to not be here. In roughly that order."

* * *

Noor Nakamura had found their sister in a collapsing vault, and getting her out was like carrying a lamp made of smoke.

Wren walked. That was the miracle Noor kept returning to, the small true thing they held onto while everything else came apart: Wren walked, put one foot after the other, her hand cold in Noor's grip. But she weighed almost nothing. Twenty years of being burned as fuel had left her the way a candle is left—shaped like herself, translucent at the edges, light coming through where substance used to be.

"Noor?" she'd whispered, once, in the vault, when the rain-song finally landed. Just the name. Just that. Noor had decided it was enough to last a lifetime, and if the lifetime ended tonight, so be it.

Now the vaults were shedding their ceilings, and the soul-fire that lit the corridors was guttering as its fuel—freed, fleeing, *leaving*—rose away from it, and Noor ran through the flickering dark with their sister's arm over their shoulder and thought, with the strange clarity of the poisoned: *I have maybe an hour of legs left in me. Use them well.*

The venom was awake now. Noor had taken the full dose an hour before dawn of the third day, alone, in a side chamber, the way you take medicine: quickly, without ceremony, eyes closed. It had tasted like nothing at all, which was somehow worse. And now it was working—Noor could feel it working, could feel the barrier between living flesh and the deep places of the world going soft around them, like wax, like a door left open. It was also, unmistakably, burning through twenty years of immunity like fire through a library. Every hour, something in them got quieter. Their heartbeat had developed opinions. Their vision kept offering to trade sharpness for silver.

But Wren's hand was in theirs. That was the arithmetic. Everything else was rounding errors.

"There!" Odile's voice, ahead, echoing down the tributary corridor. The astronomer appeared around a bend, hair full of dust, face lit from below by a rising star, looking like a woman who had personally invented joy and was still deciding whether to patent it. "The main shaft—it's open, the whole vein of it is lit, the stars are going up like—like—"

"Like stars," Noor said.

"Like stars," Odile agreed, laughing and crying about it. "I have waited my entire life to say something that stupid. Come *on*."

* * *

The main mine shaft had become a river running the wrong way.

That was how Odile's mind insisted on describing it, and she was an honest scientist, so she wrote it down that way later, in a journal that would be copied a hundred times: *The shaft was a river of light running upward.* Freed stars climbed past them in the dark—some slow, drifting like lantern-fish; some fast, streaking for the surface with two centuries of impatience. Their combined light turned the tunnel walls to gold and threw four small running shadows ahead of them, always ahead, like guides who knew the way better than anyone.

Sable led, because Sable had memorized the shafts the way guilty men memorize the roads to the scenes of their crimes. Aurel took the rear, sword drawn against wardens who mostly weren't coming—most of the mountain's servants had simply stopped, mid-stride, mid-sentence, when their own contracts voided, standing in the corridors like furniture waiting to be told what they were. One young warden they passed was weeping quietly and holding his badge in both hands, and Aurel paused beside him for half a second and said, "Go home. Whatever home is now. Go find out." And the warden went.

The mountain shook again. Dust came down in curtains. Somewhere below them, Odile knew, the Ledger Keeper's hall was folding in on itself, contracts unraveling into blank paper, blank paper becoming pulp, pulp becoming nothing. She tried to imagine what a creature stitched entirely from agreements became when the agreements died, and decided she didn't want to know, and was grateful, for once, that some questions would stay unanswered.

"How much farther?" Noor called forward. Their voice was wrong. Odile had noticed it in the vaults and hated that she'd noticed—too thin, too careful, the voice of someone spending breath like coins.

"Two bends," Sable shouted back. "Then the long climb. Then sky."

"Sky," said Wren.

Everyone stopped.

It was the first word she'd said since *Noor*. She said it the way you taste something you'd forgotten existed—slowly, suspiciously, hopefully. Her head tipped back, following a star as it streamed up past them into the dark above, and her face, that translucent, worn-thin face, did something complicated and young.

"Yes," Odile said gently. "Sky. Real ones. All of them. You'll see."

Wren looked at her—at the stranger with ink-stained fingers and dust in her hair—and asked, with terrible politeness, "Did you keep count? While they were gone?"

Odile's throat closed. Because of course this woman, this burned-down candle of a woman, had been told—had *known*—that the stars were missing. Had perhaps spent twenty years in the dark wondering if anyone up there missed them.

"I'm going to keep count now," Odile said. "Every single one. I promise you that. You'll get your sky back with receipts."

Something happened to Wren's mouth that had clearly once been a smile and was relearning the shape.

* * *

The long climb nearly killed them all, and did, briefly, kill Noor—or that was what it felt like from the outside, which was the only outside anyone had.

Halfway up the switchbacking shaft, between one star-stream and the next, Noor's legs quit. No announcement. No drama. They simply sat down on a ledge of wet rock with Wren still half in their arms and said, conversationally, to the middle distance, "Ah. There it is, then."

"No." Sable was already turning. "No, no, we're close, the air's changing, I can feel the draft—"

"Sable." Noor smiled at him, and it was the worst thing any of them had seen yet, because it was fond. "Get her up. Get her to the top. That was always the job."

"It was never the *whole* job—"

"*Sable.*"

What happened next, Odile would describe in her journal with a scientist's precision and a poet's failure: Sable Ives, disgraced twice-over, drunk for six years, testimony-taker and testimony-breaker, knelt down in the golden light of climbing stars and gathered Wren Nakamura into his arms like something rescued from a fire, and stood up, and started climbing.

"You," Wren said to him, faintly, from the crook of his shoulder.

"I know," Sable said. His voice cracked straight down the middle. "I know. Climb first. Hate me at the top. I'll wait. I'm good at waiting. It's practically my profession."

Aurel hauled Noor upright and got their arm over his royal shoulders, and the prince who had never carried anything heavier than a crown carried a poisoned taster up a mountainside by fits and starts, arguing the whole way, because Noor was still Noor.

"Left foot," Aurel ordered.

"That's my *bad* leg."

"All your legs are bad legs now. Left foot."

"You're very bossy for someone whose father sold souls."

"My father sold souls, my council lied for a century, and my kingdom's protection was a subscription service. I've earned a little bossiness. Left foot."

Odile brought up the rear with her journal clutched to her chest, cataloguing stars as they passed—*forty-one, forty-two, forty-three, that one's definitely Vega, oh, oh, it's really Vega*—and crying without any embarrassment whatsoever, because some data deserves tears.

* * *

They came out of the mountain's mouth into the last hour of night, onto the scree slope below the old minehead, and the sky was *busy*.

There was no other word for it. Stars were arriving. Odile watched them come up out of the earth behind them in long bright ribbons, wheel once, bewildered, and then find their places—an old constellation settling into itself like a dog turning three times before lying down. The patterns were imperfect. Some stars were missing still, or dim, or in the wrong seats entirely. It was the most beautiful sky Odile Ferrant had ever seen, and it was only half-mended, and she loved it more for that.

"Count," Wren whispered, from Sable's arms. Her eyes were wide, reflecting everything. "Start counting."

"One," said Odile, voice steady as a sextant. "Two. Three..."

They made it perhaps two hundred yards down the slope before the mountain spoke.

Not collapsed—*spoke*. A sound rose out of the minehead behind them, deep and vast and strange: half a roar, half a sigh, the sound of an entire hidden civilization's worth of bargains expiring at once. The scree jumped under their feet. Far below, through Vespergate's high windows, Odile could see lights kindling—people waking, people pouring into streets, people pointing up at a sky that had been empty for weeks and was now, visibly, gloriously, under new management.

And then, threading through the triumph like a wire through silk:

"Noor?"

Noor had slid out of Aurel's grip and was kneeling in the scree, and the silver was winning in their eyes now, and they were smiling up at the rekindling constellations with an expression Odile recognized from mirrors—the expression of someone checking a life's work and finding it, against all odds, finished.

"Wren's out," Noor said. "Stars are out. Contracts are ash." A pause. A small frown, as if consulting a ledger only they could read. "Someone should probably catch me."

Three people moved at once. None of them were fast enough.

Behind them, at the minehead, something that was not quite a voice and not quite wind sighed out of the dark: the last thread of the Ledger Keeper, unraveling, and whether it was gratitude or a curse or simply the sound of a door closing forever, none of them could say. But the stars overhead flared—all of them, all at once, every star in the sky, one great indrawn breath of light—

—and Noor Nakamura fell face-forward into the scree, and did not move.
