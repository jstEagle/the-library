## 31. The Long Climb Out

Sable Ives had spent thirty years learning the weight of things. The weight of a ward-stone in his palm—about as much as a sleeping cat. The weight of a testimony given badly—more than any stone ever forged. And now, climbing out of the dying mountain with Wren Nakamura's soul slung across his back like a sack of wet wool, he learned the weight of a debt paid in flesh.

It was heavier than all of it.

"Left," Noor rasped from somewhere behind him. "The shaft forks left."

"You said right last time."

"I was wrong last time."

"You were *right* last time," Odile corrected, her voice floating up from below like a woman discussing weather at the bottom of a well. "You're wrong now. The left fork is collapsed. I can see the collapse. It is very visibly collapsed."

Noor didn't answer. That was the thing that scared Sable most—not the tremors running through the rock, not the distant sound of the mountain court coming apart like a knot pulled loose strand by strand, but the silence where Noor's voice should have been. Twenty years of knowing them only as a rumor and a charm-seller's signature, and in three days Sable had learned to read their voice the way he once read ward-lines: by its absence.

He shifted Wren higher on his back. She weighed almost nothing, which was somehow worse. A soul torn free of its contract should have been heavy, he thought. It should have dragged him down through the floor of the world. Instead she was light as an unlit lantern, her head lolling against his shoulder, her breath making small fog-shapes in the dark.

"Talk to me," he said to her, because Noor had told him once—three days ago, in the dark of the mine shafts, when he'd confessed what he'd done—that talking helped. "Wren. Your brother—your sibling—" He stumbled over it even now. "Noor says you used to sing on the shop roof. Badly, they claim. They're lying, probably. Noor lies professionally. It's their whole trade."

A faint sound against his neck. Not words. But something.

"Right," Sable said, and turned right.

* * *

The mountain was dying around them, and it died loudly.

Every contract voided had sent a shudder through the rock, and there had been so many contracts. Sable could feel them going the way he'd once felt wards failing—a high thin note in his back teeth, a wrongness in the air like a held breath. Somewhere above, stars were streaming up through the old veins of the mountain, freed after centuries in their cages, and each one that passed overhead lit the shafts for a heartbeat: silver, then gold, then a blue so deep it looked like drowning.

In those heartbeats of light, Sable counted his companions like a miser counting coins.

Odile Ferrant, climbing with ink-stained fingers and an expression of furious joy, occasionally stopping to sketch a freed star's trajectory on her own forearm because she'd run out of paper two days ago. Aurel, prince of Vespergate, in borrowed miner's clothes that fit him like an insult, moving with the careful grace of someone who had never once been asked to climb anything. And Noor—

Noor was failing.

Sable had watched poison work before. He'd been a ward-smith; half his colleagues had died of fume-sickness, and he knew the progression: the steadiness first, then the sweat, then the way a body started negotiating with itself, trading one function for another. Noor had taken the full dose twelve hours ago. They were past negotiation now. They climbed on will alone, one hand on the rock, gray-faced, lips nearly blue, and every time the starlight flashed Sable saw them measuring the distance to the next handhold with the expression of someone doing arithmetic they already knew would come out short.

"Stop," Sable said, for the fourth time that hour.

"We don't stop," Noor said, for the fourth time.

"Your hands are shaking."

"My hands have been shaking since chapter one of my life. Climb."

"I can carry you too."

That got a laugh out of them—a real one, cracked and awful, more cough than humor. "And what will you carry me with? Your guilt? There isn't room. You've packed it all around Wren already."

"Guilt packs light," Sable said. "I've traveled with it for twenty years."

Noor opened their mouth—to argue, Sable assumed, because arguing was the last thing they had strength for—and then their knees simply quit. No drama. No cry. One moment they were upright, the next they were folding down against the shaft wall like a coat slipping off a hook, and Sable was moving before his mind caught up, catching them under the arms, lowering them gently to the stone.

"Noor." He patted their cheek. It was cold. "Noor. Hey. You don't get to do this here. This is terrible geography for dying. There's no view."

Their eyes focused on him slowly, with enormous effort, and there was fury in them, which reassured him more than anything else could have. Fury meant alive.

"Wren," they whispered.

"On my back. Breathing. Annoyed, probably, that you're stealing attention."

"Take her out." Their hand found his wrist with a grip far stronger than the rest of them. "Take her out first. Don't wait for me. Promise."

"I promise nothing," Sable said. "I'm a known liar. Ask anyone. Ask your sister—oh, wait."

"*Sable.*"

"I know." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "I know what you're asking. And I'm telling you: I spent twenty years paying interest on a debt, and this—" he hauled Wren's weight higher, knelt, and began maneuvering Noor's arm across his shoulders, arranging them both, the living soul and the fading body, into something he could actually carry— "this is the principal. So shut up and let me be heavy."

* * *

He carried them both.

Not well. Not gracefully. There was no technique for it—he'd carried ward-stones up scaffolding for decades and thought himself strong, but stones didn't whisper, stones didn't go limp at the wrong moment, stones didn't murmur fragments of songs against the back of your neck in a voice that hadn't been used in twenty years. Wren had started humming somewhere in the last hour. Small, broken phrases, like a music box with water in it. Noor, half-conscious against his other side, hummed along whenever they had breath to spare, correcting the tune.

They harmonized in their sleep, or near it. Sable found his eyes stinging and blamed the dust.

Behind them, Odile narrated the climb in a low, steady stream—partly navigation, partly, he suspected, to keep everyone's minds off the arithmetic of how much mountain was still above them. "Fork ahead, take the right, the left one smells like sulfur and regret. Aurel, stop touching the walls, some of these dead wards still bite. Yes, that one just tried. Well done, you've discovered why we don't touch the walls."

"I was testing it," Aurel said with dignity.

"You were testing it with your *hand*."

"A scientist tests with what's available."

Ahead of them all, the shafts were filling with light. Not starlight now—the stars had mostly gone, streaming up and out toward the sky that had missed them—but the raw unbinding of the mountain court, contracts unraveling into nothing, and the nothing glowing. Sable had built wards his whole life and watched them fail for one long night, and he understood now, in his bones, what he'd only theorized before: that every ward in Vespergate had been a straw in a great slow drink, and the drinker lived beneath the mountain, and the drink had been people.

His people. His city. His testimony had helped bury a girl whose soul went into the cup.

He climbed faster.

* * *

The last shaft was called the Throat, according to Odile's charts, and it deserved the name. Narrow, near-vertical, with rusted iron rungs driven into rock that shuddered every few minutes like a throat clearing. At the top, a hundred feet up, a coin of gray daylight—the first real sky any of them had seen in three days.

"One at a time," Odile said. "Lightest first. Aurel, you're up. Then me. Then—"

"Then me and the cargo," Sable said.

Noor stirred against his shoulder. "I can climb."

"You cannot climb."

"I can climb *badly*, which is still climbing."

"Spare me the taster's pride." He set them down gently against the wall, propped between two beams, and crouched to look them in the eye. Up close they looked translucent, like paper held to a lamp. "Listen to me. I testified against your sister and I have owed you since before you knew my name. Let me pay it. All of it. In flesh, like the ledger says."

"That's not how ledgers work," Noor whispered. "Someone has to witness the payment."

"Then witness it." He took their hand and pressed it flat against Wren's back, where she rode against him. "Feel that? That's the receipt. Now stay awake until I get back up here for you. That's the last thing I'll ever ask of you, Noor Nakamura. Stay awake."

Something moved in their face—too complicated to name, grief and fury and something that might, in better light, have been forgiveness adjacent—and they nodded once.

Sable climbed.

It was the worst climb of his life, and he'd once scaled the outside of the Wardens' Spire at night to fix a fault line while drunk, which he did not recommend. The rungs groaned. Wren's weight swung with each reach. Halfway up, a shudder went through the whole shaft and dust fell like gray snow, and below him he heard Odile shouting encouragement in the tone of a woman who has calculated exactly how discouraged to allow herself to be.

But the light grew. The gray coin widened. And then his head broke through into open air and wind and evening sky, and Sable Ives lay gasping on the mountainside with a stolen soul breathing softly against his back, and laughed until it hurt.

The sky was full of stars. Every one of them new-old, returned, scattered across the dark like salt across a table. He lay there and let them look at him.

Then he tied the rope around his chest and went back down for Noor.

* * *

He made four trips. Noor last, because Noor would have fought him otherwise, and by then Noor couldn't fight a curtain. He hauled them up the Throat rung by rung with their arms locked around his neck and their breath shallow against his ear, and he talked the whole way, because talking helped, he'd learned that much.

"The shop roof," he said. "Tell me about the shop roof. No—you can't talk. Fine. I'll tell you about it. I heard about it secondhand, from a trial transcript, which is the worst possible way to learn about anyone's roof. Wren mentioned it in her defense statement. Did you know that? She stood in front of the tribunal and said, 'I am a charm-maker of small things, luck-knots and roof-songs,' and the whole hall thought she was confessing to something grander, and I—" his foot slipped, caught, held— "I sat in the witness box and I let them think it. Because they offered me my license back. My *license*, Noor. For a sentence. And I gave them the sentence."

Noor's grip tightened on his neck. Not forgiveness. Acknowledgment. He'd take it.

"And now I'm carrying you both up a mountain," he went on, hoarse, "and it doesn't balance. Nothing balances. But it's paid in the only currency I've got, which is my back and my breath, and if you die anyway I swear I will find whatever's after this life and testify against myself there too, at length, with exhibits—"

"Exhibits," Noor breathed, almost a laugh.

"Charts. Diagrams. I was a ward-smith, we love diagrams."

The last rung. The last pull. He rolled them both onto the mountainside grass, into the starlight, and collapsed beside them, and for a long moment the four of them—five, counting Wren, who was beginning to glow faintly, like a banked fire remembering itself—lay in a row on the slope of the world, breathing.

"We made it," Odile said, to the sky, wonderingly.

"No," Aurel said.

Sable lifted his head.

Down the mountainside, torchlight. A column of it, snaking up the switchback trail from the warden-post at the mountain's foot—dozens of torches, moving fast, and above them the thin cold shriek of warden-whistles, the signal-call Sable knew from his years in the guild: *fugitives, close the ring.*

"They held a reserve," Odile said numbly. "Outside. Of course they held a reserve. The court's collapsing and they held a reserve to catch whoever crawled out."

"How long?" Aurel asked, already on his feet.

"Minutes," Sable said. He tried to stand and his legs filed a formal complaint. "Maybe less. We can't run. Look at us. We've got two dead-weight passengers and a prince who thinks testing wards with his hand is science."

"Then we don't run." Aurel was digging in the breast pocket of his ridiculous miner's coat, and his face in the starlight was the strangest thing Sable had seen all night—calmer than it had been in the palace, calmer than it had been in the dark. "Everyone always asks what a royal seal is worth. Paper, wax, authority. Nobody asks what it weighs."

"Aurel," Odile said carefully, "what are you doing?"

"What it's for." He drew it out: a disc of heavy gold on a chain, stamped with the sun-and-tower of Vespergate, and Sable felt it from ten feet away the way you feel a struck bell in your teeth. Royal authority wasn't metaphor. It was a ward—the oldest active ward in the city, renewed by every coronation, fed by oath and law and the accumulated weight of a crown. Sable had studied its charter as an apprentice and been told no one would ever spend it, because spending it meant the crown itself stepped into the ward-line, personally, bodily, as the power source.

"It will hold them," Aurel said. "A ring of command, anchored to me. As long as I stand inside it, no warden crosses. And it will burn through everything I have—my title, my claim, my father's whole architecture of authority—in about an hour. Maybe less."

"So we run in that hour," Sable said.

"So you run in that hour." Aurel smiled, and it was his father's smile worn backward, and Sable thought: *there's the king he could have been, and look what it costs.* "Get them down the mountain. Get Wren to the dawn. I'll hold the door."

"No," said a voice like gravel and iron.

Noor was sitting up. Nobody had helped them. Nobody dared. They were gray as ash and swaying like a drunk, but their eyes had caught the starlight and were holding it, and their voice came out cracked but level.

"You are not staying behind to burn for us, Your Highness. I've buried enough people for bargains I didn't consent to." They held out one shaking hand. "Give me the seal."

"You can barely sit."

"I can *sit* in a ward-ring perfectly well. Sitting is my best event." Their mouth twitched. "You run. Take Wren—she needs the dawn more than any of us. I'll hold the ring until it burns out, and then I'll follow, or I won't, but either way it'll be my choice, made with my eyes open, which is more than our parents ever managed."

For a moment nobody moved. Above them the returned stars wheeled, indifferent and gorgeous. Below them the torches climbed.

Then Aurel knelt, and pressed the seal into Noor's palm, and folded their fingers closed over it, and bowed his head over their hands like a man knighted in reverse.

"Hold the door, taster," he said.

Noor looked up at Sable, and Odile, and the faint bright weight of Wren, and grinned with bloodless lips.

"Now *run*," they said. "Before I change my mind and make it dramatic."

They ran. Down the switchbacks, through the dark, Wren glowing brighter against Sable's back with every step toward the horizon where the sky was turning the color of a healing wound—and behind them, faint and steady, the golden hum of a royal ward lighting up around the smallest, stubbornest person Sable had ever known.

At the tree line Sable stopped, once, and looked back. Far up the slope, a ring of gold burned in the darkness like a coin held against the night, and inside it, a figure sat upright, keeping watch.

The first light of dawn touched the peaks.

Noor's ring was still burning.
