## 32. Dawn Without Stars

The last hundred feet of the mine shaft were not climbed so much as survived.

Noor remembered it afterward in fragments: Sable's shoulder wedged under their arm, hot as a forge; the rope ladder swaying where Odile had anchored it to rusted pitons that should by every law of engineering have sheared away; Aurel above them, cursing in a register no tutor had ever taught him, hauling on the rope with hands gone soft from twenty-two years of palace bread.

The mountain was dying around them. They could feel it in the stone—a long shudder rolling up through the rock like the last breath of an enormous animal, and then a stillness worse than the shaking, and then another shudder. Somewhere below, contracts were tearing loose one after another, a chain of paper fires burning down through the dark. The soul-lanterns had gone out or fled or both. The cavern city they had crawled through—the terraces of pale ministers, the vaults, the ledger halls—was folding inward like a fist unclenching after two hundred years.

"Talk to me," Sable said. His voice came out scraped. "Noor. Talk to me."

"I'm thinking about breakfast."

"Good. Good, keep doing that." He shifted their weight higher against his back. "What's for breakfast?"

"Eggs. If I'm being honest, probably terrible eggs. Palace eggs are always overcooked because the head cook is terrified of—" A cough took the sentence apart. Something copper-flavored slid up Noor's throat, and they swallowed it back down with the discipline of twenty years' practice. Swallowing poison had been the work of their life. It seemed fitting that the final dose would require the same skill.

Sable felt the shudder go through them. He didn't say anything about it. He just climbed faster.

Above, at the mouth of the shaft, there was a rectangle of gray—not lantern-light, not soul-fire, nothing bought or bargained or caged. Just gray. The color of a sky before morning.

"Almost," Odile called down, her voice thin with exhaustion and something else, something that sounded like a woman about to see the first thing she'd believed in for years. "Almost. Aurel, your hand—"

* * *

They surfaced into the ruins of the old observatory, which was to say they surfaced into open air, which was to say Noor lay on their back on cold flagstones and looked straight up and forgot how to breathe.

The stars were coming back.

Not all at once. That was the thing no one had thought to expect—that it would be slow, tender, almost shy. One star kindled high in the east like a struck match. Then three more, scattered, as if someone were walking through a vast dark house lighting lamps in the rooms where people needed them most. Then a handful near the horizon, and then a spill of them across the whole bowl of the sky, dozens, hundreds, streaming upward out of the shattered mountain behind them in long ribbons of light that bent and gathered and rose.

Odile made a sound Noor had never heard from her before—half laugh, half sob, entirely undignified—and fell to her knees in the observatory rubble with her charts fluttering forgotten around her. She wasn't writing anything down. For perhaps the first time in her adult life, Odile Ferrant was simply looking.

"They're going home," she whispered. "Look at the trajectories—they're not scattering, they're *reclaiming*. Every star has a place it remembers."

Aurel stood very still, his father's seal hanging spent and cracked in his fist, its magic burned out holding the wardens below. He watched the sky come back with the expression of a man reading a letter he'd been promised since childhood and finding it addressed to someone better than himself.

And Wren—

Wren was kneeling beside Noor.

Whole. Faint, translucent at the edges, lit from inside by whatever remained of a soul that had spent twenty years in fragments—but whole, and *there*, and looking down at them with eyes Noor had last seen across a trial court when they were eleven years old.

"You climbed," Wren said. Their voice was rusty, a door opening in a house long shut. "You stupid, stubborn little thing. You actually climbed."

"Had to," Noor managed. "Someone had to complain about the state of your vault floors."

* * *

It was after that that the world began to tilt.

Noor had known it was coming. They had done the arithmetic weeks ago, alone, by candlelight, with the steady hand of a professional: the full dose would carry them through the barrier, and the full dose would also burn out everything they had built in themselves, every immunity, every tolerance, every carefully cultivated resistance—and what was left behind would be a body with no defenses at all, flooded with the residue of twenty years of accumulated venom finally free to do what venom does.

They had signed the ledger page anyway. Consent of the bloodline. Truth chosen over safety.

But knowing a wave is coming and standing in front of it are different professions entirely.

The cold arrived first—not the cold of night air but a deep, interior cold, spreading outward from the center of them like frost crossing a windowpane. Then their hands stopped answering. Then the flagstones came up to meet them, faster than expected, and Sable's shout seemed to arrive from somewhere very far away, and the beautiful returning stars smeared sideways into streaks.

"Noor!" Hands on their shoulders. Sable's face above them, gray in the new starlight, terrified in a way he hadn't permitted himself to be in the mines. "Stay with me. Eyes open. Look at me."

"'m looking," Noor said. It came out slurred. "You look terrible, by the way. Have you been drinking?"

"Constantly," Sable said, and laughed, and the laugh broke in the middle. "Gods, you're the worst patient in Vespergate. Twenty years of tasting poison and now you can't even—"

He couldn't finish it. Neither of them could. Because they both knew what was happening, and saying it plainly felt too much like signing another contract.

Odile knelt on Noor's other side, pressing two fingers to their wrist with clinical precision, counting a pulse that wandered like a drunk leaving a tavern. "Slow," she reported, to no one, to everyone. "Irregular. But present." She looked up, and her astronomer's calm held by its fingertips. "He needs a physician. Not a hedge-witch, not a charm—a real physician, with real medicine, and quickly."

"The city's in chaos," Aurel said. "Half the guard deserted, the wards are—"

"Then find a way, Your Highness. You're good at finding ways. We've all watched you do it for days." Odile's tone was flat as a chart. "Or have we misjudged you?"

Something crossed Aurel's face—wound, and resolve, and the strange new gravity he'd been carrying since the archive. He turned and ran for the shaft road without another word.

* * *

Wren took Noor's hand.

That was all. In the middle of the collapsing world—in the ruins, under the rekindling sky, while Sable paced and Odile counted pulses and somewhere below the mountain finished its long unraveling—Wren sat down in the dust, gathered their sibling's cold fingers into both of theirs, and held on.

Their grip was weak. Everything about Wren was weak now, worn thin as old cloth, a soul stretched across two decades of servitude. But they held on the way they used to hold on to Noor's wrist crossing the market crowds when Noor was small: not tightly, just certainly, as if to let go had never once occurred to them.

"I've got you," Wren said.

"You're dead," Noor said. It seemed important to establish this. "Officially. There was a trial. I was eleven. There were pastries afterward, which everyone pretended weren't celebratory, which somehow made it worse."

"I remember the pastries." Wren's mouth curved. "Honey ones. From Ferro's cart. You cried into one."

"I did not cry into one."

"You cried directly into one. Ferro charged us extra."

A sound escaped Noor that was either laughter or its opposite. The cold was in their chest now, settling in around the heart like a guest who intended to stay. Each breath cost more than the last, an economy failing fast. And yet underneath the failing, underneath the fear, there was a small stubborn astonishment that refused to die: *I found you. Against ledgers and courts and a mountain full of stolen light, I found you.*

"Wren." Their voice was going quiet. They made themselves say it clearly, because some things deserved clarity. "The letter. Under the third floorboard, wrapped in oilcloth, with the knot you taught me. I read it. I've read it a thousand times. I built my whole life out of it."

"I know." Wren's thumb moved across their knuckles, back and forth, the way rain moves on a roof. "I hoped you'd find it. I hoped you wouldn't. Both. That's what loving someone is, I think—hoping opposite things forever."

"I drank it, Wren. The poison. All of it. To get you back." A pause. The stars wheeled slowly overhead, settling into their old houses. "Was it worth it? Tell me it was worth it, so I can be insufferable about it later."

Wren didn't answer right away. When they did, their voice had gone thick.

"It was worth it. Now stop talking and breathe, you impossible creature. Breathe."

* * *

Somewhere in the gray hour before true dawn, Wren began to sing.

Softly at first. Barely a thread of sound, as if the song itself had been kept underground for twenty years and needed coaxing back into the air. Noor knew it immediately, knew it before the second note, the way you know the shape of your own name: the shop-roof song. The nonsense rhyme their mother used to sing during storms, when the two of them huddled upstairs listening to rain hammer the tiles, counting thunder, pretending to be brave.

*Rain on the roof and wind in the eaves,*
*two little thieves beneath the leaves—*

"You remembered it," Noor whispered.

"I never forgot anything," Wren said. "That was the trouble with being sold. You go on remembering everything, with no one to tell it to." They sang on, low and steady, their faint voice fraying at the edges and mending again. "*One for the silver and one for the bread, one for the words we left unsaid—*"

Sable had stopped pacing. He stood at the edge of the flagstones with his hat in his hands—the first time Noor had seen him bareheaded since the trial, twenty years ago, when he'd stood in a witness box with that same ruined expression. He didn't ask forgiveness. He just stayed, and kept watch, and let the song pass through him like weather.

"*—two little thieves beneath the leaves,*" Wren sang, "*and the storm can't find us, the storm can't see, 'cause we're only rain on the roof, my love, only rain on the roof—*"

Noor's vision was going soft at the borders, the returning stars blurring into halos, into smudges, into a darkness that felt less like an ending than a held breath. The cold had reached everywhere now. It was almost peaceful, in the way that deep water is peaceful. They thought: *so this is what it's like, trusting someone else to hold on.* They thought: *twenty years invisible, and at the end, witnessed.*

They thought: *the eggs really will be overcooked if I don't survive to complain about them.*

"Breathe," Wren said again, and pressed their forehead to Noor's forehead, faint as candlelight, warm as a shop in summer. "Breathe, Jude."

The childhood name. The secret one, spoken aloud for the first time since before the trial, since before the world decided who they had to become. Noor breathed.

Down the shaft road, boots hammered—fast, urgent, more than one pair. A voice carried ahead of them, breathless, imperious, cracking at the edges with something that was absolutely not fear:

"Don't you dare die before I get there! Do you hear me? I am the *prince*, and I am ordering you not to die!"

Noor smiled into the dark, and let go of consciousness like letting go of a rope at the top of a climb—trusting, for the first time in twenty years, that there were hands below to catch them.
