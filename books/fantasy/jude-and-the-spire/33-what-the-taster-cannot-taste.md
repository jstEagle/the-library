## 33. What the Taster Cannot Taste

Aurel had learned early that the most dangerous thing in a palace was not poison. It was paperwork.

Paperwork left a trail. Poison, in his experience, left only a body and a great deal of convenient silence. So when he decided to smuggle a dying food-taster through his own corridors at three in the morning, he did it the way his father would have done it: with a forged requisition, a bribed gatekeeper, and no witnesses worth the name.

"You realize," Noor said, slumped against the wall of the linen passage with their arm over his shoulder, "that this is the third time you've carried me somewhere against my stated wishes."

"The first time was a stretcher," Aurel said. "You were unconscious. I don't think that counts."

"It counts. I'm counting it."

"Then I'm at two for one, and I'll take those odds." He shifted their weight. Noor was lighter than they should have been—twenty years of tasting other people's meals had taught them to eat like a bird in a room full of cats—and their skin had the gray undertone of old candle smoke. The poison was still in them, doing its slow work, the way a guest does who has been invited to stay and intends to stay forever.

The physicians' wing was three floors down and one corridor over, past the tapestry of the Founding (which Aurel had privately begun to think of as the tapestry of the Fraud, given what they'd learned beneath the mountain) and through a door that officially opened onto storage.

Officially.

He knocked twice, paused, knocked once.

The door opened on Master Ellery Voss, physician to three generations of Vespergate royalty, a small round woman with ink-stained fingers and the unimpressed expression of someone who had spent forty years being lied to by people wearing crowns. She looked at Noor. She looked at Aurel. She looked at the ceiling as if petitioning it directly.

"Your Highness," she said. "It is the middle of the night."

"Yes."

"You are carrying a person."

"I am."

"A person who is, unless my eyes have rotted in my head, the royal food-taster, who is officially recorded as having taken a month's leave to visit an aunt in Lowmarsh."

"Do I have an aunt in Lowmarsh?" Noor asked faintly.

"You do now," Aurel said. "I filed the paperwork."

Voss stepped aside and let them in, because she had been the palace physician long enough to know that when a prince carried someone through a secret door at three in the morning, the interesting questions came later, and the urgent ones were standing right in front of you, going gray.

* * *

The examination room smelled of clean linen and bitter herbs, and Noor lay on the table looking, Aurel thought, like a map someone had folded wrong and couldn't quite unfold again.

Voss worked in silence for a long while. She pressed fingers to Noor's wrists, looked at their eyes, made them breathe in and out with her hand flat on their back. She drew blood with a needle so fine Aurel barely saw it, and held the drop up to a lamp, and frowned at it the way Odile Ferrant frowned at the dark.

"Well?" Aurel asked.

"Well," said Voss, "I would like to know what they drank, so that I never drink it, and also so that I can write it down and then burn the paper."

"No," said Noor.

"No?"

"No. You can know what it does. You can't know what it is."

Voss's eyebrows climbed toward her cap. "I am the chief physician of the palace."

"And I am a dead woman if that recipe exists anywhere outside my head," Noor said. Their voice was hoarse but level. Aurel had come to know that levelness. It was the voice they used when the alternative was shaking. "What it does: it lets a living body pass a soul-barrier. It does that by unmaking everything else a body has built up against the world. Every tolerance. Every immunity. Twenty years of it, gone in a night. My blood is, as of four days ago, the blood of an ordinary person who has never once been poisoned on purpose."

Voss was quiet. Then she sat down on her stool with the small decisive motion of a woman rearranging her entire understanding of a patient.

"Twenty years," she said. "You built an immunity to what, precisely?"

"Everything I could get my hands on."

"That's—" Voss stopped. Started again. "That is the single most disciplined act of self-destruction I have ever heard described, and I once treated a man who wrestled river-drakes for money."

"He sounds fun," said Noor.

"He died," said Voss. "Which brings me to my point." She turned to Aurel, and her voice shifted into the register she used for delivering bad news to royalty, which was the same register she used for everything, only slower. "Your Highness. Your taster is going to live. I want to be clear about that first, because I can see you've been standing there like a man waiting for a verdict."

Aurel realized his hands were clenched behind his back and made them unclench. "And the verdict after that?"

"After that: they will live the way everyone else lives. Which is to say, precariously." Voss ticked points off on ink-stained fingers. "Their body has forgotten every defense it ever built. A bad oyster could kill them. A mislabeled mushroom. Honey from the wrong flowers. A cook with a grudge, a rival with a ring, a kitchen boy who doesn't wash his hands. Anything. Everything. The world is full of poison, Highness; most of us just have bodies that argue back. Theirs has lost the argument and been dismissed from the field."

The room was quiet. On the table, Noor stared at the ceiling with an expression Aurel couldn't read, which was unusual, because he had gotten fairly good at reading them, in the way of people who had shared a conspiracy and a mine shaft and a collapsing mountain.

"Say it plainly," Noor said at last.

Voss folded her hands. "I can treat you. I can keep you. But I cannot give you back what you spent. You bought your sister's life with your armor, and armor doesn't come home from that kind of war. From today forward, you will taste nothing before it's tested by other means. You will eat what I approve, when I approve it. You will carry an antidote kit and the knowledge to use it, and you will let me teach you, and you will be patient with your own body, which is going to feel like a stranger for a long while." She paused. "And you will never again put anything in your mouth on faith."

"That last one," Noor said, "I'm good at."

"I imagine you are," said Voss, and there was, Aurel thought, something almost like gentleness under the dryness. "Faith was the thing you were paying against."

* * *

They kept Noor in the physicians' wing for nine days, under a false name—Aurel had gone with "Mistress Fern of Lowmarsh," and Noor had looked at the paperwork and said, "You have a gift for this, and it worries me"—and Aurel visited every day, which was its own kind of scandal-in-waiting, and so he did it the way he now did everything that mattered: quietly, and by the linen stairs.

He told himself it was duty. The taster had been poisoned in his service, technically, in the sense that everything Noor had ever done had been in service of surviving his household. He told himself it was debt. He told himself it was the same political calculation as always: a living taster who knew where the bodies were buried was worth more than a dead one.

He stopped telling himself these things around day four, when he caught himself sitting in the corridor outside Noor's room reading a report on grain tariffs and realizing he had read the same paragraph six times because he was listening to the sound of Noor's breathing through the door. Just listening. Making sure it continued.

His mother had died when he was nine, and no one had let him listen at her door.

"You're doing it again," Noor said on the ninth day, when he came in with the morning's tray. They were sitting up, which was new, propped against pillows with a cup of Voss-approved barley water and the expression of a cat that has been confined against its will and is composing a letter of grievance.

"Doing what?"

"Hovering. You hover like a heron. You think you're being subtle, but herons are not subtle, they're just patient."

"I brought you breakfast."

"You brought me barley water and bread so white it's practically a rumor, and you watched me eat it like a man defusing something." Noor set down the cup. "Aurel. Sit down."

He sat. It occurred to him, sitting, that Noor had never used his name before. Not once, in all the years of tasting his food, all the weeks of conspiracy, all the dark miles under the mountain. It had always been *Your Highness* with the flat delivery of someone naming a piece of furniture, and then, somewhere in the last month, nothing at all—because you didn't call a person anything when you were afraid the name might be the last thing you said to them.

"I want to say something," Noor said, "and I want you to let me finish it, because if I stop I won't start again."

"Understood."

"I spent twenty years being useful so I'd be invisible. Useful people don't get looked at too closely. And then I spent a month being useful to *you*—to the plan—and somewhere in there I stopped being invisible, and I keep waiting for it to kill me. That's the truth. That's the whole truth. Every time you walk through that door I think, this is it, this is the day the usefulness runs out and he remembers what I am and what I cost him." They took a breath. "And you keep bringing barley water. And I don't know what to do with a man who keeps bringing barley water."

Aurel considered several responses. Most of them were princely. He discarded them.

"I don't know what to do with it either," he said. "I was raised in a house where love was a ledger and everyone's column was being audited. My father bought souls, Noor. Actual souls, with actual names, and he did the bookkeeping himself, in his own hand, and I have his handwriting in my head now and I can't get it out. So I don't— I don't have a model for this. For caring about someone without it being a transaction." He looked at the tray, the rumor-white bread. "But I can tell you what it isn't. It isn't a debt I'm collecting. You don't owe me survival. You paid more than enough under that mountain."

"Voss says I'll live."

"Voss says you'll live *carefully*."

"Yes." Noor turned the cup in their hands. "That's the strange part. I spent twenty years making myself safe, and now I'm going to be unsafe for the rest of my life, and I keep waiting to feel like the floor's gone. And it's not there. The floor. It's more like—" They searched. "It's more like the floor was never there. I was just standing very still on the air and calling it ground. And now I'm falling, but there are people falling with me, and somehow that's—" They stopped, jaw tightening, and Aurel watched them wrestle the sentence the way they'd once wrestled a poison, refusing to let it master them.

"That's better?" he offered.

"That's *bearable*," Noor said. "Don't push it."

He laughed. It surprised him, the laugh; he hadn't done much of it since the stars went out. Noor's mouth twitched—the ghost of the smile they kept in reserve, the one he'd seen exactly four times and could have listed all four.

* * *

The problem, as it turned out, was not the surviving. The problem was breakfast.

On the eleventh day, Voss declared Noor well enough to leave the wing, and Aurel made a decision that his councilors would have called reckless and that he privately considered the most honest act of his reign so far, which was a low bar, but still.

He had a table set in the small morning room—the one with the window over the herb garden, the one his mother had liked—and he had the kitchen prepare two breakfasts, and he dismissed every servant within a hundred feet, and he sat down across from Noor with two plates and a small brass bell.

Noor looked at the bell. Then at him. Then at the plates.

"What," they said, "is that."

"That," said Aurel, "is a testing bell. Silver. It rings false over most poisons—I've had it since I was fifteen, it was a gift from a paranoid aunt, the one from Lowmarsh, apparently, since we're inventing her. And that—" he nodded at the second plate, the one in front of himself "—is the other half of the test. We eat the same food. From the same dishes. At the same time. If it's poisoned, we find out together."

"That is the stupidest security protocol I have ever heard."

"It's how friends eat."

The word landed between them. Noor looked at it, the way one looks at a charm that might be harmless or might be the thing that finally does the trick.

"I can't taste for you anymore," Noor said quietly. "You understand that. You need a new taster. A real one. I'm— I'm just a person now. I'm the most poisonable person in this palace. I'm a liability with a title."

"You're the person who walked into the dark and came back with my mother's soul and yours and half the sky," Aurel said. "You're not a liability. You're the reason there's a palace left to be liable in." He picked up his fork. "Besides, I'm not asking you to taste. I'm asking you to eat. There's a difference. Tasting is a job." He cut a piece of the plum—plums, always plums; the universe had a sense of humor—and held it a moment. "Eating is a choice."

Noor looked at the plum for a long time.

Aurel watched them do the arithmetic he was only beginning to understand: the years of small careful deaths, the ledger of the body, the discipline that had been the only wall between them and the world. And then he watched them set the wall down.

Noor picked up their fork and cut the plum and ate it, and their eyes went wide, and then wet, and they said nothing at all, and neither did he, because some things you don't narrate. But he saw it. The flavor landing like a first snowfall on ground that had never once been allowed to hold it. Sweetness arriving without a body of twenty years of vigilance standing in front of it, checking its papers.

"It tastes," Noor said finally, voice unsteady, "like being a child. I didn't know I remembered what plums tasted like to a child."

"Is that good?"

"It's terrifying," said Noor, and ate another piece. "It's the best thing I've ever eaten and it's terrifying, and I think—" they laughed, wet and surprised, "—I think those might be the same thing, from now on."

Aurel raised his cup of barley water. Noor raised theirs.

"To Lowmarsh," he said. "And its aunt."

* * *

He was still smiling about it an hour later, which was why he was unprepared for the knock.

Not the linen-stairs knock. The official one. Three measured raps, the kind that came with a sealed letter behind them, and when he opened the door there was a page in council livery, pale as milk, holding a message with the chancellor's seal already broken, which meant the chancellor had read it first, which meant it was already everywhere.

"Your Highness," the page said. "You're asked to come at once. The council has received testimony." He swallowed. "From a ward-smith. Sable Ives. He's been speaking in the market square since dawn, Your Highness. About the soul-bargain. About your father. About—" the page's voice dropped to a horrified whisper, "—*names*, Your Highness. He's reading names. And there's a crowd, and it's growing, and the chancellor wants to know what the crown intends to *do*."

Aurel took the letter. He did not read it. He already knew what it said; he'd have known if it were blank.

Somewhere down in the city, Sable Ives was standing in front of thousands of people doing the thing he had failed to do twenty years ago—telling the truth, all of it, with his name attached—and the old regime was coming apart in the square like wet paper, and every eye in Vespergate was about to turn toward the palace and ask the only question that mattered.

*What does the new reign know, and when did it choose to know it?*

Aurel folded the letter, and found, to his own surprise, that his hands were steady.

"Tell the chancellor," he said, "that the crown intends to go down there and stand next to him."
