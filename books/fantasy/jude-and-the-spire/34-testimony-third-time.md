## 34. Testimony, Third Time

Sable Ives had testified twice in his life, and both times he had lied.

The first time, he'd been twenty-three years old and terrified, standing in a warden's anteroom that smelled of ink and wet stone, reciting words a senior smith had written for him on a slate. *I observed the accused practicing unlicensed craft. I observed her selling charms of unknown provenance.* He had observed no such thing. He had seen a girl of nineteen mending a luck-knot for a lamplighter's daughter and charging nothing at all, and he had said nothing, because saying something would have cost him his apprenticeship, and silence was free.

The second time had been worse, because by then he'd known better. He'd stood in the great hall beneath the mountain — though he hadn't known then what lived beneath the mountain, not really — and he'd sworn on a ward-stone that the charter work he'd done for Wren Nakamura's family was fraudulent, that their signatures were forged, that the girl herself had coerced him. His voice hadn't even shaken. That was the part that haunted him most, all these years later. Not the lying. The steadiness of it. He had been *good* at it.

Now he stood in the same great hall — or what remained of it; half the ceiling had come down when the mountain court collapsed, and the light came through the wound in the roof like an apology — and his hands would not stop shaking.

"You could breathe," said Odile Ferrant, who had appointed herself his keeper for the morning. She was holding a sheaf of star-charts against her chest like a breastplate, as if the assembled council might demand she account for the sky at any moment. "It's a thing people do before speaking. In through the nose."

"I'm breathing."

"You're vibrating."

"That's different." He looked down at the paper in his hands. He had written the testimony out four times. The first draft was too angry. The second was too careful. The third had been honest but organized, which felt somehow like another kind of lie, truth arranged neatly enough to be comfortable. The fourth draft — the one in his hands now — was just true, in the order it had happened, ugly seams showing.

Below them, the hall filled. It filled slowly, then all at once, the way water finds a channel. Wardens without their old black badges. Ministers whose offices technically no longer existed, sitting in the front rows anyway because men like that never quite believe a room has stopped belonging to them. Shopkeepers. Lamplighters off shift, still smelling of oil. A surprising number of children, hoisted onto shoulders, because word had gone round that this was the day the city would hear how its walls had really been paid for, and Vespergate had discovered, in the weeks since the stars came home, that it was hungry for its own history.

And there, near the back where he could watch the doors — old habit, Sable supposed, or maybe new habit, learned in a warden cell — sat Noor Nakamura, arms folded, expression unreadable, wearing the face of someone who had come to observe rather than be saved. Beside them, Wren, alive and thin and blinking at crowds like a cat returned to a house that had changed the furniture. And beside *her*, Prince Aurel, in plain clothes that fooled absolutely no one, because princes cannot help how they sit.

Sable's stomach performed a slow, deliberate revolution.

"They're going to hang me," he said.

"No one hangs anyone anymore," Odile said. "There was a decree."

"There was a *proclamation*. Proclamations are things princes say while they decide."

"He's your friend."

"He's a prince who is my friend. Those are different animals, and only one of them has ever signed an execution order." Sable wiped his palms on his coat. The coat was his good coat, which meant it was his only coat, which meant it smelled faintly of the observatory cellar where he'd spent the last decade drinking. He'd scrubbed up. He'd shaved. He'd even, in a fit of morning madness, attempted to braid his beard the way ward-smiths used to braid them for formal proceedings, before giving up halfway and leaving it looking like a rope that had lost an argument.

A herald — a boy of perhaps fifteen, promoted into the job because the old herald had fled the city during the collapse and no one had caught him yet — banged a staff three times.

"Sable Ives," the boy called, in a voice cracking between registers like ice on a pond. "Ward-smith, second class, struck from the rolls. Come forward and give testimony."

*Third time*, Sable thought. *Third time's the charm.* And then he thought: *Noor would hate that joke,* and then he thought: *Noor would make it first,* and somehow that steadied him more than any breathing exercise.

He walked down.

* * *

The hall had changed since the mountain court fell, but not entirely. The dais where the ministers had once sat above everyone was still there, and Aurel had refused to have it demolished — "symbolism is expensive," he'd said, "and we haven't the budget" — so the council now sat at floor level among everyone else, at plain tables, which meant that when Sable reached the center of the hall he was standing *above* them, and found he didn't know what to do with that.

An old woman at the nearest table — Dame Corvane, formerly Chief of Wards, currently the closest thing the city had to a judge — nodded at him. She had been his examiner once, twenty years ago. She had failed him on his final certification for "unorthodox theory," which was the polite way of saying he'd asked too many questions about where the wards' power actually came from.

"Sable Ives," she said. "You are here voluntarily."

"Yes."

"You understand that under the old law, everything you are about to say could be entered as confession, and confessions carry penalties."

"I understand that under the old law, everything anyone ever said to me was potentially evidence," Sable said, "so yes. I've read the fine print my whole life. I wrote some of it."

Someone in the crowd laughed. It wasn't a cruel laugh. It startled him more than jeering would have.

"Then begin."

He unfolded his fourth draft. Then he folded it again and put it in his pocket, because he suddenly understood that reading it would be the third lie — not false, but *distant*, words chosen in advance, safe behind paper. What he owed this room was the version that came out crooked.

"My name is Sable Ives," he said. "Twenty-two years ago I certified the border wards of Vespergate as sound. I want to start there, because everything else follows from it, and because I want you to understand that I did not certify them honestly. I certified them the way you certify a thing when you've been told the answer in advance."

He told them about the charter. Not the public charter — every apprentice learned the public charter, the beautiful one about the founders weaving protection into the stones — but the working documents, the ones kept below the waterline, the ones with columns that didn't add up. Power going *in* to the wards, always in, never cycling back. Draw schedules. Tribute lines. He'd noticed it as a young man. He'd brought it to his superiors. He'd been told the accounting was above his grade, and he'd accepted that, because accepting things was how you survived in the guilds, and he had been very committed to surviving.

"The wards weren't broken," he said. "That's the first thing. Everyone keeps asking me what broke them, and the answer is: nothing. They worked exactly as designed until the night they stopped being paid for. They were never ours. We rented them. And the rent—" His voice caught. He let it. He'd decided, somewhere around draft three, that he was allowed to let it catch. "The rent was souls. Sold by this city's own court, through ledgers kept beneath the mountain, year after year. I helped maintain the machinery of collection. I didn't know all of it. But I knew enough, and I chose not to know the rest, and I am here to tell you that choosing not to know is not innocence. It's just cowardice with better posture."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

Dame Corvane's face gave away nothing, but her pen had stopped moving, and Sable, who had spent years watching wardens take notes, knew what it meant when the pen stopped. It meant the note-taker had stopped recording and started listening.

Then he told them about Wren Nakamura.

He made himself say her name out loud, in front of her brother, in front of her — she was sitting right there, twenty years dead and alive again, and he had not yet managed to speak to her directly, because some debts you can't pay in conversation, only in public, on the record, where it counts.

"She was nineteen," he said. "She ran a charm stall in the night market. Her magic was the smallest kind — knots and blessings and charms against damp — and it was illegal, because our laws were written by people who needed magic to be frightening so they could sell us protection from it. I was sent to observe her. I observed her. And what I observed was a girl charging nothing to a starving child, and I wrote it down as evidence of *pattern of deception*, because that's what I was instructed to find, and I was young, and I wanted to keep my place."

He turned, deliberately, and faced the benches where Noor sat.

"And when the trial came, I testified. Twice. Once in the anteroom, once beneath the mountain. I said her signatures were forged. I said she coerced me. Neither was true. She went to the mountain court on my words, and the mountain court bought her soul, and I went home and drank, and called it grief, and grief does not require anything of you, which is why I preferred it to guilt."

His voice held. He was almost surprised. Somewhere in the crowd, a lamplighter — Tam, he thought, the old man he'd once watched buy a luck-knot — took off his cap.

"I'm not telling you this to be forgiven," Sable said. "Forgiveness isn't mine to ask for; it's hers, and she hasn't given it, and she may never, and that's correct. I'm telling you because the old regime needs to fall all the way down, and regimes don't fall from the top. They fall when the people in the middle stop lying. I was the middle. Here is the truth, all of it, in the order it happened, and I'll answer any question this hall wants to put to me, and then whatever penalty you decide, I'll stand for it."

He sat down on the plain wooden chair they'd left for him in the center of the hall, because his knees had informed him the speech portion was concluded whether he liked it or not.

For a long moment, nobody moved.

Then Wren Nakamura stood up.

* * *

Later, Sable would try to remember what she'd said and find that memory had kept only pieces: that her voice was rough from disuse, that she talked about the lantern-vaults, about what twenty years of being *fuel* did to a person, about the exact weight of a soul that has been entered into a ledger. That she said, at one point, "I don't remember much of who I was. But I remember the shop roof. I remember rain on it. And I remember that the person who sat under that roof with me never sold anyone, ever, not even when selling would have saved them." That she looked at Sable for a long moment and said, "You were the one who lied about me," and the hall had gone very quiet, and then she'd said, "And you're the one who carried my brother out of a mountain. Both things are true. I'm told that's how people are."

She hadn't forgiven him. She'd said so plainly. But she had *testified alongside* him, and when she finished, she'd nodded at him once, formally, the way one smith acknowledges another across a workshop, and Sable had discovered that this hurt more and healed more than forgiveness would have, and he suspected that was the point.

After her came Noor, who spoke briefly and devastatingly about the ledger — names read aloud, one after another, the whole market square outside falling silent as the sound carried through the doors — and then Prince Aurel, who laid his father's ledgers on the table with the care of a man handling something venomous, and said, "My family did this. I am the family. Judge us accordingly."

But Sable barely heard any of it. He was sitting in his chair in the middle of the hall, shaking again, watching Dame Corvane write, and waiting.

When all the testimony was done, the council conferred for what felt like a season. Sable used the time to catalog his probable futures: the warden cells (familiar), exile (novel), labor on the wall repairs (fair), hanging (see earlier anxiety). Odile sat beside him and narrated the crowd instead, quietly, because she could tell he needed a voice in his ear that wasn't his own. "The baker from Copperlane is crying. The one who sells the twist rolls. I think those are his grandchildren on the railing. Oh — the herald boy is eating an apple. Someone should tell him that's not ceremonial."

"Sable Ives," Dame Corvane said at last, and he stood, and his knees behaved this time, mostly.

"The council has heard your testimony and finds it credible, complete, and — I will say this plainly, because you said things plainly and it seems catching — long overdue." She set down her pen. "Under the old law, your crimes were real. Under the old law, you would leave this hall in chains, and no one in this hall would have objected, because the old law was built to protect liars. That law is dissolved. What remains is this question: what do we do with a man who knows exactly how the wards were built, exactly how they failed, and exactly how they might be built again — honestly, this time, on our own power, paid for by no one's soul?"

Sable's mouth was dry. "I — respectfully — I'm a drunk, Dame. I've been struck off the rolls for twenty years. My certification is—"

"Your certification is worthless," she agreed. "Which is why you won't be certified. You'll be commissioned. Commissioned work is judged by results, not papers." She slid a document across the table toward him. It bore the new seal — Aurel's seal, redrawn, the spire and an open sky above it where the closed crown used to be. "By order of the council and the prince regent: Sable Ives is appointed Master of the Rebuilding, with authority over all ward-work in Vespergate, answerable to this council publicly, in open session, minutes posted where any citizen may read them. Your first task is to draw up a charter for wards powered by consent — volunteers, covenants, the small magics your late colleague used to sell for free. You will need smiths. You will need to train them. You will need, I suspect, to forgive yourself enough to do the work, though the council notes that this last item is outside our jurisdiction."

Sable stared at the document. Then at her. Then, helplessly, at the benches, where Noor was wearing an expression that on anyone else's face would have been called a grin.

"Dame," he managed, "I once failed my examination because I asked where the power came from."

"Yes," said Dame Corvane, and for the first time in twenty years, the old woman smiled. "In retrospect, that was the correct answer. Take the commission, Master Ives. The city's walls are down, winter is coming, and I'd like the borders humming again before the first snow — powered by nothing but what we're willing to give freely. Can you do it?"

Sable picked up the document. His hands were steady now. Of course they were. They'd only ever shaken when he lied.

"I can do it," he said. "On one condition."

Corvane raised an eyebrow.

"Everything gets posted. Every drawing, every ledger line, every covenant, every name of every volunteer. If we build the new wards in the dark, we're just building the old ones with nicer handwriting."

"Posted," she agreed. "Publicly. Permanently."

He signed. The herald boy banged his staff, badly, and dropped the apple.

* * *

They ended the day — all of them, the whole strange found family of it — on the steps of the hall, eating twist rolls out of a paper bag while the baker's grandchildren interrogated Wren about whether being dead was cold. (It was, Wren reported, "like standing in a doorway in October. For twenty years.") The evening sky overhead was doing the thing it had done every night since the stars came home: showing off.

"So," Odile said, around a mouthful of roll. "Master of the Rebuilding. Do I get a title? I've been charting the dark for a decade; I feel I've earned a title."

"Astronomer Royal," said Aurel, "if you want it. Fair warning: it comes with meetings."

"I'll take the title and decline the meetings."

"That's the spirit." Aurel looked tired in the specific way of a man who had spent the day dismantling his own inheritance in public, but underneath the tiredness was something lighter than Sable had ever seen on him. "Sable. A word?"

Sable braced. Princes asking for *a word* had historically gone poorly for him.

But Aurel only glanced toward the others — Noor and Wren were arguing cheerfully about shop signage, Wren insisting the sign should say NAKAMURA, Noor insisting it should say nothing at all, "signs attract attention" — and lowered his voice.

"Tomorrow, the council convenes on the tribute. The full reckoning of houses. My father's ledgers, the ministers' complicity, all of it, in the open. There are people in that room who will call it treason. Some of them are armed with votes instead of knives, which is worse." He paused. "I'd like you there. Not to testify. Just — you've stood in rooms full of powerful liars more times than anyone I know. You'll see the knife coming before I do."

Sable chewed his roll. Considered. Twenty years ago, a frightened young smith had kept silent in a room full of powerful men because silence was free.

"Prince," he said, "I'll be there at dawn. And afterward, when you've finished burning down what's left of your father's regime — I've got a favor to ask. There's a shop on Lantern Row with a lease that's been sitting empty for twenty years. The paperwork's a nightmare. Owner was executed; heir was declared dead; both of them are currently eating twist rolls on these steps. Somebody with a seal ought to fix that."

Aurel followed his gaze to the arguing siblings, and something in his face went soft and rueful.

"Consider it my first act of the new reign," he said. "Though fair warning — I've read Noor's file. If I hand them that lease, they'll want the deed rewritten in both names, and Wren will want a sign, and within a month there'll be a queue of citizens down the block buying luck-knots *legally*, and the wardens—"

"—are being retrained," Sable said. "By me. First lesson: a knot against damp never hurt anybody."

Overhead, the first stars of the evening pricked through the blue, arriving one by one like witnesses finally willing to give their names. Sable looked up at them for a long moment — all that light, home again, unpaid and unowned — and thought that tomorrow would bring knives, votes, and the reckoning of houses.

Let it come. He'd testify a fourth time if the city needed it.

This time, he'd tell the truth from the very first word.
