## 36. Wren Learns the New Streets

The first thing Wren learned about the new Vespergate was that it smelled different.

Twenty years ago, the city had smelled of tallow and river mud and the sharp vinegar tang of the tanneries by the east wall. Now there was something else underneath—something green. She stopped in the middle of Lamplighter's Row, nostrils flaring like a hound, and Noor nearly walked into her back.

"What is that?" Wren demanded.

"Morning glories." Noor pointed up, and Wren followed the finger to a trellis that had swallowed an entire balcony three stories above them, a riot of white and purple blooms spilling over the railing like a drunkard over a fence. "They came back after the wards fell. Nobody planted them. They just—" Noor made a vague gesture that encompassed the flowers, the sky, the entire improbable business of things growing where they pleased. "Started."

"Magic," Wren said, tasting the word. It had been a death sentence once. Now it was a gardening phenomenon.

"Nowadays, yes. Back then it was just morning glories being ambitious."

Wren laughed—a rusty sound, like a hinge that hadn't been oiled in years, which was more or less what her laugh was. She was still learning which of her own noises worked. Her voice had come back in fragments at first, in the weeks after they'd carried her out of the mountain: single words, then phrases, then, one gray dawn on the roof of the rebuilt shop, the whole song about the rain, sung through without a single stumble while Noor sat very still beside her and pretended to be watching the weather.

She was greedy for everything now. That was the thing nobody warned you about, coming back from the dead-adjacent. You didn't come back moderate. You came back starving. Wren wanted to smell every street, eat every kind of bread, learn the names of all the new lamplighters and argue with all the old ones. She had twenty years to make up for and a body that, whatever the mountain had done to it, seemed determined to enjoy itself.

But some days the hunger turned on her. Some days she'd reach for a memory—the name of a street, the face of a neighbor—and find the mountain's long dark where it should have been, and the grief would hit her sideways, mid-stride, mid-sentence. The city had moved on without her. Children she'd known were grown and gray. The baker on Coriander Street was dead; his daughter ran the shop now, and when Wren walked in, the daughter had looked at her with polite blankness until Noor said, quietly, "This is Wren. She used to buy the burnt loaves on purpose," and the woman's face had changed entirely, and then they'd both stood in the bakery crying into a bag of rolls while customers pretended not to notice.

"You're staring at the flowers again," Noor said now.

"I'm cataloguing the flowers. There's a difference. Cataloguing is professional."

"Of course it is."

"I'm going to put them in a charm," Wren said. "A small one. A *bloom*-charm. For people whose gardens won't behave."

Noor gave her the look—that particular flat, fond look Wren was still relearning, the one that meant *you've been back four months and you're already inventing charms, slow down*—and Wren grinned and hooked her arm through their elbow and dragged them onward down the row.

Because that was the other thing about the new Vespergate: the shop was legal now.

Wren still couldn't quite believe it. She kept waiting for the trick, the trapdoor, the wardens kicking the door in. Old habits. But the sign had gone up two weeks ago—painted by Sable, who turned out to have surprisingly elegant lettering hidden under all the guilt and drinking, though he'd refused payment and gotten flustered when Wren hugged him, and had then gone home and, according to Odile, drunk nothing stronger than tea for a week out of sheer emotion—and the sign said **WREN & NOOR, CHARMWRIGHTS**, with a little painted knot beneath it, and no one had arrested anyone.

Odile had helped hang it. Then she'd stood under it for a long moment with her arms crossed, looking up at the letters as if checking them against some chart only she could see, and finally said, "Huh," in the tone of a woman confirming a hypothesis. When Wren asked what hypothesis, Odile said, "That good things could be permanent," and then pretended she hadn't said it and went inside to argue about shelf placement.

*

The shelves were the first battle.

Noor wanted them arranged by function: luck, protection, mending, sleep, small mercies. A sensible taxonomy. A *taster's* taxonomy—everything sorted, everything legible, nothing left to chance.

Wren wanted them arranged by feeling.

"You can't shelve charms by feeling," Noor said. "Customers need to find things."

"Customers *always* found things in my shop. I shelved by feeling for fifteen years."

"And how did that work out?"

It hung there between them, sharp-edged, accidental. Wren watched Noor hear their own words and go pale around the mouth. Twenty years of survival had built a person who reached for the sharpest available sentence before thinking, and Wren was learning not to bleed when it happened—but this time she decided to do something braver than bleeding or pretending.

"It worked fine," she said steadily, "until Father sold me. Which wasn't the shelving's fault."

Noor set down the jar of luck-knots they were holding, very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off. "I'm sorry. I didn't mean—"

"I know what you didn't mean." Wren crossed the cluttered floor and took their hands. Their sister-brother-sibling—she'd never settled on a word and had decided she didn't need to; Noor was simply Noor, the way rain was simply rain—had hands that trembled slightly, always, since the poison. "You spent twenty years sharpening yourself into a weapon so you could come get me. I know. I read the ledger. I heard the story forty times. But Noor—you don't have to be sharp in here. This is the shop. In here we're allowed to be soft."

Noor looked at their joined hands for a long moment. "Soft gets people killed."

"Not anymore. Not here. Look outside."

Outside the window, Vespergate was doing its morning: carts and children and a lamplighter making his rounds in broad daylight because he liked the work, not because he had to; two women arguing cheerfully about whether the new mage-guild banner was ugly (it was); a boy chasing a cat that was almost certainly someone's familiar and enjoying the status enormously. Nobody was hiding. Half of them were wearing charms openly—little woven things, glass beads, knotted cords—out loud, in the sun.

"They're not afraid," Wren said.

"Some of them are. Some of them remember what we cost them, even if it wasn't us doing the costing." Noor's mouth twisted. "There was a woman yesterday who spat at the doorstep."

"What did you do?"

"I swept it up and gave her a free headache-charm, because she looked like she had headaches."

Wren stared at them, then burst out laughing, the rusty hinge swinging wide. "You're impossible. You're the most impossible person alive. That's the most Noor answer in the history of answers."

"It's called customer service."

"It's called being kind in a way that costs you, and you learned it in the worst school there is, and someday you're going to believe you deserved kindness too, and I intend to be there when it happens so I can say I told you so."

Noor didn't answer, but they squeezed her hands, and then—this was the part Wren hoarded, kept in the treasury of her heart next to the rain-song—they leaned in and rested their forehead against hers for a moment, the way they used to when they were small and the world was only as big as the shop.

Then they straightened, cleared their throat, and said, "Compromise. Shelves by function, but each section gets a feeling-name. 'Luck' becomes 'For When the World Is Against You.'"

"'Protection' becomes 'For the Nights You Can't Sleep,'" Wren said instantly.

"'Mending' becomes... 'For Things You Thought Were Broken Past Fixing.'"

They looked at each other. Neither of them mentioned that the last one described approximately everything in their family, including the family.

"That one goes in the front window," Wren said, voice not entirely steady.

"Yes," Noor agreed. "Front window."

*

Opening day, the whole ridiculous family showed up.

Sable arrived early, ostensibly to check the door-hinges, actually because he couldn't stay away, and ended up fixing three hinges, a shutter, and a chair nobody had complained about. He'd been sober eleven months now, and his hands were steady, and the ward-commission had him drawing honest wards all over the city—wards that protected instead of consumed—and he talked about load-bearing sigils the way other men talked about a lover, which Odile found endlessly entertaining and told him so at every opportunity.

Odile arrived with charts. She always arrived with charts. The sky was fully restored now—all the stars home, caged no longer—and she'd spent the months since mapping them, publishing, lecturing, and generally becoming the most famous astronomer in living memory, a fate she regarded with deep suspicion. "Fame is a weather condition," she said, unrolling a star-chart across the counter. "It happens to you. You wait it out." The chart was a gift: the autumn constellations, drawn fresh, with one new star inked in at the edge in her own hand, labeled in tiny precise script: *the Taster's Star — observed first the night the mountain fell.*

"We are not naming stars after ourselves," Noor said.

"You didn't. I did. It's already official. The guild ratified it. Argue with the guild."

Aurel came last, hooded like a criminal despite having legally been king-in-all-but-name for half a year, carrying a wrapped bundle and wearing the expression of a man about to commit a social error he'd already decided to enjoy. He'd abdicated the tribute, dissolved the old council, taken the ledgers public—and discovered that governing honestly was harder than governing corruptly, a discovery he shared with anyone who'd listen, usually at length, usually over Noor's cooking.

"For the shop," he said, setting the bundle on the counter. "An opening gift. Don't refuse it, I'll only sulk, and I'm very bad at sulking, I've been told it's undignified."

Inside the wrapping was a bell. Small, brass, old-fashioned, the kind that jingles when a door opens.

"A doorbell," Noor said flatly.

"A *warded* doorbell. It rings differently depending on who comes in. Friendly customers get a bright ring. Trouble gets a low one. I had it made by the best bell-smith in the city and warded by the second-best ward-smith, because the best one was busy fixing hinges, apparently." Aurel nodded at Sable, who went pink. "It seemed fitting. A shop that spent twenty years listening for footsteps at the door should get to hear something pleasant instead."

Wren tested it immediately, walking out and in six times. Bright ring, bright ring, bright ring. On the seventh entry she brought in the cat from outside—the familiar, who had apparently appointed himself to the household—and got a bright ring with, she swore, a slightly warmer tone.

"He likes the cat better than me," Aurel said, wounded.

"The cat has better intentions," said Odile.

The first real customer came an hour later: the lamplighter's daughter, Marta, all grown now, running her father's old rounds because he'd finally retired to a chair by the sea. She bought a luck-knot for her own daughter, paid full price over Noor's objections, and stood in the middle of the shop looking around with wet eyes.

"My da used to talk about this place," she said. "Before. He said there was a charm-wright on this street who fixed his lantern-charm once and wouldn't take coin, just made him promise to light the corner lamp extra bright on dark nights. He did it for nineteen years. Every dark night. Even after the stars went out—*especially* then. He said somebody had to keep faith till the lights came back." She wiped her face briskly. "He'll cry when I tell him you're open. He'll pretend he isn't crying, but he will."

After she left, Wren and Noor stood together behind the counter in the settling quiet, the brass bell fading, the afternoon light coming through the front window onto the shelf marked FOR THINGS YOU THOUGHT WERE BROKEN PAST FIXING.

"Nineteen years of lamps," Wren said softly.

"People keep faith longer than you'd think," Noor said. "Somebody told me that once. Took me twenty years to believe them."

Wren bumped their shoulder. Outside, the morning glories had climbed another foot up the neighbor's wall, ambitious and illegal and utterly unbothered, blooming in the open air of a free city.

"Tomorrow," she said, "we inventory the dried lavender. And tomorrow after that, I'm teaching you the bloom-charm, and you're going to pretend to be skeptical, and then you're going to be secretly delighted."

"I would never."

"You absolutely will. I know all your faces again now. All of them. It's the one advantage of dying—I came back with nothing to do but study you."

Noor smiled—that rare, whole, unhurried smile, the one that cost them nothing anymore—and began, absurdly, to hum the rain-song under their breath while they straightened jars.

The bell rang bright as the door opened, and Wren turned to greet whoever came in, hungry as ever for the day—

—and stopped.

In the doorway stood a woman Wren knew. Older, grayer, but unmistakable: the magistrate who had signed the order for her arrest, twenty years ago. She stood on the threshold of the charm shop with her hat in her hands, and behind her, filling the street, a crowd was gathering—neighbors, customers, the curious—drawn by something. By rumor. By the kind of news that travels fast in a city learning to speak plainly again.

"Wren Nakamura," the old magistrate said, and her voice shook. "I owe you testimony. And I think—" she glanced back at the crowd, then at the ledger under her arm, bound in faded blue, "—I think the whole city owes you an accounting. May I come in?"

The bell hung silent above the door, waiting to tell them what she was.
