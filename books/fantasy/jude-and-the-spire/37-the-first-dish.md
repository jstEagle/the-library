## 37. The First Dish

The kitchen of the charm shop had never been meant for cooking. It had been meant for drying herbs, for steeping charms in moonwater, for the small alchemies that kept a taster alive. But the herb racks were empty now—burned, all of them, on a night Noor still dreamed about—and so the shelves held plates instead. Chipped ones. Mismatched ones. A whole shelf of plates that had never once been used, because Noor Nakamura had never once eaten a meal they hadn't first tested against twenty years of accumulated poison.

Today, Wren was setting the table.

They did it slowly, the way they did everything since coming back from the mountain—as if their hands were still learning the weight of things. A plate here. A fork there, turned over and examined, its tines counted twice. Twenty years underground will do that to a person, Odile said. Twenty years as a fragment of light in a lantern-vault, doing the same small task forever, until the task is all you are.

Wren set down a third plate and looked at it for a long moment.

"Three," they said. Their voice was better these days. Stronger. Some mornings it even sounded like the voice in Noor's memory, the one that used to sing on the shop roof while rain hammered the tiles. "We're three today."

"Aurel's coming," Noor said, and then felt their ears go warm, because saying it aloud made it real in a way that the prince's actual presence somehow didn't.

Wren smiled—not the dim, puzzled smile of the vaults but a real one, crooked at one corner, the corner Noor had inherited. "You said that like someone confessing to a crime."

"In this city, feeding a prince in your back room *is* a crime. Or it was. Or it might be again by Thursday. Politics moves fast lately."

"Mm." Wren smoothed the cloth over the table, and for a moment their hands went still, and Noor saw them counting something invisible—the way they still sometimes counted, reaching for a ledger that no longer existed. Then they blinked it away. "Noor. Are you frightened?"

The question landed exactly where questions like that always landed: in the pit of the stomach, where Noor kept everything they'd never said out loud.

"No," Noor said.

"You're a terrible liar," Wren said. "You always were. You once told Mother the cat brought the broken teacup home itself."

"The cat *did* bring it home. I merely failed to stop it."

"You were holding the teacup when she walked in."

"That's slander," Noor said, "and also ancient history," and Wren laughed, and the sound filled the little kitchen the way lamplight fills a room—all at once, everywhere, leaving no corners dark—and Noor thought: *this*. This is what I drank the poison for. Not the mountain, not the contract, not the tearing of the page. This exact sound, in this exact room, impossible and ordinary.

Then someone knocked at the shop door, and Noor's heart did the thing it always did at knocking—because for twenty years, a knock had meant wardens—and then remembered itself, because these days a knock mostly meant customers, or Sable arriving drunk on joy rather than gin, or Odile with her arms full of star-charts and opinions.

It was Aurel.

He came in the back way, as he always did, having left his guards three streets off—a habit he claimed was humility and everyone knew was hunger. He wore plain clothes, well-made but plain, the uniform of a man practicing at being a person. In his hands he carried a covered dish, which he presented to Noor with the gravity of a state gift.

"I cooked," he said.

"Oh no," said Wren.

"It's not—" Aurel began.

"He cooked," Wren confirmed, delighted. "Twenty years I was gone and I come back to find my sibling being fed by royalty. What did you make?"

"Something safe," Aurel said, with dignity. "I asked the physicians' guild what dishes carry the least risk for an unshielded constitution. They gave me a list. I chose the item at the top."

"What was at the top?" Noor asked.

"Plain rice porridge."

Wren made a noise like a kettle giving up. "You crossed the city at dawn to bring us *porridge*."

"I crossed the city at dawn," Aurel said, "to stand beside your sibling while they eat their first meal in twenty years without a wall of poison between them and death. The porridge is incidental. The porridge is *symbolic*." He paused. "Also I may have added salt. The physicians said no salt. I decided the physicians were cowards."

* * *

Here is what Noor had never told anyone, not Wren, not Odile, not the prince standing in their kitchen holding a symbolic pot: that they could not remember the taste of food.

Not truly. Oh, they knew flavors the way a scholar knows a language they've only read—could name bitter, could identify almond, could detect nightshade under six layers of sauce from across a banquet hall. But knowing the name of a thing and feeling it are different countries, and Noor had spent two decades in the first one. Every meal of their adult life had been an interrogation. Every spoonful passed through the tongue's cold tribunal—*safe, unsafe, trace amounts, discard*—before ever being permitted to become pleasure. Taste as testimony. Flavor as evidence. The tongue as a ledger, always open, always auditing.

And now the ledger was closed. Burned, like the charms. Spent, like the immunity, poured out in one long swallow in the dark beneath the mountain so that a living body could pass through the soul-barrier and come back carrying a sister.

The physicians had been very clear about what remained. Nothing. No resistance at all. A single dish of badly stored shellfish could kill them. A cook who confused hemlock for parsley would kill them. The world, which Noor had spent twenty years making harmless through sheer stubborn chemistry, was dangerous again in every direction, at every meal, forever.

Aurel had heard the physicians say it and had gone very quiet, and later that evening he'd said, in the careful voice he used when he was about to spend something he couldn't afford: "I know what it is to have your body be a public instrument. Mine was bred for succession. Yours was built for survival. Neither of us got to choose." And then, after a silence: "So let me choose with you. Whatever you eat, I'll eat. If it kills you, it kills me too. That way you'll never sit at a table alone again."

Noor had not known what to say to that. They still didn't. So they'd said, "That's the most impractical use of a crown prince I've ever heard," and Aurel had said, "I've been assured I'm full of impractical ideas; the council keeps minutes," and that had been that. An agreement sealed without ceremony, the way the important ones always are.

Now the porridge sat steaming in its bowl, and the three of them sat around the table that had never been used, and Noor looked at the spoon and found their hand would not move.

"It smells good," Wren said gently. "That's allowed, you know. Smelling."

"I know what's allowed."

"You're stalling."

"I'm *savoring the anticipation*, which is a sophisticated pleasure unavailable to people who rush."

Aurel picked up his own spoon, dipped it, and ate a mouthful with the deliberate calm of a man proving something. He chewed. He swallowed. He did not die. "There," he said. "The kingdom stands. The succession remains intact, more's the pity for my cousins. Your turn."

Noor lifted the spoon.

The porridge was white, faintly gold at the edges where the salt had touched it, and it trembled slightly, and Noor thought absurdly of the barrier beneath the mountain, the shimmering veil they'd walked through with death in their veins, and how this was the same thing, really, just smaller. Just daily. Just the rest of their life, three times a day, forever: a small crossing, a small risk, a small act of faith that the world would let them keep what they'd bought back.

They put the spoon in their mouth.

For a moment, nothing. The old machinery tried to engage—the audit, the tribunal, the cold voice listing compounds—and found nothing to do, and faltered, and fell silent. And underneath where the auditor used to sit, something else woke up. Something that had been waiting twenty years, patient as a seed.

Warmth. Salt. The soft grain dissolving against the tongue, each grain distinct and then not distinct, becoming texture, becoming comfort. And the salt—Aurel's cowardice-defying, physician-angering salt—bloomed last, a bright clean note that made the whole bowl suddenly *mean* something, the way a single word can make a poem.

Noor put down the spoon very carefully, because their hands were shaking.

"Well?" Wren whispered.

"It tastes," Noor said, and their voice cracked in the middle of the word, and they had to stop, and press the heel of their hand against their eyes, and laugh at themselves, wetly, helplessly, because twenty years of discipline and it came undone over *porridge*. "It tastes like—it tastes like food. It tastes like eating. Do you understand? I've tasted ten thousand meals and I've never once *eaten*—"

Wren reached across the table and took their hand, and Aurel, bless him, pretended to be fascinated by the ceiling, and gave them the moment.

When Noor could speak again, they said, "It's the best thing I've ever tasted and it's porridge. Plain rice porridge. You've ruined me. Every meal for the rest of my life will be measured against physician-approved gruel."

"I contain multitudes," Aurel said solemnly, and ladled out seconds.

* * *

They ate the whole pot. It took an hour, because Wren kept stopping to ask questions about flavor—*and this is good? Salt is good? Describe good*—with the fierce appetite of someone who'd spent twenty years being light and wanted, now, to inventory everything a body could feel. Aurel answered most of them, surprisingly well; it emerged that the palace kitchens had been his refuge during his father's reign, the one place the crown prince could stand in the steam and noise and be nobody's heir. He described caramelizing onions with the reverence other men reserved for battlefields.

"And the danger doesn't spoil it?" Wren asked Noor. "Knowing every bite could be the one?"

Noor considered this properly, spoon resting, steam rising.

"It changes it," they admitted. "Every mouthful is a decision now. I have to trust the cook, the market, the water, the air. I have to trust—" they gestured at the table, the room, the two faces watching them— "*you*. All of it. There's no wall anymore. There's just me, walking around unarmored, betting my life on the world three times a day."

"And?"

"And it turns out," Noor said, "that the armor was heavier than the risk. I spent twenty years so busy surviving that I forgot to find out if I liked anything. This morning I learned I like salt. I'm thirty-four years old and I learned that *today*."

Aurel snorted into his bowl. "Thirty-five."

"We are not discussing my birthday."

"You're thirty-five and you like salt and you cry at porridge. These are the facts of record."

"These are *slander*—"

"The cat brought the teacup home itself," Wren said serenely, and both siblings dissolved, and Aurel watched them with an expression Noor caught sideways and couldn't name—something like homesickness for a home he'd never had—and decided, generously, not to mention it.

Later, when the bowls were empty and the light through the kitchen window had gone honey-colored, Wren went out front to open the shop, and Noor and Aurel stayed at the table doing the dishes, because Aurel had insisted—he'd discovered washing dishes the same month he'd discovered palace kitchens, apparently, and guarded the privilege jealously—and because neither of them seemed able to end the afternoon.

"You flinched," Aurel said quietly, after a while. "At the knock. When I arrived."

Noor scrubbed a bowl. "Old habit."

"It's not going away, is it. The fear. Even now. Even with everything changed."

"No," Noor said. "I don't think fear goes away. I think you just get to choose what it's attached to." They handed him the bowl. "Twenty years ago I was afraid of wardens and poison and my own reflection telling on me. Now I'm afraid of shellfish and losing you lot. It's a promotion, of a kind."

Aurel dried the bowl, set it down, and stood there with his hands empty, which for him was always the posture that preceded honesty. "The council reconvenes next week. On the magic question. There's a faction—I won't bore you with names—that wants the old laws restored. Registration. Collars, effectively. They're using the mountain court as proof: *see what magic does, see what it costs.*" His jaw tightened. "As if the wards weren't bought with souls. As if the danger was ever the magic and not the men who hoarded it."

"Odile knows," Noor said. "She's been reading the mood of the quarter for weeks. She says the neighbors are kind to her face and write petitions behind her back."

"Some of those petitions cross my desk." Aurel met Noor's eyes. "I'm going to fight it. Publicly. Which means everything we four did becomes public too—the blackmail, the false bargain I offered the ministers, the seal I spent. My enemies will dig. And when they dig, they'll find a royal food-taster who was an illegal mage for twenty years, and they will use you to reach me."

"They can try."

"Noor." He said it gently, and that gentleness was worse than shouting. "I'm not asking you to hide. I'm asking you to know what's coming. Because the woman who runs the bakery on Cordwainer Street—kind woman, waves to Odile every morning—she signed one of those petitions. The world doesn't change all at once. It changes the way your tongue did this morning: one taste at a time, some of them bitter."

Noor looked out the window, toward the market square where, a month ago, they had read forty thousand names aloud from a burned ledger while the whole city stood silent in the rain. The city had wept. The city had helped burn the pages. And the city had gone home and, some of it, started writing new lists.

"All right," Noor said at last. "Then we'd better give them something worth finding."

Aurel frowned. "What do you mean?"

"I mean I'm done being found out," Noor said. "Found out implies I was hiding something shameful. Next time, I want to be the one who steps forward. On my own feet, in my own name, before anyone digs anything up." They untied the apron, folded it, and set it on the table between them like a gauntlet. "Wren and I reopened the shop legally. Fine. But there are mages in this city still selling luck-knots in alleys because they think the law will turn on them again. Someone they trust has to show them it's safe to come inside."

"Noor," Aurel said slowly, "what are you planning?"

Noor smiled, and it was, they suspected, the same crooked smile Wren wore, the family smile, the one that had preceded every disaster and every miracle of their childhood.

"I'm going to hold a grand opening," they said. "And everyone's invited—including the council faction that wants me collared. Let them come and watch a mage hand out charms for free in broad daylight and see if the sky falls." They glanced toward the front of the shop, where Wren's voice could be heard cheerfully overcharging a customer for a blessing against damp. "Besides. If the world insists on tasting me, it should at least know what's in the dish."

Outside, the first stars were coming out—real stars, uncaged, scattered back across the sky like salt from a generous hand—and somewhere down the street, Noor could hear Odile arguing passionately with someone about charting rights, which meant she'd heard the news already, which meant, Noor realized with a sinking, soaring feeling, that the grand opening was about to acquire a guest lecturer.

Some risks, Noor thought, you walk into with your eyes open and your mouth watering.

They went to tell Wren to make more tea. They were going to need a bigger pot.
