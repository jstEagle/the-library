## 38. Charms for a Free City

The sky had been back for four months when Odile finally admitted she had a favorite star.

She would never say so aloud—astronomers did not have favorites; astronomers had catalogs—but every evening at dusk she climbed the observatory steps with her charts and her cold tea and found herself looking first to the northeast corner of the sky, where a small stubborn point of light sat just above the spire like a cat that had decided a rooftop was its own.

It was the third one she'd freed in the vaults. She remembered it dimming under her hands, remember the moment it caught itself, gathered, and went up through the mine shafts like a struck match going home. She'd named it in the catalog as *Object Ferrant 3*, which was the correct professional thing to do, and then privately, on a scrap of paper she kept folded inside her sleeve, she called it *Wren's Star*, because Noor had been there that night and because some things deserved better names than numbers gave them.

"Odile." Sable's voice floated up the stairwell, accompanied by the smell of pipe smoke and something burning. "Your instruments are doing the thing again."

"What thing?"

"The thing where they hum."

"They're not humming. They're *resonating*. It's different."

"It's humming," Sable said comfortably, appearing at the top of the stairs with soot on his knuckles and an expression Odile had learned over the winter to read correctly: he was happy. Disgraced ward-smiths were not supposed to be happy. He'd told her once, drunk and earnest at three in the morning, that happiness felt like wearing someone else's coat. These days the coat fit.

He crossed the dome to her workbench and squinted at the astrolabe, whose brass rings did indeed shiver with a faint tone, like a wineglass remembering a song. "That's the ward-lattice answering the stars," he said. "I've been rebuilding the city wards off the old honest principles—no purchased power, no souls, just geometry and patience—and every night when your stars come up, my lattice sings back. I keep meaning to write you a letter about it and instead I keep drinking tea with you about it, which is better."

"You could write the letter *and* drink the tea."

"I'm a busy man. I'm employed now. Do you know how strange that is? I have a *desk*. There's a nameplate. I keep touching it to make sure."

Odile smiled and turned back to her chart. Four months of returned sky, and she was still behind on everything—the positions shifted slightly each week as freed stars found their old stations, and half her reference tables were useless, and she had never in her life been so glad to be wrong about so much. The darkness she had charted all those terrible weeks, the breathing black shape above Vespergate, was gone. What remained was ordinary night: vast, indifferent, full of light that asked nothing of anyone.

Below the observatory, the city glowed. And below that—

"Come on," Sable said. "You promised."

"I promised no such thing."

"You promised in spirit. The shop opens at sundown tonight. First legal charm sale in twenty years. You think I'm letting you miss it?"

* * *

The shop had been Wren's once. That was what everyone in the neighborhood knew, though most of them were too polite or too young to say it. It had stood empty for two decades—a shuttered tooth in the row of shops along Candle Lane—until this spring, when a carpenter, a former astronomer, a royal ward-smith, and a prince who insisted on carrying his own lumber had spent six weekends putting it right.

Now the sign hung fresh-painted over the door: **NAKAMURA & NAKAMURA — CHARMS, CHARTS, AND SMALL HONEST WORK**. Noor had argued for omitting their own name entirely. Aurel had argued for gilding. They had compromised on plain letters and a coat of blue paint that Wren chose because, she said, it was the exact color of rain on a roof.

By the time Odile arrived, the lane was already crowded.

Not hostile-crowded. Curious-crowded. Which was, in its way, more dangerous, because crowds came in two flavors here: the ones who wanted to see magic legalized, and the ones who wanted to see it fail.

The shop's interior was small and warm and smelled of cedar shavings and drying herbs. Wren stood behind the counter—she still moved carefully, still spoke softly, as if twenty years underground had taught her habits her body refused to unlearn—and before her, arranged on shelves Noor had built themselves, lay the stock: luck-knots, weather-needles, sleep-sachets, mending-charms for cracked cups, a small rack of lamps that burned without oil.

Noor worked the counter the way they had always worked everything: efficiently, quietly, watchfully. But Odile noticed the difference immediately. Twenty years of being invisible had taught Noor to arrange their face into blankness. Now, watching them hand a luck-knot across the counter to a fishwife, Odile saw something else underneath—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile kept its tools.

"Three coppers," Noor said.

"For luck?"

"For luck that lasts a season. After that it frays, like everything. Bring it back and I'll re-tie it for a copper. I don't sell forever. Nobody can."

The fishwife paid, bit the knot experimentally, pronounced it good, and left beaming.

"That's the trick of it," Sable murmured to Odile, arms crossed, watching from beside a shelf of weather-needles. "Look. Half these people have never bought a charm in their lives. They're not buying magic. They're buying permission."

Aurel was there too, of course. He had tried to come disguised—a hooded cloak, a false beard of genuinely insulting quality—and had lasted eleven minutes before a grandmother recognized him, patted his cheek, and told him the beard made him look like a goat. Now he sat on a stool by the window in his plainest coat, handing wrapped parcels down to children who had figured out within minutes that the tall man by the door was the prince and had organized themselves into a queue accordingly.

"You know," Odile said, drifting over, "you could send a servant."

"I *am* the servant," Aurel said with dignity, passing down a parcel. "It's very restful. Nobody asks me anything difficult. The last person who asked me something difficult was a boy of about seven who wanted to know if I could make his frog talk. I said diplomacy was beyond even a prince. His mother laughed so hard she bought two charms."

Through the open door, the evening light came in gold, and Odile watched the line of customers move slowly forward—lamplighters, dockworkers, a nun, two students arguing over a chart of the new constellations, an old man who bought a sleep-sachet and told Wren, in a voice gone suddenly careful, that his wife had died in the famine years and he hadn't slept properly since, and did the sachet work on grief or only on tiredness?

Wren held the sachet a long moment before answering. "Only on tiredness," she said gently. "But grief is lighter when you're rested. That's not magic. That's just true."

He bought it anyway.

* * *

Trouble came at moonrise, as trouble does.

Odile saw him first from the doorway—a big man, broad through the shoulders, standing across the lane with his arms folded and his face set in the particular expression of a man who has rehearsed an argument. She'd seen that expression before. In the years before decriminalization, she'd seen it on wardens. On neighbors. Once, horribly, on a mob outside the old records office, the night they burned the mage-registers and everything in them, including three years of her own observations.

This man didn't have a mob. He had a daughter, actually—a girl of perhaps ten who stood beside him looking mortified, tugging his sleeve.

"Papa, we *came* to buy one—"

"We came to look," the man said loudly, in the voice people use when they want the whole street to hear. "We came to look at what this city's come to. Selling curses on Candle Lane now. Out in the open. My father helped build the wards that kept this city safe, and those wards were built on law, and now—" He ran out of argument and settled for volume. "Now *this*."

The lane went quiet. Customers stopped mid-purchase. Odile saw Noor go very still behind the counter—that old stillness, the taster's stillness, the body reading danger before the mind names it—and she saw Wren's hands flatten against the wood.

And then Noor stepped out from behind the counter and walked to the door.

"Noor," Odile breathed, moving to follow.

"*Wait.*" It was barely a sound. Wren's hand on her wrist, light as a moth. "Watch. This is theirs. This is the part they've been practicing."

Noor stopped three paces from the man. Close enough to talk. Far enough to leave him his dignity, which Odile understood, a beat later, was the entire calculation.

"You're Halver Senn?" Noor said. "Cooper, off Tanner's Row?"

The man blinked. "What of it?"

"You built the barrels for the relief ships during the flood year. Eight years ago. Your coopering held. People ate because your staves didn't split." A pause. "My sister told me about you. She worked the relief kitchens."

Something flickered in the man's face—the specific discomfort of a man whose anger has just been handed evidence against itself. "I don't see what that has to do with—"

"It has everything to do with it," Noor said. "You're angry because the law changed overnight and nobody asked you. You're right to be. It did happen fast. Twenty years of fear doesn't drain out of a city in a season, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something." They gestured at the shop behind them, dry as dust. "I'm literally selling something. So let me tell you what's true instead."

The lane listened. Even the children.

"The charms in there are knots and herbs and a little craft I learned from a woman who's dead because the old law killed her. Nothing in that shop can hurt you. Nothing in it ever needed to be illegal, and the only reason it was is that fear is easier to govern than trust. If a luck-knot frightens you, don't buy one. If it comforts you, buy one. Either way, you'll live, and so will I, and your barrels will still hold water, because that was never magic. That was you."

Silence. The man's jaw worked. His daughter tugged his sleeve again, harder.

"Papa. *Please.* Marta has one. Everybody has one."

"Marta's mother lets her do whatever—"

"She got it from *Grandmama*," the girl said, with the crushing authority of a ten-year-old armed with facts, "and Grandmama says if you make us leave without one she's going to tell everyone at market you cried at the wedding play."

The man closed his eyes. Odile bit the inside of her cheek very hard.

"...Two luck-knots," he said finally, to no one, to the gods, to the unfairness of daughters. "And if either of them makes my roof leak—"

"They're luck-knots, not roofing," Noor said, and something in their voice had warmed by a single degree, which from Noor was practically a festival. "Come inside. I'll show you how they're tied. It's less impressive than you think. Most true things are."

They went in together, the cooper ducking under the lintel like a bear entering a tent, his daughter already chattering. And Wren exhaled beside Odile, a long slow breath twenty years deep.

"You knew he'd come," Odile said.

"Noor's known for weeks. Keeps a list." Wren nodded toward the counter, and Odile saw it—a small notebook, tucked by the till, filled in Noor's cramped hand. Names. Trades. Grievances. Next to each name, in smaller script: *what they lost, what they fear, what they love.*

"Healing isn't a spell," Wren said, watching her sibling wrap two luck-knots with absurd care, choosing the string color the girl pointed at. "There's no charm for it. It's just showing up every day until the story people tell about you changes. Noor spent twenty years learning how to survive people. Turns out that's also how you learn to reach them."

On the stool by the window, Aurel had stopped parceling. He was watching Noor with an expression Odile recognized because she'd worn it herself, in the vault, watching stars come free: the look of someone seeing a thing done well and understanding, freshly, how much it cost.

* * *

Later—much later, past midnight, after the last customer and the sweeping and the argument between Noor and Wren about whether the shutters went up before or after the tea (after; it was always after)—the five of them sat in the shop's back room around a table that had clearly been a door in a previous life, eating a late supper of bread, cheese, pickles, and a stew that Sable claimed was his recipe and which everyone else agreed was mostly onions with ambitions.

Odile spread her charts on the table, because she was incapable of sitting near flat surfaces without covering them in astronomy, and because she had news.

"The sky's nearly settled," she said. "Ninety-one percent of the freed stars are back on their old stations. The rest are drifting—slowly, deliberately, like people taking the long way home after a long absence. I expect they'll settle by autumn." She tapped a corner of the chart. "But here's the strange part. The gaps haven't filled in."

"Gaps?" Aurel said.

"Where stars were destroyed. Not caged—destroyed, back in the early centuries of the bargain, before the court learned caging was cheaper. There are holes in the sky, real ones, and the freed stars are avoiding them. Circling them." She looked around the table. "New stars take centuries to form. But these holes are... tidy. As if something removed them cleanly. And last week, for the first time, I measured one changing."

Sable put down his fork. "Changing how?"

"Getting smaller."

The room went quiet. Outside, the new sign creaked in the night wind, and somewhere down the lane a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"So the mountain's not done," Aurel said. His voice had gone careful, princely, the voice he used when he was frightened and refusing to show it. "The bargain broke, but something of it survived. Something is still... eating."

"Or something else is still *feeding*," Odile said. "I don't know yet. That's why I'm telling you instead of the council—I won't raise an alarm on a measurement. But I've requested observation time on the far-north survey, the deep-sky plates, the ones nobody's looked at in years." She paused. "I'd like company."

"You always have company," Wren said simply.

"Yes," Odile said, and found, to her mild surprise, that her eyes had gone hot. She covered it by rolling up the chart. "Well. Someone has to carry the tea."

Noor was watching her across the table with that steady dark gaze that missed nothing, that had survived poison and courts and twenty years of listening. "How long?" they asked. "Before you know."

"A month. Maybe six weeks."

Noor nodded once and reached for the teapot. "Then we have a month," they said, pouring, unhurried, the lamplighter's daughter's ribbon still caught on their sleeve where the girl had hugged them goodbye, "to learn everything we can about holes in the sky. Somebody get me paper. I'll start a list."

* * *

Odile climbed home alone near dawn, as she always did, up the observatory hill with her collar up against the river damp. At the top, she did what she did every morning now, the ritual she'd never had before the dark months: she checked.

The sky was there. All of it—or ninety-one percent of it, the patient returning majority, wheeling westward toward morning. Her small stubborn star sat above the spire exactly where she'd left it.

Except.

She stopped on the top step, chart case forgotten in her hand.

Northeast. Just above the spire. The space where Wren's Star should be was—empty. Not dimmed, not drifted. Empty, with the neat clean edges of a hole that had been measured once before, in the north, in her own careful ink.

As she watched, heart slamming, the hole began, very slowly, to grow.

Down in the sleeping city, the rebuilt ward-lattice gave a single low note of alarm—a hum, a resonance, a warning sung in Sable's honest geometry—and Odile was already running down the hill, charts flying, toward Candle Lane, where a light was coming on in a charm shop window ahead of the sun.
