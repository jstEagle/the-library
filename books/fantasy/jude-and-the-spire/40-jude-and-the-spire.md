## 40. Jude and the Spire

The spire had been Sable's idea, which surprised no one who knew him, and had nearly been cancelled four times, which surprised everyone who didn't.

"It's a load-bearing apology," he'd told the council, gesturing at his own drawings with chalk-dusted fingers. "The old one was built on the soul-bargain. Every stone of it was paid for with somebody's name in a ledger. You don't fix that by patching the roof. You fix it by building something new, with honest mortar, and putting it where the whole city has to look at it."

The council had hemmed. The council had hawed. The council had, in the end, discovered that the city had already started calling the ruin at the old spire's foot "the place where the stars came back," and that public sentiment is a force even councilmen cannot ward against.

So they built it. Two years of building. Sable supervised every joint and every ward-line himself, and if his wards hummed a little when you touched them—warm, like a cat that had decided to tolerate you—no one complained. Honest magic, freely given, turned out to have a sound to it. Noor had never known that before. Most of what Noor had known before turned out to be wrong in ways that were, on balance, worth the finding out.

Now it was the third anniversary of the dawn without stars, and the city had declared it a holiday, and Noor was standing at the base of the new spire with a luck-knot in their pocket and a family of four insufferable people waiting for them to stop dawdling.

"You're stalling," said Wren.

"I am *savoring*," said Noor. "There's a difference. One of them is dignified."

"You've been savoring for ten minutes."

"Fine things take time."

Wren laughed—that laugh, the one Noor had spent twenty years forgetting and two years relearning, the one that still landed in their chest like rain on a shop roof—and hooked their arm through Noor's, and pulled.

* * *

The climb was four hundred and eleven steps. Odile had counted. Odile counted everything now; it was what she did instead of sleeping, she said, and since the sky had come back she'd had a great deal of cataloguing to catch up on. Three years of vanished stars, she'd explained once, over Noor's kitchen table, with the fervor of a woman describing a love affair, was three years of data no astronomer had ever collected. "Do you know what the sky looks like *after*? No one knew. Now I know. It's slightly different. It's *slightly different*, Noor, and I am going to write the paper that explains why, and it is going to be insufferable, and I cannot wait."

She was halfway up the spire already, calling down measurements to a pair of apprentices who had long since learned to nod and write down whatever she said. Her hair had gone almost entirely silver, which she claimed was from starlight exposure and which Sable claimed was from her apprentices, and which Noor privately suspected was from the paper.

Sable climbed like a man who had built every step and was checking each one's homework personally. He stopped on the two hundredth step to adjust a ward-anchor with a small brass tool, muttered something unrepeatable about mortar, and caught up looking pleased with himself.

Aurel climbed last, which was a habit of his now. Princes, Noor had learned, are watched from the front and the sides, and the only place a man like Aurel could find any peace was behind everyone, where nobody thought to look. He'd gotten better at being looked at, over the years. He still preferred not to be.

"Your pardon's still in my pocket," he said, falling into step beside Noor around the three hundredth landing. "The real one. Your name on it, in my hand, countersigned. I carry it everywhere."

"You've shown it to me eleven times."

"Twelve. You weren't counting the time at the harbor."

"I was counting. I was being polite about it."

He smiled—that crooked, self-deprecating smile that had taken him two years and one dissolved council to grow into—and looked out over the city through the spire's open lattice work. "It's just that I spent so long being the thing people were afraid of. The crown. The teeth. I like carrying the opposite of that. I like that it weighs nothing and means everything." He paused. "Also I like annoying you."

"There it is."

* * *

The top of the spire was a small round platform, open to the sky on all sides, ringed with a low parapet and a single iron hook at its highest point. Sable had forged the hook himself and warded it himself and then, on the first anniversary, had stood under it for an hour in the rain until Noor came and found him and asked what he was doing.

"Waiting," he'd said.

"For what?"

"For you to be ready. You'll know when." He'd wrung out his hat and gone inside, and Noor had stood under the hook for a while after that, and had not been ready, and had gone home, and had not thought about it constantly, except for constantly.

Now, on the third anniversary, with the luck-knot warm in their pocket, Noor was ready.

It was Wren's knot. It had always been Wren's knot—braided in a shop that no longer existed, out of red thread and river-grass, by a girl of seventeen who had believed, with the unearned confidence of seventeen, that a little braided charm could keep her little sibling safe. It had kept Noor safe. Not the way charms were supposed to work; not with warding or luck. It had kept them safe the way a promise keeps you safe. It had given them something to be *for*.

Noor had carried it through the trial and the grief and the twenty years of tasting and the night the stars went out and the dark under the mountain and the long climb out with their body failing and Wren's voice—thin, faint, whole—singing the shop-roof song. They had hung it in the new charm shop's window when they reopened, legally, with a license Wren had framed and dusted weekly. And once a year, on this day, they brought it up here, and hung it from the hook, and let it turn in the wind over the city for one full day and night.

The first year, Wren had asked why.

"Because it worked," Noor had said. "And things that work should be thanked out loud, where the whole city can see. That's the whole religion, Wren. That's all of it."

* * *

The wind at the top was the good kind, the kind that smelled like the harbor and bread from the market below and, faintly, the river-grass that now grew wild along the old ward-lines because Sable had seeded them as a living warning system and they had gotten ideas above their station. The city spread out beneath them: rooftops and market stalls and the flooded archive, now drained and open to any citizen who wanted to read what their grandparents had paid for. The lamplighters were lighting the evening lamps one by one, and Noor found the lamplighter's daughter among them—head lamplighter now, her father's route, her father's careful hands—and raised a hand, and she raised hers back.

"You're doing the face," Wren said.

"What face?"

"The one where you're pretending you're just looking at the city but actually you're doing an accounting of everything you almost lost."

"I don't have a face."

"You have eleven faces. I invented most of them. That's the accounting one."

Noor laughed and took the luck-knot out of their pocket. The red thread had faded to the color of late roses. The river-grass had gone soft as old paper. It was, objectively, a wreck of a charm, held together by three years of sunlight and stubbornness, and Noor would have fought anyone who suggested replacing it.

They hung it on the hook. The wind took it, and it turned, and turned, and caught the last of the daylight, and the whole small red weight of it swung out over the city like a held breath finally let go.

Below, in the square, someone had started the song. It spread the way songs do—shop-roof song, rain-song, the one Wren had sung in the dark of the mountain when Noor's heart was stuttering out its last counted beats. Three hundred voices, off-key, unbothered by it.

Odile sat down on the parapet with the ease of a woman who had spent her life on observatory catwalks and patted the stone beside her. "Sit. The sky's about to do the thing."

"The sky doesn't do things on schedule."

"The sky does *everything* on schedule. That's the whole point of the sky. Watch."

And the sun went down, and the sky did the thing: the first stars came out, one by one, the ones Odile had unlocked from their lanterns, the ones that had spent three years in a cage and come home changed. She'd named the changed ones. There was a whole appendix in her insufferable paper. The one directly above the spire she'd called Wren's Star, over Wren's loud objections, and it had stuck, and Wren had secretly had a charm made of it and given it to Odile, and Odile wore it every day and pretended it was nothing.

Sable stood at the parapet with his forearms on the stone, watching his wards hum in the evening air. He was sober now—three years sober, and he talked about it the way he talked about ward-work: as maintenance, as daily honest labor, as a thing you did because the alternative was a lie you couldn't live in. Some nights were hard. He came to the shop on the hard nights, and Wren fed him, and Noor sat with him, and nobody made it a lesson.

"Hey, taster," he said, without turning around.

"Smith."

"You ever think about how it started? All this. You, tasting porridge, being invisible."

"Daily."

"Me too." He turned. His face was doing something complicated and he let it, which was also new. "I testified against your sister. I built the wards that ran on her. I spent thirty years trying to drink that down and it never once worked. And then—" he gestured at the knot, turning red against the first stars, "—then you let me carry her up a mountain. You didn't forgive me. You just kept walking, and you let me walk too. I've thought a lot about that. I think that might be the bigger thing. Forgiveness is a door closing. You gave me a road."

Noor looked at him for a long moment. "You're going to make me say something sincere, aren't you."

"Absolutely not. God, no. I take it back."

"You kept walking too, Sable. That's the whole of it. That's all anybody did." Noor bumped his shoulder. "Also you still owe me for the luck-knot you bought in chapter—"

"Don't you dare cite chapters at me."

"—in the *night market*, all those years ago, and never paid for."

"I paid for it in back and breath up a mountain, you vulture."

"Debts are debts. I keep a ledger."

"You do *not* keep a ledger. You burned the ledger. It's the whole—there's a *statue*—"

"There's a plaque, and the plaque says *never again*, not *never keep accounts*, and I'll thank you to—"

"Both of you stop," Wren said serenely, "or I'm singing."

They stopped. Wren's singing was a known weapon. Aurel had once heard Wren sing in the bath at the palace guest quarters and had described the experience to the city council as "a sovereign matter of national security."

* * *

Aurel had drifted to the far side of the platform, and Noor went to him, because someone always did, on this day especially. He was looking east, toward the palace, where the lights were coming on in the council chamber. He still attended council. He'd refused the throne twice and then, when it became clear the city would simply keep asking, had accepted a third of it—a shared seat, a rotating council, a crown he kept in a drawer and referred to as "the hat."

"Your father's portrait came down last month," he said, when Noor reached him. "They put up the market square instead. The reading of the names. There's a mural." His jaw worked. "I'm in it. In the corner. Small."

"You'd hate to be large in it."

"I'd hate to be in it at all. I'm in it because Odile insisted, and because—" he exhaled. "Because it's true, I suppose. I was there. I ate off those plates. I slept in that palace while the wards drank the city." He looked at Noor. "Do you ever miss it? The tasting?"

Noor considered the question honestly, because Aurel had earned honest answers at the rate of about one per year, and this was the day for it.

"I miss the certainty," Noor said. "Knowing exactly what was safe. Knowing the rules of my own body like the back of my hand. I spent twenty years building that immunity, drop by drop, and it was the only thing in the world that was entirely mine." They turned their hands over, ordinary hands, hands that could now be killed by a badly stored mushroom. "And then I spent it. On purpose. And some mornings I wake up and the fear is right there, the old fear, the *be small, be useful, be nothing* fear, and I have to get up anyway and taste the soup with my whole mortal tongue."

"And?"

"And it's the best soup I've ever had." Noor grinned. "Every time. Wren makes it with too much pepper and I can *feel* the pepper, Aurel, it's a scandal, it's magnificent. Fear and flavor turn out to live in the same house. Nobody told me. I had to burn down my whole immunity to find out."

Aurel laughed—the real laugh, the one without the court in it—and then said, quietly, "Next week. The harvest festival. The kitchens want to make something for you. All of us. They asked what's safe."

"Nothing's safe. That's the menu. Write it on a card."

"I'll have it embroidered."

* * *

The dark came down soft and full of stars. Odile was narrating constellations to a growing crowd of children who had climbed the spire after her, which was against about six regulations and which the guards, wisely, were pretending not to see. Sable had gotten into an argument with one of his own ward-anchors and was losing. Wren stood at the parapet with the wind in their hair, twenty years older than the girl who'd braided the knot and somehow exactly the same age, and sang the song with the crowd, off-key, unbothered.

Noor stood in the middle of all of it, under the turning knot, and did the accounting after all. Not of what they'd almost lost. Of what they'd almost *been*—the taster who would have died useful and unremarkable in a palace kitchen, a body that was only a ledger, a life that was only a survival. That person had been so sure that invisibility was the prize. That person had spent twenty years getting ready for one terrible dawn and had never once imagined what came after it.

What came after it was this: pepper soup and insufferable papers and a drunk who became a builder and a prince who kept a pardon in his pocket and a sister learning the new streets one market stall at a time, greedy for every day. What came after it was a city that had heard its own debt read aloud and had chosen, mostly, to be better. What came after it was a red knot turning against the stars, doing nothing a charm is supposed to do, doing everything a promise is supposed to do.

"Hey," Wren called across the platform. "You're doing the face again."

"I don't have a face."

"You have twelve now. That's the *happy* one. It's very embarrassing. Come here."

Noor came. The wind took the song and the knot turned and the stars, slightly different than they had been three years ago, slightly different and entirely themselves, burned on over Vespergate—and over the spire, and over the four people and one returned soul who had climbed out of the dark together and kept climbing, and who would, if the fates were kind and the soup was peppered, go on climbing for a long time yet.

Survival, Noor thought, taking Wren's hand, was only ever the beginning.

The knot turned. The city sang. And somewhere below, in a shop with a legal license and a framed star-charm and a kettle always on, tomorrow was already warming up.
