## 1. The Valley That Forgot

The first thing Mara Dravenmoor did every morning was listen.

Not to the birds—Thornvale had no shortage of those, thrushes in the hedgerows and crows squabbling over the orchard windfalls. Not to the river either, though it ran loud and cold through the valley's spine. She listened for the wards: a hum so low most people lived their whole lives without hearing it, a sound felt more than heard, like the memory of a bell rung before you were born.

The hum was there. It was always there. She pressed her palm flat against the eastern wardstone anyway, the way she did every morning of her life, and waited for the faint vibration against her skin like a cat's purr through a wall.

*Still singing,* she thought. *Good.*

"Dravenmoor!" Bramwell's voice cracked across the yard from the guildhouse steps. "You're kneeling at that stone like it'll answer you. Stones don't answer. Move."

She moved. She always moved.

***

Thornvale sat at the bottom of the world, or close enough. The valley ran east to west between two ridgelines so steep the sun came late and left early, and the only road in crossed a single stone bridge at the northern gap—a bridge wide enough for one cart, narrow enough that a man with a spear could hold it against an army. Nobody had ever needed to. No army had come. No travelers came either, not really; once or twice a year a tinker would cross the bridge, sell his pots, complain about the road, and leave looking vaguely confused about why he'd come at all. The valley kept itself. The valley forgot itself.

That was the point. Mara wouldn't learn that it was the point until much later, but even then, at nineteen, she understood the shape of it without understanding the reason: Thornvale was a place designed to be overlooked.

The Warden's Guild had tended it for as long as anyone could remember. Once, according to the ledger in the archive, the guild had numbered forty sworn members. Then twenty. Then twelve. Now there were six, and two of them were too old to climb the ridge path, and one of those was Bramwell himself, who climbed it anyway because he would rather die on the ward-road than admit his knees had opinions.

Six wardens for eleven wardstones, each stone needing song and touch and oil every third day, which meant every warden walked a circuit of nearly ten miles of switchbacks and scree, singing verses older than the guildhouse beams. Mara had walked them since she was seven years old, small enough then that the wind on the high path could shove her sideways, singing words whose meanings nobody had explained because nobody remembered them.

"You don't need to know what they mean," Bramwell told her once, when she'd asked. "You need to know how they're meant to be sung. Meaning is a luxury. Precision is a duty."

She'd hated him a little for that. She hated him gently, the way you hate someone who is mostly right.

***

The morning rounds took her past the mill, where Hesta the miller's wife waved without stopping her work, and past the school, where the children were reciting the harvest blessing, and past the well, where old Tam Osgood sat every day like furniture, watching her pass.

"Warden," Tam said. He said it to all of them, though none of them were the Warden. Bramwell held the title the way a dying tree holds its last leaf.

"Not yet, Tam."

"Someone's got to be." He spat into the dust. "Valley doesn't keep itself."

Except it did, mostly. That was the strange truth of Thornvale: the people needed the guild the way a house needs a foundation—constantly, invisibly, without ever thinking about it. They brought the wardens bread at midwinter and left flowers on the guildhouse steps at springtide, and if you asked any of them what the wardens actually *did*, they'd say something vague and warm about protection, about keeping the valley safe, safe from what, well, from out there, from everything, from nothing in particular. Safe. The word was a door everyone walked through and no one opened.

Mara had tried to open it exactly once, at fourteen, standing in the archive with the ledger open on her knees, asking Bramwell what the wards kept out. He'd looked at her for a long moment—not angry, not dismissive, but with an expression she'd spent five years trying to name and still couldn't—and said, "Ask me again when you're older."

She was older now. He still hadn't answered. Lately he seemed to be running out of time to.

***

By midmorning she'd finished the eastern circuit and come down into the orchard, because the orchard was hers by unspoken agreement—the youngest warden got the trees, the way the youngest warden got the worst sleeping cell and the last pick at supper. Forty apple trees in crooked rows, planted by hands long dead, their roots going down deep into soil that was richer than it had any right to be. The apples came heavy every year, fat and sweet, more than the valley could eat. They sent the surplus down the bridge road to the lowland towns, and the lowland towns sent back salt and iron and never asked why the mountain fruit tasted the way it did.

Mara walked the rows with her pruning knife, checking for blight, and that was when she saw the light.

It wasn't sunlight. The sun was behind the western ridge at that hour, and the light she saw came from the ground—from a shallow depression between the third and fourth rows, where the grass was flattened in a circle maybe four feet across, and at the center of the circle lay a piece of something that glowed like a coal wrapped in gauze.

She stopped. Her hand found her knife without her telling it to.

The fragment was the size of her fist, irregular, faceted, silver-white shot through with veins of dim gold. It sat in the grass as if it had grown there, except grass didn't grow toward things, and the blades around it leaned inward, bent, as if bowing.

A star-fall. She knew the word from the archive, from a marginal note in a ledger three hundred years dead: *star-fall fragments occur rarely; do not touch; report to the First Warden.* There was no procedure beyond that. No explanation of what a star-fall was, or why stars fell, or what to do besides not touch and report.

Bramwell was a mile away on the northern circuit, and his knees were bad, and the note was three centuries old, and the thing was glowing in her orchard like a secret.

Mara knelt beside it.

Up close it wasn't warm-colored after all—it was cold-looking, moon-pale, and yet the air above it shimmered faintly, the way air does over a hearthstone. She extended one hand, hesitated, thought about the marginal note, thought about Bramwell's face when she'd asked what the wards kept out, and touched it anyway.

It was warm. Warm as a living thing. Warm as a bird cupped in both hands, or a cheek, or—

The hum changed.

She felt it through her whole body at once, the ward-song that ran under the valley like groundwater, the sound she listened for every morning of her life. For half a heartbeat it stuttered. Skipped. Like a singer drawing breath.

Then it resumed, steady as ever, and the fragment's glow faded—not dying, she somehow knew, but settling, banking itself down like a fire deliberately made small.

Mara sat back on her heels in the flattened grass, heart hammering, and looked at the thing in the orchard, and looked up at the pale morning sky where the sun was just clearing the western ridge, late as always, and felt—for no reason she could have explained to anyone, least of all herself—that the sky was thinner here than it should be. That somewhere above the blue there was something missing, some vastness she'd been promised and never given.

She'd felt it her whole life, that missing thing. She'd assumed it was longing, ordinary human longing, the kind that made young people leave valleys for cities. She'd assumed everyone felt it and most people were simply better at carrying it.

Now, kneeling over a fallen star with her palm still tingling, she wondered.

***

At noon she reported it, because the note said report, and because she was dutiful, dutiful down to the marrow, and hating herself a little for that too.

Bramwell was in the guildhouse kitchen, arguing with the kettle. He was seventy-three and built like the bridge at the north gap—all angles and endurance—and age had done to him what it does to bridges: taken nothing away, exactly, just thinned everything, worn the mortar, left him standing through sheer stubbornness of design. His hair had gone the color of ash. His eyes hadn't changed at all. They were grey and quick and missed nothing, and they missed nothing now as she stood in the doorway with her hands empty at her sides.

"Well?" he said. "Out with it. You've got your reporting face on. You look like a girl about to confess to breaking a window."

"A star fell in the orchard."

The kettle screamed. He didn't move to lift it. For three full seconds Bramwell went absolutely still, in a way Mara had never seen him go still, and in that stillness she caught something move across his face—something old and tired and afraid, there and gone like a fish under ice.

Then he lifted the kettle off the fire and poured two cups of willow-bark tea, and when he spoke again his voice was level, almost bored.

"Show me."

***

They walked down to the orchard together, and the fragment lay where she'd left it, dimmed to a faint pearl-grey, and Bramwell stood over it for a long time without touching it, his shadow falling across the bowed grass.

"Have you touched it?"

"Yes."

He closed his eyes briefly, the way a man does when a wound he'd been guarding gets pressed. "Of course you have. Why am I surprised. You've been touching things you shouldn't since you could walk." He crouched, slowly, joints cracking, and studied the fragment without laying a finger on it. "Does it hurt?"

"No. It's warm."

"Mm." He rose again, and looked at her, and whatever he saw in her face made him sigh from somewhere deep. "Listen to me, girl. This stays between us. Not the guild—the guild can know, Halloran and the others, fine—but not the valley. Not a word below the bridge. People see a star fall out of a sky that doesn't make stars fall, and they start asking questions, and questions are—" He stopped himself. Chose a different word. "Questions are not useful."

"Useful to whom?"

He gave her a look that would have flattened a lesser argument. "To anyone. Come on. We'll cover it with straw. If it's still there in a week, we'll think about what to do. If it isn't, we'll be grateful."

"And if it grows?"

Something flickered in him again. "Stars don't grow, Mara."

"This one might."

For a moment—one strange, suspended moment—he looked at her as if she'd spoken a language neither of them knew she knew. Then he turned and started up the slope toward the guildhouse, leaning on his walking stick harder than usual, and called back over his shoulder:

"Straw. Tonight. And Mara—" He paused. Didn't turn around. "Sing the evening verse a little louder tonight. All nine stanzas."

"We never sing the ninth stanza."

"I know what we sing." His voice was quiet now, and very tired. "Sing it anyway."

***

She covered the fragment with straw at dusk, as ordered, and sang the evening verse at the eastern stone, eight stanzas as always, and then—alone on the ridge path with the valley going blue below her and the first stars pricking out overhead, the same fixed stars in the same fixed places as every night of her life—she sang the ninth.

She didn't know the words. That was the thing; she'd heard the eighth stanza end and the silence begin a thousand times, and she'd never been taught what came after, and yet when she opened her mouth the words came, slow and strange, in a voice that felt borrowed: a rising phrase, a turning phrase, a phrase like a door being unlocked from the outside.

The wards hummed. The hum shivered.

And far below her, beneath forty apple trees and the darkening earth, something that had been patient for a very long time turned over in its sleep—and Mara, standing on the ridge with her heart going like a hammer, felt it the way you feel a change in the weather: not as a sound, not as a sight, but as a certainty.

*Soon,* said the feeling, in no language at all.

She finished the ninth stanza. The hum steadied. The stars burned on, indifferent, arranged in patterns no one in the valley had ever thought to question.

Mara walked home in the dark with straw dust on her hands and a warmth lingering in her right palm, and she did not sleep that night, and in the morning the wards were still singing, and she told herself everything was fine.

Three nights later, every star in the sky went out at once.
