## 2. Warden's Last Lesson

The guildhall smelled of beeswax and old paper and, beneath that, the faint green rot of the season's first fallen apples drifting in through the broken shutter. Mara had been trying to fix that shutter for three weeks. She'd mentioned it to Warden Bramwell twice. He'd said *yes, yes* in the tone of a man agreeing to weather.

"Again," he said now, from his chair by the cold hearth. "From the top."

Mara stood in the center of the ward-room with her palms open at her sides, the way he'd taught her—*the song lives in the body before it lives in the mouth*, he always said—and began the first verse of the ward-song of Thornvale.

It was not a beautiful song. That was the first thing she'd learned, years ago, when she was small enough that the guild's robes had to be rolled twice at the cuff. The songs the valley children sang were pretty: harvest rounds, rain-catches, a lullaby about a fox who outsmarted the moon. The ward-songs were work. They were built like fences—functional lines, load-bearing notes, harmonies designed to interlock rather than please. When she sang them alone they felt unfinished, questions waiting for answers. It took three voices to close a verse properly. There had been seven voices in the guild once. Now there were two, and one of them was dying.

She gave him all eight verses anyway, voice cracking on the fourth where it always cracked, and when she finished, the room held its silence the way a cupped hand holds water.

Bramwell nodded slowly. "Better. Your third is flat."

"My third is *always* flat."

"Yes." He smiled, and it rearranged the ruin of his face into something almost young. "Consistency is a virtue in a Warden."

He looked terrible this morning. She'd stopped pretending otherwise sometime around midwinter. His hands shook now even when they weren't reaching for anything, and there was a new looseness to his skin, as if he were gradually being unsewn. The guild's healer—a woman named Tamsin who split her time between the guild and the mill town, and whose visits grew shorter each year—had used the word *hollowing*, which was not a word healers used about bodies that intended to keep going.

"You're favoring your left side," Mara said.

"I'm favoring my left side because my right side is ninety-one years old and has earned the right to be ignored." He gestured at the bench across from him. "Sit. We need to talk about the ninth verse."

Mara sat. Something in her chest did a strange, small drop, like a step missing in the dark.

There were nine verses to the ward-song of Thornvale. Every child raised in the valley learned this the way they learned the number of fingers on a hand. And every Warden for the last hundred years had taught only eight. The ninth existed in the master ledger—the great chained book in the archive, its pages stiff as bark—but no living member of the guild had ever sung it aloud. Bramwell had told her, when she was twelve and full of the particular greed children have for forbidden things, that the ninth verse was *not for singing*. He had never said why. In nine years of asking—in the orchard, over supper, once at the graveside of the last other Warden while the dirt was still falling—he had never once said why.

"You're going to tell me," she said.

"I'm going to tell you what I can." He leaned forward, and the chair complained under the effort. "That's the whole art of it, Mara. Knowing what you can say. Your predecessor thought I was too soft because I never lied to you outright. He preferred lies. Lies are tidy."

"You're doing it again."

"Doing what?"

"The thing where you teach me by telling me everything except the thing."

Bramwell laughed—a dry rustle, like wind through the winter orchard—and then coughed, long and wet, and Mara was on her feet with the water jug before the laugh had fully died. He waved her off with two fingers, caught his breath, and fixed her with eyes that were still, stubbornly, sharp.

"If you want the truth entire," he said, "then understand what it costs. A truth given too early is just a weight with nowhere to set itself down. You'd have dropped it. Or worse—you'd have carried it wrong, and bent yourself around it." He tapped the arm of his chair. "I'm an old man in a valley that forgets things on purpose. I've had time to learn patience. You haven't. But you're about to have less choice in the matter than either of us would like."

"Bramwell—"

"The ninth verse." He said it firmly, closing the door she'd tried to slip through. "Here's what I will give you. The eight verses you know—they hold. You know this. You've walked the ward-lines with me since you could walk at all; you've felt where the songs sit thickest and where they've worn thin. Eight verses, sung true, keep the valley safe. That has been the guild's teaching for a hundred years and it is not false. But it is—" he searched for the word, and Mara watched him choose it with the care of a man laying stones across a stream, "*—incomplete.*"

"Incomplete how?"

"The wards don't do what we say they do."

"They suppress magic. Everyone knows—"

"*Everyone knows,*" he agreed pleasantly, "that the sun rises. Ask anyone in this valley how it rises and you'll get nine different answers, and most of them will involve a chariot." He pushed himself up, joints announcing each stage of the journey, and shuffled to the window overlooking the orchard. The trees stood bare and patient in the grey light, rows upon rows, the way they'd stood for longer than the town had kept records. "Come here. Look at the orchard. What do you see?"

Mara joined him. Frost still clung to the north sides of the trunks. "Apple trees. Two hundred of them. The oldest row's dying—we should graft replacements off the Cortland stock before spring."

"Mm. And underneath?"

"Soil. Roots. Rocks, probably. The cellar root-rot problem means drainage is—"

"Mara."

"—and the Thorn, obviously. The old blackthorn stump. The one the guild doesn't let anyone dig near." She said it the way you recite something learned before you understood it. Every child in Thornvale knew there had once been a great blackthorn at the heart of the orchard, cut down before anyone's grandmother's grandmother, and that the guild tended the ground where it had stood with a seriousness nobody explained. The oldest trees ringed that spot. Nobody planted in it. Nobody picnicked there. It was simply part of the shape of life, like church bells or winter.

"And why does the guild not let anyone dig near it?"

"Because the roots go deep, and the deep roots feed the wards." Another inheritance, worn smooth from handling. "You told me that. You told me the whole valley sits inside the ward-web like a fly in silk, and the web anchors to the deepest root."

"I did tell you that." Bramwell turned from the window. For a moment he looked not old but ancient—not frail but *weathered*, stone that had stood through storms precisely because it had stopped pretending to be anything else. "And I let you believe the web holds the tree down. That is the incomplete part. The part I'm giving you today, while there's still time to chew it properly."

The cold hearth seemed suddenly colder. "Then what does it hold?"

"Not down." He held up one trembling finger. "Hold that thought. Not *down*. The distinction matters more than anything else I'll say to you this morning, possibly more than anything else I'll ever say to you. The wards do not press the Thorn down into the earth. Anyone who tells you that—the few who remember it exists at all—is repeating a story built for comfort. Pressing something down requires strength forever applied. Strength fails. Songs fail. Wardens die." He spread his hands. "No. The wards don't press. The wards *wait*."

"Wait for what?"

"Forgetting," said Bramwell, "is also a kind of holding. Think of it that way if it helps. The valley forgets the Thorn. The songs help it forget. The wards help the songs. A hundred years of quiet, and the quiet does the work that chains never could." His voice went quieter. "But forgetting is not the same as healing, child. And what waits under patience is not always peace."

Mara became aware that her hands had curled closed at her sides. She made herself open them. *The song lives in the body before it lives in the mouth.*

"You said the eighth verse keeps us safe," she managed. "You said it wasn't false."

"It isn't. Eight verses hold. Nine verses—" He stopped. Closed his mouth. She watched him do it, watch the sentence come up out of him like water rising in a well and be deliberately, visibly swallowed. Whatever it cost him, and it cost him something—she saw it in the flinch around his eyes—it stayed swallowed.

"Nine verses *what*?" she pressed. "Finish it. You owe me that much. You've owed me that much since I was twelve."

"What I owe you," Bramwell said, "is a guild that still stands tomorrow. And the day after. And every day until you're ready, and you are not ready, and neither am I, though for different reasons." He crossed back to his chair and lowered himself into it with the care of a man handling something fragile, and she realized with a small cold shock that the fragile thing was himself. "Ask me again in the spring."

"There may not *be* a spring."

"Don't be dramatic. I've buried four Wardens and outlived two doctors who swore I'd go first. I intend to be insufferable for years yet." But his hand had gone to his chest, briefly, the gesture of a man checking on something, and Mara looked away to give him privacy in the lie.

They sat in silence for a while. Outside, the grey brightened by one reluctant shade. Somewhere down in the town, the baker's boy was ringing the morning bell, and the sound came thin through the shutters, ordinary as bread.

"Sing me the eighth again," Bramwell said at last. "Just the eighth. The anchor line."

So she sang it. The eighth verse was the longest and the plainest, mostly sustained tones with almost no ornament, and she'd privately thought for years that it was the dullest of the eight—a fence post of a verse. But she gave it her whole breath, and halfway through she noticed Bramwell mouthing the words along with her, and noticed something else: his mouth kept moving after the eighth verse ended. Moving through shapes she didn't know, syllables with no melody attached, silent as a prayer.

He caught her watching. He didn't stop immediately. That was somehow worse—that he let her see three full seconds of it, his lips shaping the unsung thing, before he pressed them together and folded his hands.

"That wasn't the eighth," she said.

"No."

"That was the ninth."

"It was the memory of the ninth," he corrected. "Memory and music aren't the same animal. One you can carry alone." He looked at her for a long moment, and behind the tiredness his eyes were doing something complicated—grieving something, weighing something, apologizing for something in advance. "Mara. If I die before you're ready—if the songs falter, if anything happens to the wards—there is a box in the archive. Under the floor of the ledger alcove. Third board, the loose one, you know the one, you've tripped on it since you were nine."

"I know the one."

"Do not open it. Do not open it unless the stars themselves go out. Do you hear me? Unless the sky goes dark over this valley, that box stays shut, and the ninth verse stays unsung, and you trust the eight, and you trust me, and you trust the patience." His voice cracked on the last word, or perhaps that was just the age in it. "Promise me."

The request was so strange—so specific, so *silly*, a loose floorboard and a box and a condition out of a fireside tale—that it took her a moment to realize she was frightened. Not of the box. Of him. Of the fact that Bramwell, who feared nothing except bad grafting technique and Tamsin's cooking, had asked her for a promise with his whole body leaning forward like that.

"I promise," she said.

He exhaled, and some tension went out of him that she suspected had been sitting in his shoulders for decades. "Good girl."

"I hate when you call me that."

"I know." He smiled. "Patience, Mara. That's the whole of it, if you want the lesson early. Everyone thinks the guild is here to protect the valley. Protection is for soldiers and shepherds. The guild's purpose is not protection." He settled back into his chair, eyes drifting toward the window, toward the orchard, toward the ground beneath the orchard. "It's patience. We are not the wall. We are the ones who wait beside it, and keep the vigil, and remember why the wall was built—and pray, quietly, every day, that remembering is enough."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then the ninth verse," said Bramwell, "and may every god who ever walked these hills spare us both from hearing it."

* * *

He slept through the afternoon, which was normal now, and Mara did the rounds alone, which was also becoming normal. She hated how quickly things became normal. That was the trick of the valley, maybe—everything unusual got worn smooth and ordinary within a season, the way river stones lost their edges. Seven Wardens become five become two. Songs lose a verse. An orchard grows over a stump and nobody asks what the stump was.

She walked the outer ward-line as the light failed, humming the low sustaining drone of the fifth verse to keep the near segments fed, and checked the boundary stones with her bare palms the way Bramwell had taught her—*skin remembers what the mind talks itself out of*. The stones were cold and steady. The wards hummed against her fingertips, that familiar sub-hearing pressure, like standing near a hive. Strong. Strong as ever.

Except.

At the seventh stone—the one nearest the orchard, nearest the place where no one dug—she paused. The hum was strong there. It was always strongest there. But tonight, with her palm flat against the frost-slick granite, she thought she felt something underneath the strength. A second rhythm. Slower. Far down. Like a heartbeat heard through a floor.

She stood there a long time, until her fingers went numb, telling herself it was the blood in her own hand, her own pulse reflected back through stone. The valley was quiet around her. The mountains made their usual black rampart against the sky. Above the ridge, the evening stars were coming out one by one, punctual as always, the same patient constellations that had wheeled over Thornvale for as long as anyone could remember.

She counted them without meaning to. Old habit. Bramwell had taught her the count when she was ten—*a Warden counts the stars the way a sailor checks the rigging; if the number changes, you'll want to have noticed.*

Forty-one visible from the ward-stone. Always forty-one, this time of year, from this exact spot.

Forty-one.

She turned for home, and behind her the seventh stone hummed its slow deep double rhythm, and in the guildhall window a single candle marked the place where Bramwell slept, and above the valley the forty-first star—low on the southern horizon, the one called the Shepherd, the one that never wavered—flickered once, softly, like a candle deciding whether to stay lit.

Mara didn't see it. She was already walking, humming the fifth verse into the dark, keeping the vigil.

But the stone felt it. And far below the orchard, in the deep roots of the oldest row, something that had waited a hundred years felt the flicker too—

—and, very quietly, remembered her name.
