## 3. Night Without Stars

The wards failed at midnight, and Mara knew it first by the silence.

Thornvale was never a loud valley, but it had a sound the way a body has a heartbeat—a low, constant hum beneath everything, the wards breathing in their sleep. Mara had heard it every night of her twenty-two years. As a child she'd thought it was the wind moving through the orchard. Later, when Bramwell began teaching her the ward-songs, she'd understood: it was the valley holding its breath and letting it out, over and over, a rhythm older than any of them.

That night, as she banked the hearth in her room above the guildhall, the rhythm stopped.

Not faded. Not faltered. Stopped—like a hand closing over a struck bell.

She stood with the poker still in her grip and listened to the absence. It was worse than noise. The orchard outside her window had gone quiet too; even the wind had nothing left to push against. And then the cold came, sliding under the door like something with intent.

Mara set down the poker, took her cloak from its hook, and went out.

* * *

The guildhall stood at the head of the valley, old grey stone that the moss had been quietly eating for centuries. Behind it, the terraced orchards climbed the slope in silvered rows—bare now, late winter, the apple trees waiting for a spring that was still weeks off. Beyond them the mountains closed the valley in on three sides, black against the sky.

Except there was no sky.

Mara stopped halfway across the yard and looked up, and her mind simply refused what her eyes reported. She could see the mountains' ridgelines clearly—the snow on their crests, the pines straggling up their flanks—but above them, where the stars should have been, there was nothing. Not cloud. Clouds had texture, movement, edges. This was a smooth and perfect dark, as if someone had reached up and wiped the sky clean from horizon to horizon, and taken the moon with it.

She counted without meaning to. One breath. Two. Three.

Every star. All at once. In all her years of tending wards, she had never once seen even a flicker in the night sky. The stars were constants, the way stone was stone. They did not go out. They especially did not all go out together, in the same instant, like candles pinched by one enormous hand.

"Mara."

Bramwell's voice came from the hall's doorway, thin and cracked, and she turned fast because Warden Bramwell Halloway did not sound afraid. He sounded tired, or dry-humored, or disappointed in her knot-work, but never afraid. In forty years he had not once given her a reason to be afraid of him.

He was leaning against the doorframe with one hand pressed flat to the stone, and his face in the starless dark was the colour of ash.

"The eastern anchor," he said. "Go. Now. Don't light a lamp."

"What happened to the sky?"

"Go," he said again, and this time his voice broke in the middle of it, and Mara ran.

* * *

The eastern anchor sat at the top of the orchard's highest terrace, a standing stone no taller than her shoulder, ringed by nine apple trees that were older than the guildhall itself. The guild called them the Nine Sisters. Mara had climbed them as a child. She had eaten their apples every autumn of her life.

She felt the wrongness before she reached them. The air around the terrace was thick and dead, the way air feels in the moment after lightning. When she stepped between the Sisters, the hair on her arms lifted.

The anchor-stone was split.

Not shattered—split, a single clean crack running top to bottom, as if the stone had been opened like a door. And through the crack seeped a darkness deeper than the sky's, a darkness that moved, slowly, the way ink moves when you drop it into water. The ward-lines carved into the stone's face were still visible, but they had gone grey, drained, the way a fire goes grey when it dies.

Mara knelt and put her palm against the stone, the way she'd done ten thousand times before, and began the first verse of the ward-song under her breath.

Nothing answered.

Her own voice fell into the silence and vanished. For twenty-two years, the song had been a conversation—the singer offering, the wards receiving, a warmth rising through her palm like water drawn from a well. Now her words dropped into the stone and kept dropping, and nothing came back, and the cold in the crack beside her fingers breathed out slow and long, like something sleeping turning over.

"Say your name."

Bramwell had followed her up the terrace. She hadn't heard him—he shouldn't have been able to climb this far this fast—but there he was at the edge of the Nine Sisters, gripping a low branch of the eldest tree with both hands, and his eyes were fixed on the crack in the stone with an expression she would carry the rest of her life.

"Warden, the anchor's split, I can't—"

"Your name, girl. Say it into the crack." His voice had gone strange. Flat. Ritual-flat, the way it went when he recited the verses he never let her write down. "Now."

"It remembers her name."

The whisper came from everywhere—from the stone, from the trees, from the dead air itself—and it was not Mara's voice and it was not Bramwell's, though it wore the shape of an old man's rasp, and Mara's whole body went cold to the marrow.

Because it wasn't her name it had said.

It wasn't anyone's name she knew.

Bramwell made a sound like a man being unmade. He slid down against the tree trunk, and Mara caught him before his head hit the frozen ground, and his weight in her arms was terrible—not heavy so much as emptied, as if whatever had held him upright for eighty years had finally been let go.

"Bramwell—" She lowered him down, pulled her cloak off, pushed it under his head. His skin was clammy. His lips were moving.

"Listen to me." She bent close. "The eastern anchor is split. I need to know how to mend a split anchor. Tell me the verse."

His hand found her wrist with shocking strength. His eyes opened, and they were clear—terribly clear, the clarity of a man looking at something far past her.

"It remembers her name," he whispered. "After all these years. It remembers."

"Whose name?" Mara asked, and hated how small her voice came out. "Whose name does it remember?"

But his eyes had rolled back, and his grip loosened, and somewhere down in the valley the first bell began to ring—the chapel bell, wild and arrhythmic, the panic-bell that hadn't been rung in living memory.

Mara stayed exactly one more second, kneeling in the dark with her dying mentor's head in her hands and a voice in the stones that remembered names.

Then she got up and went down toward the bells, because someone had to.

* * *

Later—much later, in the grey false-dawn of a morning that had no sun behind it—she would try to remember how she'd done it, and find only fragments. The bell tower, hauling the rope from the shaking hands of Aldous Miller the cooper, ringing the pattern Bramwell had drilled into her for emergencies she'd privately believed would never come: two short, one long, two short. *Gather at the hall. Bring fire. Stay off the roads.*

The guildhall common room filling with people by lantern-light, sixty faces out of a valley of four hundred—the rest too frightened or too stubborn to leave their houses, which amounted to the same thing tonight. The Harrow brothers demanding to know if the Crown had attacked. Widow Sennet weeping quietly into her apron. Old Tam Voss, who had tended the western anchor for thirty years, saying over and over, "My lines were green this morning. Green as grass. I checked them twice."

And Mara, twenty-two years old, invisible apprentice, last of a dwindling line, standing on the hearth-stones where Bramwell should have been standing, hearing herself say:

"The wards have failed. All of them, near as I can tell. I don't yet know why." A beat, while the murmur rose, while her heart hammered so hard she was sure everyone could see it through her shirt. "I do know that panic will kill us faster than whatever did this. So here is what happens next."

She assigned watches. She ordered doors barred and lanterns lit in every window—light wouldn't help, probably, but it would make people feel defended, and feeling defended was half of surviving a bad night. She sent the Harrows to check on the outlying farms. She put Tam Voss in charge of the granary keys because giving him a job stopped his mouth from repeating itself. Her voice stayed level. Her hands, when no one was looking, shook so badly she had to hide them in her skirts.

Somewhere in the middle of it, she realized she was doing all of this in the exact cadence Bramwell used—his pauses, his flat deliberate tone, the trick he had of looking slightly over a crowd's heads instead of into their eyes—and that realization frightened her more than the empty sky, because it meant the lesson was already written in her, and the man who wrote it might be dying upstairs.

* * *

He was.

She checked on him twice in the night. The first time, he lay silent, breathing shallowly, his face turned toward the shuttered window as if listening to something on the other side. The guild's third member, old Sister Anwen, sat with him—Anwen, who was ninety-one and mostly deaf and had refused to leave the valley in fifty years of offers. She looked up when Mara came in.

"He keeps saying it," Anwen said. "Over and over. Like a prayer said wrong."

"Saying what?"

Anwen's clouded eyes settled on her, and for a moment they weren't clouded at all. "You don't want to know, child."

"I'm the one whose wards just died, Sister. I want to know everything."

Anwen was quiet a long time. Downstairs, faintly, the valley murmured its fear.

"He says, 'she told us patience.' That's the first thing. Then he says, 'it remembers her name.'" Anwen smoothed the blanket over Bramwell's chest with a hand spotted brown as autumn leaves. "And then he says your name, Mara. Just that. Your name, over and over. Like he's trying to give it to someone."

A cold finger traced Mara's spine. "Give it to whom?"

"How would I know?" Anwen said. But she didn't quite meet Mara's eyes, and Mara thought: *she knows*, and filed it away next to the ninth verse Bramwell would never teach her, and the word *patience* that he'd once used to describe the guild's true purpose before catching himself and changing the subject, and the hundred other locked doors she'd spent her life politely not knocking on.

The polite season was over.

* * *

Near midnight—no, she corrected herself, near what should have been midnight; midnight had lost its meaning along with the stars—she went back up to the eastern terrace alone.

She told herself it was duty. Check the anchor again. Assess the damage properly, by lantern-light, methodical, the way a warden would. But she took no lantern, and she walked past the tool-shed without stopping, and she knew, in the honest place beneath her own excuses, that she was going because the voice had spoken a stranger's name into the dark, and some reckless part of her wanted to hear it again.

The crack in the anchor-stone hadn't widened. That was something. She crouched beside it, laid her palm flat, and waited.

The cold breathed out. Slow. Long. Patient.

"You're awake," she said quietly. It felt absurd, talking to a stone. Everything about tonight was absurd; absurdity was the least of it. "You've been asleep a long time, haven't you? Under my orchard. Under my whole life."

No answer. But the darkness in the crack seemed to gather slightly, the way a listener leans forward.

"Bramwell says you remember a name. Whose?"

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that is considering.

And then, faint as breath on a winter window, the voice came again—wearing that old man's rasp it had borrowed, but underneath it something else, something vast and slow and terribly sad, like grief that had been lying still for a century and had forgotten how to stand:

*"Mara."*

Just her name. Nothing else. Given, not taken. Held out into the dark like a lamp.

She snatched her hand back and stood up so fast she stumbled into one of the Nine Sisters, bark scraping her shoulder, and stood there with her heart slamming and her breath smoking in the freezing air, staring at a crack in a stone that knew her name.

Above the orchard, the sky stayed empty. No stars. No moon. No answer from anything that had ever watched over her.

Mara wrapped her arms around herself and made herself think, because thinking was the only weapon she owned. The wards had failed at midnight, all at once, cleanly—which meant whatever broke them hadn't gnawed through; it had been let out, or let in, or had simply decided it was finished waiting. Bramwell had collapsed the same hour, whispering about patience and names. The voice below spoke hers as if it had been keeping it warm.

Everything pointed one direction, and the direction was *down*, beneath the roots of the trees she'd climbed since childhood, and she was not ready, and no one was coming, and the valley full of frightened people was depending on her anyway.

"Tomorrow," she said to the stone. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Tomorrow I start pulling answers out of the archive, and out of Anwen, and out of you if it comes to that. Tonight, sleep. Or whatever you do."

She turned to go, and paused.

"And when you're ready to say more than my name," she added quietly, "I'll be listening. Apparently I always have been."

Behind her, in the crack, the darkness breathed—one long, slow exhalation, almost like laughter, almost like relief.

Down in the guildhall, a lamp burned in Bramwell's sickroom window, and beside his bed old Sister Anwen sat very still, listening to him murmur a verse no one had sung in a hundred years—the ninth verse, the one he'd forbidden, the one that was not a warding at all—and she listened, and memorized, and waited for morning, because she had promised someone, a long lifetime ago, that someone would be ready when the valley forgot why it existed.

The night had no stars.

It had Mara.

And it had, deep beneath the orchard roots, something that was beginning—after a century of silence—to speak.
