## 5. The Seed in Her Palm

The fragment woke at dawn.

Mara knew it the way you know a fever starting—not by seeing anything, but by feeling it. She had slept with the star-fall piece wrapped in linen at the bottom of her satchel, three days now since she'd pried it from the orchard soil, and every morning she checked it the way Bramwell checked his pulse: out of habit, out of dread.

This morning the linen was warm.

Not sun-warm. Not body-warm. Warm like a held hand.

She sat up in her cot in the guildhouse attic and unwrapped it with fingers that did not quite obey her. The fragment lay in its cloth like a chip of black glass shot through with veins of silver—except the veins were moving. Slowly. Like something breathing under ice.

"No," Mara said aloud, to no one. "No, you're dead. Dead things stay dead."

The fragment pulsed once, gently, as if in disagreement.

She should have taken it to Bramwell. That was the correct sequence of events: find anomaly, report anomaly, let the Warden decide. That was how the guild worked—or had worked, when there had been more than two of them left to work it. But Bramwell hadn't woken properly in two days. His fever had climbed through the night of the vanished stars and never come down, and Sister Aldith—the last other member of the guild, ninety years old and mostly deaf—had shuffled off to the neighboring farmstead to fetch willow bark and hadn't returned.

So it was Mara alone who wrapped the fragment back up, alone who carried it down the narrow stairs, alone who stood outside Bramwell's door for a long moment with her knuckles raised and not falling.

Through the door she could hear him talking. Not to her. Not to anyone visible.

"—told her nothing, that was the mercy of it," he rasped. "Let her be a girl a while longer. Let her—" A wet, rattling cough. "—let her hate the chores instead of the truth."

Mara's knuckles came down on the wood before she could stop them.

"Bramwell. It's me."

The talking stopped. When he spoke again his voice had rearranged itself into something steadier, the old Warden's voice, the one he used for ward-songs and funerals. "Come in, then."

* * *

He looked worse than yesterday, which Mara would not have believed possible. He lay propped against pillows gone grey with age, his hands folded on the blanket like two fallen birds. The window behind him showed a sky the color of dirty wool—starless still, three days running, though it was morning and stars wouldn't show anyway. That was the thing that made her stomach turn. She couldn't even check anymore. The sky had become a wound she kept prodding.

"You're talking to people who aren't there," she said.

"I'm talking to people who are very far away." He smiled, or attempted it. "There's a difference. Sit."

She sat on the edge of the bed. Up close he smelled of fever-sweat and the dried yarrow they'd been steeping in everything. His eyes, though—his eyes were clear. Terribly clear. They went straight past her face to the satchel at her hip, and something in them tightened.

"Open it," he said quietly.

Her hands hesitated. Then, because lying to a dying man felt like a sin she wasn't ready to commit, she drew out the linen bundle and set it on the blanket between them.

Bramwell looked at it for a long time. Longer than made sense. His breath came shallow and quick.

"Where did you find this?"

"The orchard. Under the third row, near the old well. The night the wards fell—it fell then, I think. It was warm when I dug it up, and it's been warm ever since, and this morning it started—" She stopped, because saying *breathing* would make it real.

"Started moving," Bramwell finished for her. "Yes. I know what it does. I've seen one before." His gaze lifted to her face, and what she saw there frightened her more than the fragment had: not confusion, not fear. Recognition. Grief so old it had fossilized into something that only resembled calm. "I'd hoped we'd have more time. There's never more time. That's the first lesson of the guild and the last one too."

"What is it?" Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "What's actually under my orchard, Bramwell? What were the wards *for*?"

He closed his eyes. For a horrible moment she thought he'd slipped away mid-conversation, but then he began to speak, low and halting, like a man walking a road he'd sworn never to walk again.

"A hundred years ago, there was a war. Not a war of kingdoms—a war of *kinds*. Magic against law, the old blood against the new order, and both sides committing horrors until the world itself sickened of them. And in the middle of it stood an entity the histories call the Thorn. It was a judge, Mara. An arbiter. It ruled on grievances between mages and mortals, and for a long time its rulings were fair. Then the war corrupted it, the way war corrupts everything, and it began ruling in blood. Whole villages sentenced. Whole bloodlines unmade." He opened his eyes. "And when the war ended—when both sides were too exhausted to continue—they didn't try it. Didn't question it. Didn't give it the reckoning it had given everyone else. They buried it. Sealed it alive beneath a valley and built a guild of wardens around the grave and called the cage a sanctuary."

The room tilted. Mara gripped the bedframe.

"Thornvale," she said. "Thornvale is the grave."

"Thornvale is the grave. The orchards are planted over it because apple roots run deep and sweet, and sweet things soothe it. The ward-songs aren't protection from magic. They're a lullaby. We are not guardians, child. We are jailers' descendants, keeping a prison no one remembers is a prison." His voice cracked on the last word. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry we lied. I'm sorry *I* lied. You deserved better than a life built on a kindness shaped like a wall."

"And that—" She pointed at the bundle, her finger shaking. "That's a piece of it. That's a piece of the thing under my feet."

"That is a seed." Bramwell's clear eyes held hers without mercy. "And it has chosen."

"Chosen what? Chosen me?" A laugh escaped her, high and wrong. "I'm nobody. I'm the apprentice who scrubs the ward-stones and gets forgotten at market. I'm not—"

"Your mother's name was Lira Dravenmoor."

The words landed like stones dropped into still water. Mara went absolutely still.

"She came to Thornvale twenty-six years ago, pregnant, half-dead, fleeing the Inquisition's purge of the Thornbound enclaves in the east. She wasn't Thornbound herself. She was something rarer. She was descended from the first Warden—the man who sealed the Thorn. The guild took her in, hid her name in our ledgers, buried her history so deep even she barely knew it. And when she died bringing you into the world, we raised you ourselves. In plain sight. As an orphan apprentice." He exhaled slowly. "We didn't hide you from the world, Mara. We hid you from *it*. Because the seed wakes for its own bloodline, and if it ever woke, it would need a hand to carry it—and that hand was always going to be yours."

The fragment pulsed again, and this time Mara felt it—not in her ears but in her palm, in the heel of her hand where she'd carried it for three days, a warmth spreading up the tendons of her wrist like water finding a channel.

"It knows me," she whispered.

"It has always known you. It's been waiting for you your whole life." Bramwell reached out with trembling fingers and touched the linen, just briefly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you didn't trust. "The wards fell because it's waking, and it's waking because the last of the old seal-songs died with the singers. There are two of us left, Mara, and I am done. Which means the valley's panic, the Inquisitors who will come, the Thornbound who will come faster—all of it lands on you. I would take it back if I could. I'd take all of it."

"Don't apologize." Her voice came out flat and strange. "Just tell me what to do."

* * *

He told her what to do.

It took most of the day, because he made her write it down, in the guild ledger, in her own hand, in ink that would outlast him. Where the archive keys were hidden. Which verses of the ward-song maintained which layer of the seal. Where the roads ran when the roads stopped being safe. He dictated and she wrote and the light moved across the floorboards from west to north, and neither of them mentioned that they were doing what people do in the last hours of things.

Near dusk, he asked her to sing.

"The ward-song? All of it?"

"All but the ninth. Never the ninth." His eyes drifted toward the ceiling. "Sing me the rest, though. I want to hear it in a young voice. Mine's worn through."

So she sang. The eight verses, the ones she'd known since she was six years old, the ones she'd thought were about protecting apple trees from frost. She sang them sitting beside a dying man over the grave of a buried god, and every note landed differently now, the way a familiar room looks different after someone dies in it. The lullaby verses. The patience verses. *Not protection—patience*, he'd told her once, and she finally understood it down to the marrow.

When she finished, Bramwell's breathing had gone thin as thread.

"Mara." His hand found hers. His grip was astonishingly strong for a man so close to gone. "Listen to me. Listen, because I'll only say this once and the fever may take the words after. The seed will ask things of you. It speaks in borrowed voices—in voices you love. Don't trust the voice. Trust the asking. A judge needs a witness, not a master. If it ever stops asking and starts commanding, that's the corruption coming back, and you burn it where it lies, do you hear me? Even if the hand it grows from is yours."

"I hear you."

"And the ninth verse—" He coughed, long and terrible, and when it passed there was blood at the corner of his mouth that he wiped away with the back of his hand as if it were an embarrassment rather than a message. "The ninth verse isn't a song. It's a summons. It convenes. It calls a court that hasn't sat in a hundred years. I never taught it because teaching it makes it real, and real things can be stolen." His eyes fixed on hers, burning through the fever-haze. "I've written it where only a Warden would look. You'll know when you're ready. Not before. Promise me. Not before."

"I promise."

"Good girl." His grip loosened. "You were never invisible, Mara. I want you to know that. We saw you. Every day. We saw you and we grieved what we couldn't give you, and if love had been enough to save anyone, you'd have had a whole life of it."

"Bramwell—"

"Say the office," he whispered. "Say it so the valley hears. Say it so *it* hears."

Her throat closed. Outside, the starless sky was going dark for the fourth night running, and somewhere down in the village a dog was barking at nothing, and the seed in its linen wrapping pulsed warm against her knee like a second heartbeat learning to keep time with hers.

She took his hand in both of hers and bent close, the way he'd bent close to her when she was small and scared of storms.

"Mara Dravenmoor," she said, and her voice cracked and she said it again, steady, "is Warden of Thornvale."

"There," Bramwell breathed. "There it is." And he smiled—a real smile, unburdened, the smile of a man setting down a weight carried for sixty years—and said, "Jude would be proud," and then he said nothing else at all.

* * *

She sat with him while the dark came down.

Sister Aldith returned after full dark with willow bark and news of panic in the lower farms—cows refusing to milk, children crying in their sleep, old Tam Fletcher swearing he'd seen lights moving in the orchard rows. Lights. Mara filed it away with everything else, in the growing ledger of things she would have to handle because there was no one left to handle them for her.

Aldith found Bramwell's body an hour later. The old woman crossed his hands on his chest, murmured the burial verse—the fourth one, the one for guild members, the one Mara had always found too plain—and then looked at Mara standing in the doorway with red-rimmed eyes and said, simply:

"So it's you, then."

"It's me."

"He fought it longer than any of them. Did you know that? Sixty years he kept the songs, knowing what they were. Some nights he'd sing them angry." Aldith's ancient face folded into something between sorrow and respect. "Angry singing holds a seal tighter than peaceful singing. Remember that."

"I will."

The old woman shuffled out to arrange the waking of neighbors, and Mara was alone with the body and the seed and the ledger open on the desk to a page in her own handwriting that already looked like it belonged to a stranger.

She should sleep. She knew she should sleep. Instead she picked up the linen bundle and unwrapped it one more time, because she needed to look at the thing that had cost her the only father she'd ever had.

The fragment had changed.

The black glass had softened at its center, gone from mineral to something organic, something veined and faintly translucent, and at its heart a single point of silver light turned slowly, patiently, like a star deciding whether to rise. As she watched, fine filaments extended from its base—rootlets, hair-thin, questing—and one of them touched the bare skin of her palm.

Warmth bloomed up her arm. Not pain. Worse than pain: recognition. Somewhere far below the orchard, beneath six generations of apple roots and ward-stones and lies, something vast shifted in its sleep and turned its attention, for the first time in a century, upward.

And in a voice that was not a voice—felt rather than heard, in the register where grief lives—it said her name.

*Mara.*

Then, softer, with something that might have been wonder, might have been hunger, might have been both:

*Daughter of Jude. At last.*

Down in the valley, every dog began to bark at once.
