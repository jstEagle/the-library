## 6. Riders in Grey

They came on the third morning after the stars died, riding out of the western pass in a column of grey.

Mara saw them first from the bell tower, where she'd gone to escape the sound of the valley weeping. Nine riders, then a wagon, then nine more. Their cloaks were the color of slate before rain, and they moved with the particular discipline of people who had never once needed to hurry. At their head rode a woman on a pale mare, and even at that distance Mara could see that she sat her saddle the way Bramwell sat his chair—like something built for it.

The warning bell was already cracked. It had cracked the night the wards fell, when Tam Hollis had pulled the rope so hard and so long that the bronze split along an old flaw, and now all it could manage was a dull, broken clank that sounded less like alarm than like grief. Mara had stopped ringing it anyway. There was no one left asleep to wake.

She counted the riders twice, made herself count them a third time, and then went down through the tower stairs two at a time with her heart going hard against her ribs.

The guildhall smelled of fever and cold ash. Bramwell lay where she'd moved him three nights ago, on the long table where the guild once took its meals, because it was the only surface tall enough and flat enough and because moving him to a bed had felt too much like admitting he wouldn't get up again. His breathing came in long, wet drags. His eyes were open more often than not now, fixed on the rafters, seeing something that wasn't there.

"They're here," she told him, because lying to him had started to feel worse than leaving him in the dark, and he'd spent enough of his life in the dark for both of them.

His hand twitched on the table's edge. "Grey," he said. The word came out shredded. "Count the grey."

"I counted. Eighteen riders. A wagon."

"Not the riders." His fingers scrabbled weakly at the wood. "The grey between. Count what rides *between* them, girl." Then his back arched and the words dissolved into coughing, and whatever he'd meant went wherever his other meanings had gone—down into the fever, into the place where he kept saying *it remembers her name* over and over in a voice that didn't sound like him at all.

Mara pressed a cloth to his lips until the coughing eased. Then she stood very still for a moment, one hand flat on his chest, feeling the stubborn, slowing drum of him.

Eighteen riders. A wagon. And in her pocket, wrapped in three layers of linen and tied with guild-knot, the seed sat warm as a sleeping bird.

She had tried, twice in the last three days, to bury it. Both times she'd gotten the hole dug and both times she'd stood there with it in her palm, feeling that faint pulse against her skin like a second heartbeat slightly out of step with her own, and both times she'd climbed back out of the hole without knowing why. It wasn't attachment. She told herself it wasn't attachment. It was simply that Bramwell hadn't finished teaching her, and you did not abandon a lesson half-learned. That was all. That was the whole of it.

The seed pulsed against her hip, patient as a held breath.

"Right," she said aloud, to the empty hall, to herself, to whichever of them needed to hear it. "Right."

* * *

By the time the column reached the valley floor, half of Thornvale had gathered at the crossroads outside the orchard gate, and they had arranged themselves the way frightened people always arrange themselves: the bold ones in front with their arms crossed, the sensible ones behind them, the children hoisted onto shoulders where they could see and be seen. Old Maren Cobb had brought her rolling pin, which was the kind of thing that seemed brave right up until it wasn't.

Mara pushed through to the front just as the lead rider reined in.

Up close, the woman on the pale mare resolved into details, and the details were worse than the silhouette. She was perhaps forty, with iron-grey hair cut short and practical, and a face that might have been handsome if it had ever once been required to express anything it didn't choose to express. Her coat was grey wool, unadorned except for a single badge at the collar: a closed eye worked in silver thread. Her gloves were grey leather. Even her horse looked grey, though Mara suspected that was coincidence, or perhaps not—perhaps the Crown bred them that way, so that everything about an Inquisitor matched, so that nothing stood out, so that nothing could be remembered wrong later.

"Good morning," the woman said, and her voice was warm. That was the worst part. It was genuinely warm, low and pleasant, the voice of someone who would help you carry your groceries up a hill and then ask precise questions about your neighbors while doing it. "I'm looking for the Warden of Thornvale."

A murmur went through the crowd. Mara felt every eye swing toward her and land there with a weight she'd spent twenty years trying to avoid.

"The Warden is ill," she said. "I'm his apprentice. Mara Dravenmoor."

"Apprentice." The woman looked at her—a full, unhurried look, top to bottom and back again, the way you'd look at a door before deciding whether to knock or open it. "Then you'll do. Serah Vane, Inquisitor of the Third Circle, by warrant of the Crown." She drew a folded document from her coat but did not offer it, only let it be seen. "We've come about your wards."

"The wards fell," said Tam Hollis, from the second row, in the tone of a man contributing to a conversation he thought was still a conversation. "Three nights past. Midnight. Every star in the sky went out at—"

"Yes," Vane said, without looking at him. "I know. I read the same sky you did." Still watching Mara. "The Crown believes the failure was not natural."

"It was midnight," Mara said. Steady. Keep it steady. "There was no storm. No tremor. The ward-stones just—" She stopped herself, because *just what* was exactly the question she couldn't answer honestly, and she watched Vane notice the stop, file it away, and continue smiling.

"Just?" Vane prompted gently.

"Went dark," Mara finished. "Like lamps. All at once."

"All at once." Vane turned her mare in a slow half-circle, surveying the crowd, the crooked lane, the orchard beyond the gate with its rows of bare trees standing like an audience. "And in all this time—three days—no one has restored them? No one has sent word down-valley?"

"Send word to who?" Maren Cobb demanded, brandishing the rolling pin. "Half the guild's dead or dying, and the other half ran. This girl and old Bramwell are all that's left, and he's—"

"Maren." Mara said it quietly, and Maren subsided, because everyone in Thornvale knew that voice—it was the voice Mara used on children about to put their hands on stove-iron—and it bought her a heartbeat to think.

Because here was the arithmetic she'd been doing since dawn, when she'd seen the dust of the column from the tower: eighteen Inquisitors did not ride four days through the passes over a fallen ward. Fallen wards happened. Valleys lost their protections; it was sad; the Crown sent a clerk. Eighteen riders and a wagon meant the Crown believed something was *in* Thornvale worth taking, or worth destroying, and either way the last thing she could afford was for anyone to look too closely at the orchard, or the guildhall, or the apprentice standing in the road with a stolen god's heartbeat sewn into her coat lining.

"We sent a runner to Millbrook the first night," Mara said. "He never came back."

Vane's eyebrows rose a fraction. "The roads are dangerous lately."

"So I've heard."

Something flickered across the Inquisitor's face—not quite amusement, but a cousin of it. "May we see the ward-stones, Apprentice Dravenmoor? And the guild archive, if there is one. My people will need lodging, provisions—we can pay." A pause, delicately measured. "And I should like to pay my respects to your Warden, if he receives visitors."

"He's dying," Mara said. "He doesn't receive anyone."

"Then I'll respect him from the doorway." Vane dismounted in one smooth motion and stood beside her mare, and up close she was smaller than she'd seemed, which somehow made it worse. "Lead on, Apprentice. We'll try to be no trouble at all."

Behind her, the column began to dismount, eighteen grey figures unfolding into the lane of a valley that had never in living memory hosted more than a wool merchant and a wedding party, and Mara turned and walked toward the guildhall with the seed pulsing against her ribs like a second, traitorous heart, and understood that she had just invited the wolf indoors because the alternative was letting it sniff around the henhouse unsupervised.

Bramwell would have known what to say. Bramwell had probably known this day was coming for forty years, which was maybe why he'd never taught her what to do about it.

* * *

She hid the seed before they reached the gate.

It wasn't cleverness, exactly—or it was, but the desperate kind, the kind that operates below thought. Somewhere between the crossroads and the orchard fence her hands had already done it: slipped inside her coat, untied the guild-knot, transferred the warm weight from the linen wrapping to the hollow at the base of the old ward-post by the gate, where generations of apprentices had carved their initials and where a knot of rotten wood opened like a small dark mouth. She packed the rotten wood back around it, pressed her palm flat over the seam, and felt the pulse dim to almost nothing beneath the old stone's dead weight.

The ward-posts were dead. But they were dense with a century of spent magic, and whatever the seed was, it seemed to go quiet near them, like a child hiding under a heavy blanket.

*Mara,* it didn't say. It had never said anything. But she heard it anyway, in the shape of its silence, and she told it—silently, furiously—to stay still, and walked on.

Vane fell into step beside her. Not behind. Beside.

"A tidy valley," the Inquisitor observed. "Well-tended fields. Good stonework on the meetinghouse." Her gaze drifted, casual as weather, across the orchard. "Your guild kept all this in order?"

"The guild kept everything in order."

"And now the guild is—" Vane tilted her head, inviting her to finish.

"Sick. Scattered. Grieving." Mara kept her eyes forward, on the guildhall steps. "You'd grieve too, if the sky went out over your house."

"I imagine I would." A few steps of quiet. Gravel crunching under boots, hers and Vane's, in rhythm. "Tell me, Apprentice Dravenmoor—what did the Warden's Guild of Thornvale actually *do*? I confess my knowledge of your order is... general."

The question slid in sideways, the way all Vane's questions did, and Mara felt the trap in it the way you feel a loose stair: too late to avoid, in time to survive. Because the honest answer—the answer every child in Thornvale learned before they learned their letters—was *they tend the wards, they keep us safe*, and the true answer was the thing she'd found in the archive three days ago, the ledger entries in Bramwell's younger handwriting, the diagrams of the orchard's roots going down and down and down to the sealed dark where something enormous slept, and she could not give Vane either one.

So she gave her a third thing.

"They sang to the stones," Mara said. "Every dusk. Every dawn. Maintenance songs—ward-songs. It's mostly chore, really. Scraping moss, replacing copper wire, keeping the ledgers." She shrugged, and was privately astonished at how natural the shrug felt coming out of her own shoulders. "Mostly it's chores. People think it's mysterious. It's chores."

"Hm." Vane smiled at the meetinghouse roofline. "My mother said something similar about prayer."

They reached the steps. Two of Vane's people peeled off toward the ward-line without being told, moving with the unhurried certainty of people following instructions they'd been given before they arrived, and Mara filed that away in the growing drawer of things to be afraid of: *they came with a map.*

"The archive is through there," she said, opening the guildhall door onto the smell of ash and fever. "Mind the—" 

But Vane had already stopped, head tilting, nostrils flaring slightly, like a hound catching something on the air. Her gaze went past Mara, down the long hall, to the table where Bramwell lay.

"Ah," she said softly. "The Warden."

* * *

To her credit—and Mara would spend the rest of the day resenting how much credit was due—Vane did not loom. She approached the table at a respectful distance, removed one grey glove, and stood with her hands clasped while her subordinates fanned out through the hall with the quiet industry of ants.

"Bramwell Halloran," she said. "Third name on the Guild Charter of 712. I've read the charter."

"You've read our charter?" Mara said, before she could stop herself.

"I read everything." Vane looked down at the old man's face—at the sunken cheeks, the lips moving around fragments of fever-dream, the eyes half-open and aimed at nothing. Something crossed her expression that in a lesser person might have been called pity. "Warden. Can you hear me?"

Bramwell's eyes moved. Found her. For one terrible, lucid instant, they focused—on Vane's face, on the silver eye at her collar—and his mouth shaped a word. Mara leaned in hoping for *help* or *run* or even her own name.

"*Sabotage,*" Bramwell whispered.

Vane straightened. "Thank you," she said gravely, as if he'd given testimony. "That confirms my working theory."

"That's the fever talking," Mara said quickly. "He says all kinds of things. Yesterday he told me the well water had turned to wine and made me check."

"And had it?"

"No."

"Pity." Vane turned from the table, and her warmth came back on like a lamp. "Apprentice, I'll be direct with you, because I suspect you're a direct person wearing a careful face—I recognize the tailoring, I wore it myself at your age. Three nights ago, every ward in this valley failed simultaneously. Not degraded. Not frayed. *Failed*, all at once, which is not how wards fail naturally any more than houses burn down in perfect symmetry. Someone did this. And whoever did it wanted the valley dark, and defenseless, and—forgive me—distracted." Her eyes were very blue and very steady. "The Crown wants to know who, and why, and whether they're still here. You were the last person tending those wards. So. Who was it, Mara Dravenmoor?"

The hall was quiet. Somewhere behind her, pages turned; a boot creaked on the archive floor. Bramwell breathed his long wet breaths. And Mara stood in the middle of her whole life's wreckage, being asked the one question whose true answer would end everything, and she found—to her genuine surprise—that her heart was steady.

Later she would wonder where that steadiness came from. She would decide, eventually, that it came from the seed: that some part of it had seeped into her bloodline long ago, that calm under scrutiny was simply another inheritance she'd never asked for. But standing there, in the moment, it felt like her own. It felt like the moment she stopped being an apprentice and became something else, though she couldn't have named what.

"No one," she said.

Vane waited.

"There's no one *to* blame," Mara said, and heard her own voice come out plain and tired and true, which was the trick of it—wrap the lie in truth until neither showed the seams. "The guild was six people. Tam Hollis. Maren Cobb. The Ellery brothers. Me. Warden Bramwell. None of us can light a candle without striking flint, let alone unravel a century of ward-work. If someone sabotaged the wards, they weren't from Thornvale." She met the Inquisitor's eyes. "Which means they're gone, or they're hiding, and either way they're not standing in this room. Search whatever you like. We have nothing to hide."

*Nothing to hide.* The seed pulsed once, faintly, under the dead stone by the gate, and Mara willed it silent with the whole force of her mind, the way you will a sneeze away during a funeral.

Vane studied her for a long moment. Long enough that Mara's steady heart had time to consider faltering and decided against it.

"You're very good at this," Vane said at last, almost warmly. "For an apprentice."

"I've had a bad week."

That startled something loose in the Inquisitor's face—a real expression, brief as a fish rising. "So you have." She replaced her glove, finger by finger. "We'll take the east rooms. Your people may keep their weapons; it saves time. And Apprentice—" she paused at the doorway, silhouetted against the grey morning, "—if you remember anything else. Anything at all. About the wards, the guild, the Warden's history. I do hope you'll bring it to me first. Before it finds its way to... other interested parties."

"What other parties?"

"The kind that make wards fall," Vane said pleasantly, and was gone.

* * *

That night Mara sat in the loft above the stable, where the hay hid her from the window-light, and took stock the way Bramwell had taught her to take stock of a failing ward: calmly, completely, without flinching at the size of the breach.

One: the Inquisitors had come with a map. They'd gone straight to the ward-line, straight to the archive, and Vane had known Bramwell's name off a charter older than her grandmother. This was not an investigation. It was a retrieval.

Two: Vane believed in sabotage, or claimed to, and had extracted the word *sabotage* from a dying man's fever like pulling a splinter. Which meant either the Crown truly didn't know what slept under the orchard—or it knew perfectly well, and *sabotage* was the story it preferred, and Mara had just handed her a signed witness statement agreeing to it.

Three: *other interested parties.* Vane hadn't said it like a guess. She'd said it like a woman who'd seen tracks.

Mara sat with her knees drawn up, listening to the Inquisitors' quiet voices below, the scrape of their map-weights on the guildhall tables they'd commandeered, and she practiced. That was the honest word for what she'd done today—she'd lied, and it had worked, and the part of her that should have been appalled was instead turning the skill over like a new tool, checking its edge. *Learning to lie with a steady heart.* Bramwell would have called it the fourth discipline, except the guild had never taught a fourth discipline, because the guild had never imagined a world where the truth was the most dangerous thing an apprentice could carry.

Below her, a door opened. Light spilled into the yard—one lantern, one figure crossing to the gate. To the ward-post by the gate.

Mara went cold from the scalp down.

The figure stopped at the post. Raised the lantern. And Serah Vane, alone, ungloved, stood for a long moment with her palm pressed flat against the old stone—directly over the hollow where the seed slept—her head bowed as if in prayer, or as if listening.

Then she lowered her hand, glanced once up at the dark windows of the stable, and walked back toward the guildhall, and Mara stayed motionless in the hay until the light went out, telling herself the Inquisitor had been checking the ward-stone, that it meant nothing, that her palm had landed inches from the hollow, that she was safe.

The seed pulsed under the stone. Once. Twice.

Like something answering a knock.
