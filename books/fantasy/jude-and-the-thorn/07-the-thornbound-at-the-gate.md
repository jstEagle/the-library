## 7. The Thornbound at the Gate

The bells woke her before the horns did.

Mara had spent three days learning the shape of Serah Vane's occupation: the Inquisitors in grey moving through Thornvale like a slow tide, polite and thorough, asking questions that were not questions. *Where were you when the wards fell. Who taught you the ward-songs. Show me your hands.* Vane herself had come to the guildhouse twice, sat across from Mara with tea going cold between them, and smiled the way a knife smiles—flat, bright, all edge.

"Your master is ill," Vane had said the second time.

"He's dying," Mara said, because lying about small things made the big lies easier.

"And you've tended him alone?"

"The guild was small. The guild is me."

Vane had written something in her ledger. Mara had thought about the seed sleeping against her ribs, wrapped in linen beneath her shirt, warm as a held bird, and had kept her face as empty as an untended room.

Now the bells were ringing wrong—not the ward-bell's steady toll but the alarm pattern, three-and-one, three-and-one, which Bramwell had drilled into her for years and which she had never once heard rung in earnest. She was out of bed and at the window before her mind caught up with her body.

Fire on the western ridge. Not wildfire—the light moved with purpose, crawling down the hillside in a line, like a procession carrying torches. And beneath it, faint but unmistakable even at this distance, came singing.

Mara knew every song the valley sang. This wasn't one of them.

It was low and layered, dozens of voices braided together, and it made the air feel thick, made her teeth ache, made the seed against her ribs pulse once—*twice*—like a second heartbeat answering its name.

"No," she whispered. "No, no, no."

She dressed in the dark, strapped Bramwell's old warden's knife to her belt, checked the seed, and ran.

* * *

Serah Vane had been awake for two hours when the first horn sounded, standing at the window of the miller's house she'd requisitioned, watching fire crawl down the western ridge in a disciplined line.

She had known they would come. The wards' collapse had been felt for hundreds of miles by anyone with the sensitivity to feel it—a cage door swinging open after a century, a pressure change in the world's deep air. The exiles would have felt it the way sailors feel a storm over the horizon. The only question had been how fast, and the answer, apparently, was *faster than the Crown's couriers.*

"Inquisitor." Corporal Dennet, breathless in her doorway. "Riders report maybe sixty on foot, coming down through the orchard road. They're not armed like soldiers—they're—"

"I know what they are." She pulled on her gloves. "Greycoats to the gate. Archers on the mill roof. No one fires until I give the word."

"They're singing something, ma'am. The men say it makes their ears hurt."

"It will make more than their ears hurt if we let them finish it. Move."

She went out into the starless night feeling almost grateful. Three days of tea and questions and watching the Dravenmoor girl lie with that maddening blank face—and now, finally, clarity. The Thornbound always brought clarity. They wanted the same thing she wanted; they simply lacked the discipline to want it quietly.

Her grandmother had been Thornbound. That fact lived in Vane the way a splinter lives in flesh—small, buried, working toward the heart. She had spent thirty years building a career impeccable enough to earn her this assignment, and she would not let a mob of barefoot zealots take from her what the Crown had promised: the seed, the rite, the unmaking. An end to the thing that had shamed her bloodline before it ever shamed her.

The valley gate was old guild work, iron and ward-oak, and beyond it the orchard road descended through trees that should have been bare and were instead budding out of season, white blossom opening in the dark like a wound deciding to bloom. The singing grew louder. Vane felt it press against her skull, felt something behind the world lean closer to listen.

Sixty figures came down the road. Barefoot, robed in undyed wool gone grey with ash, faces painted with spirals of soot and sap. They carried no torches—the fire on the ridge had been signal only. What they carried instead was worse: each of them held a small clay vessel, and the vessels glowed.

Real light. Real warmth. Magic, raw and unhidden, the first Vane had seen outside a museum case since her oath-day.

"Hold," she said quietly, and forty crossbows held.

The procession stopped twenty paces from the gate. From among them stepped a tall woman with a shaved head and spiral scars worked into her scalp, old scars, ritual ones. Her voice, when she spoke, carried without effort.

"We come for the heir. Give us what sleeps here and we go. Refuse, and we take it, and your dead will be many."

"You're trespassing on Crown authority," Vane said. "This valley is under Inquisition seal."

"This valley is a tomb," the woman said, "and tombs deserve to be opened." She raised one glowing vessel. "We are the Thornbound. We remember what you forgot. Wake, sleeper. Your people call you home."

And she poured the vessel out onto the ground.

What happened next, Vane would spend years failing to describe precisely. The light did not spread—it *sank*, straight down through packed earth and stone, and the whole valley shuddered like a struck bell. Every apple tree in the orchard blossomed at once, a white detonation of petals, and somewhere far below them all, something vast turned over in its sleep.

The seed answered. Vane couldn't see it, couldn't know where it was—but she felt the resonance pass through the ground under her boots, a pulse of heat, and she saw the painted woman's head snap up, tracking it like a hound catching scent, eyes fixing not on the gate but on the guildhouse on the hill.

"There," the woman breathed. "There she is."

"Loose," Vane said.

* * *

Mara reached the gate as the first arrows flew.

She had meant to stay hidden—to keep the seed hidden, keep herself hidden, let the Inquisitors and the Thornbound bleed each other on someone else's doorstep. But the seed was pulling at her now, tugging beneath her ribs like a fish on a line, and the singing had changed, resolved into words she could almost understand, words that felt like they were being sung *to her*, and she found herself running toward the fighting instead of away from it, hating herself with every step.

The gate yard was chaos. Crossbow bolts sank into grey wool and the Thornbound didn't scream—they *sang louder*, and where the song touched the fallen, wounds closed, blood slowed, bodies knit. Mara watched a man take two bolts in the chest and stand back up, weeping and smiling, and understood with cold clarity why the Crown feared magic enough to build cages.

Then the Inquisitors' second rank advanced, and they weren't holding crossbows anymore.

They were holding rods of black iron etched with silver script, and when Serah Vane raised hers and spoke a single crisp word, the night filled with a sound like a page tearing, and the nearest ten Thornbound dropped their vessels and fell to their knees, mouths open, songs strangled silent.

Null-rods. The Crown's answer to a century without magic: instruments that ate it.

But there were sixty of them and the vessels kept pouring light into the earth, and the orchard was blooming so thickly now that the petals fell like snow, and through the snow came the tall woman, walking untouched through arrow-fire because the arrows bent around her like water around a stone, walking straight toward the guildhouse.

Toward Mara.

Mara did the arithmetic in half a heartbeat. If the Thornbound took her, they took the seed. If the Inquisitors took her, they took the seed. There was nowhere in this valley the seed could go except deeper inside her, and so she turned and ran for the guildhouse, and the tall woman turned to follow, and between them, impossibly fast, moved Serah Vane.

Vane fought the way she did everything—courteously, precisely, and with a skill that made Mara's stomach drop. The null-rod was a blur; the tall woman's bending-light broke against it in sparks; and when the woman screamed a word that cracked the flagstones, Vane stepped inside the crack, drove the rod's butt into the woman's sternum, and put her down hard.

"Bind her," Vane said, without looking away from the gate. "And find me whoever else has vessels."

Mara stood frozen at the guildhouse door, petals in her hair, the seed hammering against her ribs, and Vane turned and looked directly at her across the burning yard. For a moment neither of them moved. Petals drifted between them like the world's most beautiful snowfall.

"You felt that too," Vane said. It wasn't a question. "When they poured the light. Something in this valley answered."

"The wards are broken," Mara said. "Everything feels strange. I'm just the apprentice."

"Are you." Vane walked toward her, slow, rod lowered but not sheathed. Around them the battle was collapsing into surrender and pursuit, greycoats dragging ash-painted prisoners into rows. "Three days I've watched you lie to me, girl, and you're good at it, I'll grant you. But you flinched tonight. You ran *here*, not away. And whatever answered that song—" she stopped an arm's length away, and her eyes were pale and patient and terrible, "—it answered *you*."

"The guild kept records," Mara said. "If something's buried here, my master's ledger would say. Come back tomorrow. He's dying, and I won't leave him tonight."

Something flickered across Vane's face—not belief, never belief, but a recalculation. A debt of patience extended, at interest.

"Tomorrow," Vane agreed. "And apprentice—if anything else sings to you in the night, you'll tell me. Because I promise you this: whatever sleeps under your orchard, I will see it destroyed. That is not a threat. It's the only kindness left to offer it."

She turned back to her prisoners, and Mara slipped inside the guildhouse and bolted the door and slid down against it with the seed in both hands, pulsing warm, pulsing *awake*, and realized she had learned something tonight worth more than any ward-song:

The Inquisitors hadn't come to investigate the wards' fall.

They'd come for the Thorn. They had always been coming for the Thorn. And Serah Vane—with her ledger and her courtesy and her null-rod moving like a dancer's wrist—wanted it dead for reasons that had nothing to do with the Crown and everything to do with the look on her face when the tall woman had said *we remember what you forgot*.

Recognition. Hatred worn smooth by decades, the way river stones wear smooth.

*My enemy's enemy,* Mara thought, pressing the seed against her heart, *is just another enemy who hasn't gotten around to me yet.*

Above the guildhouse, through the window, the sky stayed black and starless. And far below, deep beneath the roots of the blooming orchard, something that had waited a hundred years heard the song of its people and began, very slowly, to hum along.
