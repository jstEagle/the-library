## 8. Ash and Apple Blossom

The orchard was burning before dawn.

Mara stood at the guildhouse window and watched the fire crawl through the rows she had tended since she was six years old—the same trees Warden Bramwell's grandmother had planted, the same gnarled apples that made cider bitter enough to strip paint. The flames moved wrong, though. That was the first thing she noticed, standing there in the grey light with the seed pressed against her sternum beneath her coat, warm as a sleeping bird.

Fire should climb. This fire *crept*, low along the ground, hugging the roots of the trees like it knew exactly what lay beneath them. Like someone had told it where to dig.

*The Thorn's resting place,* the archive pages had said. *Sealed beneath the oldest tree.*

And now someone was burning their way down to it.

Behind her, the guildhouse groaned. Half its roof had gone in the night—she'd heard the beams let go around the third hour, a sound like a giant cracking knuckles—and nobody had gone up to fight it. There was nobody left to fight anything. Of the twelve wardens who had kept Thornvale when Mara was born, three remained: herself, and two old men too frail to hold a bucket, both of whom she had loaded onto the miller's cart at midnight and sent rattling east toward her aunt's farm with instructions not to stop for anything, not even her.

She was alone. She had been alone since the wards fell and the stars went out, and every hour since had been a lesson in how much of her life had been preparation for a duty no one had named.

The seed pulsed against her chest. Once. Twice. Like a second heartbeat learning to keep time with hers.

"I know," she said quietly. "I see it."

Talking to it had become habit in the last day. She wasn't sure if it heard. She wasn't sure she wanted it to hear. But Bramwell's last instruction rang in her skull like a bell struck once and never quite finished ringing: *It goes with you or it goes with them. Choose who holds it before they choose for you.*

She chose.

Mara took the stairs down into the smoke-thick hall. The archive room was already lost—she'd smelled the old paper going an hour ago, a century of ward-records curling into ash, and she had made her peace with that because she'd already taken what mattered. It sat in the satchel across her body now, wrapped in oilcloth: the guild ledger, the one book Bramwell had told her—fevered, half-conscious, gripping her wrist with fingers like dry branches—to save above everything else.

*"Everything else is history," he'd rasped. "The ledger is evidence."*

She still didn't fully understand what he meant. But she understood *save it*, and saving things was the one skill her whole life had trained her for.

Outside, the heat hit her like a wall. The air tasted of charred apple wood and something underneath it, older and stranger—a mineral smell, like a forge left cold for a hundred years suddenly opened. The seed warmed at it. Mara did her best to pretend that didn't mean anything.

She cut west through the kitchen garden, away from the orchard road, keeping low. The valley floor was chaos even at this hour—bells ringing in the village, cattle bawling in their pens because the sky was wrong and animals always knew first. Above the ridge line, where the stars should have been wheeling toward dawn, there was nothing. Just black. A hole in the world shaped like everything missing from it.

Three days since the stars vanished. Three days since Bramwell collapsed mid-sentence with the ninth verse dying on his lips.

She tried not to think about him lying in the infirmary bed she'd stripped to make his last hours warm. She failed, the way she always failed. Grief, she was learning, wasn't a wave you could brace against. It was weather. You just walked in it.

She was fifty yards from the treeline when she saw the rider.

He came out of the orchard smoke at a walk, unhurried, which frightened her more than a charge would have. Grey cloak, Crown colours dulled under ash, a longsword riding easy at his hip. Behind him, more shapes moved in the burning rows—inquisitors, a dozen at least, moving through the fire like it had been ordered to part for them.

The man reined in ten feet away. His face was young and bored and cruel the way only truly obedient faces can be.

"You're one of the wardens," he said. Not a question. "Girl. Where's the seed?"

Mara's heart slammed once, hard, against the thing beneath her ribs—as if the seed had flinched at the word, or as if she had. She kept her face still. Three days of practice at stillness.

"What seed?" she said.

"The Thorn's seed. Don't waste my morning." He glanced past her at the guildhouse, gauging the ruin with professional indifference. "Your master died raving about it, we're told. Raved about *you*, too."

"He was eighty-one years old," Mara said. "Old men rave."

Something flickered in the rider's eyes—not belief, but the particular frustration of a man whose script has been interrupted. He looked at her a moment longer than was comfortable. Then, over his shoulder: "Vane!"

A woman came through the fire.

Later—much later, when Mara could afford to remember things without her chest tightening—she would admit that her first thought was that Serah Vane looked nothing like an inquisitor. No black robes, no hooked instruments. Just a tall woman in a plain grey riding coat, dark hair bound back, a narrow scar crossing one eyebrow. She dismounted the way other people sat down: completely, without wasted motion.

But it was her eyes that Mara marked. They went to the burning orchard, swept the guildhouse, catalogued Mara herself—all in perhaps two seconds—and came away dissatisfied, like a merchant finding short weight.

"The apprentice?" Vane asked.

"This is all that's left," the rider said.

"Name?"

"Mara Dravenmoor."

"Dravenmoor." Vane repeated it slowly, tasting it. Something passed behind her eyes—recognition, maybe, or the shadow of it—but whatever it was, she filed it away rather than showing it. "You tended these wards your whole life?"

"Yes."

"And last midnight they fell. All of them. Every stone, every song, every binding laid down by four generations of your guild—and it happened in one breath, on your watch, while your master lay dying." Vane stepped closer. Ash drifted between them like grey snow. "That's either catastrophe or conspiracy, apprentice. Which is it?"

"It's grief," Mara said. "Ask me again when I've slept."

To her lasting surprise, Vane almost smiled. Almost. "Steady heart," she murmured, mostly to herself. Then, louder, crisp as a ledger entry: "Search the ruins. Burn what won't burn itself. If there's a seed here, I'll have it by noon." Her gaze came back to Mara, and this time it stayed. "You'll ride with us. Witnesses are evidence too."

"I'm not leaving the valley," Mara said. "There are people—"

"There are forty people in that village, and none of them are your concern anymore. Your guild is dissolved by Crown decree, effective the hour the wards failed. You're not a warden, girl. You're a remnant." Vane turned to mount, then paused. "Or you can stay and explain to my riders why the ground under that orchard is warmer than any ground has a right to be."

The seed beat against Mara's sternum. Once. Hard. And for a half-second—less—she felt it the way you feel another person's hand find yours in the dark. Not words. Not yet. Just *attention*. The full, ancient, unbearable attention of something that had waited a century under apple roots and did not intend to wait for a cage's successor.

Vane watched her face while it happened. Mara knew it, and knew Vane knew it, and knew there was nothing to be done except stand in the lie and hold it.

"Cold ground," Mara said. "Always has been. Valley clay."

"Mm," said Vane.

* * *

They gave her a horse and an escort of two and told her she was being escorted to the provincial seat for questioning, which everyone involved understood to mean prisoner transport with better manners. The column wound north along the valley road, away from the burning orchard, and Mara rode in the middle of it with the ledger against her hip and the seed against her heart and did the arithmetic of escape.

Two escorts. One green, one old. Green rode ahead; old rode behind and coughed. The horses were Crown stock, fresh. Hers was fresh too. The road forked at the mill stream—if she took the western branch she'd be in the hill country by nightfall, and the hill country was a maze of sheep paths no inquisitor had ever bothered to map, because there was nothing there worth mapping.

Nothing worth mapping. That was where she'd grown up. That was the joke her whole life had been telling, apparently, right up until the punchline.

She waited for the stream crossing. The water was high with snowmelt and loud enough to drown orders. When her horse's hooves hit the shallows, she wheeled left, put her heels in, and was through the willows before the shouting properly started.

The old one fired a crossbow bolt that parted her hair close enough to feel the wind of it. Then the willows closed, and the hill paths took her, and by the time the pursuit organized itself she was three switchbacks up and climbing, lungs burning, leading the horse over scree no sensible animal would carry a rider across.

She didn't stop until full dark, in a hollow of granite with a view back down the valley.

Thornvale burned below her. Not all of it—the village was holding, wet roofs and bucket lines—but the orchard glowed like a wound, and above it, where stars had lived for as long as anyone could remember, the sky stayed empty. Black on black. She found herself searching it anyway, the way you probe a tooth you know is gone.

Her home. Her whole world, twenty-two years of it, laid out small enough to cover with her thumb.

She had imagined leaving someday. Everyone in Thornvale imagined it; that was the valley's open secret, the restless itch under the dutiful calm that no one named and no one acted on. She'd imagined roads and cities and a sky with more stars than seemed reasonable. She'd imagined coming back, after. Telling them about it.

She had not imagined this: leaving at a canter through willow branches with a crossbow bolt singing past her ear, carrying a stolen ledger and a piece of a war god bonded to her bloodline, while her mentor's grave smoldered unmarked behind her and the people who raised her watched her go and thought, some of them, that she was running from justice.

Maybe she was. She genuinely didn't know anymore.

The seed pulsed. Warmth spread through her ribs—not unpleasant, which was somehow worse. She pulled the satchel around, unwrapped the ledger, and read by no light at all, because her eyes had begun doing something new in the starless dark. Seeing fine. Too fine. Another gift she hadn't asked for.

The ledger's early pages were Bramwell's grandmother's hand, cramped and exact. Ward-songs. Rotation schedules. And then, deeper in, entries that stopped pretending:

*Year 112. The vessel weakens. Root-seal renewed. Note: the heir-line must be kept close, kept small, kept ignorant. Ignorance is the strongest ward we have.*

*Year 140. A child born to the keeper's line with the green eyes. Watched.*

*Year 167.* — her mother's year, her mother's name — *Watched.*

*Year 189.* — her own.

Every generation, an entry. Every entry, a watching. Her whole family had been livestock, bred in place and noted in a book, and the book had called it protection, and the book had called *her* protection, and Bramwell—

Bramwell, who taught her songs. Who slipped her honey cakes when she was small. Who had held her wrist at the end and said *the ledger is evidence* with tears cutting channels through the dust on his face.

Had he known? Had he known all of it, every year of her life? Or had he only learned late, the way she had, and spent his last strength trying to arm her against the truth?

She closed the ledger. Her hands were shaking, and she let them, because there was no one to see.

Down in the valley, the orchard fire collapsed inward on itself with a sound she felt through the rock—a long exhale, like something settling deeper into its sleep. Or turning over.

"They'll dig," she said aloud. Her voice sounded strange in the hollow, flat and close. "Vane's going to dig, isn't she. Under the trees."

Warmth. Pulse. Attention.

"No," Mara said, and heard the answer arrive in her own mouth before she'd decided to say it. "We don't go back. We go forward. To the capital. Where you were sentenced." She swallowed. "Where you get your trial. The one they never gave you."

The silence that followed was not agreement and not refusal. It was the silence of a thing very old considering whether a girl of twenty-two with shaking hands and a stolen book could possibly be serious.

Then, faint as memory, warmth again. Acceptance, or patience, or the closest thing to either a caged god could offer.

Mara wrapped the ledger, slung the satchel, and led the horse down the far side of the hill, away from the firelight, into country she had never seen.

It should have felt like exile. Some of it did—there was a specific ache, low and permanent, in the fact that she could not look back without wanting to turn around entirely. But underneath the ache, quiet and shocking as a spring under frost, was something else.

Liberation. Ugly, guilty, undeniable. For the first time in her life, no one was watching her. No ledger entry. No rotation schedule. No ninth verse no one would sing. Whatever waited out there in the starless dark—the deadlands, the Inquisition, the exiles who wanted to wake what she carried—it had never once occurred to any of them to check whether *she* consented to any of it.

Well. She was checking now.

The road bent north. Somewhere out there, the ruined capital waited with its buried tribunal and its unanswered crimes, and somewhere behind her, Serah Vane was almost certainly reading the guild's remaining records by torchlight, learning the word *heir*.

Mara walked on, and did not look back again.

By the third hour of the night, the hills ran out, and the land ahead flattened into a grey waste that no map in the guildhouse had ever bothered to name, because no one from Thornvale had ever had reason to go there. The seed pulsed against her heart, steady now, patient, keeping time with her steps.

And far off—at the edge of sight, at the edge of sense—something in that darkness noticed her coming, and began, very slowly, to move toward her.
