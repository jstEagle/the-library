## 9. The Deadlands Road

The valley's edge was a line drawn in ash.

Mara stood at it for a long time on the first morning, the guild ledger wrapped in oilcloth against her back and the seed in its lead-lined box against her chest, and she looked back at Thornvale until her eyes watered from more than smoke. The orchard was still burning. From this distance the flames made no sound at all—just a smear of orange against the grey morning, like something painted on glass. The wards' last light had died with them. The valley looked smaller than it had ever looked, and older, like a face she'd only ever seen by candlelight.

She didn't cry. She'd spent all her tears somewhere between Bramwell's body and the eastern fence, and there were none left over. There was just the walking, and the walking wanted doing.

So she walked.

* * *

The deadlands began gently, which she would later decide was their cruelest feature.

For the first day there were still trees—blackened ones, their branches raised like arms mid-surrender—and still birds, though the birds flew wrong, in straight silent lines, as if they'd forgotten why they circled. The road under her boots had been cobbled once. Now it was cobblestones floating on dust, each one shifting when she stepped, so that every mile felt like walking on the backs of sleeping animals.

By the second day the trees stopped pretending to be trees. They stood as charcoal sculptures of themselves, and when the wind came through they did not rustle but rang—a faint, hollow chime, like a thousand tiny bells with no clappers. She learned not to flinch. She learned to count her steps instead, because counting was something her mind could do without asking questions.

On the third day, the seed woke up hungry.

She noticed it first as warmth. The box rode against her sternum, and the lead that should have muffled it had gone warm as a held hand. Then the warmth spread—not heat exactly, but presence, the way a room feels different when someone is standing behind you. She stopped on the broken road and took the box out of her coat and opened it, though every instinct screamed not to.

The star-fragment sat in its nest of wool, and it was no longer a fragment. It had put out filaments, fine as spider silk, pale gold against the dark, and they reached toward her fingers the way an orchard sapling reaches toward sun.

"No," she said.

She hadn't decided what the seed could hear. She wasn't sure she wanted to find out.

But when she closed the box and walked on, the warmth followed her, patient as debt. And a mile later—she counted; she was always counting now—the memory went.

* * *

It happened like this: she was thinking about home to keep herself company, the way you hum a song in the dark. She'd been running through the streets of Thornvale in her head, market day, the smell of cider pressing, old Marta Halloway shouting about fair prices while everyone paid them anyway—

—and then the thought simply ended. Not faded. Ended. She reached for Marta Halloway and found a shape where a person should have been, a name with nothing attached, a hole in the middle of market day with cider-smell around the edges of it and no one selling anything.

Mara stopped walking. She stood very still on the dead road while the wind rang through the charred trees, and she pressed both hands flat against the lead box over her heart, and she understood.

"It's eating," she said aloud. Her voice sounded obscene out here, too loud, too alive. "You're eating my head."

The seed said nothing. But the warmth pulsed once, slow and content, like a swallow going down.

She thought about throwing it into the dust right there. She got as far as holding it over the edge of the road, arm cocked, before her own hands betrayed her—because beneath the fury was arithmetic, cold and clear. Bramwell was dead. The guild was ash or lies, probably both. Vane was somewhere behind her with Crown riders and a warrant, and Ilse's Thornbound were somewhere ahead, and the only thing either of them wanted was the thing in the box. If Mara threw it away, someone else would pick it up. Someone who hadn't grown up singing ward-songs over the ground it slept beneath. Someone who wouldn't feel, every mile, how heavy it was—not in weight but in consequence.

And besides. She opened her palm and looked at the thin white scar across it, the one the seed's first blooming had left, the bond-mark that matched the bloodline the guild had hidden in plain sight for three generations. The seed knew her name. Bramwell's last whisper had told her that much: *it remembers her name.* Things that remembered your name didn't stay thrown away.

She put the box back inside her coat, next to her heart, where it apparently intended to dine.

"All right," she said. "Then we're going to talk terms."

* * *

The terms took two days to work out, and she worked them out alone, aloud, to a lump of star-metal that never answered—which, she reflected grimly, made it the best conversationalist she'd had since the valley burned.

The rules, as she carved them into her mind the way the ledger's first Warden had carved wards into stone:

One. The seed fed on memories, and it seemed to prefer the ones she held closest—warm ones, worn smooth by handling. Market days. Bramwell's voice. The taste of the valley's apples. It took what she touched most. So rule one was: stop touching. Carry the important things locked, sealed, unopened, like letters you don't dare reread.

Two. It fed more heavily when she was afraid, or exhausted, or bleeding. Fear loosened the lids off everything in her mind, and the seed drank from whatever spilled. So rule two was: sleep enough. Eat enough. Don't get hurt. A survival strategy that amounted to *be careful*, which was insulting, but there it was.

Three—and this was the one that kept her up nights, staring at the starless sky where stars should be—it fed whether she willed it or not, but it fed *more* of what she offered willingly. The first theft had been involuntary, a snatch-and-run from the edges of her mind. But on the fifth night, freezing, out of dry kindling, desperate, she had cupped her hands around the box and whispered *take the rain festival, take the whole stupid rain festival, I barely liked it anyway*—and warmth had flooded through her like brandy, and the cold had broken, and in the morning she'd tried to remember the rain festival and found she remembered only that she had chosen to lose it. A clean cut. No grief attached.

That was the horror of it, and she turned the horror over and over as the miles stacked up behind her: the voluntary losses didn't hurt. They were painless, neat, almost pleasant at the moment of giving. The seed was a courteous thief. It stole best what you handed it smiling.

Which meant that if she wasn't careful—if she let herself get cold, or lonely, or tired enough—she would start trading away her life one pleasant evening at a time, and she'd feel fine the whole way down, and one morning she'd wake up on the dead road as a woman who had come from nowhere, carrying a box, going somewhere, for reasons she couldn't quite—

"Rationing," she said aloud, to hear a human voice. "We're rationing."

She tore a page from the back of the guild ledger—the blank endpaper, thick and good—and she sharpened a bit of charcoal from one of the ringing trees, and she began to make lists.

Not a list of memories. That was the trap she saw by the second night: if she wrote down what she remembered, the list itself became the memory, a summary standing in for the living thing, and summaries were cheap. The seed would eat the meal and leave her the menu. Instead she wrote rules. Categories. Ranks.

*Never spend:* Bramwell's ninth verse, whatever fragments of it lived in her. The archive's location codes. The scar on her palm and how she got it.

*Spend only dying:* her mother's voice. (She hesitated a long time over that line, charcoal hovering, and finally wrote it small.) The sound of rain on the guildhouse roof.

*Spend freely:* everything else. Market days. Festivals. Neighbors' faces. The names of the other apprentices who'd drifted away years ago. All the ordinary wealth of an ordinary life—and God, it had seemed ordinary, it had seemed so small and dull and stifling, and now she stood in the wastes holding the inventory of it like a miser counting coins, appalled at how rich she had been without knowing.

At the bottom of the page, after a long pause, she added one more line:

*It does not get Jude.*

She didn't know why she wrote it. She hadn't yet opened the ledger past its first pages; she'd read only enough to learn what the guild was, and fled the rest. But the name *Jude* appeared in the opening lines like a key stamped into a lock, and something in her—some instinct older than understanding—drew a circle around it and wrote *mine* beside it.

* * *

The deadlands changed as she went east, the way a fever changes.

First the trees rang. Then the trees stopped ringing, because there were no more trees, and the silence that replaced them was worse—wind with nothing to blow through, a sound like the sky clearing its throat and finding no words. Then came the villages.

They stood empty in the dust, whole towns of them, doors open, tables set. She walked through one on the eighth day and found bread on the board, gone to stone. A doll in a doorway. A well with the bucket still tied on. No bodies, no bones, no struggle—just absence, total and tidy, as if everyone had stepped out to watch something in the sky and never come back.

She checked the dates scratched into doorframes where families tallied their children's heights. The marks stopped decades ago. This hadn't happened when the wards fell. This was old ruin. The deadlands hadn't died last week; they'd died slowly, starving, for a generation, and the world beyond Thornvale had simply let them.

*What did we think the wards protected us from?* she thought, walking down the center of a dead street with her boot heels ringing on stone. *Not monsters. Not the Thorn. We were protected from knowing this existed.*

That night she dreamed of the orchard, apples ripe and heavy, Bramwell's voice singing the ward-song low in his chest, and she woke with wet eyes and a warm box against her ribs and the sudden sick knowledge that the dream had cost her something—she couldn't say what, some thread of the pattern, some stitch pulled loose. The seed fed even on dreams. Even on grief. Especially, maybe, on grief.

She sat up in the dark and did the accounting, and the accounting was brutal. Eight days out. She inventoried home the way you inventory a house after a burglary, walking room to room in her mind, finding gaps. She could no longer picture the guildhouse kitchen. She knew she'd eaten ten thousand meals in it; the meals were gone, all of them, a decade of mornings deleted. She still knew Bramwell's voice—but only the last words he'd spoken, hoarse and dying. Everything before had softened into something generic, a kindly old man murmur with no particular face on it yet. For now. She could feel it fraying.

Her mother's voice she had guarded like the last lamp in a flood. She lay awake repeating it to herself, silently, over and over—*Mara, love, mind the thorns*—some scrap of a garden afternoon, refusing to let it smooth out, refusing to handle it gently, handling it instead the way you carry a blade: firmly, deliberately, knowing it cuts.

This was the work now, she realized. Not walking. Anyone could walk. The work was remembering on purpose, rationing attention like hardtack, deciding hour by hour what deserved to be real. Every survivor she'd ever heard of had rationed food or water. Nobody warned you that you could end up rationing your own past, weighing a childhood against a journey, spending birthdays to buy miles.

She thought of Vane's grey riders somewhere behind her and almost laughed. Let them come. What would they take from her? She was becoming a woman made mostly of decisions.

* * *

On the eleventh day, the road ended.

Not ruined—ended. It ran up a long shallow rise and stopped at the crest, and beyond the crest the land dropped away into a basin of grey dust that stretched to a horizon with no features at all, no hills, no ruins, no color. Just flat. Just far. In the middle distance, so faint she might have imagined it, something glimmered—a line of light lying along the earth like a fallen wire.

The seed went hot against her chest. Not the slow contented warmth of feeding. This was sharper. Eager. The filaments, when she dared check, had grown longer, straining toward the horizon like compass needles finding north.

"You know what's out there," she murmured.

The warmth pulsed. Yes.

She consulted her map—Bramwell's map, salvaged from the archive, ink faded to ghosts—and found the basin marked with a single word in the old Warden's hand: *Hollowmere*. Beneath it, smaller, almost apologetic: *the road remembers what men forget. Follow the lights after dark.*

Follow the lights. She squinted at the distant shimmer. It was noon; the glimmer was barely visible. Whatever it was, it waited for dark, like everything else in this country.

She camped early that night, high on the rise, and built no fire—fire drew things, she'd learned, though she'd never seen what things—and ate the last of the valley cheese, saving the rind because wasting it felt like another kind of forgetting. Above her the sky was a perfect, terrible black. No stars. Not dimmed—absent, as if the night had been swept clean and nothing rehung. She had grown used to it the way you grow used to a missing tooth, tongue returning and returning to the gap.

Full dark came. And out in the basin, the lights woke.

They rose out of the dust in a long wavering chain, dozens of them, hundreds—soft gold points drifting in a line that bent and swayed like a procession, like lanterns carried by walkers she couldn't see. And as she watched, the nearest light flickered, steadied, and resolved into something almost like a figure: a shape of pale fire, man-high, walking with a rolling gait. Carrying something. A staff, maybe. Or a scythe.

More shapes behind it. A whole congregation of them, moving through the deadlands on roads that no longer existed, following routes that had been plowed under or poisoned or forgotten—and the lights were the roads, she understood suddenly. The lights were the memory of the road, burned into the land the way footsteps burn into a carpet. The deadlands remembered its travelers even though the travelers were gone.

The seed hammered against her ribs like a second heart, insistent, joyful, horrible.

"They're dead," she whispered. "Whatever they are, they're dead."

The warmth pulsed again. And for the first time since the valley, she felt the seed press a thought against the inside of her skull—not words, nothing so kind as words, but a meaning, arriving whole the way you wake knowing something:

*Dead is not the same as gone. You of all people should know that by now, little heir. Come and see what we kept.*

Mara sat on the ridge with the ledger on her knees and the cold coming down off a starless sky, and she watched the procession of lights cross the basin floor, and she understood that tomorrow she would climb down among them and follow, because there was no other road and because the thing in the box wanted it and because—God help her—so did she.

She opened the ledger to mark her position, and the pages fell where they always fell lately, to the first leaf, where the old ink waited:

*In the hundredth year of the truce, Warden Jude set down these words...*

Jude. The circle around the name, and *mine* written beside it in her own hand, in charcoal, eleven days ago and a lifetime ago.

The lights below flickered in unison, all together, all at once—like a crowd turning to look at her.

Mara closed the book, held the seed-box tight against the place where her memories used to live, and started down into the dark.
