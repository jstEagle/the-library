## 10. A Thief Named Wren

The deadlands had a way of teaching you things you didn't want to learn.

By the ninth day out of Thornvale, Mara had learned that the ash plains changed color with the hour—grey at dawn, bone-white at noon, a bruised violet when the lightless sun went down behind mountains that were more suggestion than shape. She'd learned that the seed in her satchel grew heavier when she thought about home, which meant she was learning to think about home less. And she'd learned that the road east wasn't really a road at all, just a habit the land remembered from before the war, a faint depression in the ash where wheels had once rolled and people had once had somewhere to be.

She'd also learned to sleep light.

So when the sound came—a whisper of canvas, the soft click of her knife-belt buckle being lifted—she was awake before her eyes opened.

She didn't move. She lay in the lee of a collapsed waystation wall, blanket pulled to her chin, and listened to someone very carefully rob her. There was an artistry to it. The thief worked by touch alone, no lantern, no fumbling. He'd already taken the dried apples from her pack—she could hear them rustling—and he was working through her belongings with the patience of a man sorting laundry.

The ledger, she decided. If he touched the guild's ledger, she would have to do something about it.

His fingers found it.

Mara rolled, came up on one knee with her knife drawn, and said, "Put it down."

The thief froze. In the grey half-dark she could make out a slight figure, younger than she'd expected, wrapped in a coat three sizes too big for him, a scarf covering everything below his eyes. He held the ledger in both hands like it might go off.

"Evening," he said.

"It's morning."

"Is it?" He glanced up at the starless sky as if checking. "Hard to tell, out here. Everything's either dark or slightly less dark." He didn't put the ledger down. "Nice book. Heavy. Old. You don't read this, do you? People who carry books like this are usually running from whoever wrote it."

"Put it down or I'll take the hand that's holding it."

"That's fair," he said, and set it down gently, the way you'd set down someone else's baby. He raised his hands, palms out. Dried apple crumbs clung to his scarf. "For what it's worth, I only took the food because you weren't eating it fast enough. It was going to spoil. I was performing a service."

"You went through my belt."

"The belt was incidental. I was after the buckle. Good steel's worth two days' food out here, and yours has a maker's mark, which means it's worth four." He tilted his head. "You're not going to stab me, are you? You've got the look of someone who's never stabbed anyone and is hoping I won't make today the day."

Mara kept the knife level. Her heart was going hard, but her voice, when it came out, was steady, and she was quietly proud of that. Nine days ago she'd been ward-tending in a valley where nothing had ever happened. Now she was alone in the wastes with a seed that ate memories and a knife pointed at a stranger who talked like water ran downhill.

"Why follow me?" she asked. "There's nothing out here worth robbing. You saw my camp. You knew what I had before you crept in."

"I did," he agreed. "Which is why I'm confused too. A girl alone, walking east through the deadlands, carrying almost no supplies, a heavy old book, and—" he paused, and something in his posture shifted, some animal attention narrowing, "—a smell I can't place. Sweet. Like cut wood and honey. You have anything in that bag besides apples?"

The seed. She could feel it through the leather, warm against her hip, and she realized with a lurch that it had grown warmer since the thief entered the camp. It liked him. Or it liked something about him. That was worse than if it had gone cold.

"Nothing," she said. "Who are you?"

"Wren." He said it easily, like a name he'd worn so long it fit loose. "Just Wren. No family name, no village, no debts—well. One debt. But the man I owe it to is dead, so we'll call it settled." He lowered his hands slowly, testing whether she'd object. She didn't, so he sat down across the cold fire pit from her, uninvited, and helped himself to another dried apple with the calm of a cat accepting a saucer. "You're Mara Dravenmoor."

Every muscle in her body went still. "How do you know that?"

"Because everyone between here and the capital knows that. There's a girl walking east with a piece of the fallen sky in her bag. The Thornbound have your description. So do the Crown's riders. Grey coats, silver pins, asking at every crossroads and charging every ferry toll double for information." He chewed. "You're the most famous person in the wastes, and you're traveling alone with a knife and a bad attitude. Honestly, I followed you for two days mostly to see how long you'd last. My money was on five."

"Five days?"

"Out here? Alone? Without a guide?" He shook his head. "You made nine. Genuinely impressive. You drank from the seep at Hollowmere instead of the open pool—that's the kind of thing that keeps people alive, and most folk don't know it. You buried your fire ash under wet sand so the smoke wouldn't show. You even walked the ridge at night instead of the flats, which is smarter than it sounds." He spread his hands. "But you're heading into the Rustlands, and nobody crosses the Rustlands without a guide. Nobody. Not pilgrims, not soldiers, not the Crown's own couriers. The river doesn't care how clever you are."

"I don't need—"

"You've been rationing your food wrong, for starters." He pointed at her pack with the half-eaten apple. "You're eating your heaviest rations first because they're bulkiest. Wrong direction. Eat light while you can scavenge, save the dense stuff for the stretches where there's nothing—not even moss, not even beetles. There's a forty-mile stretch past the river where the ash is poisoned and nothing grows. You'd hit it starving."

Mara stared at him. The infuriating thing was that he was right; she'd been eating the flatbread first precisely because it took up room.

"And your boots," he continued, warming now, gesturing with the apple. "You're favoring the left. Blisters, yes? You should have swapped to the spare pair two days ago and let these dry out stuffed with grass. Now they're warped and you'll be limping into the Rustlands, where limping gets you dead, because the things that live out there—" he stopped himself, pleasantly, "—well. Guides get paid to explain that part."

"What things live out there?"

He smiled. It was the first time she'd seen him smile, and it rearranged his whole face—young, sharp, a little crooked, like a door that hung off its hinge but still locked fine. "See, that's the question a person asks when they're thinking about hiring a guide."

"I'm not hiring you. You robbed me."

"I returned the ledger."

"You ate my apples."

"I *rescued* your apples." He brushed crumbs from his scarf. "Look. Here's the honest truth, since you've got a knife and I've got an empty stomach and honesty costs me nothing right now. I know the deadlands better than any map, because maps are ten years old and the deadlands change every season—the river moves, the sinkholes open, the Crown burns new roads and abandons them. I've crossed the Rustlands six times. I've been to the capital twice and come back, which puts me in a category of maybe eleven living people. And I am, at this moment, broke, hungry, and bored enough to offer my services to a famous fugitive with a knife."

"Why come back from the capital at all? What's out there that anyone wants?"

Wren was quiet for a moment. When he answered, the flippancy had drained out of his voice, leaving something flatter underneath. "People go to the capital to find things. Some find them. The smart ones leave."

"And you're the smart ones."

"I'm the ones," he said. "Smartness optional."

Mara looked at him for a long moment. The sky above was the same blank ceiling it had been for nine days—no stars, no moon, just a dull grey glow that never quite resolved into daylight. She thought about Bramwell, who would have taken one look at this boy and delivered some gnarled proverb about trusting the stream that knows the stones. She thought about the seed, warm against her side, and wondered if its warmth was approval or appetite.

She thought about being alone. Nine days of it. Nine days of talking to herself, of rationing memories without letting herself think too hard about which ones she'd spent, of jumping at wind in the ash. The loneliness hadn't frightened her until this moment, sitting across from someone who'd seen her before she'd seen him—which meant that for two days, anyone could have.

That was the argument for him, and she hated that it was.

"Here's the arrangement," she said. "You guide me to the capital. You don't ask what's in the bag. You don't touch the bag. You don't sell my description to anyone, and if anyone asks, you've never seen me."

"Standard terms," Wren said. "I'll want the knife back, though."

"You stole my knife?"

"I stole your *buckle*. The knife was attached. These things happen." He produced it from inside his enormous coat and laid it on the ground between them, handle toward her, along with her belt, her flint, and—to her surprise—a small tin of salve she hadn't known she had. "Inventory complete. You can count it."

She counted it. Everything was there. Even the ledger, squared neatly on the stone.

"What's your rate?" she asked.

"For you? Nothing up front." He held up a hand before she could object. "Hear me out. Coin's useless in the Rustlands—there's nothing to buy. Food I can scavenge better than you can pay for. What I want is what you've got: passage. Whatever you're doing at the capital, whatever's waiting for you there—I want to be standing next to you when it happens, and then I want to walk away. That's the whole price. You get me there alive, I get myself back out, and we never owe each other anything again."

"That's not a price. That's a gamble."

"Everything out here is a gamble." He shrugged inside the huge coat. "I'm just choosing better odds than usual. A girl the whole wasteland is hunting, carrying something that makes dead men walk and stars fall? Whatever happens to you is going to be *interesting*, and interesting is the only currency I've ever valued."

Mara should have refused. Every instinct the guild had drilled into her—the caution, the suspicion, the iron rule that strangers were weather, to be endured and never trusted—said refuse. Send him off. Walk east alone and proud.

Instead she heard herself say, "Can you cook?"

Wren blinked. "Can I—"

"Scavenged food. Beetles, moss, whatever grows out here. Can you make it edible, or do I spend the next month chewing leather?"

A slow grin spread under the scarf. "Miss Dravenmoor, I have made soup out of a boot, some lichen, and a prayer, and a Crown sergeant wept into it and asked for seconds."

"Then you're hired. Provisional. You betray me, steal from me, or sell me, and I'll take the hand. I wasn't joking about that."

"Noted, and honestly, fair." He got up, brushing ash from his coat, and stuck out a hand. She looked at it. He looked at it. After a moment he withdrew it, unbothered. "Right. No hands yet. We'll work up to it."

* * *

They walked east as the grey light thickened toward a dawn that never quite arrived.

Wren talked the entire time. Mara learned within the first hour that this was simply a condition of him, like weather. He talked about the deadlands—this stretch was called the Larder, because before the war it had been farmland, and the ash here was richer, and certain roots still grew that tasted almost like turnips if you boiled them twice. He talked about the Crown's road crews, who'd built a watchtower visible ahead of them and abandoned it within a year because the ground beneath it started humming. He talked about a ferryman on the river they'd reach in three days, and shut up abruptly when he mentioned the fare, which told Mara everything she needed to know about the fare.

She gave him almost nothing in return. Name confirmed, destination confirmed, everything else a closed door. He didn't push. He just filled the silence the way water fills a hollow, and gradually, without her deciding it, the silence stopped feeling like something she had to defend.

Around midday—they guessed midday; the sky offered no opinion—they stopped in the shadow of the ruined watchtower, and Wren produced lunch from the landscape itself: pale roots, a handful of grey-green moss that he swore was safe "if it's growing on stone, never on bone," and, triumphantly, a beetle grub the size of her thumb, which he roasted on a flat rock until it smelled, genuinely, like bacon.

Mara eyed it. "I'm not eating that."

"You absolutely are. You're eating it and you're going to like it, and then you're going to pretend you didn't, and I'm going to let you." He pushed the rock toward her. "Protein is protein. Pride is not food. I learned that lesson at eight years old and it's kept me alive ever since."

She ate it. It tasted like bacon. She pretended it didn't.

"Where'd you learn all this?" she asked. "The deadlands. The scavenging. You said you've been out here since you were eight."

Wren was quiet for a moment, turning a root over in his fingers. For the first time since she'd met him, he seemed to be deciding whether to answer.

"Guilds," he said finally. "Plural. There are outfits out here that take kids off the ash and train them for things. Thieving, courier work, worse. They feed you, teach you, work you, and if you're useful you survive, and if you're not—" he popped the root in his mouth and chewed. "I was useful. Mostly. Until I wasn't, and then I left, and leaving cost me, and now I'm free, which is a word that means 'no one feeds you anymore.'"

Mara thought of the Warden's Guild. Eleven members when she was born. Four, when the wards fell. A guild that fed her, trained her, worked her—and hid the truth of everything inside a songbook. *We were useful,* she thought. *Until we weren't.*

"We're alike, you and I," Wren said, watching her face. "I can always tell. People who got raised by an institution have a particular way of standing. Like the building's still holding them up even after they've walked out of it."

"Don't flatter yourself."

"That wasn't flattery. Flattery would be saying you stand like someone important." He grinned. "You stand like someone *trained*. There's a difference, and it's sadder."

She threw a bit of moss at him. He caught it and ate it, which she suspected was the point.

* * *

They made camp that night in the shell of the watchtower, on the side away from the humming ground. Wren built a fire small enough to hide in a cupped hand and cooked properly for the first time—roots and moss and the last of her flatbread, transformed by some alchemy of grease and patience into something that smelled like a kitchen. Mara ate and felt warmth move through her that had nothing to do with the fire, and realized with a small shock that this was the first meal in nine days she hadn't eaten alone, and the first in longer than that she hadn't eaten in silence.

"So," Wren said, around a mouthful. "The sweet smell. Cut wood and honey. Still not going to tell me?"

"No."

"Is it dangerous?"

"Yes."

"Is it going to get us killed?"

"Probably not us specifically."

"Huh." He considered this. "That's the most honest answer anyone's ever given me. Most people say 'no' and then we die." He leaned back against the tower wall. "One more question, then I'll sleep. Everyone out here wants what you're carrying. Thornbound want it, Crown wants it, and I've heard rumors of a third party I don't like the sound of. Sooner or later, somebody's going to catch up to us, and I'll need to know which way to run. So: when they come—and they will come—do I run with you, or do I run *from* you?"

Mara looked at him across the tiny fire. His eyes were steady. Behind the flippancy, behind the stolen apples and the boot soup, there was a real question, asked by someone who had learned the hard way that the answer mattered.

"With me," she said. "If you can keep up."

Something eased in his shoulders. "Good answer." He pulled the huge coat around himself like a cocoon. "Wake me if the ground starts humming louder. Or if you hear singing. There's places out here that sing, and none of the songs are friendly."

Within minutes he was asleep, breathing slow, looking about twelve years old in the firelight.

Mara stayed up and kept the first watch. The seed sat warm against her hip, warmer than it had been all day, and when she rested her hand on the satchel she could have sworn she felt something like contentment pulse through the leather.

*You like him,* she thought. *Why do you like him?*

The seed didn't answer. It never answered questions she asked directly. But as she stared out into the starless dark, she found herself remembering—unbidden, unwelcome—the exact cadence of Bramwell's voice saying *patience, Mara, the guild's purpose is patience*, and she understood suddenly, with a cold drop in her stomach, that this memory had cost her something. She couldn't recall what Bramwell had been doing the day he said it. Couldn't picture the kitchen, the light, the season.

The seed had eaten the edges while she wasn't paying attention. While she was busy being warm.

Out past the firelight, far off across the ash, something sang—one long note, rising and falling, neither bird nor wind nor any throat she could name. Beside her, Wren stirred without waking and muttered, "Don't answer it," and rolled over.

Mara watched the dark, and the dark watched back, and somewhere behind them—days back, weeks back, closing—a grey rider turned her description over in gloved fingers and smiled.
