## 11. Vane's Ledger

The ledger had a name, though no one alive was supposed to know it. Serah Vane kept it wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of her campaign chest, beneath the maps and the writs and the spare quills, and she took it out only at night, only when the camp was quiet, only by the light of a candle she shielded with her palm as if even fire might be a witness against her.

She had inherited it the way her family had inherited everything else: badly.

* * *

Her great-great-grandmother's name was on the first page. Not written—branded. The ink had been pressed so hard it had torn through the vellum, and beneath it, in the Crown's careful clerical hand, ran the sentence that had ended four lives and begun Vane's whole existence:

*Elowen Vane. Thornbound. Confessed under examination. Sentence commuted to service.*

Commuted. As if loyalty could be extracted like a tooth and the patient sent home smiling.

Serah turned the pages slowly. She knew them all by heart—the names, the dates, the neat columns of confiscated property, the little marginal notes in some clerk's tidy script: *house burned, well sealed, children remanded to the Crown.* Her family had been farmers before Elowen. Good land, near the western edge of the deadlands, back when the western edge was just an edge and not a wound. After the purge they were nothing. Remanded children grew into Crown wards, Crown wards grew into Crown soldiers, and somewhere down the generations the Vane name had been scrubbed of Thornbound taint and reissued with a new meaning: *useful.*

That was the bargain her bloodline had lived under for a hundred years. Serve, and be permitted to exist. Serve well, and be permitted to forget—or better, to help others forget. There were families like hers scattered through the Inquisition, and everyone knew who they were, and no one ever said it aloud. They were the Crown's sharpest knives precisely because they had the most to prove.

Serah had proven it for nineteen years. She had taken every posting no one wanted. She had sat through examinations where the accused wept and begged and confessed to things that made her skin crawl, and she had written the transcripts in a hand so steady her superiors praised it. She had learned to say *the Crown requires* in a voice that never shook, and she had told herself, every single time, that this was the price of the erasure—that if she served long enough, cleanly enough, ruthlessly enough, the ledger would close and the Vane name would finally mean nothing at all.

Nothing. That was the reward. She had wanted it more than anything.

And then the stars went out over Thornvale, and the Crown handed her the one commission she had spent her whole life believing she'd never live to see.

* * *

"Report," said Captain Doss, ducking into her tent. He was a solid man, honest as a mule, and he looked at the ledger on her table the way he always did—with the particular discomfort of someone who suspected he wasn't cleared to see it but couldn't stop his eyes from flicking toward it.

"The valley's intact," Serah said, closing the ledger. "Wards failed at midnight three nights past. Total collapse, simultaneous. No burn patterns, no sabotage residue at the ward-stones."

"That's not possible."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. Which means it didn't fail. It was released." She stood, rolling the map of the valley flat. "Someone opened it from inside. And then—" she tapped the orchard, the low ground beyond it, "—something came up out of the ground there, or something was taken out of it. The scorch ring is weeks old at the center and hours old at the rim. The battle happened above the vault."

Doss frowned at the map. "Above what?"

"A cellar," Serah said. "A very old cellar." She let him have the half-truth; the full truth lived in the ledger and in the sealed dispatches she'd burned unread, because reading them would have required clearance she'd spent nineteen years earning and still hadn't been granted. The Inquisition told its knives what the knives needed. Nothing more.

"And the girl?"

"Mara Dravenmoor. Last apprentice of the Warden's Guild. Nineteen, maybe twenty. Unremarkable record—ward-tending, song-duty, inventory." Serah paused. "Except the guild roster lists seven members as of last spring. I found six graves."

Doss waited. He was good at waiting; it was most of what captains did.

"So either the seventh member died unrecorded," Serah said, "or the guild was smaller than its paperwork. Either way, the apprentice walked out of a burning orchard carrying something, and every instinct I have says it's the reason my commission exists." She looked up. "Where's the tracker?"

"Resting the dogs. We lost the trail at the river ford—rain."

"We'll find it again. She's alone, on foot, in the deadlands, heading east." East. Toward the capital. Of course toward the capital; everything in the old stories went toward the capital eventually, the way water went downhill. "She'll need food, shelter, roads. Roads have eyes. Post the bounty description—not as a fugitive, as a *witness*. Witnesses come along quietly. Fugitives run, and running girls get hurt, and I want her unhurt."

"You want her unhurt," Doss repeated carefully.

"I want the artifact intact. The girl is the packaging." Serah met his eyes and gave him the flat, official version of herself, the one that had gotten her promoted twice. "That's all."

He saluted and left, and she sat alone with the ledger and the candle and the thing she never said out loud, which was that she had watched Mara Dravenmoor across the length of a burning orchard for exactly eleven seconds, and in those eleven seconds the girl had done two things: she had hidden something against her chest with both hands, and she had looked at Serah—looked at her, straight on, no flinch—and Serah had thought, with a clarity that felt like falling:

*She doesn't know what she's carrying.*

And Serah had raised her hand and ordered the volley anyway, because duty was duty, and because the seed—if it was the seed—had to be destroyed or brought home, and because eleven seconds of a stranger's frightened face meant less than a hundred years of her family's shame.

It had meant less. It had to have meant less.

* * *

She slept badly, which was normal, and dreamed of the western farm, which was not.

In the dream the house was standing again, whitewashed, with apple trees behind it—apple trees, which was wrong, the Vanes had grown barley—but dreams didn't consult ledgers. Elowen Vane was in the doorway with her sleeves rolled, flour to the elbows, and she looked at Serah with the family face, the one Serah saw in her own reflection on bad days, and said:

"You're going to do it, aren't you. You're going to finish what they started."

"It's different," Serah told her. "They were zealots. Worshippers. They knelt to a monster and called it mercy. I'm doing the opposite. I'm ending it."

"They knelt because it *listened*," Elowen said, and her voice was flour-soft and terribly kind. "You think our people were fools. Read the examinations again. Read what your own clerks wrote down and filed away. They asked us why we followed it, and do you know what the answers all say? Not power. Not salvation. They say it *heard them.* A thing older than the war, and it heard farmers complain about their lords, and it judged fairly, and they loved it for that. That's why the Crown burned us. Not for worshipping a monster. For preferring a judge to a king."

"I don't believe you."

"You don't want to," Elowen said. "There's a difference, and you've always known it."

Serah woke with her hand closed around the hilt of a knife she didn't remember drawing, and lay in the dark listening to the camp breathe, and hated the dream, and hated more that she would remember every word of it by morning, word-perfect, the way she remembered everything.

* * *

They picked up the trail on the fourth day, east of the ford, where the rain had held off long enough for the dogs to work. What they found puzzled Doss; it did not puzzle Serah.

"She's rationing herself," Serah said, crouched over a cold campfire ring. "Look. One fire, small, built in a hollow. She ate here—one portion. Slept maybe four hours. Moved on before dawn." She rose and scanned the deadlands ahead: grey scrub, grey stone, a horizon with no stars over it even at noon, because the sky itself had gone wrong since the fall. "She's not fleeing. Fleeing looks different. This is a march."

"Toward the capital."

"Toward the capital." Serah brushed ash from her fingers. "Which means she knows what it is. Or she thinks she does. An ignorant apprentice runs for safety—a garrison, a town, anywhere with walls. She's walking into the worst place on the continent, alone, on purpose." She straightened. "Either someone told her something, or she found something in that archive. Either way, she's not the packaging anymore. She's the courier."

"And when we catch the courier?"

"We don't catch her." Serah rolled the map. "We arrive. There's a difference, and it matters. If we take the seed by force in open country, we risk damaging it, and I risk turning whatever sympathy follows her into a story about Crown butchers killing a guild orphan in the wastes. No. We follow. We stay close enough that she feels us at her back like weather. And when she reaches the capital—when she's tired, and hungry, and standing in front of the thing she thinks will answer her questions—we offer her terms. People surrender to endings. Nobody surrenders to a chase."

Doss studied her. "You've thought about this a lot."

"I've had nineteen years to think about endings," Serah said, and folded the map away.

* * *

That night she took out the ledger again, because she always did, and because tonight there was a new entry to consider making.

She wrote in her own hand, small and precise, the way she wrote everything:

*Day 4 of pursuit. Subject moving east at pace, disciplined, self-rationing. Conclusion: subject informed. Subject's route consistent with knowledge of the capital's significance. Recommendation unchanged: recovery and destruction of artifact per commission.*

Then she stopped, pen hovering, and looked at the first page, at Elowen's branded name, at the clerk's note beneath it: *children remanded to the Crown.*

One of those remanded children had been her great-grandfather. He had grown up in a Crown barracks learning that his grandmother had been a traitor, and he had joined the Inquisition at sixteen, and he had died at forty-two with a commendation and no children old enough to remember his voice—which was false, because Serah remembered his voice, from one visit, when she was six. He had crouched down to her height and said: *Whatever they tell you we were, be better than their story of us. Be so clean they can't write you down.*

Be so clean they can't write you down.

And here she was, a hundred years later, writing people down for a living. Steady hand. Praise from her superiors. Transcripts of weeping.

Serah set down the pen. The candle guttered. Outside, the starless sky pressed on the tent like a lid, and somewhere far to the east a girl was sleeping in a hollow with something warm hidden against her chest, walking toward the capital with a certainty Serah almost envied.

Because that was the crack in her, the one she guarded the way she guarded the ledger: Serah Vane had built her whole life on a simple architecture. The Thornbound had been wrong. The Crown had been right, if harsh. Her service balanced the scales. Destroy the seed, close the ledger, free the name.

But the dream had asked one question she couldn't file away: *if the Crown was right, why did it burn the answers?*

Why seal the wells? Why remand the children? Why keep a ledger of a purge in oilcloth instead of a shrine of a victory in marble? Victors displayed their justice. Only guilty men hid their paperwork.

She had spent nineteen years telling herself the harshness was necessity. Tonight, for the first time, she caught herself wondering whether it had been embarrassment.

"Damn you, Elowen," she said quietly, to a woman a century dead, in a farmhouse that no longer existed. "Even your ghosts argue better than my officers."

She snuffed the candle. In the dark she lay with her hand flat on the oilcloth bundle, feeling the shape of the ledger through the fabric like a heartbeat she'd been listening to all her life without ever once asking whose it was.

Tomorrow they would ride east. Tomorrow they would close the distance, mile by mile, until the girl in the hollow could feel the weather at her back. And when the moment came—when Mara Dravenmoor stood at last in the ruins of the capital with her questions and her cargo—Serah would offer her the terms, and the girl would surrender, and the ledger would close, and everything would be clean.

That was the plan. It was a good plan. It had been a good plan for nineteen years.

So Serah could not explain—then or ever after—why, before sleep finally took her, she unpinned the clasp of her cloak and laid it over the ledger, covering Elowen's branded name, the way you'd cover the face of someone sleeping whom you were not yet ready to wake.

* * *

Two days later, the dogs refused to go on.

It happened at noon, in a stretch of deadland where the scrub grew in strange spirals, and the handlers swore and dragged and got nowhere. Doss wanted to force them. Serah knelt, put her palm flat against the earth, and felt it: a faint warmth, deep down, rhythmic, patient. Like something enormous breathing under a blanket.

"It remembers her name," she said, before she could stop herself—an echo of a fevered Warden's whisper reported secondhand in her briefings, surfacing now from her own mouth unbidden. She stood, wiped her hand on her coat, and gave the order in her steadiest voice.

"We go on foot. Leave the dogs with the baggage train."

Doss stared. "On foot? Into that?"

"The artifact is calling to something out here, Captain. I'd rather we arrive before it finishes the conversation." She looked east, toward a horizon that held no stars and no comfort, and adjusted her sword belt out of habit rather than need. "And send a rider back. I want everything the Crown has on the Hollow Chapel. Everything. Unredacted."

"Unredacted requests require—"

"I know what they require," Serah said. "I'm asking anyway. File it under my name."

Under her name. Under the name that existed on sufferance, earned by obedience, one generation from erasure. She heard her great-grandfather's voice in her head, six years old and knee-high: *be so clean they can't write you down.*

Well. Perhaps cleanliness had been the wrong ambition all along. Perhaps what the Vanes should have been reaching for, all these hundred years, was not clean hands but honest ones.

She didn't believe that yet. She wanted to be clear about that, even alone in her own skull: she did not believe it. She believed in the commission. She believed the seed was a weapon wearing the face of a relic, and that weapons were destroyed, and that her family's shame would be burned out of history in the same fire.

But she had asked for unredacted files. She had covered a dead woman's name with her cloak. And somewhere behind her sternum, in the place where righteous certainty had lived for nineteen years like a tenant who paid no rent, she felt the first hairline fracture—thin as a pen-line, faint as a clerk's margin note—and she knew, with the word-perfect memory she could never turn off, that she would remember this night too.

Every word of it.

Word-perfect.

Forever.
