## 12. The Hollow Chapel

The storm found them an hour past dusk, rolling down from the ridge like something with intent, and Wren took one look at the sky and said, "Chapel. There's a chapel."

"You've said that about three ruins already," Mara said, pulling her collar up against the first fat drops of rain. "One was a barn. One was a hole in the ground full of bones."

"And you're alive to complain about it, aren't you?" He was already moving, quick-footed along the deadlands road, that particular lean in his stride he got when he smelled shelter the way other people smelled rain coming. "This one's real. I've slept in it before. Back when I was running cargo for a man who didn't ask questions I couldn't afford to answer."

"What kind of cargo?"

"The kind you don't carry through a storm." He glanced back at her, and even in the failing light she could see the grin. "Come on, Warden. You can interrogate me when we're dry."

She hated that the title still landed like a stone in her chest. She'd been carrying Bramwell's ledger for eleven days now, wrapped in oilcloth against her back, and every time someone used the word—*Warden*—she felt the weight of it settle differently. Heavier. Less like an inheritance and more like a debt.

The chapel rose out of the dark like a broken tooth.

It was smaller than she'd expected, squat and grey, its roof half-collapsed on one side but holding over the nave. The door had rotted off its hinges long ago; Wren slipped through the gap sideways, and Mara followed into air that tasted of dust and old stone and something else—something faintly sweet, like fruit left too long in the sun.

Wren had flint and a stub of candle out before she'd finished cataloguing the room. Pews in rows, most of them splintered or toppled. An altar at the far end, bare except for a thick layer of bird droppings and what might once have been a cloth. And above the altar—

"Stars," Wren said, tilting his head back. "Huh. Never noticed that before."

Mara looked up.

The ceiling was painted. Not stars exactly—or not only stars—but a great wheel of them, dozens upon dozens, arranged in concentric rings around a central figure that time had all but erased. She could make out hands. A suggestion of a face, featureless now, worn smooth by centuries of damp. The stars radiated outward from those hands like sparks from a struck flint.

"Whoever built this place," Wren said, "really liked their ceiling."

"Wait here." Mara was already moving toward the altar, her heart doing something strange and slow in her ribs.

* * *

The murals were everywhere once she knew to look.

They'd been painted directly onto the stone, and centuries of weather had taken their toll, but enough remained that Mara could read the story the way you'd read a book with pages torn out—gaps everywhere, but the shape of it unmistakable.

The first panel showed a valley. Green hills, orchards heavy with fruit, a river bright as hammered tin. And standing among the trees, vast and dark and rooted, a figure made of thorns.

Her breath caught.

She knew this shape. She'd known it since she was six years old, since the first time Bramwell had sat her down and told her, in his careful, gravelly voice, why Thornvale existed: *There's a thing under our valley, girl. Old and hungry and wrong. The wards keep it sleeping, and we keep the wards singing, and that's the whole of the covenant. You don't pity it. You don't speak its name. You tend your verse and count yourself lucky.*

But the figure in the mural wasn't wrong. It wasn't hungry. It stood among the trees with its arms open, and the tiny painted people came to it freely—one kneeling, one offering a basket of apples, one reaching up to touch a thorned limb the way you'd touch the hand of someone you trusted.

"It's a judge," Mara said quietly.

Behind her, Wren had drifted closer despite himself. "What?"

"Look." She pointed. "It's not devouring them. It's—" She studied the second panel, where the thorned figure sat enthroned beneath the star-wheel, and small figures lined up before it, some gesturing, some weeping, one with arms raised in accusation while another knelt in apparent gratitude. "They're bringing disputes. Grievances. It's ruling on them."

"That's not how Bramwell told it," Wren said. Then, catching himself: "I mean—that's not how you told it. On the road. The guild's version."

"The guild's version is that it's a monster we caged." Mara moved along the wall. The third panel showed the same scene continuing—a verdict rendered, the thorned figure extending a single limb toward one suppliant, and the crowd's reaction painted in careful detail: relief on some faces, grief on others, but no horror. No terror. "Look at them. They're disappointed, maybe. Angry, even. But they don't look afraid of it. They look like they trust it."

Wren leaned against a pew, arms crossed, watching her more than the mural. "You okay?"

"No." She laughed, short and humorless. "No, I'm not. Wren, I spent my whole life being taught that this thing under my home is a monster. That the guild are heroes. That the wards are mercy." Her finger traced the edge of the fourth panel, where the colors shifted—the greens going grey, the golds going brown, the little painted people now carrying weapons instead of baskets. "And here's a chapel full of evidence that they worshipped it. Trusted it. Brought their children to it."

"People worship lots of things," Wren said carefully. "Doesn't mean the things deserve it."

"No," Mara agreed. "But it means somebody, somewhere, thought it did. And nobody in the guild ever mentioned that. Not once. Not in the archive, not in the ledgers, not in Bramwell's fevered ramblings." She turned to face him. "Why would they leave that part out?"

"Because it complicates the story," Wren said simply. "Stories are easier when there's a monster and a hero and nothing in between. Trust me. I grew up on stories like that. They were lies too."

* * *

They made camp in the nave, a small fire because the chimney drew well enough and the smoke would be lost in the storm. Wren produced dried meat and hardtack from his pack, and they ate in silence while the rain drummed on the surviving half of the roof.

Mara kept looking at the walls.

By firelight, the murals changed character. The later panels—the war panels—were violent, chaotic, rendered in reds and blacks that seemed to writhe in the flickering light. Armies clashing. The thorned figure towering over burning fields. But even here, the artist had been careful: in every panel, the Thorn was shown *responding*. Armies marching toward it first. Fires lit at its roots. In one fragment, barely legible, a line of tiny figures in robes—guild robes, Mara realized with a lurch—standing in a circle around the Thorn's base, singing, while soldiers advanced behind them.

"They sang it asleep," she murmured. "Then attacked it while it slept."

"You don't know that's what happened."

"I know what the painting says." She pulled her knees up to her chest. "And I know what the archive said. And I know what Bramwell said. And none of them agree, and I'm so tired, Wren. I'm tired of every truth having another truth underneath it like—like rot under paint."

Wren was quiet for a moment. When he spoke again, his voice had lost its usual flippancy. "My sister believed stories like that," he said. "Simple ones. Monster, hero, done. She died believing one." He poked the fire. "Maybe that's why I like complicated ones better. Complicated ones, at least, admit they're hiding something."

Mara looked at him. He almost never mentioned the sister. "What happened to her?"

"Storm's getting worse," he said, and lay down facing the wall.

* * *

She woke in the deep hours to a sound she couldn't place.

Not the rain. Not the wind. Something underneath both—a low, resonant hum, felt more than heard, rising through the floor stones and into her spine. The seed, tucked in its pouch against her chest, had gone warm. Warm as the star-fragment had been, that first morning in the orchard.

Mara sat up. Wren was already awake, hand on his knife, eyes finding hers across the dying fire.

"You feel that?" he whispered.

"Yeah."

The hum pulsed once, twice, then settled into something almost rhythmic. Almost like—

"Like a ward-song," Mara breathed. "But wrong. Slower. Like it's being sung underwater."

She rose, seed-pouch clutched to her chest, and followed the sound to the altar. Up close, in the dark, she could see what daylight had hidden: carved into the altar's face, beneath centuries of grime, was the ninth verse.

Or rather—she corrected herself, heart hammering—an *older* version of the verses. The first eight matched the guild songs she'd known all her life, word for word, note for note. But below them, half-effaced, ran lines she'd never seen. A continuation. A ninth verse, older than any Bramwell had refused to teach her.

And beneath the carving, in letters worn nearly smooth:

*WE JUDGED AND WERE JUDGED. LET THE NEXT HANDS BE FAIR.*

The hum swelled. The seed burned warm against her sternum, and for one vertiginous moment Mara could have sworn she felt something on the other side of it—something vast and patient and *listening*, turning its attention toward her like a great eye opening after a century of sleep.

"Wren," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "We need to talk about what's actually buried under Thornvale."

Behind her, in the doorway, the rain stopped.

All at once. Between one heartbeat and the next, as if something enormous had drawn breath.

And in the silence that followed, from somewhere out in the dark beyond the chapel, came the sound of many feet, moving together, closing in.
