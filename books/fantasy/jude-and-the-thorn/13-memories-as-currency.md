## 13. Memories as Currency

The deadlands had a way of making you count things.

Mara counted her steps because counting was all that kept the silence from pressing in. Four hundred and twelve since the last standing fencepost. Four hundred and thirteen. The road beneath her boots had once been cobbled—she could feel the rounded stones through the soles, worn smooth by traffic that had stopped a century before she was born. Now the cobbles were furred with grey lichen that wasn't quite alive and wasn't quite dead, and the fields on either side stretched out flat and colorless to a horizon that had no stars above it and no birds in it.

She had been walking for six days. She had eaten the last of the orchard apples on day four, and the last of the hardtack this morning, and now her pack contained a waterskin, the guild's ledger wrapped in oilcloth, a knife, and the seed.

The seed rode in a pouch against her chest, warm as a sleeping bird.

She had tried not to talk to it. That had been easy at first, in the way that not thinking about a thing is easy until you think about not thinking about it. But the nights were long, and the stars were gone—every one of them, a black ceiling from horizon to horizon—and a person alone under a sky like that will eventually talk to anything.

"You're not a person," she reminded it, on the fifth night, as she fed a scrubby fire with dead gorse. "You're a war crime in a seed casing. I read the ledger. I know what you did."

The seed did not answer. The seed had never answered. It sat in its pouch and radiated warmth and made her feel, in the worst moments, less alone, which was exactly the kind of trick something like the Thorn would know how to play.

She had named it, privately, the way you name a horse you don't trust. She called it *nothing*, deliberately, because naming things gives them a handle and she refused to give it one.

On the sixth night, the storm came.

* * *

She saw it coming an hour out, a wall of grey rolling across the deadlands like a tide of dirty wool. Wren would have known what it was. Wren, who was two days behind her and a day's worth of trust ahead of her, which was to say she still wasn't sure she hadn't been a fool to hire him, and she checked the ledger's strap every morning to make sure it was still in her pack.

But Wren wasn't here. It was just her, and the storm, and the flat country with nowhere to hide.

She ran for a ruin anyway—a farmhouse, or what was left of one, a knee-high ring of foundation stones and half a chimney leaning like a drunk against nothing. She got inside the ring as the storm hit, and learned within thirty seconds that the foundation stones were not going to be enough.

The deadland storms weren't weather. She'd figured that out on day two, when the first one had passed at a distance and she'd watched it strip a stand of dead trees down to white bone in the space of a minute. There was no water in them. There was no wind in them, not really, not air moving the way air should. They were *absence* given motion, a rolling hunger, and when one touched you it didn't tear your skin. It took other things. Things you carried inside.

The first storm had missed her by half a mile. She had watched it go and felt, without understanding why, that she had gotten lucky in a way she didn't yet have words for.

This one was not going to miss her.

It came over the foundation ring like fog pouring through a window, and the temperature dropped—not cold, exactly, but *less*, as if warmth itself were being subtracted from the world—and the grey pressed against her skin and she felt it *looking* at her. Feeling for her. Sorting through her the way a customs officer sorts through a traveler's bags.

And then, inside her chest, the seed woke up.

Not physically. It didn't move, didn't pulse, didn't do anything her eyes could have found. But she felt it the way you feel someone standing very close behind you in a dark room, and then—

"*Little Warden.*"

She spun, knife out, though there was nothing to point it at. The voice had come from inside her own skull, from behind her own ears, and it was—

It was her mother's voice.

Her mother, who had died when Mara was seven, who had sung the ward-songs off-key and burned the bread on purpose because Mara liked the crust, whose voice Mara had not heard in fourteen years and could suddenly hear *perfectly*, every grain of it, the little rasp at the end of a sentence when she was tired—

"Stop it," Mara said aloud. Her breath fogged. The grey pressed closer. "Stop it. You don't get to use her."

"*You need guidance,*" the seed said, in her mother's voice, gentle as a hand on her hair. "*And I need what you have. Let us trade, little Warden. It is the oldest bargain there is.*"

"I know what you are. I read the ledger. You're the thing the guild caged. You're the reason the stars are gone."

"*I am the reason the stars are gone,*" the voice agreed, and there was something in it that wasn't her mother at all, something vast and tired underneath the borrowed warmth, like an ocean wearing a dress. "*And you are the reason I am still here. So. The storm is at your ribs, little Warden, and I am the only shelter in this valley of ash. Ask me how to live through it.*"

The grey had reached her knees. She could feel it now, the way she'd felt the star-fragment's warmth in the orchard a lifetime ago—a wrongness, a *pull*, like something tugging loose threads inside her. It brushed against a memory—the taste of the ward-hall's morning tea—and she felt the thread go *slack*, and then it was simply gone, and she couldn't remember what she'd been mourning.

It had taken the tea. She hadn't even said goodbye to it.

"*It will take all of you,*" her mother's voice said, and for one horrible moment Mara couldn't tell if she was hearing the seed or remembering her mother saying it, and couldn't remember which was worse. "*Unless you pay. Give me a memory—a real one, a whole one—and I will spend it in your place. The storm takes what is offered freely before it takes what must be torn. That is its nature. That is my nature. We are old kin, the storm and I.*"

"You're lying."

"*Almost certainly,*" the voice said, and the honesty of it was somehow more terrifying than a lie. "*But consider your options. You are kneeling in a ruin with a knife pointed at fog. I am in your chest. One of us is better positioned to negotiate.*"

The grey reached her waist. Another thread went slack—she felt it go and reached for it and found nothing there, a smooth blank place where something had been. What was it? What had she just lost? She would never know. That was the horror of it. The ledger of the deadland storms kept no record of its withdrawals.

"*Quickly, little Warden.*"

Her mother's voice. Using her mother's voice. And the thing—the awful, unspeakable thing—was that it *worked*. She wasn't afraid of the storm anymore. She was afraid of the voice, and comforted by the voice, both at once, and the two feelings braided together into something she had no name for. Intimacy with the thing she was supposed to fear. The guild had spent a century teaching her to hate the Thorn, and had never once thought to teach her how to *refuse* it when it spoke kindly.

"Which memory," she said. Her teeth were chattering. "You want me to choose."

"*Yes.*"

"You want me to *choose*." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Do you know what I've already lost? Six days. The tea. Something else I can't—there's a hole and I can't even see the shape of it. And now you want me to stand at the counter and *pick*."

"*Yes,*" the seed said. "*Because a memory given freely is worth ten torn away. Because choice is the only dignity the storm respects. And because,*" and here the mother-voice softened into something that might have been genuine, might have been the oldest performance in the world, "*I would know what you love, little Warden. What you choose to save tells me who you are. I have been asleep a very long time. Everyone who knew me is dead or lying about me. You are the first honest witness I have had in a century.*"

The grey was at her shoulders. The blank places inside her were multiplying. She thought, wildly: *the ledger. The ward-songs. Bramwell's—*

Bramwell.

She thought of him and the thought came whole and blazing: the old man in the ward-hall, his hands like tree roots on the lute strings, his voice cracking on the high notes of the seventh verse, the way he'd look at her when she got a verse right—not approval, exactly. Recognition. *There you are,* his face said. *There's the Warden.*

She thought of his face. She held it in her mind the way you cup a matchflame in the wind.

And she understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, what she was about to do.

"No," she said.

"*No?*"

"Not—not that one. Anything else. I'll give you anything else. Take the—take the orchard, take the first snow, take my seventh birthday, I don't even—" Her voice broke. "Not his face. Not his voice. He's the only one who told me the truth at the end, and he's dead, and his face is all I—" 

The storm took something else while she was talking. She felt it go and didn't know what it was, and that not-knowing was its own kind of scream.

"*Ah,*" said the seed, in her mother's voice, and it sounded—satisfied. "*There it is.*"

"There *what* is."

"*The one you love best. You showed me by trying to hide it. Little Warden, I have watched mortals bargain for a very long time, and I will tell you the oldest truth of the trade: you cannot protect a memory by refusing to spend it. You can only decide who takes it. The storm will have him eventually—it is patient, and you are poor, and the road is long. Or I will have him, now, and in exchange I will keep you whole enough to reach the capital, where perhaps—*perhaps*—he can be given back. The deadland storm returns nothing. I,*" the voice paused, and for a moment it was not her mother's voice at all but something older, something that had once judged kingdoms, "*keep accounts.*"

The grey closed over her head. The world went silent and soft and wrong.

And Mara Dravenmoor, last apprentice of the Warden's Guild, kneeling in a ruin above a dead road, made the choice she would carry for the rest of her life.

"His face," she said. "You can have his face. But you keep it *whole*. You keep it exactly as it is. And when this is over—when the reckoning is done, if there's anything left of me—you give it back."

"*Agreed,*" said the Thorn, and for the first time, it did not use her mother's voice. It used its own, and its own voice was like a courthouse door closing.

The memory left her.

She didn't feel it torn. She felt it *handed over*—lifted from her with a strange gentleness, the way Bramwell had taken the lute from her the first time she played the ninth verse wrong, saying *here, let me show you*. And then there was a space in her where his face had been, a Bramwell-shaped absence, and she knew all the facts about him—old Warden Bramwell, taught her the songs, died naming her Warden—but when she reached for his face there was only a warm blankness, like a portrait hung in a dark room.

The storm broke around her like water around a stone.

It didn't pass so much as *lose interest*. The grey thinned, shredded, unraveled into ordinary darkness, and the cold lifted, and she was kneeling in a ruin with her knife still out and tears on her face that she had no memory of starting.

The seed was warm against her chest. Warmer than usual.

"*You did well,*" it said, in her mother's voice again, and she flinched.

"Don't," she whispered. "Don't use her voice anymore. Please. If you ever want me to trust you—don't use her voice."

A long silence. Long enough that she thought it had gone back to sleep.

"*Whose voice, then?*" it asked, and there was something almost—curious—in it. Almost humble. "*I have been asleep a hundred years, little Warden. The voices I have are all dead. I have your mother because your mother sang the ward-songs over my cage when she was a girl, before she knew what she was singing. I have kept her voice all this time. It is one of the few things I have kept.*"

Mara knelt in the ruin and pressed her hands over the pouch and cried for her mother, whom she had barely been able to remember before tonight and could now hear perfectly, and for Bramwell, whose face she had just sold to the thing beneath the orchard, and for herself, who was becoming a person who could make trades like this and keep walking.

* * *

She walked at dawn because staying still was worse.

The deadlands looked the same in every direction, which meant that any direction was as good as another, which meant the ledger was her compass now. Bramwell had marked it—not in words, never in words, but in the margins, little notations she'd taken for accounting: a thorn-rune beside certain entries, a sequence of them, and she'd worked out on the third night that the runes pointed. *North by northeast,* they said, in the old guild cipher. *Toward the capital. Toward the palace. Toward the truth.*

She checked the ledger's strap. Still there. She checked the seed's pouch. Still warm.

"Talk, then," she said to the grey morning, because the silence was worse and she was done pretending otherwise. "You wanted a witness. You said I was honest. So witness this: I don't trust you. I don't forgive you. You're wearing my dead mother like a coat and you just bought my dead teacher's face, and the only reason I'm still talking to you is that you're the only thing between me and the storms."

"*A fair opening statement,*" the seed said. It had stopped using her mother's voice, mostly. Mostly it used something neutral now, a voice like dry paper. She didn't know if that was respect or strategy. With the Thorn, she suspected those were the same thing. "*And yet you are talking to me.*"

"Because you know the way."

"*I know more than the way.*" The seed was quiet a moment. When it spoke again, the paper-dry voice had something underneath it, something she was learning to hear the way you learn to hear weather coming. "*The storm you survived last night—it knew me, little Warden. It bowed. Did you feel it? The deadlands are not empty. They are *attending*. Everything out here remembers the war, and everything out here has been waiting to see what I would become when I woke. And now I have woken in the chest of a guild-brat with a knife and a ledger, and the land is watching to see what *she* will become.*"

"Then it can keep watching."

"*It can. And so can the others.*"

Mara stopped walking. "What others."

"*The woman in grey is two days behind you and closing,*" the seed said. "*She carries Crown steel and an old grief, and she is not the only one. There is a boy behind her who lies beautifully and means well, which is the most dangerous kind of person. And ahead of you, little Warden—*" the voice paused, and Mara felt the seed's warmth deepen, like an ember deciding whether to catch—"*ahead of you, in the chapel on the hill where the road splits, someone has lit a candle. No one has lit a candle in the deadlands in a hundred years.*"

Mara looked north. The horizon was the same flat grey it had been for six days, but now it felt different. Now it felt *occupied*.

"Who lit it?"

"*I don't know,*" the Thorn said, and she believed it, and the fact that she believed it frightened her more than anything the storm had taken. "*But they lit it in my name. And they are waiting for you.*"
