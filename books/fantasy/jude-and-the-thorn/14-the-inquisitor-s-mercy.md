## 14. The Inquisitor's Mercy

Serah Vane had hanged men for less than what the boy had stolen from her saddlebag.

She stood at the edge of his camp in the grey hour before dawn, watching him sleep beside a fire that had burned down to its bones, and she did the arithmetic she always did before an arrest: one thief, unremarkable; one fugitive, absent — gone to the stream by the sound of it, her boots leaving prints in the frost-crusted mud not twenty minutes old; and between them, somewhere, the seed of the thing that had eaten the sky.

The boy slept like someone used to being woken badly. One hand curled near his belt knife, the other under his head, his bedroll positioned with his back to a rock — instinct, not training. He was perhaps seventeen. His ribs showed when he breathed. There was ash worked so deep into the creases of his knuckles that no amount of washing would ever lift it, and Vane knew that kind of ash. She'd carried it herself once, scrubbing at it in a barracks basin until her sergeant laughed and told her some stains went all the way down to who you were.

She should wake him with steel. That was procedure. A known associate of the fugitive Dravenmoor, caught in possession of guild property — the ledger was visible even now, poking out of the boy's pack like a tongue lolling out of a dead mouth — was subject to detention, interrogation, and if he resisted, summary judgment. She had written three such judgments herself this year. Her seal sat heavy on her hip in its lead case, and it had never once hesitated.

But the boy had done something in the last mile of tracking that had stayed her hand, and she hated that it had.

He'd doubled back twice to confuse pursuit, which was ordinary. He'd laid a false camp up-slope, which was clever. But at the false camp he'd left food — hardtack and dried apples arranged neatly where Mara Dravenmoor would find them if the two of them got separated in the night. And beside the food he'd set a strip of cloth torn from his own shirt, knotted in some pattern Vane didn't recognize, though the Thornbound had used signal-cloths in their heresy and she'd burned enough of them to know the habit when she saw it.

A thief who fed the person he was robbing. Or a guide who provided for the person paying him. Either way, it was a complication, and Serah Vane had learned long ago that complications were how duty went rotten. You spared the boy because he left apples, and then you spared the next one because he had a mother, and then you were the sort of officer whose mercy had a shape, and shapes could be mapped by enemies.

She stepped over the fire's remains and put her boot on the boy's knife-belt.

He woke the way she'd expected — fast, silent, hand snapping to empty leather — and then her blade was at his throat, resting in the soft hollow above his collarbone, and he went very still. In the pre-dawn light his eyes were the color of river water over stones.

"Don't," she said.

"Wasn't going to." His voice cracked on the second word, which he covered with a crooked half-smile that didn't reach anywhere important. "Though I'll note for the record you're robbing *me* right now. There's a symmetry there I find insulting."

"What's your name."

"Wren." He said it easily, too easily, the way people say names they've worn out through use. "And before you ask, no, I don't know where Mara is, yes, I'm traveling alone, and no, I won't beg, it's undignified and my knees are bad."

"Your knees are fine. You climbed the scree at Harrow Ford two nights ago faster than my lieutenant could."

Something flickered across his face — recalibration. She watched him do the same arithmetic she'd just finished: *she knows more than I thought.* Good. Fear worked better on people who understood the size of the machine they were caught in.

"Here's what happens now," Vane said, keeping the blade exactly where it was, level as a sentence. "You're going to answer questions. If you answer them honestly, you walk away. If you lie to me, I will know, because I have spent eleven years learning to hear lies, and I am very good at my work."

"That's a lot of confidence for someone holding a knife on a boy in his underwear."

"I don't need confidence. I need truth, and you happen to be sitting on it." She nodded at the ledger. "The guild book. Where is the seed?"

"Seed?" His eyebrows went up in a performance of incomprehension so lazy it was almost an insult. "I'm carrying an apple core or two, sure—"

"Wren." She said his name quietly, and let a moment pass. "My name is Serah Vane. I am an Inquisitor of the Third Chair, Crown Warrant 4471. Two years ago I sat in a cellar in Marrowgate and listened to a smuggler swear on his mother's grave that he'd never moved contraband in his life. He lied for four hours. On the fifth hour I told him I already had his ship manifests and that his mother was dead — she wasn't — and he wept and confessed everything, including crimes I hadn't asked about. I tell you this not to frighten you." She leaned closer. "I tell you because I want you to understand that lying to me is simply a slower way of telling me the truth, and slow ways cost more. So. The seed."

The boy looked at her for a long moment. The performance drained out of him, not all at once but in layers, like frost lifting off grass, and underneath it he looked very young and very tired.

"I don't have it," he said. "She does. She keeps it on her, always, and she doesn't sleep deep enough for anyone to take it, believe me, I checked the first night." He paused, and something almost like shame crossed him. "I stopped checking after that. It felt wrong. She trusts me, is the stupid thing. Nobody's trusted me since I was nine years old, and here she is, handing me her whole pack every time she goes down to the water."

"Why does a street thief guard a fugitive's camp instead of robbing it?"

"Because she pays me," Wren said. Then, quieter, looking at the fire: "And because she asked me to. Out loud. Like it was a real question, like 'no' was a word that existed." He shrugged, and the shrug cost him something; she saw the wince of it. "You wouldn't understand. People like you never have to be asked. Things are just given to you or taken from you."

Vane let the words sit. They stung more than they should have, and she noted that, filed it away in the drawer where she kept everything that would need examining later, alone, with wine.

"You're Thornbound," she said. "The knot-cloth. The way you tracked through the scree. Someone taught you the old signs."

"I'm nothing." It came out flat and fast, a door slammed. "Whatever I was, I'm not anymore. Ask me anything else."

"The guild ledger. Why carry it? It's worthless paper."

"It's not." He met her eyes, and for a second there was something older than seventeen in his face. "It lists everyone the guild ever registered. Every apprentice. Every ward-tender. Dates of assignment." He swallowed. "There are children in it, Vane. Little ones, handed over with payments received noted in the margin. I looked myself up. Page sixty-one. 'Male child, approx. six years, delivered by cart, fee settled.'" His jaw tightened. "So no, it's not worthless. It's the only proof any of us existed. She doesn't even know why I keep it close. I told her it was for kindling."

*People like you,* he'd said. Vane turned the phrase over and found, to her irritation, that it had edges.

She withdrew the blade from his throat and stepped back, and she watched him register that — the confusion of a boy who had already written his own ending and was now standing in a scene that refused to follow the script.

"Get up," she said. "Slowly."

* * *

They walked to the edge of the camp together, and there Vane did something she had never done in eleven years of service: she broke procedure completely, and she did it deliberately, with her eyes open, knowing the exact weight of what she was doing.

She let him go.

Not because he'd charmed her. Not out of pity, which was poison dressed as virtue. She released him for three reasons, and she enumerated them in her head the way she'd been trained to enumerate everything, because enumeration was armor against sentiment.

First: killing or detaining the boy would tell Mara Dravenmoor exactly where the hunt stood, and a hunted animal that knows the shape of the trap becomes something harder to catch. An unexplained absence kept the girl guessing, and guessing cost her sleep.

Second: the boy was a conduit. Whatever Dravenmoor believed, whatever she planned, some of it would leak through him, whether he meant it to or not. A live line into the enemy's confidence was worth more than a corpse.

Third — and this was the reason she would not write in her report, the reason that made her jaw tighten even now — the boy had said *she trusts me* with a face like a man describing sunlight after a lifetime underground, and Serah Vane had been nine years old once too, in a house where the Thornbound mark was hidden behind a plastered-over wall, and she remembered precisely what it was to be given to an institution by people who were paid for the delivery.

No. She corrected herself, walking back toward her patrol's cold camp. That was not a reason. That was a wound talking. Reasons were procedural. Wounds were just reasons wearing grief's clothing.

She had sworn, at fifteen, on her family's shame, that she would be the instrument that ended the Thorn forever. Her grandmother had died in the purges — or worse, had deserved to die in them; the records were ambiguous and ambiguity was its own punishment. Every step of Vane's career had been a payment against that debt. She did not get to flinch now because a starving boy reminded her of ledgers and lost sisters.

Still. Her hand shook slightly as she wrote the message, and she despised the hand for it.

* * *

Before she released him, she gave him the message. She made him repeat it back twice, word for word, because messages were sacred things and garbled mercy was worse than none.

"Tell her this. Tell her: *The Inquisitor Vane offers terms. Deliver the seed into Crown custody, freely given, and the warrant against Mara Dravenmoor is dissolved. She walks free. Full pardon, sealed by the Third Chair, witnessed under law. No conditions beyond the seed. No tricks. She has my oath on it, and my oath has never been broken, not once, not to friend or enemy.*"

Wren repeated it back, both times, perfectly. Then he said: "That's it? That's the whole message?"

"That's the whole message."

"No threat at the end? No 'or else'? Usually there's an 'or else.'"

"The 'or else' is implied by my profession," Vane said dryly. "It goes without saying, which is what makes it effective. If I say it aloud it becomes a negotiation, and I am not negotiating. This is an offer. Offers expire."

"When does it expire?"

"When I catch her without her choosing to accept." She studied him a moment. "You may tell her that part too, if you like. I don't deal in half-truths. Half-truths are how the guilds got where they are, and how the Thornbound did, and I have spent my life being the broom that sweeps up after both of them."

"And what happens to the seed, if she gives it up?" Wren's voice had gone careful. "Officially. What does the Crown do with it?"

"It is destroyed. Rite of Unmaking, performed under supervision, documented in triplicate." She said it evenly, and watched him flinch at the ritual phrasing — he'd heard those words before, somewhere, in whatever life he'd burned behind him. "The thing beneath our feet for a thousand years ends, and the stars can come home, and no more children get sold into wardenships to feed it. That's the whole of it. That's the dream, anyway. Dreams require paperwork."

"You really believe that," the boy said, and it wasn't quite a question. "You really believe destroying it fixes anything."

"I believe a debt unpaid accrues interest," Vane said. "And we have paid interest on this thing for a thousand years. Time to settle the principal."

He looked at her for a long moment, and she recognized the look — it was the look of someone deciding whether to say a true thing to a dangerous person. She waited. Patience was the first tool of her trade and the only one she'd never dulled.

"My sister," he said finally, "would've liked you. She was like that. Everything in columns. Debts and payments." His mouth twisted. "She'd have understood the offer, too. Taking the deal that saves the person in front of you, even if—" He stopped himself, hard, like a man stepping back from a ledge. "Never mind. Message received. Both times, word for word, I swear it on—" He paused, considering what he had to swear on, and came up visibly empty. "On nothing. But I swear it."

"That's the most honest oath I've heard this year," Vane said. "Go. Walk south-east until the stream bends, then lose yourself properly. If my trackers find you within a mile of her camp again, this conversation never happened and I proceed accordingly."

He gathered his things — the ledger went into his shirt, against his skin, and she pretended not to notice — and then he paused at the edge of the trees.

"Inquisitor." He didn't turn around. "When she asks me what you wanted — because she will ask, she notices everything, it's the most annoying thing about her — what should I tell her you wanted?"

Vane held the silence for three breaths.

"Tell her the truth," she said. "All of it. I told you I don't deal in half-truths, and I meant it. Tell her exactly what was offered and exactly what it costs. What she does with the choice is hers." She paused. "I would rather she hate me for a clean offer than trust me for a dirty one."

The boy glanced back at her then, and there was something in his face she couldn't read — not gratitude, not fear. Something more like recognition, unwelcome on both sides.

"You're not what I expected," he said.

"Nobody ever is," Vane said. "That's the tragedy of the whole profession. Go."

He went. She watched until the treeline swallowed him, then stood alone in the greying light with her hand resting on her seal-case, doing the examination she'd promised herself she'd do later, alone, with wine. It was barely past dawn and she was doing it now, cold sober, which she suspected said something damning.

*An unexplained absence kept the girl guessing.* That was reason one. Clean, procedural, defensible before any tribunal.

But beneath it, in the drawer she kept locked, another entry had opened itself, and it read: *You let him go because you wanted her to choose. Because a pardon given under force is worth nothing, and you are tired — God, you are so tired — of taking things from people who were never asked.*

Her grandmother had never been asked either. That was the whole horror of the purges — not the burning, but the paperwork, the efficiency, the complete absence anywhere in the record of a single question put to the condemned. Vane had built her entire life on the promise of being different from the people who did that. And here she was, hunting a girl across the waste to take from her the one thing she carried, offering mercy with one hand while the other hand held the rite of Unmaking.

*It is different,* she told herself. *The Thorn is not a person. The seed is not a soul. It's a weapon left loaded in the world's cradle.*

The argument was sound. It had always been sound. She had recited it to herself for twenty years the way soldiers recited drill.

So why, she wondered, staring at the place where the boy had vanished, did it suddenly sound like something written in triplicate?

* * *

By mid-morning her trackers reported movement at the stream camp — the girl returning, the boy arriving an hour behind her, the two of them sitting by the re-lit fire talking for the better part of an hour. Too long for a simple report. Vane watched through her far-glass from the ridge, close enough to see the girl's face change as Wren spoke, and she found herself leaning forward despite herself, hungry for the answer to a question that had no business mattering to her:

Would she consider it?

The girl stood abruptly. Walked to the edge of the camp. Stood with her back to the boy for a long time, hands opening and closing at her sides. When she turned around, whatever she said made Wren look down at the ground.

Then they struck camp and moved north — not east, toward the capital road as Vane's maps predicted, but north, into country her charts marked with a single word in faded ink: *Rustwater.*

The poisoned river. The ferry.

Vane lowered the glass slowly. North meant the girl hadn't accepted the offer — but she also hadn't dismissed it. North meant she wanted to cross the river before deciding, which meant she wanted distance, options, time. A woman running would go east. A woman convinced would surrender. Only a woman still weighing the scales went north.

"She's thinking about it," Vane murmured, and heard her own voice catch on the hope in it, and hated the hope, and could not put it down.

She turned to her lieutenant. "Break camp. We ride for Rustwater ford. And send a rider ahead to the ferryman — I want to know his price before she reaches him."

The lieutenant blinked. "His price, ma'am?"

"Everything on that river costs something," Vane said, gathering her reins. "I intend to know what it costs *her* — and whether I'm willing to let her pay it."

Behind them, far to the south, the sun rose over a sky that had held no stars for thirty-one days. Vane did not look at it. She had stopped looking weeks ago. Some absences you could endure only by refusing to count them.

She spurred her horse north, toward the rust-colored water, telling herself she rode to intercept a fugitive.

Knowing, in the locked drawer where she kept her truest entries, that she rode to learn the price of a mercy she was no longer certain she could afford to give.
