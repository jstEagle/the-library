## 15. The Ferryman of Rust

The river found them before they saw it.

Mara smelled it first—something between old coins and burnt hair—and then the ground began to change, the grey deadland dust giving way to a crust of rust-colored flakes that crunched underfoot like beetle shells. Wren stopped at the top of a low rise and made a sound in the back of his throat that she'd learned to read as *trouble*.

"That's not water," he said.

It wasn't. The river ran slow and thick as syrup, and where it moved it didn't catch light so much as swallow it. The banks were lined with the skeletons of machines Mara had no names for—great ribbed hulls half-buried, wheels taller than houses, all of them furred with orange corrosion that flaked away on the wind and drifted across the surface like pollen. Rust. Rust for miles. It was the first thing in the deadlands that looked like it had been *made*, once, by people who thought they'd have a future.

"It's the Verrin," Wren said. He said it the way you'd say the name of a dead relative. "Poisoned since the war. Nothing crosses it alive except the ferry."

"There's a ferry?"

"There's always a ferry." He squinted down at the near bank, where a wooden dock jutted out over the rust-water, warped but standing, and beyond it a flat-bottomed barge moored to a post with chain gone the same orange as everything else. "And there's always a price."

"I don't have coin."

"No," Wren agreed. "Nobody crossing this river has coin. That's kind of the point."

They picked their way down through the machine-graves, and Mara kept one hand closed around the seed in her pocket. It had been quiet for three days—not silent, exactly, but *polite*, the way a guest is polite when the household is grieving. She was almost out of memories worth rationing. She'd spent her mother's lullaby on day two of the wastes, her grandmother's kitchen on day five, and last night, in the worst hour of the cold, she had very nearly spent Bramwell's voice teaching her the ward-songs before she caught herself and burned a memory of a festival instead—a whole festival, drums and honey-cakes and her cousin's face, gone, just to stay warm enough to walk.

She was starting to understand what the seed actually ate. Not memories themselves. It ate the *having* of them. And every time it fed, she felt it settle deeper into her bloodline like a root finding soil.

At the dock, the ferryman was waiting.

He was an old man, or something shaped like one, wrapped in layered coats gone stiff with rust-dust, his skin the same color as the river. His eyes were the only living thing about him—pale, quick, and terribly awake. He sat on the barge's gunwale mending a net with hands that never seemed to look at their work.

"Two," he said, without looking up. "One heavy with cargo, one light with nothing."

"Excuse me?" Wren said.

"You heard me." The ferryman bit a thread and tied it off. "Fare's a memory each. One true, one yours, given free. I don't take the ones you'd sell cheap. I can tell the difference." Now he looked up, and his gaze went straight past them both—to Mara's pocket, where the seed sat. He smiled with no warmth in it at all. "Yours will be a rich fare, Warden."

"Don't call me that."

"Then don't carry the office around in your pocket." He rose, joints crackling like the rust itself. "Board when you've paid. The river doesn't wait after dark, and after dark, the things in the rust come up to see what the rust tastes like now."

Mara's stomach turned over. "A memory. Just—one? Gone?"

"Gone is gone." The ferryman shrugged. "I keep them. That's my work. I hold what the river takes from people so it isn't wasted. Somebody has to remember the remembering." He gestured at the far shore with his chin. "Or swim. I hear it's a quick death. Slow drowning, technically, but quick enough."

Wren laughed—one sharp bark with no humor in it. "Charming profession you've got."

"Somebody has to be charming." The old man settled back onto his perch. "Pay up or walk upstream. It's four hundred miles to a ford, and the ford's guarded by worse than me."

Mara sat down on the edge of the dock and let her legs hang over the rust-water. Below her, the surface moved like cooling metal, and somewhere deep beneath it, she could feel the faint wrong hum of whatever had poisoned it—a residue of the old war, soaked so far into the world that it would outlast everyone who remembered why.

She thought about what she had left. Her inventory, kept the way other people kept coin purses: Bramwell's face—*no, not that, not yet.* The smell of the guild hall's morning bread. The way frost looked on orchard branches. The ninth verse—she wasn't sure that even counted as a memory, more a shape in her throat, but she'd die before she spent it. Her mother's hands braiding her hair. Her own name spoken by someone who loved her—that one frightened her, because lately, when she reached for it, the edges felt soft, like bread left in water.

"What do you do with them?" she asked the ferryman. "The ones you take."

"I listen to them." He resumed his mending. "Every night, after the river goes still. A man's last summer with his brother. A woman's wedding, all four hours of it, including the part where the cake collapsed. A child's first snow." His hands paused. "That's how I know your cargo's heavy, girl. You carry grief like ballast. It makes you sit different in a boat."

"I'm not getting in any boat," Wren muttered. But he sat down next to Mara anyway, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, which was how he said things he couldn't say out loud.

"Wren," she said quietly. "You should go first. If you're going to do this."

"Why?"

"Because if you watch me pay, you'll talk yourself out of it, and if we're stuck here after dark—" She nodded at the rust-fields behind them, where, now that she looked properly, shapes were standing very still among the machine-hulls. Watching. Waiting for dark with the patience of things that had waited a long time already.

Wren followed her gaze and went pale. "Right. Yes. Fine." He stood, cracked his knuckles like he was about to arm-wrestle someone, and addressed the ferryman. "How does it work? Do I think hard about something? Is there a ritual? Blink twice?"

"You just give it," the ferryman said. "Hold it in mind, all of it, every corner, and let it go toward me. Like handing over a lamp. Most people can't help letting go, once they decide. The body knows a lie from a gift."

"And you'll know if I shortchange you."

"Oh, I'll know." The ferryman's pale eyes glinted. "But here's a kindness, since you asked none: pick something you can survive losing. Everyone has at least one."

Wren was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, mostly to himself, "Yeah. Yeah, I've got a few of those. That's sort of my whole situation."

He closed his eyes.

Mara watched him do it—the way his face changed, the way his breath caught and held. Whatever he'd chosen, it cost him. She saw it leave. There was no light, no drama; the memory just went out of him the way heat leaves a room when a door opens, and afterward he swayed slightly and gripped the dock's edge, blinking, looking around like a man who'd walked into a room and forgotten which door he'd come through.

Then his expression did something strange. It went through confusion, and landed somewhere much worse than grief.

"Huh," he said softly. "Huh. That's—" He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "There's a hole. There was something right there and there's a hole, and I can't even—" He looked at the ferryman with sudden naked fear. "What did I give you? What was it?"

"A fair fare," the ferryman said gently. "You'll live. Some people find out what they gave years later, when they reach for it. Some never find out at all."

"That's monstrous."

"Yes," the ferryman agreed placidly. "Board the boat."

Wren boarded. Mara stayed on the dock one moment longer, because she'd seen his face, and she understood now that the ferryman hadn't taken just any memory from him. He'd taken one Wren hadn't known the weight of until it was gone. And the hole it left was bleeding.

*Pick something you can survive losing.*

She stood, walked to the barge, and held out her hand toward the ferryman—not touching, just offering, the way you'd offer a palm to a bird.

"I'll pay," she said. "But I choose knowing what I lose. Tell me what you're taking while I give it. Promise me that much."

The ferryman studied her for a long time. Up close, she could see that his rust-colored skin wasn't age—it was saturation, decades of holding other people's precious things until they dyed him through.

"You're the first one in eleven years to ask for terms," he said. "All right, Warden-that-isn't. Terms accepted. Give."

She chose the festival after all—or rather, what remained of it, since the seed had already thinned it. The drums. Honey-cakes. Her cousin laughing with sticky fingers. She gathered every scrap of it, held it up in her mind like a lamp, and let it go toward the pale-eyed man on the barge.

It hurt less than spending it on the seed. That was the terrible part. Giving it to the ferryman felt almost like *telling someone about it*, one last time, and being heard.

When it was done, the ferryman exhaled slowly, like a man tasting wine. "Drums," he murmured. "Honey-cakes. Someone's cousin. A good one—loud, kind, got cake frosting on their nose and didn't care." He nodded once, formally. "Paid in full. Board."

She boarded. The chains came loose with a shriek of rust, and the barge slid out onto the poisoned river, pole-driven, silent, the far shore a thin dark line ahead.

* * *

Halfway across, the rust-things came to the banks.

Mara saw them first on the near side—shapes unfolding from among the machine-hulls, too tall, too many joints, faces like eroded stone. They didn't enter the water. They stood at the shoreline and watched the barge pass with the fixed attention of beggars outside a lit window. On the far bank, others rose to meet them, and for a few minutes the river ran between two lines of silent hunger.

"They want the memories," the ferryman said conversationally, poling. "Rust remembers nothing. That's its whole nature. These things are what forgetting looks like when it gets up and walks. Every crossing I make, they hope somebody falls overboard. A memory floating free in the water—" He shook his head. "Feast of centuries."

Wren had wedged himself in the bow with his knees drawn up, arms locked around them. He hadn't spoken since paying. Now he said, without looking at anyone, "I know what I gave."

Mara waited.

"It was my sister." His voice was flat, careful, like a man walking on ice he didn't trust. "Not her—I mean, I knew it would be something about her, I've got a hundred memories of her, that was the trick of it, I figured I could afford one. But I can't remember which one. That's the thing. I still remember *her*. I remember her name, her face, that she liked—she liked—" His jaw worked. "I remember that there was something she liked. I can't remember what. There's a gap shaped like it. And now every memory I have of her has this hole in it, like a tooth missing in a smile, and I keep going to the gap and pressing on it, and it just—" His breath shuddered. "It just hurts. All of them hurt now. I traded one memory and lost a piece of all of them."

The ferryman poled steadily and said nothing. This, Mara suspected, was a speech he'd heard before.

"My sister's dead," Wren said, to the rust-water. "Has been since I was nine. And the guild that raised me—raised is a strong word—the guild sold us. Sold the children. Little ones, orphans, strays, whoever they could scoop up. To families, to factories, to anyone with coin and no questions. My sister went to a tannery owner upcountry. I went to a cutpurse crew because nobody bid on me." He laughed, awful and small. "So when I tell you I know thieves, Warden, I'm being modest. I was inventory. Tagged and shelved. And the guild elders wore grey robes and talked about service and duty, and every word of it was a price tag."

Mara felt something turn over in her chest, slow and heavy.

"The Wardens' Guild," she said. "Mine. We took in orphans too. 'Children of the valley,' Bramwell called us. Raised us in the guild hall, fed us, taught us songs. And the whole time—" She looked down at her pocket, at the seed. "The whole time, some of us weren't children at all. We were *placements*. Insurance. Bloodlines seeded into the valley generations deep so that when the cage needed an heir, there'd be one ready." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Bramwell might not even have known. Or he knew and told himself it was protection. Patience. They loved that word."

"So your guild hid you inside a life," Wren said, "and mine sold me out of one."

"And we both ended up on the same barge."

"Yeah." Something that wasn't quite a smile crossed his face. "Funny old world."

The ferryman cleared his throat. "If the two of you are finished discovering kinship, mind the shallows. The rust runs deep here, and it likes to taste boats."

They rode the rest of the way in silence, but it was a different silence than before—shoulders nearly touching again, the shared silence of two people who'd been used by institutions that called it love, and had survived it anyway, and were still, against all odds, walking.

The barge ground against the far dock. The rust-things on that shore had withdrawn, sulkily, back among the hulls.

As they stepped off, the ferryman spoke once more.

"Warden." Mara turned. He was watching her with those wide-awake eyes, and for a moment he didn't look like a toll-collector at all. He looked like a librarian guarding a burning archive. "Your fare was thin. Thinned before you gave it. Something's been eating your past for days—I can taste the leavings." His gaze dropped, briefly, to her pocket. "Whatever you're carrying, it doesn't just feed. It *keeps*. Things given to it aren't always gone. Remember that, when you get where you're going. When you stand in front of what you're standing in front of."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"It means the river's been carrying rumors downstream for weeks." He pushed off from the dock, already retreating, pole dipping into the rust. "It means everyone on this water is talking about the city where the stars went out. It means—" The mist of the far bank swallowed him for a heartbeat, and when his voice came again it seemed to come from everywhere. "It means there are riders on the road behind you, girl. Grey cloaks. And they paid *me* a visit yesterday, asking after two travelers. I told them nothing. But I'm old, and Inquisitors are patient, and Serah Vane doesn't ask twice."

The rust-mist closed over the barge. The last thing they saw of him was his pale eyes, and the last thing they heard was the creak of his pole, keeping time like a slow heart.

Wren exhaled. "Grey cloaks. How far behind?"

"Close enough that he bothered telling us." Mara turned south, toward the horizon where the ruined capital waited, starless and patient. The seed pulsed once against her palm—warm, attentive, almost eager. "We walk tonight. We rest when Vane rests, eat when Vane eats."

"And if she catches us?"

Mara started walking. "Then she learns what a Warden does."

Behind them, in the rusted hulls along the shore, the forgotten things settled back down to wait—and far off, at the very edge of hearing, drums played a festival neither of them could quite place anymore.
