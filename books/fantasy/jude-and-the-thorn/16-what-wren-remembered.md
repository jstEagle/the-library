## 16. What Wren Remembered

The fire had burned down to a red heart, and Wren sat apart from it the way he always did—close enough to be warm, far enough to watch.

Mara slept with her back against a fallen beam, the seed wrapped in cloth and cradled in both hands even now, even unconscious. Like a child that wouldn't let go of its mother's finger. He'd watched her do it for three nights running. She never once let it out of reach, not to eat, not to wash, not to sleep.

He understood that better than she probably knew.

The river crossing still sat wrong in him. Not the water—the water had been its own misery, cold enough to make his bones sing—but the payment. The ferryman had held out his rusted scale, and Wren had reached into himself the way you'd reach into a pocket, feeling for something loose, something he could spare without missing.

He'd found it fast. Too fast.

That was the part that kept him awake. When the ferryman asked, some part of him had already known what was there to give, had already flinched toward it like a tongue finding a broken tooth. And he'd given it anyway, because the alternative was staying on that bank until the poison fog ate them both, and because—

Because he hadn't wanted to carry it anymore. That was the ugly truth under the truth. Some nights the memory of her had been so heavy he'd thought about leaving it somewhere on purpose. A ditch. A gutter. Anywhere.

Now it was gone, and all he had left of it was the shape of the hole.

* * *

Her name had been Sera. He knew that much still—the name survived when the rest went, the way a hook survives the fish being eaten. Sera. Small, sharp-eyed, six years old when the Guild of the Kindly Hand bought her off a famine cart for two loaves and a promise. Bought him too, but that was later; he was nine and worth less per pound.

They'd grown up in the guild's warrens beneath Corroway, in the years before Corroway died. She used to sleep curled against his side because the dormitories were cold and he ran hot, and she'd press her freezing feet against his calves and laugh when he yelped. That had been their whole economy: warmth for laughter, laughter for warmth.

She'd been quick. Quicker than him, though he'd never said so out loud where anyone could hear. She could pick a lock by feel in the dark by the time she was eight, could read faces like other children read primers, could tell which of the handlers were in a mood just by the sound of their boots on the stairs. He'd been proud of her the way you're proud of a blade you helped sharpen.

And then the guild had decided she was more useful elsewhere.

He didn't remember the details anymore—that was the mercy or the cruelty of it, he couldn't decide which. The memory he'd paid at the river had contained everything after the night the boots came up the stairs too quiet, the night she'd gripped his sleeve and whispered *don't make noise, Wren, don't look* — no. Wait.

No, that wasn't right either. He was reaching for scenes and finding only smoke. He remembered the *fact* of her being taken upstairs to serve the guildmaster personally. He remembered the fact that she'd stopped laughing within a season. He remembered the fact that one winter morning there was a small box, and the guildmaster had put a hand on his shoulder, heavy as a roof beam, and said, *You should be proud. She served.*

He remembered that he had run that same year, and that he'd never gone back, and that the not-going-back had been the single act of cowardice his whole life was built around.

But her face—he'd paid that at the river. Her voice. The weight of her feet against his legs in the dark. The specific blue-black of her hair. All of it, poured into a rusted pan on a poisoned river and carried away by a man who smelled of lamp oil and rot.

What remained was arithmetic. Facts without flesh. A sister named Sera who had been sold, used, and boxed, and a brother who hadn't looked back.

Wren fed a stick into the fire and watched it catch.

* * *

"You're brooding."

He almost jumped out of his skin. Mara stood at the edge of the firelight, hair loose, eyes half-shut, swaying slightly like a woman walking in her sleep.

"Go back to bed."

"I heard you moving around." She lowered herself onto the beam opposite him, the seed settling into her lap like it belonged there. "You've been strange since the river."

"I'm always strange. You noticed late."

She didn't smile. That was the thing about Dravenmoor—she took things seriously past the point of comfort. Most people would have let the joke land and moved on. She just looked at him across the coals with those tired, stubborn eyes and waited, the way Bramwell's ghost apparently used to wait on her, until the silence got expensive.

"The ferryman," she said finally. "What did you give him?"

"Nothing important."

"Liar."

"It's the polite kind of lying," he said. "Social lying. Keeps friendships intact."

"We're not friends?"

The question caught him sideways, mostly because she asked it flat, without performance, like she genuinely wanted the ledger balanced. He looked at the fire instead of her.

"We're colleagues," he said. "Colleagues don't audit each other's pockets."

She was quiet for a moment. Then: "I gave the storm my teacher's face."

He looked up despite himself.

"Bramwell," she said. "The man who raised me. I traded his face so we wouldn't die in the ash-storm last week. So." She shrugged, a small, hard motion. "Audit mine first if you want. Mine's worse."

"It's not a contest, Dravenmoor."

"No," she agreed. "It's worse than a contest. It's a tax. And we're both paying it." She pulled her cloak tighter. "So stop pretending you gave the ferryman nothing. Because I know what nothing costs, and it isn't a crossing."

* * *

He didn't tell her. That was the decision he made that night, sitting across from a girl who'd given away her father-figure's face and called it fair trade.

He told the night instead.

Later, when her breathing had gone long and even again, he walked to the edge of camp where the deadlands opened out flat and starless and endless, and he stood with his hands in his armpits and spoke to the dark like a man confessing in an empty chapel.

"Her name was Sera," he said. His breath hung white in the cold. "She liked the cold mornings best, I think—I think she liked them. I can't remember if she liked anything anymore, that's the thing. I know facts. I know she existed. I know what was done to her. But I gave away the parts that made her a person instead of a report."

The dark didn't answer. Out here nothing answered. That was the whole problem with the deadlands—no stars, no witnesses, just miles of ground where magic had been wrung out like blood from a rag.

"She'd have hated this," he went on. "Hated all of it. The mud, the walking, the—" he gestured vaguely at the sky, at the absence overhead. "She'd have found a way to make me laugh about it, though. She was good at that. Making bad things smaller by laughing at them first."

His throat tightened around nothing. There was nothing left to tighten around. That was the trick of spent memories—you grieved the loss of the grief itself. You stood at an empty grave knowing you'd buried someone in it but unable to remember who they'd been, only that the digging had hurt.

"And then today," he said, and laughed, a short ugly sound. "Today I catch myself doing it again. Standing watch over *her*. The seed. Over a sleeping girl and a lump of old magic wrapped in wool, standing guard like it matters, like I matter to it."

He crouched, pressed his palm flat against the dead earth.

"But here's the thing, Sera. Here's the thing I figured out while she was asleep and I was being pathetic by the fire." He exhaled. "Nobody ever needed me before. Not really. The guild needed my hands. Marks needed my absence. You—" his voice cracked, and he hated it, and pushed through. "You needed me to be warm, maybe, when you were little. Before. And I couldn't even manage the one job that came after."

He stood.

"This thing needs me. Or it will. It's a seed, and seeds need carrying, and feeding, and guarding, and someone willing to walk it through hell to get where it's going. And that stupid sleeping girl is going to wear herself down to nothing paying its tolls, trading pieces of herself mile by mile until there's nothing left of Mara Dravenmoor but duty and a name. Somebody has to be the one who watches out for *her*, so she doesn't spend herself down to nothing. And nobody else is applying for the job."

He wiped his face roughly with his sleeve, angry at the wetness there, at the way grief for a person he couldn't remember still found new doors to walk through.

"So that's it. That's my confession. I lost you twice—once to the guild, once to a rusty pan on a dead river—and I'm done losing things I chose to keep. This seed gets where it's going. She gets where she's going. Both of them. Whatever it costs."

The stars stayed gone. They'd been gone so long he'd almost stopped noticing, the way you stop noticing a wound that's become the shape of your life.

Almost.

* * *

Dawn came grey and sour, the way it always did out here. Wren had the fire rebuilt and a pot of barley going before Mara stirred, and when she woke she checked the seed first—always the seed first—and then looked at him with that assessing stare he was starting to find less annoying and more familiar.

"You were talking last night," she said. "Out past the camp line. To yourself?"

"To the night."

"Did it answer?"

"Night never answers," he said. "That's why you can tell it anything."

Something moved behind her eyes—not suspicion exactly. Recognition, maybe. She knew about talking to absences. She carried one around in a cloth bundle and another one behind her ribs where her valley used to be.

They ate. They walked. The deadlands stretched ahead, featureless and hungry, and somewhere beyond the horizon a ruined capital full of walking ghosts waited for them with the patience of the grave.

Around midday, Wren paused on a ridge of slag and glass and looked back the way they'd come—a habit, checking for pursuit—and saw, far off across the flats, a thin line of riders moving parallel to their trail. Grey. Disciplined. Keeping their distance like wolves deciding whether the herd was worth the trouble.

"Mara," he said quietly. "Company."

She came up beside him, shaded her eyes, and went very still.

"Inquisitors?"

"Or something wearing their coat." He counted. "Nine. Maybe ten. Moving smart—they're not tracking us, they're herding us. See the angle? They're pushing us east. Toward the low country."

Toward the ruins. Which meant either Vane had guessed their destination and meant to let them lead her to it—or something worse was driving grey riders through the deadlands, something that wanted them exactly where they were going.

Mara's hand drifted to the bundle at her chest, and Wren saw it settle there like a heartbeat.

"Then we don't give them the easy road," she said. "We cut north."

"North's the Glass Waste."

"Then we'll want extra water."

He looked at her—at the set of her jaw, the exhaustion underneath it, the sheer unreasonable stubbornness of her—and felt the thing he'd confessed to the night settle into place in his chest like a bone setting straight. Fine, then. If she was determined to spend herself piece by piece, somebody had to stand between her and the tiller.

"All right," he said. "North. But I'm carrying the water."

They turned off the trail together, and behind them, far off, the grey line paused—then turned to follow.
