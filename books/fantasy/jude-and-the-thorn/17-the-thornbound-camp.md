## 17. The Thornbound Camp

The rope around Mara's wrists was woven from something that hummed.

She'd felt it the moment they cinched it—low, patient, like a second pulse sitting just under her own. Ward-rope, she thought. Or its opposite. Rope spun to hold magic in, or to hold a person who was made of it. She couldn't tell anymore which one she was.

Wren stumbled ahead of her on the same line, his wrists bound, his mouth—mercifully—for once shut. They'd been walking since dawn, prodded along by six figures in ragged grey-green cloaks who hadn't spoken a single word. Not to each other, not to their prisoners. Their silence had a practiced quality, like a vow worn smooth by years.

The deadlands had changed as they walked east. Where the wastes near Thornvale had been flat and ash-grey, here the ground buckled into ridges of black glass, and things grew out of it—pale, rootless shrubs that drank no water and bloomed without flowers. The seed against her sternum, tucked beneath her shirt where she wore it like a coal that never cooled, had been warm all morning. Warmer than usual.

It liked this place.

*Don't*, Mara told it silently. *Whatever you're doing, stop.*

The seed didn't answer. It never answered. But the warmth spread anyway, down through her ribs, out along her arms, and one of the silent cloaked figures turned and looked at her—not at her face, but at her chest—and made a sound for the first time all day. A low note, half word, half prayer.

*"Hers,"* the figure said.

They crested a ridge of glass, and the camp opened below them.

Mara had imagined a war camp: tents in rows, weapon racks, watchfires, hard faces. What she saw instead stopped her breath.

It was a village. A real one, built from salvaged timber and wagon-beds and stitched sailcloth, terraced down the inside of a vast crater like a town built into an amphitheater. Smoke rose from cooking fires. Children ran between the structures—actual children, shrieking, chasing a dog made of sticks and rope. Laundry hung on lines strung between broken siege engines repurposed as posts. Someone was playing a fiddle somewhere, badly, with joy.

There were hundreds of them. Maybe a thousand. Thornbound, the Inquisitors called them—heretics, exiles, the purged bloodlines. Mara had pictured fanatics in bone masks. She saw grandmothers mending nets. She saw a smith's forge running cold, its owner asleep beside it with a book open on his chest.

"They look like us," Wren said quietly behind her. "That's the trick of it. Everything that wants your soul looks like home first."

"Shut up," said one of their captors, without heat, and pushed him forward.

* * *

They took her not to a jail or a tent but to the center of the crater, where a tree grew out of black glass.

Not a tree—a *thorn*. One branch, enormous, leafless, rising forty feet from a wound in the earth, its bark dark as old iron and shot through with veins that faintly glowed when Mara came close. The seed flared hot enough that she gasped. Around the thorn's base, the ground was worn smooth by knees. Thousands of pilgrims, over years, had knelt here until the glass went soft as clay.

A woman waited for her there.

She was older than Mara expected—not ancient, but weathered the way river stone is weathered, all sharp edges rounded off by long pressure. Silver hair braided with strips of red cloth. Her eyes were pale, almost colorless, and they fixed on Mara with an intensity that made the wardrope feel like a kindness.

"Ilse," Wren breathed beside her. "That's the priestess. That's *her*."

"You know her face?" Mara whispered.

"Everyone in the deadlands knows her face. They burn it onto coins." He swallowed. "Coins people hide from the Crown."

Ilse raised one hand, and the crowd of silent escorts fell back. Then, gently—gently, like untying a bird—she loosened the rope from Mara's wrists herself.

"You're bleeding," Ilse said. "From the rope."

"It holds magic."

"It holds *fear*." She set the rope aside as if it were something dead. "We don't use it on our own."

"I'm not your own."

"No." Ilse smiled, and it was a terrible smile, because it was kind. "You're better. You're the heir."

The word went through the gathered crowd like wind through wheat. People had drifted close—dozens now, drawn from the terraces, from the cookfires, children hoisted onto shoulders to see. They weren't looking at Mara with hatred or hunger. They were looking at her the way the people of Thornvale used to look at the stars.

With longing. With relief.

"She doesn't know," someone murmured. "Look at her face. Nobody told her anything."

"The guild kept her blind," said another voice, bitter. "Like they keep everything."

"Hush," Ilse said, and the crowd hushed. She turned back to Mara. "You've come a long way carrying something heavy. Will you sit? Will you eat? We are not your enemy, Warden's child, whatever lies you've been fed. And I think—" her pale eyes flicked to Mara's chest, to the shape of the seed beneath the cloth, "—I think you already know that the ones who raised you were not your friends."

Mara thought of Bramwell dying. Of the archive, and the cage, and the ledger that listed her bloodline like inventory. She said nothing, which was answer enough.

Ilse nodded slowly. "Sit," she said again. "Eat. And then let me show you what actually happened. Not the guild's version. Not the Crown's version. The war itself. You carry its last living piece—you deserve to know what it lived through."

"And then?" Mara asked. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "After you show me?"

"Then," Ilse said, "you decide. Freely. That is all we have ever asked of anyone—that anyone be allowed to decide *freely*. Your guild decided for the world for a thousand years. We only want the choice given back." She extended her hand. "Will you see?"

Against every instinct Bramwell had drilled into her—the ward-songs, the ninth verse he'd refused to teach, *patience, girl, patience above all*—Mara found herself reaching out.

Because she wanted to know. Because she was so tired of half-truths. Because a thousand people were looking at her like she mattered, and she could not remember the last time anyone in Thornvale had looked at her at all.

She took the priestess's hand.

* * *

The visions did not come like dreams. They came like being *remembered*.

One moment Mara stood in the crater among the Thornbound; the next, she was standing in a field under a sky full of stars—real stars, dense and blazing, more than she had ever known existed—and the field was burning.

She knew, somehow, that these were true memories. Not stories. The Thorn's memory, poured through the seed and into her, and Ilse guiding it, shaping it, choosing what she saw.

She saw the war from above, the way the Thorn must have seen it: two nations tangled together like fighting snakes, and the Thorn moving among them, vast, luminous, *listening*. It heard oaths and broke none of them. It heard lies and named them. It was a judge, she realized—the thing the chapel murals had shown her, the fair arbiter of the old world—and for a while, a generation maybe, it had worked. Disputes settled. Blood spilled in smaller quantities. Kings forced to say why, out loud, before a witness that could not be bribed.

Then the vision shifted, and she watched it fail.

She watched a border lord feed his whole village false grievances, hundreds at once, a flood of manufactured injustice meant to drown the Thorn's attention while his real army crossed the river elsewhere. She watched the Thorn, overwhelmed, render judgment too fast—condemning innocents in its haste to find the truth. She watched those innocents' families take up arms not against the lord but against the *judge*.

She watched the other side respond in kind. She watched a coalition of realms decide that no arbiter should have power over kings, and build weapons for that single purpose. She watched them burn the Thorn's groves—groves, it had *had groves*, living bodies of it scattered across the world like courthouses, each one slaughtered. She watched refugees from the grove-lands flee into cities that closed their gates. She watched famine engineered as strategy, both sides, deliberately, fields salted so the enemy's children would starve into surrender.

And she watched—at the end, in the last terrible years—watched the Thorn do the thing that got it sentenced. She saw it stop judging and start *punishing*. She saw a city that had butchered a grove-refuge column wake to find every oath-taker in it struck mute, every liar's tongue marked with black, and some—she couldn't count how many, the Thorn itself seemed unable to count how many—dead. Not murdered, exactly. *Judged past bearing.* The line between sentence and slaughter had worn away like a path under feet.

Both sides, Ilse had promised. Both sides, delivered. The Thorn's crimes were real. So was everyone else's.

Mara came back to herself on her hands and knees in the glass-crater, weeping without knowing when she'd started, the seed roaring against her sternum like a trapped heart.

"There," Ilse said softly, kneeling beside her. Not touching her. "Now you know. Now you *carry* it, the way we do. Every Thornbound born carries a piece of that grief. It isn't comfortable. It isn't supposed to be." She crouched, brought her colorless eyes level with Mara's. "Your guild looked at that grief and built a cage around it and told the world the war was over. We looked at it and swore it would be *answered*. Tell me, Warden's child—which of us lied?"

* * *

They fed her after. Stew, actual meat in it, bread that wasn't deadlands-hard. Wren ate like a man making up for a decade, and Mara noticed no one had bound either of them again. There were guards, certainly. There were always guards. But the rope stayed coiled and unused, and that restraint was its own kind of argument.

Children had gathered nearby, pretending not to stare. One of them, a girl of seven or eight with red cloth braided into her hair like Ilse's, finally worked up the courage.

"Are you going to wake it?" the girl asked.

Mara looked at her—at the earnest, unguarded face, the question asked the way children ask whether it will snow—and felt something in her chest turn over.

"I don't know yet," Mara said honestly.

"My mama says when it wakes, everyone who was ever wronged gets their day. Everyone. Even the dead." The girl's voice dropped to a confiding whisper. "My brother died before I was born. In a purge. Mama says I'll get to meet him."

And there it was. Mara understood, sitting there with her bowl going cold, exactly what Ilse had built. Not an army—an *inheritance of grief*. A thousand people whose pain had nowhere to go except toward a single hope, tended for generations like a fire that must never go out. How could any of them ever accept less than everything? How could they be told *no*, after waiting lifetimes, after naming children for a dawn they'd never see?

It was beautiful. It was monstrous. It was both, and it was working on her, and she knew it was working on her, and it was still working.

Wren leaned over, quiet. "You've got the look."

"What look?"

"The look of someone about to join something." He tore his bread. "I've seen it. Recruitment's the same everywhere, whether it's gods or gangs. They show you the worst thing anyone ever did to you, then hand you a family. Works nine times out of ten." He chewed. "Don't be the ten."

"They showed me the truth, Wren."

"Yeah. The truth's the best bait there is. Costs 'em nothing. Everybody's got some." He nodded toward the great thorn tree. "Question's never whether it's true. Question's what they want you to *do* about it."

* * *

Ilse came to her at dusk, alone, dismissing even her guards.

The priestess sat across the small fire without invitation, wrapped in a grey-green cloak identical to a hundred others, and for a while she simply watched the flames.

"You're resisting," Ilse said at last. "Good. I'd worry if you weren't. Only fools and zealots surrender quickly, and I have plenty of both already."

"Why show me?" Mara asked. "If you need the seed awake, you could take it. You have a thousand people and I have one thief."

"If we took it, we would be the Crown with different banners." Ilse's mouth twisted. "The seed answers bloodline, not force. It chose *you*. Which means the reckoning, when it comes, must come through you—or not at all. I have waited thirty years, Mara Dravenmoor. I can wait a few days more for you to choose rightly."

"And if I choose wrongly?"

"Then you'll walk out of this camp tomorrow, free, and I will grieve, and my people will follow you anyway, and eventually the Crown will catch you and burn the seed, and the last chance this world has at a true accounting dies in an Inquisitor's fire." She said it without drama, like a woman reading a weather report. "So I would prefer you chose rightly."

Mara stared into the fire. The seed pulsed slow beneath her ribs, and she couldn't tell anymore whether it was agitated or *comforted*. That frightened her most of all. This camp, this cause, this woman—something in the seed recognized them. Something in it was *home*.

"You saw what it did," Mara said quietly. "At the end. The city it punished. The people it killed without trial."

"I saw."

"It did to them exactly what was done to it afterward. Judgment without hearing." She looked up. "How can you worship that?"

Ilse was silent a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had lost all its polish.

"I don't worship it. My people call me high priestess because they need one, and I was foolish enough to be right often enough to be promoted." She drew the red cloth of her braid through her fingers. "I want it *awake*, child. Awake and standing in front of the world, answering for all of it—every mute city, every marked tongue, every death. Let it defend itself if it can. Let it apologize if it can't. But it will *speak*. No more silence. No more cages. Silence is what the guild gave us, and silence is what the Crown offers now, and I have buried two generations who died in that silence waiting for a voice."

She leaned forward, and the firelight caught her colorless eyes and made them burn.

"You want justice for it. I can see that already—it's written all over you, the same sickness I have. Good. Then hear me, and think on it tonight." She rose, cloak settling around her. "Wake it *now*, with us, surrounded by people who will shield it with their lives while it finds its strength. Or carry it on alone to a capital full of ghosts and Crown steel, and pray you're strong enough to do whatever you're planning without an army, without a people, without anyone at all."

She paused at the edge of the firelight.

"One of those paths ends with it speaking. The other ends with it burning. Choose, Warden. But choose soon—the Crown is closer than you think, and my people will not wait forever."

She left Mara alone with the fire, with the sleeping camp, with the seed beating against her sternum like a second heart that had already chosen.

And far off, at the rim of the crater, a watcher signal-fire kindled—one short flame, then another—relay-code racing westward through the dark.

Someone was coming.
