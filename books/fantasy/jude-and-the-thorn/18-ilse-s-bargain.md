## 18. Ilse's Bargain

They gave her a tent of her own.

That was the first thing that should have frightened her. In the Thornbound camp there were perhaps two hundred people, packed into a hollow between deadlands ridges where the wind couldn't strip the fires bare, and every one of them slept three to a canvas lean-to, children layered like cordwood against the cold. But Mara had a tent with a real floor, a wool blanket that smelled of cedar oil, and a clay cup left beside her bedroll each morning, filled with something warm and honeyed that she never saw anyone bring.

The second thing was how they looked at her. Not with curiosity. With hunger held politely in check, the way starving men look at bread they've agreed to share.

"You slept," Ilse said, when Mara emerged into grey morning light. The high priestess sat by the central fire with her hands open on her knees, palms up, as if permanently receiving something. Her hair was white though her face wasn't old, and she wore it braided with strips of red cloth that Mara now understood were dyed in blood—not symbolic blood, actual blood, drawn from each priestess's own palm on the day of her ordination. Everything about these people was literal. That was part of what made them dangerous.

"Barely."

"Grief sleeps badly." Ilse smiled. "It will sleep better when you understand where you are."

Mara wrapped both hands around the offered cup and drank. It was good. That was the third thing. Nothing here was allowed to be unpleasant. The food was rich, the songs around the night fires were beautiful, the children were clean and laughing. A camp of exiles in the middle of a starless waste should have smelled of ash and desperation. Instead it smelled of baking bread.

She had spent two days here, and she had not yet been permitted to leave.

* * *

Ilse walked her through the camp after breakfast, and Mara let her, because watching was still the safest thing she knew how to do. Wren was somewhere—she'd caught one glimpse of him yesterday, being fed by a woman who wouldn't stop touching his hair, and he'd worn the expression of a boy trying very hard to remember that kindness could be a trap. She hadn't seen him since. That worried her more than she let show.

"This is the nursery," Ilse said, lifting a flap. Inside, six infants slept in a row, each cradle carved with spirals—the Thorn's spiral, the same mark that had bloomed under Mara's skin when the seed had first taken hold of her blood. "Every child born Thornbound is shown to the seed's memory. We speak its name over them."

"It has a name?"

"Many. You know one of them." Ilse turned to her, and her eyes did the thing that unsettled Mara most: they went soft and certain all at once, like a door closing gently. "You call it the Thorn because the guild taught you to. We call it the Judge. The Fair One. The Hand That Weighed. Before your Warden's Guild put it in the ground, it settled disputes for half the world. A farmer and his neighbor, a kingdom and its rival—it heard them all. It never lied. It never took bribes. It never grew tired or old or cruel." Her voice dropped into something like tenderness. "And then the powerful discovered that a judge who cannot be bribed is more dangerous than any army."

"The tribunal—" Mara started.

"Was a massacre wearing robes." Ilse's voice didn't rise at all. That was the terrible thing about her; nothing about her ever rose. "You saw the visions. You know what I showed you was true."

Mara did know. Two nights ago, in the smoke of Ilse's censer, she had stood inside the memory of the war's ending: the guild wardens singing the sealing verse while the Thorn screamed without a mouth, the Thornbound faithful burned in their chapel for refusing to renounce it, the Crown soldiers salting the fields of anyone who had ever benefited from the Judge's rulings—which was everyone, which meant anyone. Both sides' atrocities, laid end to end like corpses in a ditch. She had woken weeping and hadn't fully stopped being angry since.

"Yes," she said carefully. "It was true."

"And yet you hesitate." Ilse studied her with something close to fondness. "Good. Hesitation means you're actually thinking. The ones who don't hesitate are the ones who frighten me." She gestured toward the ridge at the camp's far edge, where the ground rose into a shelf of black rock. "Walk with me. There's something I want you to see before I ask you the question I brought you here to ask."

* * *

What Mara saw, from the top of the ridge, made her breath catch.

Below the camp, in a natural bowl of stone, hundreds of people knelt in perfect silence—more people than lived in the camp above. They knelt facing inward, toward a flat altar-stone, and on the altar lay a single object: a shard of black glass the size of a hand, veined faintly with green light that pulsed slow as breathing.

"That's not possible," Mara whispered. "The stars are out. Magic is dead out here. I've felt nothing but the seed since Thornvale."

"Magic isn't dead." Ilse stood beside her, hands folded, serene. "Magic is *muzzled*. There's a difference, and the difference has a name, and the name is the wards—or rather, the absence of them. When your guild fell, it didn't release magic into the world, girl. It released the world from magic's prison. What you feel as deadness is the silence after a throat is cut." She nodded at the bowl below. "Those are pilgrims. They've been arriving for weeks, from every direction, walking across the wastes on faith alone. Because they felt the wards fall too. All of them. Everywhere. Do you understand what that means? Every cage in the world opened the same night. And every prisoner inside them felt the doors swing wide."

The green pulse in the shard slowed, quickened, slowed again. Mara's palm ached in answer, the seed beneath her skin turning over in its sleep like a dog hearing a familiar whistle.

"That shard is a fragment," Ilse said. "One of seven we've recovered. It remembers the Judge's voice. Through it, we can hear whispers. Guidance. Sometimes warnings." She turned to face Mara fully, and now the softness was gone from her eyes, replaced by an intensity so total it was almost restful to look at—a person who had never once doubted anything. "And sometimes, it speaks of *her*. The heir. The one whose blood the seed chose. Do you know what my people called you, before you ever reached us? They called you the Answer. Three generations of Thornbound have died in exile praying that someone like you would exist. And here you are, standing on our ridge, carrying everything we've bled for, looking at me like I'm the dangerous one."

"Aren't you?" Mara asked quietly.

Something flickered across Ilse's face—amusement, maybe, or respect. "Come down," she said. "Kneel among them. Hear the shard yourself. Then decide who's dangerous."

* * *

Mara knelt.

She told herself it was reconnaissance. She told herself that a Warden gathered information, and Bramwell had taught her that you learned more kneeling among people than standing apart from them. These were lies of a sort, but they were useful lies, and besides—the pull of the shard was genuine, a low tidal drag in her blood, and she wanted, with an honesty she couldn't deny, to hear it.

The pilgrims sang. Low, wordless harmonies, four notes cycling like waves. The sound went through her chest and found the seed and the seed *hummed back*, and for one vertiginous moment Mara felt herself held by something vast and grieving and patient, something that had waited centuries in the dark and was so, so glad to be remembered—

Then the shard flared green, and a voice came through it, and the voice was her mother's.

"*Little star,*" it said. "*Come home.*"

Mara's whole body went rigid. Her mother had died when she was six. She had one memory of her—hands kneading dough, flour to the wrists, humming—and the seed had already used that voice once on the deadlands road, bargaining guidance for memories, and Mara had sworn never again. But hearing it now, broadcast through a shard of the thing sleeping under an orchard ten days behind her, in front of two hundred kneeling strangers—that was different. That was a violation performed as a gift.

The song faltered. Pilgrims turned to look at her. Ilse raised one hand and the harmony resumed, swallowing the moment.

"She hears you," Ilse murmured, kneeling beside Mara, her voice pitched for her alone. "Even here. Even asleep. You are the closest thing to waking it has known in a hundred years, and it reaches for you the way a drowning man reaches for a rope. Can you imagine, Mara Dravenmoor, what it will reach for when it's *awake*?" She let the question hang. Then, gently: "Come. Walk with me. It's time I asked you properly."

* * *

Ilse asked her in her own tent, over tea, with the red-braided white hair loose down her back and her hands folded in her lap like a woman settling in for the most important conversation of her life. Perhaps it was.

"I'm going to offer you everything," Ilse said. "I want you to hear all of it before you refuse, because I know you're going to refuse, and I want your refusal to cost you something. That's not cruelty. That's respect."

Mara set down her cup. "Say it anyway."

"A place." Ilse spread one hand. "This camp, this people, this life. You have been invisible your entire existence—you told me that yourself, the second night, after the visions, when you were too wrung out to lie. The last apprentice of a dying guild, tolerated, overlooked, given duties instead of love. Here you would never be invisible again. You'd walk through this camp and every eye would soften."

"A family." The other hand. "Two hundred people who would die for you and mean it. Children who would grow up calling you aunt. Elders who would argue theology with you at the fire until dawn because your opinion mattered. You've never had siblings. You could have forty."

"And purpose," Mara said flatly.

"Purpose." Ilse leaned forward. "Not the guild's purpose—patience, endurance, silence, all the virtues of people keeping a secret they're ashamed of. Real purpose. Wake the Thorn, Mara. Wake it *now*. Bring it into the world with its faithful around it, with its heir at its side, with priests who remember the old rites and can teach it to be what it was before the war broke it. A fair judge for a lawless age. The Crown's Inquisitors are burning hedge-witches in three provinces—the guards told me, they talk in front of me because they think I'm just a refugee grandmother. The world is sliding back into the dark your guild pretended to prevent. It needs the Judge. And the Judge needs *you*, because without its heir it wakes blind and furious, and blind and furious is exactly the monster the old stories warn about. With you? With you it might wake wise." Her voice went quiet and fierce. "You could be the reason it doesn't become the thing everyone fears. Isn't that worth more than running to a ruined capital to dig up dead tribunals?"

For a moment—one long, treacherous moment—Mara let herself feel it.

That was the trap's genius, she understood later. Not the lies. The truths. Everything Ilse offered was real: the belonging, the warmth, the children, the firelight, the sense of mattering. Mara had been invisible for twenty years. She had watched her guild dwindle from thirty wardens to four, had eaten dinner in a hall built for hundreds, had learned every duty and no affection. And here was a place where strangers wept at the sight of her, where her bloodline made her holy, where the loneliest fact of her life could be rewritten overnight into its opposite.

All she had to do was wake the thing.

"I have a question," Mara said.

"Ask."

"When you showed me the visions—the guild burning the faithful, the Crown salting the fields—I believed you. I still believe you. Both sides did monstrous things, and my guild lied to me my whole life, and I'm angrier about it than you'll ever know." She met Ilse's eyes. "So tell me true. If I say no. If I stand up right now and walk out of this camp with the seed and Wren and the ledger and keep walking to the capital. What happens?"

Ilse didn't hesitate. That was somehow worse than hesitation would have been.

"Then my people take it from you," she said. "Some will die doing it. Possibly many. Possibly you. I would grieve every death, Mara, sincerely, and I would order them anyway, and I would sleep afterward, because I have done arithmetic like that my whole life and my dreams are quiet. That's what conviction costs. I pay it gladly. Do you see? I'm not offering you a lie. I'm offering you the truth, and the truth is that everyone in this story believes they're the healer. Your guild believed it. Vane believes it. I believe it. The only question that has ever mattered is: whose belief survives contact with the others?" She smiled, warm as the tea. "Now. My offer stands. Place, family, purpose. Say yes, and tonight you'll sing with us, and tomorrow we begin teaching you the rites, and within the year you will help deliver the fairest mind in creation back into a world that has forgotten what fairness looks like."

Mara looked at her for a long time.

She thought about Bramwell, dying, whispering *it remembers her name*—and how even at the end, drowning in betrayal, he had pressed the ledger into her hands and said *don't let either side have it*. She thought about the ghost-tribunal waiting under the ruined capital, the trial that had never happened, the sentence passed in fear. She thought about the seed in her palm, patient and strange, feeding on her memories, speaking in her dead mother's voice, wanting—what? She didn't know. That was the whole point. Nobody knew what the Thorn wanted, and every faction in the world had decided the answer in advance and built their armies on it.

Waking it now, surrounded by worshippers, handed its verdicts before it could render any—that wasn't justice. That was just another cage, gilded. Another tribunal with the outcome written first. Ilse loved the Thorn the way the guild had feared it: completely, and without ever once asking it a question.

"No," Mara said.

She said it quietly, and it landed louder than a shout. Outside the tent, the camp's noise seemed to dim, as if the whole hollow had leaned in.

Ilse closed her eyes. When she opened them, the warmth was still there, and beneath it, visible now for the first time, was the steel. "I hoped I was wrong about you," she said. "I find I'm rarely wrong. Will you at least stay for the evening meal? Refusal shouldn't mean rudeness. Let me feed you before we're enemies."

"That depends," Mara said, "on whether Wren eats with us. I haven't seen him since yesterday."

"He's being well cared for." A beat, smooth as poured honey. "As you were."

And there it was. The fourth thing, the thing Mara had been cataloguing since she arrived, finally spoken aloud: nobody had locked any gates. Nobody had posted guards. They hadn't needed to. The hospitality had been the walls.

"I think," Mara said, standing, "we'll eat on the road."

* * *

Getting out was easier than it should have been, which told Mara everything about how Ilse expected her to run.

She found Wren at the camp's edge, sitting on a supply crate with a plate of stew in his lap and a young Thornbound woman explaining something earnestly at him, and the look on his face when he saw Mara was pure relief dressed up as irritation. "About time," he said, dumping the stew. "These people are *nice*, Mara. It's horrifying. One more hour and I'd have started believing in their cause out of sheer politeness."

"We're leaving."

"Yeah, I figured. The guy guarding the south path asked me this morning if I thought you'd see reason. Very casual. Extremely threatening." He fell into step beside her, matching her pace without being told to hurry. "Which way?"

"South. The way we came is watched. Ilse will assume I double back toward the river ferry." Mara kept her eyes moving, mapping: cookfires, tents, the kneeling-bowl, the ridgeline. "We go east instead, over the rocks, then cut south at the broken tower. Two hard days puts us past their outriders."

"You've been planning this since the visions, haven't you."

"Since the tea got good," Mara said. "Nobody feeds refugees this well unless they want something expensive."

They were fifty yards from the ridgeline when the singing started.

It rose from the kneeling-bowl behind them—not alarm, not pursuit. The same low four-note harmony, but faster now, denser, dozens of voices braided into one, and Mara felt it hit the seed like a fist striking a bell. Pain lanced up her arm. She stumbled, caught herself on the rock, and the seed *opened*, and the voice that poured out of it was her mother's again, and this time it wasn't tender:

"*Stay.*"

"Mara." Wren grabbed her elbow. "Mara, your eyes—"

"They're pulling at it." She clenched her fist against her sternum, breath sawing. "The shard—the singing—they're trying to wake it *through me*, right now, here, without my consent—" And she understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that this had been the plan all along. Not the bargain. The bargain was for the version of her that said yes. For the version that said no, there was the song, and two hundred voices, and a fragment that remembered how to call the seed like a mother calls a child home.

She did the only thing she could think of. She did the thing the seed itself had taught her on the deadlands road, the ugly arithmetic of survival.

She opened the channel first—on her terms—and gave it something.

Not a skill. Not a name. She reached for a memory deliberately, the way you'd reach for a coin in the dark, and picked the one that hurt least and mattered most, the small bright coin she'd been saving without knowing why: her ninth birthday. Bramwell letting her light the orchard lanterns early. Cake with too much frosting. The whole guild, all eleven of them remaining, singing badly on purpose to make her laugh. The last night, she realized even as it peeled away, the last night the guild had ever been happy, and she hadn't known it at the time, because you never do—

The memory went out of her like heat off a stove. She knew it had existed the way you know a room exists behind a locked door: she could see its shape, its weight in the ledger of her life, *ninth birthday, orchard lanterns, cake*—but the door was shut and would never open, and the warmth was gone, and the space where it had been was smooth and cold and hers no longer.

The seed went quiet. The distant harmony lost its grip, unraveled, faded into ordinary evening wind.

Mara stood on the rocky slope with her hand pressed to her mouth, shaking.

"What did it cost?" Wren asked softly. He knew. He'd paid the ferryman. He knew exactly what the ledger looked like.

"My ninth birthday." Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "The last night my guild was ever... it doesn't matter. It's gone. I kept the fact of it. That's what I get to keep, apparently. Facts."

"Mara—"

"Don't." She lowered her hand. Above the eastern ridge, the sky was the color of a bruise healing, starless, enormous, indifferent. Somewhere south of them lay a ruined capital full of ghosts who remembered more about justice than any living soul. Somewhere behind them, Ilse was already giving orders, calm and grief-stricken and utterly certain, and her riders would come at dawn if they came at all. "Don't be sorry for me. Be sorry for her—Ilse. She gave me everything she had and it was all the wrong things, and she'll never understand why that's sad instead of insulting." She started climbing. "Move, Wren. Dawn's in six hours, and I'd rather spend them putting distance between us and the only people in the world who think I'm precious."

He scrambled up beside her. After a while he said, "Where exactly are we going? Concretely. As in, do you actually know?"

Mara looked south, toward the horizon where the capital waited under its shroud of extinguished stars, and felt the seed shift against her heart—not reaching anymore, not singing. Waiting.

"The palace crypts," she said. "There's a truth buried under that city that neither the Crown nor the Thornbound wants found. Which means it's the only thing left worth digging for."

Behind them, faint and final, the camp's fires blinked out one by one—as if the valley itself had closed its eyes to watch which way they'd run.
