## 19. The City That Waited

Mara's POV

They smelled the city before they saw it.

Not rot—Wren had warned her about rot, and there wasn't any. The deadlands didn't rot things anymore; whatever had emptied the sky had also emptied the world of its patience for decay. What she smelled instead was dust and cold iron and something faintly sweet underneath it all, like apples left too long in a cellar, and when the road crested a low ridge of slag and broken glass, she understood why.

The capital filled the valley ahead of them like a held breath.

It had been beautiful once. She could see that the way you could see the shape of a face in a skull—the bones were still there, the great avenues radiating from a central hill, the aqueducts striding across the low ground on legs of pale stone. But every surface wore the same grey patina, as if the whole city had been dipped in ash-water and left to dry. No birds. No smoke. No wind moving through it, though wind moved everywhere else on the plain.

And above it—nothing. The starless sky came down to meet the rooftops like a lid.

"That's it," Wren said quietly. "Corvale. Or what's left of it." He'd stopped calling it *the capital* two days ago, after the third ruined waystation whose signs still read CORVALE — 40 LEAGUES in faded gold. "People used to say you could smell the mint gardens from the eastern gate. My gran talked about it like it was made of sugar."

"What happened here?" Mara asked, though she already suspected. The seed had been warm against her sternum since dawn, warm the way a hand is warm, and it warmed further now, a slow pulse like something leaning forward inside her chest.

"Nobody knows," Wren said. "That's the thing. One day the stars over Corvale went out. Just over Corvale. Then the people started—" He stopped. Rubbed his mouth. "You'll see."

* * *

They saw at the eastern gate.

A woman stood in the gateway arch, hands raised, mouth open in a scream that made no sound. She was dressed in the style of forty years past—a courier's leathers, a satchel slung across her body—and she was translucent at the edges, thin as breath on glass. As they watched, frozen on the road, she ran the same three steps toward the gate, turned as if something behind her had roared, raised her hands again, and screamed her soundless scream.

Then she did it again.

"Loops," Wren said. "The dead walk their last moments. Over and over. Gran said the priests argued for years about whether their souls were still in there or whether it was just—echoes. Imprints. Wax recordings of dying." He swallowed. "She said either answer was worse."

Mara stepped closer before she could stop herself.

Up close, the courier was worse and better than distance suggested. Worse because her eyes were open and fixed on something forty years gone; better because there was no pain in the loop itself. Whatever terror had lived in that scream had been spent long ago. What remained was repetition, pure and clean as a bell struck forever.

The seed pulsed against Mara's ribs.

*She ran the east road,* it seemed to say, in no voice at all, just pressure and knowing. *She carried word of the first falling. No one received it.*

Mara staggered back. Wren caught her elbow.

"You went white," he said. "What happened?"

"It told me about her." Her voice came out strange. Flat. "The seed. It knew who she was. What she was carrying."

"The seed talks to you now?"

"Not talks." How could she explain it? *Pressure and knowing.* "It remembers. Everything this city forgot, it kept." She looked up at the gate, at the endless small tragedy playing out in its arch. "I think—I think that might be what it *is*, Wren. The part of the war that wouldn't stop remembering."

He looked at her for a long moment. In the grey light his face had sharpened over the weeks of road, cheekbones standing out, but his eyes were still the same quick brown ones that had been going through her pack by firelight back in chapter one of their acquaintance. "Right," he said finally. "Well. If we're walking into a city full of ghost-echoes with a talking seed strapped to your chest, I want it noted that I'm only being paid half enough."

"You're not being paid anything."

"And yet somehow still underpaid." He hoisted his pack. "Come on. Palace hill's dead center. If your Thorn has an origin, that's where it'll be buried."

* * *

Mara's POV

The avenues were rivers of the looping dead.

Not all at once—at first they picked their way down the great central boulevard between scattered figures, a baker kneeling forever before a spilled basket, two soldiers locked mid-duel, blades meeting at the same angle each time they passed and passed again. But the closer they drew to the palace hill, the thicker the echoes stood, until Mara was moving through a crowd of the dead, shoulder to shoulder with people who had died forty years ago and did not know it.

None of them touched her. None of them saw her. She walked through the middle of their final moments like a ghost among ghosts, and the horror of it was not the screaming—there was surprisingly little screaming—but the ordinariness. A mother braiding her daughter's hair, hands moving through the same motions, the girl's laugh caught halfway out of her throat. A man watering a window box that would never grow. A clerk locking an office door, checking it, checking it again, checking it again.

"They didn't run," Mara whispered. She hadn't meant to say it aloud. "When the stars fell. They just—kept living. Until they couldn't."

"People do that," Wren said. His voice was rougher than usual. "My gran did that. Kept making bread right up until the guild took her. Said the day you stop kneading is the day you're dead." He nodded at the clerk, endlessly locking his door. "Maybe he's not trapped. Maybe that's just where he decided to be."

The seed burned steady against her sternum now, a constant low warmth, and with every step into the crowd it fed her more—not memories exactly, more like captions under paintings. *The mint gardens burned on the ninth night. The aqueducts were cut by our own side, to slow the Thornbound advance. The children were moved to the crypts because the crypts were warded.* Each fact landed like a stone dropped down a well, and the well never gave back a splash, only deeper dark.

By the time they reached the foot of the palace hill, Mara had learned three things, none of which she wanted:

The war had come to Corvale slowly, then all at once.

The last tribunal had convened in the palace's great hall, and its records had been sealed—not destroyed, *sealed*—by the first Warden's own hand.

And beneath everything, below the crypts, below the foundations, something vast had once lain down and gone quiet, and the city above it had grown like scar tissue over the wound.

"The Thorn was here," she said. "Before Thornvale. Before any of it. This is where they caught it."

"Yeah," Wren said. "And look how well that ended." He gestured at the boulevard, the loops, the lidless sky. "Whole capital's a graveyard that doesn't know it's dead, and the thing that did it—or the thing they *say* did it—is sleeping under the pretty house on the hill. And we're walking straight to it."

"Why'd you come, then?"

He shrugged, looking anywhere but at her. "Someone's got to make sure you don't do anything stupid alone. You've got a gift for it."

* * *

Wren's POV

The palace had been grand the way mountains are grand—it hadn't tried, it simply was. Even gutted, even grey, even with ivy the color of old bone climbing its flanks, it made Wren feel like something you'd scrape off a boot. He hated places like this. Places like this were where the people lived who'd sold him, and he'd promised himself at nine years old that he'd never walk into one again unless it was on fire.

So far it was not on fire. Disappointing.

"Mara." He caught her sleeve as she reached the great doors—bronze, taller than three men, hanging half-open on twisted hinges. "Rules. We don't split up. We don't touch anything that glows. And if anything down there asks us our names, we lie."

"You lie. I'm the Warden. My name is sort of the point."

"Your name is bait, is what it is." He kept his voice low, though the nearest echo—a guard frozen mid-salute, forever—was hardly going to gossip. "Every person hunting that seed knows a Dravenmoor is carrying it. Down there, in the dark, under a palace? Keep the name in your pocket till we know who's listening."

Something flickered across her face—not annoyance. Consideration. She'd gotten better at considering him, these last weeks. He tried not to notice how much he liked that.

"Fine," she said. "We lie."

"Attaboy." He shouldered past her through the doors.

Inside, the great hall opened up like the inside of a bell, and both of them stopped dead.

The tribunal seats still stood. Seven of them, arranged in a crescent on a dais of black stone, each carved from a different pale wood, each draped in dust thick as snowfall. Before them, the floor of the hall was scored with a circle thirty paces wide, inlaid with silver that had gone black, and around the circle's rim ran words in a script older than any alphabet Wren knew—except he *did* know it, in the way you know a song you heard before you could talk, and the knowing crawled up his spine like cold water.

Because the script was ward-script. Guild-script. The same letters Bramwell's songs used, worn like a borrowed coat by whatever had happened in this room.

"This is it," Mara breathed. She was walking the circle's rim, fingers hovering an inch above the blackened silver, not touching. "This is where they sentenced it. The tribunal. The ghost-echoes—" She stopped. Turned in place, slowly, looking at the empty seats. Looking at the dust. Looking at the dark doorway beyond the dais, where stairs descended into the hill.

"Warden Halloran," Wren said carefully, "you've got your courtroom face on, and I want you to hear me before you put it to use. That staircase goes down. Things that go down in this city don't come back up the same. Every story my gran ever told about Corvale ends at the bottom of a staircase just like that one."

"I know."

"And?"

"And the seed hasn't stopped pulling since we crossed the wall." She pressed her palm flat over her sternum, over the place where it slept. "It's not dragging me, Wren. It's—asking. It's been asking since the gate. Politely. For forty years' worth of politely." Her jaw set. "Somebody finally asked it to wait. Nobody ever asked it what waiting cost. I'm going down there to find out."

Wren looked at the staircase. Looked at her. Did the arithmetic he'd been doing since the orchard fires—her life against his, his freedom against her mission, the seed against everyone who wanted it—and arrived, as always, at the same useless sum.

"Right," he said. "But I go first. If the dark wants a toll, it can start with someone cheap."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny." He pulled his knife, checked the edge out of habit rather than hope, and started down.

* * *

Mara's POV

The stairs descended farther than the palace was tall.

That was Mara's first coherent thought, twenty minutes into the descent, when the last grey daylight had fallen away behind them and Wren's shuttered lantern showed walls of seamless black stone that predated every mason's mark she knew. The steps were worn in their centers—worn by feet, by centuries of feet—and yet nothing about this place felt visited. It felt *kept*. Like a room closed after a funeral, preserved exactly as grief had left it.

The seed was singing.

Not aloud. Not in her mother's voice, not in any voice. It sang the way the wards used to sing under Bramwell's hands, a structure of pressure and silence that Mara felt in her teeth, in the wet coils of her ears, in the memory-bones of her that the road had already spent. It grew louder with every step down, and stranger—layered, polyphonic, as if many singers had begun in unison and drifted apart over centuries until they were all holding different notes of the same chord.

"Do you hear that?" Wren whispered.

"You can hear it?"

"Heard it since the gate." He didn't look back at her. His knuckles were white on the lantern. "Thought it was the loops. It's not the loops." A pause. "It sounds like my gran's lullabies, a bit. Which is either very sweet or the single worst thing I've ever felt, and I haven't decided."

The stairs ended in a door.

Not a grand door. That was the thing that undid her. After the bronze gates, after the seven-seat dais, after the circle of dead silver and the stair into the mountain's heart, the door at the bottom was small, and wooden, and ordinary—a servant's door, iron-banded, with a latch worn smooth by thumbs. Above it, carved shallow and nearly erased by time, ran a single line of guild-script.

Mara read it without meaning to. The letters assembled themselves in her mind the way the ward-verses always had, and her breath went out of her all at once.

*"Here lies the judgment no one survived to appeal."*

Below it, smaller, newer, cut by a different and shaking hand:

*"Forgive us. —J."*

Wren's lantern guttered. Steadied.

"J," he repeated. "Who's J?"

"I don't know." But the seed knew. The seed surged against her ribs like a heart remembering its purpose, and through the little wooden door, from the dark on the other side, something answered its song—one note, low and vast and impossibly patient, a note that had been held since before Bramwell was born, since before the wards, since before the valley that forgot—

The latch, worn smooth by centuries of thumbs, began to lift on its own.

Wren's knife was out. Mara's hand found his wrist and held it, gently, the way you hold someone back from a cliff edge—or walk with them to one.

"No," she said. "I think it's expecting us."

The door swung inward onto a darkness full of stars.

Not the stolen stars of the vanished sky—these were small, and close, and growing, hundreds of pale points kindling in the deep of the chamber like a constellation learning its own shape, and at the center of them, coiling upward out of the earth, the seed rose glowing from her chest and *answered.*

Somewhere far above them, through forty years of silence, the dead of Corvale—all of them, every looping soul in every street—stopped mid-motion.

And turned their faces toward the palace.
