## 20. The Court of Loops

The dead did not rest in the capital. They rehearsed.

Mara understood that within the first hour of walking its streets. The city had died mid-sentence, and now it repeated itself forever—shopkeepers sweeping doorways that had already burned, a woman calling a child's name from a balcony with no railing, soldiers marching down an avenue that ended in rubble and then beginning the march again at the avenue's start, boots striking stone that remembered every step.

"They don't see us," Wren said, walking close. He'd been quiet since the river, quieter since the ferryman's toll. "Or they don't care. Either way, keep moving."

"I'm moving."

"You're staring."

She was. A baker stood in the doorway of his shop, flour to his elbows, looking up at a sky that no longer held stars. His mouth moved on a loop of three words. *Any moment now.* Any moment now. Any moment—

"What are they waiting for?" Mara asked.

"Same thing everyone waits for," Wren said. "For it to be over. Only it never got to be over for them."

The seed pulsed against her sternum where she'd bound it beneath her shirt, warm as a sleeping bird. It had been warmer since the river. Warmer since the chapel murals showed her a judge instead of a monster. It pulled, gently, always northward, toward the palace at the city's heart—and Mara followed, because there was nowhere else the story could end but where it began.

They crossed a plaza where a tribunal had once been held.

She didn't know that yet. She only knew the plaza felt different from the streets around it—the echoes here were not fragments but a whole, hundreds of figures arranged in rings around a raised dais, frozen not in death but in attention. And then, as she stepped past the first ring, they moved.

Not toward her. Through her. The plaza lit with ghost-light, and the day of the tribunal began again.

* * *

She should have kept walking. Wren said so later, more than once. But the dais bore the seal of the Warden's Guild—the interlocked thorn and star she'd grown up polishing on brass plaques and stitching into apprentice hems—and her feet stopped as if the ground itself had claimed them.

The echoes assembled. Judges in grey robes took their benches. Guards lined the rings. And at the center of the dais, bound in chains of woven light, stood something vast that the eye kept sliding off—a shape like a tree, like a storm, like a man, like none of these. Its voice, when it came, was neither deep nor monstrous. It was tired.

"I have answered every question put to me," the Thorn said. "I have confessed what I did at Redford Crossing. I have named my own dead. I ask again: who speaks for my accusers?"

A judge rose—an echo worn thin by centuries of repetition, his features blurred like a face seen through water. "This is not a trial," he said. "It is a sentencing."

Mara's breath went out of her.

"That is not lawful," the Thorn said. Even the echo of its confusion sounded ancient. "Your own charter requires—"

"The charter was suspended by emergency decree." The judge's voice was flat, procedural, terrible in its calm. "You are declared an entity of war. Entities of war are not tried. They are ended or contained. The guild has chosen containment."

"Chosen," the Thorn repeated. "By whom? I never saw the faces of those who chose. Bring them. Let them stand where I stand and say what I have done. That is all I have ever asked. Not mercy. *Procedure.*"

One of the judges leaned toward another, and though the echo had degraded across three hundred years, Mara caught the words clearly enough:

*"If we give it a hearing, we may have to hear it."*

* * *

The loop played through. She watched all of it, rooted to the stones, while Wren paced the plaza's edge and pretended not to listen.

She watched the Thorn ask, seventeen times by her count, for counsel. Denied—no advocate would accept the brief. She watched it offer terms of its own confinement, terms that would have kept it asleep and harmless without the wards' cruelty. Rejected—the guild would not negotiate with an entity of war; negotiation implied standing. She watched the final exchange, the one that would follow her into sleep for the rest of her life.

"Understand what you do here," the Thorn said. "You are not caging a beast. You are burying a question. And buried questions do not die. They wait. They grow roots. One day your descendants will dig this city open and find me still asking, and they will have to answer for your silence."

The chief judge signed the sentence without looking up. "Then let them answer. We will be dust."

"No," said the Thorn, and for the first time its voice carried something other than weariness—something cold and patient and terribly certain. "You will be echoes. You will stand here forever, saying these words, until someone comes who can hear you. I intend to be very well heard."

The light of the binding flared white. The plaza emptied. The echoes reset—a sweep of the broom, a call from a balcony, boots on stone—and began again somewhere out of sight.

Mara sat down hard on the steps of the dais.

* * *

Wren crouched beside her. He didn't touch her shoulder; he'd learned that much about her. "Say something, so I know you're not looping too."

"They sentenced it without a trial." Her voice came out level, which surprised her. Level was worse than shaking. Level meant the fury had gone all the way down and found bedrock. "My guild. Bramwell's guild. The charter I could recite before I could write—they wrote *procedure* into every line of it. Every rite, every ward-song, every rule about how a grievance must be heard. And when it mattered, when it mattered most—" She stopped. Started again. "They were afraid of what it would say."

"Maybe it deserved what it got."

"Maybe." She looked at him. "But you don't know that unless you hold the trial. That's the whole point of a trial, Wren. It's not a gift to the accused. It's protection for everyone else. It's how you know the thing you caged needed caging." She pressed her palm flat against the stone. "Bramwell taught me the ward-songs as lullabies. *We keep the valley safe. We keep the dark things down.* He made it sound like shelter. It was a gag. Three hundred years, and my whole valley was a hand over its mouth."

"And the ninth verse?"

"Never sung." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "He wouldn't teach it. I thought he was protecting me from something dangerous. He was protecting me from this. From knowing the song wasn't a cage-song at all. From knowing the first Wardens built a prison because they didn't have the nerve for a courtroom."

Wren was quiet a moment. "The people who sold me," he said finally, "had rules too. Ledgers, oaths, little brass plaques. I used to think if I could just find the rulebook and read it, I'd understand why. Then I found one of their ledgers, and it was just numbers. Rules don't stop people from being afraid. Fear writes the rules."

"Fear wrote mine."

"So burn it."

She shook her head slowly. "No. That's what Ilse wants. What Vane wants. Burn it, break it, wake it, kill it—everyone has a plan that starts with destroying something and ends with never having to hear the answer." She stood. Her legs held. "It asked for procedure. Three hundred years ago it asked for procedure, and nobody in this plaza had the courage. Fine. Then I'll be the courage. I'll give it the trial. All of it—Redford Crossing, the war, the guild's crimes, mine. Everything on the record."

"That's a big promise for one apprentice."

"I'm not an apprentice anymore." She touched the seed through her shirt, and it pulsed once, like a heartbeat answering. "Bramwell named me Warden. I didn't understand what the word meant until tonight. It doesn't mean keeper of cages." She looked up at the palace rising black against the starless sky, at the hill where the whole rotten inheritance had been decided. "It means keeper of courts."

The seed pulled northward, urgent now, tugging at her ribs like a hook set in flesh. Toward the palace. Toward whatever waited beneath it—the origin, the root, the first stone of the cage.

And behind her, faint under the sound of marching boots and sweeping brooms, the plaza's echoes reset once more, and the tired voice began again:

*"I have answered every question put to me—"*

Mara walked north, and did not look back.
