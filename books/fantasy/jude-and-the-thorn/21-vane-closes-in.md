## 21. Vane Closes In

The resonance woke Serah Vane three miles from the ruined capital, in the hour before dawn when the deadlands were at their quietest.

It came through her bones before it came through the air—a low, sweet pressure behind her sternum, like a tuning fork pressed to the chest. She had felt nothing like it since she was a child in her grandmother's house, before the purges, before the name Vane became something you buried under three generations of loyal service. Her grandmother had called it *the hum*. She had said it was how the Thorn spoke to those who remembered how to listen.

Vane had spent forty years making sure no one would ever call her a listener.

She rose from her bedroll without waking the sentries and stood facing the dark horizon, where the capital's silhouette crouched against a sky that held no stars. The hum pulsed once, twice, and then settled into a steady thread, thin as wire, running toward the palace.

*There*, she thought. *You're there.*

Behind her, the camp stirred. Two hundred Crown regulars, four ward-engineers with their portable suppression rigs, a quartermaster who complained about everything, and Captain Aldric Reeve, who complained about nothing, which Vane trusted less. They had marched eleven days out of Harrowgate at forced pace, following an instrument that shouldn't have worked—the resonance compass, salvaged from the Inquisition vaults, built by the same guild that had built Thornvale's wards. It had pointed them unerringly across the wastes, and now it hung in its case beside her bedroll, its needle glowing faint amber, straining toward the palace like a hound at the end of a leash.

"Ma'am." Reeve's voice came out of the dark. He'd learned to approach her quietly; most men hadn't survived learning that. "Engineers say the needle's gone solid. Whatever we're tracking, it's stopped moving."

"It's not stopped," Vane said. "It's arrived."

"You believe Dravenmoor's already inside?"

"I believe the seed has been pulling her across the deadlands for a month, Captain, and I believe it knows exactly where it wants to go. She's not chasing it anymore. It's leading her."

Reeve was silent for a moment. He was a good officer—solid, unimaginative, loyal in the way of men who had never been given a reason to be otherwise—and Vane had chosen him precisely because he would follow any order she gave, including the ones she hoped never to give.

"And when we find her?" he asked. "Orders are to take the artifact and the girl into custody. The girl part's going to be complicated if she doesn't come quiet."

"The girl comes quiet or the girl comes bound. Either way, she comes alive. That order hasn't changed."

"And if she's woken something?"

Vane turned to look at him. In the grey pre-dawn his face was all angles and shadow, and beyond him the camp fires burned low, orange points in a darkness that had swallowed every star in the sky a month ago and given none of them back.

"If she's woken something," Vane said, "then everything I'm about to do is already too late, and I'll do it anyway. Because it will still be the right thing, Captain. That's what right things are. They're what you do when it's too late for them to matter."

Reeve nodded slowly, the way men nodded at sermons they didn't understand but respected anyway, and went to rouse the camp.

Vane stood alone a while longer, one hand pressed flat against her sternum, feeling the hum beat there like a second heart.

*Grandmother*, she thought. *Your blood brought us here.*

She almost wished the old woman could see it. She was very glad she couldn't.

* * *

They reached the outer walls of the capital by mid-morning, and Vane gave the order to halt because even her engineers, who were trained to look at magic the way surveyors looked at terrain, needed a moment simply to stand and stare.

The city had died mid-breath. Streets of pale stone ran between buildings whose windows were dark but whose interiors moved—faint shapes, translucent as breath on glass, walking the same dozen steps over and over. A woman on a balcony wrung her hands, lifted them, wrung them again. A child chased a ball down an alley and reappeared at the alley's mouth to chase it again, forever, the ball always just ahead of its small grasping fingers. Somewhere out of sight, a bell tolled a quarter-hour that never ended.

"Ghost-echoes," said Engineer Corvas, the senior of the four, consulting a slate covered in equations Vane only half understood. "Residual imprints. The whole city's soaked in them. It's like—" he paused, searching, "—like standing inside someone's memory of a place instead of the place itself."

"Can they hurt us?"

"They can't do anything, ma'am. They're not ghosts, they're echoes. A bell doesn't know it's ringing."

But Vane watched the child with the ball, and the child turned its head—not toward the soldiers, not quite, but along the line of them, the way water finds a channel—and for half a heartbeat she could have sworn its expression changed. Not fear. Not welcome. Recognition, maybe. The way you recognize a tune you heard in childhood and can't name.

"Keep moving," she said. "Palace district. Double watch."

They advanced through streets where the dead rehearsed their last morning, and Vane kept her eyes forward and her hand on the resonance compass, and told herself the tightening in her chest was only the altitude, or the dust, or the eleven days of hard riding. It was none of those things. It was the city remembering, and her remembering with it—her grandmother's kitchen, the smell of burnt honey, the night the Inquisitors came for the neighbors and her grandmother hid the family's shrine-stones in the flour barrel and sang lullabies very loudly until they went away.

*You serve the Crown so they'll let you destroy what shames your blood,* her father had told her, the last time they'd spoken. *That's not service, Serah. That's a leash you've mistaken for a spine.*

She had not spoken to him since. She had gotten commendations instead. The commendations were heavier, but they stacked better.

The palace rose ahead of them, black-roofed and broken-crowned, its great gates hanging off one hinge. And there—small against that vast ruin, seated on the steps with a thief's pack beside her and a sword across her knees that she clearly barely knew how to hold—sat Mara Dravenmoor.

Waiting for them.

*Of course she is*, Vane thought, and felt something that was not quite admiration move through her. *She's known we were coming since the river.*

* * *

Vane rode ahead alone, which made Reeve unhappy and which she did anyway, because the first thing you showed a hunted animal was whether you came in a herd or came honest.

Mara Dravenmoor watched her dismount from the palace steps. She was thinner than the reports described, browned by the deadlands sun, with a new scar along one forearm and eyes that had aged a decade in a month. But it wasn't the eyes that struck Vane. It was the stillness. The last time Vane had seen her—in Thornvale, in that little guild-house smelling of apple wood and lamp oil—she had been a frightened apprentice performing ignorance badly. Now she sat like a magistrate waiting for a late petitioner.

"Inquisitor Vane," Mara said. "You've added an army."

"I've added two hundred men and four engineers. Calling it an army flatters the budget." Vane stopped at the base of the steps, close enough to speak without shouting, far enough that courtesy remained possible. "Where is your companion?"

"Gone."

"For how long?"

"Long enough that asking about him is a way of checking whether I'm alone." Mara's mouth curved, not quite a smile. "I'm not alone. But not in the way you mean."

"The seed, then. It's here."

"It's here."

Vane had prepared for denial, flight, deception—for the girl she'd met in Thornvale. She found herself briefly wrong-footed by honesty. She recovered, because recovery was the trade she practiced.

"Then hear me, Dravenmoor, because I will say this once and I will not enjoy saying it twice." She kept her voice level, almost gentle; gentleness disarmed better than force. "You're standing in the seat of a war that ended before either of our grandmothers drew breath, holding an object that both my order and the Thornbound would burn the world to possess. You've crossed the deadlands. You've seen what the wastes are. Now imagine that everywhere, forever, if the wrong people get what they want. The Crown doesn't want to hurt you. The Crown wants to make sure no one—no one—ever holds that thing again. Give it to me. Surrender yourself into protective custody. You'll be treated honorably. You'll be fed, sheltered, questioned fairly, and in time, when this is settled, released. I give you my word on my commission."

"That word," Mara said quietly. "Is it worth more than the word the Crown gave the Thorn?"

The hum behind Vane's sternum pulsed, sharp as a struck nail, and she had to keep her face still while it rang.

"What did you say?"

"You heard me." Mara rose, unhurried, and picked up a leather satchel from the steps. "You've followed me a month, Inquisitor. You've read the guild ledgers—I know you have, because you left Harrowgate the week after the archive requisitions went through, and your engineers carry copies. So you know what Thornvale was. You know it wasn't a sanctuary. You know it was a cell. What you don't know—what the ledgers don't say, because the ledgers were written by the jailers—is what happened before the cell was built." She descended one step. "Because it happened here. In this palace. And the palace remembers."

"Echoes don't testify, Dravenmoor."

"No. But they repeat. Faithfully. Better than any witness I've ever met." Another step. She was two above Vane now, near enough that Vane could see the fine tremor in her hands that the stillness elsewhere was working to conceal. She was afraid. She was doing it anyway. Vane knew that discipline intimately; recognizing it in an enemy was like looking into a mirror angled wrong. "I've spent six days in these halls. There's a chamber beneath the east wing where the final tribunal convened—the real one, not the story the guild tells. The echoes replay it. Every hour, on the hour. Would you like to see it?"

"What I would like," Vane said, "is for you to hand me that satchel."

"And I'd like the stars back," said Mara. "We're both wanting things today. Come and watch the tribunal, Inquisitor. Then decide. If after seeing it you still want the seed, I'll consider your offer. That's fair. That's more than fair—that's procedure. And procedure is supposed to be your religion, isn't it? Fair process. Due order. The Crown's whole claim to legitimacy rests on the idea that it does things properly, where others do them by force." Her voice dropped, and there was something underneath it now, something raw. "So do it properly. Once. For the thing nobody ever did it properly for. Or admit that the Crown's justice has a shape, and the shape is a cage."

For a long moment the only sound was the distant, endless tolling of the bell that never finished its quarter-hour.

Vane was aware of Reeve at the edge of the square, of two hundred soldiers watching their Inquisitor stand unarmed at the foot of steps below a fugitive, aware of how it looked and caring very little. She was aware of the compass in her saddlebag, its needle surely straining. She was aware, most of all, of her grandmother's flour-barrel, and the shrine-stones hidden inside it, and the question she had buried at nine years old and never answered: *if what we worship is evil, why does hiding it feel like saving something?*

"You're trying to delay me," she said at last. "Every hour in that chamber is an hour the Thornbound close the distance. I know Ilse is coming. My outriders found their sign two days west. When she arrives, this square becomes a battlefield, and whatever ceremony you're imagining becomes impossible."

"Then we should hurry," said Mara.

And that—that simple, terrible readiness—was the moment Serah Vane understood that she had not been tracking a courier carrying stolen goods across a country. She had been tracking a Warden walking to her own enthronement.

"One hour," Vane said. "One viewing. If what you show me is the propaganda of a dead cult, the offer expires and I take the seed from your body if that's what it costs. If what you show me is true—" She stopped. Started again, more carefully. "If it's true, then the Crown has been guarding a lie for a century, and I will need to think about what that makes me."

"It makes you someone who can still change her mind," Mara said. "That's more than the Thorn ever got."

Vane turned and signaled Reeve forward with two fingers: *secure the square, engineers on the gates, no one enters the palace but me.* Then she climbed the steps past the girl who had cost her a month, a campaign, and possibly a career, and stood before the doors of a palace her order had helped empty a hundred years ago.

The hum in her chest had gone very quiet. Waiting. The way a courtroom waits.

"Lead, Warden," she said, and heard the title leave her mouth a half-second too late to call it back.

Mara Dravenmoor looked at her for a long moment—looked at her the way one looks at a door that might open—and then pushed open the palace doors onto the dark, and the sound of the tribunal beginning again, as it had begun ten thousand times, without anyone to hear it.

Until now.
