## 22. The Trial of Serah Vane

Serah Vane had attended tribunals before. She had stood in the marble halls of the Crown's High Court, in the cold annexes of the Inquisition's own chambers, and watched men and women be unmade by procedure—stripped of titles, of names, sometimes of more than that. She knew the architecture of judgment. She had trusted it the way other people trusted their own hands.

So when the ghost-echoes began to gather in the ruined tribunal hall beneath the palace, she recognized what was happening, and that recognition frightened her more than any blade could have.

They came out of the walls first. Not ghosts, exactly—the dead of the capital did not haunt so much as *repeat*—but these were different from the loop-walkers above. These echoes moved with purpose. They arranged themselves along the broken tiers of the old courtroom, filling seats that had been empty for three hundred years, and though they had no faces she could hold in her mind for longer than a blink, they had attention. She felt it settle on her like snowfall.

Mara Dravenmoor stood at the center of the hall, on the Warden's dais, wearing a dead man's guild-badge and an expression Vane was beginning to hate: calm, patient, terribly certain.

"This is not how justice works," Vane said. Her voice carried well; she'd been trained for courtrooms too. "You cannot convene a trial out of smoke and grief."

"The last tribunal held here disagreed," Mara said. "It convened out of fear and called itself law. I'm only correcting the precedent."

Behind her, at the edge of the dais, the thief—Wren—shifted his weight from foot to foot, his hand nowhere near his knife, which Vane noted with professional disappointment. He looked like a man standing in a house fire trying to decide which portrait to save. His eyes kept going to Mara, then to Vane, then to the seed.

The seed sat on a stone plinth between them, exactly where Vane's soldiers' chains had placed it after they took it from her at the gate. It pulsed faintly, a slow green-gold rhythm, like something breathing in its sleep. Crown banners hung over the plinth now. Vane had ordered them raised herself. Symbols mattered. Order mattered.

She repeated that to herself as the echoes finished seating themselves.

"Mara," Vane said, quieter now, "you are outnumbered forty to one. My writ gives me authority to destroy this thing. Whatever theater you stage here changes nothing."

"Then it should cost you nothing to watch." Mara lifted her chin. "Unless you're afraid of what the record says."

Vane should have refused. Every instinct honed by twenty years of service told her to refuse—to give the order, end the farce, burn the seed and the girl and the thief and be done with a war older than the Crown itself. Instead she heard herself say:

"One hour. Then my writ resumes."

And the tribunal began.

* * *

There was no gavel. The eldest echo—a shape in robes so faded they might have been grey or might have been white—simply rose, and silence fell the way water fills a bowl.

Mara spoke first, and she did not raise her voice.

"We convene under the ninth verse," she said, "which no one has sung in a century, because singing it would have meant admitting what this court did. This session is not about the Thorn. The Thorn will have its hearing tomorrow. Tonight we try a smaller crime, committed later, against fewer people—but by the same logic, and in the same name."

She turned, and looked directly at Vane.

"I charge the Order of Inquisitors, in the matter of the Vane purges, with the destruction of innocents and the falsification of history."

The words went through Serah Vane like a spear through wet paper.

Her family name. Spoken aloud, in this place, by this girl—as if it were evidence. As if it were *relevant*.

"You know nothing about my family," Vane said, and was distantly proud that her voice stayed level.

"I know what your archives wouldn't show you," Mara said. "Because I've read the guild ledger—the real one, the one Bramwell hid inside the ward-songs. And the guild kept records of its neighbors. Shall I read them?"

She opened the ledger. It was a plain book, water-stained, unremarkable—the kind of book that gets left behind in evacuations and forgotten in attics. Vane had seen a thousand like it in confiscated libraries.

Mara read.

"On the third of Frostmonth, in the year the wards were sealed, the Inquisition entered the town of Hollowmere. Forty-one residents were recorded as Thornbound sympathizers. Of these, nine were practitioners. The remaining thirty-two were kin, tenants, and children. All forty-one were executed under writ of contagion. The presiding inquisitor noted in the margin: *'No evidence of active binding found. Proceeding per doctrine.'*"

"No evidence," Vane said sharply, "means the threat was latent. Doctrine exists precisely because—"

"Hollowmere," Mara said, not raising her voice, "was your family's town."

The hall went very quiet. Even the seed's pulse seemed to slow.

Vane stood absolutely still. Somewhere behind her ribs, her heart was doing something undignified.

"My family wasn't from Hollowmere," she said. "My family *died* in the purges. That is the whole of it. That is why I wear this coat." She touched the grey lapel without thinking, and hated herself for the gesture. "You don't get to stand there and recite numbers at me as if I haven't spent my life—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "I have read those records too, apprentice. I requested them when I was nineteen. I read every name. I wept over every name. And then I understood that the purge was the price of a valley that slept safely for three hundred years, and I chose the valley."

"Did you?" Mara asked. "Or did you choose the story?"

She turned the ledger around, so the page faced the tiers of echoes—and, incidentally, faced Vane.

"Read the next entry."

Vane did not want to read the next entry. She read it anyway, because she had never once in her life declined to look at a document, and she was not going to start in front of a room full of the dead.

*"Follow-up survey, spring following. Hollowmere site examined. Remains interred per protocol. Note: among effects recovered, a child's primer bearing the Vane household mark. Cross-reference: the presiding inquisitor, Aldous Vane, is recorded as having signed the writ for his own district."*

"Aldous Vane," Mara said gently, horribly, "signed the order for Hollowmere. Your ancestor didn't die in the purge, Serah. He conducted it. On his own neighbors. Possibly his own kin—the primer suggests a household connection the record doesn't fully explain. And afterward, the family story inverted. The executioners became the victims. It's a remarkably effective way to launder guilt. Grief makes excellent armor. No one asks a mourner for documents."

"That's a forgery," Vane said. "Guild records against Crown records—the guild had every reason to—"

"To what? Exonerate the Inquisition?" Mara almost smiled, and there was no kindness in it. "The guild built the cage, Inquisitor. The guild and the Crown wrote the purge together. If we wanted to forge anything, we'd have forged ourselves innocent."

* * *

Vane walked the tiers while the echoes murmured—or seemed to murmur; sound in this place behaved like light through water. She needed to move. Standing still, she could feel the document working its way into her like cold into a boot seam.

She had built her life on a foundation of grief, and grief had always seemed the most honest material available. You couldn't argue with grief. Grief had no agenda. Everyone who had ever tried to console her—mentors, chaplains, the occasional kind superior—had accepted her sorrow as fact, and fact was the one thing an inquisitor owed everyone.

But facts had documents. And the document said her great-great-great-grandmother's name appeared not on a martyr's roll but on a warrant.

Aldous Vane. She knew the portrait. It hung in the Inquisition's Hall of Founders, a severe man with a scholar's stoop, captioned *Died in service of the clean world.* She had stood before it at nineteen, newly sworn, newly orphaned by history, and promised him she would finish what the purges started.

Finish what he started. That was the accurate sentence. She saw that now with terrible clarity, the way you see a miscounted ledger column the moment after you've already reported the sum.

"You're doing it," Wren said.

She hadn't heard him approach. The thief stood a few feet away, arms crossed, radiating the specific discomfort of a man who has decided to be honest against his own interests.

"Doing what?" she asked.

"Grieving louder so you don't have to think. I know the trick. I invented it."

"You know nothing about me."

"No," Wren agreed. "But I know about being told a story about yourself until it fits so well you stop checking the seams. Somebody sold me a version of my childhood once. Took me years to notice the price tag still on it." He nodded toward the ledger. "That book isn't asking you to hate your family. It's asking you to count them right."

"And if I refuse to count?"

"Then you're just another person burning things because the arithmetic hurts." He shrugged. "I've met a lot of those. They all think they're the reasonable one."

He wandered back to Mara's side. Vane remained among the echo-seats, alone with three hundred years of the dead, and let herself do the thing she had trained her whole life to do without flinching.

She followed the evidence.

* * *

The session resumed. Mara read for an hour. Names, dates, marginalia. The purges had not been chaos—they had been *procedure*, and that was somehow worse. Vane had always imagined the purge-era inquisitors as fanatics, fire in their eyes. The ledger showed something colder: men ticking boxes. *Latent sympathy presumed. Kinship contamination assumed. Interred per protocol.* One inquisitor had complained, in writing, about the paperwork.

An echo near the front—a woman's shape, a mother's posture—leaned forward at every child's name. Vane found herself watching the echo instead of Mara, watching grief perform itself in a body that no longer existed, and thought: *this is what I have been feeding on for twenty years. Someone else's grief, worn like a uniform.*

When Mara finally closed the ledger, the silence had texture.

"What is your verdict, then?" Vane asked. Her voice sounded distant to her own ears. "What does the apprentice want? An apology from a corpse? My resignation? You can't punish the dead, and you can't unmake the Crown by shaming one officer."

"I don't want punishment," Mara said. "I want a witness. That's all this ever was. The guild sealed the Thorn rather than judge it because judgment requires listening, and listening requires admitting you might be wrong. The Inquisition burned your family's history rather than examine it for the same reason. Every atrocity in that ledger starts with someone deciding the answer in advance." She stepped down from the dais—not as a judge descending, Vane realized, but as one petitioner approaching another. "I'm not asking you to abandon duty, Serah. I'm asking you to serve it honestly. A verdict reached on false evidence isn't duty. It's just a slower purge."

"And if I hold to my writ anyway?" Vane asked. "If I destroy the seed tomorrow, as ordered?"

"Then you'll do it knowing what your order is," Mara said. "Knowing what the word *clean* has always meant when people like us use it. I can't stop you—I know that. But the echoes will remember. That's the whole point of them. Nothing done in this room gets to be forgotten again."

Vane looked at the seed on its plinth, under its Crown banner. It pulsed. Patient. It had waited three hundred years; it could wait through one more night of her indecision.

She thought of the Hall of Founders. Of Aldous Vane's stooped, severe portrait. She thought of herself at nineteen, swearing an oath to a dead man whose signature she'd never actually read—only been *told* about, the way you're told about weather in a country you've never visited.

Empathy was supposed to flow across lines like hers and Mara's like water finding a crack. What Vane felt instead was worse and better: recognition. The girl across the dais had also been raised on a story. Had also loved the teller. Was also standing in the wreckage of the telling, holding documents she wished she'd never opened. They were not enemies, Vane understood suddenly. They were the same wound, dressed in different coats.

That didn't make them allies. It made them legible to each other, which was more dangerous.

"The hour is ended," Vane said at last. "Your theater adjourns. My writ stands." She turned to her officers. "The seed remains secured. Double the watch. At dawn, we begin preparations for the rite."

Mara didn't argue. That frightened Vane most of all. The girl simply gathered her ledger, exchanged a look with the thief, and said, quietly, as she passed:

"For what it's worth, Inquisitor—you counted. Most people in your position wouldn't have. Whatever you decide at dawn, that counted for something."

Vane watched her go, and stood a long time in the emptying hall, among the echoes who did not leave, who never left, who remembered everything.

At dawn, she told herself, she would give the order. At dawn, duty would be simple again.

Then a runner came down from the palace gate, breathless, saluting badly, and said the words that made simplicity impossible:

"Inquisitor—banners on the western ridge. Hundreds of them. Thornbound sigils. They're marching on the city."

And behind them, Vane's scouts reported, walking alone and unhurried through the starless dark, came a woman in white with a priestess's staff, smiling like someone arriving at last to a wedding long overdue.

Ilse had come to wake the Thorn—and she had brought an army to do it.
