## 23. Betrayal at the Palace Gate

The palace gate had been a door once. Now it was a suggestion of a door, two pillars of black stone leaning against each other like drunkards, the arch between them holding nothing but starless sky and the smell of old ash.

Mara stood beneath it with her hand closed around the seed, and the seed pulsed against her palm like a second heartbeat—faster now, quicker than her own, as if it knew what waited on the other side of those pillars.

"Say it again," Wren said behind her. "The plan. Slowly."

"There's no plan."

"That's what I was afraid you'd say."

She turned to look at him. In the grey light of the dead capital he looked thinner than usual, all elbows and sharp edges, his coat hanging off him like it belonged to a bigger man—which, she supposed, it once had. He'd given up thieving for guiding and hadn't quite figured out how to carry himself without something in his hands. Right now his hands were empty, and they wouldn't stay still.

"The tribunal chamber," Mara said anyway, because he needed words even if they weren't a plan. "Beneath the palace. The ghost-echoes replay the sentencing there—the original one. If I can reach it before Vane reaches me, I can use what's written there. Evidence. Proof that the Thorn was never tried. That everything the guild built, everything the Crown has sworn to destroy—it all stands on a sentence passed without a hearing."

"And if Vane reaches you first?"

"Then I run."

"You're terrible at running," Wren said. "You run like a woman carrying something precious. Because you are." He jerked his chin at her closed fist. "Everything wants that thing, Mara. The Crown wants it dead. The Thornbound want it awake. And you want—" he squinted at her, "—honestly, I've stopped being sure what you want."

"So have I," she admitted. "But I know what it deserves. A reckoning. Not a knife, not a crown of thorns. A hearing."

"A trial for a monster." He said it lightly, but there was no lightness under it. She'd learned to hear the weight beneath Wren's jokes the way you learn to hear water under ice—a hollow sound that tells you where not to step.

"It judged others fairly once," she said. "The murals in the chapel. Before the war bent it. It deserves to be judged by the standard it held."

Wren looked past her, through the empty arch, at the palace rising in its own ruin—towers slumped like tired sentries, windows dark, banners long rotted to threads on their poles. Somewhere inside it, ghosts walked their last moments over and over, dying eternally at tables, in corridors, on stairs. He'd been quiet since the ferryman. Since the river. Since whatever memory he'd paid to cross.

Mara hadn't asked which one. There were only so many memories a boy like Wren would have carried worth spending, and she'd watched his face when the ferryman took his coin. She'd decided some doors were kinder left shut.

"Stay close," she said. "If I fall, take the seed east. Don't look back. Don't be clever about it."

"I'm always clever about it."

"Wren."

He met her eyes. Something moved across his face—quick, unreadable, gone. "Yeah," he said. "I know."

They went through the gate together.

* * *

The palace interior was worse than the outside, because the outside was merely dead and the inside was still happening.

In the first corridor they crossed, a woman in court dress walked the same twelve steps and turned and walked them again, her mouth opening around a scream that never came, her arms reaching toward a doorway that had burned away centuries ago. Mara made herself watch for three loops. On the fourth, the woman reached the doorway and seemed, for half a heartbeat, to see her—and then the loop caught her back, and she was reaching and screaming and reaching again.

"Don't look at them too long," Wren murmured. "Wren's rule of haunted palaces: don't make eye contact with the dead. They get ideas."

"They're not dead. Not exactly."

"Worse. Got it."

They descended. The seed grew warmer with every stair, beating against her palm like a bird testing a cage, and Mara found herself talking to it without meaning to—not in words, just in attention, a steady thread of *I'm here, we're going, hold on.* It had been speaking to her in borrowed voices since the deadlands. Her mother's voice, mostly. Bramwell's, once, terribly. She wondered whose voice it would find next, and hated herself for wondering whether she'd welcome it.

The stair opened into an antechamber where the air itself felt older, pressed flat like flowers in a book. And there—

Torches. Real ones, burning with real flame, throwing real light.

No ghost-echo burned torches.

Mara stopped so fast Wren walked into her back.

"Well," said Serah Vane, from the far end of the chamber. "You came after all. I confess I expected more hiding."

She stood alone before a sealed door of black stone, the kind of door that had been warded shut by people who meant it. Six Crown soldiers flanked her in grey lacquered armor, faces hidden, pikes grounded. Vane herself wore no helmet. Her silver hair was braided tight, her pale eyes calm, and in her right hand she held a slender rod of white metal that Mara recognized from the attack on Thornvale: a ward-breaker, Crown-forged, capable of unmaking a century of patient work in a single stroke.

Behind her, the black door. Behind the black door, presumably, the tribunal chamber. The evidence. Everything.

"You followed us across the whole deadlands," Mara said. "Through the storm country. Past the ferry."

"The ferryman owed me a crossing." Vane's mouth curved slightly. "We settled an old account."

"You let us lead you here."

"I let you do the walking. I'm not a tracker, Mara Dravenmoor. I'm a listener. The seed resonates. Every mile you carried it, it sang louder, and I simply followed the song." She tilted her head. "You must understand—I never needed to catch you. I only needed you to keep running in the right direction."

Mara's stomach dropped. All those miles. All those spent memories—the taste of Bramwell's stew, the name of the creek behind the guildhall, the color of her mother's dress—all of it spent on a road Vane had already mapped.

"You could have taken it at the river," Wren said quietly. At some point he'd drifted to stand between Mara and the soldiers, casual as anything, hands loose at his sides. "Why didn't you?"

"Because taking it from a corpse in the mud is easy, and easy things teach nothing." Vane's gaze slid back to Mara. "And because I wanted you to arrive here. I wanted you to see this place. To see what the Thorn cost the world, standing in the ruin it made. I wanted your certainty to crack a little, the way mine did, the first time I stood where you're standing." She stepped aside from the door, gesturing with the ward-breaker. "So. One last time, apprentice. Give me the seed. Walk out of here alive. You have my word—you've seen how I keep my word."

"I've seen how you release prisoners with messages," Mara said. "That's not the same as mercy."

"No," Vane agreed pleasantly. "It's better. Mercy is a feeling. A kept promise is a structure."

The seed pulsed. Hard. Once, twice—like knocking.

And Mara understood, with a cold slide of clarity, that it wasn't knocking at the door. It was answering something behind it. Something down there in the dark, in the chamber where it had been sentenced without a hearing, something that had felt it coming for forty miles and had begun, at last, to hope.

Her hand tightened. *Not yet,* she told it. *First the reckoning.*

Vane saw her grip and sighed, genuinely regretful. "Then we do this the difficult way. Take the seed," she told her soldiers. "Harm the girl only if she forces the issue. I promised her safe passage, and unlike some Wardens, I honor my promises to the letter."

Six pikes came up.

Wren moved.

Later, Mara would try to reconstruct exactly what happened in the next ten seconds and fail, because grief does that—grief takes the sharpest moments and blurs them, like ink in rain. What she remembered: Wren stepping wide, drawing the soldiers' eyes, doing the thing he did best, which was being exactly where you weren't looking. What she remembered: shouting, the scrape of boots, a soldier stumbling into another. What she remembered: Vane turning, fast and fluid, the ward-breaker rising—

And what she remembered, most clearly, most horribly: Wren's voice, pitched high over the chaos, ringing off the black stone:

*"Wait! WAIT! I'll trade you!"*

Everything stopped.

The soldiers froze mid-lunge. Vane's arm lowered an inch. Mara stood with her heart hammering and her mind refusing, actively refusing, to parse the sentence she had just heard.

"Wren," she said. His name came out very small.

He wasn't looking at her. He was looking at Vane, and his face was wet, and his jaw was working like there were words stuck sideways in his throat.

"You said—you said you gave my friend a message," he rasped. "Back at the camp. When you had me. You said give me the seed and the girl walks free. That was the message, wasn't it? You told him to tell me—" his breath hitched, "—and he didn't tell me, because he thought I'd—" 

"Wren." Mara's voice cracked like green wood. "What are you doing?"

"My sister." He said it to Vane, not to Mara. He couldn't look at her; she could see him trying and failing. "The ferry. The memory I paid. It was her. My sister. I gave the ferryman my sister and I can't—" his voice broke apart and reassembled, "—I can't remember her face anymore. I remember that I loved her. I remember that I lost her. But her face is just—it's a shape where a face used to be, and it's killing me, every hour it's killing me, and you're the Crown, you have archives, you have records, you have *magics*—"

"Records can be restored," Vane said quietly. "Crown archives hold echoes of the dead. Given sufficient cause."

"Sufficient cause," Wren repeated. His laugh was awful. "Right. Cause." And then, finally, horribly, he turned and looked at Mara, and what was in his face was worse than betrayal, because it was apology, apology already blooming, apology in advance. "I'm sorry. God, Mara, I'm so sorry. I have to have her back. I have to. I didn't know what it would cost to lose her until she was gone twice."

The seed beat against Mara's palm, frantic now, a trapped heart.

"Wren," she said, and she kept her voice low, kept it steady, because someone had to, because that was the job, that had always been the job. "Listen to me. Whatever she shows you won't be her. An echo isn't a person. You know that. You've walked these halls. You've seen them looping—"

"She'd rather loop than be nothing."

"—and if you do this, you do it knowing that the thing they pull out of an archive will be shaped like her and sound like her and it will not be *her*, and you'll have traded the one true thing you ever—"

"Mara." His voice was gentle. That was the worst part. Gentle. Like she was the one who needed handling. "You've been trading memories since the deadlands. You gave up your mentor's *face*. Don't tell me what it costs. Don't you dare tell me what it costs."

The words went in clean, between ribs.

Because he was right. That was the poison of it—he was right, and she had no high ground, none at all. She had rationed her own past like bread in a famine. She had fed the seed piece after piece of herself and called it necessity. Who was she to tell a boy he couldn't buy back his dead?

Vane extended her free hand, palm up, patient as winter.

"The seed," she said. "And in return: full restoration from the Crown archive, verified, witnessed. Your sister's face, her voice, her name-day, all of it. And the girl walks free—my word on it, witnessed by six soldiers who will swear it under seal." A pause, delicate as a blade. "Or we take it, and the offer dies, and you spend the rest of your life with a shape where a face should be."

Silence. The torches crackled. Somewhere above them, faintly, a ghost screamed its twelve steps' worth of scream.

Mara couldn't move. Couldn't speak. Her whole body had become the closed fist around the seed, and the seed was hammering, hammering, and she thought absurdly, desperately: *it trusted me. It chose me. It knocked on my skin like a door and I said hold on, I said we're going, I said—*

Wren crossed the space between them in four strides.

His hands came over hers—warm, trembling, careful, the way he handled stolen things he actually cared about. And she felt him peel her fingers back one by one, gently, unbearably gently, like a man defusing something, like a thief lifting a purse from a sleeping king, and the whole time he was crying and whispering, "I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I'm sorry," and she was letting him, she was *letting* him, because her body had betrayed her too, because some part of her had already understood, from the moment he said *my sister*, that he was always going to do this, that love makes thieves of everyone eventually.

The seed came free.

It sat in his palm between them, small and warm and pulsing, and for one suspended heartbeat Mara saw it clearly—the thing she had carried across a kingdom, the thing that had spoken in her mother's voice, the thing that had chosen her bloodline, that had knocked and hoped and trusted. It pulsed once toward her. Toward Wren. Back toward her. Confused. Frightened.

*Hold on,* she had told it. *We're going.*

Then Wren turned and set it in Serah Vane's open hand.

The moment it left his fingers, his face changed. Mara watched it happen—watched the transaction complete in his flesh before it completed in his mind. The desperate animal need that had driven him across the room drained out of him all at once, like a tide going out, and what rushed in behind it was everything else: the chapel murals, the ferry, the nights by the fire, the fact that the seed was the first thing in his whole crooked life that had ever needed him, that had ever been *his* to protect, that had made him a person instead of a survivor.

Mara saw the exact instant he understood what he'd done.

"Oh," Wren said. Very softly. To himself. To the seed, already closing in Vane's fingers. "Oh no."

"Done," said Vane, and snapped a restraint-cuff around the seed, and it dimmed like a lamp shuttered, and the warmth went out of the chamber, and Mara felt it go the way you feel a tooth leave your mouth—a wrongness, a gap, a tongue returning again and again to the absence.

Soldiers closed around Wren. He didn't resist. He stood with his arms half-raised, staring at his own empty palms as though they'd acted without him, as though he could charge them with treason for what they'd done.

"Your archive," Mara said. Her voice sounded far away, like someone else's. "Restore him. Now. As witnessed."

"In due course." Vane tucked the cuffed seed into a case at her belt, and Mara watched the lid close on the last warm thing in the world. "Restoration requires the Crown archive at Cael Saren. Three weeks' travel. You may accompany us, if you like, as my guest—" and here her pale eyes flicked to Mara with something almost like respect, "—or you may walk free, as promised. Either way, the Thorn comes with me, and it will be unmade properly, cleanly, with full rite and record. No more cages. No more valleys full of lies. You wanted justice, Warden. Destruction with documentation is the closest thing the Crown offers."

"You call that justice?" Mara whispered.

"I call it the only sentence anyone will sign."

They were moving now, forming column, escorting Wren between them. He wasn't fighting them. He was looking back at her over his shoulder, and his face was wrecked, and his mouth was shaping words—*I'm sorry, I didn't—Mara, I—*—words that meant nothing and everything, because the deed was done and apologies are just receipts for things already sold.

At the edge of the torchlight he stopped resisting entirely and said, loudly enough for her to hear it clearly:

"For what it's worth—I regretted it before my hands let go."

And Mara, standing alone in the antechamber with her empty hands and her emptied self, heard the black door behind Vane's departing soldiers groan—slowly, ponderously, drawn by something that had felt the seed pass by and been denied—begin, at long last, to open.
