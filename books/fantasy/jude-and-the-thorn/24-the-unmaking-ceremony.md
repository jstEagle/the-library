## 24. The Unmaking Ceremony

Serah Vane had performed the Rite of Unmaking twice before in her life, and both times it had been on things smaller than a fist.

The first was a charm-tooth pulled from the mouth of a smuggler in the Saltmarshes, a knuckle of bone that whispered when you held it near your ear. The second was a girl of perhaps nineteen who had grown ivy through her own veins and could not stop it growing. The rite had taken four hours. The girl had thanked her at the end. That was the part Serah still thought about at night—not the burning, not the ash, but the thanks. The relief in it. *Thank you,* the girl had said, with ivy flowering out of the corners of her mouth. *I couldn't stop.*

Magic did not want to be stopped. That was the first lesson of the Inquisition, taught before you were allowed to carry the grey cloak. Magic wanted to continue. It wanted to spread, to root, to sing itself louder until there was nothing left that wasn't singing. Every witch they burned, every relic they cracked, every ward they tore down—each one had been a mercy administered to someone who could no longer ask for it.

That was what Serah told herself as she knelt in the ruined great hall of the old palace and laid out the instruments of the ceremony on a slab of black marble that had once, according to Wren's nervous chatter before he'd been released, been a throne.

She did not believe it tonight. She noticed that she did not believe it, and she noted the disbelief the way she noted weather—objectively, without letting it change her plans—and she began anyway.

* * *

The Crown camp filled the palace courtyard like a wound filling with pus. Two hundred soldiers under Captain Aldery, who was competent and unimaginative and therefore exactly what she wanted. A quartermaster's train. Six acolytes from the Chapterhouse, young men and women in undyed wool who looked at the dead city the way pilgrims look at a shrine—which was its own kind of sickness, and Serah had already marked two of them for reassignment.

And at the center of it all, in an iron reliquary lined with salt and cold-iron mesh, the seed.

It had not glowed since they took it. That unsettled everyone but her. The soldiers expected a captured thing to struggle. Instead the seed sat in its cage like a prisoner who knows something the jailers don't, patient as geology, and every night one more man asked to be transferred off seed-watch, and every morning Serah approved the transfer without comment because she understood. It was easier to guard a thing that hated you. Hatred you could brace against. This—the waiting, the silence, the sense of being *watched back*—this wore grooves into a person.

Tonight the grooves would end. Tonight she would burn it out of the world.

She had chosen the throne room for the geometry. The Unmaking required a space where power had once gathered; the palace's heart was saturated with it, even now, even dead. The acolytes had chalked the circle in the old pattern—nine concentric rings, each inscribed with a verse of the Warding Litany, the same litany every guild warden in the empire had sung for three hundred years. There was a symmetry in that which Serah found satisfying. The wards had caged the Thorn. The litany would unmake what remained. Closure upon closure.

She checked each ring herself. She did not trust acolytes with geometry; geometry was where sloppy rites went wrong, and wrong rites did not fail cleanly—they failed *outward*.

The ninth ring gave her pause. It was blank.

Every text agreed on this: the ninth verse was lost, or sealed, or never written, depending on which authority you preferred, and the ninth ring was therefore left empty as a sign of humility before the incompleteness of human knowledge. Serah had always found that formulation suspicious. Institutions did not leave gaps out of humility. They left gaps because something had been removed, and the removal had been made doctrine so no one would go looking.

She chalked the empty ring anyway. Humility. Doctrine. Whatever kept the circle closed.

* * *

They brought the seed up at moonrise—or where the moon should have been. There was no moon. There had been no moon, no stars, nothing above but a smooth black ceiling of sky since the night Thornvale's wards fell, and Serah had stopped looking up months ago because looking up felt like being inside a closed mouth.

Four soldiers carried the reliquary on poles, as if it were plague-bearing, which by Inquisitorial definition it was. They set it in the center of the rings and withdrew at a walk, not running, because running suggested fear and fear was contagious in a garrison.

Serah dismissed them all to the courtyard door. Then she stood alone in the vast dark hall with the iron box, and she let herself feel the moment. You were supposed to. The Chapterhouse taught that an executioner who rushed the solemn parts became a butcher, and Serah Vane was many things the Chapterhouse disapproved of, but she was not a butcher.

"I am Serah Vane," she said to the box, formally, because the rite began with naming. "Of the line of Vane. Of the Order of the Grey Cloak, Third Chair. I come to unmake what cannot be permitted to remain."

Silence. Of course silence.

She opened the reliquary.

The seed sat on its bed of salt, and it was smaller than she remembered—a walnut of dark wood veined with faint gold, like something carved and then buried for centuries and then dug up again by hands that didn't know what they'd found. Except it hadn't been carved. She knew that now. It had *grown*, once, in the orchard dirt of a valley full of people who thought they were gardeners.

She lifted it out with tongs of cold iron and set it in the center of the innermost ring.

"By the authority of the Crown," she recited, "by the grief of the burned, by the memory of the war—"

The seed opened.

Not broke. Not cracked. *Opened*, the way a hand opens, the way a book opens, the way a door opens when the person inside has been waiting politely for you to knock. The woody shell parted along seams she hadn't seen, and the light that came out was not light. It was memory. She understood that immediately, the way you understand a language you didn't know you spoke: the gold glow spilling across the marble was made of other people's yesterdays, and it was showing her one.

She should have stepped back. The protocols were explicit—an open seed was a vector, an open seed was how the Thornbound recruited, an open seed was a weapon aimed at the mind. Her hand was already moving for the tongs.

Then the light touched her, and she saw her ancestor.

* * *

His name was Edric Vane, and she had known his name since she was six years old, kneeling on cold flagstones while her grandmother told the story the way it was always told, the way it had been told for nine generations, in the tone reserved for martyrs and saints.

Edric Vane, Thornbound. One of the faithful who kept the old worship alive after the war, when the Crown's new guilds hunted them through the valleys. He had sheltered the persecuted. He had carried relics. And when the Grey Cloaks finally came to the Vane farmstead in the year 214 of the Interregnum, he had refused to give up the names of his fellows, and they had taken him, and the family had never learned where they burned him.

*We are the descendants of the hanged,* her grandmother used to say, gripping Serah's small hands hard enough to hurt. *Remember that. Remember what they do to us, and remember why we serve them anyway—because someone must hold the leash, and better a Vane than a stranger.*

Serah had built her whole life on that sentence. She had joined the Inquisition at sixteen, the first Vane to do it openly, wearing the grey cloak of the order that killed her forefather, and she had told herself it was strategy—it was, it was also penance, it was also, though she'd never once said it aloud, a kind of courtship. To serve the thing that destroyed your family so completely that it forgot you were ever its enemy. To become indispensable. To earn, someday, the right to stand over the last relic of the Thornbound faith and be the one who ended it, so that the shame would die with her consent rather than against it.

That was why she wanted the seed. She had told herself many stories about why—duty, security, the danger of waking the Thorn—but underneath them all, plain as a scar, was this: *let me be the one. Let a Vane close the account.*

The light showed her the account.

Edric Vane stood in a stone chamber beneath an orchard, and he was not bound. He was not a prisoner. He wore the grey cloak of the Inquisition with the Third Chair's chain of office around his neck—he had been Third Chair himself, her chair, her exact office—and he was holding a brand of white fire, and he was reading charges aloud from a scroll in a voice like a closing door.

The seed remembered him perfectly. That was the horror of it. Seeds kept memories; Mara Dravenmoor had screamed that at her across the ruins of a chapel, *it eats memory, it runs on memory, everything it shows you is true*—and Serah had filed the claim as manipulation, and now the manipulation was showing her a man with her own jawline and her grandmother's eyes, and the memory had the grain and weight of truth the way lies never did.

In the memory, Edric Vane finished reading the charges. The thing he addressed—she could not see it whole, only a suggestion of vastness coiled in the dark below the floor, a presence like a storm seen through a wall—asked, in a voice that came up through the stones rather than the air:

*May I answer them?*

And Edric Vane said, "No."

He said it gently. That was the worst detail, the one Serah knew she would keep forever: he said it with kindness, with the weary tenderness of a doctor declining to tell a patient the full diagnosis. "No. There will be no trial. A trial would legitimize you, and legitimizing you is the one thing this empire cannot survive. You are to be sealed. Your name will be struck from every record. Your worshippers will be purged—I begin that work tomorrow." He paused, and the brand lowered, and the white fire began to pour down into the dark like water finding a crack. "For what it is worth, I believe some of the charges are false. It does not matter. Truth is a luxury of the victorious, and we intend to remain victorious for a very long time."

The dark below the floor said nothing. But the memory held one more moment—one long moment in which the presence regarded Edric Vane with something that was not hatred, and was not fear, and took Serah years of context to identify because she had never once been on the receiving end of it herself.

It was pity.

*You will need to forget this too,* the presence said. *You know that. You have already decided to.*

"Yes," said Edric Vane, Third Chair of the Order of the Grey Cloak, executioner of the innocent and architect of her family's martyrdom, and he sounded tired, and he sounded ashamed, and he did it anyway.

The memory closed like a hand.

* * *

Serah Vane was still standing in the center of the ritual circle. She did not know how long. The tongs hung from her fingers. The acolytes at the far door were watching her through the gloom, and she could feel their attention like weather, and none of them approached, because Serah Vane's face in repose had always discouraged approach.

Her grandmother's voice was in her head, saying *we are the descendants of the hanged*, and beneath it now, laid over it like a palimpsest, the true inscription: *we are the descendants of the hangman.* Nine generations of Vanes had served the Crown, and every one of them had known—had they? Had her grandmother known? Had the story been a lie all the way down, or had it simply eroded, the way stories do, guilt sanding itself smooth into grievance, century by century, until the executioner's descendants wept at their own invented funeral?

She thought of the purge records she had read in the Chapterhouse archive—she had read them hungrily, at nineteen, looking for her ancestor's name among the victims. She had found forty-one Thornbound executed in the spring of 214. She had assumed Edric was the forty-second, the one record they'd lost.

He wasn't lost. He'd signed them. She could see the signature in the memory, quick and slanted, at the bottom of the warrant that sent forty-one people to the fire—people whose only crime was worshipping a thing that had asked, politely, in the dark beneath an orchard, whether it might answer the charges against it.

*May I answer them?*

"No."

Serah set down the tongs. Her hands were steady. That was the training, and the training held even now, even here, and she hated the training a little for it. A woman whose entire inheritance had just inverted ought to shake. Ought to drop something, scream something, give the watching acolytes some human signal that the world had tilted.

Instead she heard herself say, in her flat command voice, "Prepare the second stage."

Because here was the thing—the thing she would examine later, or never: the evidence changed nothing operational. The seed was still the seed. The Thorn was still the Thorn. Whether her ancestor had been a martyr or a murderer had no bearing on whether a sleeping god should be permitted to wake. She could hold both truths. She had held worse.

But the rite required a name. The Unmaking's central invocation named the target—named it truly, named its nature, because you could not unmake what you misidentified. Three hundred years of doctrine said the words: *Thorn, remnant of the war, enemy of the peace, I name you and I end you.*

And the seed had just shown her that the name was false. Enemy of the peace. The peace was a cell. The peace was a man saying *no* to a question that deserved an answer, and then going home to a farmstead where his children would be raised on a story about how *they* were the victims.

If she spoke the doctrinal words, the rite would proceed. Doctrine did not require her belief; that was the whole point of doctrine. The circle was chalked. The acolytes were waiting. Somewhere out in the starless dark, Mara Dravenmoor was presumably crawling toward this city with her ledger and her grief, and every hour Serah delayed was an hour closer to interference.

"Third Chair?" The senior acolyte, at the edge of the light. Young. Earnest. Looking at her the way she had once looked at her own superiors, hungry for certainty to borrow. "Shall we begin?"

Serah looked down at the seed. It had closed again, seamless, patient. Waiting, she thought, the way it had waited in its iron box for weeks. Not struggling. Watching. It had shown her exactly one memory—precise, surgical, aimed—and it had shown her nothing else, and it had asked for nothing, and somehow that restraint was more persuasive than any argument. The Thornbound offered her belonging. The Crown offered her purpose. The seed had offered her only the truth, unadorned, take it or leave it.

Like a fair judge. Like a thing that had once, before the war, before the corruption, before the sealing—held court.

"The ninth ring," she heard herself say. "The texts say the ninth verse is lost."

"It is, Third Chair. Sealed or never written."

"Or removed." She turned to face him, and her voice did not shake, and she wished to God it would. "Send a rider to the Chapterhouse. Full access to the sealed archive, my seal, tonight. I want every document touching the Interregnum tribunals, the founding of the Warden's Guild, and the Vane warrants of 214. And ready a second courier for the road south."

"South, Third Chair?"

"There's a valley called Thornvale," Serah Vane said, "and I suspect the answer has been sitting in its archive for three hundred years, and I intend to know what my family actually did before I speak one word of this rite." She picked the seed up from the circle—bare-handed, protocol abandoned, feeling its small wooden warmth against her palm like a pulse—and closed her fingers around it. "Delay the ceremony. If anyone asks, the geometry was flawed."

The acolyte hesitated. "And if the Crown asks?"

"Then tell them Third Chair Vane has gone to consult her ancestors," she said, and walked out of the throne room with the seed in her fist, into a city of looping dead and a sky with no stars, toward the one thing she had spent her whole life avoiding:

the possibility that she had been on the wrong side of a story her family had written themselves.
