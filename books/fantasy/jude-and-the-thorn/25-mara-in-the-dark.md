## 25. Mara in the Dark

She woke in a crypt with no name for herself.

That was the first thing. Not pain—though pain came later, blooming slow through her ribs like ink through water—but the absence. She lay on cold stone and reached inward for *Mara Dravenmoor, last apprentice of the Warden's Guild of Thornvale* and found nothing where it should have been. No Bramwell's face. No orchard. No mother's voice singing ward-songs over a cradle she could no longer picture. The memories weren't gone the way forgotten things are gone, dusty and retrievable. They were gone the way teeth are gone. She kept reaching with her tongue into the empty spaces.

She sat up. Her hands shook. There was a bruise on her palm shaped like a star.

Someone had dragged her here—she knew that much without knowing how she knew it. The crypt was deep beneath the ruined palace, one chamber among hundreds, and its walls were lined with niches where the dead of the old capital had been laid in their finery. Dust had taken them. Their bones were gray suggestions under gray cloth. Above each niche hung a small brass plate, and the plates bore names, and the names meant nothing to her at all.

"Think," she said aloud, because silence was worse. "You can think."

She could think. That was the horror of it—her mind worked fine. It was only the furniture that had been carried out. She could remember what a ward-song was *for*, in the abstract, the way you can remember what a word means in a language you no longer speak. She could remember that she had been traveling. That there had been a boy named Wren who laughed wrong when he was scared. That there had been a woman in grey with courteous eyes who wanted something she'd carried. But the remembering was all edges and no center. A map of a country that had burned.

*The seed.*

Her hand went to her chest, pressing flat against her sternum as if she might feel it humming beneath the bone. Nothing. Of course nothing. Vane had it. Wren had given it to her—or Wren had tried to take it back, or Wren had—

The shape of the betrayal floated there, complete and bloodless, like a diagram in an anatomy text. She could see every part of it except what it had cost her. She knew she should be weeping. She knew that somewhere inside her there had been a girl who loved that boy, or trusted him, or both, and that girl's grief was a room she now stood outside of, looking in through a window at an empty chair.

"I don't even get to hate him properly," she said to the dead. "He took the seed and I can't even remember why I followed him."

The dead said nothing. The dead of this city never said nothing, actually—that was the thing about them. But tonight they were quiet, and she understood dimly that their loops ran above, in the halls and courts where they'd died, not down here among the honest bones.

She got up because lying down felt like agreeing to something.

* * *

The crypt had three exits: the stair she'd come by, a collapsed passage choked with rubble, and a low door of blackened oak that opened onto a corridor she did not explore, because exploring required wanting things and wanting hurt. Wanting hurt because every want pointed backward. She wanted water; the thought of water brought the taste of a well she couldn't see. She wanted warmth; warmth was a hearth in a house whose walls had been scooped out of her. Every small need of the body tugged a thread that led back into the dark where her life used to be, and pulling on those threads only made the holes bigger.

So she sat against the wall instead and rationed herself, the way she'd once rationed memories for the seed—except now she was rationing the dregs. What do you spend when the purse is empty? She spent the present. She counted the niches. Four hundred and six. She counted the brass plates until her eyes blurred. She recited numbers, prime numbers, because primes had no associations, no ghosts attached. Two, three, five, seven. Eleven. Thirteen.

Somewhere around two hundred and twenty-nine she stopped, because she realized she was waiting to die, politely, quietly, the way Bramwell must have waited in his fever—and the thought of Bramwell slid off her like water off glass, leaving no ache at all, and that absence was the worst thing yet. Grief requires a face. She had traded hers away in a storm, or spent it crossing a river, or lost it to a priestess with kind mad eyes. She didn't know which. She didn't know which trades she'd made, only that her purse held lint.

"They took everything," she told niche number ninety-one. "And the joke is, I gave most of it away myself. Voluntarily. Memory by memory. To buy miles."

Ninety-one did not answer.

"To save a seed," she went on, because talking was breathing, "for a thing I can't remember believing in. For a valley I can't picture. For people whose names I—" She stopped. Tried. *Bramwell.* The word came. It was just a word now, a label on an empty jar. *Wren.* Same. *Vane.* Same. *Ilse.* Same. Even her own name came when she called it, but it arrived like a stranger's coat left behind in a rented room: *Mara.* She put it on. It fit badly.

Here is what no one tells you about being emptied: it isn't the big losses that break you. It's the small ones. She could not remember whether she liked apples. She could not remember if she was afraid of heights. She could not remember her own laugh, and she suspected—without evidence, without anything but the hollow way the suspicion landed—that she had once laughed easily, that someone had told her so, that someone had loved that about her, and now that someone's love was a coin spent on a road she couldn't see.

She pressed her forehead to her knees.

*I could just stay here.*

The thought arrived gently, the way surrender always does—not as defeat but as rest. Stay in the crypt. The dead don't ask questions. In a few days thirst would finish the work the Inquisitors started, and it would be quiet, and no one would mourn her because no one living knew her, and the version of her worth mourning had been sold piecemeal across the deadlands to buy passage for a seed that now belonged to Serah Vane.

It would be so easy.

Her thumb found the star-shaped bruise on her palm and pressed it, and the pain was clean and real and hers—the first thing in hours that belonged to her outright, unspent, unbargained. She held onto it like a rope.

"Not yet," she whispered. Not to the dead. Not to herself. To whatever was left in the bottom of her when everything else was gone.

* * *

She heard them before she saw them.

A sound like many voices speaking at once, but arranged—layered the way a choir is layered, each voice holding its own line. It drifted down from the corridor behind the blackened door, and it had a cadence she recognized in her body before her mind caught up: the cadence of procedure. Of formality. Of a court in session.

She should have hidden. She had no reason not to hide. But her legs took her to the door instead, and her hand pushed it open, and she followed the voices up a narrow stair into the dark of the palace's under-halls, because some part of her—some part too stubborn to have been spent along the road—needed to know what a dead city sounded like when it talked.

The hall above was vast. Moonless black, except that it wasn't black: figures stood in it, translucent as breath on glass, ranked in tiers the way a courtroom ranks its benches. Ghost-echoes. The capital's dead, caught mid-loop, replaying their final hours forever. She had walked among them before—she knew that the way you know a fact in a book—and she had seen them repeat their deaths, their arguments, their last small human business. They were supposed to be fixed. Recorded. Playing back.

They were looking at her.

Every pale face in the tiered dark had turned. And the loop-sound had stopped, and in its place was silence so total she could hear her own wet breathing and the tick of her heart working too hard.

Then a voice spoke from the highest tier. An old voice, worn smooth as a stair-stone.

"Warden."

Not *Mara*. Not *girl*. Not *thief of seeds* or *bearer* or any of the words the living had spent months pinning to her like labels on evidence.

Warden.

"You're late," said a second voice, dry, almost amused. "Again. You were late to the founding session too. Some things hold."

"I—" Her voice cracked. She hadn't spoken to anyone in—how long? A day? Three? "I'm not—I don't know what you think I am. I'm nobody. I had a—a title, maybe. Someone else has the—the thing I was carrying. I lost it. Or it was taken. I don't fully remember." She heard how it sounded. She laughed, and the laugh came out broken. "I don't even remember enough to be ashamed properly."

"We do not recognize the woman," said the old voice, unhurried, terrible in its gentleness. "We recognize the office."

Another echo, younger, sharper: "The office does not depend on the woman. The woman depends on the office. This is the first lesson of the bench. Apparently it must also be the last."

"I don't understand."

"No," said the old voice. "You wouldn't. They stopped teaching it. That was rather the point of everything that went wrong." A pause, and then the figure at the top of the tiers descended—not walking, exactly; the darkness between where it was and where it chose to be simply closed—and stood before her, close enough that she could see it had been a woman once, tall, with ink stains ghosting her fingers. "Look at me, child. What do you see?"

"A ghost," Mara said. "An echo. A—a recording of someone dead."

"Incorrect. Try again. You are permitted three attempts. That is also tradition."

Mara looked harder. The figure was faintly luminous, yes, but it wasn't flickering the way the loop-echoes flickered, stuck and skipping. This one moved continuously. Deliberately. And its eyes—its eyes were focused, present, aimed at her with an attention that no recording could manage.

"You're... still doing your job."

Something like satisfaction moved through the assembled echoes, a ripple, a murmur like wind through wheat.

"The loops," the old woman said, "are what happens to those who died with unfinished sentences in their mouths. We died with ours finished. We finished the tribunal. We passed sentence. We sealed our verdict with our lives when the palace fell on our heads, and because our work was *complete*, we were not condemned to replay it. We remained. As witnesses remain." She tilted her head. "And a witness who remains, remains for a purpose. We have waited a hundred years for the office to walk back into this room."

"I told you. The seed is gone. Vane has it. Whatever the Warden was supposed to do, I can't do it anymore. I'm—" She gestured at herself, at the hollow of her. "Empty. You're addressing an empty jar."

"Mm," said the old woman, in the tone of a judge who has heard a thousand bad defenses. "When a ledger burns, is the debt erased?"

"That's not—it's different."

"When a seal is broken, does the law it sealed cease to exist?"

"It's *different*—"

"When a sword is dropped in battle, does the war end?" The old woman's voice didn't rise. It didn't need to. "You are thinking like a courier. The seed is cargo. The memories are cargo. You are thinking that you were the cart, and the cart is emptied, and therefore the journey is void. But the office was never the cargo, child. The office is the *road*. Roads do not empty. Roads wait."

Around the tiers, the other echoes had begun to speak—not to her, to each other, low and formal, resuming something. She caught fragments. *—the ninth convening—* *—the office persists, precedent holds—* *—summon the record, summon the record—* It sounded like a court waking from a century's recess, clerks calling to clerks, and the sound raised gooseflesh on her arms because it was *procedure*, it was the thing she had been raised inside of without ever being told, ward-songs that were really statutes, verses that were really articles, a guild that was really a court in exile.

"Why me?" she asked, and hated how small it came out. "There must have been others. Heirs. Candidates. Anyone whole."

"There were none whole," said a new voice, further down, bitter. "The guild saw to that. They hid the heir in plain sight and salted the ground around her. That was their genius and their crime."

"And the ones who broke," said another, "broke toward the seed or against it. Zealots both. The office cannot be held by zealots. It requires someone who has been emptied."

"That's insane," Mara said.

"It is jurisprudence," said the old woman. "A vessel that is full can only pour out what was poured in. A vessel that is empty can receive. You arrive with nothing—no faction, no inheritance in your heart, no stolen certainties. Do you imagine that is damage? Child, that is *qualification*."

Mara's legs were shaking. She sat down on the steps where she stood, because standing had become negotiable, and looked at her empty palms, and the star-shaped bruise, and the hundred-year dust.

"I don't remember my teacher's face," she said. "I don't remember my home. I don't remember why I started any of this. If you give me this—if you call me Warden and expect me to *do* the Warden—I'll be improvising. I'll be guessing. I'll fail you. Whoever I was, whoever you're honoring, she's gone. You're getting the residue."

"Yes," agreed the old woman. "We are getting the residue. And a hundred years ago, we got the pride, and the fear, and the certainty—and look what those built. A cage. A stolen verdict. A valley taught to sing lullabies over a prisoner." She extended her ink-stained hand, not to shake—to offer, the way you offer a hand to someone at the bottom of a stair. "The woman who sentenced the Thorn was full to bursting with rightness. We have had a century to study the results. Send us someone empty. Send us someone who knows what it costs to lose everything, who will weigh loss before she weighs judgment. The office does not require your past, Warden. It requires your attention. Have you any of that left?"

Mara looked at the offered hand.

And slowly, for the first time since she'd woken in the crypt with a stranger's name, something in her chest turned over—not hope, hope was a luxury she couldn't afford, but something sturdier and less comfortable. The feeling of being *addressed*. Of mattering not because of what she carried but because of what she was willing to carry next.

"I have that," she said. "That's about all I have."

"It is the entire treasury," said the old woman. "Rise, Warden. The court reconvenes."

Mara took the ghost's hand. It was cool, and weightless, and it pulled her to her feet anyway—and the moment she stood, every echo in the tiers bowed at once, four hundred years of dead judges inclining together, and the sound they made was not applause.

It was a gavel.

Far above, muffled through a hundred feet of stone, boots were moving—many boots, disciplined, descending through the palace toward the crypts. Vane had found the tribunal records. Vane was coming to end this before it began.

The old woman turned her head toward the sound, and her translucent mouth curved.

"Good," she said. "We were worried she'd lost her nerve."
