## 26. Wren's Choice

Wren had stolen a lot of things in his life. A loaf of bread when he was six and still believed the world owed him something. A lockpick set from a man who'd beaten him for it and then taught him how to use it anyway, because that was the kind of teacher Corvus ran. A silver hairpin off a duchess at a funeral, which he'd kept for years until he traded it for boots.

He'd never stolen anything back before.

That was different. That was a whole different animal, and it was sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone as he lay flat on the ridge above the Crown camp and counted torches through the dark.

Forty-two guards on the perimeter. Six on the supply wagons. Two on the command tent, where Serah Vane slept with the seed in an iron box beside her pillow, because of course she did—she was the only person in that whole army who understood what she was carrying, and understanding made people careful.

Wren had been careful his whole life too. It hadn't saved anyone.

* * *

He'd told himself he wasn't going to do this.

He'd told himself that walking out of Mara's camp three days ago, after she'd looked at him with those hollowed-out eyes—all her memories scraped down to nothing but duty and grief—and said, "You should go, Wren. There's nothing left here to steal."

She hadn't meant it as a joke. He'd laughed anyway, because that's what he did, and then he'd walked east toward the coast like a sensible man, and sensible men don't turn around.

He turned around at the crossroads shrine where he'd once paid a ferryman a memory of rain on a tin roof. He stood there a long time trying to figure out why, and the honest answer—the one that sat ugly in his throat—was this: somewhere between Thornvale and the dead capital, protecting the seed had become the first thing Wren had ever done that mattered. Not the first good thing. He wasn't fool enough to call himself good. The first thing that *mattered*, where the doing of it changed the shape of the world instead of just the shape of his pocket.

And he'd sold it. For a memory of his sister that lasted eleven seconds and tasted like nothing, because memories spent by other people's magic always came back thinner than they went away. Sela had smiled at him from across a kitchen fire, and he'd held that smile like it could pay his debts, and then Vane's soldiers had taken the seed off Mara's unconscious body while Wren stood there clutching eleven seconds of smoke.

He'd tried to give it back. He'd walked into the camp the next morning, hands empty, and found Mara gone—walked into the dark looking for her—and come up against Crown pickets instead. Turned away twice. Nearly killed the third time.

So now: the fourth time, over the ridge, under the moonless sky that the whole world had gotten used to and that Wren still couldn't look at without flinching. Forty-two guards. One thief.

The math was bad. He knew it was bad. He did it again anyway, because numbers were honest and everything else tonight was a lie he was choosing on purpose.

* * *

He went down through the drainage cut on the north side, the one he'd marked the night before when he was still telling himself he was only scouting. The cut was knee-deep in ash-melt and old flood silt, and it stank of the river's poison even this far upstream, and it came up inside the picket line behind the horse lines, which was the one place in a military camp where nobody looks, because horses are boring.

He waited under a wagon bed for the watch change. Counted to two hundred. Slid out.

The command tent glowed faintly from within—a lamp behind canvas, Vane working late, because people who carry their shame in an iron box don't sleep much either. Wren knew that about her. It was the worst thing about her, that he kept finding pieces of himself in her. Her family had been burned for Thornbound blood; his had been sold by a guild for ward-fodder. She'd chosen the people who did the burning. He'd run from everyone who did anything. They'd both ended up in the same mud outside the same ruined city, wanting the same iron box, for reasons that almost rhymed.

Almost. Hers ended with destruction. His didn't have to.

Two guards at the tent door, backs to each other, facing out. Professional. But professionals guard against armies, not against one thin man coming up through the anchor-ropes on the tent's blind side with a knife between his teeth and a prayer he didn't believe in stuck behind them.

He lifted the canvas edge with a sliver of whalebone—he'd stopped using knives for canvas years ago, after a sentry heard the cut—and looked in.

Vane was asleep at the desk. Actually asleep, head on her folded arms, ink drying on a half-written report. Younger-looking asleep. More tired-looking too, if such a thing was possible.

The iron box sat on the cot beside her bedroll. Plain. Locked. Three feet from her hand.

Wren crouched there in the dark of the tent and felt the whole plan go soft in his knees.

Here was the thing nobody tells you about stealing: it's easy when you hate the person you're stealing from, or when you don't know them, or when what you're taking is just things. It gets hard when the person is asleep and human and has ink stains on her fingers from writing reports at midnight about a war she inherited instead of chose.

He could kill her. The thought arrived uninvited, the way those thoughts do. She was right there. One knife, one motion, no more Inquisitor, no more hunt, maybe no more army marching on whatever Mara was trying to build in that crypt. And Wren had killed before—one time, necessary, a man from Corvus's guild who'd caught him skimming—and he remembered every second of it the way you remember a bone you broke.

"No," he mouthed, to himself, to the tent, to whoever kept score.

Not because she deserved mercy. Because killing her would make this a theft after all, and he needed it not to be. He needed to walk in there and walk out with the seed and be able to tell Mara—I didn't take it, I gave it back. There was a difference, and the difference was the entire point, and he clung to that difference like a rope over a drop.

The lock was Crown work but the box was old, older than the Crown's current smiths, guild-made probably—ward-guild, maybe even Thornvale work—and old locks have habits. He listened to it with his picks the way Bramwell must have listened to wards, feeling for the tumbler that wanted to stick, easing it, waiting for the little sigh of metal giving up its habit.

It opened without a sound.

Inside, on black velvet, the seed sat like a dying coal. Not glowing, exactly. Remembering light. Up close he could feel it against his skin, a warmth with weight to it, and underneath the warmth something almost like breathing. Like something asleep that trusted the person holding it, and hadn't decided about him yet.

"Hello," Wren whispered, because apparently he talked to magical objects now, and because it was true: hello. We meet again. I owe you an apology.

The warmth flickered. Just once. Like a heartbeat noticing him.

Then the lamp behind Vane's arms guttered, and she stirred, and Wren did the thing he was actually best at in all the world: he vanished. Out through the anchor-gap, flat along the ground, into the drainage cut, down through the ash-melt, moving fast and low while behind him the camp stayed asleep around its missing heart.

* * *

They woke up anyway. Of course they did. Somewhere around the third bell a horse screamed—not his fault, he swore later, some animal sense of wrongness—and the camp boiled up like a kicked hive, torches blooming along the perimeter, horns starting that flat two-note alarm he'd learned to dread.

Wren ran.

Not cleverly. Not with routes and contingencies. He ran like a man being chased by his own decision, straight up the slope toward the ruined palace, because the crypt was there and Mara was there and the distance between him and the thing he owed was measured in exactly that many steps.

A crossbow bolt took the ground a foot from his heel. Another clipped his shoulder—not a hit, a graze, tearing sleeve and skin—and he ducked behind a fallen colonnade drum and breathed and thought, finally, like a professional.

They'd ring the palace. Vane would guess the destination in about thirty seconds; she was smarter than everyone else in her army combined, including him. Which meant the direct road was closed and every gate would have eyes on it within minutes.

But Wren had grown up in a city built by people who hated each other, in the warrens under it, in the spaces buildings leave when they're ashamed of themselves. And the capital's ruins were the same kind of city, just deader. Every fortress shows you its front door so you'll forget it has a spine. He'd seen the collapsed processional way on his first day here, seen the crack where the aqueduct fed into the palace understructure, seen water stains meaning water still moved down there, meaning space, meaning a way in.

He went down through the crack with the seed pressed to his chest and the army's torches shrinking above him like a sky full of angry orange stars, since the real ones weren't available.

* * *

The underways were dark in the old way, the complete way, and his spent-memory soul knew that dark intimately. He navigated by touch and trickle-sound and the seed's warmth, which grew as he went deeper, patient, directional, like a compass that wanted something.

His shoulder bled. His legs shook. At some point he sat down against wet stone in the absolute black and let himself shake properly, the delayed terror of the bolt and the tent and Vane's sleeping face all arriving at once, and he held the seed and shook, and the seed warmed against his palms, and that was somehow worse than comfort—it was kindness, from the thing he'd betrayed, and he didn't deserve kindness, he'd earned a knife between the ribs and gotten a warm coal instead.

"I'm not doing it for forgiveness," he told the dark. "Don't get any ideas. I'm doing it because it's right. There's a difference."

The seed pulsed, slow, unbothered. Things as old as it probably didn't distinguish. Or maybe they distinguished better than people did. He honestly didn't know anymore.

He got up. Kept walking.

* * *

He found her by sound.

She was in the tribunal chamber—the great cracked hall where the ghost-echoes replayed the old trial on loop—and she was arguing with the dead. Her voice carried down the corridor, raw and cracked itself, worn down to wire: "—then show me the sentence! Show me the lawful sentence! You can't, can you? Because there wasn't one—"

Wren stopped in the doorway.

Mara Dravenmoor, last apprentice of the Warden's Guild, stood in a shaft of pale echo-light with her back to him, and she looked smaller than he'd ever seen her and taller too, both at once, the way people look when they've been broken and reassembled along different lines. The echoes drifted around her—grey figures mid-gesture, mouths open on words four hundred years stale—and she stood in the middle of them demanding justice from a room that had never given it, and Wren's chest did something complicated and painful.

He'd come to return an object. Standing in that doorway, he understood he was returning something else, and that the something else had a name he didn't want to say out loud yet.

His boot scraped stone.

She spun. He watched recognition arrive, then disbelief, then something guarded and furious and hopeful all fighting for her face.

"You," she said.

"Me." He held up the seed. Its warmth lit his fingers faintly, the first light in that hall that belonged to neither torch nor echo. "Delivery."

She crossed the floor fast—too fast, stumbling on rubble, catching herself—and stopped an arm's length away, staring at the seed like it might dissolve. "How?"

"Broke into Vane's tent. She was asleep." He shrugged, and immediately regretted it because his shoulder reminded him it had recently met a crossbow bolt. "Ink on her fingers. Sad desk-lamp situation. Very humanizing. Made it harder, frankly."

"Wren." His name in her mouth, flat and dangerous. "Why?"

And here was the moment he'd rehearsed for three days, all the jokes lined up like lockpicks, all the deflections, the shrug-and-grin that had gotten him through every bad night of his life.

He put them all away.

"Because I took something that wasn't mine to take," he said. "Your sister-memory trick doesn't work on me, so Vane bought me with my own sister instead, and I let her. Eleven seconds, Mara. I traded your whole future for eleven seconds of a kitchen fire." His voice went rough and he let it. "I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm not even sure I'd want you to—that'd be too easy, and I've had too much easy in my life. I'm asking you to take it back and finish what you started, because it's right. That's the whole speech. That's all I've got."

Silence. The echoes murmured their ancient loop around them. Mara looked at him for a long time, and whatever moved behind her eyes, most of her memories were gone and she was running on the bones of herself, and somehow that made her gaze worse—cleaner. Harder to hide from.

"You're bleeding," she said finally.

"Bolt grazed. Barely counts."

"And you came back through the underways alone, past an army, with no plan past the door."

"I had a plan. The plan was you'd be here yelling at ghosts, and you were." 

Something cracked in her expression—not quite a smile, but the place where smiles live. She reached out and took the seed from his palm, and the moment her skin touched it the warmth bloomed visibly, a soft gold pulse rolling outward through the ruined hall, and every grey echo in the chamber stopped mid-loop and turned its head.

All of them. At once. Looking at Mara.

Or—at the seed. Or at the pair of them holding it together, thief and warden, betrayer and keeper, and Wren felt the hair rise on his neck because in four hundred years of looping, the dead had never once looked at anything new.

"Mara," he said quietly.

"I see them."

One echo detached from the rest. An old man, robed, grey as fog, and it walked—*walked*, off-script, off-loop—across the tribunal floor until it stood before them, and its mouth moved, and for the first time the capital's dead spoke words that hadn't already happened.

"*Warden,*" the echo said, in a voice like wind through a crypt. "*The ninth verse waits. And the armies are coming.*"

Far above, muffled through tons of stone, Wren heard it too: horns. Lots of them. Two sets, in two different keys—Crown calls from the south, and from the north, wilder, stranger, something that could only be the Thornbound answering.

Mara closed her hand around the seed and lifted her chin, and the hollowed-out woman from three days ago was suddenly nowhere in sight.

"Then we'd better hurry," she said.
