## 27. The Ninth Verse

The crypt beneath the palace did not smell of death.

It smelled of apples.

Mara stood on the third stair down, one hand pressed flat against the cold stone wall, and breathed it in—green and sweet and impossible—and felt something in her chest crack along a seam she hadn't known was there. She couldn't remember why the smell mattered. She knew that it mattered the way you know a wound is yours even after the memory of how you got it has been taken from you.

Behind her, Wren's boots scraped the stair. "Tell me again why we're going deeper," he said. "Every time we go deeper in this city, something tries to eat us."

"Because the tribunal echoes came from below." She kept her voice level. Below them, in the ruined halls above, the dead still walked their last moments—the courtiers frozen mid-flee, the guards replaying their final orders—but here at the crypt's mouth the loops thinned, as if whatever anchored them had its roots further down. "And because Bramwell sent me here."

"Bramwell's dead."

"Bramwell left me a ledger with half its pages torn out and a dying instruction I didn't understand until three hundred miles too late." She started down. "*Find what we buried deepest.* I thought he meant the Thorn. He didn't. The Thorn was never buried deepest. It was buried shallow, under an orchard, where everyone could tend it without looking at it." Her boot found the next step. "What he buried deepest is this."

The stairs went down farther than the palace had gone up. Mara counted two hundred steps before the walls changed—from quarried grey to older stone, darker, veined with lines that might have been carving or might have been veins—and the air warmed by a degree she could not explain and chose not to fear. The seed had been quiet since they'd entered the capital. Not silent—she'd learned the difference over the long miles of the deadlands, learned to feel its attention like weather on her skin. Quiet was the seed listening. She no longer trusted quiet.

"You're doing the thing," Wren said behind her.

"What thing?"

"The face. The one where you've decided something terrible is a good idea and you're daring the world to argue." A pause. "It's a good face. Very wardenly. Still hate it."

She almost smiled. Almost. "Keep watch at the top of the stairs."

"Not a chance. You go into the creepy apple-scented hole alone, I'm coming. That's not loyalty, that's self-interest. If something eats you, I have to walk out of these deadlands by myself, and I've seen the price of memories lately." His voice dropped, roughened. "I'm not spending another one getting home."

She let that stand between them, because arguing would have cost her more composure than she had left. What remained of her childhood fit inside a thimble now—a handful of images, a name or two, the shape of feelings whose causes were gone. She hoarded them like a miser. Every step deeper into the crypt felt like walking on the ice over her own past.

* * *

The crypt opened without warning.

One moment corridor, the next a chamber vast enough that the light of Mara's lantern failed to find its ceiling. Pillars rose around them in concentric rings—not pillars, she realized as she lifted the flame higher, but *stelae*, each one carved floor to unseen height with script so dense it looked like rain frozen mid-fall. And in the center of the rings, on a low plinth of black glass, lay a book.

Not a grand book. That was the first wrongness of it. She had expected, after everything—the war, the cage, the centuries of silence—something monumental. Instead: a slim volume bound in grey leather, worn at the corners, small enough to hold in two hands. The kind of book a man writes when he expects no one will ever read it but cannot bear for the truth to die with him.

Beneath it, etched into the plinth itself, a single line:

*Here lies the ninth verse. Forgive us for burying justice.*

Mara's breath fogged the cold air. She crossed the ring of stelae slowly, reading as she went, and the script resolved as she drew near—not old-tongue, not guild cipher, but plain trade-script, the language of ledgers and grain prices and letters home. Whoever had carved these had wanted them understood by anyone. By *everyone*. That had been the point, once. That had been the whole point.

She read the nearest stele aloud, because some things need a voice to be real.

"*In the four hundredth year of the Accord, the entity called the Thorn was brought before no court. It was offered no counsel, no accuser's list, no right of answer. Nine wardens voted in secret. Eight feared it. One feared what we were becoming.*"

Her voice caught. She steadied it.

"The ninth verse isn't a ward-song," she said. "Wren. It's not a song at all. It's a *procedure*."

"I understood maybe six of those words."

She knelt beside the plinth. Her hands shook as she reached for the book—not from cold. From recognition. She knew that binding. She knew that grey leather, that stitching pattern, the way the corners were wrapped twice against damp. She had spent her whole life handling ledgers bound exactly this way in the Thornvale archive, told they were guild-standard, guild-old, guild-anonymous.

They had all been his. Every record worth keeping in that archive bore his hands somewhere in its making. She could not remember his face—she had spent that memory in a storm on the deadlands road, trading it for her life, and the loss of it was a tooth-shaped absence she touched with her mind the way a tongue finds a socket—but her hands remembered his craft. Her hands remembered *him*, even where she could not.

She opened the book.

*If you are reading this, then the wards have fallen, and you are either the heir or the fool who followed the heir down. Both are welcome. I wrote this for both.*

*My name is Jude Halloran. I was the first Warden of Thornvale. Before that, I was the ninth vote—the one that refused.*

*You will have been taught, as every warden was taught, that we protect. This is the first lie. We do not protect. We never protected. Protection was the story we told the valley so the valley would help us keep the door shut.*

*Our purpose was patience. Not the patience of guardians—the patience of cowards waiting for a problem to become someone else's. We sealed the Thorn without trial because a trial would have required us to hear it. To hear it would have required us to answer questions about our own conduct in the war. The Crown did not want answers. Neither did the Guilds. So we built a cage and called it a cradle, and we taught our children songs with eight verses, and we cut out the ninth, and we called the cutting mercy.*

Mara sat down on the cold floor. She didn't decide to; her legs simply ended their argument with her. Wren lowered himself beside her, close enough that his shoulder nearly touched hers, and said nothing, which from Wren was its own kind of verse.

*But I could not cut it out of myself,* the book continued. *So I wrote the ninth verse here, in full, in the plainest tongue I knew, and I hid it beneath the capital where the Thorn was made, trusting that if anyone ever came far enough and cared enough to find it, they would be the right person to read it.*

*The ninth verse does not cage. Caging was verses one through eight, and look what they bought us: a sleeping god under an orchard and a sky going dark one star at a time.*

*The ninth verse does not kill. Killing requires the courage we lacked.*

*The ninth verse convenes.*

*It opens the cage and builds, in the space where the cage was, a court. The old tribunal—true one, the one the ghosts of this city still rehearse in their death-loops because they died waiting for it to be honest. The verse names a judge. It names an accuser. It names a voice for the accused—for the accused must always be given a voice, even, *especially*, when everyone agrees it deserves none.*

*I named no one in this book. Names rot. But if the wards are fallen and you carry the seed, then the choice has already been made for you, and I am sorry for it, and I am proud of you, and I have never met you, and I love you the way one loves a student one will never see: desperately, and with total confidence that you will do it better than I did.*

*Sing the nine together. All of them. In order. The first eight bind the court to truth; the ninth opens the doors.*

*One warning, and mark it well: a fair trial can end in any verdict. That is what makes it fair. If you cannot accept that the Thorn might walk free at the end of this—if you need it caged or destroyed or crowned—then close this book and leave, because you will only make another cage, and cages are how we got here.*

*A fair reckoning is the only inheritance worth leaving. Everything else is just a nicer word for vengeance.*

*—J.H.*

Mara closed the book and held it against her sternum and did not move for a long time.

*Jude.* The word turned over in her like a coin showing a new face. She had carried the title *Jude and the Thorn* through her head for days now—words that had surfaced from the ledger's torn pages, meaning nothing, meaning everything, and she had assumed Jude was the Thorn's true name, the name before the war dirtied it. She had been wrong, or only half right. She would work out which later. Later there would be time. Right now there was only the weight of the book and the smell of apples rising from nowhere and the understanding, arriving whole and merciless, of what Bramwell—of what *Jude*—had spent a century preparing her for.

He had known. From the beginning. He had chosen an orphan with no family and no faction and no future, raised her invisible among a dying guild, taught her eight verses and withheld the ninth—not to hide it from her, but so that she would find it herself, in the place it was hidden, at the moment it was needed, carrying the proof of her own authority in her own blood. He had built her a path and then gotten out of the way.

"You're crying," Wren observed quietly.

"No I'm not."

"It's fine. Everyone cries in crypts. It's practically the law." He squinted up at the stelae. "So the old man wrote a court case instead of a weapon."

"He wrote the thing nobody had the nerve to do." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, impatient with herself, and stood. "Four hundred years ago, nine wardens decided they were too afraid to hold a trial. So the trial never happened. The war never ended—it just stopped being spoken aloud. And every horror since, every purge, every exile, every burned village—" she thought of Vane's ledger, of Ilse's visions, of the Thornbound camps and the Inquisitors' pyres, "—every one of them grew out of that silence. Because nobody was willing to stand in a room and say: *we were wrong, and so were you, and here is what actually happened.*"

"And now you're going to say it."

"Now I'm going to *convene* it." She looked down at the seed where it rested against her skin, dim and patient, and for the first time since the night the stars vanished, she spoke to it directly. "You heard him. You get a voice. An accuser gets a voice. A judge. And a verdict that could go any way—including against me. That's the deal. That's the only deal on offer."

The seed warmed against her palm. Not speech. Not yet. Something more like a held breath—or perhaps, she thought, the stillness of someone who has waited four hundred years in a courtroom and just heard their case called.

* * *

They worked through the night.

By lantern-light, Mara transcribed the ninth verse from the stelae while Wren climbed the pillar-rings, calling out gaps where centuries of damp had eaten the script. There were gaps. Of course there were gaps. But the book filled most of them—Jude had written it three times over, paranoid and thorough, and where the stone failed, the grey leather held. Twice Mara found phrases she already knew: fragments braided into the eight ward-songs she'd sung since childhood, disguised as ornament, as grace notes, as nothing. The ninth verse had never been wholly lost. It had been *distributed*. Hidden in plain hearing, sung by generations of wardens who didn't know they were rehearsing a trial.

"He hid it in the songs," she whispered. "He hid it in the *songs*, Wren. Every warden who ever tended those wards was carrying a piece of this. The guild wasn't just keeping the cage. It was keeping the key, and it never knew."

"Old man sounds like my kind of thief." Wren dropped from the lowest stele, brushing dust from his knees. "Except for the part where he waited a century to use the loot."

"He was waiting for the wards to fall. They weren't supposed to fall this soon." She paused over a line near the book's end, one she'd read four times now and still couldn't pass without stopping. *The judge must be someone the accused has chosen. Ask the seed. If it accepts you, you may sit. If it does not, put the book down and run.* "Wren. When we get to the tribunal—I won't just be judge. I'll be standing trial too, in a way. My guild. My inheritance. Everything I was raised on turns out to be a lie with good posture." She laughed, short and unamused. "Vane will prosecute. Ilse will speak for the Thorn. And the jury will be the ghosts of everyone the silence killed."

"Sounds festive." But his eyes were serious, and when he looked at her there was something in his face she'd learned to read on the road: the expression he wore when he was deciding whether to tell her something and choosing, again, to keep it. He'd been wearing it more often lately. She filed it away, the way she now filed everything—carefully, knowing how little room she had left.

"It'll be a massacre of reputations," she said instead. "Mine included. That's how you know it's fair."

Dawn—or what passed for dawn in a starless city, a grey thinning of the dark—found them climbing back toward the surface. At the top of the stairs Mara stopped, the book wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, and looked back down into the warm apple-smelling dark.

"Thank you," she said to the crypt. To the man in it, wherever men went when they'd finished their work. "I'll do it better than you did. Or I'll die trying, and either way the silence ends."

The dark said nothing, but the smell of blossoms strengthened for a moment, like a hand briefly squeezing hers, and then let go.

They emerged into the ruined great hall, where the ghost-echoes looped and the dust hung motionless—and stopped.

The doors at the far end of the hall stood open. Through them, across the shattered plaza, fires were burning. Real fires, new fires, ringed in patterns Mara knew from Ilse's camp: the Thornbound sigil, drawn in flame.

Hundreds of figures moved among them. And at their head, robed in white gone grey with road-dust, staff planted on the palace steps like a claim staked, stood the high priestess of the exiles, looking directly at the doors Mara stood in—as if she had known, all along, exactly where the ninth verse slept.

"Mara Dravenmoor," Ilse called, and her voice carried across the dead plaza without effort, pitched for ghosts and armies alike. "We have come to witness the waking. Open your court, little warden—or we will open it for you, and it will not be a trial. It will be a coronation."
