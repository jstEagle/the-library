## 28. Ilse Returns

They came at dusk, which was the only hour the dead capital still pretended to be alive.

Mara stood on the shattered colonnade above the palace steps and watched them cross the ruined plaza in a long grey line—two hundred, maybe more, torches burning with that green-tinged flame the Thornbound favored, the kind that gave light without warmth. Their banners were strips of unbleached wool, dyed at the hem with thorn-black ink. They moved like pilgrims and armed like soldiers, and both of those things were true, and both of those things frightened her.

"They've been gathering for three days," Wren said beside her. He'd found a vantage point earlier—a half-collapsed bell tower—and had spent the afternoon counting. "Two hundred and forty by my last count. Maybe sixty of them can actually fight. The rest are families. Old people. Kids."

"Kids," Mara repeated.

"Thornbound don't leave their children behind. That's the whole point of them, isn't it? They think they're going home." He glanced at her. "You could still run."

She thought about running. She'd been thinking about it for three days, honestly, since the seed had begun to hum against her sternum like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing, since she'd understood what the resonance meant: *something is coming to answer you.* The seed wanted to be woken. Of course it did. It had been waiting a thousand years, and now here came two hundred and forty people carrying torches and faith, ready to do exactly that.

"No," she said.

"You didn't even think about it."

"I thought about it the whole time I said no."

Wren huffed something that was almost a laugh. Below them, the Thornbound reached the base of the palace steps and stopped, as if they'd hit an invisible line—which, in a way, they had. Mara had spent the day doing the only thing she knew how to do. She had walked the ruins singing the ward-verses, all eight of the ones Bramwell had taught her, laying them down like a net over the palace's heart. Not a cage. She'd been very careful about that. A threshold. Something that would slow them, not hold them. Something that would give her time to talk instead of fight.

Whether it would work depended entirely on Ilse.

* * *

The high priestess came up the steps alone.

That was Ilse's way. Mara had learned that much about her in the Thornbound camp, before the escape that had cost her her own birthday—the strange, hollow absence where a memory used to be, like reaching for a stair that wasn't there. Ilse never sent messengers when she could deliver a thing herself. It was a form of respect, or a form of dominance, or both; with Ilse it was always impossible to separate the two.

She climbed slowly, leaning on a staff of blackthorn wood, her white hair loose down her back. Up close she looked older than Mara remembered—or maybe Mara was simply older now too, in the way that mattered. Ilse stopped six paces away, at the edge of the verses, and inclined her head.

"Warden."

"That word doesn't mean what you want it to mean."

"It means exactly what I want it to mean." Ilse's voice was dry as old paper. "It means you carry the seed. It means the Thorn chose your bloodline out of every bloodline in the world. It means you are standing between us and our god, and calling yourself a door." Her eyes, pale as frost, went past Mara to the dark mouth of the crypt stairs behind her. "Is it true? What our scouts whisper. That you found the ninth verse."

Mara kept her face still. Three days ago she hadn't known how to lie with a steady heart. Serah Vane had taught her that, in a way—every hour Mara had spent playing the ignorant apprentice while the Inquisitor smiled and asked gentle questions had been a lesson, though neither of them had known it at the time.

"What if I have?"

"Then you are holding the most dangerous words ever written, and I am asking you—begging you—to give them to me." Ilse spread her hands. The staff stayed upright, planted, but her palms were open, and Mara remembered that gesture from the camp, from the visions, from the night Ilse had shown her the war's atrocities in all their double-sided horror. "You've seen what I've seen. You know what was done to us. To *it*. A thousand years of silence in a hole under an orchard. And you would convene a *trial*?"

"Yes."

"A trial." Ilse said the word like she was tasting spoiled milk. "The guild sealed it without trial. The Crown hunted its people without trial. Your Inquisitor burned my grandmother's village without trial. And your great justice is to put the victim on trial and call it mercy."

"The tribunal goes both ways," Mara said. "You know that. I told you in the camp. The Thorn will answer for what it did. Both sides testify. The ghost-echoes remember everything—they'll be the jury. No one gets to lie, Ilse. Including it. Including you."

"Everything lies," Ilse said softly. "Memory lies. Grief lies. Even the dead, replaying their last moments forever in these streets—they lie, because dying froze them mid-sentence." She took one step closer, right up against the threshold of the verses, and Mara felt the wards register her like a hand pressed against glass. "But waking doesn't lie. Waking is simple. Wake it, and it speaks with its own voice, and no jury of ghosts gets to stand between the living and their judge."

"And if it wakes angry?" Mara's voice cracked despite herself. "You've felt what it feels like through the seed, same as I have. A thousand years in the dark, Ilse. You want to open that door and hope what comes out is grateful?"

"I want what comes out to be *free*." For a moment the zealotry fell away and there was just an old woman, exhausted, standing in a ruin. "I have buried four generations of my people waiting for this. Children born in exile who died in exile, singing songs about a god they'd never meet. If it wakes angry, then let it be angry *in the open air*, under a sky we'll give back to it star by star. Anger is honest. Your trials are just another cage with better manners."

Behind Mara, at the top of the crypt stairs, the seed pulsed. She felt it like a second heartbeat, and worse—she felt it *agree* with Ilse. Just a little. Just enough.

That was the trap she'd been living inside since chapter one of her life, if she was honest: the seed liked her. It spoke in her mother's voice. It bargained sweetly. Every mile of the deadlands it had eaten pieces of her home and given her guidance, and some traitorous part of her had started to trust it, the way you trust anything that knows your name.

"Ilse," she said. "I'm going to say this once, because I respect you, and because you showed me the truth when you didn't have to. Walk away. Take your people—take the *children*—and go south, past the river, and wait. However long the trial takes. Days. Weeks. When it's done, whatever the verdict is, you'll have your answer from its own mouth. But if you bring your army up these steps tonight, I will stop you. I don't want to. I will."

Ilse studied her for a long moment. Then she smiled, and it was the saddest smile Mara had ever seen.

"You sound like him," Ilse said. "The first Warden. Jude. Did you know that was his name? We kept his name, all these years, as a curse. The man who buried our god and called it peace." She turned, staff clicking on stone, and began to descend. "We come at moonrise, Warden. Bring your verses. Bring your ghosts. My people have been patient for a thousand years, and patience is the one thing we have left to spend."

* * *

Moonrise came early, or so it seemed, the sky still bruise-dark where stars should have been. There were no stars. There hadn't been since the night the wards fell, and Mara had stopped expecting them, the way you stop expecting a dead friend to walk through the door—but tonight, watching the green torches kindle across the plaza, she caught herself looking up anyway. Habit. Longing. The restless ache that had lived in her chest her whole life in Thornvale, back when she'd thought it was just wanting to see the world.

Maybe it had always been the seed, calling her toward this.

"Positions," Wren murmured. He'd organized the defense without being asked, because that was what Wren did—he stole structure from chaos the way he stole bread from markets. Vane's twenty Crown soldiers held the east colonnade, grey and silent, bayonets fixed on pikes. Wren had scattered the rest of their small company—himself, two defector Thornbound who'd chosen Mara's path at the ferry, and a handful of palace ghosts who could, strangely, be reasoned with—along the west wall. "They'll send the faithful forward first. Unarmed. They always do. Dares you to cut down pilgrims."

"I know."

"And behind them the fighters, and behind *them*—" he nodded at a covered litter near the rear of the Thornbound lines, "—that. Been carried the whole march. Whatever's under that cloth, it's why they're confident."

Mara looked at the litter and felt the seed shudder against her ribs. Not fear, exactly. Recognition.

"Ilse brought a piece of it," she said quietly. "A relic. Old bone or old wood, something from the war. Enough of the Thorn to anchor a waking rite." She closed her eyes. "If they get that litter to the crypt, it won't matter what I do. The ninth verse convenes a trial. It needs the accused present and *willing to attend*. A forced waking short-circuits all of it. There's no verdict. There's just—it. Loose. Angry. With two hundred worshippers telling it what it wants to hear."

"So we burn the litter."

"We don't touch the litter." She opened her eyes. "If we destroy a relic of the Thorn in front of its faithful, we confirm every story Ilse ever told them. And if the relic is what I think it is—if it's actually part of the Thorn—" she shook her head. "I'm not killing a piece of it to save the rest. That's the guild's logic, Wren. That's the whole mistake, in one sentence."

He looked at her for a long moment. "You know that's insane."

"I know."

"Good. Just checking." He rolled his shoulders and pulled his knives. "Moonrise, then."

* * *

It began, as Wren predicted, with the faithful.

They came up the steps singing—an old Thornbound hymn in a language Mara didn't speak, though the seed translated it against her heart in fragments: *sleeping king, sleeping judge, wake and weigh us.* Two hundred voices, unarmed, walking into the verses she'd laid across the palace. The wards flared pale gold and the hymn faltered—not stopped, but bent, the singers stumbling as the threshold pushed back.

"Hold!" Mara shouted, stepping out onto the colonnade, arms raised. "Hold! Nobody moves!"

For one suspended breath, the plaza went quiet except for the hymn's dying echo.

Then Ilse's staff struck the stone at the base of the steps, once, like a gavel, and the Thornbound fighters surged forward around the singers, and the night broke open.

What followed was not a battle so much as a collision of stubbornnesses. The verses held the center of the steps, glowing, and the Thornbound couldn't cross them, so they poured around the edges instead, and Vane's soldiers met them at the east colonnade with disciplined, terrible efficiency—flat of blade where they could manage it, edge where they couldn't. Mara ran the west wall, singing verse after verse, throwing thresholds up like sandbags, and each one cost her. The ninth verse wasn't a weapon; the eight verses weren't either; they were architecture, and architecture built in a hurry collapses in a hurry. Sweat froze on her spine. Somewhere below, someone screamed her name—not her name, *Warden*, the office, the ghosts of the palace crying it from windows like a chorus.

And through it all, the litter advanced.

Four bearers, moving with terrible calm through the melee, carrying their covered burden up the steps' outer edge, straight at the crypt mouth. Mara saw them and understood, with cold clarity, that this had been the plan all along. The assault was noise. The hymn was noise. *She* was noise. Ilse had never needed to beat Mara's defenses. She only needed to get four patient men and a piece of her god past them while everyone else was busy being brave.

"Wren!" Mara pointed. "The litter!"

"On it—"

"*Don't touch it!* Just—slow them! Slow them however you can!"

He grinned, wild, and went over the wall like water going over a ledge.

Mara turned and ran for the crypt stairs, taking them three at a time, down into the dark beneath the palace where the tribunal chamber waited with its benches of ghost-light and its empty judge's seat. The seed was screaming now, or she was; there was no difference anymore. Behind her, above her, the battle roared, and somewhere in it Ilse's voice rose clear and terrible, chanting the waking rite in that dead language, and the seed answered it whether Mara willed it or not—pulsing, pulling, *leaning* toward the relic like a compass needle finding north.

She burst into the tribunal chamber and slammed her palm against the judge's seat, and sang.

Not the ninth verse. Not yet. The ninth verse needed witnesses, needed the accused present, needed a quorum of truth—and none of that existed yet. So she sang the first verse, the oldest one, the one Bramwell had taught her when she was seven years old and thought it was just a song about keeping orchards safe. *Root and rain and patient stone.* The chamber lit up around her, gold flooding the ghost-benches, and the palace itself groaned awake around her, every dead echo in every corridor turning its face toward the sound.

Above, the chanting stuttered.

Below, the seed went quiet—listening.

And at the top of the crypt stairs, framed against firelight and the chaos of the failing battle, Ilse appeared, alone, having abandoned her own army the moment she felt the palace wake. She looked down at Mara standing in the ancient seat of judgment, and her face did something complicated—fury and awe fighting for the same ground.

"You'd wake it *yourself*," Ilse breathed. "Without the rite. Without consent. After everything—you're just another Warden after all."

"No," Mara said, and her voice echoed off a thousand years of stone. "I'm calling the court to order."

The first ghost-echo stepped onto the bench, and then another, and another, and the trial nobody had planned began whether any of them were ready or not.
