## 29. Three Banners

**VANE**

Serah Vane had commanded soldiers in the salt marshes, in the ash-forests of the north, in the narrow streets of ports that changed hands twice a year. She knew what an army looked like when it broke, and she knew what it looked like when it held.

The column marching up the Processional toward the palace ruins was holding. Barely.

"Form ranks," she said, and her voice carried the way it always did—flat, unhurried, the voice of a woman who had never once shouted an order in her life. "Shields forward. No one fires until I say."

Ahead, half-buried in rubble and two centuries of silence, the palace gates stood open. They had stood open since the fall, she'd read in the archives—no one had ever bothered to close them, because no one had ever come to take the city back. The Crown had written the capital off as a tomb. Tombs were cheaper than wars.

Now three armies were converging on it.

She could see the Thornbound coming from the east, down the old pilgrim road—a ragged river of refugees and fanatics, hundreds strong, their grey robes streaked with the dust of the deadlands. At their center walked Ilse, tall as a bannerpole, and even at this distance Vane could feel the pressure of her working: the air around the priestess bent slightly, like heat over a forge.

And somewhere inside the palace, small as a candleflame against all this, Mara Dravenmoor.

Vane's hand drifted to the seed's resonance at her hip—the empty lead-lined case where it had been, before Wren stole it back. She still didn't understand how the thief had gotten past forty soldiers. She understood even less why he'd returned it to the girl instead of selling it, or running, or simply vanishing into the deadlands where no Inquisitor would ever find him.

*Because it needed him,* the reports said Wren had told them, laughing, bleeding. *It's the first thing that ever needed me.*

People kept doing inexplicable things for that seed. Vane included. She thought of her great-great-grandmother's name scratched out of the family ledger, of the purge records she'd memorized by candlelight, of the oath she'd taken at nineteen: *I will end what shamed us.*

"Commander." Lieutenant Ossa, young, competent, frightened in the controlled way she'd trained him to be. "The Thornbound are deploying. They're not forming battle lines—they're... kneeling?"

Vane raised her glass. It was true. Along the pilgrim road, the Thornbound had stopped, and now they knelt in rows facing the palace, foreheads to the broken stones, hundreds of them, silent.

"They're not here to fight," Ossa said. "They're here to witness."

"Witness what?"

Ossa didn't answer. He didn't have to. Because at that moment the air above the palace split open with a sound like a struck bell, and every ghost in the capital turned to look.

* * *

**ILSE**

Ilse had waited thirty years for this morning, and now that it had come, she found she could not pray.

She stood at the head of her kneeling people and watched the sky tear, and felt the seed's resonance move through her like a second heartbeat—the Thorn waking, remembering, reaching. Around her, the faithful wept. Old Marta, who had buried three children in the exile camps, was laughing through her tears. Young Tam, who had never known anything but the wandering, gripped his mother's hand so hard she'd have bruises for a week.

*Soon,* Ilse told them all, silently. *Soon you will be a people again, and not a rumor.*

She had built the Thornbound out of nothing but grief and stubbornness. The Crown had called them heretics; the guilds had called them madmen; the world had agreed to forget they existed. And so they had wandered, generation after generation, keeping the old prayers alive in whispers, keeping the memory of the judge who had ruled fairly before the war corrupted everything—and waiting for the heir. The seed would choose a bloodline. The seed had chosen. All Ilse had to do was bring the girl home.

Except the girl refused to come home.

*Mara.* Ilse tasted the name like something bitter. She had offered her everything—belonging, purpose, a family forged in shared exile—and the girl had looked at her with those Warden's eyes and seen only the zealotry underneath. As if conviction were a disease. As if wanting something badly enough, for long enough, were itself a corruption.

Behind her, the sky continued to tear. Echoes were rising off the streets now—not just the capital's dead walking their loops, but older things, war-things, summoned by the seed's song. Ilse watched a column of soldiers in armor four centuries out of date march out of a wall and dissolve mid-stride. Watched a burning field bloom and fade in the middle of the Processional, over and over, a harvest of fire that never ended.

The war was waking up. Good. Let the Crown see what it had buried. Let them look at what their precious peace was built on.

"Mother Ilse." Brother Anselm, breathless, his grey robes torn at the hem. "Crown troops are advancing on the gates. Vane's column. They'll reach the palace before we do."

"Then we walk faster," Ilse said. "We do not fight unless they force us. We are not invaders—we are witnesses coming home."

"And if the Warden refuses us again?"

Ilse looked up at the tearing sky, at the bell-sound rolling out of it, and felt something she had not allowed herself to feel in decades: doubt, small and cold, coiling under her ribs.

"Then," she said quietly, "we will wake it without her permission. And she will thank us after."

She did not quite believe it. But faith, she had learned, was mostly the art of saying things you did not quite believe until they became true—or until you died saying them.

* * *

**MARA**

Mara stood in the tribunal chamber with the seed cupped in both hands, and it was singing.

Not aloud. Not in sound at all. It sang in the way the capital itself was singing—in the ghosts turning their heads, in the echoes of the war rising off the cobbles, in the bell-note hanging in the torn sky above the palace. The ninth verse was half-spoken, its words lodged in her chest like swallowed embers, and every hour she held the ritual open, the city remembered more.

"We can't hold this room," Wren said. He was at the shattered window, looking down at the Processional. "Mara. We can't hold this room. There's a thousand Thornbound coming up the east road and Vane's got maybe two hundred regulars on the west approach, and we've got—" he counted, gesturing at the survivors clustered in the chamber's corner, "—me, you, a ghost jury, and whatever's left of Bramwell's notes."

"Then we don't hold it," Mara said. "We open the doors."

Wren stared at her. "That's your plan? *Open the doors?*"

"The trial needs all of them, Wren. That's the whole point of the ninth verse—it doesn't cage anyone and it doesn't kill anyone. It convenes. Everyone who has a grievance gets to speak it. Everyone who has a crime gets to hear it named." She looked down at the seed, at the faint green light pulsing beneath its skin like a heartbeat. "If I shut the doors, this becomes a siege, and sieges become battles, and battles become exactly what happened last time. Four hundred years ago someone chose the cage instead of the courtroom. I'm not making that mistake twice."

"You're betting three armies' worth of lives on a legal procedure."

"I'm betting them on the truth." Her voice came out harder than she intended. "It's the only thing I have left to bet. You know what I've spent getting here. You know what I gave at the river, in the storm, at Ilse's camp. I don't have memories left to spare, Wren. I barely have a childhood. What I have is this—" she lifted the seed, "—and the verse, and the room. So we open the doors."

He was quiet a moment. Then, because he was Wren, he grinned—that crooked, street-taught grin that meant he'd decided the plan was insane and was coming anyway. "I'll get the doors."

Below, in the courtyard, the first clash came—not of arms but of wills. Vane's shield line met the vanguard of kneeling Thornbound, and for one terrible moment Mara saw spears level, saw the Inquisitors' faces set for slaughter—

—and then the seed flared, and the courtyard filled with echoes.

* * *

**VANE**

The battlefield disappeared.

One moment Serah Vane was giving the order to advance, the next the Processional was gone and she was standing in a wheat field under a sky the color of a bruise, watching a village burn. The flames made no heat. The screaming made no echo. She knew, distantly, that her body was still in the courtyard, that her soldiers were still around her—but the seed had reached into the city's memory and pulled her into it, and the war did not care about rank or orders.

A woman ran past Vane carrying a child. Both of them wore the grey of the Thornbound's ancestors. A soldier in Crown colors cut them down without breaking stride, and Vane recognized the uniform—it was her own regiment's pattern, four generations older. Her ancestor's regiment.

"This is what you serve," said a voice beside her, and Ilse was there, walking through the burning field untouched, her eyes bright with terrible vindication. "Look at it, Inquisitor. Look at what your oaths are built on."

"I know what my order has done," Vane said. "I've read the ledgers. I've spent my life reading the ledgers."

"Then read this one."

The field shifted. The burning village became a tribunal hall—she knew it, she'd seen its ruin upstairs—and now the accused sat in the dock, and the accused was not a woman or a soldier but a vast presence wearing the shape of one: dark-haired, dark-eyed, its edges smoldering like a banked fire. The Thorn, in the shape it had worn when it still walked among people.

And it was speaking. Judging. Settling a land dispute between two villages with a patience and precision that made Vane's chest ache, because she had spent twenty years doing the same work and doing it worse.

"That's not real," she said. "That's the thing that burned the north."

"That is the thing that *judged* the north," Ilse said, "before your guilds taught it what Crowns do to judges who rule against kings."

The vision wavered. The hall dissolved. And suddenly Vane was back in the courtyard, on her knees in the dust, her soldiers around her, some weeping, some praying, one laughing hysterically at nothing. Down the Processional, the Thornbound were rising from their knees, and Ilse stood at their head with her arm raised, and the sky was still torn, and the bell-note was still ringing, and every person on that field had just seen the same four hundred years from a different side.

"Commander!" Ossa hauled her upright. "Orders?"

Vane looked at the palace gates. Open. Un defended. The girl standing in them, small and straight-backed, the seed glowing in her hands like a piece of the vanished sky.

Every instinct she had said: *take it, break her, end this.*

Every instinct she had also said: *you saw what your regiment did. You saw whose side the ledgers put you on.*

"Hold position," she heard herself say. "No one advances. No one fires." And then, because she was who she was, she added: "But no one retreats either. If this is a trial, the Crown attends as a party to it. We will answer for what we've done. And then we will see whether her justice is any different from our war."

* * *

**MARA**

They came through the gates slowly, the way people enter a church they're not sure they're welcome in.

Vane first, alone, her soldiers holding at the threshold. Then Ilse, with Anselm and a dozen elders, the rest of the Thornbound flowing into the courtyard behind them like water finding its level. Two hundred Crown regulars. Eight hundred exiles. One thief at Mara's shoulder, and a ghost-jury filling the tribunal chamber's benches with figures that flickered between centuries.

Three banners, and none of them hers.

"Mara Dravenmoor," Vane said. Her voice was steady, but Mara had learned to hear the cost of steadiness. "You've convened a court. The Crown acknowledges it—under protest."

"Noted," Mara said. "Protest is allowed. That's rather the point of a court."

Someone in the Thornbound ranks laughed, and quickly stifled it.

Ilse stepped forward, and her eyes went straight past Mara to the seed, and Mara watched the hunger in them—thirty years of exile distilled into a single look. "Daughter," she said softly. "You brought it home. Whatever else happens in this room, know that you brought it home."

"It isn't yours to wake," Mara said. "And it isn't theirs to burn. It's going to be *heard*. That's all any of you get today—all any of us get. A hearing."

"A hearing." Ilse's mouth twisted. "My people died in ditches for two hundred years, and you offer us a *hearing*."

"Yes," Mara said. "Because the last time someone skipped the hearing, we got the cage, the purge, the deadlands, and every ghost in this city. I'm done skipping it."

The bell-note overhead deepened. The seed pulsed in her hands, faster now, and the ghost-jury stirred on their benches, and Mara felt the ritual straining at its edges—felt how much power it took to hold a court open while three armies stood in its yard with grievances older than nations.

Wren leaned close. "Whatever happens next," he murmured, "I want it noted I was against opening the doors."

"Noted," Mara said. "Overruled."

She raised the seed, and the torn sky bent down toward the palace, and every voice in the courtyard—Crown, Thornbound, thief, and Warden—fell silent at once.

"The trial resumes," said the Warden of the Truce. "First witness: the accused."

And the seed opened like an eye.
