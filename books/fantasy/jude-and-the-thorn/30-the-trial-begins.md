## 30. The Trial Begins

The crypt beneath the palace was not a tomb. Mara had understood that the moment she first descended its stairs, but standing at the center of it now, with the seed cupped in both hands and three armies waiting above, she understood it completely. It was a courtroom. It had always been a courtroom. The guild had built a cage over a court and called the silence peace.

The walls rose in concentric rings of black stone, each ring carved with seats—thousands of them, tiered like an amphitheater buried alive. Most were empty. Some were not. The ghost-echoes sat in them the way they sat everywhere in the ruined capital: caught mid-gesture, mid-breath, mid-sentence, replaying the last moments of their lives in loops too faint to hear. But here, in this place, their loops bent toward her. She could feel it—the way iron filings feel a magnet, if iron filings could feel.

Wren stood at her shoulder, close enough that she could hear him breathing. He hadn't spoken since they'd come down the stairs. He'd tried, twice, on the way—*you don't have to do this alone, you know, there's still time to run*—and she'd shaken her head each time, and he'd stopped trying, which from Wren was its own kind of eloquence.

Above them, muffled by forty feet of stone, the city was screaming. Crown banners on the western terraces. Thornbound war-horns from the east. Somewhere between them, Ilse's voice, pitched to carry, promising liberation to anyone who would listen. Mara had left them up there deliberately. A trial held under the eyes of armies would be a performance. This one would have to be something else.

She set the seed on the plinth at the center of the room.

It had changed since Vane's people had taken it and Wren had stolen it back. It was larger now—or denser—and when the torchlight touched it, light came back out of it wrong, bent, as though the seed were remembering stars it had no right to remember. It pulsed slowly, patiently, like something breathing in its sleep.

"Tell me again why we're doing this underground," Wren said quietly. "With the two groups who want to kill us directly above us."

"Because the last time anyone judged the Thorn, they did it in a palace hall with wine and applause." Mara unrolled Bramwell's ledger across the plinth's edge, weighting the corners with stones. "And because the ninth verse doesn't work in daylight."

"That's not reassuring."

"It's not supposed to be."

She found the page. She had read it so many times in the past days that the ink had begun to fade under her thumb, or perhaps that was only her imagination—perhaps she was wearing the words away by wanting them. The ninth verse wasn't a ward-song at all. That was the trick of it, the joke a hundred years of Wardens had missed or ignored. Verses one through eight wove barriers. Verse nine *opened a door*. It convened. It called every bound thing to account—took the magic that sealed and turned it inside out into a summons.

Bramwell had known. Of course he had. He had spent his whole life singing eight verses over a cage and keeping the ninth folded away like a letter he couldn't bear to open, and at the end, dying, he had pressed the ledger into her hands and said, *not protection—patience*, and Mara finally understood what he'd been patient for. Not for the Thorn to die. For someone brave enough or foolish enough to give it what it had been denied.

Her hands were shaking. She looked at them and let them shake. There was no point pretending otherwise; the ghosts in the seats would see through her anyway. Ghosts always did.

"Wren," she said. "If this goes wrong—if the verse does something I can't stop—you run. You take the ledger and you get above ground and you don't look back."

"I'm a thief," Wren said. "Running is my best skill." He didn't move toward the stairs. "I'm also a terrible listener. Ask anyone."

"Wren."

"You gave me a job," he said. "First thing in my whole life that ever needed me. I'm not quitting before payday." His voice cracked slightly on *needed*, and he covered it by grinning, which fooled neither of them. "Sing your song, Warden."

Mara closed her eyes.

She began with the first verse, the way she'd sung it ten thousand times in Thornvale, tending wards that were actually bars. Her voice was thin at first, echoing off the black stone, and the ghost-echoes stirred in their seats—not waking, exactly, but turning, the way sleeping people turn toward a familiar sound. The second verse. The third. Each one she sang slower than she needed to, because each one was a lock she was choosing to open, and she wanted to feel every tumbler give.

By the fifth verse her voice had steadied. By the seventh it filled the crypt without effort, as though the room itself were carrying it, handing the melody upward through the tiers of seats. The echoes were rising now, standing in their places, their loops unraveling into stillness. Hundreds of translucent figures, frozen mid-life, all facing the center of the room. All facing her.

The eighth verse ended on a note that hung in the air like a held breath.

Then the ninth.

She had expected it to be hard. It was easy. That was the terrible part—it poured out of her as if she'd sung it all her life, as if some part of her had been waiting in her throat since the night the stars went out. The melody was simple, almost gentle, a lullaby's shape with a judge's weight, and as she sang it the seed began to glow, and the glow spread outward along the floor in lines, tracing channels in the stone she'd never noticed, running up the walls, igniting the empty seats one ring at a time.

And the dead woke up.

Not the way they walked above—looping, broken, trapped in their final moments. Here, in the court the guild had buried, they woke the way witnesses wake. They straightened. They looked around. They saw each other, and Mara watched faces flicker through recognition and grief and something like relief, hundreds of people realizing simultaneously that they had been waiting a very long time for this.

An old man in the front ring spoke, and his voice was dry as pages turning. "The court convenes. Who calls it?"

"The Warden," said a woman beside him, reading something in Mara that Mara herself couldn't see. "The office recognizes the office."

Mara's mouth was dry. "I call it," she managed. "I am Mara Dravenmoor, Warden of the truce—or—I'm trying to be. I convene this court to finish what it started. The trial of the Thorn."

"The trial of the Thorn," the old man repeated, and the words moved through the tiers like wind through wheat, echoed and re-echoed, until the whole crypt murmured with them. "Never concluded. Sealed unfinished. A thousand years pending." He turned his hollow eyes to her. "Then the debt comes due at last. State the parties."

Mara's heart slammed against her ribs. "The Thorn stands accused. And—" she hesitated, then made herself say it— "and the accusers stand accused. Both. That's how justice works, isn't it? Nobody gets to be only a judge."

A ripple through the seats. Approval or alarm, she couldn't tell.

"The court requires voices," the old man said. "A prosecutor. An advocate for the accused. A judge who does not speak for either."

"I know," Mara said. "They're upstairs."

* * *

Getting them down was the hardest thing she had ever done, harder than the deadlands, harder than leaving Thornvale, because it meant walking out of the crypt with the trial half-convened and trusting it would still be there when she returned.

She surfaced into smoke and shouting. The palace terraces had become a battlefield's edge—Crown soldiers in ranks behind barricades, Inquisitors' grey coats among them, and beyond the shattered eastern wall, the Thornbound massed in their ragged hundreds, Ilse at their head with her arms raised and her eyes fixed on the palace like a woman watching a door she intended to walk through.

Mara walked out onto the terrace between them, alone, hands empty and visible, and the fighting stuttered. Not stopped—she could hear it grinding on somewhere below, in the streets—but here, in her immediate radius, weapons lowered out of sheer confusion.

"Inquisitor Vane," she called. "I need to speak with you. Alone-ish."

Serah Vane came through the Crown ranks the way she did everything—precisely, unhurried, her grey coat immaculate despite everything, her sword sheathed because she had never once needed it drawn to be dangerous. She stopped six feet away and studied Mara with those pale, patient eyes.

"You have it back," Vane said. Not a question. She could feel it; Mara could see her feeling it, the way one feels weather change. "The thief stole it from my camp. I should have executed him."

"He's very good at his job."

"You're offering me something," Vane said. "You don't come out here otherwise. So offer."

"A trial," Mara said. "The real one. The one the Thorn never got. Downstairs is a courtroom older than your Crown, and the jury just woke up, and they're waiting, and they've been waiting a thousand years. I'm asking you to prosecute. To bring every crime the Thorn committed, every atrocity, everything your order was built to avenge—and put it in front of witnesses who were actually there."

Vane was silent for a long moment. "And if the verdict goes against the Crown's interest?"

"Then it goes against the Crown's interest. That's what makes it a trial instead of an execution."

"And the Thornbound?"

Mara turned. Ilse had come forward to the edge of the broken wall, flanked by her zealots, and her face wore the expression Mara had learned to dread—the rapturous certainty of a woman about to receive what she believed she was owed.

"Ilse!" Mara called. "You wanted to wake it? You wanted it to speak?" She gestured at the stairwell behind her. "Come down and be its voice. Stand in the court where it was condemned and argue its case. If you believe in it, prove it the honest way."

Ilse descended slowly, her people parting for her, and when she reached the terrace she looked at Mara with something almost like tenderness. "You think ceremony will save you. You think words are a substitute for victory."

"I think words are the only thing that was ever missing," Mara said. "That's the whole tragedy. Everyone chose a weapon because nobody chose a verdict."

For a moment—one long, teetering moment—Ilse's certainty wavered, and Mara saw beneath it the refugee, the believer, the girl whose people had carried a grievance for generations like a bone they couldn't stop gnawing. Then it hardened again, but differently. Curiosity, maybe. Or the hunter's patience of a woman willing to let a trap spring to see what was inside it.

"I will speak for the Thorn," Ilse said. "And when it wakes fully—and it will wake, child, trial or no trial—you will wish you had chosen our way."

"Everyone keeps telling me what I'll wish," Mara said tiredly. "Nobody's ever right."

* * *

The descent was a funeral procession in reverse. Vane came with two Inquisitors who waited at the stairs, unwilling or unable to enter a place where the dead sat in judgment. Ilse came with none—her faith was company enough. Wren brought up the rear, and Mara noticed he had positioned himself near the exit, and loved him for it.

When they entered the crypt, the ghost-echoes were waiting. All of them. Standing now, filling the tiers, thousands strong, silent, attentive. The air itself felt formal. Vane stopped two steps in, and for the first time since Mara had known her, Serah Vane looked genuinely unsettled.

"They're not loops," Vane said quietly. "My order taught me these were echoes. Residue. Nothing more."

"They were residue everywhere else," Mara said. "This is where they're still themselves. This is the court. They never got to leave because the case never closed."

The old man from the front ring stepped forward. "Parties are present. Prosecutor—inquisitor of the Crown law, blood of the purged house. Advocate—priestess of the Thornbound remnant. Judge—holder of the Warden's office." His gaze moved across each of them in turn, weighing. "The court notes that all three offices descend from the original crime. Proceeding."

"What does that mean?" Vane asked sharply.

"It means everyone in this room inherited something broken," Mara said, before her courage failed her. "Including me. Especially me. My guild built the cage. Your order burned the survivors. Her church worshipped the prisoner. None of us gets to pretend we're clean, so we do this anyway. That's the point."

She took her place at the plinth. The seed pulsed against the stone, faster now, and she could feel it listening—could feel the attention coiling inside it like a spring, a thousand years of silence gathering itself to speak.

She opened Bramwell's ledger to the ninth verse's final instruction, the line she'd read a dozen times and only now understood:

*The Warden speaks last, and least, and holds the scale.*

"Tribunal of the Thorn," she said, and her voice rang off the black stone and up through forty feet of rock into the burning city above, "is convened. Prosecutor, present your charges."

Vane drew a breath, squared her shoulders, and stepped forward—and the entire crypt leaned in, the dead and the living together, as the first charge of a thousand-year-old trial broke the silence:

"The entity called the Thorn stands accused of the slaughter at Cael Harrow—"

And the lights in the seats flared white, and somewhere far above, the first star of the night flickered uncertainly back into being, as if the sky itself had turned to watch.
