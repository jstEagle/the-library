## 31. Testimony of the Seed

Mara had expected a monster's voice.

She had prepared for it, in the way you prepare for a blow—braced, jaw set, the seed cupped in her palms on the tribunal dais like something that might bite through her skin. Around the ruined throne room of the capital, the ghost-echoes stood in their hundreds, translucent and patient, dead jurors who had waited a century to finish what they'd started. Vane stood at the prosecutor's bench in grey and silver. Ilse stood where the Thorn's advocate should stand, hands folded, eyes wet with anticipation. Wren sat against a broken pillar with his arms crossed, pretending he wasn't afraid.

The hall was silent. Even the walking dead outside had paused in their loops, as if the whole city were holding its breath.

"Speak," Mara said. Her voice cracked on the word. She tried again. "In the name of the ninth verse, and the office that convenes you—the Thorn will give testimony."

The seed warmed.

Then it spoke, and it was not a monster's voice at all.

It was everyone's voice. It was Bramwell's low rasp and her mother's hum and Wren's street drawl and Ilse's ringing cadence, all layered, all braided, because the Thorn had learned its words from every mouth that had ever cursed it or prayed to it or sung wards against it in the dark. The sound filled the throne room without effort, without volume, the way starlight fills a valley.

*I was made to judge,* it said. *Before I was anything else, before I was a weapon or a prison or a fear—I was made to judge.*

* * *

The echoes shifted. The air itself thickened, and Mara understood that the Thorn was not describing the past—it was showing it, laying memory over the ruined hall the way you lay a cloth over a table.

They stood in a world that still had stars. A world of green hills and walled cities, two hundred years gone.

*The old kingdoms warred as kingdoms do—over borders, over gods, over pride. And when the wars grew too cruel for mortal courts to hold—when kings burned cities and claimed necessity—the free peoples made me. Not born. Made. A court given flesh, so that no one, however crowned, would stand above reckoning.*

The ghost-echoes murmured. Some of them had been there. Some of them *remembered being there*, and their remembering made the images sharper: a figure of bark and iron and slow green light standing in a market square while a warlord's soldiers laid down their weapons rather than face its questions.

*I did not kill first,* the Thorn said. *Understand this, whoever hears. I asked. That was my function. I asked until the truth came out, and then I weighed it. I sentenced kings. I sentenced farmers. I hanged a beloved general whose troops wept at the rope, because he had ordered a village burned to slow an advance. They called me fair. For sixty years they called me fair.*

Vane's hand had drifted to the edge of her bench. She didn't seem to notice.

*And then came the war that had no sides worth naming.*

The vision changed. Fire now, vast and rolling, the sky black with ash. Mara watched armies march under banners she recognized from the guild archive—from Bramwell's fevered drawings, from the murals of the hollow chapel.

*Both crowns learned magic in the same winter, and both used it the same way: on each other's civilians. There was no justice left to administer because both parties wanted none. They wanted victory. So they came to unmake me—not to defeat me. Unmake me. I was the last court either side feared.*

*So I did the thing I was made never to do.* The voice slowed. It did not tremble—Mara sensed it couldn't tremble, that trembling was a mercy built into throats and not into whatever the Thorn had instead—but the weight of it pressed on the hall like deep water. *I chose a side. I judged the war itself, found one crown less guilty than the other, and gave them my power. I became not the judge but the judgment.*

*You know how that ends. It always ends the same. Power spent in anger stops asking questions. My soldiers committed atrocities wearing my sigil, and I knew, and I let them stand because we were winning, and winning felt like righteousness. I burned the city of Callowmere. There were eleven thousand people in it. I told myself they had voted for their king.*

Silence.

Wren had stopped pretending not to listen. He was leaning forward, mouth open slightly, like a boy hearing a story he'd been told was about a monster and finding out it was about a man.

*When the tide turned—and it turned, because anger is a poor long-term ally—they did not bring me to trial. Understand: I expected trial. I went to them. I walked into the capital with my hands open and asked to be judged for Callowmere, for the hanging marches, for everything. I asked for the rope if the rope was just.*

The Thorn paused, and in that pause Mara heard something she would spend the rest of her life trying to name. Not grief. Something older than grief. The sound of a thing that had believed in a system learning the system was gone.

*They feared me too much to try me. Fear does not deliberate. So they wrote no verdict. They sang a cage instead—the first ward-song, sung by your first Warden, whose name was Jude—and they buried me beneath an orchard and told their children the valley was protected.*

*Beneath apple trees,* the Thorn said, and for the first time there was something almost like humor in it, dry and terrible. *I have listened to a century of harvests. Do you know what it is to be sentenced to silence and have spring happen over your grave every year?*

* * *

Ilse broke first.

"You lie." Her voice rang off the broken vaulting, high and fierce. "You were betrayed, yes—this part we know, this is scripture—but you do not get to wear guilt like a garment to make them pity you. You were our god. You were *wronged*—"

"I am telling you what I did," the Thorn said, in a voice that was suddenly only Bramwell's, quiet and level. "You were not there. You inherited a grievance and called it faith. I am telling you about Callowmere. Eleven thousand."

"They were your enemies—"

"There were *children* in it." The warmth in the seed spiked; the torch-glyphs along the walls guttered. "Do not defend me, priestess. Your defense is the same disease that killed me the first time. Someone stands accused, and their friends decide the accusation cannot be heard, and so the court burns. I have seen it from every chair in the room. Do not make me watch it again from yours."

Ilse stood frozen. Mara watched something move across her face—the first crack in the certainty, small but real, the look of a woman hearing her god refuse her worship. It was more devastating than any rebuttal. Ilse sat down slowly, like her knees had been quietly removed.

* * *

Vane spoke next, and she spoke like a prosecutor, which was to say carefully.

"The Crown's position has always been that you are a weapon that must be dismantled. This testimony—" Vane paused, and Mara saw her choose her next words with visible effort, "—does not change the sentence it deserves. But it changes the crime." She looked down at her notes and did not read them. "My family was purged for serving you. I have spent my life believing I served the correction of that error. If what you say is true—that you surrendered yourself, that they refused you trial—then the purge was not justice. It was tidiness." Her jaw tightened. "I want it noted that I hate saying that."

"Noted," the Thorn said gently. "Hate is honest. Continue."

"Why did you stay silent?" The question came out of Vane raw, off-script. "A hundred years. You could have whispered into dreams. You could have rotted the orchard. Why wait?"

*Because a court that argues with its own imprisonment confirms the fear that imprisoned it,* the Thorn said. *If I struggled, they would be right to hold me. So I held still. I practiced the thing they denied me: patience. And I waited for a Warden who would sing the ninth verse.*

Every eye turned to Mara.

She hadn't moved. She stood with the seed warm in her hands, and she realized her cheeks were wet, and she couldn't remember starting to cry. Because she had grown up singing the eight verses over that orchard. Because she had tended the bars of a cell and called it a wall against wolves. Because Bramwell had died whispering *patience*, and she finally understood that he had meant the Thorn's patience as much as anyone's—the long, awful discipline of waiting for a justice that might never come.

"Why me?" she asked. Her voice was very small in the huge hall. "Why the seed? Why my blood?"

*Because the first Warden repented.* The voice softened, all the borrowed voices settling into one. *Jude sang the cage and then spent forty years trying to write a way out of it. He could not undo his own song—no singer can. But he wrote a ninth verse. A summons, not a spell. And he bound it to his line, so that someday a descendant would inherit not the guilt but the duty. The guild hid you in plain sight, child. They raised you to tend a lock. You were always meant to be the key.*

"And if I'd refused?" Wren said suddenly, to the room. "If she'd just—chucked the seed in a river?"

*Then I would have waited another hundred years,* the Thorn said simply. *Justice delayed is not nothing. It is not nothing. I am the proof of both halves of that sentence.*

* * *

The testimony went on through what should have been night and was only a deeper grey, the sunless sky of the starless city pressing at the broken windows. The Thorn recounted its failures with a thoroughness that bordered on mercilessness—not only Callowmere but smaller things, crueller things, judgments made in fatigue, sentences carried out on thin evidence, the slow corrosion of a creature that had been built to weigh truth discovering that it had begun weighing only what was convenient.

It did not ask forgiveness. Mara noticed that. It stated its crimes the way it once stated others', evenly, completely, leaving the weighing to someone else.

That was the moment the hall truly broke.

Not with weeping—with silence. Vane closed her ledger. Ilse bowed her head over her folded hands. The ghost-echoes, the dead jurors of the old world, stirred and murmured among themselves, and one of them—an old woman in robes of a vanished court—spoke aloud in a voice like wind through wheat:

*"The witness may step down."*

The seed cooled in Mara's hands. The presence withdrew, folding itself away with a courtesy that hurt worse than any roar would have, and the throne room was only a ruin again, dust and broken glass and three living people and a few hundred dead ones waiting on a verdict.

Mara looked at Vane. Vane looked at Ilse. Nobody had a script left. Everything either woman had marched across the deadlands believing was now evidence in a record neither could refute, and the monstrous thing they had come to destroy or enthrone had turned out to be legible after all—which was somehow far more frightening.

"We deliberate," Mara said. Her voice steadied as she said it, finding the floor beneath her feet. "Tomorrow, at dawn. All parties will speak. The court will rule."

She looked down at the seed, dim and quiet in her palm.

It pulsed once—slow, patient, like a heartbeat that had learned to wait.

And outside, in the streets of the dead city, the ghost-echoes began, very softly, to sing the first eight verses—not as a ward, not as a cage, but the way you sing a hymn before the sentence is read.

Dawn was coming. Whatever it brought, nothing in the capital would ever be sealed again.
