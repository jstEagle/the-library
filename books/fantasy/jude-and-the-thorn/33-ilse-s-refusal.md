## 33. Ilse's Refusal

The verdict was still hanging in the air when Ilse understood that she had been wrong about everything, and that she would rather die than say so.

*Guilty of wartime acts. Guilty of being denied trial. Sentence: stewardship.*

Stewardship. The word drifted through the ruined throne room like ash through a broken window. Around her, the ghost-echoes of the old tribunal stood in their frozen rows—dead judges, dead clerks, dead witnesses, all of them flickering with the light of the returning stars, all of them watching the verdict settle over the seed in Mara Dravenmoor's cupped hands.

Ilse had waited three hundred years of accumulated faith for this night. Her grandmother's grandmother had whispered the Thorn's name into her cradle. She had crossed the deadlands barefoot as a penance at fourteen. She had buried eleven of her own people when the Crown's purges came, and she had told each of them, as they died: *He wakes. He wakes, and he will judge them all.*

And now the Thorn had spoken, and it had not asked for vengeance.

It had asked to be *kept*.

"No," Ilse said.

Her voice came out small in the vastness of the palace crypt. No one turned. Vane stood rigid at the prosecutor's mark, her grey uniform scorched from the battle outside, her face a mask she hadn't quite finished putting back on after Mara had put her family's purge on trial before these same ghosts. Wren hovered near the door with the look of a boy who had learned, very recently, what regret weighed. And Mara—the girl, the apprentice, the *Warden*—stood with the seed glowing against her palms like a coal refusing to die, waiting for the echoes to swear the sentence.

"No," Ilse said again, louder. "You don't get to do this."

This time the room heard her. The nearest echo—a judge whose face had been worn smooth by centuries of replaying his own shame—turned his translucent head toward her with something like pity.

"You," Ilse said, pointing at him, at all of them, at the whole glittering machinery of the dead. "You sentenced him without trial. That is the one truth everyone in this room agrees on. And now you gather here, in your pretty returned starlight, and you call it justice to hand him to *her*?" Her finger swung to Mara. "To a child who has known him for a season? To be a *steward*, like he's a garden? Like he's an orchard?"

"Mara's orchard," Wren said under his breath, "is actually kind of the point—"

"Silence." Ilse didn't look at him. She couldn't afford to look at anything but the shape of the wrongness spreading through her chest. "Three hundred years my people kept his name alive. Three hundred years we were hunted for it, burned for it, erased for it. We were the only ones who *remembered*. And now the remembering is done, and the verdict is—" she tasted the word and hated it— "*mercy.*"

"It's a reckoning," Mara said quietly. "It's what he asked for."

"He doesn't know what he asks for. He has been asleep beneath an orchard since before your guild had a name. He is a wounded thing, and wounded things accept whatever hand feeds them." Ilse stepped forward, and felt the Thornbound behind her shift—what remained of them, two dozen survivors of the battle at the gates, bloodied and bewildered, looking to her the way they always had. "I have seen what he is when he wakes whole. I have *dreamed* it every night of my life."

"You've dreamed what you wanted to see," Vane said. Her voice was flat, exhausted, and utterly without cruelty, which made it worse. "I know something about that."

"You know nothing."

"I know my ancestor stood where I'm standing," Vane said, "and pulled the lever on a sentence no one argued for. I spent my life thinking she was the victim of that story. Tonight I learned she was the executioner. It broke something in me, priestess. But it broke it *open*." She lifted her chin. "Don't make the same mistake in reverse. Don't let your grief tell you what the god wants."

"He is not a god," Ilse snapped. "He is better than a god. Gods are distant. He *judged*. Every grievance between mage and mortal, every stolen field, every burned village—he weighed them, fairly, for a hundred years, and both sides hated him for it because fairness feels like loss when it isn't yours. That is why they sealed him. Not because he was monstrous. Because he was *right*, and being right forever is unbearable." Her voice cracked and she hated that too. "And now you would take the only just power this world ever had and give it a *jailer's title*."

"The verdict isn't a jail," Mara said. "Read it again."

"I have read enough verdicts."

"Ilse." Mara took one step toward her, and the seed's light moved with her, and Ilse saw—involuntarily, helplessly—that the girl was crying. Not from fear. From the sheer weight of holding a thing that everyone wanted and no one could carry alone. "Come stand at the witness mark. Swear with us. Your people kept his name when no one else would. That counts. That has *always* counted. He said so himself, ten minutes ago, in front of everyone."

And there it was. The knife inside the kindness.

Because the Thorn *had* said so. Ilse had heard it with her own ears, felt it in her teeth, in the scar tissue of her old faith: *the ones who remembered are witnesses, and witnesses are the bones of any court.* He had honored them. He had named them. And then he had declined, gently, terribly, to be their weapon.

That was the wound she could not stop touching. Not the verdict. The *refusal*. Three hundred years of martyrdom, and the martyred god had woken up and chosen a farm girl with dirt under her nails and a dead mentor's songs in her throat.

"You want me to swear," Ilse said slowly, "to a truce."

"To a stewardship. A living court. Grievances heard, judgments made, stars as witnesses. Forever, or as long as anyone keeps the oath."

"A court." Ilse laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged. "A court needs criminals, Warden. A court needs wars to adjudicate. You haven't ended the conflict—you've *institutionalized* it. You've built him a temple with paperwork." She spread her arms. "My people did not bleed for paperwork."

Behind her, one of the Thornbound—a young man called Doss, who had lost his brother at the valley gates—shifted his weight. "High Priestess," he said carefully. "The seed spoke. We all heard it. Maybe—maybe what it wants matters more than what we—"

"What it wants." Ilse turned on him, and he flinched. "It has been asleep for three centuries, fed on ward-songs and apple roots. It wants what its keeper tells it to want. That girl has had its heart in her pocket since the valley, whispering to it in borrowed voices, spending its strength mile by mile. You think this verdict rose from the Thorn? It rose from *her*. From the guild that caged him. They couldn't kill him, so they taught him to love his cage, and now they've dressed the cage in robes and called it a throne."

"That's not true," Mara said.

"It is the truest thing in this room."

"It's not." Mara's voice steadied, and the seed brightened, and Ilse felt the truth of it press against her like heat from a forge—and she stepped back from that heat, because if she let it touch her, everything would burn. "You can feel it. Everyone can. He's awake, Ilse. Fully awake, for the first time in three hundred years, and this is what he chose. If you keep calling it my lie, you're not protecting him. You're protecting the only version of him you can survive believing in."

The words went in clean, the way only true words can.

Ilse stood very still. Around her the crypt hummed with returning starlight; above, through the shattered vault, the first new stars were visible, igniting one by one as the echoes swore themselves as witnesses. Each flare was a small death of the dark she had loved. Each flare was proof.

She had built her entire soul on a simple architecture: *the world wronged him; therefore the world owes him; therefore the debt comes due in fire.* Take away the fire and the architecture collapsed, and underneath it there was nothing but a woman who had given everything to a cause, standing in the ruins of the need for it.

A cause outliving its need. She could feel it outliving hers right now, feel it pacing inside her ribs like an animal whose cage had been opened and who refused to leave.

"There is another way," she said.

Mara closed her eyes. "Ilse, don't."

"The ritual convenes a court. Fine. Courts can be overturned. Verdicts can be contested. The ninth verse calls the tribunal—but the Thorn's own law allows appeal, does it not? He taught us that. *Every judgment may be reweighed by greater witness.*" She raised her hands, and the old words rose in her throat, the waking-words, the ones her line had guarded through purge and pyre—not the full rite, she didn't have the strength for the full rite, but the *shattering* verse, the one that cracked a court mid-session and scattered its authority to the four winds. "Greater witness. Let the deadlands testify. Let the unburied speak. Let him wake *whole*, outside your sentence, and choose for himself—truly choose—between your mercy and my memory."

"You'll tear the ritual apart," Mara said. "Mid-verdict. With the stars half-sworn. You'll unmake the truce before it exists, and everything we paid—all the memories, all the deaths, Wren's sister, my whole childhood—it'll be for nothing."

"Then it will be for nothing *honestly*," Ilse said, "instead of for nothing *gently*."

She began the verse.

*

The words were older than the guild, older than the war's end, cut into the walls of chapels the Crown had burned down to their foundations. Ilse had learned them at her mother's knee in a cellar lit by tallow, and she had never once said them aloud until now, and they fit her mouth like a blade fits a hand that has held nothing else.

The crypt shuddered. The ghost-echoes flickered, their outlines fraying. At the witness marks, the half-sworn stars guttered overhead, and somewhere far above, in the recovering sky, a star that had just relit trembled like a candle in wind.

"*Reweigh,*" Ilse sang. "*Unbind the bench. Let the greater dark attend.*"

"Stop her!" Vane was moving, drawing steel, but the echoes between her and Ilse had gone dense as glass, and her blade skated off a dead judge's shoulder with a sound like a bell struck wrong.

Wren grabbed for Ilse's arm and she threw him off without looking—one burst of the old waking-magic, the last of it, the hoarded reserve she'd carried across the deadlands for exactly this moment—and he crashed into a pillar and slid down it, dazed.

Only Mara came straight at her. No magic, no weapon. Just a girl running at a priestess through a collapsing miracle, shouting the ninth verse over the top of the shattering one, trying to drown it, trying to hold the court together with her voice alone.

And the Thorn woke the rest of the way.

Ilse felt it happen. Felt the seed's light go from ember to sun. Felt the presence she had worshipped in dreams rise up through the palace foundations, through the crypt floor, through the soles of her feet—vast, patient, *lucid*—and she exulted for one wild heartbeat, *he's free, he's free, my verse worked—*

And then he looked at her.

Not with the dreaming face from her visions. Not with the wrathful judge from her sermons. With something she had no icon for: attention. Complete, undivided, sorrowful attention, the kind no one had ever given her in her entire hard life, and it was worse than wrath, it was infinitely worse, because wrath she knew how to answer.

*I know you,* the Thorn said, in the voice that was all voices, in the voice she had heard in cradle-whispers and deathbed promises. *Ilse, daughter of the remembering line.*

"Yes," she gasped, still singing, still tearing at the edges of the court. "Yes—I am the one who kept you—wake, wake *whole*, throw off their sentence, I have carried you, I have *earned*—"

*You have.* The attention did not waver. *You kept my name when names were capital crimes. You buried your dead with my sigil hidden in their shrouds. You crossed the wastes barefoot. I have watched you for three hundred years from under the orchard, and there was no night so dark that you did not speak to me in it.*

The verse faltered in her throat.

*And I must show you what I showed no one else tonight,* he said, *because you are the last one carrying the old weight, and I will not have you break under a misunderstanding.*

The memory came down on her like the sky.

*

Not a vision. A *return*. The Thorn had spent the whole trial reclaiming his scattered self—every fragment of him looted, buried, sealed into wards and relics and the marrow of his faithful—and now, rising, he gathered the last of it home. And some of him, Ilse understood with a lurch, had been *in her*. In the dreams. In the name. In the faith itself, which had never been empty, only borrowed.

So she saw.

She saw the war from the inside of him. Saw the fair years, the judgments, the grievances weighed on scales of root and starlight. Then saw the corruption come—not sudden, but seeping, the way rot seeps—saw herself, in his memory, begin to err. Saw the first unjust sentence, delivered in exhaustion. The second, delivered in anger. The tenth, the hundredth, each one small, each one defensible, each one a grain of sand in a scale that slowly stopped balancing. Saw the moment he stopped being a judge and became a power, and powers do not weigh; they *decide*.

Saw the burning of Callowmere—not the Crown's atrocity version, not the Thornbound martyr version, but the truth: both armies in the valley, and the Thorn, mad with grief for a murdered herald, pulling the sky down on all of them, guilty and innocent together, six thousand dead in an afternoon, and waking afterward to the silence and knowing, knowing, *knowing* what he had become.

Saw the guild come for him. Saw the first Warden—Jude, young Jude, Jude with his terrible gentle face—stand before him not with chains but with a question: *Will you submit to judgment?* And saw the Thorn, who had judged others for a century and could not bear to be judged, refuse. Saw him fight. Saw him lose. Saw the sealing not as murder but as the only mercy either side could agree on: sleep, until such time as he could bear a mirror.

Three hundred years of sleep. And in all that time, one thing had sustained him in the dark, one thread of warmth through the cold of the ward-song: the small voices. The remembering line. Mothers in cellars, whispering his name to their children. *He wakes. He wakes. He will judge them all.*

They had thought they were keeping his cause alive.

They had been keeping *him* alive. The man, not the weapon. The prisoner, not the power. Every prayer had been a candle passed hand to hand down three centuries of darkness, and he had lived on them, and he had loved them, and he had never once intended what Ilse had built out of them.

"Oh," Ilse said.

The verse died in her throat. The court stabilized around her, the echoes re-knitting, the stars steadying overhead, because the shattering had needed her faith to fuel it and her faith was gone—poured out, spent, revealed as having been something else entirely all along.

She sank to her knees on the crypt floor.

The Thorn gathered close—not physically, not yet, but in the way a great presence can fill a small space—and the last of the memory unfolded, gently now, the way you unwrap a gift you're afraid will be refused.

*You wanted to be remembered,* he said. *All your line wanted it. Burned women in forgotten villages, children with hidden sigils, preachers in cellars. You gave me remembrance when I had none, and I give it back to you now, doubled, whole. Look at yourself as I have always seen you.*

And she looked.

She saw Ilse of the Thornbound not as the High Priestess, not as the avenger, not as the last flame of a holy cause. She saw a girl in a cellar learning forbidden words by tallow-light because her mother was frightened and someone had to be brave. She saw a fourteen-year-old doing penance on deadland gravel because grief needed somewhere to go. She saw a woman who had buried eleven of her people and told each of them a promise she had believed with her whole heart, and she saw—clearly, finally, without mercy and without cruelty—that the promise had never been a plan. It had been a *flotation device*. Something to hold onto so the water wouldn't take her.

The cause had outlived its need. No—the cause had *been* the need, dressed in glory. And now the need was met. The name was remembered. The trial was held. The world had done the one thing her dead had begged the sky for.

There was nothing left to carry.

"I don't know how to put it down," she whispered. "I don't know how to be a person without it."

*No one does,* the Thorn said. *It is the hardest sentence of all. Harder than mine.*

"Then unmake me," she said, and was surprised to find she was weeping, and more surprised to find the weeping felt like relief. "You have the power. I tried to break your court. I would have scattered your verdict to the winds. Punish me. Give me a sentence. Give me *anything* with weight—I know how to carry weight. I don't know how to be light."

The presence considered her for a long moment. Above, a new star flared into being—Vane swearing her oath, maybe, or one of the echoes, the count climbing toward completion.

*Death would be easy,* the Thorn said. *And you have never once taken the easy road, Ilse of the remembering line. So I will give you the hard one.*

He reached into her—not into her body, into her *name*, into the three-hundred-year chain of whispers that had made her—and he did not erase it. Erasure would have been mercy. Instead he *finished* it. He took every hidden sigil, every cellar prayer, every burned chapel, every mother's midnight whisper, and he wove them into the fabric of the returning sky, into the record of the court, into the testimony that would be read for as long as the truce stood. The remembering line would not be a secret society anymore. It would be *history*. Public. Honored. Over.

And Ilse, who had been the vessel of it, the last living link, felt the link complete itself and dissolve—not destroyed, but *delivered*. A letter finally reaching its address. A burden set down at the correct door after three hundred years of being carried by the wrong people.

She felt herself thinning. Not painfully. The way morning thins a lamp-flame—unnecessary, outshone, gratefully dim.

"This is dying," she said.

*This is being remembered,* the Thorn said. *Which is what you asked for, the first time you ever prayed to me. You were seven. You said: please don't let them forget us. I am only now able to answer.*

Around her, the crypt was bright with starlight. Mara had stopped singing and stood frozen, tears on her face, one hand half-extended toward the kneeling priestess. Vane had lowered her sword. Even Doss and the battered remnant of the Thornbound had gone to their knees, feeling the change in the air, feeling the old hunger for apocalypse quietly starving to death inside them.

Ilse looked up at Mara Dravenmoor, the farm-girl Warden, the jailer-turned-steward, the person she had crossed a continent to destroy.

"Keep the court," Ilse said. Her voice was already becoming echo, already taking on the faint shimmer of the dead. "Keep it honest. When it gets tedious—and it will, oh, it will, justice is ninety percent tedium—when you're tired and no one is watching and it would be so easy to fudge a verdict, remember that a woman unmade herself rather than let you fudge this one."

"Ilse—"

"And Warden." The shimmer was taking her hands now, her arms, turning her to translucent gold at the edges. She found she was smiling. It had been a long time. "The seventh verse. My line preserved a variant of the seventh verse that your guild lost—the mourning cadence. It's in the chapel at Hollow Ford, under the altar stone. You'll want it. For the funerals. There are going to be so many funerals, building a peace out of a war this size."

"I'll find it," Mara said. "I swear it. On the office."

"On the office," Ilse repeated, and laughed softly, genuinely, for the first time in thirty years. "Good answer. Bramwell trained you well."

Then the light took the rest of her, and Ilse of the Thornbound—last high priestess of the remembering line, keeper of a promise that turned out to be a prayer, the woman who refused a verdict because the alternative was grieving—came apart into the starlight like smoke coming apart into morning.

She did not experience it as ending.

She experienced it as being *read aloud*. Every hidden name, every burned village, every cellar prayer, recited at last into the open record of the world, witnessed by returning stars, entered into evidence in the court of the truce. Remembered. Finally, completely, publicly remembered.

It was everything she had ever wanted.

It had only ever required the death of everything she had built to get it.

* * *

In the silence after, the last star of the count ignited directly above the throne dais, and the verdict's swearing completed itself with a sound like a bell underwater.

Mara stood alone at the center of the crypt, the seed warm in her hands, tears drying salt-stiff on her cheeks. Wren limped over and stood beside her without a word. Vane sheathed her sword and did not look at anyone.

Doss was the first of the Thornbound to rise. He looked at the place where Ilse had knelt, and then at Mara, and his face was the face of a man watching his entire cosmology file quietly out of the room.

"High Priestess Ilse," he said at last, formally, to the empty air, "is remembered."

"Remembered," murmured the other Thornbound, raggedly, one by one.

And beneath the palace, deeper than the crypt, deeper than the old tribunal hall, something vast and newly whole settled back into the world—not as a weapon, not as a god, but as a court awaiting its first case—and in the seed against Mara's palms, faint but unmistakable, the Thorn spoke one final word before the ritual's anchor came due:

*Mara. The verse requires payment. It is time to decide what you can live without.*
