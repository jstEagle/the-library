## 34. The Cost of the Verse

The verdict was sworn at dusk, and the stars had begun to burn again—one for each witness who knelt and gave their name to the tribunal—but the ritual was not finished. I knew that before anyone told me. The ninth verse has a shape, and a shape has an ending, and the ending was waiting inside me like a debt I'd been carrying since the day Bramwell first refused to sing it.

The seed sat in my palm where it had rested through every hour of the trial. It was lighter now. That should have warned me. Things grow heavy with what they hold, and the Thorn had just given up centuries of silence—had spoken its whole terrible testimony into the air of that ruined court while ghosts wept and Inquisitors forgot their orders. It had emptied itself out. Which meant there was room.

"Mara." Vane stood at the edge of the dais, her grey coat scorched at the hem, one arm bound where Ilse's people had caught her during the fighting. She looked older than she had in the orchard at Thornvale, which felt like another life, because most of it was. "The stars are returning. The dead are resting. Whatever you did—it worked."

"It isn't done." I said it quietly, but the ghost-echoes heard me. They always heard me now. The nearest one—an old man in robes that predated the war by two generations—turned his translucent head, and though he had no eyes left to see with, I felt his attention settle on me like snowfall.

"The verse requires an anchor," I said. "Bramwell never said what kind. But nothing in this city anchors itself. The wards didn't. The tribunal didn't. Everything here was held down by someone willing to stand still."

Wren was beside me. He had been beside me since he came stumbling back across the palace square with the seed clutched against his chest and blood on his teeth, not looking at me, not asking forgiveness, just holding it out like a boy returning something stolen from a temple—ashamed and certain all at once. He hadn't let go of my sleeve since. Now his grip tightened.

"You don't know that," he said. "You don't know it needs anything more. Maybe the verdict was enough. Maybe—"

"Wren." I turned my hand over so he could see the seed. It pulsed once, slow, like a heart remembering its rhythm. "It's hungry. Not for memory—not the way it fed off me on the road. For *witness*. The trial made it a thing that can be remembered instead of a thing that must be forgotten. And things that are remembered need somewhere to live."

I looked up at the sky through the broken vault of the throne room, where three new stars burned where there had been none a month ago. Each one was a sworn witness: Vane, kneeling not to me but to the verdict; Wren, who had signed his name with a smear of ash because he'd never learned to write it properly; and the ferryman of rust, who had appeared at the edge of the ruins two days ago without explanation, as if the river had finally let him go. Three stars. Three hundred more needed, the echoes said, before the truce could hold on its own weight.

But stars were witnesses. An anchor was different. An anchor was the thing that stayed behind when everyone else went home.

* * *

We found the crypt beneath the palace by following the verse itself. That sounds like poetry, but it wasn't—it was mechanics. Bramwell had taught me the ward-songs as patterns, as music, and I had spent thirty years of my life humming them without knowing why some verses ended in silence and one verse—the ninth—ended in a question. A question is a door held open. When I sang the eighth verse in the crypt, the last note hung in the cold air and would not resolve, and the wall opposite me grew warm, and then grew thin, and then was a doorway because I needed it to be one.

Beyond it: a small round chamber, no larger than the guild kitchen at Thornvale. No treasure. No throne of bone. Just a basin of black stone in the center, dry as old grief, and carved into the floor around it, in a script older than the war, a single line:

*A ward is a promise kept by a person.*

I understood then, standing there with Wren's breath loud behind me and the seed pulsing in my palm like a second heartbeat, why Bramwell had never sung the ninth verse. Why no one had sung it in a hundred years. Why the guild had chosen cages instead.

Because the cage was easy. The cage cost nothing but everything else—the valley's freedom, the sky's honesty, the truth about what lived under the orchard. You built a cage once and then you maintained it, generation after generation, and you called it protection, and you died comfortably knowing you had never once had to keep your promise in person.

The ninth verse asked for a person. Not their death. Their *continuance*. Someone who would stand at the hinge between magic and law for as long as they drew breath, hearing grievances, weighing them, refusing both sides the comfort of being right. And to bind that promise so it couldn't be broken or bartered away, the verse required payment—not blood, not gold, but memory. The anchor's own past, poured into the stone, so that the office would always be heavier than the woman holding it.

"Every recollection," the echo of the old juror said behind us. He had followed us down, or perhaps he had always been here; the dead of this city moved like weather. "Of the place that made you. That is the price. The valley that raised you will become a story someone else tells you."

"That's not fair," Wren said, and his voice cracked on the word, because fairness was the whole reason any of us were here, and here it was, failing us already. "She gave up enough. On the road, in the storms, at Ilse's camp—she paid and paid. Why does it have to be *everything*?"

"Because a partial promise is a cage," the echo said gently. "And we are done with cages."

* * *

I sat down on the edge of the basin. The black stone was warm. Of course it was. Everything the Thorn touched carried the heat of that star-fall fragment I'd found in the orchard a lifetime ago, warm in my mittens, warm in my pocket through every mile of the deadlands, warm now against my palm like a living thing saying *I know, I know, I don't want this either.*

The seed spoke. Not in my mother's voice this time—it had used that voice on the road because it was the lever that opened me fastest, and I had forgiven it for the cruelty of that, mostly. Now it spoke in its own voice, which turned out to sound like many voices braided together, judges and accused and the long dead chorus of a war nobody alive remembered correctly.

*You may refuse,* it said. *The verdict stands regardless. I will be stewarded. The stars will return. You owe me nothing further—you owed me a trial, and you gave me one, and no one in the history of this world has given me anything before.*

"That's not true," I said. "Bramwell gave you patience. A hundred years of it, even if he didn't know that's what he was doing. Even if the guild called it a cage and meant it as one." I laughed, and it came out wrong, wet and thin. "He told me the guild's true purpose wasn't protection. It was patience. He knew. Some part of him knew, all along, and he taught me the songs anyway and refused the ninth verse because he couldn't bear to ask it of me. He left it for me to choose. That was his gift. Terrible, stubborn old man."

*Mara.* The braid of voices softened. *If you pour your valley into that stone, you will not grieve it later. Grief requires the thing lost to remain somewhere in you. This will not. You will wake tomorrow in a world where Thornvale is a word other people use, and you will feel its absence the way you feel the absence of a country you never visited—curious, distant, secondhand. Is that a mercy or a mutilation? I honestly do not know. I have never been a person. I have only ever been a judgment that wanted to stop.*

"And now you're going to be a truce," I said. "Which needs a keeper."

*I am asking you not to do this.*

That was the worst part. Not the demand—the refusal. The monster under the orchard, the war-entity, the thing two armies had crossed a continent to seize or destroy, begging me to spare myself. I pressed my thumb against the seed and felt it tremble.

"Wren," I said. "Come here."

He came. He sat down next to me on the cold stone with his knees pulled up and his elbows on them, the way he used to sit at our campfire, and for a while neither of us said anything, because there was nothing left to negotiate and everything left to mean.

"When you stole my sister's memory back—or tried to, when you took the deal—" I started.

"Don't," he said.

"—you regretted it before you finished the trade. I saw your face. You handed over the seed and you were already dying of it. And then you walked into a Crown camp alone, knowing you might not walk out, and took it back. Not for forgiveness. Because it was right." I looked at him. His eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "So tell me if I'm wrong. Tell me if giving this up is the same as what you did—selling something that can't be bought back."

He thought about it. He actually thought about it, Wren, who answered everything fast and cheap because thinking slowly got street kids killed. Then he shook his head.

"No. What I did was trade something of hers for something of mine—my comfort, my guilt, gone, and she never even knew. That was theft twice over." He swallowed. "This is—you're not selling the valley. You're spending it. There's a difference. Spending goes somewhere. Buying the truce, buying the stars coming back, buying every village from here to the coast getting to argue about ward-lines in front of somebody fair instead of burning each other's fields." He shrugged, helpless, furious. "It's a bad price. But it buys the truest thing I've ever seen anybody buy."

"And you'll remember it for me?" I asked. "Not the details—I know those go too. Just... that I chose it. That I wasn't robbed. That I stood in front of the stone and put it down myself."

"I'll remember it so hard it'll annoy you," he said, and his voice broke clean through the middle of the joke, and he grabbed my shoulder, and I let him, and we stayed like that while the echo waited with the courtesy of the dead.

* * *

There was no ceremony. That surprised me. I had expected—something. Chanting, light, the basin filling with silver fire, the sky cracking open. Instead the ninth verse was almost plain. Eight verses of pattern and harmony, and then the ninth, which was four notes and a held breath, and the instruction carved into the floor, and the choice.

I sang it badly. My voice shook on the third note. The echoes sang it with me anyway, hundreds of translucent throats, the whole buried court of the capital rising through the floors above like water finding its level, and the sound of it was not beautiful—it was *true*, which is worse and better—and the basin began to fill with darkness that was not darkness but depth.

I knelt. I put both hands into the stone basin, and the seed settled between them, and I began.

I gave the orchard first. Every tree, every ladder, the exact green of unripe apples, the way Bramwell's cider smelled of smoke because he refused to clean the press properly. I gave the ward-walks at dawn, frost in my boots, the hum of healthy wards under my palm like a cat's purr. I gave the guild hall with its dwindling ranks—old Fenwick who snored through meetings, Tamsin who left before I could learn why. I gave my mother's hands kneading bread, though she herself had been taken by fever when I was nine; the hands survived, at least for one more minute. I gave the taste of well water. The sound of the valley wind in the pines, which I had complained about my entire childhood and would apparently never complain about again. I gave my tenth birthday, my twelfth, the winter Bramwell taught me to read star-charts by candlelight and lied beautifully about what the charts were for.

It did not hurt. That was the cruelest part. It felt like exhaling after holding your breath underwater—a long, smooth release, each memory sliding out of me and settling into the dark of the basin like coins dropped into a well, each one making the water rise a little, and the rising water rising up through the floor and the foundations and the bedrock of the whole wounded country, sealing the truce into the bones of the earth the way a scar seals skin.

I gave the last thing last: the night the stars vanished. Midnight, the orchard, the sky going blank all at once like a page wiped clean. Bramwell collapsing in the doorway, whispering *it remembers her name*. My terror sharpening into responsibility, my whole self changing shape in a single breath.

I held that one a moment longer than the others. Then I let it go too.

The basin filled. The verse resolved. Somewhere far above, I am told, the fourth star ignited—mine, the echoes said afterward, the anchor-star, the one that will burn as long as I live and mark the place where a person kept a promise.

Then it was done, and I was kneeling on cold stone in a crypt under a ruined palace, and there was a young man beside me crying openly and pretending he wasn't, and there was a seed in a basin that was only a seed now, quiet, spent, its work begun, and there was a hole in me shaped like a valley.

"What was it like?" Wren whispered. "The place. The one you gave up. Can you—do you know what you lost?"

I searched, honestly. I reached into myself the way you reach for a word on the tip of your tongue.

"There's a name," I said slowly. "*Thornvale.* People say it like it matters. Vane said it yesterday like it mattered. And I can hear that it's supposed to matter, the way you can hear music through a wall—there's a rhythm there, something important happening on the other side." I flexed my empty hands. "But I don't miss it. I can't. Missing needs a door, and mine's bricked over. I just know the door exists."

He nodded, jaw tight, and helped me stand. My legs held. That seemed like a lot to ask of them, and they managed anyway.

Above us, faintly, through six floors of ruin, someone shouted. Then several someones. Running feet, the clash of steel being drawn, and a voice I recognized—Ilse's high priestess voice, cracked now, ragged, but unmistakable—crying out words I couldn't make sense of yet, except that they were not words of surrender.

Wren and I looked at each other.

"They're not done," he said.

"No," I said, and picked up the ledger, and started up the stairs.
