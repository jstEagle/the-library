## 36. A New Warden's Oath

They gave her three days to refuse.

That was the tribunal's own rule, dug out of the ghost-echoes and the crumbling law-books of the palace archive: no office could be accepted in the heat of the verdict's aftermath. A judge sworn too soon was a judge sworn in fear, and fear made tyrants. So for three days Mara Dravenmoor sat in a half-collapsed chamber that had once been a records office, with dust on her sleeves and a quill she barely knew how to use, and waited to see if she would say no.

Wren brought her food. That was most of what he did now—appeared at odd hours with bread or stewed apples or, once, a suspiciously good pie he refused to explain—and sat on the windowsill while she read. The window still had glass in it, which made it one of the finest rooms in the city. Through it she could see the sky, and the sky was wrong in the best possible way: crowded. Stars hung over the ruined capital like lanterns over a market square, dozens of them, each one a sworn witness from the trial, each one burning with a steadiness that no natural fire had ever managed.

She'd stopped flinching when they looked at her. Mostly.

"You're going to do it," Wren said on the third evening. It wasn't a question. He said it around an apple, talking with his mouth full because he'd decided some time ago that formal occasions were no excuse for manners.

"I'm going to do it."

"Could've saved yourself three days of dust."

"The rule exists for a reason." She set down the page she'd been reading—the fourth copy of the oath, transcribed by four different ghost-echoes who disagreed about punctuation but not about substance. "If I swear tomorrow and regret it next year, there's no unswearing. The last Warden who tried—" She stopped. The last Warden who tried had been Jude Halloran himself, three hundred years ago, and what had happened to him was written in a ledger she'd carried across the deadlands: his name struck through, his office dissolved, his valley turned into a cage to hide what he'd done.

"What?" Wren asked.

"Nothing." She looked at the oath again. "I want to be sure I'm choosing this. Not inheriting it."

He chewed thoughtfully. "You know those aren't actually different things, right? You choose your inheritance or it chooses you. Either way you end up carrying it."

"That's very wise for someone eating stolen fruit."

"It wasn't stolen. I bought it." A pause. "With money I found."

Mara laughed—actually laughed, short and surprised, the first time in longer than she could measure. She couldn't measure it, in fact. That was the thing about the deadlands and the ritual and the cost of the verse: her childhood was gone, all of it, every recollection of Thornvale spent like coins from a purse. She knew the facts of her life the way you know a story someone told you. She had been born in a valley. She had been raised by a guild of liars. An old man named Bramwell had taught her ward-songs and died naming her Warden, and she could recite every detail and feel none of it, the way you can describe the taste of something you've never eaten.

The Thorn had given back one memory—a single grace, Bramwell's face lifted in song—but one memory is not a childhood. One memory is a door into a house where all the furniture has been sold.

So yes. She wanted to be sure.

* * *

The swearing took place at dawn, because the ghost-echoes insisted. All oaths of the old court had been sworn at first light, when the witnesses of the night and the laborers of the day could both attend. There were no laborers anymore, not in the capital, but there were the dead walking their loops through the streets below, replaying their final moments, and there were the stars overhead, and between those two congregations Mara supposed she had an audience enough.

They gathered in the great hall where the trial had been held. It had been a throne room once; the tribunal had used it as a courtroom instead, and now it would be neither. Vane stood on the left side of the hall with a contingent of Crown soldiers who had arrived two days after the verdict—stiff, uncertain men and women who kept looking up at the ceiling as if expecting it to fall. Ilse's people were gone. What remained of the Thornbound after their high priestess was unmade had scattered into the provinces, carrying word of the verdict like embers on the wind. Some would come seeking justice. Some would come seeking revenge. Both would find the same door.

Vane herself looked different. Not softer—Serah Vane did not soften, Mara suspected, in this life or any other—but lighter, the way a blade is lighter after you've finally sharpened it. Her family's name had been dragged through the tribunal's light, her ancestor exposed as executioner rather than victim, and she had knelt afterward—not to Mara, never to Mara—to the verdict itself. She wore grey now instead of the Inquisition's black. Nobody had ordered her to. She'd simply stopped wearing the other color.

"You could still refuse," Vane said, as they waited for the first light to reach the broken roof. "The office dies with your refusal. The truce holds anyway—it's sworn in the stars now—but the grievances would go unheard. Cases would pile up. In thirty years there'd be another war."

"You're trying to talk me into it?"

"I'm telling you what happens if you don't. I find people make better decisions when they know the price of refusal." Her mouth twitched, almost a smile. "It's how I was trained. It turns out my training works better when I aim it honestly."

The first light came through the gap in the roof and laid a long pale stripe across the floor, and the ghost-echoes began to arrive.

They came the way they always came now—not trapped in their loops, since the verdict had freed the city's dead to rest, but summoned, called up from wherever the rested go by the simple mechanism of being needed. They filled the hall in ranks: judges and soldiers and farmers and a woman in a scholar's robe who had been the tribunal's clerk three hundred years ago and apparently still resented the handwriting of everyone around her. At the head of them stood the echo of the first tribunal's presiding judge—an old man with a voice like a landslide, whose sentence without trial had begun everything.

He didn't look at Mara with guilt. Ghosts, she had learned, did not work that way. He looked at her with attention, which was somehow worse and much more useful.

"Mara Dravenmoor," he said, and his voice went through the hall like weather. "The office of Warden stands vacant. The truce stands sworn. Do you come to fill the office or to bury it?"

"I come to fill it," Mara said. Her voice held. She'd rehearsed this part against the window glass for three days and it had paid off. "Not because I inherited it. Not because an old man died saying my name. Because somebody has to hear the grievances, and I'm still standing."

A murmur ran through the echoes—not disapproval. Recognition. The clerk-woman nodded sharply, as if Mara had finally formatted something correctly.

"The oath," the old judge said. "Speak it as written. We will know if you change the words."

"I wouldn't dare. Your clerk scares me."

Someone in the ranks of the dead laughed. It echoed strangely, laughter three centuries old finding new use.

Mara stepped onto the floor's pale stripe of light. The seed was gone—given up, transformed, its essence scattered into the returning stars—but she could feel the truce itself now, faintly, the way you feel a large animal breathing in a dark room. It lived in the sky. It lived in the wards she would rebuild, not as cages this time but as fences, borders, lines on a map that meant *this far and no further, and here is a door.*

She spoke the oath.

It was long. The old court had believed in long oaths; they'd believed, correctly, that anything worth swearing should take long enough that a liar would get bored halfway through. Mara swore to hear every grievance brought before her, from the smallest ward-line dispute to the largest plea of blood. She swore to hear both sides, and to hear the side she hated last, so that hatred would have time to tire. She swore to hold no army, command no prison, and keep no secrets from the sworn record. She swore to serve until she died, and to train a successor who might choose differently, because an office that could only be inherited was just a prettier kind of cage.

"And the last clause," the old judge said, when she reached it.

Mara paused. The last clause was the one the four transcriptions had argued about, and in the end she'd gone to the oldest version, the one carved into the foundation stone beneath the palace, and found that the argument was pointless because the original clause was only six words long.

"I swear to be fair," she said, "when fairness costs me."

Silence. Then the whole hall of the dead bowed—not to her, she understood suddenly, the way Vane had understood it kneeling in the ruins. To the office. To the idea. To the six words.

Above them, through the broken roof, the stars brightened. Just slightly. Just enough.

* * *

"There's paperwork," the clerk-echo said afterward, materializing beside Mara with the air of someone delivering bad news she'd been saving up for three hundred years. "There is an enormous amount of paperwork. The old court filed everything. Every ruling, every dissent, every complaint about the quality of the ink. You have inherited four hundred years of unresolved appeals that were pending when the war ended, and I have catalogued them, and there are nine hundred and forty-one."

"Nine hundred and forty-one."

"Some are duplicates. Complainants filed twice when they felt ignored. Several complainants are dead, but the dead can still be heard—you saw that tonight—so we cannot simply strike them." She pressed a ledger into Mara's hands. It was heavy enough to be a weapon. "The first hearing is scheduled in eleven days. A village in the eastern provinces disputes a ward-line. Both parties are living. This was considered a simple case, once."

"Eleven days."

"You'll want time to prepare. Also, the office requires staff. A Warden cannot hear cases alone; the oath forbids solitary judgment, because solitary judgment is how the last one went wrong." The clerk looked at her over the top of spectacles that had been dust for centuries and somehow still worked. "Have you anyone in mind?"

Mara opened her mouth to say no, and then a familiar voice came from the doorway of the hall, where a young man had been leaning against the frame eating something he'd almost certainly found rather than purchased.

"As a matter of fact," Wren said, "she does."

* * *

He swore at noon, in the smaller chamber that had been designated the Warden's hearing room—a modest space with one intact window, two tables, and chairs that Vane's soldiers had repaired well enough to sit on. No stars overhead, no ranks of the dead. Just Mara behind one table, Wren standing in front of the other, and the clerk-echo hovering with the oath of a sworn witness, which was shorter than the Warden's oath but stricter.

"You understand what you're swearing," Mara said. "This isn't following me around and picking pockets for the cause. A sworn witness is bound. When you testify, you testify true. If you lie under oath, the truce itself knows, and the stars know, and—" she gestured vaguely upward "—they're all watching now. Permanently. It's a whole thing."

"I've been watched my whole life," Wren said. "Usually by people who wanted to hang me. These ones glow. Honestly it's an improvement." But then he was quiet for a moment, turning something over, and when he spoke again the flippancy had drained out of his voice the way water drains from a cracked cup. "You should know why I'm doing it. Before I swear. Because the clerk says the oath binds true, and I'd rather the truth be said now than dragged out of me later."

Mara folded her hands. "Say it, then."

"I spent a memory crossing the river. My choice, my coin. And the memory I spent was my sister." His jaw worked. "I don't remember her face anymore. I remember that I had a sister. I remember that the guild sold her, and that I got out and she didn't, and I remember that losing the memory of her hurt worse than losing her did, which doesn't make sense, but grief never does. And then at the palace gate I traded the seed away to get her memory back, and it was a lie, the trade was a lie, and I stole the seed back knowing I probably wouldn't live through it." He shrugged, awkward, a boy again for one unguarded second. "I didn't do it for forgiveness. I said that. I meant it. I did it because it was right, and because the seed was the first thing that ever needed me, and now—" he looked around the shabby little room, at the repaired chairs, at Mara "—now maybe this is the second thing. Being needed. On purpose. By choice. I never got to choose anything before. Sold, recruited, robbed, ran. So I'm choosing this. Sworn witness to the Warden of the truce. First one. Only one, unless you count the glowing dead lady."

"I count the glowing dead lady," the clerk said primly.

"Then second one. Fine."

Mara found she had to clear her throat before she could speak. "For what it's worth," she said, "I chose you too. Out of everyone living, everyone I met on that road—I chose you. So whatever else this oath is, it's also me saying: you were worth choosing."

He blinked fast, twice, and covered it by standing up straighter. "Right. Read me the words, then, before I say something embarrassing."

The clerk read. Wren repeated. He swore to testify true, to keep the record, to stand witness against the powerful even when the powerful were his friends, and—this clause made him snort—to surrender all stolen goods entered into evidence, though the clerk conceded that goods acquired before the swearing fell outside the office's jurisdiction, a loophole Wren received like a knighthood.

When it was done, the truce marked him. Mara felt it happen—a small bright thread tying itself from the boy to the sky—and above the hearing room's single window, visible through the glass, one star burned a shade warmer than the others.

"First sworn witness of the new court," the clerk announced, making a note. "Recorded. Filed. Witnessed by the undersigned, the presiding echoes, and approximately forty stars, several of whom have opinions about your posture."

"My *posture*?"

"They're very old," the clerk said. "Everything bothers them."

* * *

Evening came, and with it the first quiet hour Mara had owned in what felt like years. She sat in the hearing room alone, the ledger of nine hundred and forty-one appeals open in front of her, and did not read it. Instead she watched the light move across the table and let herself feel the shape of what she'd done.

Purpose chosen, not inherited. She turned the phrase over in her mind like a stone picked up on a road. Bramwell had named her Warden with his dying breath, and she had carried the title across the deadlands like a wound. But wounds close. Titles, it turned out, could be set down and picked back up again on purpose, and the difference between the two was the difference between a chain and a tool. She was still the guild's heir. She was still the Thorn's chosen. None of that had changed. What had changed was that she had stood in the light of forty stars and six words and said *yes, and here is why,* and the why belonged to her.

A knock at the door. Vane leaned in, still in her grey, holding a letter.

"Courier came up from the south," she said. "Fast rider. Wouldn't give it to anyone but the Warden." She set it on the table and, after a moment's hesitation, stayed in the doorway rather than leaving. "It's from Thornvale."

Mara went still.

She hadn't written to the valley yet—that was a task she'd been circling for weeks, drafting and burning drafts, unsure how you tell a place the truth about itself when you can't remember loving it. She knew the facts: the survivors had rebuilt, the orchard was replanted, the guild's name was ash in every mouth. But a letter from them—

"Are you going to open it?" Vane asked.

"Yes." Mara broke the seal. Unfolded the page. Read it once, quickly, the way you touch a hot pan. Then again, slowly.

It was from the valley council. Formal, stiff, written by someone unaccustomed to writing to power. It informed her that the people of Thornvale had heard of the trial, the verdict, the new court. It informed her that they had debated long and hard among themselves. And it informed her that a delegation was already on the road, carrying with them the first formal grievance ever to be filed before the Court of the Truce:

*We, the survivors of Thornvale, bring suit against the estate of the Warden's Guild—for the lies we were raised on, the magic we were denied, and the dead the guild let us bury without ever telling us why.*

Mara set the letter down. Her hands were steady. Her heart was not.

"Wren," she called toward the hallway, where she could already hear him coming, drawn by the smell of news the way he was drawn by the smell of pie. "Get the record book. Our first case just walked three hundred miles to find us."
