## 37. Letters to a Valley

The first letter took her three days.

Not because she didn't know what to say—she knew exactly what to say, had rehearsed it in the dark of the tribunal's antechamber a dozen times—but because every time she sat down to write it, she reached for a memory to anchor the words and found nothing there. She wanted to open with *I remember the orchard in autumn* and could not remember an orchard at all. She wanted to say *the smell of Bramwell's pipe on the ward-stones at dusk* and the sentence died on the page, because Bramwell's face was a blank oval wearing a voice, and his pipe was a fact she knew the way she knew the capital's founding date: true, recorded, unfelt.

So she wrote around the holes.

*To the people of Thornvale,* she began, in her careful Warden's hand, the one Vane had once said looked like it had been copied out of a law book. *You do not know me. Some of you will know my name anyway, because names travel faster than truth, and mine has been traveling badly.*

She told them everything. That was the point of the letters—that was the only point. That the guild they had tithed to for two hundred years was not a guild of protectors. That the wards they sang at dusk were not songs of shelter but the bars of a cage, and that beneath their orchards something ancient slept without trial, without verdict, without even the mercy of being named. That their grandmothers' grandmothers had been lied to so thoroughly that the lie had become weather.

That she herself was the proof, and the instrument, and the apology.

*I am the last apprentice of the Warden's Guild,* she wrote, *and I am also its first honest officer, which is not a compliment to either of us.*

She wrote forty-one letters. One for every household in Thornvale—or what she believed was every household; the guild census in her ledger was sixty years old, and she had no way to check it, and no memory to check it against. Forty-one letters, each written by hand, each sealed with the new seal of the truce—a star with nine points, the ninth slightly larger, because Mara had insisted, and the sigil-cutter had grumbled, and Mara had won.

Wren found her on the third evening, asleep at the desk with her cheek on the fortieth letter, ink smudged along her jaw like a bruise.

"You've got," he said, when she woke, "ink on your face."

"I know."

"It's on your *teeth*, Mara."

"I licked the pen. It seemed efficient." She sat up. Her neck crackled like green wood. "Is the courier ready?"

"The courier is a mule and a man who charges by the league and complains by the mile. He's ready. Are you?"

She looked down at the stack. Forty-one envelopes, squared and bound with cord, weighing almost nothing and more than anything she'd ever carried across the deadlands.

"No," she said. "Send them anyway."

* * *

The answers began coming back in the spring, which was sooner than she expected, and worse.

The first return came unopened. The courier's note pinned to it read: *Refused at the door. The Hallorans said no Dravenmoor writes to them.* Mara read the note twice, set it down, and went to water the young orchard behind the tribunal hall—the saplings she'd ordered planted the week after the trial, apple stock from the eastern slopes, nothing yet but sticks and stubbornness. She stood there a long while with the watering can going cold in her hands.

*The Hallorans.* She turned the name over. It should have meant something. It was probably the family three doors from the guildhall, or the miller's line, or the people whose fence she'd climbed as a child—and she would never know, because she had poured every recollection of Thornvale into the ninth verse's anchor and sealed the truce with them, and the truce did not give refunds.

That was the arithmetic nobody warned her about. Justice had cost her the exact thing justice was supposed to be *for*. She had bought a valley its truth with the loss of her own home, and now the valley was sending the purchase back marked *refused*.

The second return came unopened too. And the third, and the fourth. By midsummer she had eleven returned letters stacked in a drawer, seals intact, corners softening from handling. The courier's notes grew terse. *Refused. Refused. Would not take it from my hand. Refused, and the man spat.*

"They hate you," Wren observed, not unkindly, over dinner. He said things like that the way other people passed the salt.

"They don't hate me. They've never met me." She pushed stew around her bowl. "They hate what I told them. There's a difference."

"Is there? From their side of the door?"

Mara considered this. "From their side of the door, probably not."

"That's what I thought." He ate for a while. Then: "My mother used to say you can't unsell a lie to someone who built their house on it. They'll burn the messenger before they move the walls."

"Your mother sounds wise."

"She sold children to a guild, so, mixed record." He shrugged. "But she was right about that part."

Twelve returns. Fourteen. Seventeen. Mara kept writing—not new letters, but copies, patient duplicates of the same truth, sent to the households that had refused, then to the households that hadn't answered at all, which was its own kind of refusal, quieter and somehow heavier. She told herself it was duty. She told herself the office required it. Both were true, and neither was the whole truth, and she knew it: some part of her kept writing because every envelope addressed to Thornvale was the closest she could come to touching a home that had been amputated from her, and scar tissue aches toward what it lost.

Then, on a grey morning in late summer, one came back different.

It was addressed to her in a hand she didn't recognize—small, careful, old. Not refused. *Answered.* Her own letter was folded inside it, opened, read, creased from rereading. And pressed between the pages of the reply, flat and brown-edged and impossibly fragile, was an apple blossom.

Mara sat very still for a long moment before she touched it. The petals had gone the color of weak tea, papery, translucent at the edges. Someone had pressed it recently—it still held a ghost of shape, a suggestion of the roundness it had worn on the branch. Someone had walked out under an apple tree in Thornvale, picked a blossom, carried it inside, laid it in a book or between linens, waited days for it to dry, and then wrapped it in paper and paid a courier to carry it across half the deadlands to a woman they had every reason to despise.

She made herself read the letter first. Discipline. Bramwell's voice in her head—*read the whole page before you feel any of it*—though the voice came without a face, and that, as always, was a small knife between the ribs.

*Mara Dravenmoor,* it began.

*My name is Edith Marsh. You will not remember me, and I understand why, and I want you to know that I understand, because I think most of this valley does not understand, and may never.*

*I was a girl when the wards fell. I saw the stars go out. I heard the screaming in the lane and I did some of it myself. When your letters came, I burned the first one. I want you to know that too. I burned it in the kitchen stove and felt righteous for a full day.*

*Then my grandson asked me why the stars were coming back one at a time, and who was lighting them, and I found I could not tell him the story we have always told, because your letter had put a crack in it and the crack has been widening ever since. A story with a crack lets the light through or lets the cold through, and I have spent the winter finding out which.*

*It is the light. I am sorry to report this, as I was quite comfortable.*

*I cannot speak for the valley. Half of them will tell you the guild protected us and your letter is treason, and they are not fools—they are people whose whole lives were built on a floor that you pulled up to show them the pit underneath, and a person standing over a pit is allowed some anger. But I can speak for one old woman with an apple tree that was planted, I am told, the year the guild was founded, over the thing your letter names. My grandmother tended that tree. Her grandmother tended it. We called it the Warden's Tree and we were proud of it, and I have since learned what we were proud of, and I have not decided whether to cut it down or to guard it with a spear.*

*I lean toward guarding it. Things that grow over graves are not the grave's fault.*

*The blossom is from that tree. It seemed right that some part of it should go to the person carrying the heaviest part of the truth. You gave up your remembering to give us ours. That is not nothing. That is possibly everything.*

*Come in the autumn, if you can. Come quietly, and come to the back door, and I will introduce you as my niece from the east, because there are doors in this valley that would stay shut if they knew you, and I would rather you see one open one. I make a cider that would make a dead man nostalgic. Bring your thief. I have heard about the thief.*

*With respect, and more grief than fits in a letter,*

*Edith Marsh*

Mara read it four times. On the fourth reading she discovered she was crying, which annoyed her, because she had budgeted for grief and this wasn't grief, it was something else, something with no name in any ledger she owned. Homesickness, maybe—for a home that was now a stranger. For a tree she couldn't remember, planted by people she couldn't name, over a prisoner she had freed, tended by a girl who had screamed in the lane on the night the stars went out and had grown into an old woman brave enough to apologize to a piece of paper.

She lifted the blossom out with both hands, the way you lift a sleeping bird.

"Wren," she said. Her voice came out wrong and she cleared it. "Wren."

He appeared in the doorway with the wariness of a man who has learned that when Mara says his name in *that* tone, it precedes either a mission or a feeling, and he preferred missions. "What."

She held up the blossom. "An old woman in Thornvale sent me an apple blossom from the tree that grows over the cage."

He looked at it. He looked at her face. "And you're crying about a dead flower."

"It's not—" She laughed, wetly. "No. Yes. Shut up."

"Edith Marsh," he read upside-down, squinting. "She says bring the thief. Do I get cider?"

"You get cider."

"Do I get to be introduced as your notorious associate, or am I also a fake nephew?"

"You're a thief, Wren. We're not hiding it. Hiding it never works—I have extensive evidence."

He grinned, and took the blossom from her with unexpected gentleness, turning it in the light. "Huh. It's from the tree over the cage." He handed it back. "You know what that makes it?"

"A flower."

"It makes it a witness," Wren said. "Everything from that ground is a witness now. That's the whole point of you, isn't it? Stars as witnesses. Might as well have trees."

Mara looked at him sometimes—moments like this—and marveled that she had once caught him robbing her camp with a stolen pie in each hand. "When did you become wise?"

"When the alternative stopped paying."

* * *

She wrote back that evening, and the letter came easily, the way the first ones never had—because now she wasn't writing into a void, she was writing to a person, and persons forgive the holes in you that voids only widen.

*Dear Edith Marsh (who I will call Aunt Edith at the back door, and who should feel free to correct my manners),*

*I burned nothing of yours, but I burned plenty of my own nights over these letters, so I think we are even on small fires.*

*I will come in the autumn. I will come quietly. I will eat your cider until you regret the invitation. And I will stand under the Warden's Tree, because I need to see it—I need to see the thing that grew over the grave, since I am apparently the person responsible for deciding what grows there next. I have already begun planting a new orchard here, over the old palace crypt, which the masons assure me is structurally insane and which I have done anyway. If it works, I will send you a cutting. If it fails, I will send you an apology and a better plan.*

*Tell your grandson the stars are lit one at a time because each one must be sworn as a witness before it burns. Tell him the newest one is called the Marsh Star, because I have just decided that, and Wardens are allowed some sentiment. Tell him that when he is old enough, there is work in the new court for anyone who can listen to two people shouting and find the third thing they actually mean. It is mostly that. Nobody tells you that. Justice is ninety percent listening and ten percent paperwork and the stories leave out both.*

*Thank you for the blossom. It sits on my desk where I can see it. It is doing more work than half my staff.*

*Your niece from the east (at the back door),*

*Mara*

She sealed it with the nine-pointed star and set it atop the outgoing pile, and then she sat for a while in the lamplight, looking at the seventeen refused letters in the drawer.

She didn't throw them away. She had thought about it—had genuinely stood over the drawer with kindling in mind—but in the end she'd taken them out, smoothed them, and filed them in the archive under a heading she'd written herself: *Returned Unopened — To Be Delivered Again When Asked For.* Because that was the thing about truth, she was learning. It kept. It waited in drawers and archives and pressed blossoms and old women's kitchens, and it did not expire just because the door was shut. Someday—ten years, fifty years, a hundred—some child in Thornvale would ask the question Edith's grandson had asked, and some parent would fail to answer it honestly, and then someone would walk to the archive of the new court and ask, awkwardly, whether the Warden had left anything for them.

And she would hand them the letter. Seals intact. Corners soft. Truth doesn't rot. It just waits to be let in the back door.

* * *

Autumn was six weeks off, and the court filled them the way courts fill—slowly, then all at once.

Word had spread, as word does, that the Star Court—the commoners had named it that within a month, over Mara's mild objection and Wren's open delight—would hear *anything*. Not just great matters of magic and law, though those came: a border village claiming a hedge-witch had cursed their well (she hadn't; she'd cleaned it, and charged fairly, and the real culprit was upstream tannery runoff, which took two weeks and one furious tanner to untangle). A Thornbound remnant petitioning for the return of confiscated relics. A Crown surveyor disputing a ward-line with a farmer whose grandfather had plowed across it for forty years.

But mostly it was small. A widow and a beekeeper arguing over hives on shared land. Two brothers splitting an inheritance and a grudge in equal measure. A man who claimed his neighbor's charm-mongering had devalued his property, which, upon investigation, was true, and was settled with a fine and a public posting of what charms actually did—which was, mostly, nothing, a ruling that made Mara briefly the most hated woman in three parishes and then, oddly, a trusted one.

Vane came to observe, once, sitting at the back with her arms crossed and her face arranged into official neutrality. Mara caught her eye during the bee dispute, and Vane's mouth did something that on a less disciplined woman would have been a smile.

"You enjoy this," Vane accused her afterward, in the corridor.

"I endure this. There's a difference."

"There isn't. From my side of the door." And she left before Mara could decide whether she'd been insulted or understood.

Wren kept the docket. He'd turned out to have a gift for it—an ear for which grievances were real and which were armor, which complainants needed a verdict and which needed only to be heard. "Third thing they actually mean," he'd mutter, scribbling. "Old lady Marsh was right. This is all just listening with furniture."

On the last day of summer, with the docket closed and the light going long and gold through the hall's new windows, a clerk brought in the season's final item—a single envelope, forwarded through four hands, postmarked from somewhere in the eastern hills.

Mara opened it expecting another grievance.

It was not a grievance. It was a list. Eleven names, written in several different hands, some shaky, some firm—one of them, she realized with a jolt, matching the handwriting of a letter she had received months ago and answered with a promise.

*These are the households in Thornvale that ask you to come,* the covering note read. *Eleven, so far. More are asking, slowly. The others are not asking yet, but some of them have stopped burning your letters, which Edith Marsh says is how it starts. Come in the autumn. Come to the back doors. The tree is heavy with fruit this year, and the valley does not know what to do with a harvest grown over a grave, and I think perhaps you do.*

Mara read it twice. Then she looked up at Wren, who was pretending very hard to be absorbed in the docket, and who had absolutely already read it over her shoulder, because he was Wren.

"Autumn," she said.

"We'd need to leave within the fortnight to make the mountain passes before snow."

"I know."

"And you'll want to pack the good coat, because you refuse to look like a vagrant in front of your own valley, even though technically you can't remember it well enough to be homesick, which you insist is a technicality."

"I know, Wren."

"And the mule—"

"*Wren.*" She folded the list of eleven names and put it inside her coat, over her heart, which was ridiculous and which she did anyway. "Start packing."

He grinned and went. Outside the window, the first of the evening stars was coming up—the Marsh Star, third from the horizon's edge, burning steady, sworn and watching. Mara stood alone in the emptying hall for a moment, one hand flat against the list of names, and let herself feel the thing she'd been rationing all year:

She was going home. To a home that had forgotten her face, that had burned her words, that was learning, one back door at a time, to open again.

Eleven households. Seventeen refused letters waiting in a drawer. One pressed blossom on her desk, and a tree full of fruit over an empty cage, and an old woman's cider, and whatever waited for her at the end of the road she had once fled down with a seed in her palm and the sky falling.

She blew out the lamp and went to pack.
