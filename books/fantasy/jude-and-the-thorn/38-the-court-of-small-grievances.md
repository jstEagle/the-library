## 38. The Court of Small Grievances

The case, as it turned out, was about a fence.

Not a fence, exactly. A ward-line. But the two farmers from Hollowbrook had been arguing about it so long that by the time they stood before the tribunal in the restored great hall of the old capital, they had both forgotten which came first—the fence or the line—and were now simply arguing on principle, the way people do when a grievance has outlived its cause.

Mara sat at the center of the curved bench, the Warden's seat, and listened.

To her left, Serah Vane sat with her ledger open and her pen moving in small, precise strokes. She no longer wore grey. The Crown had formally released her from the Inquisition six months ago—*retired with honor*, the writ said, though everyone knew what it actually meant—and she had surprised precisely no one by taking up residence in the capital anyway, serving as the tribunal's prosecutor-of-record. It was, she'd told Mara once over terrible wine, the only honest work left for someone who had spent her whole life believing in rules. *Someone has to argue the other side properly*, she'd said, *or your verdicts are just sermons.*

To Mara's right sat Wren, who held no office whatsoever and had refused every one offered to him. He was the tribunal's first sworn witness, which meant his job was to remember things and say them aloud when asked, and he treated this responsibility with the gravity of a man who had once been paid to steal. He was currently eating an apple.

"Master Aldous," Mara said. "You've stated that the ward-line runs through the middle of your well."

"It does." Aldous was a broad man with sunburned forearms and the particular stubbornness of someone who had dug that well himself. "It has always run through the middle of my well. My father dug it. His father helped dig it. The line was drawn *after*."

"That's not true," said the other farmer, a thin woman named Petra Coyle whose voice had the worn smoothness of long argument. "The line was drawn first. The survey-stone is still there. I've shown it to him eleven times."

"You showed me a rock."

"A *marked* rock."

"With a scratch on it."

"The scratch is a rune, you absolute—" Petra caught herself, glanced at the bench, and composed her face into something almost dignified. "The scratch is a rune, Warden. Old script. It marks the boundary between our holdings as they were granted after the Reckoning."

Mara nodded slowly. "And the well?"

"My grandfather," Aldous said, "dug the well because the line gave him water rights on his side, and then the river shifted, and now the water sits mostly under her field, and I'll be damned if I'm going to let a river decide where my family's water lives."

There was a pause. Vane wrote something. Wren took another bite of apple.

"And you, Mistress Coyle," Mara said, "your objection isn't really to the well. It's to what happens if we move the line."

Petra's shoulders dropped half an inch, which for her was a confession. "If the line moves to follow the water, then my eastern pasture goes to him. And if my eastern pasture goes to him, then the road I built through it—" she stopped, jaw tight. "I laid that road myself. Fourteen years. Every stone."

"So the dispute is the road."

"The dispute is that nothing stays where you put it," Petra said, with sudden raw honesty. "The river moved. The wards fell. The stars went out and came back different—I've looked, Warden, I've *looked*, the constellations aren't all in their old places. And now you're telling me the one thing I can hold still is a line in the dirt, and I'm supposed to believe that will hold either."

The hall was quiet. It was a good hall for quiet—high vaulted ceiling, pale stone, the ghost-echoes drifting in the upper galleries like dust motes that occasionally remembered being people. Since the verdict, the echoes had stayed. Not haunting, exactly. Witnessing. They gathered whenever the tribunal sat, drawn to the practice of judgment the way moths were drawn to anything that resembled light.

One of them drifted closer now—an old woman's shape, translucent, tilting her head at Petra with something like recognition.

Mara had learned, over months, to read them. This one had been a surveyor. She could tell by the way the echo kept glancing at the floor, measuring.

"Mistress Coyle," Mara said, "you're right. Nothing stays where you put it. Rivers move. Stars drift. I have given up more than I can name to learn that lesson, and I don't recommend the tuition." She leaned forward. "But here is what I know. The line doesn't hold because it's true. It holds because two people keep agreeing to it. That's all any law ever was. So we're not going to find the true place for your boundary. We're going to find the place you'll both agree to keep marking, year after year, even when the river argues otherwise."

Aldous frowned. "And the well?"

"Shared," Mara said. "Draw rights alternate by season. The road stays hers, with a right of passage for his carts, toll-free, in perpetuity—which means, Master Aldous, that your grandchildren will be bound to keep their hands off a widow's road, and her grandchildren bound to let yours cross. That's the bargain. Neither of you wins. Both of you are bound. Welcome to justice."

Vane's pen had stopped. She was looking at Mara with an expression she would have denied under oath: approval.

"The tribunal will draft the accord tonight," Mara said. "Sign tomorrow. And if either of you comes back here claiming the other broke it, you'll both stand before me again, and I warn you—" she let the weariness show, just a little, "—I have heard this exact argument eleven times already this year, and I am running out of patience in alphabetical order."

That got a laugh, even from Aldous. The echoes in the gallery rustled like leaves.

* * *

They cleared the hall as the light went amber through the tall windows. Wren finished his apple and set the core on the bench beside him with ceremony, as though it were evidence.

"Eleven times," he said. "Was that real or rhetoric?"

"Nine. I rounded up for effect."

"See, this is what nobody tells you about saving the world." He hopped down from the bench and stretched. "Nobody tells you it turns into *this*. Fences. Wells. Two farmers who hate each other more than they've ever hated anything, including the actual war."

"The war was abstract," Mara said, gathering the day's petitions. "The fence is Tuesday."

"Hm." Wren considered this. "Write that down. Put it over the door."

Vane approached, rolling her ledger closed. "The Hollowbrook accord, the mill-river arbitration, and the Tarnish ward-tithe," she recited. "Three cases resolved, none of them involving the fate of creation. Are you bored yet, Warden?"

"No," Mara said, and found that it was true, and that the truth of it still surprised her sometimes. "Are you?"

Vane smiled the small dry smile she rationed like coin. "I spent twenty years hunting apocalypses. It turns out I'm good at wells." She paused at the bench, and her voice lowered—not soft, Vane didn't do soft, but quieter. "The echo in the gallery today. The surveyor. She spoke to me while the farmers argued."

Mara went still. The echoes rarely spoke. When they did, it mattered. "What did she say?"

"She asked how many boundaries I'd enforced in my career." Vane's jaw worked. "She wanted to know if any of them had been fair. I told her I'd tried. She said—" and here Vane's composure flickered, just at the edges, the way it only did when her family's history surfaced through the cracks, "—she said *trying is the whole of the law, everything else is commentary.* Then she drifted back up into the galleries and started measuring the floor again."

"She's been measuring that floor since spring," Wren said. "I think she's building something. Or remembering building something."

"Let her," Mara said. "Memory needs work to do, same as anyone."

Vane nodded, hesitated a moment longer than usual, and left. Through the window Mara watched her cross the courtyard—straight-backed, unhurried, a woman who had lost every certainty she'd been raised on and replaced them, one unheroic day at a time, with something sturdier: the habit of showing up and doing the next right thing.

"She's going to stay forever," Wren observed. "Isn't she."

"Yes."

"Good. Someone has to argue the other side properly." He grinned. "Your sermons need balance."

* * *

Evening brought the letters.

Mara had taken to reading them in the small chamber behind the hall that served as her office, her quarters, and—when the nights ran long—her kitchen. The correspondence of a Warden of the Truce was vast and strange: petitions from villages she'd never seen, reports of ward-lines and star-shrines, occasional furious screeds from remnants of the Thornbound who still believed Ilse had been murdered rather than remembered, and rarer still, letters from Thornvale itself.

She couldn't read those last ones without bracing. She had written to the valley months ago, telling them everything—the cage, the guild, the seed, the trial—and the replies had come back a mixture. Some unopened, returned with the seal unbroken, which was its own kind of message. One, she kept in a drawer: a single pressed apple blossom, no note. She chose to believe it meant forgiveness. Some days she managed to.

Tonight's letter was from Thornvale. Her hand paused over the broken seal.

*Warden Dravenmoor,* it began—no *Mara*, no warmth, but also no frost, which was progress. *The orchard is coming back. We planted where the old one burned, per your letter. Bramwell's grave gets apples now, which he'd have found funny. The children ask about you. We tell them you're the one who gave the sky back. We don't tell them the rest yet. Maybe when they're older. Maybe never. The valley remembers what it chooses.*

*Come home sometime. If you want. No hurry. The valley keeps.*

She read it twice. *The valley keeps.* It wasn't forgiveness. It was better—it was patience, the thing Bramwell had said the guild's true purpose was, finally pointed in a kind direction. She folded the letter and put it with the others, and sat for a while in the amber lamplight, feeling the specific ache of a woman homesick for a home that was now a stranger, and grateful for it anyway. Gratitude and grief had stopped being opposites somewhere on the deadlands road. They lived together now, like the farmers of Hollowbrook, bound by an accord neither would have chosen.

A knock. Wren's head appeared around the door without waiting for an answer, because Wren.

"There's a petitioner downstairs," he said. "Came in at dusk, wouldn't give a name, asked for the Warden specifically. I checked him for weapons, which he appreciated, and for lies, which he didn't."

"Weapons?"

"One knife. Declared. Bread knife, allegedly." Wren's grin faded into something more careful. "Mara. He's odd. Not dangerous-odd. *Old*-odd. He says he's walked from the coast, and he says—" Wren hesitated, choosing the words, "—he says he knew the first Warden. By name."

The lamplight seemed to pull inward. Mara heard herself say it before she decided to: "Jude."

"Yeah." Wren studied her face. "How'd you know?"

Because she had planted an orchard over the old cage and named it for two men—one her mentor, one a stranger dead a thousand years—and because names, she had learned, were never finished with you. Because the ninth verse had come from Jude's hand, and the truce was Jude's penance, and some part of her had been waiting since the verdict for the past to finish arriving.

"Show him up," Mara said. "And Wren—stay. As witness."

He sobered. "Always."

The man who climbed the narrow stair moved like erosion—slow, weathered, inevitable. He was ancient in a way that had nothing to do with years, wrapped in a traveler's cloak gone the color of dust, and when he reached the top step he stood a moment in the doorway, looking at her with eyes the grey of starless skies.

Then he bowed. Deeply. To *her*.

"Warden," he said. His voice was dry earth and old rivers. "Forgive the hour. I've carried something across the deadlands for a very long time, and I'm told—" a faint, tired smile, "—I'm told you're the one who gives things their hearing."

Mara rose from her chair. The ghost-echoes in the walls stirred, faintly, like wind through a house.

"What did you carry?" she asked.

The old man reached into his cloak and drew out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord gone soft as cloth itself. He laid it on her desk with the tenderness of a man setting down a weight he'd borne so long it had become part of his spine.

"The first Warden's own hand," he said. "His journal. The one the guild burned copies of and buried the original beneath the orchard—except I dug it up, a thousand years ago, and I've been walking with it ever since, waiting for someone worth giving it to." He untied the cord. "Jude wrote a final entry the night before he sealed the Thorn. It's not what the histories say. It's not what *you* say, Warden, in your verdict, wise as it was. It's worse. And it's the missing piece, and I think—I've thought for a century—that the truce isn't finished until it's read aloud."

Outside the window, far above the capital, a star flickered. Just once. Like a held breath.

Mara looked at the journal, at the old man, at Wren standing witness by the door, and felt the familiar gravity settle over her—the weight of the next right thing, patient and necessary and unheroic, waiting to be done.

"Then we'll read it," she said, "at dawn. Before the full tribunal. With Vane, and the echoes, and every sworn witness in the city."

The old man nodded, unsurprised, as though he'd known her answer before she did—as though he'd been walking toward it for a thousand years.

"Dawn, then," he said. "Jude always did prefer to confess in daylight."
