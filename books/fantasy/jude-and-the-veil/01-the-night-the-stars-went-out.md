## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The lanterns were lit before dusk, as they always were on Founding Eve, and Dominic Thornbury spent the last hour of ordinary light on his grandmother's roof, rehanging the festival bunting that the wind kept trying to steal.

He should have been down in the square. Everyone was down in the square — hauling trestle tables into rows, stringing paper stars from eave to eave, arguing about whether the cider barrels should go by the well or the old millstone. Thirty years since the wards went up and the valley sealed itself against whatever waited outside its borders; thirty years of Founding Festivals, each one a little louder than the last, as if volume alone could prove they'd made the right choice. Tonight's would be the thirtieth. There would be speeches. Dominic had been drafted to give one.

Instead he crouched on the ridge tiles with a fistful of ribbon and let the wind push at him like a hand testing a door.

The wind was wrong. He'd known it since morning. It came off the mountains in slow, deliberate gusts, warm when it should have been cold, carrying a smell he couldn't name — something mineral and old, like the air inside a cave that has been closed for centuries. The other valley folk noticed nothing. They never did. But Dominic had spent his whole life half-listening to a frequency no one else could hear, and today that frequency had gone strange, then strained, then silent in a way that felt less like quiet and more like held breath.

"Dom!" His cousin Pell stood at the base of the ladder, hands cupped around his mouth. "They want you at the square. You're late, and Gran's asking after you."

"I'm coming." He tied off the last ribbon, and his fingers, without any instruction from him, lingered on the shingle. The slate under his palm was humming. Not vibrating — humming, a low sustained note like a struck bell heard through miles of stone.

He climbed down slowly, looking back once at the western sky, where the sun was going down fat and orange behind the peaks that ringed Thornbury Valley like a wall. Above the sun, the first stars should have been pricking through.

There was nothing there. Just a clean, empty blue.

*Too early,* he told himself. *Stars come later.*

But the sky over the eastern peaks, where true dark already pooled, was bare as a swept floor.

---

His grandmother's cottage sat at the edge of the village, close enough to the boundary that you could see the ward-stones from her window if you knew where to look — seven grey monoliths spaced along the valley rim, older than the church, older than any record, humming their silent thirty-year hum. Children were told the stones had always been there. Adults said the same thing in a different tone. Nobody said what they were for, because everyone knew what they were for: outside was death, or plague, or war, or simply the world, and inside was the valley, and between them ran a line no Thornbury had crossed in three decades.

Dominic had never even touched one of the ward-stones. He'd walked near them, once, at fifteen, on a dare, and had turned back at forty paces with his teeth aching and blood running freely from his nose, as if the stones had flicked him away like an insect. After that, the elders had spoken to his grandmother, and his grandmother had spoken to him, and the lesson had been understood without ever being put into words: *the valley keeps us, and we keep the valley, and you especially keep away from the edges.*

You especially. As though there were a reason it applied to him.

Gran was in her chair by the fire when he came in, small and folded like a letter someone had given up trying to read. Her eyes found him the moment the door opened. They were sharper tonight than they'd been all winter.

"You felt it," she said. Not a question.

"Felt what?" He hung his coat on its peg. "Pell said you wanted me."

"Don't do that." Her voice cracked like a whip across a quiet room. "Don't lie to me in my own house, boy, not tonight. What did you feel?"

He opened his mouth to give her the easy answer — *nothing, Gran, just tired* — and stopped, because she was watching him with an expression he'd only seen once before, the day his father died, and because lying to her suddenly seemed like the most dangerous thing he could do.

"Like something's holding its breath," he said. "Like the whole valley is standing in a doorway and someone's coming up the stairs. My bones ache. All of them, deep, like the ache before a storm, except there's no storm. And my hands—" He looked at them. They were trembling, fine and constant, and beneath the skin of his forearms something shifted, some awareness turning over in its sleep. "My hands feel like they remember how to do something I've never done."

Gran closed her eyes. When she opened them again she looked ten years older than she had that morning.

"Sit down," she said. "And listen to me very carefully. Whatever happens at the festival tonight — whatever you see, whatever you feel — you stay away from the ward-line. Do you hear me? Away from the line, whatever happens."

"What's going to happen?"

She didn't answer. Outside, far off toward the square, the fiddlers began tuning, and the sound drifted up the lane thin and bright and utterly unprepared.

---

The square was beautiful. That was the thing Dominic would remember afterward, the thing that made the rest of it worse: how beautiful it all was, right up until the moment it wasn't.

Paper stars swung on their strings above the crowd. The bonfire threw orange light up the faces of half the valley — farmers and millers and weavers, children weaving between legs, old men who remembered the Before telling their stories too loudly for the fourth time. The cider had started moving early. Someone had brought out the great wheel of cheese that only appeared on Founding Eve. On the platform by the well, Elder Maren stood adjusting her notes, catching sight of Dominic and beckoning with two imperious fingers.

"You're late," she said, as he reached her. "You're on after the blessing. Keep it short, keep it warm, remind them why we're here. Thirty years of safety, thirty years of keeping faith with the founders' wisdom—" She paused, frowning at him. "Are you well? You look ill."

"Do you hear that?" Dominic asked.

"Hear what?"

And that was the horror of it, he thought. No one else heard it. A note had entered the world somewhere below hearing, vast and rising, like the drone of a cathedral organ swelling toward a chord it would take hours to reach. It lived in the stone under his boots. It lived in his own marrow. Every tooth in his head resonated with it. And four hundred people danced and drank and laughed directly on top of it.

"Nothing," he said. "Nerves."

Maren patted his arm and turned back to her notes, and Dominic took his place at the edge of the platform and watched the bonfire and tried to breathe.

The blessing came first. Elder Sable, oldest of them all, raised her arms to the starless eastern sky and gave thanks for the founders' foresight and the wards' protection and the valley's long peace, and her voice was strong and certain, and every word of it landed on Dominic like ash.

Then it was his turn. He stepped forward. Four hundred faces turned toward him, firelit and expectant. He gripped the edge of the lectern, found Jude's face in the crowd — of course he found it, he always did, the way water finds the lowest ground — and opened his mouth to speak.

Every star in the sky went out.

Except there were no stars in the sky. That was the thing. There had been no stars all evening; he'd noticed, he'd told himself it was haze, cloud, anything. But the moment the words left Elder Sable's mouth and the crowd murmured its amen, Dominic understood that the sky hadn't been empty all night. It had been *dimmed*. Dimmed like a lamp turned low, and now, all at once, silently, it was turned out altogether. Not covered — *removed*. The darkness overhead changed texture, became absolute, a blackness with weight and depth, a blackness that pressed down like deep water.

The crowd didn't notice. The bonfire was too bright, the lanterns too many. Only Dominic saw it, standing at the lectern with his speech still folded in his hand, and only Dominic felt what happened next — felt it in his skeleton before he saw it with his eyes.

The wards broke.

Thirty years of silence ended in a single instant. Along the whole rim of the valley, seven ward-stones screamed — not audibly, not in any way the crowd could hear, but in the frequency Dominic had listened to all his life without knowing its name. It was glass breaking, but the glass was the size of the sky. It was a chord resolving wrong. It was every lock in the world opening at once, and the sensation tore through him so violently that he cried out, both hands flying to his chest, and the cry was drowned by something far larger:

Thunder. From a cloudless sky.

Lightning split the darkness above the eastern peaks — a single white fork, hanging impossibly, lighting the valley from rim to rim — and in its light Dominic saw the faces of the crowd turn upward, saw the joy drain out of them, saw Maren's notes scatter from her hands. And he felt, flooding up through the soles of his feet and the roots of his spine, the thing his family had buried for thirty years and perhaps much longer than thirty years.

The storm woke in him.

It rose through his bones like water through dry ground, and it was agony, real agony, white-hot and enormous, and it was also — this was the part that shamed him, the part he would carry like a stone in his chest for everything that followed — it was *glorious*. Every cell in his body lit up with recognition. This was what his hands had remembered how to do. This was what the ache before every storm had meant, all those years, all those nights lying awake while thunder rolled harmlessly beyond the wards: *that is yours, that is yours, you are the storm's and the storm is yours.* He could feel the lightning overhead the way he could feel his own pulse. He could feel the pressure systems stacking over the mountains, the cold fronts queuing beyond the valley walls, the whole vast machinery of weather stretching away north and east into the starless dark. He could feel it, and it felt like coming home to a house he'd been locked out of all his life.

He laughed. God help him, standing in a square full of terrified people, he laughed out loud with the sheer unbearable joy of it, and hated himself for the laugh even as it tore free.

Then the exhilaration crested, and dread came in under it like cold water under a hull, because the wards were broken — truly broken, he could feel the absence of them the way a man feels a missing tooth — and the valley lay open for the first time in thirty years, and the storm was not the only thing pressing at the new-made gap. Something else was out there in the dark beyond the peaks. Something patient. Something that had been waiting exactly this long, and no longer, and whose arrival the storm's waking felt less like a coincidence than a summons.

"Dominic." Maren had seized his arm, her composure gone, her knuckles white. "What was that? What *was* that?"

"The wards," he said. His voice came out roughened, doubled, as if something spoke underneath it. "They're gone. All of them. The valley is open."

Around the square, panic was finding its feet — shouts, a child crying, someone knocking over a cider barrel, the bonfire throwing wild shadows. People were looking to the elders, and the elders were looking at one another, and none of them, Dominic realized, knew any more than the miller's boys did. None of them except—

Gran. Gran had known this morning. Gran had known before the sky went dark.

He pushed through the crowd, past the overturned barrel, past Sable standing frozen with her arms still raised in a blessing that had curdled into a plea, and ran up the lane toward the cottage with the storm singing in his blood and the taste of lightning at the back of his throat.

Behind him, above the square, the second bolt fell — closer, bluer, striking the old millstone dead center and splitting it with a crack like the world's spine giving way. The crowd's screaming rose. Somewhere in it, cutting through everything, a voice he knew better than his own heartbeat called his name.

Jude.

He didn't stop. The cottage door banged open under his hand. Gran was out of her chair, standing upright by the window, and she had dressed — fully dressed, boots laced, a travelling cloak over her shoulders, and a bundle wrapped in oilcloth held against her chest with both hands.

"How long have you known?" Dominic demanded. Lightning strobed through the window, and in its light her face was ancient and grim and unbearably sad. "How long have you known what we are? What's out there? What the wards were actually *for*, Gran, because I don't think they were protection—"

"No," she said quietly. "I don't suppose you do."

The bundle in her arms shifted. Beneath the oilcloth, faintly, something glowed — a sigil, a shape, a symbol he almost recognized, burning the color of deep water and old iron.

"Sit down, Dominic," said his grandmother, as the storm broke over Thornbury Valley for the first time in thirty years. "It's time you learned what our name was traded for."
