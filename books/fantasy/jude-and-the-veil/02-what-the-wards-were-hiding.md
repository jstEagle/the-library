## 2. What the Wards Were Hiding

The ward-stones had not shattered all at once.

That was the first thing Dominic understood when he reached the eastern boundary at dawn, boots sinking into frost-stiff grass, breath tearing white from his lungs. He had expected rubble. He had expected the aftermath of some great violence — a crater, a scorch-mark, the valley's edge blown inward like a window broken by wind.

Instead the stones stood.

There were nine of them along the eastern line, each taller than a man, each older than any building in Thornbury village, and they were still upright, still planted in the earth as they had been for — for how long? No one knew. The elders said the wards predated the Founding Festival, which was itself older than the oldest grave in the churchyard. The stones had simply *stood*, humming with a silence so constant that the people of the valley had stopped hearing it generations ago, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat.

Now they stood cracked.

Not fallen. Not toppled. Cracked, like eggs, like bells, like ice on a pond in late spring — hairline fractures running through them in patterns too regular to be accidental, radiating outward from a point at the center of each stone where something had once been written and now was not. And through those cracks came no hum at all. The silence where the wards had been was a different silence than ordinary quiet. It was the silence of a held breath. Of a room where someone has just stopped speaking.

Dominic put his hand against the nearest stone before he could think better of it.

The cold went through him like a shout.

He staggered back, arm ringing to the elbow, and the thing in his chest — the thing that had woken last night when the stars went out, the coiled pressure behind his sternum that he had spent thirty years pretending wasn't there — *turned over*. It pressed against the inside of his ribs like a bird testing a cage it had only just noticed. Painful. Exhilarating. Shameful. All three at once, braided together so tightly he couldn't have separated them with a knife.

Because here was the truth he had told no one, not even Jude, especially not Jude: when the stars vanished and the wards broke, in that first terrible instant before dread arrived, Dominic had felt *glad*. For one lightning-flash of a moment, standing in the festival square with a cup of mulled wine going cold in his hand, watching the sky come up empty, he had felt the way other men described feeling when a door they'd stared at their whole life finally swung open.

Then the screaming started, and the gladness curdled, and he had run toward the boundary along with half the village because running toward disaster was the only kind of courage he'd ever been able to afford.

Now he stood alone among the cracked stones, and the storm in his bones was awake, and it wanted things.

"Shouldn't be out here."

The voice came from behind him, dry as old paper. Old Marta Halloway, the eldest of the village council, wrapped in three shawls despite the fact that she had walked the two miles from the village without appearing to breathe hard. Her eyes — pale, watery, famously kind when she doled out cough syrup to children — were fixed on the stones with an expression Dominic had never seen on her face before.

It looked like guilt.

"The whole valley's been ordered indoors," she said. "Council's word."

"I heard." He didn't turn fully around. "I didn't obey."

"No," Marta said. "You never do. Quietly, though. You disobey quietly, which is why we've let you." She came closer, slow steps on the frost, and stopped an arm's length from the cracked stone as though approaching a sick animal. "Step back from it, boy."

"It's already broken."

"That's not what I'm afraid of."

He stepped back anyway. Partly because her voice had gone strange — thin, careful, the voice of someone walking across a frozen river — and partly because the stone, up close, in the flat gray light of a starless morning, was showing him something he needed to see properly.

The carvings.

They had always been there, he supposed. Every child in the valley grew up knowing the ward-stones were marked with old lines and loops, worn smooth, meaningless, decorative the way knots in wood are decorative. You touched them on dares. You traced them with fingers sticky from festival sweets. Nobody read them, because there was nothing to read; they were just *old*, and old things in Thornbury Valley were permitted to be meaningless.

But now, with the wards dead, the carvings had changed.

Or rather — Dominic circled the stone slowly, heart climbing into his throat — now that whatever power had filled them was gone, he could see that the smooth worn lines had never been worn smooth at all. They had been *filled*. Packed full of light or force or whatever substance the wards were made of, packed so completely that the true carving beneath had been invisible for centuries. Now the filling had drained away through the cracks, and what remained cut into the granite was sharp-edged, deliberate, and dense as a legal document.

Because it was a legal document.

Dominic had spent his exile learning things the valley considered useless — bookkeeping for the miller, letters for anyone who'd pay, and in the long winter evenings, the odd tangled history of contracts and charters from a merchant's discarded library. He knew what a seal looked like. He knew what witness-marks looked like. He knew the little hooked notation that meant *consideration given* and the double-line that meant *binding upon heirs in perpetuity*.

And he knew, because every soul in the valley knew it the way they knew their own hands, what the Thornbury mark looked like: a thunderbolt split by a horizontal bar, the sign that had hung over the mill his family had lost before he was born, stitched into the one blanket that had come down to him, branded — he pushed up his sleeve, though he didn't need to — into the skin of his left forearm, faint as a scar, present since birth.

It was there on the stone. Center of the carving. Larger than all the rest.

Around it wound clauses in a script older than anything in the merchant's library, older than the valley's charter, older — his mind supplied this unhelpfully, with terrible calm — older than the valley itself. The stone was not part of the valley's defenses. The stone was part of something the valley had been built *on top of*, the way a lock is built around the thing it holds.

"Thornbury," he said aloud. His voice sounded wrong in the wardless air, flat, unechoed. "Why is my name on the border?"

Marta Halloway did not answer.

"Marta." He turned. The storm behind his sternum had gone very still, the way the air goes still before lightning. "Why is my name on the border of the only place I've ever been allowed to live?"

"You should go home, Dominic."

"I've never had a home. I've had an *assignment*." The words came out of him with a bitterness that surprised him, that had apparently been waiting thirty years for a crack to escape through. "I'm the Thornbury. Last of the name. Everyone knows what that means here — it means I don't leave. It means my family's magic made trouble and the valley keeps me the way you keep a banked fire, contained, useful, watched. That's what I was told. That's what everyone was told. So tell me why the cage has my name carved on the *outside* of it."

For a long moment the old woman just looked at him. Frost was forming on her shawls. Behind her, down the slope, the valley lay in its morning grays — chimney smoke rising straight in the windless air, the festival bunting from last night still strung between buildings, sagging, festive colors gone funereal under a sky with nothing in it. Somewhere a dog was barking at the empty heavens. People would be at their windows, watching the boundary, waiting for the elders to explain.

"Elders are ordering everyone indoors," Marta said again, but it had become clear by now that the repetition was not stubbornness. It was a woman reciting the only sentence she had been given permission to say.

"And if I don't go?"

"Then you'll learn something it took me fifty years to wish I didn't know." Her voice dropped. The guilt on her face deepened into something older and more tired. "And knowing won't save you, boy. Knowing never saved anyone in this valley. That's the first thing you should understand about us. We are not ignorant. We have never been ignorant. Ignorance would be a mercy."

The words landed in the silence between the dead wards and sat there.

Dominic looked at the stone again. At the thunderbolt-split-by-a-bar, at the dense ancient script flowing out from it like roots from a tree, at the smaller marks further along — and his stomach dropped, because he recognized one of those too. A second sigil, cruder, newer in the way that four hundred years is newer than a thousand: the founding seal of Thornbury Valley itself, the plow-and-star that hung in the meeting hall.

The valley's own seal was on the contract. As a party to it. Or as collateral. He genuinely could not tell which, and the not-knowing felt like standing on ice and hearing it speak.

"What happened last night?" he asked quietly. "The stars. The wards. What actually happened?"

"They came due."

The words were so soft he almost missed them. Marta's eyes had gone to the horizon beyond the stones — to the outside, the world Dominic had never seen, which lay gray and starless and somehow *expectant* under the ruined sky.

"Thirty years ago, when you were born, the wards were renewed. Renewal isn't free, Dominic. Nothing in this valley is free. Everything looks like generosity because someone else paid the bill ahead of you." She pulled a shawl tighter. "Last night the bill came due again. And this time—" she stopped. Started again, quieter. "This time the payment couldn't be made in the usual coin."

"What coin?"

"Go home, boy."

"*What coin?*"

But Marta Halloway only shook her head, and turned, and began the slow walk back down the slope toward the village, leaving Dominic alone with the stones and the script and the storm turning over in his chest like something waking from a long and unhappy sleep.

* * *

He did not go home.

He told himself he was going home. He walked in that direction for nearly a mile, past the fallow fields, past the mill-race running low and quiet, and then his feet made the decision his pride wouldn't, and he cut east along the hedgerow toward the foundling house.

Jude was already in the yard.

Of course Jude was already in the yard. While the entire valley obeyed the elders' order and shuttered itself indoors, Jude — foundling, placeholder, the child with no recorded parentage who had grown up in the space between other people's families — stood in the frost with a lantern in broad daylight, staring at the sky as though it might apologize.

"You're supposed to be inside," Dominic said.

"So are you." Jude lowered the lantern. Their face was pale, smudged under the eyes, and they had the look of someone who had not slept — which was impossible, because nobody in the valley had slept. "Everyone's indoors. Everyone except the two people this is most about."

"This isn't about me."

"Your name is on the ward-stone, Dominic. I saw it. Half the village saw it before the elders chased us off — the thunderbolt, the bar, your mark, big as life, right in the middle of the oldest thing we have." Jude's voice stayed level, but their hands, around the lantern handle, were white-knuckled. "So don't stand there and tell me it isn't about you. And don't tell me it isn't about me either."

Dominic went still. "What do you mean?"

Jude hesitated. And Dominic had known this person since both of them were small enough to hide under the same table — had known every version of Jude's face, every weather of it — and he had never, not once, seen them look afraid to say a thing.

"When the wards broke," Jude said slowly, "last night. In the middle of everything, the screaming and the dark and old Tam falling down in the square. There was a voice. Underneath it all. Coming from the direction of the mountain." A pause. "It called a name."

"Not yours?"

"It wasn't 'Jude.'" The lantern trembled slightly. "Nobody's ever called me anything but Jude. But I *knew* it. The way you know a tune you've never heard sung. It fit somewhere in me like a key fits a lock you didn't know you had." Jude looked up at him, and their eyes in the lantern-light were fierce and frightened and achingly familiar. "And Dominic — the voice was calling it the way you call in a debt."

The storm behind Dominic's ribs woke fully then.

It rose through him like water finding its level, like a tide answering a moon that wasn't there anymore, and for one vertiginous second he felt it reach — outward, past the dead wards, past the boundary he had never crossed, toward the mountain and the gray country beyond — and something on the far side of that darkness *felt him back*. Something patient. Something old. Something that kept accounts.

The elders had ordered everyone indoors. The elders had refused to explain. The elders had walked away from the stones with guilt written on their faces like a confession they'd been carrying for fifty years.

Protection, or a cage. Depending on who you asked. And now, standing in the frost with Jude's fear hanging in the air between them and his own blood singing treason in his veins, Dominic understood that the question had never really been which one it was.

It had been: whose protection? Whose cage?

And what, exactly, had his family been paying with, all these years, to keep the walls humming?

"We need to talk to my grandmother," he said.

Jude nodded once, and blew out the lantern, because the daylight gave no light worth the name, and together they turned toward the house where the last keeper of the Thornbury debts lay dying.
