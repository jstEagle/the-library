## 3. Jude

The night the stars went out, Jude was mending a net in the doorway of the fish-house, and the first thing they felt was not fear but recognition — as if something long owed had finally been called in.

Then the screaming started, and Jude understood that everyone else had felt it too, only differently. For the valley it was an ending. For Jude it was, absurdly, almost a welcome.

* * *

Foundlings in Thornbury Valley were given names the way boats were given paint: for identification, not affection. The priest who'd pulled Jude from the reed-beds of the Miren twenty-two years ago had squinted at the bundle, at the river, at the gray sky, and said, "Jude. It'll do." That was the whole ceremony. There was no record beyond a line in the parish book, no parentage, no kin-mark, nothing to say where the child had come from or why anyone had bothered to leave it among the reeds instead of letting the current take its course.

Jude had read that parish line a hundred times, running a finger under the ink: *Foundling, sex uncertain, age one season. Named Jude. God and the valley provide.*

*God and the valley provide.* As if Jude were weather. As if Jude were a crop.

Growing up, Jude learned early how to be useful enough that nobody asked questions. Useful children didn't need histories. Jude could gut a fish faster than boys twice their age, could mend net, mend fence, mend quarrels between neighbors who'd forgotten why they'd started shouting. The valley liked Jude the way it liked its bridges — for what they carried, not for what they were.

But there was this: on certain nights, when the wind came down off the mountain with a strange mineral smell, Jude would climb to the top of the tannery roof and sit with their arms around their knees and feel *wrong-sized*. Not unhappy, exactly. Unfitted. Like a key cut for a lock somewhere else, carried for years in a pocket belonging to someone else's coat.

"You brood like a Thornbury," Dominic used to say, hauling himself up beside them, all elbows and storm-colored eyes, breathless from the climb because he was never allowed to run anywhere without half the village clucking about the frail heir of a fallen house.

"I'm not anything," Jude would say. "That's rather the point."

"You're the only person in this valley who talks like a book," he'd say, "and there's no book."

He'd meant it as a compliment. He always did. And Jude would look sideways at him — at the boy who'd shared his bread with a foundling before anyone told him foundlings were beneath his family's notice, back when they were six and he'd held out half a honey-bun with the solemnity of a king ceding territory — and feel the wrong-sizing ease, just slightly. With Dominic, Jude was not a placeholder. With Dominic, Jude was simply present, and presence seemed to be enough.

They never spoke of it. Twenty years of sitting close on rooftops and dock-edges and neither of them ever once spoke of it, which is its own kind of speaking, if you're patient enough to listen.

* * *

Now the stars were gone — every one, all at once, like snuffed candles behind a closing door — and the wards that had ringed the valley in silence for thirty years lay shattered along the border stones, and the village elders had ordered everyone indoors, and Jude was walking the opposite direction, toward the broken line of the wards, because a voice had called them.

Not *called for* them. Called them. By name.

Except it wasn't Jude's name.

It had come an hour past midnight, threading through the shattered ward-line like smoke through a cracked window. Jude had been lying awake in the loft above the fish-house, listening to the village weep and pray and bar its doors, and then — clear as a bell struck against the inside of the skull —

*Vespera.*

One word. A woman's voice, or something wearing a woman's voice the way a thief wears a stolen coat. Warm. Patient. Certain.

*Vespera. Come home.*

Jude had sat up so fast they'd cracked their head on the low beam and sat there in the dark, bleeding a little, heart hammering, whispering the word back to themselves. *Vespera.* It fit in the mouth strangely, sweetly, like fruit from a country they'd never visited. It was not a valley name. It was not a name anyone had ever called across a market square or shouted up at a tannery roof.

And yet some door deep in Jude's chest had swung open at the sound of it, and behind the door was — nothing. Just a draft. Just the shape of a room they couldn't see. But the hinges remembered swinging.

That was the horror of it. Not that a stranger knew their name. That their own bones seemed to know it better than they did.

* * *

In the morning, the valley was unrecognizable in the way that grief makes familiar things foreign. Old Mother Cass stood in the square burning rosemary and refusing to speak above a whisper. The miller had nailed his shutters closed from the inside. Children who had spent thirty years being told the wards were a story for frightening priests now picked through the fallen stones like archaeologists of a catastrophe.

Jude went to the border road because their feet decided before their head did, and found Dominic already there, kneeling by a fallen ward-stone, his sleeves rolled up, his hands hovering over carvings he clearly couldn't read but plainly recognized. His face was the color of old ash.

"They're older than the valley," he said, without looking up. "The carvings. Older than the Founding. And there's a sigil on this one that—" He stopped. Swallowed. "It's my family's mark, Jude. On a stone older than my family's land."

"Maybe your family's older than its land."

"No one is older than their land," he said, and there was something raw under the words, something that sounded like a boy who had just learned the floor of his house was paper.

Elders came then — three of them, moving fast, faces pinched — and ordered everyone indoors again, and away from the stones, and *especially* you, Master Thornbury, and the way they said his name made Jude's skin prickle. Not respect. Fear dressed in respect's clothing.

Dominic went. Of course he went; he always obeyed, always folded himself small for the valley's comfort. But as the elders herded folk homeward, Jude lingered, and laid a palm flat on the fallen ward-stone, and felt it: faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, the residue of whatever had broken here. And underneath it, threaded through the break like a root through a cracked pot, that same warmth. That patience.

*Vespera,* the stone seemed to hum. *Soon.*

Jude pulled their hand back as if scalded.

* * *

Here is what Jude knew about themselves, in full:

They had been found in the reeds. They had no recorded parentage. They had a scar on their left shoulder, old and deliberate, shaped like a crescent moon turned on its side — a birthmark, the priest had said, though Jude had met birthmarks and none of them looked like they'd been made *on purpose*. They could call fish in the Miren by humming, which everyone laughed off as a knack, though no one else in the valley had the knack. And twice a year, on no particular calendar, Jude dreamed of a hall lit by cold white light, full of tall figures whose faces they could never quite turn toward, and woke with the taste of iron and the certainty of having been *expected* somewhere.

That was the whole inventory. Twenty-two years, and it fit in a paragraph.

Everyone else in the valley had more. Even the meanest cottager had a grandmother's grave and a grudge and a recipe. Jude had a scar, a knack, a recurring dream, and a name chosen in the time it takes to glance at a river.

So when the voice came — warm, patient, *certain*, saying a word that opened doors in Jude's chest — the terrible thing was not that Jude distrusted it.

The terrible thing was how much Jude wanted it to be true.

* * *

On the second night, the voice came again, and this time it said more.

Jude hadn't slept; nobody in the valley had slept. The dark outside was total in a way dark had never been before — starless, moonless (the moon, too, had dimmed to a bruise), the kind of black that pressed against windows like a face. Jude sat in the loft with a single candle, mending the same inch of net over and over, and listened to the village below them hold its breath.

*You remember the hall,* the voice said, gentle as a hand on hair. *You dreamed of it because part of you never left it. Vespera. Little flame. We kept your place.*

"We," Jude whispered into the empty loft. "Who is we?"

The voice didn't answer questions. It answered fears, which was worse, and more effective.

*You thought you were left in the reeds. Abandoned. You built your whole life on that small grief, didn't you? That no one wanted you. But you weren't abandoned, little flame. You were placed. Hidden. Kept safe until it was time.* A pause, warm as banked coals. *We have missed you.*

And that was the knife of it, turned just so: not *we want you* but *we have missed you*. Not desire but absence. As if Jude were a chair drawn out from a table, and the table had noticed.

Jude sat very still. The candle guttered. Somewhere below, a dog began to howl and couldn't seem to stop.

Because here was the trap, and Jude saw it plain, even through the longing — especially through the longing. A voice that tells you what you most ache to hear is not thereby telling you the truth. Every beggar knows a coin shines brighter in a liar's palm. If Jude had been content in the valley, the voice would have had no purchase. It was buying exactly what Jude had for sale: the want. The unfittedness. The paragraph-long life with room left over for a bigger story.

*They will tell you to stay,* the voice murmured, reading Jude's hesitation like large print. *The elders. The priest. Even the boy with the storm in his eyes — especially him. They will say it is safer indoors. But indoors is only another word for inside the cage, Vespera. And you were never meant for cages. You were meant for us.*

"Don't," Jude said aloud, to the dark, to the voice, to themselves. "Don't use him to sell me."

The voice laughed — softly, without malice, which somehow made it worse — and withdrew like a tide, leaving behind it that open-door feeling in Jude's chest, and the word hanging in the air like incense.

*Vespera.*

* * *

Jude went to find Dominic on the third day, because if anyone would tell them the truth even when it cost him, it was Dominic. He was in the Thornbury orchard, standing under the bare apple trees, staring at the mountain with an expression Jude had never seen on him before — hunger and dread sharing one face.

"The elders won't say what broke the wards," he said. "My grandmother's taken to her bed. She keeps asking for me and then, when I come, she can't meet my eyes." He turned. "Did you sleep? Anyone sleep?"

"No." Jude hesitated. This was the moment to say it, and saying it felt like stepping off a roof. "Dominic. Something called me. Two nights now. Through the broken wards."

His head came up sharply. "Called you? Called you how?"

"By name." Jude watched his face carefully. "Not *my* name. A name I've never heard. *Vespera.*"

Something moved through his expression too fast to catch — not recognition, but its shadow. "What did it want?"

"It says I belong to it. That I was hidden here, not abandoned. That I'm—" Jude's voice caught on the absurdity and the terror of it. "That I'm expected. Somewhere else. That I've been *kept*."

The word landed on him visibly. *Kept.* He looked at Jude then with an intensity that had nothing casual in it, and for one suspended moment Jude thought — hoped — feared — he might say something true about the two of them, about twenty years of rooftops, about why he'd come looking for Jude first of all people in this breaking valley.

Instead he said, quietly: "Then we're both lies, aren't we? Your name and my inheritance." And he laughed, short and bitter, and it was almost the old laugh, the rooftop laugh, and it broke something loose in Jude's chest that was either grief or courage.

"Dominic." Jude stepped closer. Under the bare trees, in the starless noon light, it was as good a confessional as any. "I've spent my whole life feeling like a placeholder. Like the real person got lost, and I was set here to hold their place until they came back. And now something's come to collect the real one, and I don't know whether to run *toward* it or—"

"Or whether the wanting is the trap," Dominic finished.

"Yes."

He was silent a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was low and steady, the voice he used for truths. "Whatever it is, whatever it calls you — it doesn't get to decide what you are. Whatever name it has for you, you were Jude first. You were Jude on the tannery roof. You were Jude when you shared your bread with a stupid rich boy who'd been told to ignore you." His jaw tightened. "Nobody collects you. Not while I'm—"

He stopped himself. Jude waited. The valley held its breath around them, starless and waiting.

"Not while I'm what?" Jude asked.

But he didn't answer, because that was the moment the ground shuddered — a long, rolling tremor from the direction of the mountain, deep as a struck bell — and every remaining bird in Thornbury Valley took wing at once, and far off, thin as thread, Jude heard the voice again, no longer warm.

*Three days, little flame,* it said, patient still, but with teeth in the patience now. *Come home. Or be collected.*

Jude looked at Dominic. Dominic looked at Jude. Between them, unspoken, the same decision taking shape like weather.

Neither of them intended to be collected.
