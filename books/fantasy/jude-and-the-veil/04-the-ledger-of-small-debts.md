## 4. The Ledger of Small Debts

The house on Candlemaker's Row had always smelled of beeswax and dried lavender, but tonight it smelled of dying.

Dominic noticed it the moment he crossed the threshold — a sweetness underneath the familiar scents, thin and wrong, like fruit going over. His grandmother's sickroom occupied the front parlor, the room with the good windows, and for thirty years it had been the warmest room in Thornbury Valley. Now the warmth was leaving it. He could feel the heat draining through the walls, out past the shattered wards, into a sky with no stars to hold it.

"Shut the door," his grandmother said, without opening her eyes. "The cold comes in sideways now. It didn't used to do that."

He shut the door. The latch clicked, a small domestic sound, absurd against everything else — the broken ward-stones, the elders' curfew, the memory of glass shattering in the air above the valley at midnight, silent and total, like the world's oldest window giving way.

Maren Thornbury lay propped against three pillows, her hands folded on the quilt as if she were already posing for her own effigy. She was ninety-one years old and had been the sharpest mind in the valley until roughly four days ago, when the stars went out and something inside her seemed to go out with them. Dominic had told himself it was coincidence. Age, he'd told himself. The heart keeps its own calendar.

But she had sent for him. Not for the healers. Not for the priest. For him — the grandson who had never once set foot outside the valley line, who had spent his whole life pressing his palms against an invisible wall and calling it home.

"You look terrible," she said, opening her eyes at last. They were grey, storm-grey, the family color, and they found his face with the old precision. "Sit down. Don't argue. You've inherited my arguing, and I haven't the strength for both of us."

He sat. The chair beside the bed creaked under him. Somewhere below, in the cellar, the house's old stores shifted and settled, and he flinched at the sound before he could stop himself. Everything sounded like falling glass now.

"You felt it," Maren said. "When the wards broke. Don't lie to me; I can see it sitting in your shoulders like a yoke."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Dominic." She said his name the way she'd said it when he was six and had lied about breaking the greenhouse pane — patiently, mercilessly. "You came into this world during a thunderstorm that shouldn't have been possible inside the wards. The midwife swore the lightning came *down* from you, not up to you. I held your mother's hand and I watched the sky bend toward my grandson, and I knew what you were, and I knew what we'd done about it, and I have been waiting thirty years for this night." Her breath caught, steadied. "So yes. You felt it. Tell me what it felt like."

He looked at his hands. They were trembling, faintly, the way they had trembled since midnight — since the moment the last star vanished and the border wards tore apart and something vast and dormant in his bones had rolled over like a sleeping animal waking underground. Painful. Alive. *Exhilarating*, in a way that shamed him even now, even here, at his grandmother's deathbed.

"Like a door opening in my chest," he said quietly. "Like something that was locked up finally stretching its legs. And I hated myself for being glad."

His grandmother smiled. It was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of someone watching a debt come due exactly on schedule.

"Good," she said. "That's honest. Hold onto the hating yourself part as long as you can, love. You'll need it for what I'm about to tell you, and after tonight you won't be able to afford it anymore."

"Maren—"

"Grandmother. If you're going to interrupt me, at least use the name properly." She pulled one hand free of the quilt and gripped his wrist. Her fingers were light as paper and strong as roots. "I'm dying. The healers gave me a week; I intend to be finished by morning, because I am not spending my last hours being gentle. Do you understand? Everything I'm about to say, I say once. There will be no second telling. So listen."

* * *

She made him fetch it herself.

From the cedar chest at the foot of her bed, beneath forty years of winter woolens, wrapped in oilcloth stiff with age: a book. Not a large book. Ledger-sized, ledger-bound, its cover cracked black leather gone soft as skin at the corners. There was no title on the spine. There was a sigil instead, pressed into the leather in silver that had tarnished almost to nothing — a mountain with a door in it, and above the door, a name.

*Thornbury.*

The same sigil he'd found carved into the fallen ward-stone that morning, half-buried in the hillside above the mill, older than any masonry in the valley. He had touched it and felt the stone hum against his fingertips like a struck bell that hadn't finished ringing.

"What is it?" he asked. His voice came out strange.

"The truth," his grandmother said. "Which is heavier than it looks. Open it."

He opened it.

The first page was dated two hundred and eleven years ago. The handwriting was fine, formal, in iron-gall ink gone brown as dried blood: *Received of Aldous Thornbury, one third of all arable land east of the river, in consideration of debts owed and continuing obligations.* Below it, a signature. Below the signature, a seal — the mountain with the door.

He turned the page. *Received of Edith Thornbury, the luck of the eastern fields, being all fortune pertaining to weather, harvest, and fair chance, for a term of ninety-nine years.* Page after page after page. The handwriting changed across the decades — different hands, different generations, but the same formal cadence, the same seal, the same mountain-with-a-door. *Received of Tomas Thornbury, sixty years of life, taken from the ends of his descendants' spans.* *Received of Aldous the younger, the family's voices in matters of law, being their right to plead their own cause.* *Received of Wren Thornbury, the gift of storms entire, being all command of thunder, lightning, and the wild air, held in trust until called.*

Dominic stopped on that page. His finger rested on the word *storms*, and the thing in his chest — the thing that had woken at midnight — pressed up against the inside of his ribs like a hand against a window.

"Held in trust," he read aloud. "Until called."

"Now you know why you never left the valley," Maren said. "It wasn't exile, love. It was storage."

The words went through him colder than the starless night outside. Storage. Thirty years of borders, of elders' warnings, of the valley line that burned like ice if you so much as dreamed too hard about crossing it — and it had never been punishment. It had been a warehouse. He was the heirloom. He was the thing in the vault.

"Keep turning," his grandmother said.

He kept turning. And then, near the end — pages dated within his own lifetime, within his father's lifetime — the entries changed. The handwriting became cramped, hurried, less certain of itself. And the goods being received changed too.

*Received of Edmund Thornbury,* written in a hand Dominic recognized with a jolt as his father's, *one soul, being the soul of a child born foundling at the valley's edge in the month of the thaw, in full settlement of debts accrued and penalties incurred, said debt being irrecoverable by any other means.*

Below it, the seal. Below the seal, a single annotation in different ink, older ink, as if the clause had been waiting in the contract long before anyone wrote the entry to fill it:

*Foundlings are the court's by ancient right. The valley holds them in trust.*

Dominic did not remember standing up. He was on his feet, the ledger in his hands, and the room had gone narrow and dark at the edges, and somewhere very far away his grandmother was saying his name.

The child born foundling at the valley's edge in the month of the thaw.

Jude.

Jude had been brought to the valley in the month of the thaw. Jude, who had appeared at the foundling-house door wrapped in a blanket with no name stitched into it, no record, no parentage, nothing — Jude, whom the elders had always treated with a peculiar carefulness, a courtesy that had never quite made sense, the way you're careful with something that belongs to someone else. Jude, who used to joke about it, laughing, *maybe I'm a changeling, maybe I'm somebody important's lost baby* — and everyone else had laughed too, and no one had ever laughed quite hard enough.

"They sold them." His voice didn't sound like his voice. "My father sold Jude. Before—" He couldn't finish it. Before Jude could walk. Before Jude could talk. Before Dominic had ever—

"To settle *his* debts," Maren said. "Yes."

"What debts? What could he possibly have owed that cost a—" He couldn't say *soul*. His mouth refused the shape of it. "What did he do?"

"He gambled," his grandmother said simply. "Not with cards. With the court. That's how our family has always gambled — we stake things we have against things we want, and the mountain always collects, and the mountain always wins, because the mountain writes the rules in a language we only half speak. Your father wanted the wards renewed. The wards were failing — you were three years old, and there had been signs, and the court demanded payment for another generation of protection. He had nothing left to give. The land was gone, the luck was gone, the years were gone, the storms were already in trust." She paused. "But the contract had a clause. Foundlings are the court's by ancient right. All he had to do was... designate one."

"There were other foundlings," Dominic said. "There were always foundlings. Why Jude?"

"Why does the mountain take the best stone from the quarry?" Maren closed her eyes. "Because it can. Because the brightest flame burns cleanest, and the court has always had a taste for clean burning. You knew it yourself, love, even as a boy. Everyone knew it. Jude was the quickest, the kindest, the most *alive* of all the children ever raised in that foundling-house, and every adult in this valley looked at that child and thought, quietly, in the place where we keep the things we don't say: *that one isn't really ours.*"

"That one isn't really ours." Dominic repeated it like a curse. "And nobody said anything. Nobody *did* anything. My father signed a piece of paper and a person stopped belonging to themselves, and the whole valley just — went along with it. Kept raising them. Kept feeding them. Kept loving them, even—" His voice cracked. "Even me."

"Especially you," his grandmother said, without flinching. "That's how these things survive, Dominic. Not through cruelty. Through ordinariness. Your father was not a monster. He was a frightened man holding a failing wall together with whatever he had, and what he had was a child nobody would miss — or so he told himself, because telling himself that was easier than telling himself the truth. And his father before him, and his mother before him, back and back and back, each generation selling a little more, each generation telling itself the same story: *it's necessary, it's temporary, it's not really people.*" She opened her eyes and fixed him with the storm-grey stare. "Two hundred years of small debts, love. That book is a history of respectable people doing monstrous arithmetic."

Dominic looked down at the ledger in his hands. At his father's cramped signature. At the annotation in old ink, patient as a trap: *foundlings are the court's by ancient right.*

Jude had grown up beside him. Had shared bread with him, stolen apples with him, sat with him on the mill-roof and named constellations that weren't there because the real ones were hidden behind the wards' false glow. Jude had a laugh that could turn a bad day around, a habit of humming when nervous, a scar on one knee from the summer they were nine. Jude was not an entry in a book. Jude was the whole point of every good day Dominic had ever had, and some part of him had known it for twenty years and called it by no name because naming it would have meant admitting he wanted things the valley wouldn't let him have.

And Jude's soul had been signed away before either of them could speak.

"When does the court collect?" he asked. His voice was flat now. Quiet. The fury had passed through grief and come out the other side as something with edges.

Maren was silent a long moment. When she answered, her voice had gone very soft, which from her was more frightening than shouting.

"I don't know. That's the truth. The terms were never shown to us — that's part of how it works; you sign for what you're given and the rest stays in the mountain. But the wards are broken, and the stars are gone, and the court doesn't break its own seals by accident. Something has begun collecting. I feel it the way you feel weather in an old wound." She reached out and took the ledger from his hands, closing it with a gentleness that was somehow worse than slamming it. "And here is the last thing I have to tell you, and it is the worst thing, so brace yourself."

He braced.

"The sigil on that cover," she said. "The mountain with the door. Look at your forearm, love. Inside of the left arm, near the elbow, where you've had that birthmark your whole life and never thought twice about it."

Dominic pushed back his sleeve.

The birthmark was there. It had always been there — a small pale mark, unremarkable, the kind of thing you forgot you carried. But tonight, in the candlelight, it had changed. The lines of it had sharpened, darkened, resolved into a shape he now recognized with a sickness that started in his stomach and climbed.

A mountain. With a door in it.

"It's not a birthmark," his grandmother said. "It never was. It's a countersignature. Every Thornbury heir carries it, and it wakes when the contract wakes. The court didn't break those wards, Dominic. *You* did — or rather, the inheritance in you did, reaching for what it's owed. You are the living seal on two hundred years of sold lives, and the mountain knows its own mark, and the mountain will come for its signatory." Her breath rattled. "Unless you get to it first."

"Get to what first?"

"The mountain." She gripped his wrist again, harder than she should have been able to, her eyes blazing with the last of her strength. "Everything in that book was given willingly — coerced, cheated, forged in spirit, but signed, signed, *signed*, and willing signatures bind tighter than chains. But you — you haven't signed anything yet. You're the first heir in two hundred years who still has a choice. Whatever you're going to do — save that child, end that book, burn that mountain's ledgers to the ground — you do it *before* you inherit. Once you accept what's yours, every debt in that ledger becomes yours to pay. Do you understand me? The moment you claim the Thornbury name, you claim the Thornbury debts."

Outside, far off, a bell began to ring. Not the festival bells — the alarm, the deep one under the meetinghouse that hadn't sounded in living memory. Voices rose in the street. Someone was running.

Maren's grip tightened.

"And Dominic — the child must never know how they were paid for. Promise me. Some truths are a kind of theft—"

"No." He said it before he understood he was saying it. "No. I'm done keeping secrets that cost other people everything. That's the family trade, isn't it? Selling pieces of people and calling it necessity." He stood, tucking the ledger inside his coat, against his heart, where he could feel it like a second heartbeat. "I'm sorry, Grandmother. I'm sorry for all of them — every one of us who signed. But I'm not carrying this the way you carried it."

For a moment she just looked at him. And then, slowly, terribly, she smiled — the same smile from the beginning of the night, the smile of a debt coming due.

"Good," she whispered. "That's my boy. Now go. The mountain's already moving, and you've got a foundling to warn — though I suspect, knowing that child, they'll insist on coming."

He was at the door when her voice stopped him one last time, thin and clear as a wire.

"Dominic. The valley line will let you out now. It's been waiting for you to try." A pause. "And whatever waits beyond it — it's been waiting longer."

He ran.

Through the parlor door, down the hall, out into the street where the alarm bell was still tolling and neighbors stumbled from their houses with lanterns held up against a sky that offered them nothing back. Above the rooftops, where stars should have been, there was only a smooth black nothing, vast and patient, like the inside of a closed ledger.

And Dominic ran toward the foundling-house with his grandmother's book against his chest and his blood singing its inherited storm, knowing one thing with absolute certainty:

Jude deserved the truth. Even if the truth destroyed everything either of them had ever believed about the valley, the family, the name they'd grown up under.

*Especially* then.
