## 5. Crossing the Line

The elders gave us until dawn to bury our questions, and I spent the night digging them up instead.

I sat in my grandmother's cold kitchen with the contract-sigil spread across the table, turning it under the light of a tallow candle because the lamps had all gone strange since the stars went out — they burned steady, obedient, and I found I no longer trusted anything that behaved as it was told. The sigil was a disc of black slate no bigger than my palm, carved with the Thornbury mark: the oak split by lightning, the roots drawn downward into spirals that went on far too long, as if the tree grew down instead of up. Down toward the mountain.

My grandmother's hands had trembled when she pressed it into mine. *They will tell you the valley is your punishment,* she'd said, her voice already thinning toward the place where voices go when their owners are dying. *It is not. It is your signature.* Then she'd coughed blood into a rag and refused to say more, and by morning she was sleeping too deeply to wake, and the healer woman shook her head at me in a way that meant soon.

So: the valley was not a prison. The valley was an inkwell. And somewhere beneath the mountain, something held thirty years of my family's handwriting.

I wrapped the sigil in wool and tucked it against my ribs, where it sat like a second heartbeat. Then I took down my father's knife from above the hearth — the one thing he'd left me besides the name — and I went to find Jude.

* * *

Jude was waiting at the edge of the barley field, exactly where I knew they would be, because Jude had always been waiting at edges. When we were children it was the fence line behind the tannery, the lip of the millpond, the boundary hedge where the valley's wards hummed so low you felt them in your teeth rather than heard them. Jude never crossed those lines either. But Jude stood at them the way other people stood at windows.

Now the wards were broken glass underfoot, and the whole valley could feel the absence like a pulled tooth — everyone touching the gap with their tongue, everyone flinching.

"You're going," Jude said. Not a question. They sat on the fallen ward-stone with their knees drawn up, and the stone that had sung for thirty years was silent now, its carvings open to the sky like a mouth mid-sentence.

"I'm going."

"The elders said—"

"The elders said stay indoors and pray." I crouched beside the stone, running my thumb over the spiral roots. "Prayer didn't keep the stars up, Jude."

"No," Jude agreed softly. "But it might have been trying."

That was the thing about Jude. Everyone in Thornbury thought of them as gentle, and they were — they were the gentlest person I knew — but underneath the gentleness ran a current of iron that the village had never bothered to notice, because foundlings are expected to be grateful, and gratitude gets mistaken for softness. Jude wasn't soft. Jude simply chose, every single day, to be kind, which is a harder discipline than any I've ever practiced.

"Come with me," I said, and hated how much my voice wanted it.

Jude looked out past the boundary line, to where the world outside waited gray and starless. "You don't know what's out there."

"Neither do you."

"That's why you should leave me here, where at least I'm a known quantity." But they were already standing, brushing straw from their trousers, pulling their coat tight. "Where are you going?"

"Away from the road. If Bram Halloway catches us—"

"Bram caught you leaving the forge an hour ago," Jude said. "He told me to talk you out of it. He also gave me this." They produced a bundle from inside their coat: bread, hard cheese, a flask, and — absurdly, touchingly — a coil of good rope. "He said if talking failed, feeding was the next best thing. He said—" Jude stopped. Their jaw worked. "He said his grandfather used to say the wards weren't for keeping things out."

My chest tightened. "What did his grandfather say they were for?"

"He wouldn't say." Jude handed me the bundle. "But he packed for two."

* * *

We went north along the river, where the boundary ran through a stand of birches that had gone white as bone in the starless dark. There were no stars. I want to write that sentence again because even now, walking toward it, I could not make myself believe it: there were no stars. Above the birches the sky was a smooth black ceiling, unbroken, unmarked, like the lid of something. I kept catching myself looking for constellations my grandmother had taught me — the Plowman, the Weeping Sisters, the Long Antler — and finding only blankness where my memory insisted shapes should be. It was like reading a page where the ink had been scraped away. You could see the ghosts of letters. You just couldn't read them anymore.

And through my bones, all the while, the storm-magic moved.

I had lived twenty-nine years without it, or so I'd believed. Now I understood that dormancy isn't absence; it's pressure. Every year of my life the storm had been coiled somewhere below my sternum, muzzled by the wards, and with the wards shattered it woke like a creature whose cage had rotted around it. My teeth ached. My fingertips prickled with static that jumped and snapped when I touched bark, when I touched Jude's sleeve. Once, crossing a stretch of dry grass, I felt every blade lean toward me, very slightly, like faces turning.

It felt wonderful. That's the confession. It felt like the first deep breath after thirty years underwater, and I was ashamed of the pleasure of it, because the same force that filled me had emptied the sky.

"Dominic." Jude's hand closed around my wrist. "Stop."

I hadn't realized I'd stopped walking. The grass within arm's reach of me had risen two inches off the ground, straining upward, trembling. A breath of wind circled us that came from nowhere — no weather front, no valley draft, just air deciding to move because I was near it. I let the breath out slowly and the grass lay back down.

"It's getting stronger the closer we get to the line," Jude said quietly.

"I noticed."

"Do you think that's a coincidence?"

"No," I said. "I think the valley has been holding me like a dam holds water, and I'm about to find out what I look like spilled."

Jude studied me in the dark — that level, searching look they'd given me since we were children, the look that made me feel less like a person and more like a book someone intended to finish reading. "Does it hurt?"

"Yes."

"And?"

"And it's the truest thing I've ever felt," I admitted. "Which frightens me more than the pain does."

Jude nodded once, as if I'd confirmed something they'd long suspected, and walked on.

* * *

The boundary itself was nothing to look at. That was the joke of it, the cruel joke: thirty years of dread, thirty years of stories about what happened to anyone foolish enough to cross, and the line was a shallow drainage ditch between a birch and a boulder, marked by a moss-eaten stone no taller than my knee. On our side, the grass grew thick and familiar and smelled like home. On the far side, the grass grew the same way. The same soil. The same night.

Except the silence.

Inside the valley there was always noise — insects, owls, the river muttering to itself, the vast low hum of the wards that we'd all stopped hearing the way you stop hearing your own heart. Outside the line, all of that was gone. Not quiet. Absent. The night beyond the boundary made no sound at all, as though sound itself had been collected, called due, taken as payment.

I stood with my boot over the ditch for a long time.

"Thirty years," Jude said beside me. "You've never been across."

"Nobody has. Not in my lifetime."

"And now you're going first."

"Somebody has to be first," I said. "And nobody else has a storm in their chest demanding to see the sky."

Jude was quiet a moment. Then: "When you step over, I'm stepping with you."

"Jude—"

"Don't argue. I've watched you stand at edges your whole life. You always look back to see if anyone's watching, and then you're disappointed when they aren't, and then you go anyway, alone, and come back having learned something you can't explain to anyone." Their voice didn't waver. "Not this time. Whatever's out there, you're not carrying it alone. That's the deal. That's been the deal since the millpond, and you know it."

The millpond. I was eleven; Jude was ten or maybe nine — no one knew, not even Jude, because foundlings arrive without dates attached. I had gone onto the ice against every rule and gone through it, and the last thing I saw before the black water closed was Jude sliding toward me on their belly, arms out, shouting my name — my actual name, Dominic, the name that mattered, not the surname everyone whispered about. They pulled me out. They never once told the elders it was my fault. And afterward, warming by the forge fire, they'd said: *Next time you fall in something, I'm coming with you. It's easier to drown together.*

I had laughed. I should have understood then that they meant it as law.

"All right," I said. "Together."

I stepped over the line.

Nothing struck me down. No alarm, no curse, no hand rising from the earth. Just the absence of the wards' hum — a silence so total it rang — and the storm in my bones surging free like a horse feeling the gate swing wide. Power flooded down my arms and out through my palms before I could stop it; the birch beside me shed its leaves all at once, a white flurry falling upward, and somewhere far off thunder answered, though the sky stayed sealed and starless and dry.

I staggered. Jude's shoulder took my weight, steady as a wall.

"Still with me?" they asked.

"Ask me again in a minute." The magic kept moving through me, vast and unhousebroken, and I gripped the knife hilt at my belt and the sigil against my ribs and breathed. In. Out. The storm settled — not caged now, just leashed by my own two hands, which was a different thing entirely and would take years to learn. "All right," I said. "All right."

We crossed.

The ground on the far side felt the same under my boots, and utterly wrong. Home soil knows you. This dirt didn't care whether I lived or died, and after twenty-nine years of being cared for — however narrowly, however grudgingly — that indifference hit me like cold water. Freedom, I thought. So this is what it feels like. Like falling with nowhere in particular to land.

Then Jude made a small sound, and pointed.

Down the slope, in the gray pre-dawn murk, there were fires. Dozens of them. Hundreds — a scatter of embers stretching along the old north road as far as I could see, and around each fire, shapes. People. Families. Carts piled with belongings, oxen standing hipshot, children asleep in ditches. A whole nation of campfires, and not one tent pitched facing the road ahead. Every single soul was oriented south. Toward the valley. Toward us.

"Refugees," I said, and the word felt foreign in my mouth. Nothing in my life had ever required it.

"They're fleeing," Jude murmured. "From what?"

From behind us — from the north, from whatever lay beyond the mountain and beyond the mountains beyond that — came an answer we couldn't hear yet, only feel: a faint vibration in the ground, rhythmic, patient, like footsteps of something that had all the time in the world.

Or like a ledger closing.

A woman near the nearest fire saw us standing on the rise and rose to her feet. She shouted something in a dialect I half-recognized from my grandmother's lullabies — words the valley still spoke but no longer quite understood — and others stood too, dozens of faces turning toward the boundary line, toward the two of us, toward the silent ward-stone behind us.

The woman raised her arm and pointed past us, at the valley, and her voice carried clear across the dead air:

"Open the gate! Open it, in mercy's name — it's coming, and your walls won't hold what's owed!"

Behind me, Jude exhaled slowly. "Dominic," they said. "I don't think we left the story."

"No," I said, watching the fires, listening to the ground's slow pulse. "I think we just reached the part with the bill in it."
