## 6. The Road of Unpaid Tolls

### Dominic

The valley line was a scar in the world, and they had crossed it.

Dominic felt it in his teeth for the first mile — the low hum of the wards he'd lived inside for thirty years, gone silent behind them, replaced by something thinner. Air without a roof on it. He kept expecting the sky to notice him, to press down like it always had at the boundary, that invisible hand flattening him back toward home. But nothing pressed. The gray sky simply went on and on above the road, indifferent, enormous, and starless — starless everywhere, not just over Thornbury. That was the part that made his stomach clench. He had woken to an empty sky and assumed, in some private corner of himself, that it was their punishment. Their debt. Instead the whole world stood under the same stripped ceiling, like a house whose roof had been carried off in the night.

Jude walked beside him with the hood up and the knife at their belt, which was absurd — Jude had never held a knife in anger in their life — but Dominic hadn't argued. Arguing with Jude about coming had already cost him half the morning and all of his dignity.

"You keep looking back," Jude said.

"I'm checking the wards."

"The wards are broken. You're checking whether you regret it."

He didn't answer, which was an answer. The road unspooled ahead through country he'd only ever seen from the high pasture: hedgerows gone ragged, fields half-harvested and abandoned, a shrine to some road-god tipped into the ditch with its painted eyes flaking. Thirty years of stories said the outside world was dangerous, lawless, hungry. What he saw instead was *interrupted*. A world stopped mid-sentence.

They came on the first refugee by midmorning — a woman pulling a handcart with a sleeping child in it and a cage of chickens that had given up complaining. She looked at them the way people look at weather: wearily, calculating whether it would turn.

"Road's bad past Harrow Ford," she said, before either of them asked. "Bad meaning empty. Bad meaning if you go looking for Harrow Ford you'll find a field where it used to be."

Dominic's mouth went dry. "Collected?"

She spat, a habit rather than contempt. "You're valley-folk." She nodded at the sigil hanging at his throat — his grandmother's contract-mark, which he still hadn't decided whether it was a key or a leash. "Figures. You lot slept through it. Whole villages, love. Not burned, not chased. *Taken*. Like rent."

"Who takes them?"

"Same as always takes things." She jerked her chin northward, toward the mountain that even here dominated the horizon, a black shoulder against the gray. "The court under it. Debt collectors. Only nobody I ever met remembers signing anything." She gathered her cart handles. "Keep off the toll roads after dark. They weigh travelers now."

"Weigh them?"

But she was already walking, and the child had woken and was crying quietly, and the chickens had started again, and the road went on being interrupted in both directions.

* * *

### Jude

Jude counted the empty places the way other people counted coins, because counting was the only thing keeping the fear from spreading out and filling them entirely.

Harrow Ford was first: a field, exactly as promised, but not an empty field. The grass grew in rows. Faint ones, like pressure-marks in a carpet where furniture stood for years. Jude crouched and put a palm flat against the ground and felt — or thought they felt — a hum underneath, patient and organized, like a ledger waiting to be read. The village wasn't destroyed. It was *filed*.

"Dom." Their voice came out smaller than intended. "Come feel this."

He came. He knelt. His storm-sense — new, raw, a door blown open in him — read the ground faster than Jude's hands could. Whatever crossed his face then, Jude didn't want a name for it.

"It's not rubble," he said. "It's... catalogued."

"That's worse."

"Yes," he said. "That's much worse."

They walked on. By afternoon there were more: a millstone sitting alone in a beet field with no mill; a chapel bell half-buried in a streambed, ringing faintly whenever the water moved, though no one had pulled its rope in what looked like years; a milestone whose carved distances had been scraped smooth, as if the places it named had been removed from the map's memory too. Jude found themselves talking to fill the silence, the way they always had, telling Dominic about the festival that wouldn't happen this year, about old Marta's pies, about nothing — and Dominic let them, because Dominic understood that the alternative was thinking.

Near dusk they smelled woodsmoke and followed it off the road to a crooked cottage grown into a hawthorn hedge so thoroughly it was hard to say which was sheltering which. A woman sat outside on a stool, sorting dried herbs into jars by touch more than sight, her hair iron-gray and cut short as a soldier's. She looked up before they knocked.

"Valley-folk," she said. "And one of you is leaking storm like a cracked jug. Come in before something notices."

Her name was Marisol. She fed them nettle soup and didn't ask questions until they'd eaten, which Jude decided was the mark of a civilized person.

* * *

### Dominic

Marisol's cottage was one room, and every inch of it was evidence. Bundles of herbs hung from the beams, yes — but also maps, dozens of them, layered and annotated in ink that changed color. Bones sorted into trays. A wall of small slate tablets, each scratched with names, hundreds of names, and beside many of them a single word: *taken*, or *paid*, or — most chillingly — *unclaimed*.

Dominic stood in front of that wall too long.

"My sister's name is there," Marisol said from behind him. Not unkindly. "Third row. Sera Marisol Vane. Taken forty-one years ago, spring, for a debt our grandfather supposedly ran up buying seed during the blight year. Nobody in my family ever bought seed from anyone under a mountain." She set down another jar. "I've been reading fine print ever since."

Dominic turned. "We're going to the mountain."

"I know. Everyone who comes down that road eventually goes toward it. Grief walks north." She studied him with pale, unhurried eyes. "What's your grievance? Land? Luck? Years?"

He hesitated. It felt obscene to say it aloud when her wall held hundreds of names. "A person," he said finally. "Someone sold before they could consent. Before any of us knew there was anything to consent to."

Marisol went very still. Then she rose, crossed to a chest, and drew out a ledger — an actual ledger, leather-bound, swollen with inserted pages — and laid it open on the table between them.

"Then sit down, valley-boy, because you need to understand how big this is before you stand in front of it. Your valley is one account. One." Her finger moved down columns of entries. "Here — the Weeping Downs, collected entire, two hundred eleven souls, against a drainage debt from three generations back. Here — Candlemere, four hundred souls, against a treaty nobody living signed. Here—" the finger stopped, tapped twice, "—the Ashford vale. Nine hundred. Nine hundred people, Dominic Thornbury, traded for rain rights, and do you know what the entry says the creditor received?" She looked up. "*Consideration received: unspecified.* Unspecified. Nine hundred lives balanced against a blank."

The soup turned to lead in Dominic's stomach. "How does a court collect on debts no one agreed to?"

"Because agreement isn't the point. *Record* is the point." Marisol's voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "The court beneath the mountain doesn't enforce contracts. It *writes history as debt*. If it says your grandfather owed, then somewhere in its archive your grandfather owed, and the archive has never once been wrong, because the archive decides what wrong means." She closed the ledger gently, like closing a coffin. "Every renewal, every transfer, every collection — all of it lawful, all of it witnessed, none of it ever answered to anyone above ground. There is no appeal because there is no above. Do you understand? The whole world is below."

Jude spoke from the corner, quiet. "And the stars?"

Marisol's face changed — the first crack in her composure, and it frightened Dominic more than the ledger had.

"The stars," she said, "are the payment that hasn't been collected yet. Or the payment being collected very slowly, depending on how you count." She went to the window, to the gray dark gathering outside. "The Veil between worlds runs on light. Old light, spent light, the light of everything that's finished burning. And the Veil is open wider than it should be — someone has been unpicking it thread by thread for longer than I've been alive, widening the seam, and a widened seam must be *fed*. So the court feeds it. Souls, mostly. But souls burn fast and bright and then they're done. Stars last." She turned back to them. "Every star that's gone out since the wards fell — it's not vanished, children. It's been *moved*. Fed through the seam to hold it open. The night sky isn't going dark. It's being spent."

* * *

### Jude

Jude lay awake on Marisol's floor afterward, watching the rafters, doing the arithmetic they couldn't stop themselves from doing.

Souls in glass. Villages filed like grain. Stars torn out of heaven to feed a seam. And somewhere in the middle of it, one small flame with their name on it — not *Jude*, apparently, since Jude wasn't a real name, just the label the valley had stuck on a foundling like a jar in a pantry. One small flame among thousands, entered in a ledger against a debt incurred by a man Jude had called father once, in the days before they learned what fathers could sign away.

It should have made them feel small. Instead — and this was the shameful thing, the thing they'd never tell Dominic — it made them feel *located*. All their life they'd been a placeholder, a gap shaped like a person, waiting for a story to be told about them. Now they knew the story existed. It was written in someone else's hand, in a book kept under a mountain, and it was monstrous — but it was *theirs*. Even a prison is an address.

Beside them, Dominic breathed in the uneven rhythm of someone failing to sleep. Jude reached over in the dark and found his wrist, where the contract-sigil sat under the skin like a buried brand, warm as a banked coal.

"Dom," they whispered. "If we get my soul back. If we actually do it. What am I, afterward?"

He was quiet long enough that Jude thought he'd drifted off. Then: "Whatever you decide. That's the whole point of getting it back. It stops being an entry and starts being yours."

"And if I find out I volunteered? If I find out I'm not in that vault because of your father's debt at all, but because of something I — some version of me — chose?"

His hand turned and closed around theirs, callused and certain. "Then we'll know the truth instead of the ledger's version of it. Truth's heavier. But it's the only thing worth carrying north."

Outside, in the starless dark, something passed along the toll road — slow, rhythmic, like footsteps, like scales swinging, like a chain dragged link over link. Neither of them moved. Neither of them slept again.

In the morning, Marisol packed her herbs, closed her shutters, and took down her sister's slate from the wall.

"Well," she said, tucking it into her coat like a knife. "Grief walks north. Mine's overdue. We leave before the collectors finish weighing the crossroads — and Thornbury-heir, you'd best pray your name opens doors, because if it opens the wrong one, all three of us are entries by nightfall."
