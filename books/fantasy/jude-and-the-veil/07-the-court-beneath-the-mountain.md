## 7. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The mountain had been visible from the valley all of Dominic's life — a grey shoulder on the northern horizon, so constant he'd stopped seeing it the way you stop seeing your own nose. Now it filled half the sky, and it was not what he had expected.

He had expected rock. He got a door.

It stood alone at the end of a defile where the road narrowed to a throat: a gate of black stone forty feet high, carved with spirals that seemed to turn when he wasn't looking directly at them. No wall attached to it. The mountain rose sheer on either side, and the gate simply stood there in front of the living rock like a picture frame hung on nothing, framing a slab of granite that was, unmistakably, a door.

Jude stopped three paces back. "It's watching us."

"It's stone."

"Stone doesn't make my teeth itch." Jude's hand had found the knife at their belt — his grandmother's knife, actually, though they'd been carrying it since the ward-stones fell. "Dominic. My teeth itch."

He understood. Every hair on his arms was standing, and not from cold. The storm that had woken in his bones the night the stars went out had been quiet for two days — a sullen, banked coals feeling, as if it disapproved of the journey — but here, in front of the gate, it stirred. Not painfully. *Eagerly.* That was worse.

"I'm going to knock," he said.

"With what? Your fist?"

"With this." He drew out the contract-sigil. His grandmother had pressed it into his hand with her last lucid breath, and it had changed since then: the lead-grey disc had darkened to something like iron, and the Thornbury spiral at its center now glowed faintly, a thread of blue-white light no brighter than a dying ember. It had grown warmer the closer they came to the mountain. It knew where it was going. It had been here before.

He held it up to the door.

For a long moment, nothing. Long enough that he began to feel foolish, standing in a starless wasteland presenting a coin to a boulder. Then the spirals on the gate's surface began to move — all of them, together, unwinding outward from the center like ripples in reverse — and the granite within the frame went soft as water and slid aside.

Warm air breathed out. It smelled of cold iron and, absurdly, of beeswax candles.

A figure stood in the opening. A woman, tall, silver-haired, dressed in a gown the colour of deep water, cut in a style three centuries out of fashion. She was beautiful the way winter is beautiful, and she looked at Dominic with an expression of mild, professional satisfaction, as if he were a delivery she'd been expecting and had only wondered about the day.

"Thornbury-heir," she said.

The words went through him like a hook through meat.

"That's not my name," he said. "My name is Dominic."

"Yes," she agreed pleasantly. "And also you are Thornbury-heir. Both things are true; they have been true since before your birth. I am Lady Vess, steward of this court. You are expected. You have been expected for thirty years — since the night the wards went up, in fact. We do try to be patient. It is one of our virtues." Her gaze slid past him to Jude, and something flickered across her face — too fast to read, gone before it landed. "Your companion may enter under guest-right. Neither of you will be harmed under guest-right. I give you my word on it, and my word is the oldest thing in this mountain."

"And if we don't come in?" Jude asked.

Vess smiled. It did not reach her eyes, which were pale as frost on a windowpane. "Then you will stand outside in the dark, in a world with no stars, while the things that ate the stars finish eating. But you'll be free of us, which I understand is considered a virtue above ground." She stepped back, into candlelight. "Come in or don't. The door is patient too."

*Don't,* said every sensible bone in Dominic's body.

He walked through the door.

* * *

Inside was not a mountain.

Or rather, inside was exactly a mountain, but a mountain that had been hollowed by someone with infinite patience and no respect for geology. The passage opened after fifty paces into a cavern so vast its ceiling vanished into darkness overhead, and the darkness was full of lights.

That was the first thing. Before the architecture, before the courtiers, before anything — the lights.

They hung in the air at every height, hundreds of them, thousands, each one a small white flame burning inside a sphere of glass no bigger than an apple. They were strung along galleries, clustered around pillars, suspended over staircases that spiraled up into the unseen dark. And they were not lamps. Dominic knew what firelight looked like. This was colder, whiter, steadier, and looking at it made something in his chest ache with a homesickness for a place he'd never been.

"Those are stars," Jude whispered beside him. "Dominic. Those are *stars.*"

Some of them, maybe. Or pieces of them. Threads unpicked from the sky and wound into glass.

"They are caged starlight," Vess said, falling into step beside them with the unhurried grace of someone who owned time itself. "We find it superior to flame. Flame flickers. Stars burn whether or not anyone deserves them." She gestured ahead, down a gallery floor of polished black stone that reflected the hanging lights so perfectly it looked as if they walked between two skies. "You will want to eat, I imagine. Rest. The audience with the court will be arranged once you've recovered from your journey. We are civilized. Whatever stories the valley tells about us — and I'm sure they tell such interesting ones — we are civilized."

"What stories does the valley tell about you?" Dominic asked.

"None at all, if the wards worked properly." She glanced at him sidelong. "Which is rather the point, isn't it?"

The gallery opened onto a hall, and the hall was the most beautiful thing Dominic had ever seen, and he hated it instantly and completely.

Pillars of veined marble rose to a ceiling painted — no, not painted; *grown*, some organic process of mineral and light — with constellations that drifted slowly, re-forming themselves into new patterns as he watched. Fountains ran with water so clear it seemed lit from within. Music came from somewhere, thin and sweet, a melody that felt familiar in the way a word feels familiar on the tip of the tongue. Courtiers moved through the space in ones and twos: tall figures in jewel-toned silks, faces lovely and slightly wrong, the way a mask is wrong — symmetrical where faces shouldn't be quite so symmetrical, smooth where lines should live. None of them aged, he realized. None of them had a single grey hair or weathered crease, and none of them looked at Jude and him for longer than a polite moment, the way you don't look too long at livestock being driven through a market square.

And everywhere, the caged stars. Hundreds in this hall alone. Their combined light cast no shadows at all, and Dominic found the absence more unsettling than any darkness could have been. Everything in this place was illuminated and nothing in it was revealed.

"You're staring," Vess observed.

"The lights." He kept his voice level. "Where do they come from?"

"From the sky, originally." She said it the way a housekeeper might report on the provenance of the silver. "From debts, more recently. Come. The east wing has rooms prepared."

"Prepared for how long?"

She paused at the foot of a staircase, and for just a moment her pleasantness thinned enough to show the machinery underneath. "Since the night the wards went up," she said again. "Thirty years ago, a man named Aldric Thornbury came to this court and asked us to seal his valley and hide his children. We asked what he would pledge. He pledged the usual — years, luck, land-rights deferred. And he pledged something unusual." Her frost-pale eyes settled on Dominic with terrible gentleness. "He pledged the heir unborn. You. Your father signed for you before you drew breath, Dominic-who-is-also-Thornbury-heir. So yes. The rooms have been ready thirty years. We keep our accounts current."

The storm in his bones woke fully then, roaring up his spine, and for one blinding second Dominic saw the hall lit in blue-white — saw the courtiers flinch, saw Vess take an actual step back, saw the caged stars tremble in their glass like birds hearing a hawk. Then he clenched his jaw and swallowed it down, and the light died, and the hall returned to its shadowless calm, and everyone pretended nothing had happened, which was somehow the most horrifying part.

"My apologies," Vess said dryly, smoothing her sleeve. "Do try to keep that leashed indoors. The last heir who discharged raw inheritance in the great hall took a decade off my restoration budget." She resumed climbing. "This way."

* * *

The rooms were beautiful. Of course they were. A bed hung with curtains the colour of dusk, a washbasin of white marble, a window — an actual window, cut into the mountain's flank, looking out onto the starless dark — and a table laid with food that steamed and smelled of rosemary and warm bread, real bread, the first either of them had seen since leaving the refugee road.

Jude shut the door and put their back against it. "I hate it here."

"I know."

"No, I mean I *hate* it here. My skin hates it. Look at my arms." They pushed up their sleeves. Fine hairs risen, all the way from wrist to elbow. "It's like walking into a spiderweb you can't see. Everything's so *nice*. Nothing is nice, Dominic. Nice is what it looks like right before something eats you."

"I know." He sat on the edge of the bed, because his legs had decided independently that they were done. The sigil lay in his palm, warm as a held bird. "Thirty years these rooms have been waiting for me. My father sold me before I was born. Did you hear that? He didn't even sell me for something. He sold me for a locked door and a quiet valley."

"He sold you because he was terrified," Jude said. "Everyone was terrified, that year. Even I've heard about the year the blight came." They crossed the room, took the sigil gently out of his hand, turned it over. The blue-white thread of light pulsed against their fingers. "Doesn't make it right. But terrified men sign terrible papers. That's how all of this works, isn't it? That's what Marisol said. Nobody remembers agreeing, because agreeing happened in the worst moment of someone's life, and then everyone spent generations pretending the paper was sacred."

Dominic looked up at them. Candle-starlight caught the angles of Jude's face — the face he'd known since he was six years old, the foundling the valley never explained and never questioned, because questioning would have meant admitting nobody knew. A placeholder. That's what Jude had called themselves once, drunk on festival wine, laughing, and he had laughed too and hated himself for a week.

"Whatever they think you are," he said quietly, "whatever's written in their ledgers — you're not a debt. You're not collateral. I need you to know that before we go any further in."

Jude's hands stilled on the sigil. For a moment their expression did something complicated and unguarded, and then they set the disc down on the table with exaggerated care, next to the bread, as if putting distance between themselves and the conversation.

"Eat," they said. "Sleep if you can. Tomorrow we find out what heir means." They hesitated at the window, looking out at the lightless sky. "And Dominic — those stars in the glass. Some of them were flickering. Did you notice? Just a few. Like they were trying to go out and couldn't."

He hadn't noticed. He noticed it now, replaying the gallery in his memory: here and there among the steady white flames, a stutter. A struggle. As if whatever burned inside some of those spheres was still, after all this time, refusing.

He slept badly, in a bed that smelled of cedar and old promises, and dreamed of his grandmother counting on her fingers in a language he didn't speak.

* * *

They came for him at what might have been morning — there was no way to tell; the window showed only the same starless dark — with an invitation written on vellum so fine it was nearly translucent. *The court requests the pleasure of the Thornbury-heir's presence.* Below it, in smaller script: *Alone.*

"Not alone," Jude said flatly.

"Guest-right protects you here," said the courtier who'd delivered it, a youth with cheekbones like cut glass and eyes the colour of shallow water. "No harm comes to guests. It is the oldest law we have, older than the court itself."

"Older than the court," Dominic repeated. "What's older than the court?"

The courtier's smile didn't waver, but something behind it did — a flicker, quickly smoothed, like the struggling stars in their glass. "The debt, of course," he said, and bowed, and left.

Vess was waiting for him at the doors of the great hall, alone, which struck him as significant. She wore grey today, he noticed. Mourning colours, or working ones.

"A word before you go in," she said, low. "Free advice, which in this place is the rarest commodity. When the magistrates offer you terms — and they will offer you terms, they always offer terms, it is cheaper than taking — listen carefully to what is *transferred* and what is *erased*. In this court, those words look identical on the page and could not be more different in the world." Her frost-pale eyes held his. "You strike me as a young man who reads fine print. Good. Read all of it. Read it twice. And ask yourself, before you sign anything, what your signature costs that isn't written on the page."

"Why are you telling me this?"

Something moved behind her composure — brief, buried, decades deep. "Because I signed," she said, "a very long time ago. And I have had three hundred years to read the fine print." The great doors swung open before he could answer, spilling shadowless starlight over them both, and her face closed like a ledger snapping shut. "Go in. Be courteous. Be careful. And heir—" she caught his arm, one instant of pressure, "—when they show you what you stand to inherit, remember to also count what everyone else stands to lose."

Then she released him, folded her hands, and became once more the perfect steward, and Dominic walked alone into the court beneath the mountain.

The hall had been rearranged for him. At its far end, on a dais of black stone, sat seven figures in robes of woven shadow, faces hidden behind masks of polished obsidian. Between him and them, floating at the height of his heart, hung a single object: a scroll, unfurled, its text written in a hand he recognized with a sick lurch — because it was his own handwriting, though he had never written a word of it. His blood had written it, thirty years before he existed, and it had been waiting ever since, patient as the door, patient as the rooms, patient as everything in this beautiful, starving place.

The central magistrate spoke, and its voice was the sound the mountain would make if the mountain could speak.

"Thornbury-heir. Welcome home. We have prepared your inheritance."

The scroll drifted toward him.

Dominic thought of Jude's itching teeth, of the flickering stars, of Vess's hand on his arm, and did not reach for it — yet.
