## 8. Terms of Inheritance

The tea was the color of old blood and tasted like nothing at all.

Dominic set the cup down on a table carved from a single slab of black stone, its surface polished to a mirror that showed his face warped slightly wrong — older, or perhaps just more honest. Around them, the court's great hall breathed cold light from ten thousand caged stars, each one sealed in glass the size of a fist, hung on silver chains at every height, so that walking through the hall felt like wading through a drowned sky.

He kept thinking of Jude somewhere in this mountain, and it made the light obscene.

"You don't drink," Lady Vess observed. She sat across from him in a chair that seemed grown rather than made, pale wood spiraling up around her like the ribs of some enormous instrument. Her hair was white — not aged, but white the way frost is white, deliberate and complete — and her eyes were the grey of slate before rain. "Most of your kind drink. The living always want something warm in their hands down here."

"My kind."

"Thornburys." She said the name the way a jeweler says the name of a stone she is appraising. "You've been down here four hours and you haven't asked for anything. That's unusual. Debtors usually beg within the hour."

"I'm not a debtor."

"No." Something moved behind her face — not quite a smile, not quite pity. "You're an heir. There's a difference, though I'll grant you it's smaller than most people hope."

* * *

They had come down into the mountain through a gate that should not have fit them.

It stood in the cliff face like a wound stitched shut with iron, and when Dominic had pressed his grandmother's contract-sigil against it, the metal had parted the way a mouth parts — wetly, reluctantly, with a sound he would spend years trying not to remember. The corridor beyond had been lined with names. Thousands of them, cut into the rock in scripts that shifted when looked at directly, and Dominic had found THORNBURY repeated down one entire wall, generation after generation, each name followed by a small notation he couldn't read until suddenly, horribly, he could: *paid*. *Paid in part*. *Deferred*. *Paid*.

Jude's hand had been in his for that walk. He could still feel the pressure of it, the way their grip tightened at every name that wasn't theirs. Then the steward's servants had appeared — tall, courteous figures whose faces were smooth as eggs — and "for the comfort of guests," they had separated them. Jude taken to be shown hospitality. Dominic brought here, to the hall of caged stars, to wait.

He had waited three hours before Vess arrived. Now she watched him across the black table with the patience of someone who had outlasted empires.

"Tell me what you know," she said. "I find negotiations go faster when we establish how much lying will be required."

Dominic leaned back. The chair beneath him was warm — that was the worst part, everything down here was subtly, thoughtfully comfortable, as if the mountain itself had been upholstered by something that studied pleasure from the outside.

"I know my family sold things to this court," he said. "Land first, then luck, then years. My grandmother told me before she died. And people." His voice went flat and hard. "Jude. Sold at birth, to settle a debt my father ran up. That's what you're holding. That's what all this light is made of — people who were spent paying other people's bills."

Vess did not flinch. He had expected her to flinch, or deny, or perform outrage. Instead she simply nodded, slowly, like a scholar confirming a citation.

"Accurate," she said. "As far as it goes. Your grandmother was honest with you, which makes her nearly unique among the signatories I've dealt with." She rose, and her gown moved like smoke deciding which direction to fall. "Come. If we are to discuss terms, you should see what is actually being discussed."

* * *

She led him along a gallery above the hall, where the caged stars hung thickest. Up close he could see that each lantern was not merely glass — there were hinges. Tiny, delicate, functional hinges, worked into the silver of each cage.

"They open," he said.

"Everything here opens," Vess said. "Nothing here leaves. It's an important distinction, and the whole architecture of this court rests upon people failing to notice it." She touched one lantern, briefly, the way you'd touch the shoulder of a sleeping child. Inside, the captured starlight dimmed and brightened — once, twice — like breathing. "Your family's magic is stored in the deep vaults. Storm-work, seven generations of it, siphoned at each renewal of the contract. Do you know what your valley was, Dominic Thornbury? Before the wards?"

"A prison," he said. "My exile."

"A battery," she corrected, without cruelty. "The wards didn't keep you in. They kept the storm quiet — yours specifically, because yours is the largest remaining share of it. Every Thornbury born inside those wards was born into a reservoir. The court draws it down slowly, a lifetime at a time, and calls the arrangement inheritance." She turned to face him fully, and up close her slate eyes held reflections of the caged stars, dozens of tiny imprisoned lights burning in each iris. "Which brings us to why you're really here. You came to buy back a soul. You cannot afford it. So let us discuss what you can afford instead."

* * *

The room she took him to was small, round, and utterly bare except for a table, two chairs, and a sheet of vellum laid out with weights at the corners. The vellum was blank. Dominic knew better than to believe it.

"Sit," Vess said, and sat herself, folding her hands. "I am going to offer you a bargain, and I want you to understand something first: I have made this offer eleven times, to eleven heirs of eleven families, and I have never once lied in the making of it. Everything I say now will be true. That is the mercy this court permits me, and I use all of it." A pause. "Judge the truth harshly anyway. Truth can be a trapdoor as easily as a floor."

"Understood," Dominic said, and sat.

"The debt against the soul called Jude stands at maturity on the next new moon. Seventeen days." She said it plainly, and he was grateful and sickened in equal measure that she didn't dress it up. "Collection means exactly what you fear. The soul is drawn, ledgered, and vaulted. What walks away afterward breathes and eats and answers to its name, but the flame that made it a person belongs to this court until the account is settled, and accounts of that size do not settle. Not in a lifetime. Not in ten."

Dominic's hands had gone cold. "And your offer?"

"The Thornbury inheritance is unclaimed. Seven generations of accrued storm-magic, the original land-grant outside the valley, the standing rights of a founding house — all of it sits in our vaults, drawing interest, waiting for an heir to present himself." She slid the blank vellum toward him one inch. "Accept it. All of it. The power, the lands, the name entire. And the debt against Jude does not disappear—" her eyes flicked up, sharp, honest, merciless— "it transfers. It becomes a lien against the inheritance itself rather than against the person. The court collects from the estate, not from the boy. As long as the estate holds value, Jude walks free."

"As long as," Dominic repeated. "Meaning forever, or meaning until the estate runs dry, and then you collect Jude after all."

"Meaning until the account balances," Vess said. "Which, if you accept the full inheritance, will not occur in your lifetime or your children's. I am not offering you erasure. Erasure isn't mine to give. I'm offering you substitution. Yourself in their place — structurally, permanently, legally." She spread her hands, and for a moment the gesture was so human, so weary, that it frightened him more than anything else in the mountain had. "That is the bargain. Power beyond anything your valley ever dreamed, in exchange for being the collateral. Refuse, and in seventeen days we take the flame, and you keep your freedom and your clean hands and your grief."

Silence. Somewhere far above, or far below — distance worked strangely down here — a bell tolled once, soft as a heartbeat heard through a wall.

Dominic stared at the blank vellum.

Here was the trap, and he could see every wire of it, and that was the cruelest thing about it: it wasn't hidden. Accept, and he became the thing his family had been for seven generations — a reservoir, a battery, a rich man whose wealth was other people's lives, wearing a crown of stolen storm. He would hold power enough to crack the sky, and every ounce of it would be mortgaged, and every day he spent it, he'd be spending down the margin between Jude and the vaults. He would live as a hostage with excellent manners.

Refuse, and Jude burned in seventeen days while he stood outside the gate holding nothing but righteous anger and a knife.

Complicity dressed as mercy. Mercy dressed as complicity. He genuinely could not tell, looking at the vellum, which direction the disguise ran.

"You said you've offered this eleven times," he said quietly. "Eleven heirs. How many accepted?"

"All of them," Vess said.

"And how many regretted it?"

She considered the question with real care, the way she seemed to consider everything — as if answers were debts too, and she hated adding to anyone's account.

"Regret," she said finally, "is not a category this court recognizes. But if it were — all of them."

* * *

He walked the gallery alone after she left him — she had withdrawn with the courtesy of someone who understood that bargains need darkness to grow in — and stood among the caged stars with his palms flat on the railing, breathing.

Seventeen days.

He thought about his father, who had died when Dominic was nine, a man worn smooth and silent like a river stone, who had never once raised his voice and never once explained why the family flinched at thunderstorms. He thought about his grandmother's last words — *the sigil, take the sigil, it's the only piece of us they can't rewrite* — pressed into his palm with fingers already going cold. He thought about the wall of names, THORNBURY after THORNBURY after THORNBURY, each one followed by *paid*, and wondered how many of those payments had been made in exactly the currency Vess was now offering him. How many ancestors had sat in that bare round room, staring at blank vellum, doing this same arithmetic with the same cold hands?

All of them accepted. Of course they had. Love made cowards of everyone, eventually; it just took different amounts of time.

But — and here his thoughts snagged, caught on something Marisol had said on the road, the hedge-witch with her sister-shaped grief and her eyes like banked coals — *contracts are only as strong as the weakest clause, boy, and coercion leaves scars in ink. Read everything. Trust nothing signed under famine.*

There was something wrong underneath all of this. He could feel it the way he'd felt the storm wake in his bones the night the stars went out — a pressure, a wrongness, a vast weight balanced very precisely on a point too small to see. Eleven heirs had accepted, and eleven estates had held, and the machine ground on century after century, fed and fat and patient. A system that neat didn't happen by accident. A system that neat was *maintained*.

And Lady Vess — he turned her over in his mind, the frost-white hair, the caged lights reflected in her eyes, the terrible careful honesty of her — she had said *everything here opens, nothing here leaves.* Why say that to a mark? Why show him the hinges?

Unless she wanted him to look.

A servant appeared at his elbow, smooth-faced and courteous, bowing. "The heir is asked to forgive the interruption. His companion has been shown every courtesy, and requests — " the egg-smooth face did something that was almost an expression — "requests to know whether he is well. We are instructed to convey that the new moon arrives in sixteen days, and that the court hopes for a swift resolution, for everyone's comfort."

Everyone's comfort. Down here even threats came upholstered.

"Tell her," Dominic said, "that I haven't decided."

It was true. He hadn't. But as he followed the servant deeper into the mountain — down corridors where the walls began, faintly, to hum with stored weather, down toward wherever they were keeping Jude — one thing had crystallized in him, hard and bright as hail:

He would not sign tonight. He would not sign tomorrow. Whatever he did, he would do it knowing what this court actually was — not the version of itself it showed to debtors, but the machinery underneath. And if the machinery had a flaw, he would find it, even if he had to read every forged line of seven generations of contracts with his own eyes.

Sixteen days was not enough time.

He intended to make it enough anyway.
