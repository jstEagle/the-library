## 9. The Soul-Vaults

They took my boots at the door.

Not my knife — I wasn't allowed a knife anyway — and not the little iron nail Dominic had pressed into my palm on the road, telling me to keep it hidden and to keep it *close*. They didn't find that. But they took my boots, with great ceremony, as though boots in the presence of the court were some small blasphemy, and gave me slippers of grey silk so soft I couldn't feel the floor beneath them.

That was the first thing I understood about the court beneath the mountain: everything they did to you would be dressed as kindness.

"You'll be shown courtesy," Lady Vess had said, in her voice like water over stone. "The heir negotiates. The guest waits." She had looked at me a moment too long when she said it — not at my face but somewhere near it, the way you look at a word written in a language you almost remember. "Come."

So while Dominic sat in a chamber full of cold starlight, learning what his name was worth, I followed a servant made mostly of shadow and good posture down corridors that breathed.

That's the only word for it. The mountain breathed. Slow, tidal inhalations in the walls; a warmth in the floor that rose and fell. Somewhere far above, muffled by a hundred feet of living rock, wind moved over stone — the world I'd come from, going on without me. I kept one hand against the wall the whole way, partly for balance in those ridiculous slippers, and partly because if the mountain decided to inhale all the way down, I wanted to be touching something that had once been sky.

The servant did not speak. It poured tea when we stopped, in a room hung with tapestries so old their colors had gone the color of remembering, and it stood by the door while I drank, and when I set the cup down it opened the door again without being asked, which I understood to mean: *you are done resting now.*

*Courtesy*, I thought. *This is what courtesy looks like when it has never once been sincere.*

* * *

The vaults announced themselves before I saw them.

It began as a sound under the sound — a hum I felt in my back teeth before my ears caught it, thin and many-voiced, like a choir heard through the wall of another house. Then light: not the caged, cold white of the court's chandeliers but something warmer and wronger, a gold that flickered in rhythms no fire makes. Fire leaps. Whatever burned ahead of us *breathed*, in time with the mountain, in time with that distant choir.

"The collection," the servant said. Its voice was dry paper. "You may look. You may not touch. You may not speak to them."

"Why not?" I asked, because I have spent my whole life asking questions people wish I wouldn't.

"Because they answer."

I stopped walking. The servant went three paces before noticing and turning, patient as furniture, its face a smooth absence where a face should have details.

"They *answer?*"

"Souls in storage retain awareness. It is part of the preservation." A pause. "Conversation accelerates decay."

*Decay.* As if these were fruit. As if these were cheese.

We came through an archway cut in the shape of nothing I had a name for — a shape that made my eyes slide off it, that hurt slightly to look at directly, the way the sun does — and then I was inside the vaults, and I forgot how to breathe properly for a long moment.

Shelves. Shelves like a library, row upon row upon row, rising into a darkness the lights didn't reach, carved from the mountain itself. And on every shelf, glass.

Jars. Some small as teacups, some tall as wine-flagons, some no bigger than a thimble. Each one held a flame. Not orange, not blue — pale, pale gold, the color of candlelight seen through milk, each one burning upright and still and *patient*, and each one leaning. That was the detail that undid me. Every single flame leaned toward the aisle. Toward me. Thousands of small fires straining gently, silently, toward the warmth of a living body walking past, the way sunflowers follow the sun, the way starving things follow bread.

The hum resolved as I listened. It wasn't a choir. It was whispering. Thousands of voices, worn down to threads of themselves, murmuring at the edge of audibility — names, maybe, or prayers, or just the sound of consciousness rubbing against the inside of a jar.

"You may look," the servant repeated, behind me.

I looked.

I don't know how long. Time went strange in there, stretched like taffy, and my feet carried me along the rows without my permission, reading labels. Because yes — there were labels. Little tags of vellum tied to every jar with thread, written in a hand so elegant it looked like it was showing off:

*Harrow Fen, cooper. Debt of 4 crowns, 2 hens, 11 years.*

*Maren Ostler. Debt inherited from father. Balance: indefinite.*

*A child, unnamed. Debt of the parish of Lowmere. Paid in full.*

I read that one three times. *A child, unnamed.* The jar was very small. The flame inside it was smaller. It leaned hardest of all.

Something hot crawled up my throat. I thought of Dominic, somewhere above me, being offered lands and power in rooms lit with stolen light, and I thought: *this is what the ledger buys. This is the fine print. All those debts nobody remembers agreeing to, and here is where the remembering ends up — on a shelf, in glass, leaning toward anyone warm who walks by.*

"You're monsters," I said to the servant.

"We are stewards," it said. "Monsters take. We keep safe."

"Safe." My voice cracked on it. I turned away from the small jar, because looking at it any longer was going to make me do something that would get me shelved next to it. "Which one is mine?"

I hadn't meant to ask. The question came out of me the way blood comes out of a cut — sudden, pressurized, inevitable. But the moment it left my mouth I knew it was true: I hadn't been brought down here to wait. I'd been brought down here to *see*. Vess had said *the guest waits* with that look that wasn't quite at my face. Courtesy, dressed around cruelty. Or maybe — and this thought arrived cold, sliding between my ribs — courtesy dressed around a lesson. *Look where you live now. Look what you are.*

The servant considered me for a long moment.

"Thirteenth row," it said at last. "West face. The jars marked with the sigil of Thornbury."

*Of course,* I thought, walking. *Of course there's a whole section.*

* * *

The Thornbury shelves were newer than the rest. The stone around them was paler, less worn, the vellum tags less yellowed. Three rows of them, and fewer jars than the great public aisles — perhaps forty, fifty — and every tag written in the same show-off hand, and every debt recorded against the same name, generation after generation after generation, like a family Bible written in someone else's blood.

*Edmund Thornbury. Debt renewed, spring census.*

*Alaric Thornbury. Debt renewed, plus interest.*

*Cassian Thornbury. Debt of the father, transferred.*

I walked the row slowly, reading names I half-knew from gravestones in the valley kirkyard, names the elders said with their hats in their hands. And then, third shelf from the bottom, at eye height, positioned neatly — *positioned*, deliberately, the way you position a document you want found —

A jar the size of a lantern. A tag bearing no debt at all. Just a date. The night I was born, or the night I was found, or whatever the valley chose to call that night, since I'd never been given the difference.

And below the date, four words:

*Foundling. Paid in full.*

My flame burned inside it.

I knew it was mine the way you know your own reflection — instantly, totally, before thought. Not because of the tag. Because of the *color*.

Every other flame in that vault had been pale gold, candlelight-through-milk, uniform as coins from the same mint. Mine was different. Mine was the color of a storm cloud at the exact moment lightning decides. Grey-white, shot through with veins of something darker, silver-black filaments coiling through the gold like hair caught in amber. It burned faster than the others, restless, guttering and recovering, guttering and recovering — and it didn't lean toward me the way the others did.

It leaned *away*. Pressed itself against the far side of the glass like a cat avoiding a stranger's hand, and even through the jar I could feel it — feel it the way you feel weather coming in your knees — a low, familiar dread-hum I had known my entire life without knowing its name.

Because I *had* felt this before. In dreams, my whole childhood. The dream of standing in a field under a black sky while something enormous and patient counted me, itemized me, and found me correct except for one line, one entry, one thing about me that never matched the record. I'd woken from that dream a thousand times with the certainty that I had forgotten something vital, something *written down about me somewhere*, and by breakfast it was always gone.

Here it was. Written down. Burning in front of me.

Someone had tampered with my soul.

There was no gentler word for it. The grey-black veining wasn't damage exactly — when I pressed my fingertips to the glass (breaking the rule, breaking it immediately and completely) the dark threads *moved*, gathered toward my hand like iron filings to a lodestone, and through the contact came a feeling so violent and familiar I staggered back. Recognition. The flame knew me. Knew me and was afraid — no. Not afraid.

*Angry.* Cold, patient, banked anger, older than my memory. My own anger, stored outside me for longer than I'd been alive.

And threaded through it, faint as a watermark, a second signature. Someone else's work laid over mine, a seal stamped into wet wax before it set — deliberate, skilled, and *old*. Older than the tag. Older, I understood with a lurch, than the debt it supposedly paid. The tag said *foundling, paid in full*, but the flame itself said otherwise. The flame said: *look closer. The accounting is wrong.*

Hope hit me sideways. If the flame had been altered — if someone had sealed something into it, or out of it, before the sale was ever recorded — then the ledger's version of me was forged. Then whoever I truly was hadn't been sold, because what was in the jar was only partly me, and the rest of me was standing right there, breathing, uncollected, *loose*.

Then suspicion caught up with hope and they circled each other, snarling.

Because who alters a foundling's soul before birth? Who has the skill, the access, the *reason*? Not the mother the ledger implied had traded me — hedge-folk and farmers don't hold souls in their hands like clay. No. Someone powerful had worked on me. Someone close enough to the court to know its seals, close enough to my cradle to touch what became me. Someone who had needed a child that would pass inspection as sold — and had hidden something inside the flame so well that the court's own elegant hand had written *paid in full* and never questioned it.

Or worse. Worse, whispered the cold voice under my ribs, the voice that had kept me alive through every orphan winter: *someone altered you so you'd be accepted here. So you'd fit a slot. So you'd end up exactly where you're standing right now, in the thirteenth row, west face, touching the glass.*

I looked down at my palm. The iron nail Dominic had given me sat in it, ordinary and warm from my skin, and beside it — faint, raised along the lines of my hand like an old scar I'd never noticed — a mark had begun to show. A sigil. Small, intricate, unmistakably the same hand as the watermark in the flame.

It hadn't been there this morning.

Behind me, soft as snowfall, Lady Vess said: "Now you understand why you were shown courtesy."

She stood at the end of the row with her hands folded, her face finally, fully aimed at mine, and in the storm-grey light of my own stolen flame I saw that she wasn't looking at me like a guest at all.

She was looking at me like a key she'd been waiting decades to use.
