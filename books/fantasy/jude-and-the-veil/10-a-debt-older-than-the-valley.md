## 10. A Debt Older Than the Valley

The archive beneath the mountain smelled of cold iron and old paper, and it was lit by the same caged starlight that burned in the court above — only here the flames were smaller, meaner, shut into lanterns of black glass that drank more light than they gave. Dominic stood at the threshold for a long moment after Lady Vess's steward left him, listening to the door seal itself behind him with a sound like a held breath.

*Three hours,* the steward had said. *The heir may consult the public records. The sealed shelves are not available to guests.*

*Public records.* Dominic turned the phrase over as he walked the first aisle, his boots loud on stone that had never known weather. The court's idea of "public" was probably another kind of cage. But a cage with books in it was still an opportunity, and he had spent thirty years learning how to live inside borders someone else had drawn.

He had come down under pretense — the heir familiarizing himself with his inheritance, as any sensible Thornbury would before accepting Vess's terms. He'd smiled the way you smile at a trap when you intend to spring it from the inside. His grandmother's contract-sigil lay against his chest, wrapped in linen, warm as a sleeping bird. And in the pocket of his coat, where no one had thought to look because no one imagined a guest would dare, was Marisol's list: three titles, three shelf-marks, copied out in her cramped, furious hand. *Where they keep the founding documents of the old families,* she'd said. *Every court keeps its crimes close. That's how you find them.*

The first shelf-mark led him to a section so far back that dust lay on the floor in unbroken grey drifts, printed with the single line of his own footprints. The books here were not books, exactly. They were slabs — sheets of pressed vellum thick as roof-slate, bound in covers of pale leather that Dominic refused, firmly, to think too hard about. Each was lettered on the spine in silver script that rearranged itself as he passed, offering him titles in whatever language sat closest behind his eyes.

He found the third title first. *Thornbury: Instruments of Obligation, Being a True and Complete Record.*

*A true and complete record.* He almost laughed. He pulled the slab from the shelf and carried it to one of the reading tables carved into the rock, where a black-glass lantern waited, and opened it.

The first page was beautiful. He hated how beautiful it was. The court had written out the history of his family in a hand so fine it looked less like writing than like frost forming — and there, at the head of the first leaf, was the sigil he'd found carved into the fallen ward-stone, the sigil that burned faintly against his skin through two layers of linen. The Thornbury mark. Except here it wasn't a mark. It was a signature block, and above it, in letters slightly larger than the rest:

*Let it be entered in the record that Aelius Thornbury, freeholder of the valley lands, did bind himself and all issue of his blood to the Court Beneath the Mountain, in perpetuity, of his own free will and without coercion, on this day of famine's end—*

Dominic read the date four times.

Famine's end. Not famine's height — the *end*. The ink said the bargain had been struck after the hunger broke, when the valley was already recovering, which made no sense. No man signs away his bloodline in a good harvest year. Unless the signing hadn't happened in a good harvest year at all. Unless the date had been chosen later, by someone who wanted it to look voluntary, and had gotten sloppy about the seasons.

His heart had begun to knock against his ribs. He turned the page carefully, the way you turn the page of something sleeping.

There was a second document bound in behind the first — older, cruder, the vellum yellowed and patched. It was written in a different hand entirely, hurried, pressed hard enough into the surface to tear in places. And it was dated eleven days *earlier*.

*To whomever finds this, I set down what was done. The winter took forty of us and would have taken the rest. My children were eating bark. Then came the lady from under the mountain, and she stood in my doorway with grain in her arms and said: sign, or watch them starve by spring. She said the court is generous. She said the court does not coerce. She said if I loved my family I would see the mercy of it.*

*I signed. What else was there. But understand me, whoever reads this — I did not give. I was not asked. I was emptied. There is no free will in a doorway with dead-eyed children behind you, and let any court that calls this consent stand before their own law and say it aloud.*

*Aelius Thornbury*

Dominic sat very still.

Around him the archive breathed its dry, patient breath. Somewhere deep in the stacks, a page turned — or something that sounded like a page turning — and he made himself ignore it. He read the confession again. Then a third time. Eleven days between the coerced signing and the "voluntary" one. Eleven days in which someone had gone back through time on paper, tidying the crime into a gift. And then he understood what he was actually holding: the original, and the forgery, bound together in the same cover by the same archive, because the court kept everything. Because the court's whole power rested on being able to produce any document, any proof, at any moment — and somewhere along nine hundred years, some clerk too honest or too tired had filed the truth next to the lie and left it there.

*Every court keeps its crimes close.*

His hands were shaking now. He turned forward, past the founding instruments, into the renewals — and the pattern repeated, generation after generation, each renewal dated and witnessed and stamped with the sigil, each one accompanied somewhere in the binding by a scrap, a fragment, a marginal note in a different ink. Here a witness statement: *I saw the lady put fear in the room before the pen touched paper.* Here a ledger entry showing land taxes tripled the season before a renewal was "freely" agreed. Here, once, a child's letter — a Thornbury boy of perhaps ten, writing to his grandfather: *Father says we must sign again but father cried after the man left and I don't think crying men are choosing.*

And every renewal bore the same tell, small enough that only someone who had spent the last week staring at contract-sigils would ever catch it: the flourish on the final stroke of the Thornbury name changed over the centuries, but within each renewal it matched the *forged* founding signature, not the true one. Whoever drew up these papers was copying a copy. Nine hundred years of obligations resting on a signature no living Thornbury had ever made.

The court knew.

Not suspected. Not erred. *Knew.* Had known since the beginning, had curated the evidence of its own fraud with archival devotion, had renewed the lie every generation because the truth was sitting right there on a shelf where anyone — anyone who could get this deep — might read it. And nobody ever had, or nobody ever survived reading it, or the ones who read it were the ones whose souls were already in the vaults, flames in glass, safely unable to testify.

That was the shape of it. That was why testimony mattered. A document could be dismissed, reinterpreted, locked away. But a soul that had been taken by a fraudulent contract — a soul standing in the court's own hall, speaking — that was a different instrument entirely.

Marisol's voice in his memory, flat and certain: *A contract voided by fraud needs no magic to break it. Only proof, spoken where the law can hear. That's the terror of it, for them. That's why they keep the proof so close.*

Dominic closed the slab. Sat with both palms flat on its cover, breathing, letting the fury settle out of his blood and into something harder and colder and far more useful. All his life he had believed the valley was his punishment — exile for a family failing he couldn't even name, magic sealed away, stars overhead like a ceiling he was born beneath rather than under. Now he knew: the valley was the *evidence locker*. His whole family, nine generations, had lived and died inside a forged document, and called it heritage.

He thought of Jude. Of the pale flame he hadn't seen yet but knew was waiting in a vault upstairs — Jude's soul, sold at birth to settle a debt incurred under a contract that had never been valid. Sold with counterfeit money. Every debt the court had collected from the Thornburys — the luck, the years, the land, and finally the people — every one of those collections had been made against a fraudulent instrument. Which meant, if the old law meant anything, that every one of those debts was still owed *back*.

He was still standing there, the slab under his hands, when he noticed the silence had changed quality. The archive's dry rustle had stopped. Even the black-glass lanterns seemed dimmer.

"You read quickly," said a voice behind him, "for someone with three hours."

He turned. Lady Vess stood at the end of the aisle, her silver-grey dress making almost no sound, her face arranged in the courteous blankness she wore like armor. But her eyes went — deliberately, unmistakably — to the slab on the table, and stayed there a moment too long.

"The records were open to me," Dominic said.

"They were." She crossed the aisle slowly, stopping an arm's length from the table, and looked down at the forged founding page as if greeting an old acquaintance. "Nine generations of your family have sat at that table," she said quietly. "Some of them read this exact book. Do you know what they all had in common?"

"They didn't survive?"

"They *signed*." Her voice didn't rise. "They read the truth beside the lie, and they signed the renewal anyway, because the alternative was watching the valley's wards fail and the valley's people be collected piecemeal, family by family. Your ancestors chose the cage they could see over the fire they couldn't." She lifted her gaze to his. "What makes you different, Thornbury-heir?"

Dominic looked at her — really looked — and saw what he'd half-seen since the gate: not cruelty. Exhaustion, centuries deep, wearing courtesy as a mask. A prisoner guarding the prison.

"What makes me different," he said, "is that I'm not signing."

Something flickered across Vess's face — hope, maybe, or grief, or the ghost of a woman who had tried exactly that once and lost everything. She glanced at the slab again. When she spoke, her voice was lower still, pitched for him alone.

"The archive closes at the bell. Take the hour you have left." She turned, then paused, her back to him. "There are other copies," she said. "Of the confession. Sealed ones. The archivists file duplicates of everything — it's the law beneath the law, and even the magistrates obey it. If you're going to do what I think you're going to do, one document is a curiosity. Three, produced publicly, is a proceeding."

She walked away without waiting for thanks, and the aisle swallowed her grey figure, and Dominic stood alone among nine hundred years of filed crimes with his blood singing like a storm front.

He worked fast after that. The duplicate shelf was where Marisol's third shelf-mark pointed — of course it was; she'd known — and the archivist's stamp-book hung open on its chain beside it, because the law beneath the law required the filing to be verifiable. He pulled three copies of Aelius Thornbury's confession, each bearing the archive's own seal attesting it was a true duplicate of a true original. Three documents. One proceeding.

At the bell, the door unsealed behind him. He walked out past the steward, past the guards, carrying stolen nothing and everything, the sigil burning against his chest like a second heartbeat.

Above, in the corridors of caged starlight, he heard the court preparing for the new moon collection — and he knew, with sudden terrible clarity, that Vess had not warned him out of kindness.

She had warned him because she knew what he didn't yet: the new moon was coming early.
