## 12. The Steward's Confession

There is a particular silence that lives in the soul-vaults, and Lady Vess has spent three hundred years learning to listen to it.

It is not the silence of emptiness. The vaults are never empty — they hold eleven thousand four hundred and six flames as of this morning, each one a soul in glass, each one burning with the small patient light of a life paused rather than ended. No. It is the silence of held breath. Of things waiting to be called upon. She has walked these corridors so long that she no longer hears it as silence at all; she hears it as attention.

Tonight the attention has sharpened. Tonight there is a Thornbury-heir somewhere above her, digging through her archives like a mole after roots, and a nameless foundling standing in front of case 9,207 with an expression Vess recognizes the way one recognizes one's own face in dark water.

She does not go to them. Not yet. First she does what she always does when the ledger shifts: she descends to the lowest vault, the one no steward is supposed to visit alone, and she looks at the oldest flame of all — her own.

It burns in a case of black glass, set apart from the others, and its color is wrong. Not wrong the way Jude's flame was wrong, tampered and patched by some clerk's careful fraud. Wrong the way a candle is wrong when it has been burning inside a sealed jar for centuries. Dim. Starved. Hers.

"Still here," she says to it. Her voice comes out flat. It always does, down here. "Still mine. You'd think I'd be used to it."

The flame flickers, or seems to. Three hundred years of talking to your own soul teaches you not to trust the difference between response and hope.

She remembers the day it went into the case. That is the cruelty of the court's accounting: they let you remember. They take everything else — her lands on the southern coast, drowned now beneath water no map admits to; her name, which was not *Vess*, which was longer and warmer and belonged to a girl who sang; her sister, whose flame had gone into a different case, a smaller one, and who had lasted only sixty years before the court declared the debt settled and *consumed* the balance — but they let you keep memory, because memory is what makes service possible. A steward who forgets why she hates her masters becomes merely obedient. A steward who remembers becomes useful. Fear with a long view, they called it, in the old ledgers, in ink that had been mixed with something that was not quite blood.

*You will keep the vaults,* they told her, when she knelt where Dominic Thornbury had knelt this morning, on stone worn smooth by every other throat that had ever bargained here. *You will keep them well. And in exchange we will not collect your mother.*

Her mother had died anyway, four years later, of a fever any hedge-witch could have broken. The court expressed regret. Regret, Vess learned, was the one currency they minted freely.

She turns from her own dim reflection in the black glass and climbs the stairs, because the hour has come when she must decide what kind of steward she is going to be for one more night.

* * *

She finds the foundling first.

The girl — no, not a girl, Vess corrects herself; the archives say *foundling*, age uncertain, sex unrecorded because the clerks who processed a soul sold in infancy rarely bothered — is still standing before case 9,207. Jude, the valley calls them. A made-up name, a placeholder, and Vess knows something about placeholders too.

"You've been here an hour," Vess says from behind them, and enjoys the way they startle and then refuse to show it. Good spine. The good ones always have spines. It makes consuming them harder, which is perhaps why the court prefers the broken ones.

"You said I was free to look," Jude says.

"I said you were shown courtesy. Courtesy and freedom are cousins, not twins." Vess comes to stand beside them. Up close, the foundling smells of road dust and hedgemagic — Marisol's work, unmistakably, wards woven into the seams of their coat. Clever woman. Stubborn. Dead within the decade if she keeps this up, but clever. "Do you know what you're looking at?"

"My soul."

"Your soul as *recorded*. Which is not the same thing." Vess lifts the case from its bracket. It is lighter than people expect; souls weigh almost nothing, which is how the court gets away with carrying so many. The flame inside gutters pale green at its heart — wrong, all wrong, a bruised and mended color. "This has been altered. Dozed over, we call it. Someone paid a dozing-fee and had a piece of you rewritten before you ever arrived here. Do you know what a dozed flame means, in law?"

Jude's jaw tightens. "Tell me."

"It means whatever is written over the top of it is *not true*. The court's records are perfect. Its flames are honest. When the two disagree, the flame wins — if anyone ever thinks to ask the flame." Vess sets the case back with exaggerated gentleness, the way one handles a thing that might go off. "No one asks the flames. Asking the flames is how stewards end up as exhibits."

"And you're telling me this because?"

Because three hundred years is a long time. Because her sister's case is empty and has been for two hundred and forty years. Because tonight, for the first time since she took the oath, the arithmetic has changed — a coerced founding contract, forged renewals, an heir angry enough and stupid enough to dig. Because the Veil is starving, and starving things stop pretending, and when they stop pretending everything built on the pretense comes down at once, and Vess would very much prefer to be standing on the right side of the wall when it falls.

"Because," she says aloud, "I am about to commit treason, and I would rather not do it twice."

* * *

She finds the heir in the archive, exactly where the ledgers said he'd be, surrounded by the founding documents with his hands flat on the table like a man holding down a storm.

Dominic Thornbury looks up at her entrance, and Vess watches him decide, visibly, whether to hate her. He decides yes. It suits him. Hatred is fuel, and he will need every drop.

"Lady Vess," he says. "Come to gloat?"

"Gloating is for creditors. I'm staff." She closes the archive door behind her and seals it — not with the court's sigils, which report to the court, but with an older working, one she has kept oiled and hidden for three centuries the way other prisoners keep files. The room goes quiet in a new register. Dominic feels it; she watches the hair rise on his arms. "Your storm-calling. You felt that."

"What did you just do?"

"Bought us an hour. Possibly less. Sit down, Thornbury-heir, because I am going to tell you the shape of the trap you're standing in, and you are going to want to be sitting."

He doesn't sit. Of course he doesn't. But he stops glowering, which is nearly the same thing, and she takes it.

"The contract your grandmother confessed to," Vess begins. "The one that sold land, luck, years, and finally souls. You've read the founding version. You know it was coerced — famine year, a creditor wearing a kind face, a signature extracted under duress. You know the renewals are forged. You came here thinking that if you could prove it, the court would be *embarrassed*." She lets that hang. "Thornbury-heir. There is no one to embarrass. There is no throne above the magistrates. There is no king behind the stewards. I have served this court for three hundred years and I am telling you, as the person who keeps its books: the chain of command ends in a room with nothing in it."

"That's absurd—"

"There is a chamber at the mountain's heart," Vess says, "with a chair in it, and the chair is empty, and it has always been empty, and every magistrate who sits in judgment below it knows it is empty and rules anyway. Because the authority they invoke isn't a person. It's the thing the contracts are *written toward*." She leans forward. "The Veil, Thornbury-heir. Your family didn't sell to the mountain court. The mountain court sells to the Veil. We are not your jailers. We are your middlemen."

Something moves behind his eyes — the same vertigo she'd felt, once, kneeling on that worn stone, understanding for the first time that the hand on her neck belonged to a hand that belonged to a hand that ended in darkness.

"Why?" he says. "What does it want with souls? With stars? What kind of hunger eats *sky*?"

"Not a hunger for food. I thought that, at first. Everyone does." Vess walks to the window slit cut into the archive's outer wall, high on the mountain's shoulder, where outside the starless gray presses against the stone like a tide against glass. "The Veil is a seam, heir. Between what is and what might have been. It was sewn — someone sewed it, ages ago, to keep two worlds from grinding against each other, and like everything sewn it wants to come undone. But it cannot simply tear. A seam rips; it does not *choose*. So it needs hands. It needs agents. It needs a court that collects debts and feeds it — not the souls themselves, not mostly. It feeds on the *witnessing*." She turns back to him. "Every soul in my vaults is a story interrupted. Every interrupted story is a thread pulled loose. And a seam with enough threads pulled can be unpicked without ever tearing — quietly, lawfully, one notarized injustice at a time. The stars aren't food. They're witnesses. The last ones left who saw the seam being sewn. Your valley put them out because your valley's debt came due, and the Veil wanted the sky blind."

Dominic has gone very still. Stillness, in a storm-caller, is more frightening than shaking. "And if it finishes unpicking?"

"Then both worlds come apart along the seam, gently, thread by thread, forever — no catastrophe, no apocalypse anyone could fight. Just everything gradually becoming less real." She smiles, and knows it's a bad smile. "That's the horror of it, heir. There's nothing to stab. The enemy is a process. It has lawyers."

For a long moment he says nothing. Then: "You've known all this. How long?"

"Since before your valley existed."

"And you kept the books anyway. You kept the *vaults*." His voice drops, and there it is — the fury, banked now but lit, aimed at her at last instead of the abstract court. "Eleven thousand souls, and you dusted their cases and filed their numbers and called yourself a prisoner."

"Yes," Vess says.

She lets him have the word. She owes him at least that much honesty before the rest.

"You think I haven't stood where you're standing? In this room, with this knowledge, hating myself in exactly the register you're using right now?" She pulls open her collar — an indecency, in this court, and she does it deliberately — and shows him the scar at her throat: a sigil, old and faded, twin to the one his grandmother carried, twin to the one she knows is written into his skin under the sleeve. "My contract is three hundred years old, Thornbury-heir. I tried to break it. I was young, and brave, and I had a sister, and I thought righteous anger was a weapon. The court did not kill me. Killing creates martyrs, and martyrs make bad staff. Instead they gave me a choice: my soul into the vaults and my sister freed — or my service, and my sister's debt *transferred upward*, doubled, compounded annually." She fastens the collar with steady fingers. Steady is a skill. She has practiced. "I chose her. And then I watched the court consume her balance anyway, forty years early, on a technicality, and I understood that I had not chosen anything. That is what complicity dressed as mercy looks like from the inside. You sat across a table from it yesterday. Vess offers you the inheritance and the transfer. I *was* Vess."

"Then help me," Dominic says. "If you know the flaw in the machine—"

"The flaw is testimony." She says it quickly, before her nerve goes — three hundred years of caution does not die easily. "A contract voided requires the wronged party to speak. Publicly, before the assembled court, under the old law your hedge-witch friend has been digging out of her own bones for thirty years. But hear me, heir, and hear me carefully, because this is the part the court counts on everyone not knowing until it's too late." She steps close. Up close, his eyes are the exact grey of weather coming. "A sold soul that speaks — truly speaks, gives testimony, asserts itself against the ledger — spends itself doing it. The words burn the flame. Testimony is not free. The last soul that testified in this court spent everything it had in nine sentences, and what was left afterward wouldn't light a wick."

"So it costs them everything."

"It may cost them *everything*, yes. Or part. No one knows in advance, and the court certainly won't warn them." Vess holds his gaze. "Which means you cannot save him by finding a witness. Understand? Whoever speaks for you chooses their own consumption. You would be asking a soul to pay your debt with its existence. That is the trap under the trap. That is how the Veil stays fed even when it loses — because our victories cost exactly what our defeats would have."

Dominic Thornbury closes his eyes. When he opens them again, something has shifted in his face — not despair, worse, better: calculation. The look of a man who has stopped trying to win cleanly and started asking what clean is even worth.

"Jude's flame is already dozed," he says slowly. "Tampered. You said it yourself — the records lie, the flame tells the truth. If Jude testifies—"

"If the foundling testifies, they testify as *themselves*, not as the debtor recorded in your ledger. The mismatch is your loophole. Their identity is evidence." Vess allows herself one breath of something adjacent to admiration. "Yes. That's the crack. And the court will see it coming the moment you do, which is soon."

"Then we move before they seal it." He's already gathering the founding documents, folding them against his chest like armor. "How long do we have before the new moon?"

And there it is. The question she has been dreading, the answer she descended from her own dim flame to deliver, the reason she bought this hour with workings she cannot afford.

Vess crosses to the window slit and looks out at the blind sky, and for a moment she lets herself be, simply, an old woman who misses stars.

"Heir," she says. "I have to tell you something, and you must not shout, because the walls in this mountain listen for shouting." She waits until he turns. "The new moon isn't in nine days. It hasn't been in nine days since yesterday evening. The court accelerated collection — breached hospitality, they're calling it, your snooping in the archive. The rite is in *three days*. And this morning the summons was already written." She holds his gaze and delivers the rest flat, without mercy, because mercy now would just be a slower knife. "It wasn't addressed to the debt. It was addressed to the foundling. *Jude*, by the only name the court has for them: case 9,207. Collection at the next moonrise. Attendance mandatory."

Dominic doesn't shout. To his credit — or his ruin — he doesn't shout. He goes white, then grey, then very quiet, and the air in the sealed room begins, faintly, ionized and metallic, to taste of lightning.

"Three days," he says.

"Three days," Vess agrees. "And the court has already moved the vault keys. Except—" she draws a breath that tastes of three hundred years of held silence, and commits, fully, irrevocably, to treason— "*except* mine. I still hold mine. I was meant to surrender them tomorrow, at dawn. So you have until dawn, Thornbury-heir, and one traitor's help, and a witness who may burn to ash giving it. Go wake your foundling. Tell them what testimony costs. Let *them* choose."

She unseals the door, and the mountain's listening silence pours back in around them like cold water.

Above them, faintly, through miles of stone, something vast and patient turned over in its sleep — and began, thread by thread, to pull.
