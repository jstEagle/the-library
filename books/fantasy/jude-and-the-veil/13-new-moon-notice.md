## 13. New Moon Notice

The summons came at dawn, and it did not come as paper.

I woke to a sound like a bell being rung inside my own skull — one clear, silver note that went through bone and marrow and left my teeth aching. Around me the guest-chamber of the mountain court shivered: the caged starlight in its wall-lamps flared white, guttered, and steadied into something colder. When I sat up, the note was hanging in the air above my bed, drawn in light the way frost draws itself on glass.

A circle. Inside it, three words in a script older than any alphabet I knew:

*THREE DAYS. NEW MOON.*

Below that, smaller, almost courteous:

*Collection of account: JUDE (unregistered). Attendance of debtor's kin is customary but not required.*

Not required. The courtesy of it was the cruelest part. They were telling me I could stay away. That I could sit in this beautiful cold room and drink their starlit wine while they took Jude apart beneath the mountain, and no one would think less of me, because no one down here thought anything of anyone at all. We were entries. We had always been entries.

I dressed in the dark because I couldn't bear to look at the light. My hands were shaking — not with fear, or not only fear. Three days. Marisol's loophole needed time we didn't have: a public renunciation, yes, but above all a testimony, a soul willing to stand in the court's own hall and speak its coerced contract aloud. And the only soul we'd found willing — a flickering scrap of a woman named Ellery, sold two centuries ago for a harvest that never came — had needed days just to remember how to form words. Days we now had three of.

I went looking for Jude and found the corridor outside their door already occupied.

Lady Vess stood at the threshold like a statue someone had abandoned mid-thought, her grey gown still as poured stone, her hands folded. She was not knocking. Stewards, I was learning, did not knock; they *arrived*, and rooms rearranged themselves around them.

"You received the notice," she said, without turning. Her voice was level as a frozen lake. "Good. I dislike delivering it twice."

"Three days." I came up beside her. Through the door's seam I could hear Jude moving, awake, unhurried — they hadn't heard it yet, then, or hadn't understood. "The new moon isn't for eleven days. Everyone on the refugee road knows it. Your own calendar knows it."

"The calendar," Vess said, "is a courtesy extended by the court to those who honor its hospitality." Now she turned, and her eyes — pale as the vault-flames, pale as everything down here — settled on me with something that in a warmer creature might have been regret. "You entered the archive, Thornbury-heir. You handled records sealed under bond of guest-right. You consulted a soul in custody without sanction."

"I read a ledger."

"You *read a ledger*," she agreed, "that has not been read by an outsider in four hundred years. The court does not distinguish between theft and curiosity. Curiosity is merely theft with better manners." A pause, thin as a knife laid flat. "You breached hospitality. The terms of your visit are therefore amended. Collection proceeds at the true new moon. Three days."

"And if I refuse to attend?"

"Then you will watch from wherever you're standing when it happens. The mountain does not require your presence. Only his debt."

*His.* Something in my chest went tight and hot. "Jude isn't a he or a she or an it. Jude is a person."

Vess regarded me for a long moment, and I swear — I will swear it until I die — something moved behind her face, quick and drowned, like a fish under ice. "Down here," she said quietly, "a person is what a debt becomes when no one is left to collect it. You should hope he stays a debt. Debts can be transferred. Forgiven. Even—" she stopped herself, jaw tightening, as if the word had cost her something. "Three days," she said again, louder, official, done. "The rite begins at moonset."

She walked away down the corridor, and the lamps dimmed respectfully behind her, and I stood there hating her with a hatred so clean it frightened me — because underneath it, I was beginning to suspect, was something worse. Recognition. She wasn't the jailer. She was another entry, one that had learned to do arithmetic.

The door opened. Jude stood in it, hair sleep-tangled, wearing the borrowed court clothes that made them look like a stranger dressed as themselves.

"I heard," they said. "Walls here whisper. Or maybe you were shouting through them with your whole face." Their eyes searched mine. "Three days?"

"Three days."

They nodded slowly, and I watched them do the same arithmetic I'd done, watched it land. "Then we stop being careful," they said.

* * *

We gathered in Marisol's rooms — she'd been given quarters two levels down, which she had immediately filled with drying herbs, chalked sigils, and a general atmosphere of cheerful insubordination that the court apparently tolerated the way a body tolerates a splinter. She listened to the news with her arms crossed, and for once she didn't smile.

"Accelerated collection," she said. "Of course. It's the oldest trick in the ledger. Give a debtor time and he starts thinking; take his time away and he starts panicking, and panicked men sign anything." She looked at me. "Or break anything. Which is it going to be, storm-child?"

"Don't call me that."

"It's what you are. Your grandmother told me before she died — well, shouted at me, mostly, but dying women get to shout." She unfolded her arms and started pacing, boots striking the stone in a rhythm like a countdown. "Here's where we stand. The renunciation you can do tomorrow. Tonight, if you want. Stand in their hall, say the words, burn the sigil off your skin. That part was always going to be easy — it only costs you everything you are."

"But it's void without testimony," I said. "Chapter and verse. The wronged party speaks, or the contract stands."

"The wronged party." Marisol stopped pacing. "And there's our beautiful problem. Ellery can't do it. Two hundred years in glass, and her flame's worn down to a whisper — she tried to practice with me last night and got out four words before she guttered. Four words won't unmake a contract. The court needs to *hear* it, whole, in the old legal cadence, every clause of coercion named aloud." She rubbed her face. "We need a stronger soul. A recent one. One who remembers being alive."

The silence that followed had a shape, and the shape was Jude.

"No," I said, before anyone said anything. "No. Absolutely not."

"I didn't say it," Marisol said. "You said it."

"Because you were thinking it loudly enough to crack stone!" I was on my feet and I didn't remember standing. "Jude's soul is the one thing in this mountain we cannot risk. It's already tampered with — wrong color, wrong weight, someone's fingerprints all over it from before either of us was born. If Jude testifies and the speaking destroys them—"

"*If*," Jude said.

They were sitting by the window — the one narrow window, looking out onto rock, because the court gave its guests views of nothing — and they turned now, calm in that way that had always terrified me more than their anger ever could.

"Dominic. Sit down."

"I won't—"

"Sit *down*." And because twenty years of growing up beside them had taught my body obedience ahead of my pride, I sat. Jude leaned forward, elbows on knees, and looked at me the way they used to look at me across the schoolroom fire, back when we were children pretending the valley was the whole world.

"Listen to me. My soul is already sold. Already in a vault. Already burning the wrong color in somebody's glass. The question isn't whether it gets spent — the court's decided that. The question is whether it gets spent *buying something*." Their voice didn't rise at all. That was the worst of it. "Ellery can't speak. Fine. But I'm not two hundred years worn. I'm fresh. I remember everything — the valley, the wards, the night the stars went out, you standing in the rain looking like the sky had personally betrayed you. And if what's under the tampering is what I think it is — if my flame was altered to hide a proxy bargain, a mother's bargain — then I'm not just a witness, Dominic. I'm *evidence*. The court hates evidence."

"That's exactly why they'll destroy you the moment you open your mouth."

"Probably," Jude agreed, and smiled, and my heart broke along a seam I'd been keeping patched since we were nine years old. "But they were going to destroy me anyway. In three days. At moonset. With bells." They reached over and took my hand — took the hand with the contract-sigil coiled under the skin of the wrist, faintly warm, faintly itching, like a leash remembering it was a leash. "You keep trying to save me *instead of* winning. You have to win first. Then save whoever's left. That's the order. That's the only order."

Marisol had gone very still by the hearth. When she spoke, her voice was gentler than I'd yet heard it. "There may be a middle road. Testimony doesn't have to come from the strongest soul — it has to come at the right *moment*, in front of the right audience, and be *heard*. If the vaults were opened—"

"They won't be opened. Not until after the rite. Vess told me the seals only loose during collection — some law about debts needing witnesses." I stopped. Blinked. "Wait."

Marisol's grin came back slow and wolfish. "There it is," she said. "Go on."

"Collection requires witnesses. The rite is public — court law, Vess said, the magistrates insist on it, they *preen* in it. Which means on the night of the new moon, the hall will be full, the vaults will be accessible, and every soul in this mountain will be watching the ceremony." I felt my pulse climb, that old storm-pressure building behind my sternum, the magic waking in my bones like weather. "We don't testify before the renunciation. We do both at once. Renounce, invoke the old law, demand testimony — and pull it out of the vaults in front of the entire court, while they're all standing there in their finery with nowhere to hide."

"Bold," Marisol said. "Suicidal. Possibly both. I've worked with worse plans." She was already chalking something on the floor. "It hinges on the vaults, though. Someone has to open them at the right moment, and the seals answer to exactly three people: the magistrates, the Veil's voice, and Lady Vess."

"Vess won't help us," I said. "She made that very clear this morning."

"Did she?" Marisol didn't look up from her chalk. "Strange. Because last night, when I couldn't sleep, I went walking — I go walking, it's a hedge-witch condition — and I found Lady Vess standing in the vault corridor at the hour of the dead shift, alone, with her hand flat against the seal-glass. Just standing there. For the better part of an hour." She straightened, dusting chalk from her fingers. "People don't stand with their hands on prison doors unless part of them wants to open them."

* * *

That night I couldn't sleep either, so I went walking too.

The mountain at night was worse than the mountain by day. By day the caged starlight made everything beautiful; by night the cages showed. Every lamp was a small imprisonment. Every polished surface threw back reflections slightly wrong, slightly delayed, as if the mountain were considering whether to let you see yourself. I passed the great hall where the rite would be held — scaffolding already rising, garlands of dead moths' wings being hung by servants who never lifted their eyes — and I kept walking, down and down, following a sound I half-thought I imagined.

Singing. Very far below. Thin as thread.

I knew that song. Jude hummed it sometimes, in the valley, on evenings when the storms rolled in — a foundling's lullaby with no known origin, the kind of song that gets passed hand to hand without anyone remembering learning it. Hearing it now, rising up through the mountain's bones from the direction of the vaults, I felt the hair lift on my arms.

I didn't go down. Some instinct older than sense told me that whatever was singing, tonight was not the night to meet it. Instead I pressed my palm against the cold wall, over the sigil sleeping in my wrist, and made myself a promise in the old cadence Marisol had taught me, the legal cadence, the one contracts respected:

*Three days. Then everything.*

Behind me, deeper in the dark, the singing stopped — as if it had heard.

And somewhere far above us all, beyond miles of stone, the sky began, early and silent, to darken toward the new moon.
