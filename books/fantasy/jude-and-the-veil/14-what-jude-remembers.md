## 14. What Jude Remembers

The courtesy they show Jude is worse than cruelty would have been.

Cruelty could be fought. But this — the soft grey room with its hearth that never needs feeding, the tray of honeyed bread and pale wine, the window that looks out on a garden no one tends because nothing here needs tending to live — this is designed to make a person *comfortable* while the hours run out. Three days. The steward's servants had said it gently, the way you'd tell someone the bathwater was ready.

Jude sits at the window and watches the starless dark press against the glass like a face.

Below, in the corridors of the court beneath the mountain, Dominic is arguing with Lady Vess. Jude can't hear the words through the stone, but they can feel him the way you feel weather coming — a pressure behind the eyes, a taste of iron on the tongue. He has been arguing for two days. He has not slept. And every hour he spends down there, Jude knows, the court is writing something into the record that will bind him tighter than any chain.

*I should be helping,* Jude thinks, and then, bitterly: *I don't even know what I am. How can I help him save something I can't name?*

The thought has been circling for days, patient as a carrion bird. It lands now, finally, and starts to feed.

Because that's the truth of it. That's always been the truth of it.

*Jude.* A foundling's name, given by a village that couldn't be bothered to ask questions. *Found at the ward-line, wrapped in a blanket, no note.* That was the story. That was the whole story. And Jude had lived inside it the way you live inside an old coat — grateful for the warmth, aware always that it wasn't made for you, that somewhere there were shoulders this coat had been cut from, and they weren't yours.

Except now the coat doesn't fit at all. Now the story itself is a lie, or half of one, and the other half is locked somewhere behind Jude's own eyes.

There are memories missing. Jude has always known it the way you know a tooth is gone — by the hole where the tongue keeps going. Whole years simply absent. Not childhood fuzziness; something cleaner than that, surgical. A gap between being very small and being old enough to walk beside Dominic along the ward-line, and in the gap, nothing. Smooth walls. Sealed doors.

Tonight, with three days left, the doors are rattling.

It starts as a smell. That's how memory comes back when it comes back honestly — not as pictures but as sensation. Honeyed bread on the tray, and underneath its sweetness, something else: woodsmoke and wet wool and a woman's hands smelling of lye soap. A kitchen. A small kitchen with a low ceiling, steam on the windows, and a woman singing — not well, not on purpose, just the tuneless humming of someone who works with her hands and lets her mind wander where it wants.

*Mama.*

The word arrives whole, unbidden, and Jude's breath stops.

Not a foster-mother. Not one of the village women who'd fed them in rotation during those early years everyone politely called "the difficult time." *Mama.* A specific woman. Dark hair going grey early, tied back with whatever string came to hand. A scar on her left forearm shaped like a crooked lightning bolt, from a kettle, she said, though she smiled when she said it, and Jude remembers — remembers now, impossibly, from inside a body too small for remembering — that she smiled *because* it wasn't from a kettle at all, and she liked keeping one small true thing private even from her children.

Her children. Plural. Jude can see them now, dim figures around the kitchen table — older ones, younger ones, a boy who stole the crusts off the cooling loaves, a girl who could make the cat dance on its hind legs by humming at it. A crowded table. A loud house. A poor house, poor the way mountain villages were poor, where poverty wasn't an event but a climate.

And Jude — small, so small, small enough that the world was mostly knees and table-edges — sitting on this woman's hip while she stirred something, watching her face turn toward the window where the light was wrong. Grey. Starless-grey, the way everything outside the valley was grey now, except Jude hadn't known that yet, hadn't known there was any other color the sky could be.

The woman looked at the grey sky, and then she looked down at the child on her hip, and her face did something terrible. It went calm. The way water goes still right before it freezes.

*They'll take one,* she said, to no one, to the room, to God if the mountains kept one. *That's what they'll want. One soul to settle it, and they'll let the rest of us breathe.*

And the child on her hip — Jude, tiny Jude, barely walking, barely speaking, and yet somehow understanding every word, understanding it the way children understand things before they have the language to lie to themselves about them — the child reached up and touched her cheek.

The memory should end there. A child that age can't consent to anything. A child that age can barely choose which fist goes in its mouth.

But this was not a choosing like other choices. Jude feels it now, surfacing through thirty years of sealant, and it does not feel like a memory of being forced. It feels like a memory of *deciding*. The seals on Jude's memories weren't placed by the court. They were placed by Jude. By a soul so young it didn't yet know it was supposed to be helpless, looking up at a mother's frozen-calm face and reaching out along some thread only the very young and the very old can see — reaching out to whatever waited in the grey beyond the wards, and saying, in the only language souls speak:

*Me instead. Take me instead.*

A proxy. Not sold at birth. Offered.

The room tilts. Jude grips the windowsill, and the honey-bread goes cold and forgotten, and the sealed years come open all at once like a door kicked in.

* * *

There is a bargain-room. Not underground — above. High in a tower, or what passes for a tower in the grey country, a round room with a round table and chairs that no one sits in because the negotiation happens standing, happens in the air itself. The mother stands in the center holding her smallest child, and around her the air is full of voices. Not the court's magistrates — something older, wearing their shapes loosely, like robes borrowed from taller people. The creditor-shapes. The Veil's hands.

The debt hangs over the family like weather. Jude cannot read the numbers of it — the memory won't give up its arithmetic — but can feel the weight of it, the way the whole household had learned to move quietly under it, the way the father had aged ten years in one season, the way the older children had stopped asking for things. A debt nobody remembered agreeing to. Except someone had agreed. Generations back. Someone had signed, and the signature had been renewed and forged and renewed until the original crime was buried under centuries of paperwork, and the interest had compounded into something monstrous, and now the collectors stood in the kitchen-light of a poor mountain house saying *one soul settles it, one soul, and we'll mark the rest paid.*

The mother's arms tighten around her baby. Her voice, when it comes, is steady in the way that costs everything: *"You'll take what I give you and nothing more. Swear it. Swear it on the ledger itself."*

And the smallest child — warm, heavy, drowsing against her collarbone — lifts its head.

Here is the part Jude has spent a lifetime not knowing, and knowing it now is like swallowing broken glass: the infant didn't cry. Didn't fuss. Looked at the grey shapes with eyes too old for its face, and felt — this is the unbearable part — felt *sorry* for them. Felt the hunger coming off them like heat off a stove, and understood with a baby's terrible clarity that hungry things are also suffering things, and reached out its small hand toward the grey, and offered.

The mother felt it happen. Felt her own child slip the bargain out from under her — because the proxy needed a willing soul, that was the law of it, that was the loophole she'd been gambling everything on finding, and she'd braced herself to forge that willingness, to trick her own infant into agreement somehow, and instead the infant had *volunteered*, had made her lie unnecessary, had saved her from damnation without ever knowing it was doing it.

She broke then. Right there in the tower room, in front of the creditors and the ledger and the ancient starving things, she broke, and kissed the small head over and over and whispered into its hair —

The name.

* * *

Jude comes back to the grey room on their knees, having no memory of falling, hands flat on cold flagstones, breath sawing in and out.

The name is there. It fills them like water filling a vessel, and it fits, oh, it fits, it slides into every hollow place the word *Jude* never quite reached, and it is —

It's a common name. That's the strange grace of it. Nothing grand. A workaday name, the kind given in poor villages to children expected to live ordinary lives: a name for a farmer's daughter or a miller's son, a name worn smooth by generations of use. The mother chose it because her own mother had carried it, and hers before that, all the way back down a line of women who baked bread and mended wool and kept one small true thing private even from their children.

*Sable.*

No — that's not — Jude reaches for it again, and the memory gives it up clean this time, no fog, no seal: spoken into an infant's hair in a breaking woman's voice, syllables like a latch clicking shut on a door that had been open thirty years.

*Wren.*

Jude says it aloud, testing, tasting, and the sound of it moves through the room like a struck bell. Every candle flame leans toward them. In the walls, something vast and cold turns over in its sleep and takes notice.

"Wren," Jude says again, steadier. Their own voice sounds different carrying it. Older. "My name is Wren."

And it's true, it's *true*, and the truth of it hurts worse than the lie ever did — because the name came from love, real love, fierce and desperate, and the sacrifice came from love too, and there is no untangling them. That's the cost of the memory, the price tag stitched inside the gift: Jude was not stolen. Jude was *chosen*, twice over — chosen by a mother who would have damned herself to spare her children, and chosen by their own infant self, who looked at a hungry grey god and offered the only coin it had.

Every year of exile, every orphan's ache, every night of lying awake feeling like a placeholder in someone else's story — all of it was voluntary. All of it was purchased at the fairest possible price by people who loved them, and none of it, none of it, makes the loneliness hurt less. Knowing why you were abandoned is not the same as not being abandoned.

Jude kneels on the cold floor of the courtesy room and weeps — not softly, not prettily, but the way you weep when grief and relief arrive in the same cart and neither will unload first.

Then, because they are their mother's child after all, they stop.

They wipe their face. They stand. And they look at the memory again, deliberately this time, turning it over the way Marisol taught them to turn over contracts, hunting the fine print.

*A willing proxy settles the account.* That was the law the mother invoked. But the account wasn't settled, was it? The court still holds Jude's flame in a vault, still claims the debt runs through to Dominic's generation, still means to collect at the new moon. Which means either the mother's bargain was broken — or it was never honored. Either the court took the willing soul and then kept charging, or —

Or the flame in the vault isn't the whole of it. Jude thinks of the vault, the pale flames in glass, their own burning the wrong color, tampered with, someone's fingerprints all over it. If the soul was given willingly, freely, as payment in full — then the contract for it closed the moment the flame crossed the ledger-line. Anything the court claims after that isn't collection.

It's theft.

Jude's heart is hammering now, but it's hammering with something better than fear. This is what testimony is, Marisol said. A soul willing to speak. And Jude is willing — Jude has been willing since before they could walk, apparently; willingness is the oldest thing about them.

Three days until the new moon. Two, maybe, the way the court cheats time down here.

Jude crosses to the door of the courtesy room and finds it unlocked — of course it is; what threat is a prisoner who doesn't know their own name? — and steps out into the corridor, barefoot, still in the court's soft borrowed clothes, with a name in their mouth like a drawn knife.

Downstairs, Dominic is still arguing with Lady Vess. Jude is going to walk into that room and interrupt him, and say the words that will change the shape of the whole negotiation:

*"I remember. I wasn't sold — I was given. Freely. And your ledger has been charging interest on a debt that was paid the day I was born."*

Behind the walls, the vast cold thing stirs again. Hungry. Listening.

Somewhere far above, past leagues of stone, the new moon is already climbing toward a sky with no stars in it — and it is coming early.
