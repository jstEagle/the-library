## 15. The Renunciation

They dressed him for it.

That was the first obscenity — that the court beneath the mountain had clothes waiting, tailored in the days since his arrival to measurements no one had taken with a tape. A coat of storm-grey silk shot through with threads of silver, the color of his magic when it woke, the color of the sky before lightning. High collar. Buttons of dark glass that caught the caged starlight and held it prisoner all over again.

*You are being presented as an heir,* Lady Vess had said, laying the coat across her arms like an offering or an accusation. *Dress accordingly.*

Dominic put it on because refusing would have told them he was afraid, and because — though he hated himself for noticing — the coat fit like a second skin, like something he might have chosen if he'd been raised in this cold beautiful place instead of a valley that smelled of woodsmoke and rain. That was the court's real craft, he was learning. Not the vaults, not the ledgers. The making of cages you wanted to wear.

He looked at himself in the obsidian mirror of the guest chamber and did not recognize the man there. The man there looked like someone who belonged to a ledger.

"Good," Dominic told his reflection quietly. "Remember what they're dressing you as. Then remember what you came to do."

His hands were steady. He made a point of noticing that.

* * *

The hall of audience was carved from the mountain's living bone, if mountains could be said to have bones — pillars of pale stone veined with silver, rising into a darkness where the ceiling should have been and wasn't. Instead, suspended at impossible heights, hung the lights: spheres of glass, each one holding a star.

Not representations. Stars. He could feel them now that his blood had remembered what it was — feel their heat like small suns pressed behind glass, feel their suffering as a low continuous note under everything, the way you can hear a dog whistle only when your ears are young enough. Thirty years of wards had kept that sound out of Thornbury Valley. Now he heard it everywhere in the mountain, a keening so constant the court had surely stopped hearing it at all.

The magistrates sat in a crescent on a dais of black stone: nine of them, robed in colors that hurt to look at directly, hues that existed here the way the stars used to exist overhead. Their faces were smooth and ageless and utterly indifferent, faces like the inside of shells.

And below the dais, filling the hall's floor, the court itself: the gentry of the deep places, tall and lovely and wrong, wearing beauty the way soldiers wore armor. They murmured among themselves as Dominic walked the long aisle alone. He felt their attention like weather.

Jude stood to one side, flanked by two attendants who were not touching them but were nonetheless unmistakably guards. Jude wore white — the court's courtesy, its little joke, dressing the debt in the color of surrender. Their eyes found Dominic's down the whole length of that terrible aisle, and what passed between them had no words in any language spoken above ground. It didn't need any. *I'm here. Do it. I'm not afraid.* Jude had never once in thirty years been afraid in front of other people; fear was a private thing for them, done alone, at night, silently, and then folded away like laundry.

Three days since the new moon notice. Three days of Marisol scratching law into slate in a cell they'd given her because she refused to leave, three days of Vess standing in doorways saying nothing with tremendous eloquence, three days of Dominic reading the coerced contract until he could recite its forged renewals in his sleep.

*Every acre. Every advantage. Every heir, until the debt is paid.*

Fine print is just a door you haven't opened yet. Marisol had taught him that, spitting the words like gristle.

He stopped at the foot of the dais. The herald — a creature whose voice sounded like coins being counted — began to speak the formal words of presentation, and Dominic let it get exactly four syllables in before he spoke over it.

"I have a statement."

The counting-voice stopped as if cut. Somewhere in the crescent of magistrates, something shifted — not alarm, nothing so honest as alarm. Interest. The court loved novelty the way a collector loves a new specimen.

"You are here to be presented as Thornbury-heir," said the magistrate at the center, a figure whose robe was the red of banked coals. "Presentation precedes inheritance. Inheritance precedes—"

"I know how your arithmetic works." His voice carried further than it should have; he let it. Let the storm in it show, just a little, just enough to make the nearest gentry lean back half an inch. "I've had three days to study it. So let me save us all the ceremony."

He reached into his collar and drew out the chain his grandmother had pressed into his hand with her last strength — and hanging from it, the sigil. The contract-mark, older than the valley, older than the lies. But he didn't hold it up. That was the thing Marisol had made him understand at the end, pacing her cell like a woman defending a fortress with a broom:

"The mark isn't on the pendant, boy. The pendant is just where they keep the receipt. The mark is on *you*. It's always been on you. Look at your own skin in starlight sometime and count the fine lines."

He had looked. In the guest chamber, in the light of a caged star, he had turned his forearm to the glass sphere and seen it: a filigree of silver scars tracing up from his wrist like frost ferns, like roots, like handwriting. The contract written in his own blood, generation after generation, renewing itself in the flesh of every Thornbury heir. His father had carried it. His grandmother. The first of them, starving in a famine winter, had carried it out of a room much like this one believing she had signed a rescue.

Dominic rolled up his sleeve in front of the entire court and let them see it too.

Murmuring, from the floor of the hall. The gentry craned their lovely necks. And the magistrates went very still, in the way of things that have watched ten thousand years pass and believe nothing can surprise them.

"I stand before this court," Dominic said, and the old words rose in him — Marisol had drilled them until they lived in his teeth — "not as heir, but as claimant. I invoke the Law of Open Hands. First-written. Older than this dais. Older than your oldest forgery."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

"There is no such—" began the coal-red magistrate.

"There is," said a voice from the back of the hall, cracked and human and delighted, "and everyone in this room knows it, which is why none of them has ever said it aloud."

Marisol stood by the far doors, arms crossed, grey-haired and unbowed, and the gentry near her edged away as if age itself were contagious. "Go on, boy. Say the rest. Say it *loudly*."

So he did.

"I renounce the Thornbury inheritance." Each word like setting down something heavy. "Every acre. Every advantage. Every right, every title, every debt of honor owed to this house and by it. I renounce the restored magic offered me. I renounce the lands. I renounce the name's protection and the name's obligations both. I am not the heir of Thornbury. I am the last freeborn child of a family that was never free, and I decline the yoke."

The hall erupted — not in anger. In laughter.

It rolled down from the dais and through the crowd of the beautiful and the bound, bright and cruel and relieved, the laughter of people watching a mouse challenge a mountain. The coal-red magistrate leaned forward, and its face finally moved, arranging itself into something almost like pity.

"Heir-child. The inheritance is not a gift you may return. It is a debt you were born carrying. Renunciation without payment is theft. Renunciation without testimony—" and here the laughter around it swelled again, feeding it, "—is a child tearing up a map and calling it the end of the road."

"Testimony," Dominic said. "Yes. I know about testimony."

And he thought of Jude in the soul-vaults, standing before a flame the wrong color, and of what Marisol had told him with her hand gripping his wrist hard enough to bruise: *the wronged party must speak. Not you. Not me. Them. That's the price, boy, and there's no version of this where nobody pays it.*

But that was not yet. That was the next door. This one first.

"I renounce," he said again, louder, and now he did the thing he had sworn he wouldn't do unless the words required it: he reached down into himself, into the place where the storm had slept for thirty years behind the wards, and he *pulled*.

Lightning is just a sky changing its mind. His grandmother had told him that once, drunk on festival wine, laughing at the thunder. *Remember that, Dom. Lightning is only ever a change of mind.*

The storm woke in his bones and he did not aim it at anyone. He aimed it at the signature.

Silver fire ran up his arm along the filigree of scars, and the contract-sigil — the true one, the one in his skin — began to burn.

Not pain, at first. Heat, then light, then a sound like a page tearing very slowly, and the gentry's laughter died mid-note as every eye in the hall fixed on his arm, where the silver roots of the Thornbury contract were lifting off his flesh like mist off morning water, unraveling thread by thread, and where each thread unmade itself the air rang with a sound like a struck bell, and somewhere far above them, beyond the mountain's bone, something vast and hungry turned its attention downward.

The magistrates rose. All nine at once.

"*Counter-clause,*" said the coal-red one, and its voice was no longer amused, no longer indulgent, no longer anything but cold. "Article Nine. Sealed at the founding of this account and binding upon all signatories and their issue." It lifted one hand, and the whole hall's caged stars flared at once, every sphere blazing, the keening rising to a shriek. "A renunciation is void — *void*, heir-child — if the renouncing party stands to benefit from another's obligation. You did not come here alone. You did not bargain alone. You have spent these three days scheming toward the release of a soul entered in our ledger under the name of Thornbury-debt."

The burning on his arm stopped.

Just — stopped. Half-unraveled, the sigil frozen mid-tear, silver threads hanging off his skin like cut strings. And the cold that flooded back into the space the fire had left was worse than any pain, because he understood, all at once, with total clarity, what Article Nine meant.

They hadn't stopped him. They had *waited* for him. Let him burn away as much of the contract as he could bear to burn — and now the remainder, the part still written in his blood, the part that named him, bound him twice over: once as heir, and once as *interfering party*.

"The court rules," the magistrate said, and the nine voices spoke the next words together, in unison, in a harmony that made the pillars hum, "that the renunciation of Dominic Thornbury is entered as *null*. The inheritance reverts. The debts revert. And the interfering party, having attempted to defraud a lawful creditor, is joined to the account."

Down the hall, Jude had gone white as their borrowed clothes. Two attendants stepped toward them, unhurried, courteous, final.

"No—" Dominic started forward.

"And," the coal-red magistrate continued, savoring it now, letting the hall taste it, "in consideration of the interference, collection is advanced. Not the new moon. Not the next new moon. *Tonight.* Both entries. The heir and the debt, together, as the account requires."

The great doors at the end of the hall swung open onto darkness, and out of the darkness came a sound Dominic had heard only once before — the night the stars went out, the night the wards shattered: a sound like glass remembering it was fragile.

Marisol was already moving, already shouting something, her voice lost under the rising chorus of the court.

And Lady Vess, at the edge of the dais, looked at Dominic across the blazing hall — looked at him with an expression he would spend the rest of his life trying to name — and mouthed two words.

*The vaults.*
