## 17. Riot of Small Flames

**Dominic**

The vault doors came apart like a held breath finally let go.

Vess had turned the key — some ancient thing of black iron and star-silver that had hung at her belt for centuries, if the stories were true — and the locks did not so much open as surrender. A thousand doors down a thousand corridors, and every one of them sighed at once.

The sound that came out was not a sound. It was a *reading*.

I had expected screaming. I think we all had. What rose from the soul-vaults instead was voice upon voice upon voice, layered like storm-clouds, and every one of them speaking. Reciting. Names, dates, terms, conditions — the fine print of ten thousand lives poured out into the cold air of the mountain's heart, overlapping and interweaving until it stopped being language and became weather.

"*—in consideration of famine relief, the party of the first part does grant—*"

"*—one daughter, aged six years, to be delivered at the turning of the—*"

"*—all luck pertaining to the eastern fields for three generations, said luck to be collected in—*"

"*—my name, my face, my firstborn's first breath—*"

Marisol grabbed my arm hard enough to bruise. Her eyes were wet and furious and more alive than I'd ever seen them. "They can testify," she breathed. "Dominic. Do you understand what's happening? The old law — a coerced contract is voided by public renunciation *and testimony* — they're testifying. They've been locked behind glass for decades waiting for someone to give them a mouth."

"Then we keep the door open," I said.

Above us, the magistrates' gallery rang with the strike of the court's bell — a sound like a glacier cracking. The nine of them stood in their robes of ledger-grey, faces carved from patience, and the eldest raised her hand, and the temperature of the great hall dropped toward something older than winter.

"This proceeding is unlawful," she said. Her voice cut through the chorus like a blade through reeds. "Souls held in surety are property of the court. Property cannot testify. Seal the vaults."

Flames moved along the walls — the court's wardens, pale shapes with snuffer-rods of void-iron, descending the vault stairs in silent formation. Each rod was capped with a cage of black glass, and I knew, with a sick certainty, what those cages were for. Not killing. *Recollection.* You could put a flame back in its glass. You could put it back and make it very quiet this time.

"They're coming," Marisol said. She planted herself in the vault doorway, small and grey-haired and immovable as a root. From her coat she drew things I hadn't seen before: bundles of dried herbs, knotted cords, a handful of grave-dirt wrapped in linen. Hedge-witch tools. Kitchen-magic. The kind of magic nobody writes contracts about because nobody thinks it's worth stealing. "Go. Find your Jude. Find the Veil. I'll hold the door."

"You can't hold a door against nine magistrates and an army of wardens—"

"I've been holding doors against worse for forty years." Her grin was feral. "Every debt-collector who ever knocked on a witch's door learned that a hedge has thorns. *Go.*"

* * *

**Jude**

I heard the chorus before I saw it.

I had been running — Vess's parting words still ringing in my ears, *the Veil doesn't want worship, it wants witnesses* — when the sound found me in the corridors above the negotiation hall. It came up through the stone like water through a well-shaft, and it was made of voices, and every voice was saying the same word over and over beneath all the legalese, under all the contract-language:

*Witness. Witness. Witness.*

And then, cutting through everything, one voice I would have known anywhere:

"*JUDE!*"

I ran toward it. Of course I ran toward it. Some things don't require thought; they're written deeper than thought, in whatever part of you survived being sold before you could walk.

He was climbing the vault stairs with the contract-sigil burning off his skin in flakes of light, Marisol's knot-cords trailing from his fist, and when he saw me he didn't stop or slow — he just caught me against him for one fierce moment, forehead to forehead, and I felt the storm in his bones answering the storm in mine, two weather-systems recognizing each other across thirty years of silence.

"The flames," he gasped. "They're reading the contracts aloud. All of them. Every coerced clause — the mountain can't hold it, listen—"

I listened. And underneath the roar I heard something that stopped my heart: a voice I knew. Small and clear and burning the wrong color no longer — burning *gold* now, gold shot through with green, my color, my fire — and it was speaking.

Not my name. Something older.

"*...in exchange for the life of the infant Wren Ashdown, the party known as the mother does offer herself and all subsequent issue... the proxy named herein shall stand surety until the debt of blood is... until the debt...*" The voice faltered, frayed. "*I volunteered,*" it said, in a tone of terrible wonder, as if realizing it for the first time in twenty-three years. "*I wasn't taken. I chose. That has to count for something. That has to—*"

"That's you," Dominic whispered. "That's your flame. It's remembering."

"It's *testifying*." My throat closed around the words. "It knows things I didn't know. Dom, it knows my mother's bargain — she traded herself and all her children except one — except *me*, I was the proxy, I stood surety for *her*—"

The mountain shuddered. Somewhere far above, stone cracked along lines no architect had drawn.

* * *

**Lady Vess**

I have served the court for four hundred and eleven years, and in all that time I had never once seen a magistrate run.

They did not run. Magistrates do not lower themselves to running. But I watched the eldest — Sereth, who had signed my own contract, whose signature sat on my soul like a scar — descend from the gallery with her void-iron rod drawn, and I watched her move with the particular haste of someone whose entire architecture of power is collapsing, and I confess it gave me pleasure. Cold, ancient, long-buried pleasure, but pleasure nonetheless.

"Steward," she said. "You will restore order."

"Will I?" I asked.

Her eyes went to the vaults, where the flames were streaming out now in their hundreds, their thousands — not fleeing but *advancing*, pouring into the great hall like a river deciding to change course. Each one blazed brighter as it spoke. Testimony, I understood suddenly, was fuel. The old law was older than the court itself, older than the magistrates' authority, and it fed on exactly this: truth spoken publicly, freely, by those wronged. The court had built its whole edifice on the assumption that the wronged could never speak. We kept them in glass. We kept them *quiet*. We forgot that quiet is not the same as gone.

"Seal the hall," Sereth ordered the wardens. "Snuff every flame that crosses the threshold. Steward, open the deep cells — we will need room for the—"

"No," I said.

The word fell into the chaos and, absurdly, everything near it went still. Four hundred years of obedience, undone by a single syllable.

Sereth stared at me. "You are bound," she said softly. "You signed. Your contract compels—"

"My contract," I said, and here I did something I had never done in four centuries of service: I smiled, "was witnessed by a hedge-witch's sister, whose flame you hold in vault seven. And she has been *screaming* the terms of my coercion into this hall for the last quarter hour. Haven't you, Lira?"

From the flood of small fires, one flame detached — pale gold, trembling, a girl's voice in a woman's fire — and rose to hover beside my shoulder. "*She tried to refuse,*" Lira's flame sang out, clear as a bell over the roaring chorus. "*They threatened her village. They threatened her sister. She signed with a knife at her throat and they recorded it as consent, consent, consent—*"

"Voided," I said to Sereth. "As of tonight. By public renunciation and testimony. Look it up. I'll wait."

Sereth's rod came up. The other eight magistrates fanned out behind her, and the temperature dropped again, and the wardens began to advance on the vault door where a grey-haired hedge-witch stood alone with her arms spread wide.

* * *

**Marisol**

They came at me in formation, which flattered me, honestly. Forty years of chasing this court and they'd finally sent professionals.

The first warden's snuffer-rod whistled down toward a flame darting past my shoulder — a young man's fire, chattering out the terms of a fishing boat sold to settle a gambling debt he'd never incurred — and I stepped into the arc and caught the rod on a knotted cord. Simple work. Knot-magic. You tie a binding while naming what binds, and the cord holds as long as the name holds true. I named the warden's master. I named the magistrate who forged the warrant. I named the debt that had never existed.

The cord snapped taut. The rod froze midair.

"Hedge-magic," the second warden sneered, circling. "Kitchen charms. Granny knots."

"Aye," I agreed pleasantly, and threw grave-dirt in his face.

You'd be amazed what the dead will do for a witch who keeps their graves tidy. The dirt remembered every hand that had ever been lowered into it, and it *objected*, loudly, to being used by void-iron. The warden staggered back, clawing at shadows only he could see, and behind me the flames poured past — dozens, hundreds — each one pausing just long enough to brush against me like a hand on the shoulder as it went. *Thank you. Thank you. Thank you.* I hadn't freed them. Dominic and his steward-traitor had done that. But I'd spent forty years learning the law that made freedom stick, and laws, like hedges, only work if somebody tends them.

"Fall back!" one of the magistrates shouted — the youngest, I thought, the one whose face still did things. "The testimony is contaminating the wards—"

"Contaminating?" I laughed, and it came out ragged. "Lass, it's not contamination. It's *audit*."

Then the eldest one — Sereth, the iron one, the one whose signature I'd spent decades dreaming of burning — pointed her rod directly at me, and I felt the void gather at its tip like a held breath, hungry and absolute, and I thought: *well, Marisol, love. Forty years is a fair run.*

Behind me, the door I was holding groaned. Beyond it, thousands of souls poured toward the mountain's heart, toward the Veil, toward whatever came next. My job was only to keep the way open a little longer. Just a little longer. Long enough for a storm-caller and a stolen soul to finish what the first Thornbury started.

The void gathered. I tied another knot.

* * *

**Dominic**

We reached the heart-chamber as the mountain began, genuinely, to come apart.

The corridor walls were cracked like riverbeds in drought. Through the fissures poured light — starlight, caged starlight escaping its fixtures, joining the flood of testimony overhead. The chorus had grown so vast it had become a single voice with ten thousand mouths, reciting the complete history of the court's predations in real time, and the stone itself seemed to be listening. Stone remembers. I'd learned that from my grandmother, of all people — she used to say the valley's wards weren't walls at all but *memories* set standing upright. And now the memory of the mountain was waking up, and it was furious.

Jude's hand was ice in mine. Their flame — their actual soul-flame, loose from the vaults, blazing green-gold ahead of us like a lantern with opinions — kept pace, occasionally calling out fragments of testimony in a voice that was unmistakably theirs and unmistakably not.

"*The proxy stands surety,*" it said. "*The proxy volunteers. The proxy's name is—*"

"Not yet," Jude whispered. "Not until we're in front of it. Whatever it is."

Ahead, the corridor opened into the heart-chamber, and the heart-chamber was full of the Veil.

But between us and it stood nine magistrates in ledger-grey, and Sereth's void-iron rod was leveled not at me, not at Jude, but at the flood of flames pouring in behind us — and at Marisol, who had somehow arrived ahead of us, scorched and bleeding and grinning like the last lit window in a drowned town, still gripping a cord in each fist.

"Enough," Sereth said. And for the first time, her voice carried something other than patience. It carried *fear*. "This court has stood since before your valley had a name. It will stand after your bones are dust. The debts are owed. The ledger balances. That is *order*—"

"Your order," Jude said, stepping forward, "is a filing system for theft."

The chamber went quiet — even the chorus, even the flames, as if the whole mountain leaned in. My heart hammered against the sigil-scars on my chest. Beside me, Jude lifted their chin, and their flame rose to hover at their shoulder like a second self, and I saw Sereth's composure crack, just slightly, at the sight of a soul walking free beside its own body.

"The testimony is incomplete," Jude said clearly, into that listening silence. "One contract remains unread. Mine. And I'm the only witness who can read it *alive*."

Their flame flared. The wrongness in its color burned away entirely — gold, pure gold now, with that thread of green — and when it spoke, Jude spoke with it, two voices braided into one:

"*In exchange for the lives of her children, the mother Wren Ashdown offers the infant who stands as proxy. The proxy consents. The proxy chooses. Let the record show:* ***the proxy chooses.***"

The mountain shook. Not in collapse this time — in recognition. Somewhere in the court's deepest archive, I knew, a page was turning itself. A contract, coerced in the letter but chosen in the spirit, wobbling on its foundations like a table with one short leg.

Sereth raised her rod. "The court finds this testimony irregular—"

And every freed soul in the chamber answered at once.

* * *

**Jude**

Ten thousand voices. One word.

"*WITNESS.*"

It hit the magistrates like a physical thing — like the storm Dominic carried in his blood, except this storm was made of everyone the court had ever silenced. Sereth staggered. Her rod flickered. The void gathering at its tip guttered and died, starved of darkness by ten thousand points of light refusing to be property.

The flames rose. All of them. Filling the chamber from floor to the cracked ceiling, a riot of small fires in every color fire could be, and each one blazing brighter with every syllable of testimony, feeding the others, feeding the old law, feeding something vaster that stirred in the seam of the world behind the magistrates — the Veil itself, unfolding like a letter finally being opened.

Marisol laughed — a wild, cracked, triumphant sound — and threw her last knot-cord to the floor. "No more doors," she declared. "Let them all in."

And they came. Down every corridor, up every stair, from vaults I hadn't known existed — souls beyond counting, generations of the bought and borrowed and stolen, streaming into the heart of the mountain to say their piece. The magistrates retreated to the gallery, rods useless, robes smoking where stray flames brushed them. Sereth's voice, when it came, was thin as old paper:

"This changes nothing. The ledger—"

"The ledger," said Lady Vess, emerging from the crowd of fire with Lira's flame at her shoulder and an expression four hundred years overdue, "is about to be read aloud. In full. With annotations."

The Veil unfolded wider. Behind it, something looked out — something wearing a face I almost recognized, a face from a famine-generation ago, a creditor's patient smile.

And it looked at Dominic.

"Dominic," I said, grabbing his hand. Because whatever came next, whatever it offered, whatever it wanted — he wasn't facing it without me. "Whatever it says — remember what we learned down here. Everything it owns, it took. And everything it took, it wrote down."

He squeezed back. Around us, the riot of small flames settled into a ring, a jury, a congregation — ten thousand witnesses awaiting the final testimony.

The creditor's smile widened.

"Thornbury-heir," it said.
