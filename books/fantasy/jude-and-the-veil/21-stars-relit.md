## 21. Stars, Relit

The first star came back over the mountain.

Marisol saw it from the refugee road, standing in the churned mud where the hedge-camps had been, her hands still blackened from holding a door against the dark. She had spent forty years learning to read omens, and she knew at once that this was not one. It was too small for an omen. Too quiet. Just a pinprick of white fire punching through the seamless grey above the mountain's crown, as if someone on the far side of the sky had lit a single candle and left it there to see who would notice.

She noticed. She was probably the first in the world to notice, unless you counted the dead, and Marisol had long since stopped pretending she didn't count the dead.

Then a second star. A third. A handful, scattered like spilled salt, and then dozens, and then the sky remembered what it was supposed to be doing and began, all across its whole dark breadth, to do it.

Marisol sat down in the mud. Her sister's name was in her mouth — Ana, Ana, Ana — but what came out instead was a sound with no words in it at all, and it went on for a long time, and the stars kept coming back while she made it, indifferent and generous, the way they always had.

* * *

In Thornbury Valley, the stars came back over a village that had not slept in nine days.

Dominic did not see them. He was lying on the cold stone floor of the emptied court beneath the mountain, and he could not remember his name, and the strangest thing about that — stranger than the vaults standing open, stranger than the ledgers stacked in the great hall like cordwood waiting to be burned or read, stranger than the magistrates' empty thrones with their cushions still dented from centuries of sitting — the strangest thing was how little it frightened him.

He knew something enormous had happened. He knew it the way you know weather is coming: in his bones, which were quiet now for the first time in thirty years. The storm-magic that had woken painfully in him the night the wards fell was gone. Not suppressed. Not sealed. *Given away.* He could feel the shape of the absence precisely, the way you feel a tooth pulled — the storm was out there now, loose in the sky, in everyone, and when thunder rolled distantly over the mountain he felt nothing at all except that it was beautiful and that it belonged to no one and everyone, and that this was correct.

He just couldn't remember who had done it.

He got up. His legs worked. That seemed like information worth having. He walked through the court — vast, cold, gorgeous, its caged starlight fixtures now empty glass lanterns swinging gently in a draft that came from everywhere and nowhere — and found the great doors open onto the mountainside, and beyond them the night, and the night full of stars.

He stood there a long time, watching. He didn't know why the sight made his chest ache. He didn't know why his eyes were wet. Some things, apparently, survived even the loss of a name.

Behind him, somewhere down in the dark of the vaults, a small pale flame was burning the color of dawn, and a voice he didn't recognize yet was saying his name over and over, like a person learning to hold water in cupped hands.

* * *

Jude did not stop saying it until sunrise.

They had carried Dominic out of the heart of the mountain themselves, half-dragging him through corridors where freed souls drifted past like lanterns loosed from strings, each one pausing — Jude would swear to this later — each one pausing to look at him as it passed, as if checking. As if counting him among their number and finding him, for now, mercifully absent from the list.

By the time they reached the valley line, dawn was greying the east and the stars were fading into it, and Jude was hoarse, and Dominic was walking on his own feet and looking around at the ward-stones with the mild curiosity of a man seeing a place for the first time.

"You grew up here," Jude said. It wasn't a question. They'd told him everything twice already. He absorbed it the way sand absorbs rain — patiently, without visible result.

"Did I," Dominic said.

"There's a house. There are people who—" Jude stopped. Started again. "I'm going to keep telling you. Every day if I have to. I'm going to tell you your name every morning until it sticks."

Dominic looked at them. Storm-grey eyes, no memory behind them, and yet something in his face moved when Jude spoke — some groove worn so deep by years of use that even erasure couldn't smooth it away entirely.

"That sounds exhausting," he said. And then, after a moment: "Thank you."

The elders met them at the boundary stones. Old Wren Harrow stood at the front of the crowd, and her face when she saw Dominic was terrible to behold — guilt and relief and grief all fighting for the same ground. She opened her mouth to speak, and Jude watched her take in his blankness, and watch her understand, and watch her close her mouth again without saying anything at all.

Good, Jude thought. Let her carry it. Let all of them carry it. They had lived thirty years inside the protection of other people's sold lives, comfortable in a cage built of stolen souls, and none of them — not one — had ever asked what the walls cost.

The crowd parted to let them through. Someone had hung the Founding Festival garlands back up, days late, half-heartedly, as if decoration could answer for anything. Jude walked beneath them holding Dominic's arm and thought: we will take these down tomorrow. We will take down everything that was built on this. And we will build something else, slowly, badly, honestly.

But that was tomorrow's work. Today there was only the walking, and the name, said aloud, again: "Dominic."

And the man smiled, faintly, like the word fit somewhere he couldn't find.

* * *

Lady Vess did not leave the mountain.

This surprised everyone, including, visibly, herself. When the vaults stood open and the ledgers lay stacked in the great hall and the last magistrate's throne sat empty, she had walked out the great doors expecting — she admitted later — to keep walking until the world ended or she did. Instead she had stopped on the mountainside, looked back at the court she had served for four hundred years, and found that she could not make her feet go further from it.

Not loyalty. Nothing so clean. Habit, maybe. Or penance. Or the simple fact that a prisoner released after four centuries does not immediately know what to do with open sky.

So she stayed, and became, improbably, the court's curator.

It was Vess who organized the reading of the ledgers. She stood in the great hall before a crowd of survivors and refugees and freed souls' grieving kin, and she read the contracts aloud, all of them, beginning with the first — the famine year, the coerced signature, the first Thornbury's trembling hand guided by hunger and threat. She read for eleven days without stopping. When her voice gave out, Marisol read. When Marisol's gave out, a farmer's daughter from the eastern villages read, and then a shepherd boy, and then anyone who could manage the old script, because it turned out that half the valley had been taught to read by grandmothers who never explained why literacy mattered so much to them, and now everyone understood.

Every debt. Every sale. Every land grant, every luck-tithe, every year taken off a life, every soul signed away in proxy or fraud or plain extortion. Read aloud, witnessed, entered into copies that traveled out along the refugee roads in saddlebags and peddlers' carts, so that no village, no family, no orphaned descendant anywhere would have to learn from rumor what had been done to them.

"Some of these can't be undone," Vess said on the twelfth day, standing before the assembled crowd in the hall that had once been a courtroom and was becoming something else. Her voice was flat and careful. "Some of the sold are ash. Some of the lands are salted. Some of the stolen years are simply gone, and no ledger entry returns them." She paused. "I am not asking forgiveness. I am asking what you want done with what remains."

It took three more days to answer her. But the answer, when it came, was unanimous and simple: everything that can go back, goes back. Everything that can't gets named, mourned, and marked. And the ledgers stay public. Forever. No contract written in the dark again, not here, not by anyone, ever.

Vess bowed her head — the first time in four centuries she had bowed to anyone above ground — and began the work.

* * *

Restitution was slower than justice had been. Justice, it turned out, had taken only one night: a renunciation, a testimony, a riot of small flames, a hungry Veil refused. Restitution took seasons.

The luck-tithes went home first, because they were easiest — jars and knots and charms of borrowed fortune returned to farmsteads and fishing boats and birthing rooms across three counties, each one tagged with a ledger reference, each delivery accompanied by a reader who spoke the terms aloud so the receiving family knew exactly what had been taken and exactly what was being given back. Old men touched the returned luck of their fathers and wept without knowing why. Fields that had grown thin for generations came back green in a single season, and the farmers who worked them kept looking over their shoulders, unable to trust it.

The land was harder. The court had held parcels going back six hundred years, and boundaries had shifted, and towns had grown over graves, and in some cases the rightful heirs were dust and their claims could only be honored by marking the ground and letting it lie fallow. Vess supervised this personally, walking the old lines with surveyors, and people came out of their houses to stare at the tall grey woman from under the mountain, and some spat, and some thanked her, and most simply watched, because no one yet knew what to do with a jailer who had turned key-witness.

The souls were hardest of all.

Most of the freed flames had gone out — not destroyed, but *spent*, released into the sky along with the storm, feeding the Veil its final meal until the seam closed and the stars relit. Their families mourned them without bodies, without ashes, with nothing but a ledger line and a date. In the valley they raised a marker field on the hillside where the third ward-stone had stood: uncut granite, one stone per name, and the names read aloud every new moon by whoever wished to come and listen.

A few flames remained — those whose contracts had been broken rather than consumed. These were kept, temporarily, in the open glass lanterns of the old court, tended by volunteers, visited by kin. No one pretended this was reunion. A flame is not a person; it is the residue of a person, the way a footprint is not a walker. But families sat beside the lanterns and talked to them anyway, told them the news, introduced grandchildren. And sometimes — this was reported often enough that no one doubted it — the flames leaned toward the voices, the way flowers lean toward light.

Marisol came to see one, only once. Ana's flame burned steady and pale gold, and Marisol sat with it for an hour and told her sister about the door she'd held, about the riot of voices, about the mountain shaking with thousands of testimonies roaring at once. Then she stood, and kissed two fingers and touched the glass, and walked out of the mountain and did not come back inside again.

"I'll see her properly someday," she told Jude on the road outside. "Or I won't, and I'll have deserved that too. Either way, the work's not done. Half those ledgers point to debts owed by courts *other* than this one. You know that, don't you?"

Jude did know. The mountain court had been one account in a vast book. Somewhere, other stewards were watching their own ledgers burn and wondering if their turn was coming.

"Let them wonder," Marisol said, and shouldered her pack, and headed east toward the next name on her sister's list.

* * *

Winter came early that year, and gentle, and the valley spent it doing the slow arithmetic of repair.

The elders resigned — all of them, publicly, at the winter assembly, standing before the community they had governed in enforced ignorance for thirty years and confessing what they had known and what they had chosen not to ask. Wren Harrow spoke longest, and hardest, against herself. Some in the crowd wanted them punished. Most, exhausted, wanted only for it never to happen again, and the compromise was this: the elders would serve out their lives on the restitution crews, hauling stones and copying ledgers, and no one born after the wards fell would ever hold unaccountable office in the valley again.

New laws were drafted in the open air, in the market square, read aloud sentence by sentence to anyone who came, amended by shouting, argued over by children. Jude attended every session and said little and listened much, and it was Jude who insisted on the clause that everyone thought strange at first and later considered the heart of the whole charter: *No person shall be recorded in any register without their own spoken consent, witnessed aloud.*

"Names are not property," Jude told the assembly, in a voice that carried. "The moment a name belongs to a ledger instead of a person, the ledger owns the person. We write nothing down that hasn't been freely given."

Dominic attended too. He sat near the front every session, wrapped in a good coat someone had made him, listening with his head slightly tilted, and every morning Jude told him his name, and every evening Jude told him again, and the groove in him grew — slowly, unevenly, like frost forming on glass. He began to remember things. Not his history, not exactly. Textures. The smell of the river in flood. The weight of a knife handle. The specific quality of silence inside a falling ward. Once, hearing thunder, he laughed out loud for no reason he could explain, and Jude, sitting beside him, laughed too, and neither of them tried to explain it.

Once — only once — he dreamed. He described it to Jude at breakfast, puzzled: a great hall of glass lanterns, and himself standing in the middle of it, and every flame turning toward him as he passed, and a voice like wind through a broken window saying, *You gave it all away. Do you regret it?* And himself answering — he was sure of the answer even now, though the dream-words slipped away — *Take the name. Keep the rest.*

Jude wrote it down afterward, in the growing archive, in a hand that shook slightly. Some things needed witnesses. Even dreams. *Especially* dreams.

* * *

On the first anniversary of the night the wards fell, the valley held no festival. Instead, at dusk, the whole population climbed the hill to the marker field, and stood among the uncut granite, and waited.

Nobody had planned what happened next. It was simply that when the sun went down and the sky came out, the stars were there — all of them, every one, the whole scattered inheritance of the night — and an old woman near the front began, quietly, to sing the naming-song that valley mothers had once sung to children before anyone decided the sky was dangerous, and one by one the crowd picked it up, hundreds of voices in the blue dusk, naming the stars the way the ledgers had been named: aloud, freely, witnessed.

Dominic stood in the middle of it, holding Jude's hand, looking up.

"I don't know why," he said, "but I feel like I've seen these before."

"You have," Jude said. "Everyone has."

"No." He frowned, working at something. "I mean — I think I *put* some of them there. Is that mad?"

Jude looked at him — at the storm-grey eyes full of starlight and confusion and something underneath both that was unmistakably, stubbornly himself — and felt the truth move in their chest like a swallowed coal.

"No," Jude said. "I don't think it's mad at all."

Above them the stars burned on, returned, unowned. Below them the valley lights kindled one by one, windows and lamps and cookfires, and beyond the boundary stones the refugee roads glimmered with travelers' fires all the way to the horizon, and somewhere east of everything Marisol walked with her pack and her list, and somewhere beneath the mountain Lady Vess trimmed the lanterns of the remaining flames and copied ledgers by lamplight, and the world — poorer, sadder, freer — went on.

Jude squeezed Dominic's hand and thought: *a year ago you were a name in someone's ledger, and so was I, and now we are only names in each other's mouths, which is the only registry that ever mattered.*

Tomorrow the archive would need tending. Tomorrow a cart would arrive with contracts from the eastern courts, and readers would be wanted, and teachers. Tomorrow the work continued.

Tonight there were stars, and a name said aloud into the dark:

"Dominic."

And the man smiled, like the word fit, like it had always fit, like somewhere deep beneath the erasure a door had been left unlocked on purpose — and behind his eyes, for just a moment, lightning flickered.
