## 22. Jude and the Veil

The archive smells of cedar and rain.

That's not what the elders predicted, back when they argued about whether the valley should keep records at all. Records, they said, were how the court had found them. Paper was a wound you kept opening. Better to burn the ledgers, salt the ash, and let the mountain forget their names.

But Marisol had stood in the doorway of the old tithe-house with her arms crossed and her sister's name inked on the inside of her wrist — one of the first things any of the freed souls had managed to write down about themselves — and said, *You don't heal by forgetting. You heal by writing it down in your own hand this time.*

So the tithe-house became the archive. The shelves where grain had once been counted against the valley's debts now held contracts: thousands of them, pried out of the mountain's vaults after the court emptied, each one tagged and copied and read aloud at least once so that the words could never again be spoken only in the dark. Freed souls came on market days to find their own names. Some wept over theirs. One old man laughed until he had to sit down, because his contract listed his soul's value as *two pigs and a good winter*, and he had always suspected he'd been undervalued.

Jude keeps the archive. Nobody voted on it; it simply happened, the way Jude had simply happened, all those years ago, appearing in the valley without parentage or papers. There was work no one else wanted — reading the fine print, learning the court's cramped ledger-hand, teaching farmers and shepherds and refugee children how a clause can be shaped like a kindness and close like a trap — and Jude did it. It turned out that a person who had spent a lifetime feeling like a placeholder in someone else's story made an excellent reader of other people's fine print. Jude knew what it was to be written by another hand.

The morning light comes through the high windows in long dusty bars. Outside, Thornbury Valley is loud in the way it has learned to be loud again: hammers, arguments, someone's terrible singing, the river running full with snowmelt off the mountain. A year since the stars went out. Eight months since they came back.

Jude is explaining compound interest to a fourteen-year-old named Wren when the bell over the door rings.

Wren is a refugee from the eastern roads, sharp as a thorn, and she has decided that being cheated is worse than being hungry. She leans over the table where Jude has spread a copy of a wool-tithe contract from three generations back. "So if they took a tenth every year," she says, jabbing her finger at the fourth clause, "and the tenth was supposed to be of profit, not of everything — then they owe somebody a lot of sheep."

"They do," Jude says. "And the descendants of whoever signed get to decide what fair repayment looks like. Not the court. That's the whole point."

"That's a weird kind of justice."

"It's the kind you have to do yourself." Jude slides the copy toward her. "Read the exception clause. Bottom left. Tell me why it doesn't apply."

Wren scowls at it, lips moving. Jude watches her, and feels the thing that still surprises them most days: usefulness. Not the useful-in-spite-of-themselves usefulness of a proxy, a stand-in, a soul spent to cover someone else's debt. Just useful. A person whose presence makes something lighter rather than cheaper.

The bell rings again. Someone has come in out of the rain.

Jude looks up expecting a farmer with a question about land boundaries, or one of the freed souls who comes every week to touch the shelf where his contract lives, the way some people touch the graves of people they hated.

Instead it's a stranger.

He stands just inside the door, dripping, and Jude's first thought is that he must have walked a long way, because his boots are ruined and there is road-grime in the creases of his knuckles. His second thought arrives slower and stranger: there is something familiar about him the way a half-remembered melody is familiar. Not his face. Something under the face. The way he holds still while he takes in a room, cataloguing exits, counting people — no, that isn't right either. The way he holds still like he's listening for weather.

"Can I help you?" Jude asks.

"I hope so." His voice is low and roughened, like he hasn't used it much lately. "I'm looking for — I don't actually know what I'm looking for." He glances around at the shelves of contracts with an expression Jude can't read. Recognition, maybe. Or its ghost. "I was told this place exists. I've been walking since before I knew where I was walking to."

"You walked here?"

"Everywhere felt wrong except this direction."

Wren is staring at him openly. Jude touches her shoulder. "Take the afternoon," they say quietly. "Practice on the exception clause. We'll argue about it tomorrow."

She goes, but slowly, craning backward through the window until the mist swallows her.

* * *

The stranger sits where Wren sat, and Jude brings him tea because that's what you do, even now, especially now, when everyone in the valley has learned that hospitality can be weaponized. There are rules now, posted by the door in Marisol's blunt handwriting: *No bargain made in this building is binding. Nothing served here is payment. You may leave whenever you want.* It seems absurd to need such rules. It would be more absurd not to have them.

He wraps his hands around the cup like he's cold all the way through.

"Do you know your name?" Jude asks. They've learned to ask it plainly. Half the freed souls couldn't answer, at first. Names are the last thing the ledger lets go.

"No." He says it without flinching, which means he's had time to make peace with it, or to stop trying. "There's a blank where it should be. I remember everything else, or — almost everything else. I remember how to read. I remember that storms feel like home. I remember a valley behind wards, and a festival that never happened, and—" He stops. Looks up. His eyes are grey, storm-grey, the grey of sky deciding what it wants to be. "I don't know why I remember that. Everyone else remembers the Founding Festival. It happened. I checked. But my memory of it is standing on a wall watching the stars go out, and nobody else stood there."

Jude's hands have gone very still around the teapot.

"The archivists in the east told me," the stranger continues, "that if a person loses their name, sometimes the name can be given back. Not restored — restored implies it was taken wrongly, and mine wasn't, apparently, or it was taken rightly, which amounts to the same thing. Given. Voluntarily. By someone who knew it." He laughs, short and uncomfortable. "They said I should come here. They wouldn't say why. They just looked at me like I was carrying something heavy and said, go to Thornbury, go to the archive, ask for—"

He falters. Of course he does. He doesn't know what to ask for. He has been walking toward a word he cannot say.

Jude sets down the teapot very carefully, because if they don't give their hands a task they will shake.

A year ago — thirteen months, two weeks, four days; Jude stopped pretending they weren't counting shortly after the stars relit — Dominic Thornbury gave away everything. Not to the court. Not to the Veil. To the sky, to everyone, equally, the way rain falls and lightning strikes without checking ledgers. And the contract, having nothing left to bind, unraveled, and the unraveling demanded balance, and the ledger took his name.

Jude had held the last trace of him — a scrap of his coat, a splinter of ward-stone warm in their palm — and spoken *Dominic* aloud into the Veil, and refused to let it be collected. Everyone said it was brave. Nobody said what it actually was, which was insufficient. The Veil took the name anyway. The valley forgot him within a day. His grandmother's grave lost its inscription overnight; the letters didn't vanish, they simply became meaningless, marks without referent, a headstone that said nothing at all. His house stood empty and no one could recall who had lived in it. Even the storm-magic seemed not to remember him; the summer thunderstorms passed over his roof without comment.

Only Jude remembered, because Jude had refused. Refusal is testimony, and testimony is the one currency the Veil honors above all others. So Jude carried the name alone for thirteen months, saying it into the dark each night like tending a small flame in glass — and oh, they understand now why the vaults were lit that way, why Lady Vess had watched over those pale fires with such terrible tenderness. Keeping a soul is keeping watch. It is showing up every night to witness something that cannot witness itself.

But a name kept by one person, in secret, in grief — that is not the same as a name given back.

"Ask for me," Jude says. "You were told to ask for me."

The stranger looks at them. Rain runs off his hair. Somewhere outside, far off over the mountain, thunder rolls — the first of the season, early, unseasonal, and both of them glance toward the window at the sound, and something passes across his face like weather.

"Who are you?" he asks.

"My name is Jude." It is the truest sentence Jude owns. They earned it in a vault of pale flames, holding their own tampered soul-flame and understanding at last that identity is evidence, that a self is something you can prove. "I was sold to the court beneath the mountain before I could walk, and I got myself back, and now I keep the record of everyone else who did or didn't. I knew someone who gave up everything so the rest of us could have ours. Including you, probably. Including your name, if you're missing one."

"I don't understand."

"You will." Jude stands. Their legs feel strange, too light, like the moment before stepping over a boundary line. "Or maybe you won't, and it won't matter. Because that's the thing none of the old law ever understood. Names aren't owned. They never were. The court thought a name was property, something you could hold in a ledger, levy against, collect. But a name is—" They stop, laugh a little, surprised by tears that arrive without permission. "A name is just what happens when someone witnesses you and chooses to speak. That's all it ever was. That's why giving it back works."

The stranger has gone very still. Listening-for-weather still.

"I don't remember you," he says softly. "Should I?"

"Maybe. Maybe not. Memory's a ledger too, in its way." Jude comes around the table and stands in front of him, close enough to see the road dust in the lines of his face, the exhaustion of a person who has walked a very long way toward a word. "Thirteen months ago I made a promise I haven't been able to keep. Not because I failed. Because I was the only one speaking, and a name spoken by one voice in the dark is just grief with a shape. It needs two. It needs the whole valley, maybe. But it starts with two."

"Tell me what to do," he says. No hesitation. As if some part of him has been waiting all this time for instructions, for fine print, for a contract — and Jude understands with a lurch that the kindest thing they can do is offer him the opposite: no terms, no clauses, no debt. Just a door left open.

"Nothing," Jude says. "You do nothing. I'm going to say a name, and you're going to let it land wherever it lands. If it fits, it fits. If it doesn't, we'll find you a better one, and it'll be yours because you chose it, not because anyone sold or signed or sacrificed anything. Either way you stay for supper. Those are the only conditions, and they aren't conditions, they're just supper."

His mouth twitches. Almost a smile. Waiting.

Outside, the thunder circles closer, curious, like a dog that has forgotten its home but not its people.

Jude takes a breath. Thirteen months of breath all drawn at once.

"Dominic," they say.

* * *

It is not dramatic. That's the thing Jude will try to explain later, to Wren, to Marisol, to anyone who asks — and people will ask, because the story gets out, the way true things do, leaking through the valley like water finding level ground.

It is not dramatic. The name simply lands on him like rain on ground that has been waiting for it. His eyes close. His shoulders drop an inch, tension leaving muscles that have been braced for so long he'd forgotten they were braced. And then he smiles — slow, crooked, astonished — the smile of a man trying on a coat and finding it fits better than he remembered, finding it fits like it was cut for him, finding that he is the one who grew into it.

"Dominic," he repeats. Testing the weight. "Dominic." He opens his eyes, and they are storm-grey and wet and looking at Jude like Jude is the first solid thing he's touched in a year of walking. "Yes," he says. "Oh — yes. That's — it's not a memory. It's more like remembering forward. Like the word was waiting ahead of me instead of behind."

"That's how it works," Jude manages. "I think. I'm inventing this as I go."

"Good." Dominic — Dominic — laughs, and the sound cracks open something in the room, some pressure that has lived in the archive's cedar-and-rain air for thirteen months. "Someone should. All the old law was invented by people who wanted to own things. Someone ought to invent the other kind."

Thunder cracks directly overhead — close, joyful, unmistakable — and the lamps flicker, and neither of them moves toward the window because they are both looking at each other instead, and there will be time for windows later. There will be time for everything later. That is what a year of grief has taught Jude: not patience exactly, but the certainty that some things survive their own erasure, that a name spoken twice by two voices is a contract the universe itself must honor, that love entered into freely — witnessed, chosen, renewed daily like a signature — outranks every ledger ever kept beneath any mountain.

Later, much later, when the storm has moved off eastward trailing relief across the dry fields, they will walk out together under the returning stars and Jude will point out the constellations the valley is relearning, the ones with no contracts attached, the ones that belong to everyone equally because Dominic gave the sky back. And Dominic will tilt his head back and listen to the distant grumble of his old inheritance, distributed now among ten thousand clouds and every living thing beneath them, and he will say, "I don't miss it," and mean it, and Jude will believe him.

But first — supper. First the small ceremonies: bread, and tea refilled, and Wren bursting back through the door soaked and breathless because she saw the lightning and knew, somehow knew, and standing in the archive dripping and demanding to know who the stranger is and why the whole valley's dogs started howling happy all at once. First the slow, careful work of introduction — not restoration, introduction, the difference mattering more than anything — Dominic meeting Marisol, who grips his hand and says only, "She'd have liked you," meaning her sister, meaning the vaults, meaning everything; Dominic standing before his grandmother's blank-headed grave and reading aloud the name Jude wrote there months ago in pencil, faint, awaiting this exact hour to be gone over in ink.

First the valley learning his name again, one voice at a time, the way dawn learns a landscape.

And late that night, when the archive is dark and the two of them sit in its doorway sharing a blanket against the autumn chill, Dominic turns and says, "You said my name into the Veil. Before tonight. You said it every night, didn't you. I could almost hear it, on the road. Like weather coming."

"Every night," Jude says. "For thirteen months."

"Why?"

And Jude, who has spent a lifetime being a placeholder, a proxy, a fine-print clause in other people's bargains, and who has finally become instead a keeper of records, a reader of truths, a witness — Jude answers the only way that has ever been true:

"Because you're worth witnessing. All the rest is commentary."

Overhead, unhurried and certain, the stars burn on — no longer caged, no longer counted, belonging to no one and therefore to everyone — and below them the valley sleeps, its ledgers empty, its debts forgiven, its people free to write the next page in their own hands.

Whatever comes next, they will write it together.
