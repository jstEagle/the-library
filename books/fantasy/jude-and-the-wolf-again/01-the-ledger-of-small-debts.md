## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The counting house smelled of tallow and old paper and, beneath that, the sweetish rot of bone waiting to be worked. Cyrus Ishikawa had long ago stopped noticing it as unpleasant. It was simply the smell of the trade: the Guild of Impossible Things bought and sold what could not be weighed, and everything unweighable had a smell—grief like wet wool, luck like struck flint, memory like dust burning on a lamp.

They sat at their bench in the corner of the great room, where the lamplight came slantwise through leaded glass and fell in grey diamonds across the wood. Around them, the guild's clerks scratched at their desks with pens whose nibs were cut from raven quills, because raven quills held ink that remembered what it had written. That was the point. Everything in this building remembered. The walls were lined with ledgers bound in hide whose animals had died knowing they would become books; the floors creaked in the rhythms of footsteps that had crossed them decades ago; even the air seemed to have entries filed against it.

Cyrus worked bone because bone was honest. It held a shape once given to it, but it did not gossip about the hands that gave it.

The piece before them was small—a whistle, no longer than a thumb, carved from the shinbone of a sheep that had lived and died unremarked in the hills above Vessany before there was no longer a Vessany to live above. The client, a cooper named Aldous Wrenn, had paid the guild three months' wages for it. His mother had been dead eleven years, and he wanted, for one evening, to hear her whistle again—the tune she'd whistled while kneading bread, high and wandering, always losing its way home near the end.

Impossible things. This one was almost modest.

Cyrus turned the whistle under the lamp and cut another line into the mouthpiece with a burin worn smooth as a river stone. The trick of such work was not in the carving. Anyone could carve. The trick was in the listening. Every dead person left residue in the things they had touched often enough, the way candle smoke leaves residue on glass, and if you held the right object against your ear at the right angle—in this case, a wooden rolling pin Mr. Wrenn had lent the guild from his mother's kitchen—you could hear the shape of her still caught in its grain. Then you carved the vessel to fit her, the way a key is fitted to a lock. The whistle would not raise her ghost. Ghosts were a different department, on the third floor, with different fees. But for the length of one song, the whistle would remember her breath, and her breath would remember the tune.

Cyrus lifted the half-finished whistle to their ear out of habit more than need.

Nothing yet. Just the room's own murmurs: the ledger-room below breathing its slow paper breath, a clerk two desks away humming through his nose, and—always, everywhere, faint as frost forming—the dead. They clung to the guild like barnacles to a hull. People came here to trade with the impossible, and the dead were the most impossible thing of all: present, insistent, and utterly without funds.

One of them was closer than usual tonight.

Cyrus set down the burin and flexed their fingers. They were tired. They had been tired for years, which was different from being weary—tiredness was a craft, too, something you maintained the way you maintained a blade.

On the desk beside the lamp lay the master ledger, open to the page assigned to this quarter's intake. It was a vast book, chained at its spine to a brass ring bolted through the desk, because ledgers that remember must not be allowed to wander. Its pages were vellum scraped thin enough to read the ghosts of older entries beneath, which was fitting; the guild believed nothing should ever be truly erased, only transferred. Debt, grief, names—all of it moved from column to column, account to account, forever.

Cyrus's eyes went to the third entry from the top.

*Ishikawa, C. — carver, third bench — one (1) erasure performed, self — fee waived.*

Their own name. Their own hand. Written by themselves, at the end of last month, in the ordinary course of things: every guild member recorded each service rendered, and among the services Cyrus rendered, regularly, quietly, was the service of removing Cyrus Ishikawa from records.

It was delicate work, self-erasure. Not the crude scraping that apprentices used on spoiled pages—that left ghosts, gouges, accusations. No. What Cyrus did was finer. They had learned, over nine years, to persuade vellum to forget. A needle of fishbone, a breath held exactly so, a whisper in a language the annexing empire had tried to burn out of the world and nearly had: *forget me*, said correctly, in the correct grammar of forgetting, and the ink lifted from the page like a stain retreating up through cloth. The ledger kept the debt—it always kept the debt—but the name attached to it grew pale, then translucent, then gone, until the entry read only *carver, third bench*, anonymous as rain.

Nine years of this. Every roster, every register, every tax roll and guild manifest and parish book that had ever contained the name Jude Ishikawa—or Jude anything, the childhood name gone first, scraped so thoroughly even Cyrus sometimes struggled to recall the feel of it in the mouth. They had started with the easy records and worked inward, year by year, toward the ones that mattered, the way you might dig yourself free of a collapsed tunnel: carefully, patiently, never certain how much weight the earth above you carried.

Why? Even they could not have given a single clean answer. Fear, partly. The annexation had taught everyone in the conquered territories what happened to people who could be found: found meant counted, counted meant owed, owed meant owned. And partly something older and stranger—a feeling, persistent as a splinter, that the true name of a person was a door, and doors could be knocked upon, and Cyrus had spent their whole life wanting to be a wall instead.

So: invisibility, practiced daily, like scales on an instrument.

Tonight's entry was nearly forgotten already. The ink sat pale on the page, wavering when the lamplight shifted, as though unsure it deserved to exist. Cyrus glanced toward the provost's office—its door shut, lamplight behind the frosted glass, the provost herself audibly doing whatever provosts did at this hour, which was presumably weighing something—and took up the fishbone needle.

*Forget me,* they breathed, in the grammar of forgetting.

The ink shivered. Drew back. Thinned to a watermark, then to a suspicion, then to nothing at all, leaving the entry blank but for the words *carver, third bench*, which described a person who had never existed anywhere else in the world. Somewhere in the deep stacks below, Cyrus knew, the debt itself remained, orphaned now, ownerless, accruing interest in the dark. The guild believed nothing vanished. Fine. Let the debt sit there with no name on it. A debt with no name was a coin with no face: spendable by anyone, claimable by no one.

Cyrus exhaled and felt the small familiar ache settle into place behind the sternum. People assumed erasure was painless. It was not. Each time, it was like closing a door on a room where someone—yourself, some earlier and louder version—was still talking. You heard the voice stop mid-sentence. You walked away. The silence afterward was the payment.

*Carver, third bench.* That was what remained of them, officially. An occupation and a piece of furniture.

The whistle waited. Cyrus returned to it, grateful for the work, because the work required listening and listening left no room for the ache.

They held the rolling pin to their ear again, angling it the way old Vessanyan carvers had angled it, cheek to wood, eyes closed. And there she was—not a voice, exactly, not yet, but the *shape* of a voice, a warmth in the grain like a handprint on a winter window. A woman who had whistled while she worked. A woman who had lost the tune near the end, every time, and laughed at herself for it, every time.

Cyrus began to carve the bore wider at just the place where a breath would falter. The knife moved in small strokes. Bone dust gathered pale as flour along their wrist. Somewhere in the middle distance of hearing, the woman's laugh assembled itself out of the wood's memory, and Cyrus smiled despite everything, because some impossible things were kind.

That was when the whisper came.

Not from the rolling pin. Not from the whistle. From the room itself—from the direction of the loading door at the far end of the counting house, the heavy iron-bound door through which goods came in and payments went out, currently barred for the night.

The dead spoke to Cyrus the way weather speaks: pressure first, then wind, then word. This was pressure, sudden and wrong, like the drop before a storm.

*Someone is coming.*

Cyrus's knife stilled.

The dead who haunted the guild were mostly clients' leftovers—grandmothers waiting in teacups, soldiers lingering in medals, the mild persistent dead of objects well loved. They rarely spoke in sentences. Sentences cost them, the way speech costs the drowning. When a dead person spent words, the words mattered.

*Someone is coming,* said the voice again, closer now, or perhaps just clearer—a man's voice, young, ragged at the edges, breathless in the way that was not metaphor. He was running, wherever he was. He had been running a long time. *The door. Tell them—the heir—the door opens—*

And then, faintly, absurdly, horribly: the sound of bells. Many bells, far off, ringing in a pattern Cyrus did not know, except that every instinct they owned stood up at once and said *equinox*.

"Cyrus." One of the clerks, Master Fenwick, had paused at their bench, a folio under his arm, his raven-quill pen tucked behind his ear like a carpenter's pencil. "You're wanted upstairs tomorrow, early. The provost has buyers coming." He said it mildly, the way men announce weather. "Big ones. Empire folk."

"Empire folk buy many things," Cyrus said, keeping their voice level, keeping their eyes on the whistle.

"Not usually at equinox." Fenwick shrugged and moved off, his footsteps joining the floor's old chorus.

Cyrus looked down at their hands. Dust to the wrists. The whistle half-alive in their grip. On the ledger beside them, the entry that had held their name an hour ago sat blank and innocent, and for the first time in nine years of careful, patient, meticulous invisibility, the blankness did not feel like safety.

It felt like a page someone had cleared on purpose. Prepared. Waiting for something to be written there.

*Someone is coming,* whispered the dead man at the loading door, and this time, underneath the words, Cyrus heard what he had been trying to say all along, the sentence he had spent his final strength carrying across a country that no longer existed:

*Tell the heir the heir must sign.*
