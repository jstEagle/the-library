## 2. A Stranger at the Loading Door

The rain came down the loading stair like it had somewhere to be, and Cyrus was alone with the tallow lamp and a crate of unsold sorrows when they heard footsteps that did not belong to anyone living.

That was the trouble with hearing the dead: one learned to sort them. The guild's cellar-ghosts shuffled in soft slippers, murmuring inventory numbers from sixty years back. The river dead came up through the drains as a wet whispering, all of them counting coins that had slipped their fingers. But these footsteps were *heavy*, and they stumbled, and they dragged on the stone like a body being carried by its own stubbornness.

Cyrus set down the bone whistle—half-finished, the dead mother's voice still coiled inside it waiting for her daughter's breath—and lifted the lamp.

At the bottom of the loading stair, where the canal barges put in and the guild took delivery of things that had no business existing, a man stood swaying. He was soaked past any help the rain could do him; the water on him was sweat. His coat had once been courier's green. Now it was mostly mud, and the mud was mostly blood.

"Locked," he said, and then, seeing Cyrus, seeing the lamp, he smiled the way drowning men smile at lights. "No. Good. You're— you're the carver."

"I'm nobody," Cyrus said automatically. It was not quite a lie. It was the closest thing they had to a creed.

"No." He came down the last steps wrong, knee buckling, catching himself against the wall with a sound like tearing paper. "You're the one who signs the small debts. I asked in three towns. They said come to Vessany Street, find the one whose name isn't anywhere, ask for the carver." He laughed, wet and awful. "'Whose name isn't anywhere.' As if that were a thing a person could be."

*As if,* thought Cyrus, and felt the blank space where their entries used to be ache like an old break in cold weather.

"You shouldn't be here," they said. "Whatever you're carrying, the guild will weigh it, price it, and own it before morning. Put it down and walk away."

"Walk." He looked at his legs as though they had betrayed him personally. "Yes. I meant to."

He made it three more steps. Then the fourth step simply wasn't there for him, and he went down onto the loading dock stones with a crash that Cyrus felt in their teeth, and the folded thing he'd been clutching under his arm skidded across the flags toward the water.

Cyrus caught it without deciding to. That was how they would remember it after—the not-deciding. Their hands moved the way hands move to catch a falling child, faster than thought, faster than sense, and then the map was in their palms and the man was face-down on the stones and the lamp flame bent in a wind that came from nowhere at all.

*Careful,* whispered the dead, all around, everywhere, the whole drowned city of them pressing close to the walls. *Careful, careful, careful.*

It was the most any of them had said in months, and none of it was helpful.

* * *

He was still alive. That surprised Cyrus more than anything else that followed. Up close he looked like a man who had died twice already and argued his way out of it both times. There was frost in his eyebrows, though the night was warm. There was a smell about him of high mountain passes and burnt paper.

"Name," Cyrus said, kneeling, because the guild required names for everything, even the dying. Especially the dying. A death entered in the ledger was worth more than a death unentered; the provost said so every quarter-day, tapping her abacus like a woman scolding a slow child.

"Hask," he said. "Emmerich Hask. Courier, third rank, Vessany postal service, which—" another of those terrible laughs, "—which is a fine joke, because there is no Vessany postal service. Hasn't been for thirty years. I've been delivering for a country that doesn't exist, do you understand? Thirty years. My father served it. My daughter was going to serve it." His hand shot out and closed on Cyrus's wrist with the strength of a man holding a rope over a drop. "There is no Vessany. Say it. Say what they did."

"They struck it from the maps," Cyrus said quietly. It was a fact every child learned along with their letters: *Vessany was annexed; Vessany no longer exists.* The empire had been very thorough. Not conquered—*unmade*. Borders redrawn, records burned or absorbed, the name itself declared a clerical error. You could stand in a Vessany town square and read the imperial plaque explaining that this place had always been something else.

"Not just the maps." Hask pulled Cyrus closer. His breath was terrible, sweetish, the breath of organs shutting down. "The maps are only the *outside* of a country. Inside—" he tapped Cyrus's breastbone with two fingers, tap, tap, tap, like a knock at a door, "—inside, a country is a ledger. Names. Debts. Who owed whom a loaf of bread in the famine year. Who married whom. Whose grandmother bound a wolf." His eyes went strange and fixed. "You'd know about grandmothers and wolves."

Cyrus went cold from the boots up. "I don't know what you mean."

"Neither do I," Hask admitted, and grinned. "Someone told me to say it. Someone in the snow. They're chatty, the dead of my country. Thirty years with no registry to sleep in—they crowd around anyone who'll listen." He fumbled at his coat, at the inner pocket, and Cyrus helped him because there was nothing else to do, and drew out the folded thing's twin: a second packet, oilcloth-wrapped, tied with wire. "For the provost. Guild business. She'll know. Tell her—" he coughed, and the cough went on too long, and when it ended something in him had visibly emptied, "—tell her the buyers moved the date up. Tell her she doesn't have until equinox to be decent. Tell her Hask ran every mile and she can enter *that* in her books."

"Who are the buyers?" Cyrus asked. "Buyers of what?"

But Hask wasn't listening anymore. He had turned his head toward the map in Cyrus's other hand—or rather toward the space above it—and his face changed, gentled, the way faces change when someone beloved enters a room.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, there you are. I made it, then. I made it after all."

And then, because the dead are honest in ways the living cannot afford, he said, clearly, in the flat tone of a man reading from a document: "*The door out of Vessany opens at equinox. The heir must sign.*"

Then he said, in his own voice, much smaller: "Tell my daughter I finished the route."

Then he said nothing at all.

* * *

Afterward, Cyrus could never say how long they knelt there on the loading dock with a dead courier's blood soaking into their apron and a map in their hands that seemed to grow heavier by the heartbeat. Long enough for the lamp to gutter twice. Long enough for the rain to fill the grooves between the flagstones and run pink toward the canal.

They did not open the map. Some instinct older than sense told them not to—not here, not in the guild's own cellar, where every surface had ears and the ears reported to the provost's abacus. They wrapped it instead in the oilcloth from Hask's second packet, which was, they saw with a lurch, addressed in the guild's own cipher-stitch: *To the Provost of the Guild of Impossible Things, re: the Vessany asset. Deliver by hand. Burn after reading.*

An asset. Cyrus turned the word over and found it did not fit anywhere in their understanding of themselves. They had spent eleven years sanding themselves out of existence—every apprentice record corrected, every debt entry dissolved into arithmetic errors, every mention of *Jude Ishikawa* erased with the same bone tools they used to carve voices into whistles. They had done it well. They had done it so well that some mornings they woke and had to lie still for a while, feeling for themselves the way you feel for a missing tooth.

And now a dying stranger called them an asset, and spoke of heirs, and knocked on their breastbone talking about grandmothers and wolves.

Above, faintly, came the sound of the counting house door. Then heels on stone—measured, unhurried, the heels of a woman who had never once in her life arrived anywhere too early.

*Too late,* whispered the dead, and for once they sounded almost sorry. *She's always exactly on time for the wrong things.*

Provost Aldery Vane descended the loading stair the way she did everything: precisely, expensively, with a lantern-bearer behind her and an enforcer behind him—a big gray shape of a man whose eyes never settled on anything, because settling was a form of commitment. Her gaze went from the body, to the blood, to Cyrus's face, and Cyrus watched her perform the arithmetic in real time: one courier, dead; one message, presumably delivered; one asset, present and accounted for.

"Ah," she said softly. "So he reached us after all. Remarkable. Third rank, was he? They always overestimate themselves at third rank." She crouched beside Hask with a care that would have looked like grief to anyone who didn't know her, touched two fingers to his throat, held them there a moment longer than necessary. "Dead. What a waste of a perfectly good investment." She rose. "Did he give you anything, carver?"

"He gave me nothing," Cyrus said. "He fell. I caught him. That's all."

It was the first outright lie Cyrus had ever told inside the guild's walls, and they felt it land somewhere, felt the ledger *notice*, felt a small new entry write itself in ink they could not see. Provost Vane smiled. It was her account-settling smile, the one that preceded either a payment or a repossession.

"Of course he gave you nothing," she agreed pleasantly. "You're holding it behind your back. You have been since I came down the stairs. You're better at invisibility than you know, Jude—which is fortunate, because in a few days you're going to need to be extremely visible."

Her lantern-bearer raised the light. And in it Cyrus saw—for half a heartbeat, no more, before their fingers crushed the fold shut—the corner of the map, and on that corner, drawn in faded royal violet, a door.

Not a drawing of a door. A door, rendered the way the old cartographers rendered living things: with grain, with breath, with a hairline crack down its center like a seam waiting to split.

And beneath it, in a hand Cyrus recognized with a sick, swimming jolt—their grandmother's hand, the hand that had taught them their first knife-strokes—

*Equinox. Bring the child. The heir must sign.*

Behind the provost, in the dark of the loading stair, something breathed.

Something large. Something patient. Something that smelled, impossibly, of woodsmoke and lavender and the inside of a memory Cyrus had buried so deep they'd forgotten the shovel.
