## 3. What the Guild Cannot Price

The dead courier's voice had not stopped speaking since dawn.

It was not a voice, exactly. It was the sound vellum makes when it remembers being skin—a low, papery whisper that lived in the folds of the map now hidden inside Cyrus's coat, against their ribs, warm as a second heartbeat. *The heir must sign,* it said, over and over, patient as water wearing at stone. *The door opens at equinox. The heir must sign.*

Cyrus had learned, in ten years of keeping the guild's ledgers, to work with ghosts murmuring in their ears. The counting house of the Guild of Impossible Things was never quiet. Every shelf held its own small chorus: bones that hummed with the names carved into them, jars of bottled last-breaths that shifted like sleeping birds, the great master ledger itself, which breathed—slowly, wetly—when no one else was listening.

So Cyrus worked, and pretended not to hear, and did not look at the loading door where a man had died two days ago with his blood soaking into the threshold stones and his hand pressed flat against theirs, folding something into their grip like a mother tucking a blanket around a child.

The morning's task was a whistle. A client in the Salt Quarter had paid forty crowns for a bone whistle carved from her mother's finger-joint, so that she might call the dead woman back for one conversation—one apology, thirty years overdue—and then let her go properly. Cyrus worked the bone with fine burins, shaping the mouthpiece, hollowing the chamber, and as they carved they listened, because the mother was already in the bone and had opinions about the design.

*Make the holes smaller,* she said. *My daughter always blew too hard. Even as a girl. She'd knock the wind out of a hymn.*

Cyrus made the holes smaller.

This was the part of the trade no one understood from outside: that an impossible thing was not a trick. It was a courtesy. The guild did not sell magic; it sold the completion of unfinished sentences, and someone had to sit quietly with the dead long enough to learn what they wanted said. Cyrus was good at this. Cyrus had been good at it since before the guild found them, since a childhood spent hearing grandmothers in floorboards and soldiers in fence posts, all of them asking after names that had been struck from every record in the world.

They set down the burin and reached, as they did every morning, for the master ledger.

It lay open on its lectern like a sleeping animal, three hundred years of entries in a hundred different hands, and somewhere in its middle pages lived a small cluster of entries under the name **JUDE**—the childhood name, the one that belonged to a country that no longer existed, to a girl-shaped child who had been carried out of Vessany in a fish cart at four years old while the empire's censors burned the maps. Cyrus had spent years erasing those entries. Not tearing them out—tearing left scars, and scars could be read. They erased them the way the guild taught its apprentices to erase anything: with pumice and patience, until the page showed only the faint ghost of a word, a shadow of ink like a bruise under skin.

There was one entry left. *Jude Ishikawa, received into care, age six. Debt: one life, unpriced.* Cyrus had saved it for last because erasing it felt like the final small murder of themselves, and they had wanted to be sure they were finished living as Jude before they killed the name entirely.

They took up the pumice stone.

"You'll want to stop there."

The voice came from the doorway of the provost's office, and it was gentle, and that gentleness went through Cyrus like a blade through wet paper.

Madame Corvane, provost of the Guild of Impossible Things, stood with her hands folded at her waist. She was a small woman, silver-haired, with the kind of face that made strangers tell her their secrets in queues and on ferries. She wore her spectacles on a chain of knotted gray silk, and behind the lenses her eyes were the pale, unblinking gray of a winter harbor. She had taught Cyrus her letters. She had taught them pumice-work. She had once held their hair back while they were sick, and told them, in the soft voice she used for clients, that everyone deserved to begin again.

"Madame," Cyrus said. Their hand was still on the ledger. The pumice stone sat in their palm like a small grey heart.

"You've been very thorough," Corvane said, crossing the room without hurry. Her shoes made no sound on the floorboards; she had them soled in deer-hide, the better to approach nervous assets. "Eleven entries over nine years. I've audited each one. Beautiful work, truly. The *Ishikawa* you substituted for *Vessany-royal-line-of* on the apprenticeship bond—that was inspired. No one looks twice at a carver's name."

Cyrus's mouth had gone dry. "You knew."

"I have always known." She reached the lectern and rested one hand on the ledger's edge, the way one rests a hand on the shoulder of an old friend—or a hostage. "Sit down, dear. This will go easier sitting."

Cyrus did not sit. The map whispered against their ribs. *The heir must sign.* And Corvane's eyes flicked down, just briefly, toward the sound—or toward the place where the sound would be, if sound could be seen—and Cyrus understood, with a lurching horror, that she had heard it too. That she had perhaps been listening to it for two days, from her office, waiting.

"The courier," Cyrus said. "He came to me. He was dying. He chose—"

"He chose correctly." Corvane smiled, and it was the client-smile, the ferry-smile, the smile that made strangers confess. "That is what I paid him to do."

The words did not land all at once. They landed the way cold water lands, one degree at a time, until Cyrus realized they were standing in it up to their neck.

"You sent him."

"I sent him eight months ago, when the empire's envoys first wrote to us. He has been circling Vessany's old borders since spring, gathering proof of lineage, confirming the Door's season." She tilted her head, birdlike. "Did you imagine, all these years, that your little erasures were working? That the guild which trades in impossible things could not accomplish the simple impossibility of watching one clerk?"

Every erased entry rose in Cyrus's memory at once—the pumice dust, the careful substitutions, the nine years of becoming no one—and each one turned, in Corvane's telling, from a wall into a window. They had not been hiding. They had been ripening.

"Why?" Cyrus's voice came out smaller than they wanted. "Why keep me? Why not sell me the day I arrived?"

"Because an heir is worth nothing until the thing inherited is worth wanting." She drew off her spectacles and polished them on her sleeve, unhurried, a woman discussing wool prices. "For thirty years, Vessany has been a legal fiction. Annexed, struck from the maps, its registry sealed in the imperial archive. Worthless. But fictions decay, dear, and empires decay faster. Three years ago the annexation charter came up for renewal, and the imperial court discovered an embarrassment: by their own law, the annexation required either extinction of the royal line or a signed instrument of succession. They have neither. There are clerks in the imperial chancery who cannot sleep for fear some surviving heir will surface and unmake a province with a signature."

She replaced her spectacles and looked at Cyrus fully, and there was something almost tender in it, which was the worst thing yet.

"And here you were. In my house. With the name still in your blood, if not on our pages. Do you know what I felt, the day I worked it out? You were eleven. You were carving a whistle for a drowned boy, and you wept while you worked, and I thought: *there*. That is not grief. That is inheritance. Grief forgets. Inheritance keeps accounts."

Outside, the flooded lower city clanged and dripped. Somewhere a bell counted the hour, and the dead courier's whisper turned over in its sleep against Cyrus's ribs: *the heir must sign.*

"The buyers are already en route," Corvane continued. "An imperial legate and two assessors from the Office of Sealed Registries. They arrive within the fortnight. At equinox, the exchange will be concluded: your signature on an instrument of renunciation, legitimizing the annexation, closing the question of Vessany forever. In return, the guild receives trading rights across the whole eastern province, and you—" her voice softened further, unbearable, "—you receive a comfortable annuity, a new name of your choosing, and the freedom to finally, *finally* stop being hunted by your own history. Isn't that what you've wanted all along? To be no one?"

"To be *left alone*," Cyrus said. "Those aren't the same thing."

"No," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "They rarely are. But they're both priced, and everything priced can be traded." She extended one small hand, palm up, patient as a toll-gate. "The map, please."

And here was the moment Cyrus would think about later, in colder places, in worse hours: the moment they understood that they had already decided. Perhaps they had decided nine years ago, with the first entry erased—not to vanish, but to keep one name safe the way you keep a coal alive under ash. Perhaps the courier's blood on the threshold stones had only shown them what they were carrying.

Their hand went to their coat. Corvane's smile widened by a single degree.

Then the dead spoke—all of them at once.

It came up through the floorboards, through the shelves, through the jars and the bones and the breathing ledger: the drowned boy, the whistle-mother, the soldiers in the fence posts of Cyrus's childhood, and beneath them all a vast low chorus like a tide going out, hundreds of voices, thousands, names with no records left to live in, and every one of them said the same word in the same breathless instant:

***Run.***

The lamps guttered. The ledger shuddered on its lectern like a startled animal. And Cyrus Ishikawa—who had spent nine years learning to move silently through the guild's accounting, who knew which boards sang and which doors stuck and which windows opened onto the drainage channel behind the counting house—was already moving.

Behind them, Madame Corvane did not shout. She simply said, in the voice she used for clients, calm and clear and carrying:

"Open the kennels. Send for the Hound."

* * *

The drainage channel swallowed Cyrus in darkness and the smell of the lower city's drowned streets. Cold water to the knees, brick slick under their palms, and against their ribs the map whispering faster now, urgent, almost frightened: *the heir must sign, the heir must sign, run, run, the door opens at equinox—*

And far above, muffled by stone and water, came a sound that stopped Cyrus's breath.

Not a bell. Not a shout.

A howl—long, low, and terribly familiar, though Cyrus had never heard it before in their life. It sounded, absurdly, impossibly, like grief. Like something that had been kept in a kennel for a very long time, remembering the shape of its own voice.

Somewhere in the dark of the channel, without knowing why, Cyrus began to run toward it instead of away.
