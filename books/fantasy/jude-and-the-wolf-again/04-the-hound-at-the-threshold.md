## 4. The Hound at the Threshold

The bell in the counting house rang wrong.

Cyrus knew every voice the guild's bells had—matins and market, the toll for a debt discharged, the thin double note when an impossible thing changed hands. This was none of them. This was one note, struck once, and the silence after it went on too long, like a held breath, like a room full of accountants all looking up at once from their columns.

*They know,* said the dead courier, his voice living now in the vellum folded against Cyrus's chest. Three days since he'd died at the loading door with his blood going tacky on the cobbles, and still he spoke to Cyrus in dreams and drips and the small hours, patient as water. *The provost sent word down before you even finished locking your desk. They've opened the kennel.*

"The guild doesn't keep dogs," Cyrus whispered. They were crossing the courtyard with a basket of bone shavings they didn't need, wearing the face of a person with an errand, which was the only face Cyrus had ever been any good at wearing.

*The guild keeps one,* the courier said, and something in his tone made Cyrus's hands go cold around the basket handles. *I carried for the empire before I carried for the map. In the border towns they have a name for it. They say: if your debt is great enough, she comes.*

*She.* Cyrus filed the pronoun away and did not let themself think about it, because thinking about it meant standing still in the middle of the courtyard while the light drained out of the morning.

The loading door was behind them. The river gate ahead. The flooded lower city beyond that—a drowned quarter where the annexation had broken the levees and never bothered to mend them, where streets became canals and canals became black glass, and where, crucially, no ledger of the guild could be consulted because the guild had written off the whole district decades ago. Written off. As if a place could be a loss column. As if the people in it—

But that was the guild's grammar, not Cyrus's. Cyrus had spent thirty years learning not to think in it.

They went out through the tannery gate, past the lime pits, and took the stairs down into the wet dark.

* * *

The Hound came awake the way she always did: all at once, and already moving.

There was no slow surface from sleep for her. The binding did not permit it. The collar at her throat—a ring of worked iron and older bone, warm as a fever—opened her eyes before her mind arrived, and so she was running through the guild's long gallery with her claws scoring the marble before she knew what she ran toward or why. The provost stood at the far end with a ledger open across her forearm like a waiter's cloth.

"Retrieve," the provost said. "The item is a map, folded, vellum. The carrier is—" she consulted the page, and something flickered behind her eyes, some small distaste quickly accounted for, "—an employee. Long tenure. Do not damage the employee beyond recovery."

Beyond recovery. The phrase sat in the air between them like a coin on a counter, and the Hound understood its weight exactly: the guild would pay for Cyrus Ishikawa broken, but not destroyed. An asset waiting to appreciate.

She did not answer. She never answered. Her voice had been taken as part of the price of her making, along with her name and her shape and whatever else she had been before the collar, and in forty years of service she had learned that the things taken were also the things protected. A debt-collector with no voice could not plead. Could not bargain. Could not be bargained *with*, which was worse.

She went out through the kennel door into the grey morning, and the city opened under her nose like a book.

Scent first: tallow, lime, river mud, the copper-and-paper smell of the counting house clinging to everything within four streets of it. Then the thread she wanted—bone dust and beeswax and the particular sour note of someone who had not eaten since yesterday, because fear eats first and the fearful eat last. The thread led away from the river gate, down, into the lower city, which was either very clever or very stupid, and the Hound had been hunting long enough to know that the two were often the same animal.

She followed.

* * *

The lower city received Cyrus the way it received everyone: without judgment and with water up to the knees.

It was beautiful, in the way drowned things are beautiful. Streets became slow brown canals; doorways stood open at the waterline, their upper rooms hung with laundry gone grey as fog; somewhere a hand-bell buoy clanked in the current, keeping time for no one. Cyrus waded with the basket held high and the map pressed inside their coat, over the heart, where the courier's voice was strongest.

*Left here,* he said. *No—the other left. Sorry. Being dead has made me directional in ways I'm still adjusting to.*

"You're adjusting fine," Cyrus muttered. A woman leaning out of a third-floor window gave them a look usually reserved for people who talk to birds, and Cyrus smiled apologetically and kept wading.

*There's a ferryman at the old customs house who owes me,* the courier went on. *He doesn't know he owes me. That's the best kind of debt, the kind the debtor hasn't been told about yet. He'll take you to the archive side.*

"And after that?"

Silence. Then, gentler: *After that, carver, we get to the part of the story where I stop being useful, and I'd rather not think about it yet.*

Behind them, above them, back at the top of the tannery stairs, Cyrus felt it before they heard it: a change in the pressure of the morning, the way a room changes when a large animal enters it. The dead felt it too. All through the drowned quarter, the dead lifted their heads—or would have, if they'd had heads—and turned toward the stairs like flowers finding sun.

*Oh,* said the courier, very quietly. *Oh, carver. She's coming.*

"I know."

*You don't understand. I've heard the stories from the border towns. It doesn't matter how fast you run. It matters how much you owe, and everyone in this city owes the guild something. Everyone. If she catches your scent of debt she can follow you through stone.*

"Then I'll owe nothing." Cyrus's laugh came out cracked. "Look at me. I've spent my whole life erasing myself from every book in this building. No debts. No entries. No—"

*That's just it,* the courier said, and there was something awful in his gentleness. *That's exactly what makes you interesting. A blank page, carver. To a collector, what is a blank page?*

Cyrus did not answer. They waded faster.

* * *

The Hound reached the tannery stairs and stopped.

Not because the trail ended. The trail was loud—loud as a shout, loud as grief. Bone dust, beeswax, fear-sweat, and beneath it all something else, something that had been folded into the scent like a letter inside an envelope, and it was this that stopped her on the wet stone with the rain starting to fall.

It smelled like home.

Not her home; she had none, the guild had seen to that. But the smell of a thing she had known once, before the collar, before the ledger, in a time that lived in her body but not her memory: woodsmoke and linseed oil and carved bone curing by a fire, and underneath it a woman's hands, and underneath *that* a lullaby she could hum but not name.

She stood on the stair with rain beginning to spot the stone around her paws, and the certainty that had carried her out of the kennel developed its first hairline fracture.

Debt-collectors are not permitted hesitation. The collar warmed against her throat—not painfully, not yet, just a reminder, the way a creditor taps the counter. *Proceed.* She proceeded. Down the stairs, into the drowned quarter, nose low, following the thread of smoke and linseed and a lullaby with no name.

But some part of her, deep in the wolf-body the guild had built around whatever she had been, began—for the first time in forty years—to ask a question the guild's accounting had no column for:

*Whose home?*

* * *

The ferryman was where the courier said he'd be, poling his flat boat through what had once been Cordwainer Street, and he looked at Cyrus's coin, then at Cyrus, and said, "Who told you I'd carry you?"

"A friend."

"Dead friends don't pay debts."

"No," Cyrus agreed, stepping carefully into the boat, "they leave them. There's a difference. One closes an account. The other opens one."

The ferryman grunted, which in the grammar of the lower city was a signature, and poled them out into the wide brown water where the rooftops made islands and the chimneys stood up like reeds.

Halfway across, Cyrus saw it.

At the far stair, at the edge of the drowned quarter where the water began, a shape stood in the rain. Grey as weather. Still as architecture. Too large for a dog and wrong for anything else, head lowered, reading the waterline the way Cyrus read a ledger—one line at a time, patiently, missing nothing.

Even at this distance, Cyrus felt the eyes find them. Felt the moment of contact like a hook set behind the sternum.

And then—Cyrus would spend the rest of the day not believing this—the shape hesitated. One heartbeat. Two. The great head tilted, rain sheeting off it, and though no sound crossed the water, Cyrus's skin prickled with the unmistakable sensation of being *recognized*. Not tracked. Not scented. Recognized, the way you recognize a face across a crowded market, the way you recognize—

*Carver,* said the dead courier, and his voice had gone strange, reverent and frightened at once. *Carver, why does the wolf look at you like she knows your grandmother?*

The boat bumped the far quay. Cyrus stepped off without answering, because the answer was already rising in them like floodwater, cold and undeniable and years overdue: the figurine in grandmother's workshop. The wolf carved in whalebone, exact down to the collar. The guardian bound before her death. The debt of love the guild had been collecting on for forty years.

"She doesn't know my grandmother," Cyrus said to the rain, to the courier, to the wolf watching from the far shore. "My grandmother knew *her*."

Across the water, the Hound stood very still, and the fracture in her certainty widened into a crack, and through the crack came the smell of woodsmoke and linseed oil and a lullaby with no name, and for the first time since the collar closed around her throat, the Hound wanted—actually *wanted*, in the place the guild had never managed to invoice—

—to remember whose hands had made her.
