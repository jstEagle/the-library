## 5. Country of Ash and Registry

The map had been singing to Cyrus for two days.

Not with sound, exactly. With *pull*—a tugging behind the sternum like a fisherman's line set in the flesh, reeling them north and east through the hill country where Vessany's borders used to be, before the empire redrew the world and called the drawing mercy. The courier's voice lived in the vellum now, faint as breath on a winter window: *north past the burned mill, east where the river forgets its name.* Cyrus followed because there was nowhere else to go, and because the dead who whispered to them in the counting house had gone strangely quiet, as if even they were holding their breath.

The Hound was somewhere behind them. Cyrus could feel it the way one feels a storm building past the mountains—not yet arrived, but certain. They had slept three hours in a shepherd's bothy with their boots on and the bone whistle clutched against their chest, and they had dreamed of teeth, patient and enormous, circling a house made of ledger paper.

Now, at dusk of the second day, the pull went taut and stopped.

Cyrus crested a ridge of gorse and slag and looked down into the bowl of a valley that no map had carried for thirty years, and understood, all at once, why the empire had burned so much and built so little here. The ruins of the old Vessany archive spread across the valley floor like the skeleton of something that had once been magnificent—a long hall roofless to the sky, its walls of pale stone gone the color of old teeth, its windows empty sockets filled with ivy. Around it, the foundations of lesser buildings fanned out in careful rows: registry offices, scriptoriums, the counting rooms where a nation's names had been kept. Everything was ash-grey and green-veined and utterly silent.

Except it wasn't silent.

Cyrus heard it before they saw them—the sound of many voices, very soft, like wind through wheat, or like a congregation murmuring just before the service begins. Their hands, which had carved ten thousand names into bone without trembling, began to shake.

They went down into the valley anyway. The pull allowed nothing else.

* * *

The archive hall had been burned from the inside. Cyrus walked its length over a floor of drifted ash and broken slate, and the voices thickened around them until they could pick out words, then whole sentences, then—God help them—*names.*

A woman stood by the great hearth where the registry fire had been, though she cast no light and her feet left no prints in the ash. She wore the grey smock of a registry clerk, and she was holding out her hands as if offering something, and when she spoke her voice was clear and tired and terribly polite.

"You can see us."

"I—" Cyrus's throat clicked. "Yes."

"Then you'll write us down." It was not quite a question. She stepped closer, and up close Cyrus could see that she flickered at the edges like a candle in a draft, and that her eyes held the particular shine of someone who has waited a very long time for good news and does not dare believe it. "You're a carver. I can tell. You have ink under your nails and bone-dust on your sleeves and you listen the way listeners do. So you'll write us down. All of us. We've been waiting thirty years for someone to write us down."

Behind her, others were emerging from the walls themselves—from the stones, from the ash, from the dark seams between what remained of the world. An old man in a ferryman's coat. Two children holding hands, solemn as small judges. A soldier still wearing the uniform of an army that officially had never existed. Dozens. Then scores. They came without menace, without hunger, crowding forward with the terrible gentleness of people at a station platform waiting for a train that is finally, finally arriving.

"We were struck," the clerk said. "Every record. Every register, every roll, every parish book. They burned this hall first because everything was copied here. My name was Maren Ostrova. I kept the birth rolls for the eastern parishes. There were four thousand names in my keeping and now there are none, not anywhere, and my mother died believing I was never born." Her voice did not break. That was somehow worse. "I am not asking you to avenge me. I am asking you to *spell me.*"

"I'm not—" Cyrus pressed back against a fallen shelf, bone whistle digging into their palm. "I'm nobody. I erased myself. I'm very good at it. I'm the last person who should—"

"You're the heir," said the ferryman, simply, and the word went through the crowd like a bell through water, ripple after ripple after ripple. "*The heir has come.*"

"I'm not. Whatever the guild told you, whatever the map says—I'm a bone-carver. I keep other people's debts. I don't have any country. I don't have any blood worth signing with."

The children let go of each other's hands and looked up at Cyrus with the patience of the very young and the very dead, which is to say, infinite.

"You have our blood," the smaller one said. "It looks different on you. But it's ours."

* * *

They would not leave Cyrus alone. Not menacingly—never that—but the way a family will not leave alone the relative who has come home after too long, trailing them through the ruined scriptoriums, pressing close, each with a name to give.

Cyrus sat on the steps of what had been the Hall of Registry with a scrap of charcoal and the back of the courier's map, because there was nothing else to write on, and began, helplessly, hopelessly, to take dictation from the dead.

Maren Ostrova, keeper of births. Dovan Ilves, ferryman of the river Sere, drowned in the crossing year with forty souls aboard whom he had refused to abandon. The soldier was Captain Ede Varik, and she had held the archive bridge for six hours so the clerks could burn the rolls themselves rather than let the empire copy them into its ledgers—*better ash than their property,* she said, and asked Cyrus to note that she regretted nothing except the sixth hour, when she might have run. The children were Liss and Tam Osk, ages seven and nine, and they did not know how they died, only that it had been cold, and that they had held hands, and that they would like it written down that they had held hands.

By full dark Cyrus had filled both sides of the map's margin and was writing in the ash itself, dragging the charcoal across the flagstones in letters a hand-span high, and the dead stood around in a wide ring watching every stroke with an attention that hurt worse than any threat ever had. This was the thing no one warned you about grief: it was heavy. It had weight and mass and it settled on the shoulders and the chest and behind the eyes, and it did not want vengeance or justice or even peace. It wanted *spelling.* It wanted its vowels back.

"There are thousands of us," Maren said quietly, watching Cyrus work. "Thousands more in the villages. We waited because we were told someone would come. The last queen's cartographer hid a door in the world, and he said the blood would come back for it, and where the blood goes the remembering follows."

"The Door of Return," Cyrus said. Their voice sounded scraped. "The courier said it opens at equinox. He said the heir must sign."

"He must sign in royal blood," said Captain Varik. "That's the price and the key both. The door doesn't open for armies or guilds or empires. Only for the line. Only freely given." She paused, and her flickering form dimmed, and when she spoke again it was with the care of someone handling something fragile. "There is a thing you should know, heir, before you decide anything. The guild's wolf is in the valley below. It has been sitting at the tree line since sundown, watching this place, and it will not come in."

Cyrus went cold. "Then it's waiting for me to leave."

"It is waiting," Varik said, "and it is *grieving*, though it does not know the word for it. Look at it, carver. Truly look. You of all people should recognize the shape of a debt being collected on the wrong side."

* * *

Cyrus climbed the eastern wall of the ruin alone, because the dead could not follow past the boundary of their burning, and looked down through the blue dark at the tree line.

Two points of light hung there, low to the ground, amber as lamplight. Eyes. They did not advance. They did not retreat. The Hound sat at the edge of the ash-field like a dog outside a church, and even at this distance Cyrus could see the wrongness in its posture—the head lowered, the great body canted not toward the ruin but *around* it, as if the building itself were something it could not look at directly.

And threaded through the night air, faint but unmistakable to a listener, came a sound Cyrus had heard only once before, at their grandmother's funeral, from an aunt who thought herself unheard: a low, broken keening, half howl and half hymn.

The dead had come up silently behind Cyrus to the edge of their boundary, a congregation at a window, and Maren Ostrova spoke softly at Cyrus's shoulder.

"That animal belonged to the last queen's daughter," she said. "We remember it. We remember *everything*, heir—that is our curse and our only wealth. It was bound in blood and bone by a carver's hand, and the guild took it from her estate, and it has been collecting their debts for thirty years while its heart stayed buried in this valley." A pause. "You carve bones. You of all people know that a thing carved can be uncarved."

Below, the amber eyes lifted and found Cyrus on the wall, and held, and did not waver, and in them Cyrus saw something that turned their blood to ice water and then, inexplicably, to something warmer:

Recognition. And beneath it, unbearable and unmistakable, hope.

The equinox was three days away. The map in Cyrus's pocket had grown warm as a living thing, and the courier's voice rose from the vellum one last time, thin and urgent:

*The heir must sign. And the guild is coming to collect.*

Behind Cyrus, in the ash of the registry floor, four thousand names waited in charcoal to be remembered—and somewhere down the dark road, torchlight was beginning to move.
