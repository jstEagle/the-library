## 6. The Grandmother's Bone Knife

The workshop had been waiting for someone the way a grave waits: patiently, without complaint, with its door half-open to whatever weather chose to visit.

Cyrus slipped inside as the last grey light went out of the sky, pulling the door shut behind them until only a knife's width of darkness remained. The flooded lower city still dripped somewhere behind them—the courier's voice in their satchel had gone quiet an hour ago, exhausted, or pretending to be. *Left at the tanner's arch. The street with no name because they burned the sign and burned the man who painted it.* Vessany's streets remembered being named even when nothing else did.

Inside, dust lay on everything like a second skin. Not the ordinary dust of abandonment—Cyrus knew that dust, the indifferent grey of rooms merely left—but a finer powder, pale and faintly sweet-smelling, that their hands knew before their mind did. Bone-dust. Someone had worked here, and worked long, until the very air of the place was seasoned with it.

Their fingers found a lamp by touch alone, and oil in its belly that had not entirely spoiled, and a striker that caught on the third try. Light bloomed yellow and small across a room that stopped Cyrus's breath.

Bench along the far wall, scarred with the crescent bites of gouges. A rack of chisels sorted by fineness, smallest no wider than an eyelash. A whetstone worn into a valley. Jars of boiled sinew glue. And above the bench, pegged to the wall in careful rows like a family portrait hung crooked, tools—bone-handled, bone-tipped, every one of them carved from the same pale material and every one of them marked with the same small stamp pressed into the hafts: a circle with a line through it, like a coin seen edge-on. Like a moon refusing to show its face.

Cyrus knew the mark. They had known it before they could read.

It was stamped into the first knife they'd ever been allowed to hold, the one their grandmother had put in their four-year-old fist and guided through a scrap of sheep bone until the scrap bore something like a bird, or a leaf, or a bird becoming a leaf. *You don't carve what you want,* she had said, her hand warm over theirs. *You listen for what the bone wants, and you agree to it. That's the whole trade. Agreement.*

*Agreement.* The word sat strangely in Cyrus's chest now, in this room where the guild's shadow reached even here—because it did reach here; they could feel it, the faint pressure of a debt recorded, the way one feels a storm coming in the teeth. Everything the guild touched acquired that pressure eventually. Even love, apparently. Even this.

They crossed the room slowly, leaving prints in the bone-dust, and sat down at the bench as though they had never left it. Twenty years since anyone had. Perhaps more. Their grandmother had died—or had been made to die, the distinction mattered now—when Cyrus was nine, and the guild had come within the week with their inventory lists and their polite voices, and had carried away everything with a ledger-value. Which should have been everything. Which, by the guild's own accounting, *was* everything.

But not this. This room had been missed, or overlooked, or—Cyrus ran a finger along the bench's edge and felt the deliberate grooves cut there, tally-marks, hundreds of them—a record kept too small for anyone to think worth taking.

She had counted something here. In the last years. After the annexation, after Vessany went into the ledgers as *former territory, asset consolidated*, she had come to this room and she had cut a mark for each—

Cyrus didn't let themselves finish the thought. Some arithmetic you have to approach sideways or it bites.

Instead they opened the drawers.

The top one held offcuts, curling shavings white as frost. The second held wax tablets blank except for practice-strokes. The third stuck, swelled with damp, and when Cyrus wrenched it free something inside slid and clinked and rolled forward into the light like a thing that had been listening for footsteps.

A wolf.

Carved from a single piece of femur—ox, maybe, or older, heavier stock than ox—the figurine stood no taller than Cyrus's thumb, but it filled the drawer's opening the way a wolf fills a doorway: completely. It was good work. It was work past good, work that made the eye hurt a little, because the grain of the bone had been followed so faithfully that the animal seemed not carved but *released*, as though it had always been asleep in the bone and someone patient enough had finally agreed to wake it. Its head was up. Its ears were pricked. Its mouth was open just enough to show teeth, and its posture was not attack.

It was hesitation. Frozen mid-hesitation, the moment before a choice, held in dead material for twenty years.

And Cyrus knew it. Not the way one knows a carving. The way one knows a face.

The Hound stood exactly so. The Hound, who had cornered them twice in the flooded dark and twice paused—that terrible half-second when the great head tilted and the eyes, banked coals in a skull the size of a cartwheel, seemed to be consulting something older than the guild's orders. The Hound, whose gait Cyrus had catalogued during two nights of flight with the helpless precision of a carver's mind: the set of the shoulders, the angle of the ears, the way the weight rode back on the haunches before any lunge.

The figurine matched. Joint by joint, hair by hair. Not similar. *Identical*, the way a key matches the lock it was cut for.

"Oh," Cyrus said aloud, and the word fell into the dusty room and broke like glass.

They turned the little wolf over. On its belly, half-worn, the stamp: circle, line. Moon edge-on. And beneath it, smaller, cut with a finer tool, letters in old Vessany script that Cyrus had to sound out like a child:

*For the house I cannot keep. Guard it anyway.*

Their grandmother had bound a guardian. Before the end—before whatever the guild had done or permitted or collected—she had taken a spirit and given it shape and given it duty, the oldest working there was, the one the guild called *collateralization* and the old country had called by simpler words. She had made herself a wolf out of love and bone, and the guild had found it, because the guild found everything, and had put it in service. Had put a collar on her guardian and set it collecting debts.

Had set her grandmother's love to hunt her granddaughter.

Cyrus sat very still. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above, water moved through the ruined pipes of the lower city with a sound like slow applause.

*Not land,* they thought. *Not title.* The empire wanted Vessany's heir because an heir was a signature, and a signature was legitimacy, and legitimacy was the one commodity empires couldn't carve for themselves. The guild wanted Cyrus because assets appreciate. Everyone, everyone, wanted the inheritance that lived in Cyrus's blood.

But this drawer held the real inheritance. A debt of love, incurred honestly, owed by the dead to the living—and the guild had been collecting interest on it for twenty years without once knowing what it held. They thought they owned a hound. They were renting a grandmother.

Cyrus closed their hand around the wolf. The bone was cold, then less cold, and then—not warm, nothing so kind—but *present*, the way a sleeping animal is present, its warmth arriving through the floorboards. The courier's voice stirred faintly in the satchel, and outside, far off across the drowned streets, something began to run.

* * *

The Hound came the way winter comes: no hurry, and no possible prevention.

Cyrus heard it first as a change in the city's dripping rhythm, a silence moving through the silences. Then claws on stone, unhurried, precise. The lamp guttered as if in deference. Cyrus stood with their back to the bench, the figurine in one fist and their grandmother's heaviest chisel in the other—a ridiculous weapon, a carpenter's argument against a wolf the size of a horse—and watched the doorway go black.

It filled the door. Of course it did. It always filled whatever space contained it, not because it grew but because the eye insisted. Steam curled off its shoulders; the lower city's floodwater still clung to its coat in beads that glowed like struck iron. The collar sat at its throat, guild-grey, humming its thin note of ownership.

The Hound looked at Cyrus.

Then it looked at the figurine, and the world changed key.

Cyrus felt it happen rather than saw it—a pressure dropping, like the air before lightning. The Hound's whole body went wrong, muscles locking in patterns that weren't pursuit, weren't anything the guild had written into it. Its head lowered. Its nose touched the little wolf in Cyrus's palm, and the great ribs shuddered around a breath that sounded almost like a word, almost like a name, almost like *Jude*—the childhood name, the erased name, the name only one living person besides Cyrus had ever used, and she was dead, or collared, or both.

"You can't," Cyrus whispered, not sure which of them they were instructing. "You can't remember. If you remember while you're theirs, they'll—"

The Hound's lips drew back. Not a snarl. Grief has that shape too.

Its paw came up—huge, iron-beaded, gentle beyond all sense—and pressed Cyrus backward against the bench, pinning without breaking, a restraint that cost the beast visibly, tremors running down its foreleg. Its jaws opened above Cyrus's chest. Close enough that Cyrus could count the teeth, could smell the old bone-and-oil scent of them, the workshop's own smell returned to it after twenty years.

But it did not strike. It stood over them shaking like a thing in a high wind, collar screaming its thin note, and its eyes—banked coals, banked and banked and banked—had tears in them, or what passes for tears in a made thing, and Cyrus understood at last that they were looking at the most expensive prisoner the guild owned, and that the price had been paid by a woman with a moon-edge stamp on her tools, out of love, on credit, twenty years ago.

"The equinox," Cyrus breathed. "Two days. Hold on for two days."

Outside, across the drowned and burning-dark city, bells began—guild bells, counting-house bells, ringing the hour in a pattern Cyrus had never heard used except once, in the archives, in a text about emergency liquidation.

Somewhere in Vessany, a ledger was about to burn. And the Hound, trembling over them with its grief showing, lifted its head toward the sound like a creature hearing its master call it home.
