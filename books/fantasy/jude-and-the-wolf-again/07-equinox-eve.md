## 7. Equinox Eve

The fire began at the western stacks, because fire always begins where the paper is oldest and the men are laziest.

Cyrus watched it from the ridge above the old archive, knees drawn up in the dead bracken, the courier's map pressed flat against their chest beneath their coat like a second heartbeat. From this distance the guild's torches were small and orderly, a procession of orange points moving through the ruined colonnades of Vessany's registry, and for one foolish moment they looked almost ceremonial—almost like a festival of lights, the kind Cyrus's grandmother had described in stories she told with her hands while her voice did other work.

Then the first shelf caught, and the light turned the color of a wound.

The archive had no roof. It had not had a roof for a generation; the empire had stripped the copper the same season it stripped the country's name. Rain had gotten into the ledgers for thirty years, bloating them, warping them, but never destroying them—the dead of Vessany were damp and swollen and stubborn, and they had waited all this time in their mildewed thousands for someone to come read them.

Now the smoke rose off them in long grey banners, and Cyrus could *hear* them.

That was the thing about being a bone-carver who kept company with the dead: fire was not silent to Cyrus. Every name that curled loose from its page made a sound as it went—a small exhalation, a syllable unfinished. Somewhere in the burning stacks a woman was saying a child's name over and over, the way you say a name when you are trying to hold it in your mouth past the moment of your own ending. Somewhere else a man recited the boundaries of an orchard that no longer existed, acre by acre, tree by tree, as though the empire could be argued out of its conquest by precise measurement.

"Stop," Cyrus whispered, uselessly, to a hillside full of cold air. "Stop, I'm here, I'll remember—"

But remembering was not the same as registering, and they all knew the difference now. The dead had spent a generation learning exactly what the difference weighed.

Below, the provost moved along the burning rows with a lantern in one hand and the master ledger chained to her wrist, checking titles against flames. Cyrus could not see her face at this distance, but they knew the set of her shoulders: unhurried, thorough, a woman doing inventory. She had been kind to Cyrus once, or something shaped like kindness. She had taught them which erasures would pass inspection and which would draw attention. All those years Cyrus had thought they were slipping their own name out of the world's books, and the whole time the guild had been holding the door open, keeping the asset clean, keeping the heir unclaimed and therefore unspoiled.

Waiting for the price to appreciate.

The equinox was tomorrow. The Door of Return opened at the turning of the light, and it wanted what doors of that kind always wanted: a signature, freely given, in royal blood. Not coerced. Not forged. The old magic of Vessany had been built on consent the way a house is built on stone, and even burned and annexed and struck from every map, the Door would not take a signature pried out of anyone. The guild knew this. That was why Cyrus was still breathing. That was why there would be an exchange at all instead of simply a knife in the dark on some flooded stair.

*Sign,* the courier's voice murmured from the vellum against Cyrus's ribs. He had been murmuring all day, fainter now, worn thin by his own death. *They will offer you comfort first. Then they will offer you grief. Refuse both. Refuse both, heir.*

"I intend to refuse everything," Cyrus said under their breath, and the wind coming up off the burning valley took the words apart before they could become a promise.

Because that was the trap, wasn't it. They had understood it all afternoon as they climbed, and understanding it felt like swallowing a stone. A signature freely given—but given between what choices? Sign the empire's contract and live soft as a puppet king, legitimizing the theft with a blood-warm seal. Refuse, and watch the last ledger burn with every remaining name inside it, and Vessany die the final death—not conquered, merely *unrecorded*, which is the only death empires have ever feared for themselves.

Every offered choice was a cage. The only question left was which cage had a door in it that the guild hadn't noticed.

On the ridge, the smoke bent eastward, and somewhere inside it ten thousand names went up like startled birds.

*   *

The Hound smelled the archive burning four miles away, and its heart—if the thing in its chest could be called a heart, if the grief lodged there could be called anything so simple—turned over like a cart on a bad road.

It stood at the edge of the firelight where the handlers had left it, chained by nothing but the collar, which needed no chain. The collar was bone, old bone, carved with a script the Hound could feel but no longer read, and it sat against the Hound's throat the way a hand sits against a throat that is deciding.

*Finish the heir,* the handlers had said. The tall one had said it kindly, because he was the kind one, and in the Hound's long service it had learned that the kind ones gave the worst orders. *At the exchange. Cleanly. The buyers want the asset intact until signature, and after signature the asset is a problem, and problems are your trade.*

The Hound had bowed its great head. It had always bowed its head. For eleven years it had bowed its head, and hunted debts through the lower city, and dragged defaulters weeping to the counting-house steps, and never once asked whose hands had carved the collar, because asking was the first disobedience and disobedience was punished in the blood and marrow.

But tonight the firelight came up the hill and touched the Hound's face, and the Hound remembered a kitchen.

Not its own—it had no own; that was the cruelty of the binding, that it had been carved out of a woman and given no memory except what served the debt. But the firelight was amber, and it fell through the colonnade smoke in shafts, and shafts of amber light falling through smoke were the exact color of an evening in another country, in a kitchen where a woman sang low over a bench of bones and a child sat at her feet carving a wolf from a knuckle of antler.

The wolf the child carved had been wrong. Ears too big, legs uneven, one eye deeper than the other. The woman had held it up to the lamplight and laughed—not at the child, never at the child—and said, *Good. If it were perfect it would be mine. Imperfect, it's yours.*

The Hound's throat moved under the collar.

It knew the child's face now. It had seen it across the flooded streets two nights ago, by lightning-light, when the order had been *retrieve the map* and the Hound had cornered the small figure against a wall of rising water—and hesitated. One heartbeat. Two. Long enough for the courier's ghost to pull the heir down a drain the Hound's body should have followed them into. The handlers had noted the hesitation. Handlers noted everything. That was what the collar was for.

*You are a debt-collector,* the tall handler had reminded it afterward, crouching to look into its eyes the way no one should ever look into anything's eyes. *You are not owed sentiment.*

No. But someone had owed *it*. Someone had bound it out of love—that much survived the carving, that single grain of truth at the center, the reason the shape was a guardian and not merely a weapon—and the guild had collected on that love for eleven years without once honoring the creditor.

The fire roared. Pages went up. And deep in the Hound's chest the grain of truth cracked, and through the crack came a smell that was not smoke: tallow, and juniper smoke from a hearth, and the particular sweetness of bone freshly worked.

The Hound took one step toward the fire. Not toward the flame—toward the *archive*, toward the registry shelves where the records of bindings were kept, where its own making must be written in some ledger it had never been permitted to read.

The collar woke.

There was no better word. It woke against the Hound's throat like a mouth, like teeth, like the original hand closing, and the pain went down through the carved script into the blood and marrow and out again, and the Hound went down too, into the mud at the edge of the firelight, legs folding, a sound tearing out of it that was half howl and half something older and more human than any wolf had ever owned.

The handlers watched from a safe distance. The kind one said, quietly, "Again tomorrow night, after the exchange." As if discussing weather. As if discussing interest.

And the Hound lay in the trampled grass with the taste of its own blood in its mouth and the amber firelight on its face, and understood—for the first time in eleven years, clearly, the way the dying understand—that it could not obey tomorrow either. That whatever the collar did to it next, the grain had cracked, and cracks do not close.

Somewhere on the dark hills above, the heir was watching. The Hound could not see them. But it could smell, very faintly, under the smoke and the rain and the burning names, antler shavings and juniper and a kitchen long ago.

It put its muzzle in the mud and breathed that smell, and waited to see what it would cost.

*   *

From the ridge, Cyrus saw the wolf go down.

They saw the great shape break from the line of handlers, take three strides toward the fire, and collapse as though the night itself had struck it—and they heard the sound it made, carried up the valley on the smoke, and their hands closed hard on the frozen bracken until the stems snapped.

"That's my grandmother," Cyrus said aloud, to no one, to the hillside, to the burning dead below who had gone quiet in their final thousands. Their breath steamed and shook. "That's my grandmother, and they've had her on a chain for eleven years."

The dead courier stirred in the vellum, faint as ash settling. *Now you understand the price,* he said. *Freely given. They cannot force your hand. So they will force your heart. They will make you choose between your country's memory and the one soul they know you cannot let go.*

Yes. That was the shape of it. Cyrus had been circling it all day and now it stood fully visible against the firelight, elegant and obscene, the guild's finest piece of accounting: sign freely, heir—or watch the collar do its work forever, and call your refusal a choice.

The fire burned down toward the eastern stacks. The provost checked her figures against the flames. And on the ridge above, in the cold and the smoke-smell and the last light of a country's testimony, Cyrus Thackeray—Ishikawa, Jude, last registered heir of a nation that existed nowhere but in ledgers—took out the bone knife they had taken from the workshop, laid it across their knees, and began, slowly, with a carver's patience, to plan a signature.

Tomorrow the Door would open. Tomorrow the guild would bring its buyers and its cages dressed as choices.

But Cyrus had spent a lifetime learning what ledgers held, and who could hold them back.

Let the equinox come.
