## 10. A Wolf Unbound

The Door of Return stood open, and it did not lead out.

It led inward—into a country made of remembering: orchards that had been burned but stood now heavy with fruit no one would ever eat, streets rebuilt from the mortar of names, a sky the color of the blue dye Vessany had once traded across three seas before anyone thought to strike the trade from record. The dead walked there in their annexation-day clothes. Some of them waved.

Cyrus stood on the threshold with ink still wet on their hand—the old royal ledger closed under their arm, heavier than any book had a right to be—and watched the guild's provost try to understand what had happened to her.

She was not destroyed, exactly. That would have been simpler. She had simply been *entered*, the way she entered everything: her own debts called due by the authority of the signature above hers. The empire's buyers had already fled, their contract voided by a country that existed again, and the provost stood in the trampled grass before the door holding a ledger of her own that now listed, in her own immaculate hand, everything she owed.

"It was never personal," she said. Her voice had gone thin as vellum. "You understand that. I kept you safe the way one keeps anything valuable."

"I know," Cyrus said. "That was the worst part."

Behind them, something vast and broken was dragging itself through the grass.

* * *

The Hound had torn its collar open at the equinox hour, when the binding was transferred—when every debt the guild held became, for one breathless moment, Cyrus's debt to forgive or collect. The collar had not come away cleanly. It had come away like a tooth from a jaw, like a nail from a wall, taking pieces of whatever it had held together for thirty years, and now the wolf-shape was failing, unraveling at the edges into smoke and then into something else, something smaller.

Cyrus went to it. They did not run, though every part of them that had spent a lifetime erasing itself screamed to run, to hide, to become blank again. They walked through grass still warm from the burning of the archive on the far hill, and they knelt.

The wolf was becoming a woman.

It happened the way ice becomes water—not all at once, but everywhere at once. Grey fur thinned to grey hair. The great ribs eased and shrank until they were only the ribs of an old woman who had worked too many years bent over a carving bench. The amber eyes stayed last, and in them Cyrus saw the hesitation from the flooded lower city, the crack in the hunter's certainty, widened now into something that looked, unbearably, like recognition.

"Jude," the woman said.

Her voice was rust and honey. It was a voice from a kitchen two decades gone, from hands guiding small ones over bone and knife, from lullabies sung in a language the maps no longer admitted existed.

"You're supposed to be dead," Cyrus said. Their voice cracked down the middle. "You died when I was nine. I carved your name myself. I was *good* at it even then."

"And so I was," she said, "for a while." She tried to rise and could not, and Cyrus caught her, and her weight against them was the weight of every year she had spent as the guild's instrument, hunting debts in the shape of a wolf, including—for how long? how long had they known?—the debt of her own grandchild's hidden inheritance. "Death is a ledger entry too, little Jude. And your grandmother was careless about signing things when she was frightened. I signed a guardianship over you to a spirit the guild could bind. I thought—I told myself—a guardian would keep you safer than a dying woman could."

"The Hound."

"The Hound." She laughed, and it hurt her, and she did it anyway. "I made it from a carving of my own hands. You found my workshop. You saw the figurine. I could hear you, some of the time, when the collar let me near enough. You always talked to the things you carved. Even as a boy. Even as a girl. Whichever you were that day."

"Both," Cyrus said. "Neither. Whatever kept me invisible."

Her hand found theirs—human now, spotted and trembling—and pressed something into it. The bone knife. Grandmother Sefa's knife, worn smooth where a thumb had rested for forty years, its handle carved with a running wolf whose eye was a knot of darker bone.

"I'm here to finish dying," she said simply. "Properly this time, with someone holding my hand instead of a collar around my throat. But first—" her grip tightened, and the amber eyes fixed on Cyrus with the ferocity that had once chased them across a flooded city, "—first you will listen, because I paid thirty years to earn this warning, and I will not be erased before I give it."

* * *

"A ledger that holds a nation," she said, "is not a tool. Tools lie quiet between uses. This will not. Every night it will whisper to you the names you could restore and the names you could strike. Every night it will show you how much gentler the world would be if only you decided who deserved to be remembered. And you will be *right*, Jude. That is the poison of it. You will be right nine times out of ten, and the tenth time you will be right in a way that makes you into her—" a nod toward the provost, sitting ruined and human-sized among her arithmetic, "—and you will not feel the difference, because power never announces itself as corruption. It announces itself as bookkeeping."

"I know," Cyrus whispered.

"You do not. Not yet. But you carved a whistle for a grieving daughter once and charged her nothing, and you have talked to the dead your whole life without once asking them for anything. So maybe. Maybe." She smiled, and it was her smile, the one from the kitchen, crooked on the left side. "Keep the knife where you can see it while you write. Bone remembers what it was before it was a tool. Let it remind you what you were."

The sun was going down behind the hills, gilding the standing orchards of the remembered country. Around them, at a respectful distance, the dead of Vessany had gathered—quietly, the way they had crowded the old archive, hopeful and unhurried, because the dead had learned patience the hard way. An old man took off his hat. A woman held up a child so she could see.

Grandmother Sefa looked at them, and then at Cyrus, and her face did something complicated—grief and pride tangled past separating.

"They'll want things from you," she said. "Names restored, grievances tallied, borders redrawn. Say yes slowly. Say yes to fewer things than you want to. And when they call you Your Majesty—" her breath was going shallow now; Cyrus heard it fraying, "—tell them your grandmother says majesty is just another word for being the one who stays to clean up."

"Stay," Cyrus said. Foolish, begging, useless. "I just got you back. Stay."

"I stayed thirty years longer than I meant to already, little one." She lifted her hand—it took visible effort—and touched Cyrus's cheek, and her palm smelled of bone dust and tallow and home. "I finished dying now. Watch."

And she did. There was no light, no dissolution into motes. She simply grew quiet, and lighter, and then she was a beloved weight in Cyrus's arms and then she was less than that, and then she was a shape of fine grey ash in the grass, and then, when the wind came off the hills, she was part of Vessany—which existed again, which would exist, if nowhere else, in memory and in ash and in the ledger under Cyrus's arm.

Cyrus sat in the darkening field for a long time, holding a bone knife, surrounded by the patient dead, and wept without shame or audience, because for the first time in their life they belonged somewhere and the belonging had cost exactly this.

* * *

The guild's counting house smelled different when Cyrus unlocked it at dawn. Or perhaps it smelled the same, and Cyrus was different, which amounted to the same thing.

The provost was gone—released into her debts, sent to work them off in a debtor's colony where the ledgers were kept by people stricter than she had ever been. The clerks had scattered in the night, all but three, who came back because they had nowhere else and, one of them admitted, because someone had to see what happened next.

What happened next was this:

Cyrus opened the master ledger on the long table where they had spent seven years quietly deleting themselves, and dipped a pen, and began to restore.

It was slow work. Every erased name had to be found again—in margins, in memory, in the testimony of the dead who drifted now freely through the guildhall doors because a door that opens into a remembered country does not close neatly behind it. Cyrus wrote each name in full, and beside each name the thing owed: a whistle uncharged, a debt forgiven, a grave marked, a border acknowledged. The impossible things in the vaults were re-catalogued under new rules, posted on the loading door in Cyrus's own careful carving:

*Nothing taken that is not fairly traded. Nothing erased that cannot be restored. No heir sold, no nation struck, no grief priced.*

The clerks watched. One of them, a young woman with ink-stained fingers named Odile, asked on the third day whether the new keeper of the books intended to keep the guild's older arrangements with certain buyers in the capital, and Cyrus looked at her for a long moment and felt the ledger whisper—it really did whisper, Grandmother had been right, it whispered every night, sweetly, reasonably—and set the bone knife on the table where they could see it while they answered.

"No," Cyrus said. "Burn those pages. And put it in the book that I ordered it, so that someday, if I forget what I am, the record can testify against me."

Odile wrote it down. Something in her face suggested she would remember.

On the seventh evening, the counting house quiet, the lamps low, Cyrus turned to the page where their own name had been struck so many times the vellum had worn thin. They wrote it out in full, both names, all the names: *Jude Ishikawa Thackeray-Vessane, bone-carver, keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things, last heir of a country that exists.* Then, after a moment, beneath it, smaller:

*Who belongs here.*

Outside, the equinox moon rose over Vessany—over the burned archive and the standing orchards, over the flooded lower city drying street by street, over the hills where a fine grey ash had joined the soil. Somewhere in the dark, a wolf-shaped absence ran free at last, doing nothing for anyone, owing nothing, hunting nothing, and Cyrus felt its freedom like a second heartbeat.

They blew out the lamp. They left the ledger open, because a book that holds a nation should never again be shut where no one can watch what is written in it.

And in the doorway, silhouetted against the moon, stood a courier who had been dead for eleven days, holding out a folded map, saying, apologetically, that he seemed to have one more delivery—and that this time, the address was written in Cyrus's own hand.
