## 1. The Ledger of Small Impossibilities

The trouble with impossible things, Juniper Thackeray had learned in his eleven years as the Guild's ledger-keeper, was that they refused to stay put.

Take, for instance, the matter of the third-floor window that had begun dreaming. It had been reported by a laundress named Bess, who claimed that every morning when she opened it to shake out the linens, the view outside was not the courtyard but a green hill under three moons. The Guild's response had been swift and sensible: they'd assigned the window a number (W-114), issued it a warning placard, and charged the cost of the placard to the Department of Structural Anomalies, which did not exist and therefore could not object.

Juniper entered all of this into the Ledger of Small Impossibilities in his small, exact hand: *One (1) window, dreaming. Recurring. No action required unless moons increase.*

He dipped his pen. He paused. He added, after some deliberation: *Moons currently two and one-half. Monitor.*

The Guildhouse at midnight was his favorite version of the place — the great stone rooms emptied of their noise, the lamps turned low, the smell of ink and beeswax and old paper settling over everything like a second roof. Somewhere above him, an apprentice snored through a floorboard. Somewhere below, the gate hummed its low, constant note, the sound he had fallen asleep to since he was nine years old and first brought through these doors with nothing but a name and a talent for counting.

He had been balancing accounts ever since. Not ordinary accounts — those were handled by clerks with abacuses and short tempers. Juniper kept the *other* books. The books where a debt might be owed in years instead of coin, or in names instead of years, or in one very memorable case in the entire collected sneezes of a minor river god, which the Guild had accepted at par value and still stored, carefully, in a lead-lined box nobody sneezed near.

Tonight's stack was mercifully tame. A bottled echo needed cataloging. A hedge-witch in the Lower Terraces had reported her shadow arriving home several hours after she did, and the discrepancy was widening. And there was the usual quarterly reconciliation of the gate-wards, which meant walking the long gallery with a tuning fork and writing down whatever the stones told him.

Routine work. Good work. Work that let a man keep his hands busy while his mind wandered off to places it perhaps ought not go.

Juniper uncorked the bottled echo.

It had come in from a fisherman on the Graywater, who had caught it — so the intake slip insisted — "in a net, along with forty eels and one extremely apologetic otter." Echoes were common enough in the wild; sounds that outlived their moments and drifted about looking for somewhere to settle. Most were trivial: a door slamming in 1740, someone laughing at a joke nobody remembered anymore. This one, though, per the fisherman's description, was a voice saying the same word over and over, and the word was *soon*.

He held the bottle up to the lamp. Inside, faint as breath on glass, something moved. Not light. Not smoke. A shape like the memory of a shape.

"Catalog entry," Juniper said to the empty room, because talking to himself was cheaper than talking to colleagues, and colleagues at this hour were asleep anyway. "Item E-2213. One echo, verbal, repetitive. Content: 'soon.' Origin: Graywater shallows. Provenance: dubious. Recommendation: storage, Class Three."

The echo said, *soon*, muffled and patient, and Juniper felt the word land somewhere behind his ribs and stay there, the way a cold draft finds the gap in a coat you'd sworn had no gaps.

"Soon what?" he asked it.

The echo, being an echo, declined to elaborate. That was the whole trouble with echoes. They were all conclusion and no context. He stoppered the bottle, wrote *Class Three* in the ledger, then crossed it out and wrote *Class Two, pending review*, because his pen had developed opinions of its own tonight, and his pen was rarely wrong.

* * *

The gallery ran the length of the eastern wing, all pale stone and tall windows, and at its far end stood the gate.

Not a gate in any sense a farmer would recognize. It was a doorway set free-standing in the middle of the floor, framed in black wood older than the Guildhouse around it, older than the city, older — if you believed the founding ledgers, and Juniper professionally believed everything written down — than most of the world's better ideas. Through its arch was not the far side of the gallery but a darkness that the lamps never seemed to reach, a darkness with texture, like velvet viewed from the wrong side. The wards were carved into the frame in spirals of First Tongue script, and they hummed.

They had hummed, in Juniper's living memory, exactly one way: a low, even tone, steady as a sleeping heart. Wardens' children were lulled by it. Juniper had once, in a fit of youthful sentiment, tried to transcribe it into musical notation and had produced a single whole note, held forever, which the Guild's music master had called "technically accurate and artistically useless."

Tonight, tuning fork in hand, Juniper stopped six paces from the gate and frowned.

The hum was off.

Not broken — nothing so dramatic. Off the way a well-tuned lute is off when the weather turns, a hair's breadth flat, a hair's breadth slow. He struck the fork and held it up. The gate's tone answered it, and the answer came back a fraction late. Then again. Then again, each return lagging just slightly more than the last, like a conversation with someone who keeps almost interrupting you.

"That's new," Juniper told the gate.

The gate, being a gate, declined to elaborate. Everyone in this building was tight-lipped except the hedge-witch's shadow, and it wasn't due until Thursday.

He walked the frame slowly, pressing the flat of his palm to the stone at each ward-point, the way he'd been taught by old Master Corvin, who had held this office before him and who had taught him that wards were less like locks and more like animals: they wanted attention, regular hours, and to be spoken to in a calm voice. At the seventh point, low on the left jamb, the stone was warm. Not alarmingly warm. Warm the way a hearthstone is warm an hour after the fire dies — holding something, giving it back slowly.

He made his notes. *Quarterly attunement, autumn. All points nominal except J-7 (residual warmth) and overall tempo (retarded by approx. one beat per hundred). Cause: unknown. Action: monitor.*

Then he stood there a while longer, because his feet had opinions too tonight, and listened.

Here is what Juniper Thackeray knew about the gate, the way a man knows the rules of a house he grew up in: it separated the human realms from the wolf-kind beyond. It had stood for as long as records existed. It was bound by a covenant, renewed each generation, the terms of which lived in the oldest ledger in the archive — a ledger he had personally dusted four hundred times and read, in full, exactly zero times, because the early pages were written in First Tongue and the translations were sealed under Guildmaster's order, and because some part of him, even at nine years old, had understood that certain doors are best admired rather than opened.

And here is what he felt about the gate tonight, which was a different kind of knowing entirely: that the slowness in its hum was not decay. Decay doesn't keep time. What he heard, listening with his eyes shut and his hand on the warm stone, was rhythm. Deliberate. Patient. Something on the other side keeping count — or something in the gate itself counting down — and either way, counting toward a number it already knew.

"One," he said softly, testing, and the hum answered a half-beat late.

"Two."

Half a beat again. Steady as a metronome wound by someone with all the patience in the world and none of the hurry.

Juniper opened his eyes, told himself firmly that he was a grown man with a respectable salary and a filing system praised by two separate Guildmasters, and went back to his desk to write *monitor* in the margin with a hand that only shook a little.

* * *

At half past two, the hedge-witch's shadow problem resolved itself, temporarily, when the shadow arrived at the intake office four hours ahead of its owner, sat politely in the waiting chair, and began reading a pamphlet about chimney safety. Juniper logged it (*Item S-887. One shadow, punctual, literate. Owner en route. Do not feed*) and decided this was as good a moment as any for tea.

The kitchens at night belonged to the mice and to Wren, the under-cook, who slept in a cot by the ovens and woke for no one. Juniper moved through the dark on familiar feet, lit the small stove, and set the kettle on with the ease of long habit. While it heated he leaned against the counter and let himself do the thing he only allowed himself at this hour, alone, with no ledgers open to contradict him:

He thought about the gate.

Sixty years ago — he'd checked the dates that evening, twice, because numbers were his native language and numbers had seemed wrong — the last recorded covenant renewal had taken place. There was a page for it. A fine, official page, signed and dated and stamped with the Guild seal, everything in order, everything *balanced*. And yet the gate's hum had been slowing for months now, by fractions so small that only a man who had listened to it every quarter of his life would catch them, and a covenant sixty years untested should not be running down like a clock in need of winding unless—

Unless the renewal had never truly happened. Unless the page was a page and nothing more.

He made himself tea he didn't drink and scolded himself thoroughly. One warm stone and a lazy hum. That was hardly evidence. That was a Tuesday, in the grand scheme of impossible things. Windows dreamed. Shadows read pamphlets. Gates got tired; gates were old; probably gates were entitled to run a little slow in their dotage, the same as Master Corvin's knees.

But the echo in its bottle downstairs said *soon*, and the gate said *one, two*, and Juniper had spent eleven years learning that when two unrelated impossibilities started agreeing with each other, the Guild's official position was coincidence, and the Guild's unofficial position, held quietly by everyone who actually worked the books, was *run*.

The kettle screamed. He poured, missed the cup slightly, corrected, and carried his tea back through the dark halls toward the archive, because sleep was clearly not on tonight's schedule, and if he couldn't sleep, he could at least stand in front of the sealed cabinet that held the oldest ledger and glare at it in a professional capacity.

* * *

The archive at night was a cathedral of paper. Racks rose into the gloom, ten thousand drawers, forty thousand boxes, and the particular silence of a room where everything is waiting to be known. Juniper's lamp threw a small brave circle on the flagstones. He walked it to the far wall, to the iron-banded cabinet marked, in faded gilt, *FOUNDING RECORDS — GUILDMASTER'S KEY ONLY*, and stood before it with his tea going cold in his hand.

He didn't have the key. Of course he didn't. Veyra kept it on her own ring, and Veyra — brilliant, brisk, terrifyingly competent Guildmaster Veyra, who had promoted him twice and remembered his name-day and once defended his filing system against the entire Council of Accounts with the ferocity of a mother bear — Veyra had ordered those pages sealed before Juniper had even joined the staff, back when he was still a boy counting spoons to prove he had a gift.

There was no reason to think about them. There was every reason not to. A warm stone, a slow hum, a fisherman's echo. Three coincidences do not make a conspiracy; they make, at most, an interesting footnote.

Juniper took a sip of cold tea, set the cup on a shelf, and copied down — from memory, from the intake slips, from the tuning-fork log he'd kept faithfully for eleven years — every irregularity of the past season into his own pocket notebook. Dates. Temperatures. Beats lost. When he finished, the pattern looked back up at him from the page with the smug clarity of a thing that had been obvious all along.

The slowing wasn't random. It wasn't decay. It was arithmetic. Each week, the gate lost exactly one more beat per hundred than the week before. Whatever was counting on the other side was counting *down*, and it was doing so on a schedule, and schedules, in Juniper's professional experience, implied appointments.

He closed the notebook. He picked up his tea. And that was when, from three floors below, through stone and silence and the ordinary breathing of a sleeping building, he heard the gate's hum change — just once, just briefly — into something that was unmistakably, impossibly, a word.

Not in any language he spoke. But shaped like a name. Shaped, if he were a fanciful man, which he was not, like an invitation.

Or a summons.

Juniper stood in the dark archive with his heart going like a apprentice's hammer and told himself, in the firmest internal voice available, that tomorrow he would report the anomaly through proper channels, to the proper people, and proper people would handle it properly.

Outside, far off over the rooftops, thunder cleared its throat.

The storm was coming in early. And so, though he could not have said how he knew, was everything else.
