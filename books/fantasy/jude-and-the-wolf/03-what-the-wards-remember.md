## 3. What the Wards Remember

The child had been asleep for three days, and Juniper had spent all three of them pretending he wasn't thinking about him.

This was, he would have told anyone who asked, a matter of professional discipline. The Ledger of Small Impossibilities did not balance itself — though in fairness, it never balanced anyway, because the Guild of Impossible Things kept accounts the way a drunkard keeps promises: enthusiastically, and with no regard whatsoever for arithmetic. Tonight's entries included a bottled echo that had begun answering questions before they were asked (Juniper had catalogued it under *E*, then crossed that out and moved it to *Q*, for Querying), a self-repairing teapot that repaired itself into slightly different teapots each time, and an invoice from the Department of Unfinished Business that was, itself, unfinished.

He dipped his pen. He wrote: *Bottled echo, one (1). Disposition: shelf nine, do not ask it anything after dark.*

And then, because he was only human and the house was very quiet, he wrote beneath it, in the small cramped hand he used for things that were not strictly ledger business:

*The boy said "vess-ka-ruun" again in his sleep. Third time. The wards answered.*

He stared at those words for a long moment, then did not cross them out.

That was the trouble, really. The wards had *answered*. Juniper had been keeper of the gate's ledgers for eleven years, and in eleven years he had learned every mood the wards could fall into — the low contented hum of a quiet night, the fretful buzz when a courier tried to bring a grudge through customs, the deep sullen silence they fell into whenever Guildmaster Veyra walked past, which Juniper had always privately attributed to her personality. But he had never heard them *answer* anyone. Wards were walls with opinions, not conversationalists.

Except that on the night the boy arrived, mud-caked and half-drowned and speaking a language no one at the Guildhouse could place, every ward on the gate had flared gold from foundation to finial, like a hearth suddenly remembering fire. And when the boy spoke — those soft, strange syllables that made the hair on Juniper's arms stand up not from fear but from something embarrassingly close to homesickness — the wards had hummed back. In rhythm. In *response*.

Veyra had ordered the boy held for study. "Study" was a word the Guild used the way surgeons used knives: precisely, and with an expectation of blood. The boy had been given a warm room, warm food, and three guards at his door, which was the Guild's idea of kindness and custody occupying the same bed.

Juniper had been the one to bring him the food. This was also discipline, he'd told himself. Someone had to note what the subject ate.

What the subject ate was everything, with the desperate efficiency of a child who had learned that meals were weather — glorious when they came, gone without warning. What the subject said, around mouthfuls of stew, was *vess-ka-ruun*, and *sha-el-ometh*, and a third phrase that Juniper had not written down anywhere because writing it down had made the ink on his page shiver.

*Sha-el-ometh.* He turned the syllables over now, at his desk, in the lamplight. They felt old. Not ancient in the way the Guild meant it — the Guild slapped "ancient" on anything older than the current Guildmaster's grudges — but old the way riverbeds are old. Old underneath everything.

There was exactly one place in the Guildhouse where language like that might live.

*

The Archive of the Covenant occupied the lowest floor of the Guildhouse, below the wine cellar, below the vault where they kept the impossible debts, in a room that most clerks believed was a rumor and most rumors believed was a cautionary tale. Juniper had keys to it because keeping keys was half his job; the other half was pretending he didn't.

The stairs down were narrow and smelled of cold stone and older paper. His lamp threw his shadow up the wall ahead of him, walking a step early, as shadows sometimes did down here, and he chose not to take this personally.

At the bottom stood the door. It was iron-banded oak, and across it, fresh as of four days ago, ran a seal of black wax stamped with Veyra's personal sigil — the closed fist holding the quill. *Sealed by order of the Guildmaster,* the accompanying placard read. *Pending review. No access without direct authorization.*

Four days ago. The same day the boy had arrived.

Juniper stood in front of the door for a long time, doing the arithmetic of his life. Eleven years of service. A clean record. A reputation — mildly joked about, never seriously doubted — as the man who knew where everything was filed and filed himself somewhere in between. He had never once broken a seal. He had never once lied on a ledger. When the Department of Unfinished Business had once offered him a bribe of two perfectly good tomorrows, he had reported it in triplicate.

"Pending review," he said aloud, to the door. "She sealed it the same day. Before anyone had even translated a word of what he says."

The door, being a door, said nothing. But the wards along the gatehouse above — he could feel them even here, faintly, the way you can feel weather through a floor — hummed their low note, and it sounded, tonight, less like a wall and more like a held breath.

Here is what Juniper Thackeray told himself, standing in the cold with his hand already on the wax: that he was not breaking the seal. That he was merely consulting. That a ledger-keeper consulting records relevant to an active entry was not merely within his rights but arguably his *duty*, and that if Veyra disagreed she could take it up with the procedural committee, assuming he survived long enough to file the complaint.

All of which was true, in the way that a map is true. It showed every road except the one you were actually on.

He pressed his thumb against the wax, found the seam, and lifted it whole — because a good clerk does not destroy evidence, he *preserves* it, and because some stubborn corner of him wanted the option of putting it back and pretending this had never happened. The wax came away like a scab. The door swung inward on hinges that had not screamed in sixty years and therefore saw no reason to start.

*

Inside, the Archive of the Covenant was smaller than legend claimed and dustier than nightmare feared. Shelves of ledgers marched off into the dark, their spines lettered in hands that had died centuries ago. At the center of the room, on a reading table of black stone, lay a single book under a cloth, and the cloth was the kind of white that means *nobody has touched this recently or dared*.

Juniper lifted the cloth.

The book beneath was the oldest thing he had ever touched that hadn't tried to bite him. Its cover was grey leather gone soft as skin, and its title had been worked into the material rather than written on it, the way scars are worked into a body: *THE COVENANT OF THE GATE, BEING A TRUE AND FULL ACCOUNT OF THE TERMS BETWEEN THE PEOPLES OF THE NEAR SIDE AND THE KINDRED OF THE FAR.*

*True and full.* Juniper ran his thumb over the words and thought of Veyra's black wax and felt the first cold drop of something that wasn't quite fear yet but knew the way there.

He opened it carefully, past the flyleaf, past the frontispiece showing the gate in its youth — drawn with both doors open, which no living soul had ever seen — and began to read.

The script was archaic but legible, the covenant recorded in the formal trade-tongue of the founding era, with marginalia in at least four other hands. And there, running through the opening invocation like a thread through cloth, were words he knew.

*Vess-ka-ruun.*

He read the phrase in full: *Vess-ka-ruun, sha-el-ometh, kel-varrun iss.* *The gate remembers its keepers; the keepers remember the gate; between them runs the road that is neither door nor wall.*

The boy's words. All three phrases — the ones from his sleep, the ones from his supper, and the third, the shivering one — laid out in the covenant's opening lines like a child reciting a prayer he didn't know he'd memorized.

Juniper sat down on the reading stool rather abruptly. The stool, being Guild property, wobbled.

"He's not speaking a language," Juniper whispered to the empty archive. "He's speaking *this*. He's speaking the covenant."

Which raised a question so large that Juniper's mind, a tidy and well-trained mind, refused to look at it directly and instead busied itself with procedure. Procedure said: copy the relevant passages. Procedure said: document your sources. Procedure said, in a voice that sounded suspiciously like Juniper's own conscience wearing a clerk's collar: *and then explain to me, Juniper, why the Guildmaster sealed the covenant's own founding text on the very day a child arrived who speaks it fluently.*

He took out his notebook. He took out his pen. And he began to copy.

It went slowly, because the old script demanded care and because Juniper kept stopping. The covenant's terms were laid out in numbered clauses, and the clauses were — there was no other word for it — beautiful. Not legally beautiful, though they were that too, tight and unambiguous as knotwork. Beautiful the way vows are beautiful. Clause the First: the gate shall stand open in peace and shut in war, and neither side shall pass it in malice. Clause the Fourth: the kindred of the far side shall ward the gate's outer face, and the peoples of the near side shall keep its inner heart. Clause the Ninth—

Clause the Ninth stopped his pen entirely.

*A keeper shall be given,* it read. *Not taken, not bought, not bred to the purpose. Given. One soul in each generation, freely offered, to hold the gate's heart from within, that the wards may know a living voice and the threshold may know mercy. The giving shall be witnessed by both sides, and the name of the given shall be entered in this book in the giver's own hand.*

Below it, in the margin, in a later hand — a hurried, ugly hand that Juniper's eye disliked on sight — someone had written: *Discontinued by amendment. See renewal of Year 603.*

Year 603. Sixty years ago. The year the last renewal should have happened. The year, if the margin was honest, that the practice of giving keepers had simply... stopped.

Juniper looked at the clause for a long time. Then he looked at the marginal note. Then he did what he always did with documents that contradicted themselves: he reached for the corroborating records.

They weren't there.

He checked twice. The shelf where the renewal records of Year 603 belonged held nothing but a gap — a gap in the dust shaped exactly like a ledger, recently removed, the dust disturbed in a way that said *days, not years*. Somebody had taken it. Recently. Somebody with access, and authority, and a supply of black wax.

The lamp flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft.

Juniper sat in the oldest room in the Guildhouse, holding a stolen hour in one hand and a copied clause in the other, and understood that he had crossed a line whose existence he had honestly believed in until tonight. There was no going back to being the man who filed things. The wax was in his pocket. The clauses were in his notebook. And somewhere upstairs a child slept saying words that the founding fathers of the Guild had written as a vow, and the woman who commanded them all had locked the proof away four days before anyone could have known to lock it.

*She knew he was coming,* thought Juniper, and the thought arrived with the calm certainty of a figure finally balancing. *Or she knew what he was. Either way, she sealed this before we caught up to her.*

He finished copying Clause the Ninth, and the invocation, and the witness formula, and the list of names — the names of the given, generation upon generation, entered in the givers' own hands, until Year 603, where the column of names simply ended, mid-page, like a road washed out.

The last name entered was *Thackeray*.

Juniper read it three times. The ink was brown with age. The hand was careful and clerkish and, God help him, familiar — he had seen that same careful clerkish hand in the family letters his grandmother kept in a tin, the ones nobody read aloud.

His own name, in another century.

He closed the notebook. He replaced the cloth. He set the black wax back on the door and pressed it down with his thumb, and if the seal sat a little crooked afterward, well — seals age. Everything ages. That was rather the point of archives.

He climbed the stairs with his lamp held steady and his heart doing anything but, and behind him the archive door clicked shut on the smell of old paper and the ending column of names, and above him, faint through six floors of stone, the wards were humming.

Not their usual note. Something with more shape to it. Almost, if a tired clerk let himself imagine things, like a voice practicing words it hadn't spoken in sixty years.

Almost like it was counting.

Juniper reached his desk, sat down, and opened his notebook to check his copies against memory. They were good copies. Clean, accurate, damning.

There was only one problem, and it sat at the bottom of the last page in his own handwriting, where exhaustion had betrayed him: he had transcribed the final line of the witness formula twice, and the second time, without meaning to, without noticing, he had added three syllables that appeared nowhere in the original book.

Three syllables he had never read.

Three syllables he had heard only once before — murmured in a sleeping child's voice, two nights ago, through a wall.

*vess-ka-ruun.*

Juniper looked at the word for a long time. Then, very quietly, in the empty office, he began to laugh — the small unhinged laugh of a man whose ledgers have just informed him that the numbers are keeping themselves now.

"Well," he said to the notebook, to the wards, to the listening dark. "That's going in under *T*. For Trouble."

Somewhere above him, the gate hummed agreement — and far off across the city, out past the walls, past the river, out where the pinewoods began, something answered.
