## 5. The Guildmaster's Summons

The summons came at dawn, which was the first bad sign.

Guildhouse summonses came in three varieties, and Juniper had catalogued them the way he catalogued everything: in ink, with dates. The blue slip meant a routine query — *Ledger-keeper Thackeray, please reconcile the discrepancy in the bottled echoes inventory* — and could be answered by return of post or, more honestly, ignored until it went away. The white slip meant an audit, which was unpleasant but survivable if one had kept one's margins tidy. Juniper kept his margins very tidy.

The black slip meant come to the Guildmaster's office *now*, and it did not even pretend to be a request.

He found it folded under his door when he rose, still damp from the night fog, smelling faintly of Veyra's preferred ink — the expensive iron-gall stuff she imported from Corvess that ate through cheap nibs and, rumor held, through the careers of junior clerks. He stood there in his nightshirt holding it for a long moment while the house woke around him: boots on stairs, the clatter of the breakfast room, someone upstairs arguing with a grimoire that did not want to be argued with.

*She knows,* said the part of his mind that had spent two sleepless nights rehearsing for exactly this moment. *She has always known. She knew before you did.*

*Or,* said the other part, the one he was trying very hard to feed instead, *she suspects pages are missing from the sealed annex and has no idea who took them, because why would she suspect you? You balance accounts. You alphabetize. You once filed a formal complaint about a filing error.*

Both parts were lying, probably. That was the trouble with secrets; they got into everything, like ink into a nib, staining whatever they touched.

He dressed carefully. Not his best coat — that would look like ambition — and not his worst — that would look like guilt — but the middle one, the grey-brown one with the ink stain on the left cuff that had become, over nine years, something of a signature. Ledger-keepers of the Guild of Impossible Things were supposed to be unremarkable. It was practically a job requirement. He looked at himself in the little mirror above the washstand and practiced a face: attentive, slightly tired, entirely forthcoming.

The face in the mirror looked like a man about to lie to the most dangerous person in the free cities.

"Well," Juniper told it quietly, "we'll both have to do better than that."

* * *

Veyra's office occupied the top of the gatehouse tower, which everyone understood to be a statement. The Guildmaster of the Guild of Impossible Things sat directly above the gate itself, as though proximity to the impossible were a form of authority — and perhaps it was. The room was spare almost to severity: one desk of dark ashwood, older than the Guild charter; two chairs, one comfortable and one not, and Juniper had never once been offered the comfortable one; and a single high window through which, on clear mornings, you could see the whole sprawl of the free cities going up in chimney smoke like a prayer nobody had quite finished writing.

This morning was not clear. Rain still hung around, sulking, and the light through the window was the color of pewter.

"Thackeray." Veyra did not look up from the papers spread before her. "Sit."

Juniper sat. In the uncomfortable chair. Naturally.

He allowed himself the luxury of studying her while her pen scratched. Guildmaster Veyra was fifty-some years old and built like the answer to a question no one wanted asked twice — tall, spare, silver going to iron at the temples, with hands that had clearly done work before they'd learned to sign things. She had been kind to him once, years ago, when he'd been a raw apprentice who'd fainted during his first ward-inspection, and she had never mentioned it since, which was its own peculiar kindness. He had liked her for a long time. He had trusted her for most of that time. Both facts now sat in his chest like swallowed stones.

"Three nights ago," she said, still writing, "the gate wards flared. Full recognition pattern. Do you know what that means, Thackeray?"

"The wards recognized something," Juniper said. "Or someone."

"Mm." Pen scratch. Scratch. "And what do we hold, currently, that might be worth recognizing?"

"A boy," Juniper said. "Approximately eight years old. Feral presentation, mud-caked, half-starved. Speaking—" he let the pause hang just long enough to seem reluctant, "—something the linguists can't place. They've been calling it a private language. Trauma-induced, they think."

"And you?"

"I think it's older than trauma." Careful. Careful. He had decided on the walk over how much truth to spend, and truth, he knew, was like coin: spend too much at once and people wondered where you'd gotten it. "I've heard cadences like it in the oldest ledgers. Annex material. Pre-charter."

That, at least, was true, and it was also exactly the wrong thing to say, and he knew it, and he said it anyway, because Veyra rewarded honesty the way hunters rewarded game that walked toward them.

Her pen stopped.

For the first time she looked up, and her eyes were the flat grey of the morning, and they went through him like an auditor through a fraudulent account.

"Annex material," she repeated. "Pre-charter. Interesting. Because I've spent the last two days trying to establish precisely who has been into the sealed annex, Thackeray, and my records show that the key log was altered. Altered *neatly*, I'll grant you — the hand is good, the dates are consistent — but the wax seal on the covenant folio was re-set with the wrong grade of resin. Someone borrowed those pages and put them back imperfectly. Someone who is usually perfect."

The rain ticked against the window. Somewhere below them, muffled by three floors of stone, the gate hummed its low out-of-rhythm hum, the note it had been holding wrong since the night the child arrived, like an instrument waiting for the rest of the orchestra.

"I wouldn't know anything about that, Guildmaster," Juniper said.

And there it was. The lie. It left his mouth smoothly, in a reasonable voice, with a reasonable face attached, and it tasted — he had expected fire, brimstone, some dramatic flavor — like nothing at all. Like plain bread. That was the horrifying part: how easy it was. Nine years of honest bookkeeping, and the first falsehood of his career slid out polished.

Veyra watched him for a long moment. Then, to his considerable surprise, she smiled. It was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a woman moving a piece on a board.

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "Of course you wouldn't."

She set down her pen, aligned it with the edge of the blotter — a habit he'd seen her perform before decisions, large and small, the way some people cracked their knuckles — and leaned back.

"Let us speak hypothetically," she said. "Hypothetically, a keeper of our ledgers discovered that certain pages of the original covenant had been removed from general access. Hypothetically, he read them. What would he have found there?"

"That depends which pages," Juniper said, and cursed himself inwardly for the dodge even as he made it.

"Try me."

"Hypothetically?" He kept his voice dry, clerical, the voice of a man discussing hypotheticals. "Terms of renewal. Obligations. A schedule of keeping. The old ledgers reference a renewal every sixty years — a rite, a confirmation, the covenant reaffirmed by both parties. But the annex copies show..." He hesitated. This much he could give her, because this much she surely already knew, and refusing to say it would tell her far more than saying it. "The last renewal entry is dated, signed, witnessed. And the hand is wrong. It's a copyist's forgery laid over a blank year. Sixty years ago, somebody wrote a renewal that never happened."

Something moved behind Veyra's eyes — quick, controlled, gone — but Juniper had spent nine years reading marginalia and fine print and the small evasions of men hiding debts, and he caught it. Not shock. *Recognition.* She had known. She had known before he had.

"You've been busy," she said softly.

"I keep the ledgers," Juniper said. "Busy is the job description."

That startled an actual laugh out of her — short, rusty, genuine — and for one absurd moment they were almost the old Veyra and the old Juniper, two professionals appreciating a decent joke in a drafty tower. Then it passed, the way weather passes, and left the same cold air behind.

"Here is what happens next," Veyra said, and her voice changed register entirely — dropped its interrogation-sharpness and became something quieter, almost confiding, which was infinitely worse. "You are being promoted. Effective immediately. Warden of the Inner Archive, with access to everything, including the sealed folios, including the gate mechanisms themselves. Rooms in the east wing. A stipend that will let you stop counting your own candles, Thackeray, which I know you do, because you're the sort of man who counts his candles."

"I don't count my candles," Juniper said.

"You counted them just now, in your head, when I said it."

"...Fourteen," he admitted.

"There." Almost fond. "In exchange, the annex matter closes. Whatever pages you read, you read in the course of legitimate archival research, and whatever irregularities exist in the historical record are historical, and stay buried where history keeps its mistakes. You will not speak of the child's language to the linguists. You will not speak of the covenant to anyone. And in six months, when this storm has blown through, you will be the youngest warden this Guild has elevated in a century, and you will have earned it honestly, because you'll believe you did."

"And if I decline?"

"Then you remain a ledger-keeper," Veyra said, "and ledger-keepers who gossip about matters above their clearance tend to find themselves transferred. There's a waystation in the Saltmarshes that needs someone. Very quiet. Very far away." She let that sit. "I would rather not send you there, Juniper. I'm offering you a future. Take it."

He should have taken it. Every sensible cell in his body lined up and voted for taking it. The promotion was real, the danger was real, the Saltmarshes were *very real*, and somewhere beyond the free cities — this was the thing she hadn't said yet, but he could feel it coiling under her words like wire under snow — something worse than either of them was coming.

Instead he heard himself ask: "Why does it matter? The child's language. Why seal the folios, why gag the linguists, over one mud-covered boy?"

Veyra rose. She crossed to the window and stood looking out at the smoke-grey city, hands clasped behind her, and when she spoke again her voice had the flatness of a report delivered to a superior.

"Because there are armies in the passes," she said. "You've seen the supply requisitions cross your desk; don't pretend you haven't noticed the grain movements north. The usurper has unified the war-bands beyond the wall — all of them, Thackeray, for the first time since the covenant was struck — and he is marching them south in spring. The Council dithers. The free cities bicker about tariffs while a war-host gathers that could burn every one of these pretty rooftops by midsummer." She turned. "Except the usurper wants something else first. Something the gate has. And that boy arrived at our door speaking the First Tongue with the wards singing recognition, and either he is a coincidence — and I stopped believing in coincidences the year I made Guildmaster — or he is the single most valuable object between here and the wall. One of those two things is true. Either way, he is not a child anymore. He is an asset. Assets get secured. That is the world, Juniper. I didn't make it. I'm just trying to keep the roof on while it rains."

"The covenant says—"

"The covenant is sixty years stale and built on a forgery," Veyra cut in, coldly precise, "by a man whose name you'd find in your own family tree if you dug deep enough. So spare me the sermon about sacred obligations. The only question before this Guild is whether the gate's power belongs to dead words or living people. I intend it to belong to living people." She returned to her desk, and the interview's shape closed around him like a ledger snapping shut. "Warden Thackeray. Your answer."

Juniper looked at her across the dark ashwood desk, at the expensive ink and the aligned pen and the two chairs, one comfortable and one not, and he thought about Ash — asleep right now in the infirmary ward, small and hollow-eyed, clutching a wooden wolf some kind soul had given him, murmuring syllables in a language older than every army on the march. An asset. Secured.

His fear was still there. It hadn't gone anywhere; it sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone alongside the liking and the trust. But fear, he discovered, in the strange arithmetic of that grey morning, was not the opposite of resolve. Fear was simply resolve that hadn't been given a job yet.

He gave it one.

"I accept," Juniper said.

Veyra inclined her head, satisfied, already reaching for the paperwork.

"With conditions," he added.

Her hand paused. Her eyebrows invited him to continue, in the tone of a woman who had just been handed an invoice she hadn't ordered.

"I want unrestricted visitation with the boy," Juniper said. "As warden. If he's to be studied, assessed, secured — whatever word we're using — then someone who understands fragments of his language should be in the room. For accuracy. You bought my silence, Guildmaster; I'm selling you my competence along with it. Consider it a package rate."

A long silence. Rain. The gate humming its wrong note below them, patient as debt.

"Clever," Veyra said at last, and there was something in her voice that might have been respect, and might have been regret, and might have been both wearing each other's coats. "Very well. Visitation granted. Daily, supervised. Congratulations on your elevation, Warden." She slid the commission paper across the desk. "Sign there."

Juniper signed. His hand did not shake, which surprised him. Below his name, in the space marked *effective date*, the fresh ink gleamed wetly, and he thought: *this is the second thing I've forged today.*

* * *

He told himself he would go straight to his new office in the east wing. He told himself he would begin the transfer of files, orderly, methodical, the picture of a man ascending honestly. He told himself several such things on the stairs, going down past the breakfast room, past the argumentative grimoire, out into the wet courtyard where the morning mist was lifting off the flagstones in slow grey ribbons.

Then he walked to the infirmary instead.

Ash was awake, sitting up in the narrow cot with the blanket pooled around him like a moat, and when Juniper appeared in the doorway the boy's face did the thing it always did — the wary flicker, the calculation, the small wild animal deciding whether the approaching shape was threat or weather. Then Juniper raised one hand and shaped the greeting-sign the child used, the one from the annex pages, the one meaning *safe ground*, and the wariness melted so completely and so quickly that it hurt to watch, like watching frost give up all at once in the sun.

"*Emmeth*," Ash said. Safe. Home-ish. The word didn't have a proper Common translation; the closest Juniper had found was *the place where the counting stops.*

"Not yet," Juniper answered in the First Tongue, clumsy, careful, each syllable lifted from the old folios and handled like something breakable. "But maybe someday. Maybe soon."

Behind him, at the end of the corridor, the assigned supervisor cleared her throat and pretended to study a notice board.

Juniper sat down on the edge of the cot, produced from his coat the thing he had spent half the night making — a scrap of good paper ruled in careful columns, headed in two scripts, *Common* on the left and First Tongue on the right, the first lesson plan any Guild archivist had ever drafted in that language in sixty years — and Ash leaned forward, eyes going wide and bright and hungry, and pointed at the first drawn word, and asked its name.

Outside, the rain had finally stopped. The gate, three floors and a courtyard away, hummed its long held wrong note — waiting, counting, patient as anything written down and owed.

Juniper took up the chalk. "Well then," he said. "Lesson one."
