## 6. First Tongue, First Lessons

The Guildhouse had a room for everything, which was the same as saying it had a room for nothing in particular, and Juniper had been given the latter.

It sat at the end of the east corridor, past the aviary of carrier moths and the closet where someone had once bottled a Tuesday that refused to end. It had a bed with a frame of honest oak, a window that looked out on the herb garden, and — because the Guild could never resist decorating even its prison cells — a shelf of toys that had been confiscated from various impossible children over the last century. A wooden wolf with one ear. A puzzle box that solved itself when it got bored. A tin soldier who had marched off some long-ago battlefield and never quite stopped.

Ash sat in the middle of the floor with all three arranged before him like an audience, and Juniper sat on the floor too, because you could not teach a child anything important from a chair.

"Again," Juniper said. "Slowly."

Ash looked at him. In the grey morning light his eyes were the color of river ice over dark water, and when he spoke, the words came out in the First Tongue — that old, old language that predated the Guild, predated the free cities, predated, if the oldest ledgers were to be believed, the habit of writing things down at all. It sounded like wind deciding to be speech. It sounded like something a person's bones remembered even when their mind did not.

Juniper caught perhaps one word in five. He was getting better. That was the trouble.

*Haleth*, Ash said, touching his own chest. Then he touched Juniper's chest, expectant.

"Juniper," he said. "Ju-ni-per." He tapped his own sternum on each syllable, and Ash watched with the fierce concentration of a scholar disarming a trap.

"Ju-ni-per," Ash repeated, mangling it beautifully. "Joo... nipper." He frowned, tried again. "Nipper."

"Close enough," Juniper said, and Ash's whole face lit up, and Juniper felt the crack in his duty widen by another hair's breadth.

Because here was the thing he had not told anyone: the Guild wanted Ash studied, cataloged, and eventually *explained*. There was already a form for it. Juniper had seen it on Veyra's desk — Form 114-C, Acquisition of Anomalous Persons, with a column for *provenance* and a column for *disposition*, and both columns waiting to be filled in ink that would decide the rest of a small boy's life.

And here was the other thing he had not told anyone: every night since the storm, Juniper had lain awake translating fragments in his head, and the fragments were arranging themselves into something that looked less like a riddle and more like a sentence. A long sentence. A covenant-shaped sentence.

But that was for later. Right now there was breakfast to negotiate.

* * *

Meals had become their first lesson, mostly because Ash regarded Guild food with the deep suspicion of someone raised on better bread. The kitchens sent up porridge; Ash looked at the porridge as though it had personally wronged him.

"This," Juniper said, tapping the bowl, "*porridge.*"

"*Porridge,*" Ash agreed solemnly, and then pushed it away.

"No — you're supposed to eat it."

"You eat it," Ash said in the First Tongue, with the impeccable logic of the very young, and pushed the bowl across the table.

Juniper ate a spoonful to demonstrate. Ash watched this betrayal of solidarity with visible disappointment, then picked up his own spoon and ate with grim heroism, maintaining eye contact the entire time as if to say *I am doing this under protest and history will remember whose fault it was.*

"You're going to be insufferable when you can argue properly," Juniper told him.

"I am already insufferable," Ash replied — or so Juniper half-understood; the grammar of the First Tongue wrapped around insults like ivy, and he might have been called a turnip. He chose not to ask.

It was during the third such breakfast that Ash said the word that stopped everything.

He had been naming things, pointing around the kitchen: *bread, cup, window, fire*. Standard curriculum. Then his finger came to rest on the wall — the inner wall, the one that ran toward the gate chamber at the heart of the Guildhouse — and he said a word Juniper knew. Knew it the way you know a scar.

*Vael.*

Gate. But not just gate. When Ash said it, he laid his palm flat against the stone, and hummed — actually hummed, two low notes — and the wards in the wall answered. Faintly. Like a dog hearing its name from another room.

Juniper set down his spoon very carefully.

"What did you just do?"

Ash looked at him with mild surprise, as if the answer were obvious. *I greeted it,* he said. *You don't greet your own house?*

*Your own house.* Juniper turned the phrase over. The child had walked out of a storm out of nowhere, mud to the eyebrows, and spoken about the gate the way farm children spoke about a grandmother's cottage.

"Ash." He kept his voice light, the way you keep your voice light over thin ice. "Where is your house?"

The boy pointed — not at the wall, but *through* it. Through the stone, through the wards, through whatever distance lay on the far side of the gate. Then he folded his hands in his lap and began, patiently, the way you tell a slow friend a story they should already know, to explain.

* * *

It took three days.

Not because Ash was unwilling — he talked the way rivers run, constantly and without apparent effort — but because Juniper kept having to stop and write things down, and because half the vocabulary he needed didn't exist in any ledger he was allowed to open. He smuggled the oldest covenant folio out of the sealed archive in installments, a few pages at a time inside the false bottom of his ledger-satchel, copying phrases by candlelight and matching them against what Ash said, and the match was so exact it made his hands shake.

Here is what he assembled, sitting alone at night while the Guildhouse slept:

There was a village beyond the gate. There had always been a village beyond the gate — that was the part the ledgers skipped, the part Veyra's seals hid. The covenant was not between men and wolves in the abstract. It was between the gatekeepers' line and a people who lived within sight of the door, tending it from the other side as the Thackerays tended it from this one.

And once each generation, the covenant asked for a keeper.

*A life freely given,* the old text said, in the formal hand of the first Thackeray. *One soul to sit within the threshold, alive and willing, to hold the door true between the seasons of the world.*

Juniper had read that passage eleven times, telling himself it meant something gentle. A blessing. A vigil. A year of service, perhaps, like the Guild's own novitiates.

Then Ash, on the third evening, drawing in spilled porridge on the table because paper was for grown-ups, drew a picture that ended the argument forever.

A doorway. A small figure standing in it. Around the figure, a ring of marks — the same marks Juniper had copied from the covenant folio, the binding-script, the *holding* signs. And the figure in the doorway had no exit drawn. No path leading away. Just the doorframe, and the child inside it, and all around the edges little stick-wolves standing watch, and above them a sun and moon sharing the same sky, which in the First Tongue's picture-language meant *always*.

"Ash," Juniper said. His voice came out strange. "This is you?"

The boy nodded, pleased. He pointed at the figure. *Me.* He pointed through the window, toward the gate. *Soon.* And then he smiled — smiled, warm and unafraid, with the absolute peace of a child describing a festival — and said the sentence Juniper would carry the rest of his life:

*I was chosen. My mother wept, but I was glad. Nobody goes unwilling. That's how the door stays kind.*

*Nobody goes unwilling.*

Juniper excused himself, walked calmly down the corridor, locked himself in the records closet, and put his head in his hands among the bottled echoes until his breathing steadied.

They had told him he was honored. They had taught him to be glad. Somewhere beyond the gate, a mother had wept and let him go, and a village had dressed it in ceremony until a six-year-old could smile about walking into a doorway he would never walk out of — and the worst of it, the part that made Juniper's stomach turn over, was that according to the covenant, they hadn't lied to him. *Freely given.* The old words were probably true to the letter. The wolves had likely kept every promise ever made under that treaty.

It was men who had broken faith. It was always men. And now the debt had come due, and it had arrived at his door in the shape of a small boy who thought he was going somewhere wonderful.

* * *

"You've been spending a great deal of time with the subject."

Veyra found him in the ledger hall that evening, materializing the way she always did, soundless on carpets she'd probably chosen for exactly that quality. She looked at the translation notes spread across his desk — he'd had time to sweep the covenant pages into the satchel, but the word-lists were innocent enough — and her gaze lingered a moment too long.

"He's learning mine faster than I'm learning his," Juniper said. "Children are like that."

"Mm." Veyra picked up a sheet. *Porridge. Bread. Door. Always.* Her eyebrow rose a fraction. "An interesting vocabulary for a prisoner."

"For a guest. The forms haven't decided yet."

"The forms will decide soon enough." She set the page down and aligned its corner with the desk edge, a small precise cruelty. "The usurper's armies are moving, Thackeray. Whatever that child is, the Guild will need to understand it quickly. See that your lessons serve the Guild's purposes and not... your own sentiment."

"Of course, Guildmaster."

She left. Juniper sat in the lamplight and listened to his own heartbeat, and understood that he had just lied by omission to the most dangerous woman in the free cities, and that he would do it again tomorrow, and the day after, and that each lie was another plank in a bridge he was building toward a place he couldn't yet see.

* * *

That night he brought Ash the wooden wolf with one ear.

The boy took it with reverence, turning it over, finding the worn places where other hands had held it. *Whose was this?*

"Some impossible child before you," Juniper said. "They all go home eventually. Or somewhere."

Ash hugged the wolf to his chest and looked at Juniper with those ice-over-dark-water eyes, and said, in his careful new Common Tongue, the first full sentence he'd ever built without help:

"You are sad, Nipper."

Juniper laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him — and then found, to his horror, that it wanted to turn into something else entirely.

"A little," he admitted. "It's nothing a good ledger can't fix."

Ash considered this. Then he patted the floor beside him, an invitation, and Juniper sat down on the cold stones next to a feral child and a one-eared wooden wolf, and let the boy lean against his shoulder, and thought: *I will burn the Guild down before I let them put you in that doorway.*

It was not a thought a gatekeeper should have. It fit him perfectly.

He was still awake when he heard it — faint, from the direction of the gate chamber, threading through the walls like smoke: the wards, humming. And beneath their rhythm, unmistakable now that he knew to listen, something answering from the far side.

Counting. Patient as winter.

As if whatever waited beyond the door had finally run out of patience for the generations of men who'd let the account fall into arrears — and had begun, softly, to call in the debt.
