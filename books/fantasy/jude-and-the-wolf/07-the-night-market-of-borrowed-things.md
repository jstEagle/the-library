## 7. The Night Market of Borrowed Things

The free cities did not sleep so much as change their clothes.

Sable had learned this on her first night among them, three days after the river crossing, when she had stood on a bridge in the dark and watched the lamplighters work their way down the waterfront like slow, patient fireflies. By day the city of Vell was all commerce and argument — carts, tariffs, the eternal negotiation of awnings with the wind. But at night it put on its other face, and that face belonged to the Night Market of Borrowed Things.

She smelled it before she saw it: woodsmoke and frying dough, wet wool, tallow, and beneath it all something older and stranger — the scent of things that had been handled by too many hands. The market lived in a sunken square behind the chandlers' row, a bowl of cobbles ringed with stalls whose owners never quite showed their faces to the same customer twice.

Here you could buy anything, provided you understood the currency.

Coin worked for bread, for rope, for a bed in a loft above a cooper's yard where the cooper's wife pretended not to notice that her lodger left before dawn each morning and returned smelling faintly of pine forest and other people's blood. But for the things Sable needed — names, records, the whereabouts of men who owed favors to wolf-kind — coin was useless. The Night Market traded in weightier stuff. Years. Secrets. And most commonly, memories.

"You smell like the far side of a war," said the memory-broker, who kept the stall at the bottom of the steps and had, as far as Sable could tell, no name at all. He was a small man wrapped in a coat made of what appeared to be forty smaller coats, and his eyes were the color of tea steeped too long. "Sit. You're making my customers nervous. They can tell you'd take the whole stall apart if I gave you cause."

"I would," Sable agreed, and sat on the offered stool. It creaked in a way she chose to interpret as respectful.

Around them the market murmured. A woman two stalls down was selling the taste of a summer afternoon to a homesick sailor; Sable could see the sailor's face go soft and stupid with longing, and she looked away, because there were some hungers it was rude to watch.

"I need records," she said. "Guild records. The covenant renewal ledgers — specifically the last one. Sixty years back."

The broker's teacup eyes went carefully blank, which was itself an answer.

"Ah," he said. "You want the thing everyone wants and nobody has."

"Nobody has?"

"Nobody *has*," he repeated, with the particular relish brokers reserved for bad news delivered well. "I'll tell you what happened, warden, because you have an honest face — well, an honest *nose*, which is nearly as good. Three months ago, a buyer came through. Guild colors under a merchant's cloak, but I've sold memories to enough clerks to know the walk. She bought every record touching the last renewal. Every one. From me, from the archivists' stalls, from old Maren up the hill who keeps the temple duplicates, from the debt-collectors who trade in confession-memories. Everything. Paid clean and paid dear, and asked no questions afterward, which is how you know someone is buying to bury rather than buying to learn."

Sable felt something cold settle behind her ribs. "Describe her."

"Tall. Gray at the temples though she can't be past fifty. Hands like she's used them for more than signing." The broker shrugged. "I don't deal in faces. But she haggled like a woman who resents every coin she spends, and she counted the records twice before leaving, and she thanked no one. A Guildmaster, if I ever met one."

Veyra. Of course it was Veyra. Sable sat with that for a moment, watching the lamplight gutter in its jar between them. If the Guildmaster had spent the season buying up every scrap of paper concerning the last covenant renewal, then either she feared what those papers proved or she needed them gone before anyone else read them. Both possibilities pointed the same direction: toward a gate running on lies, and a child caught in the machinery.

"And nothing survived?" she asked. "Sixty years of paperwork, and not one sheet?"

"Oh, sheets survive. Sheets always survive." The broker leaned forward, lowering his voice into the register merchants used for genuine bargains. "But they survive in *pieces*, and pieces cost more than wholes. You want the renewal ledger? I have a fragment. Four lines, maybe five, torn from the certification page. Bought it off a Guild wastepaper-man two months ago, before I knew anyone cared." He held up a finger. "It'll cost you."

"What do you want?"

"A memory. A good one. Not a trauma — traumas are cheap and I have a cellar full. Something warm. Something *yours*."

Sable had known this moment would come. She had rehearsed it on the walk down, cataloging her memories like a quartermaster taking inventory: which ones she could lose and remain herself, which ones were load-bearing. There was the memory of her mother's voice teaching her the First Tongue — load-bearing, untouchable. The memory of her first hunt alone — load-bearing. The memory of the oath-oath, the one older than either nation, the one that bound her to a gatekeeper she hadn't yet met when it was spoken —

That one she examined for a long moment and set aside, because something about it had been itching at her since the river, and she wanted to keep the itch.

What remained was smaller. Softer. A winter evening, years ago, in the den-country beyond the gate. Her pack around a fire — real fire, which wolves rarely bothered with but her grandmother had loved — roasting roots nobody liked and eating them anyway out of love for the cook. Her youngest cousin asleep against her shoulder, drooling on her best fur. The specific quality of the laughter that night, low and rumbling, the pack-sound that meant *safe, safe, all of us, safe.*

She could lose that. She would still know it had happened. She just wouldn't be able to visit.

"The winter fire," she said. "My grandmother's den. The whole evening."

The broker's eyes went almost tender. "A good one," he agreed. "You drive a hard bargain against yourself, warden. Most people offer me their worst and hope I refuse."

"I don't hope you refuse."

"No," he said. "You wouldn't." He produced a small glass vial, no bigger than a thumb, and set it on the counter between them. "Hand on the vial. Think of it whole. Let it go when I count three. You'll feel a tug. Don't fight it; fighting is how people leave pieces of themselves in the wrong jars."

Sable put her hand over the vial. The fire came easily — it always did, lately, when everything else was pursuit and hunger and the strange new weight of a mud-caked boy who spoke the First Tongue and called her *Pack-who-runs-ahead*. She let the evening build itself: the smoke, the roots, the drool, the laughter like distant thunder that meant no storm.

"One," said the broker. "Two."

"Sable?"

She knew the voice. That was the impossible part — she knew the voice, warm and dry and slightly apologetic, the voice of a man reading aloud from a ledger he found embarrassing. Juniper Thackeray's voice, saying her name, in a memory where he had never been.

She opened her eyes. The market was still there. The broker was still counting. But layered over the winter fire, unbidden, unwelcome, unmistakably *new*, was another evening entirely: the rooftop above the chandlers' row, two nights ago, sharing stolen bread with Juniper while Ash slept between them. Juniper laughing — actually laughing, startled out of himself — at her impression of a Guild clerk trying to look dignified while climbing a drainpipe. The bread was stale. The company was impossible. She had thought, with a clarity that had frightened her: *oh. Oh, this is what the oath meant.*

"That's not the memory I'm selling," she said sharply.

"No," the broker agreed, watching her with his over-steeped eyes. "That one's growing in on its own. They do that sometimes, when a person's making new ones faster than they're spending old ones." He smiled, and it was almost kind. "Three."

The tug came. Sable let it.

It didn't hurt. That was the cruel trick of it — it felt like exhaling after holding your breath too long, a lightness that masqueraded as relief. When it finished, she still remembered that a fire had happened once, that roots had been roasted, that she had been loved in a den beyond the gate. She simply couldn't reach it anymore. It was a room in a house she no longer owned.

The broker stoppered the vial, labeled it in a script that moved slightly, and tucked it away. Then he slid the ledger fragment across the counter: four lines of cramped Guild cipher on paper gone amber with age.

Sable read them twice. Her First Tongue was fluent; her Guild-cipher was functional, learned from Juniper's patient coaching over stolen meals. The fragment was a certification page — the formal witness block where a renewal must be signed by both parties, human keeper and wolf-warden, in person, together.

Line one: the human keeper's mark. Present.

Line two: the date. Present.

Line three: the wolf-warden's mark.

Line four: the attestation — *signed in the presence of both covenant parties, at the gate, in accord.*

Both line three and line four were written in the same hand as lines one and two. One hand. One signature pretending to be two. A renewal certified sixty years ago by a single liar, with no wolf within a hundred miles.

"They never came," Sable said quietly. "The wolves were never there. The whole renewal is forged."

"Or the wolf couldn't attend, and someone improvised," the broker said, with a shrug that refused to commit. "Paper forgives easier than gates do. Though I'll note — professional interest — that whoever forged it was sloppy about the ink. Two different inks, same page. Forgers usually match their inks. Unless, of course—" his teacup eyes glinted, "—the second ink was already on the page, and our forger wrote *around* it."

Sable went very still. "Say that again."

"If the attestation line was written first — genuinely, properly, by a real wolf-warden at a real renewal — then someone later added the keeper's mark beside it. Or vice versa. Either way, half that page might be true." He tapped the fragment. "Half-truths are worth double in this market, warden. Everyone wants the whole lie or the whole truth, and almost nobody checks whether the truth got cut in half."

She paid him nothing further — the memory had covered it — but she lingered, because brokers liked to talk once they'd talked, and information followed conversation like gulls followed fishing boats.

"Who was the keeper sixty years ago?" she asked.

"Thackeray," said the broker. "Aldous Thackeray. Dead these twenty years. His grandson keeps your Guild's ledgers now, I'm told." He smiled thinly. "Small world, the world of paperwork. Everyone in it is related."

Sable climbed the steps out of the market bowl feeling lighter and heavier at once — lighter by one winter evening, heavier by one enormous suspicion. Aldous Thackeray. Juniper's grandfather, or great-grandfather — she'd heard Juniper complain about the family ledger-keeping tradition often enough to know the name carried weight in his mouth, the weight of expectation and mild resentment. What if it carried guilt too?

She was halfway across the square when she heard her name again — the real version this time, spoken aloud, breathless.

"Sable! Sable, wait—"

Juniper came pelting around the chandlers' stalls with his coat flapping and his hair doing what it always did, which was losing an argument with gravity. Ash rode on his shoulders, gripping two fistfuls of collar and surveying the market with the imperial disdain of a small emperor reviewing troops. Behind them trotted the cooper's wife's dog, who had adopted the fugitives on principle and without permission.

"You shouldn't be here," Sable said. "This market sees everything. If Veyra has watchers—"

"She doesn't watch markets. Markets make her itch. She told me once she distrusts any economy based on feelings." Juniper arrived, bent over, hands on knees, breathing like a man who had run six streets — which he had. "We ran into one of my old clerks at the docks. Marta. She slipped me a note before her escort dragged her off. Sable — she says Veyra's bought up every record of the last renewal. Every archive, every duplicate, every private collection. She's spending a fortune erasing sixty-year-old paperwork."

"I know," Sable said. "I just paid a winter evening to learn the same thing."

Juniper blinked. "You *traded a memory?*" His voice cracked upward with genuine alarm. "Which one? Are you — do you still — Sable, that's *you*, those are yours—"

"It was one evening. I have others." She said it lightly and watched him not believe her, and something about his stricken face — this man she'd known four days, this ledger-keeper turned fugitive who worried about her memories like a mother hen worrying about eggs — loosened something in her chest that had been tight since the fire left. "Besides," she added, "the market gave me change."

She held out the fragment.

Juniper took it with the reverence of a priest handling relics. He read it once in silence. Then he read it again, and Sable saw the exact moment the meaning landed, because his face did a complicated thing — horror and recognition wrestling, and recognition winning.

"This is the certification page," he whispered. "This is *my office's* certification page. The renewal of sixty years ago. And the marks—" his finger trembled slightly over line three, "—one hand. Sable, one hand signed for both sides. The renewal never happened. The covenant's been running on a forgery for sixty years, and the forgery was filed by—" He stopped. Went pale in the lamplight. "By Aldous Thackeray."

"Your grandfather's father?"

"My grandfather. Grandfather Aldous. The man who taught me cipher." He laughed, but it was the wrong kind of laugh, thin and brittle. "He taught me to check inks. He *specifically* taught me to check inks. Do you think that was a lesson or a confession?"

Ash, from his perch, said something in the First Tongue — a short phrase, questioning. Juniper translated automatically, his translator's mind clearly grateful for the interruption: "*Why does the small keeper shake?*"

"Because I just found out my family may have broken the world," Juniper said.

Ash considered this, then patted Juniper's head twice, which in the First Tongue's gesture-language meant roughly *grief acknowledged, continue functioning.* Sable had begun to suspect the child's language was better designed than any human tongue.

"There's more," she said, and told Juniper what the broker had said about the inks — about the possibility that half the page was true, that a genuine attestation might lie underneath the forgery, that somewhere there might exist a document with the true renewal buried inside the false one.

Juniper stared at her. "If the original attestation exists — if a real wolf-warden really did sign a real renewal — then the terms of the true covenant are sitting in some archive right now, and Veyra hasn't found them yet, because if she had—" He swallowed. "If she had, she'd have burned them, not bought them. Buying means she's still *searching*."

"Then we search faster."

"We need the archives. The deep stacks, the ones under the Guildhouse—" He stopped again, and this time Sable watched the idea arrive like weather. "Sable. My office. My *office*." He was already turning, already walking faster, the dog trotting after, Ash swaying like a small crowned figurehead. "I copy everything. I've copied everything since I was an apprentice — Grandfather trained the habit into me, *never trust the file, trust your own hand*. Every ledger that crossed my desk, I kept a fair-copy. In duplicate. In secret. Including—" his voice rose with something between terror and joy, "—including the renewal records. All of them. The complete file Veyra thinks she just erased."

Sable caught up to him at the edge of the square, where the lamplight ended and the ordinary sleeping city began. "Juniper. Are you telling me that while Veyra spent a fortune buying up every record in the free cities—"

"—she missed the one archive that matters, because it lives in a runaway clerk's spare shirt and a locked box under a cooper's floor." He grinned at her, wild and exhausted and lit from within. "I have duplicates, Sable. I have *all* the duplicates."

Above them, on a rooftop across the street, something shifted — a shadow detaching from shadow, brief and deliberate, gone before the eye could fix on it. Sable's ears, better than any human's, caught the whisper of it: cloth, boot-leather, and the faint metallic click of a Guild signal-whistle, muffled, reporting.

They had been seen. Someone knew the runaway clerk was in Vell, and word was already flying home to Veyra.

Juniper hadn't noticed. Ash had — the child's head turned toward the rooftop, and he made a low sound in the First Tongue, a warning-word Sable knew: *hunter-wind.*

She put her hand on Juniper's arm. "Celebrate quietly," she said. "And start walking. We need to be gone from this city by dawn — and Juniper? Whatever's in those duplicates of yours, I think we're about to learn why Veyra wants it so badly she'd burn a fortune to own it."

Behind them, in the sunken square, the Night Market of Borrowed Things went on trading in pieces of people, and somewhere in a broker's cellar, a small glass vial full of winter fire sat on a shelf, waiting to be sold to whoever asked next.
