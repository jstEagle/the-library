## 8. Ledgers and Lies

The copying room of the Guild of Impossible Things smelled of lamp oil, cold tea, and the particular melancholy of paper that had outlived everyone who wrote on it. Juniper Thackeray had spent eleven years in that smell, and he had come to think of it as the smell of honesty — because ledgers, whatever else might be said of them, did not know how to lie.

People lied. Guildmasters lied. Juniper had recently discovered, with a lurch he was still digesting, that he himself could lie, and lie to the face of a woman who had promoted him twice. But ink on vellum simply recorded what had been written, and if what had been written was false, it at least had the decency to be *provably* false, which was more than could be said for most things in the world.

He spread his stolen copies across the long table and weighted their corners with inkwells, a candle stub, and the fossilized biscuit that lived in the drawer and served, officially, as a paperweight and unofficially as a warning to anyone tempted to eat it.

Outside, the storm from three nights past had finally exhausted itself, but the gate's wards were still humming wrong. He could feel it through the floor, a faint arrhythmia like a heart skipping beats. For sixty years, apparently. He was beginning to suspect the floor had been trying to tell him something for a very long time, and that he had been very good at not listening.

"Right," he said to the empty room, because talking helped, and because Ash was asleep upstairs under the guard of two Guild wardens who believed they were guarding a prisoner rather than a child, and Sable was — where? On the roof, probably. Wolf-wardens seemed to regard roofs the way cats regarded sunbeams: as furniture placed there specifically for their benefit. "Let's find out how badly my office has disgraced itself."

* * *

The comparison itself was simple. That was the thing that kept nagging at him — how simple it was. Anyone could have done it. Anyone who had ever balanced an impossible account, which was literally his entire job description, could have caught this in an afternoon.

The covenant ledger of the Gate of the Wolf's Road ran back four hundred years. Every fifty years, give or take a season, there was a renewal: a page written in the First Tongue, countersigned by a keeper of the Guild and a warden of wolf-kind, witnessed by three members each, sealed with blood-wax that never quite dried. Juniper had seen renewals listed in the index his whole career. 1187. 1239. 1291. 1343. 1395. The rhythm of it was almost comforting, like tides.

And then 1447.

The entry for 1447 existed. It was indexed. It was referenced in six subsequent documents, including Veyra's own accession papers, which cited "the renewal of 1447" as evidence of the covenant's continued validity. The page was even there in the original ledger, in the proper place, written in a fine careful hand that Juniper now recognized with a sick twist in his stomach as the hand of Aldous Thackeray, keeper before him, his own great-grandfather, the man whose portrait hung in the copying room and whose eyes, Juniper now realized, had always looked slightly guilty.

But the page was not a renewal.

Juniper laid his secret copy of the true covenant text beside it and read them both through, twice, three times, mouthing the First Tongue phrases that Ash had taught him — or rather, that Ash had sung at him while eating porridge, which turned out to be an effective pedagogical method if you had no dignity.

A true renewal began with the phrase *vel-saharun eth*, "the road is walked again." It named the witnesses. It named the terms. And critically — critically — it ended with the countermark of the wolf-side, pressed not in ink but in a bite of silver, because wolf-kind did not sign things, they *marked* them, and the mark could only be made by a warden's own tooth against cold metal.

The 1447 page began with *vel-saharun eth*. It named witnesses — three Guild names, all of whom Juniper found in the personnel rolls, all conveniently dead within a decade, one of whom, according to the rolls, had died *before* 1447, which was the sort of detail falsifiers always missed because they assumed nobody cross-referenced the mortality schedules. Juniper cross-referenced the mortality schedules. It was, in fairness, the only kind of referencing he knew.

It named the terms — or rather, it copied the terms from the previous renewal, word for word, which a real renewal would never do, because the terms were renegotiated each time; that was the entire point of a covenant between two living peoples. Terms that never changed weren't terms. They were a tombstone.

And it ended not with a bite of silver but with a smear of ordinary red wax, pressed with a seal Juniper found catalogued in the Guild's own inventory: *Thackeray personal signet, retired 1449 upon death of holder.*

There was no wolf-mark. There had been no wolf-mark. Sixty years ago, Aldous Thackeray had sat at this very table — Juniper was almost certain it was this very table; the burn scar by the third inkwell matched the scar on the desk edge — and had written a renewal that never happened, signed it with dead men's names, sealed it with his own ring, and filed it in the ledger where it had sat, quietly poisoning everything, ever since.

"Oh, Aldous," Juniper said softly. "You absolute disaster."

The portrait on the wall said nothing, but its eyes somehow managed to look guiltier.

* * *

He worked until the lamps burned low, and what he found got worse the deeper he dug, the way cellars usually do.

The renewal of 1447 was supposed to have reaffirmed the terms: a keeping, freely given, one soul to hold the gate from within, alive and aware, honored by both peoples, rotated by consent every half-century. But because the renewal was false, the *terms* were false too — or rather, the gate had gone sixty years without terms at all. A covenant wasn't a wall. It was more like a fire: it needed feeding, tending, agreement on both sides about what it was for. Left untended, a fire didn't go out. It just started eating whatever was nearest.

That was why the wards hummed out of rhythm. That was why the gate had been "counting back," as Juniper had uneasily noted in his private journal weeks ago. The gate wasn't malfunctioning. It was *hungry*, in the patient, ancient way of things that had been promised a meal and stood up at the table for sixty years while the host pretended to be out.

And the wolves? The wolves had come to the door in 1447 — he found that too, buried in a waystation log he'd copied on instinct during his flight through the sealed archives. A delegation of seven wardens, led by one whose name rendered awkwardly into Common as *Grey-of-the-Long-Winter*, arriving at the gate on the appointed night, carrying the silver marking-plate.

They had waited three days. The gate had stayed shut. No keeper came. And then — this was the line Juniper must have read ten times, tracing it with his finger until the ink smudged — the delegation had turned back into the pinewood, and the last line of the log, in a different, shakier hand, read:

*They did not attack. They said the debt would keep. They said the debt always keeps.*

Wolves, it turned out, kept accounts too. Better ones, possibly, than the Guild.

So why hadn't Aldous simply admitted the failure? Why forge instead of confess? Juniper turned the question over like a coin he suspected was clipped. And the answer, when it came, arrived with the weight of something obvious that had been standing behind him the whole time: because admitting the failure meant admitting the covenant needed a keeper. A living soul, given willingly, to hold the gate from inside. And Aldous Thackeray — gentle, meticulous, beloved Aldous, whose letters to his grandchildren (to *Juniper's grandmother*) still survived in the family box — Aldous had looked at that term, and at his own life, and at his children, and had decided that the term applied to someone else. To anyone else. To some future generation's child, some village's son or daughter, someone far away and poor enough to be chosen.

The covenant had never demanded a death. It demanded a *keeping*. And men had spent sixty years quietly converting a keeping into a killing, because a killing could be outsourced, and a keeping could not.

Juniper put down his pen and discovered his hands were shaking.

It wasn't fear, exactly. It was the specific horror of a man who has spent his whole life polishing a beautiful piece of furniture and finally looks underneath it and finds the rot goes all the way to the floorboards. His office. His *office* — the ledgers, the seals, the solemn rituals of verification he performed every quarter with such pride — all of it built atop a single forged page, maintained by sixty years of keepers who either never checked or checked and chose not to see. His inheritance wasn't a duty. It was a debt. And debts, as any ledger-keeper knew, did not disappear. They compounded.

"He knew," Juniper told the portrait. "Didn't he. He knew exactly what he was doing."

The portrait's guilty eyes declined to comment. Coward, Juniper thought, and then felt bad, and then didn't, because honestly, Aldous, the man had options, and he'd picked forgery, and the interest payments were coming due in the form of a nine-year-old boy whom the gate wanted to eat.

* * *

The scratch at the window came at the second bell past midnight, which meant Sable, because nothing else in the free cities scratched at third-story windows with that particular combination of politeness and menace.

She came over the sill the way she did everything — silently, landing in a crouch, then rising with the exaggerated care of someone demonstrating that she *could* have been loud if she'd wanted. Her grey cloak was damp with river fog. In the lamplight her eyes caught amber, the way they always did, and Juniper had stopped pretending to himself that he didn't notice.

"You've been down here since dusk," she said. "I could hear your pen. It has an angry sound tonight."

"Pens can't sound angry."

"Yours can. You hold it like a knife when you're upset. You're holding it like a knife." She nodded at the table, at the sprawl of pages, at the fossilized biscuit standing sentinel. "Show me."

He showed her. He walked her through it the way he'd walk a junior clerk through an audit — the missing wolf-mark, the dead-before-the-date witness, the copied terms, the waystation log, the delegation that waited three days in the rain and went home saying *the debt will keep.* Sable listened without interrupting, which he'd learned was a wolf habit: they gave words room, the way they gave tracks room, reading everything.

When he finished, she was quiet for a long moment, looking at the forged page. Then she said something in the First Tongue, low, and it took Juniper a heartbeat to translate it, and when he did, it settled over him like snow:

*"We wondered which of you would finally count."*

"Sable—"

"Sixty years," she said. "My grandmother's grandmother was alive when Grey-of-the-Long-Winter stood at your door. She told the story to her daughters, and they told theirs. We have songs about it. Songs, Juniper. Your people have a *filed page*." She touched the forgery with one finger, gently, the way you touch a wound. "We thought the betrayal was malice. Malice we understand. This—" her hand swept the table, the ledgers, the neat, orderly, catastrophic machinery of it— "this is worse. This is bookkeeping."

"I know," Juniper said. "I'm the bookkeeper."

"That's not what I meant."

"No," he said. "I know it isn't. But it's true anyway, isn't it? Someone had to count eventually. And it turns out the counting was left to the one person in the building with a family stake in the cover-up." He laughed, and it came out wrong, brittle. "Veyra couldn't have arranged it better if she'd tried. When this comes out, do you know what she'll say? *The Thackeray name is compromised. The keeper's judgment cannot be trusted.* She'll use my own bloodline as the proof that I'm lying."

"She might," Sable said. "If you let her write the story first."

"And how do I stop her?"

"You don't stop her writing." She picked up the waystation log, the one with the shaky final line, and held it out to him. "You make sure the truth is louder. Wolves learned that lesson a long time ago. When the hunters' stories are lies, you don't argue with the hunters. You howl where everyone can hear."

Juniper took the log. His hands had steadied, mostly. "You realize what this means, though. Practically. If the renewal never happened, then the gate hasn't been bound by terms in sixty years. It's running on — on momentum. On habit. On *hunger*." He glanced up at the ceiling, toward the floors above, toward the distant stone throat of the gate itself. "Every ward in this building is a promise the gate thinks has expired. And Ash—"

"Ash," Sable said quietly.

"The gate doesn't want a sacrifice. The true terms never asked for one. But sixty years of hunger doesn't read fine print. It smells what it was promised. A keeper. Young. Willing. *Sent.*" He rubbed his eyes. "Ash believes he was sent willingly. He told me so himself, proud as anything. And the gate can't tell the difference between a child sent to die and a child sent to keep. Not anymore. Maybe it never could. Maybe that was always the flaw in us, not in it."

Sable crossed the room and stood beside him, shoulder almost touching his, both of them looking at the forged page as if it might apologize.

"So we finish the audit," she said.

"We finish the audit," Juniper agreed. "Which means finding out one more thing. Because there's a question this raises that I've been avoiding since sundown, and I can't avoid it anymore." He turned to face her. "Aldous forged the renewal. Fine. Terrible. But a forgery this clean takes time, and nerve, and he must have known someone would check someday — he was a ledger-man, he *invented* checking. So either he was a fool, which he wasn't, or—"

"Or he expected the check to happen somewhere he controlled," Sable finished.

"Or he left a trail," Juniper said. "Deliberately. A confession in disguise. Because a man who can't say the truth out loud might still arrange for it to be *findable* — by whoever came after him with the courage he lacked." He was already pulling the index toward him, flipping with new urgency. "The waystation log. The mortality schedule discrepancy. These aren't mistakes, Sable. Mistakes are messy. These are breadcrumbs. And there's one more reference in the 1447 index I haven't chased yet — a storage note. 'Waystation Nine, Pinewood Road, sealed strongbox, per keeper's instruction.'"

Sable went very still. "Waystation Nine is a ruin. It's been a ruin for forty years. It's also—" she paused, and her ears, if she'd had wolf ears, would have flattened— "it's three miles inside the forest. On our side of the old border."

"Of course it is," Juniper said faintly. "Why would it be anywhere sensible?"

"He hid his confession in wolf country," Sable said, and then, despite everything, despite the hour and the hungering gate and the forged page between them, she smiled — a quick, sharp, entirely wolfish smile. "Either he trusted us, or he wanted whoever found it to deserve it."

"Knowing Aldous," Juniper muttered, "probably both." He looked at the index entry a moment longer, then closed the book with a decisive thump that woke the fossilized biscuit not at all. "First light. We go at first light. Which means I need to invent a reason to take Ash out of the Guildhouse that Veyra's clerks will believe, a route out of the city that your roof-crawling expertise approves of, and a story for the wardens upstairs that doesn't collapse the moment someone sneezes at it."

"I'll get the rope," Sable said.

"Why do you already have rope?"

"There is always rope," said Sable, moving for the window. "The day there isn't rope is the day you'll need it. Wolf proverb." She swung onto the sill, paused, and looked back at him — at the table, at the forged page, at his face. "Juniper. Before first light. Sleep. Even an hour. What you found tonight — you carry it differently when you're tired. Heavier."

He wanted to argue. Instead he heard himself say, "Only if you actually stay on the roof instead of pacing. I can hear you pacing. It sounds like weather."

"That *is* how I sleep," Sable said gravely, and dropped into the dark.

Juniper blew out the lamps, all but one, and sat a moment longer in the small circle of light with his ancestor's confession spread out before him. Somewhere above, the gate hummed its broken rhythm, counting. Somewhere in the pinewood, a ruined waystation held a strongbox with a dead man's truth inside it.

*The debt always keeps,* the shaky old handwriting had said.

"Well," Juniper told the empty room, snuffing the last lamp. "So do I."
