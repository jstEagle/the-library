## 10. Running the Rooftops

The knock came at the third hour past midnight, and it came wrong.

Juniper knew every knock the Guildhouse made. The night-porter's double rap, apologetic. The wardens' flat knuckle-tap that said *inspection, pretend to be surprised*. The cooks' cheerful pounding when someone had left a ledger on the bread table again — usually him. But this was four quick raps, a pause, then two more, soft as an apology, and no one in the Guild knocked like that except one person.

He opened the door on Tam Wicker, junior clerk, holding a lantern turned down so low it was mostly a rumor of light. Tam was nineteen, freckled, chronically overworked, and possessed of the particular courage of people who had decided, quietly and in advance, exactly what they were willing to be hanged for.

"You need to leave," Tam whispered. "Now. Tonight."

Juniper's stomach performed a slow, professional inventory of itself. "Tam—"

"She's signed the writ. Veyra. Sealed it herself, an hour ago. Retrieval order for the boy, *and* a detention order for you — 'for questioning regarding missing archival materials.'" Tam's mouth twisted. "That's Guild-speak for 'we know it was you, Juniper.'"

"How do you know?"

"Because I filed it." Tam pushed past him into the room, already moving with the brisk efficiency of a man who had rehearsed this. He began sweeping Juniper's few possessions into a satchel — spare shirt, ink, the small knife he wasn't supposed to own, the copy of the covenant pages hidden inside a false cover labeled *Grain Tariffs, Vol. IX*, which Tam held up, read the label, and gave him a look of profound clerical disappointment about. "Nobody files grain tariffs at midnight, Thackeray. I've been watching your borrowing slips for two weeks."

"That's either very loyal or very alarming."

"It's both. It's always both." Tam shoved the satchel at his chest. "Hunters come at dawn. Four of them, plus Marrow from Enforcement, and Marrow doesn't knock. She kicks."

In the corner, on the narrow cot the Guild had grudgingly assigned its prisoner-guest, Ash sat up. The boy slept the way wolves did, Juniper had learned — lightly, entirely, one ear always working. His eyes caught the guttering lantern light and held it.

"What did she say?" Ash asked, in the First Tongue, and then, in the broken Common Juniper had been teaching him these past weeks: "Bad men come?"

"Hunters," Juniper said, in both languages, because some things deserved to be understood twice. "We're going on a trip."

Ash's face did something complicated — fear and excitement fighting for the same ground — and then settled on a third thing Juniper recognized with a lurch: trust. Absolute, unearned, terrifying trust. The boy believed him the way children believe, which is to say completely, which is to say *Juniper, do not break this.*

"We're going on a trip," Juniper repeated, and found that saying it a second time made it true.

* * *

The Guildhouse at night was a different animal than by day. By day it was ledgers and lamplight and the smell of ink and porridge; by night it was stone and shadow and the long hum of wards, and Juniper knew its bones better than anyone alive, because balancing impossible accounts meant walking every corridor at hours no sane person chose.

They went down the servants' stair, past the kitchens where old Marta slept sitting up with a ladle across her knees like a sword. She opened one eye as they passed.

"The back door's unlatched," she said, without moving anything but her mouth. "It's been unlatched for years. Terrible security. Someone ought to write it up."

"Marta—"

"I liked the boy," she said. "He fixed my squeaky hinge Tuesday and didn't eat all the plums. Go."

Ash pressed both hands together at her — the wolf-gesture of thanks, palms to heart, out — and Marta pretended very hard to be asleep until they were gone.

Outside, Vellum Street lay drowned in rain-wet dark. The storm from the night Ash arrived had passed days ago, but the city kept its memory in puddles, and every puddle reflected lamplight like a small accusation. Juniper pulled his hood up, took Ash's hand, and did the thing he had been dreading since Tam's knock: he stopped being a ledger-keeper.

There is a particular terror in the first ten minutes of running from your own institution. Every footstep sounds like evidence. Every shadow wears a warden's coat. Juniper's whole life had been built on the principle that if the accounts balanced and the rules were followed, nothing bad could reach you through the paperwork. He had believed, in some deep unexamined place, that the Guild was a wall between him and the world's teeth.

The wall was behind him now, and it had teeth of its own.

They cut through the tannery lane, along the drainage channel, up through Cooper's Row — and there, at the mouth of the alley, a lantern flared.

"Wardens!" someone shouted, and the night organized itself against them.

Juniper did not think. Later, he would try to reconstruct what he'd done and fail; the body, it turned out, keeps its own ledger, and his had recorded twenty years of taking shortcuts through the Guildhouse rafters. He grabbed Ash under the arms, hauled him onto a stack of barrels, up onto a low roof, and went after him hand over hand, boots scrabbling on wet slate, while below them the lantern-bearer swore and lost them to the angle of the eaves.

They ran.

Not gracefully. Juniper would never claim grace. They ran the rooftops of the Tanner's Quarter the way a cat runs from a bath — flailing, committed, occasionally sliding — but they ran, and Ash laughed.

That was the thing Juniper would remember afterward, the thing that kept surfacing like a cork: the boy *laughed*. Leaping a gap between a chandler's shop and a dyehouse, three stories over cobbles slick with rain, pursued by armed men, Ash threw his head back and laughed into the dark like the whole night was a game someone had finally invited him to play.

"What—" Juniper gasped, hauling himself over a chimney pot. "What is *funny*?"

"In village," Ash panted, "running is punishment. Running here is *flying*." He grinned, wild and mud-bright even in the dark. "You fly badly, Juniper-thief-of-ledgers."

"My name is *Thackeray*—"

"*Fly badly, Thackeray.*"

And somewhere around the fourth rooftop, something loosened in Juniper's chest — some knot he hadn't known was tied, wound tight since the night the wards first sang wrong, tighter since the sealed pages, tightest of all since he'd lied to Veyra's face and felt the lie sit in his mouth like a coin of foreign weight. The knot didn't untie. It *changed*. Terror transmuted, alchemy of the blood, into something faster and cleaner.

Exhilaration. He was fleeing everything he'd built his life on, and his body had the audacity to be *glad*.

"Oh no," he muttered, leaping a gap and sticking the landing for once. "Oh, I'm enjoying this."

* * *

They lost the hunters twice and found them once, and the once was nearly fatal.

Marrow from Enforcement had not gotten her name from being slow. She appeared on the bell tower roof ahead of them — how, Juniper would never know; he suspected she simply refused to acknowledge distance as a concept — crossbow leveled, rain beading on her bald head like she'd ordered it specially.

"Thackeray. Boy. Down on your knees and this is a formality instead of a tragedy."

Juniper put himself between her and Ash, which was stupid, which was arithmetic with no possible remainder, and did it anyway. "Marrow. You've read the retrieval writ. Have you read what it's *for*? Have you asked why the Guildmaster wants a child?"

"I don't ask why. That's why they pay me."

"And if the why is rotten?"

Marrow's jaw worked. For one heartbeat — one — the crossbow dipped a degree. "Then somebody should have asked sooner," she said, "and it shouldn't have been me," and that was when the roof behind her exploded upward in a wash of grey fur and fury.

Sable hit Marrow like a landslide with opinions. The crossbow skittered away into the gutter; the two of them went down in a tangle that resolved, three seconds later, into Sable standing with a knee on Marrow's chest and a knife at her collar, breathing hard, hair plastered to her face.

"Don't," Sable said, to whatever Marrow saw in her eyes. "Whatever you're about to say about my kind and truces — don't. You drew on a child."

"He's the Seal-Bearer," Marrow rasped. "You know what he is. You know what's coming for him. I'm the *polite* version."

"I know exactly what he is." Sable looked over her shoulder at Juniper, and the look was a conversation they'd been having for days without words: *this is real now, there's no ledger line for coming back.* "Get him off this roof, Juniper. River gate, the old ferry stairs, before the second team closes the net. I'll follow."

"Sable—"

"*Go.*" And softer, almost lost under the rain: "I keep my oaths. All of them. Even the inconvenient ones."

Juniper took Ash's hand and jumped.

* * *

The river saved them, the way rivers save everyone eventually, by being large and indifferent and impossible to file paperwork against.

The old ferry stairs were a ruin from before the free cities were free — mossy steps descending into black water, a rotted bollard, the ghost of a toll booth nobody had taxed in a century. Juniper crouched there with Ash tucked into the lee of the wall, listening to the city hunt them: whistles in the Tanner's Quarter, boots on the embankment, a horn from the bridge garrison. Somewhere above, Sable was either coming or not coming, and Juniper discovered that his heart had opinions about which, loud ones, opinions he'd been not-writing-down for weeks in the margin of every thought.

"You're scared," Ash observed. In Common, deliberately. Practicing. Children learned fastest the words that mattered.

"Yes."

"But also—" Ash tilted his head, hunting the word, found it, delighted in it: "*shining.*"

"Shining?"

"Like coins. Scared-shining." He nodded, satisfied with the translation, then leaned his small weight against Juniper's arm. "Wolves say fear and joy are same river. Different banks."

"When did the wolves teach you that?"

"The night they took me from the fire." Said simply, terribly, the way Ash said all his worst things — plainly, watching your face to see if the world could hold it. "Old Warden Grey-muzzle said: *you will be afraid all your life, little seal, so choose what to be afraid for.*" He looked up. "You are afraid *for* me. Is good bank."

Juniper's throat did something unsuitable for a Guild officer. He put his hand on the boy's head, on the mud-stiff hair he'd been slowly, patiently teaching to accept a comb.

Boots on the stairs. Juniper spun, putting himself forward again, useless arithmetic again—

—and it was Sable, bleeding from a slice along her forearm, soaked to the ribs, grinning like the rain had personally insulted her family.

"Ferry's gone," she said. "Has been for sixty years. But the *cable's* still there." She pointed across the black water to where, barely visible, an ancient iron line sagged between the far pilings and a rusted ring bolted to the stair-side cliff. "Guild records say it was decommissioned. Guild records are wrong. Imagine that."

"You want us to cross a river on a dead cable. At night. With hunters on the bank."

"With hunters on *both* banks in about four minutes, yes." She was already pulling rope from under her cloak — of course she carried rope; she probably slept in it. "I go first with the boy strapped to me. You follow the line hand over hand. If you fall, I'll be very annoyed, and you've seen me annoyed."

"I've seen you *fond*, and it's worse."

She smiled at that — quick, real, gone — and knelt to strap Ash against her chest. The boy wrapped his arms around her neck with the ease of long practice, and murmured something in the First Tongue, and Sable answered in it, fluently, softly, and Juniper stood there feeling like a man reading a letter addressed to someone else and finding his own name in it anyway.

"What did he say?"

"He said," Sable told the river, tightening the last knot, "'the ledger-keeper flies badly but holds tightly.'" She rose, stepped to the edge, and the black water reached up for them. "Hold tightly, Juniper. That's the whole job tonight."

She went out over the river like a rumor, hand over hand along the cable, Ash a small fierce bundle against her, and the wind took their sounds and gave back nothing. Then it was Juniper's turn. He gripped cold iron, stepped off the stair, and hung above sixty feet of hungry dark with his whole former life burning quietly on the bank behind him.

Halfway across, his arms shaking, lungs full of river-damp, he made himself look back.

The Guildhouse stood on its hill, windows lit, wards humming their wrong-rhythm hum. His desk was up there. His chair. Forty years of balanced accounts and a name that had meant *reliable* right up until tonight. He had spent his whole life believing that duty was a building you stayed inside.

But duty, it turned out, was a direction. And he was facing it.

He climbed the rest of the way on arms that hated him, crested the far pilings, and collapsed onto the muddy far bank beside Sable and Ash, laughing — actually laughing, breathless and ridiculous, while the hunters' horns sounded futile and distant across the water.

"There it is," Sable said, watching him with something warm and wary at once. "I wondered when you'd get to the other side of frightened."

"Is this the part where you tell me it gets worse?"

"No. This is the part where we walk all night, sleep in a ditch, and tomorrow I take you to the Council of Free Cities to beg." She shouldered her pack and offered Ash her hand, and the boy took it, and after a moment — because apparently he was allowed now — Juniper took the other one. "But between here and there, thief-of-ledgers, nobody in the world knows where we are. So for one night, the accounts are ours."

They walked north along the river, three shadows and a rising moon, and behind them the city that had been his whole world shrank to a smudge of light on the water — smaller every step, and lighter, too, though he'd never have guessed light could weigh less than home.

Ahead, somewhere past the dark hills, the free cities waited with their walls and their councils and their terrible, reasonable arithmetic. And somewhere closer than comfort, he could feel it — faint as a toothache, patient as winter — the gate humming its counting-back hum, waiting for all of them to arrive.
