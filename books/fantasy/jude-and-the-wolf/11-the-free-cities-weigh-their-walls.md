## 11. The Free Cities Weigh Their Walls

The Council of Free Cities met in a hall built to impress people who were easily impressed, and Sable had never been easily impressed.

She stood at the back of the Chamber of Nine Voices with her arms crossed and her ears — the human ones she wore for occasions like this, tucked beneath a hood of oiled wool — straining against the murmur of clerks and petitioners. The hall itself was old money made stone: pillars carved with the faces of long-dead magistrates, a ceiling painted with doves that had never once landed anywhere near a battlefield. Braziers burned sweet resin. Everything smelled like someone was trying very hard to cover up the smell of fear.

Three days they had traveled from the river. Three days of Ash riding double on Juniper's horse because the boy refused, with the absolute iron of his small will, to ride with anyone else. Three days of Sable running ahead through the pinelands in her own shape, checking trails, doubling back, learning the particular rhythm of Guild pursuit — which was patient, methodical, and worse than fast.

And now here they were, asking nine strangers in velvet whether they might be bothered to protect one child.

"They'll deliberate," Juniper murmured beside her. He had shaved, borrowed a coat that almost fit, and done something with his hair that made him look less like a fugitive ledger-keeper and more like a man who owned exactly one respectable coat. It was, Sable thought, a very effective disguise. "Deliberation is what councils do instead of deciding."

"You say that with love."

"I say it with eleven years of reading their meeting minutes." He nodded toward the dais. "Watch. The Speaker will open with gratitude for our patience. Then someone will ask if we've considered that this is really a Guild matter. Then someone else will propose a committee."

"Is there a word in your ledger-tongue for people who form committees while a child is hunted?"

"'Councillors,'" said Juniper.

* * *

The Speaker was a broad woman with a magistrate's chain and a voice like a well-oiled hinge. She opened, as predicted, with gratitude for their patience, and then — Sable gave her credit — she got to the point faster than expected.

"The petition before us is simple," the Speaker said. "Juniper Thackeray, formerly of the Guild of Impossible Things, requests sanctuary under the Accords of Meridian for himself, the wolf-warden called Sable, and the child Ash, who stands accused by the Guild of no crime whatsoever, but whom the Guild wants nonetheless. The usurper's armies, we are told, also want him. For reasons the petitioners have declined to state fully in open session."

"We stated them," Juniper said, rising. "You asked us to state them quietly, and we did."

A ripple of discomfort moved through the benches. Sable watched the councillors the way she'd once watched a treeline: not for movement, but for the absence of it. Most of them were very still. Stillness in prey animals meant calculation.

"Councillor Vess," the Speaker said, "you wished to speak."

Vess was a narrow man with narrow hands and the smile of someone who had never once been surprised by good news. "I wish only to ask a question of arithmetic," he said pleasantly. "The usurper fields, by our scouts' count, forty thousand soldiers. He has burned one border town already as an opening remark. Against this, we have walls, grain stores, and — if we surrender one child — peace. I put it to the chamber: what is one life weighed against forty thousand?"

Sable felt Juniper go rigid beside her, and she put a hand on his arm — not to stop him, exactly, but to choose the moment. She rose herself first.

"Let me answer as a wolf," she said, and let the hood fall back so the chamber could see what she was. There was a satisfying scrape of benches. Wolves did not often attend council sessions; wolves did not often attend anything with a roof. "Among my kind, when a pack is starving, we have eaten things I will not describe to this chamber. But we have never eaten a cub. Not in the worst winter of the Long Hunger. Not ever. You are asking me whether your arithmetic includes that line, councillor, and I am telling you it does not appear anywhere in ours."

"A stirring sentiment," said Vess. "Sentiment does not hold a wall."

"No," Sable agreed. "But it's what the wall is *for*. A wall that protects nothing is just a very expensive way of standing in a field."

That earned a laugh from the gallery — the public galleries above were packed, because a trial of conscience was better entertainment than the puppeteers in Fountain Square — and Vess's smile thinned by a degree.

* * *

They broke for midday, because institutions must eat even while weighing a child's life, and the three of them were given a side chamber with bread, cheese, and two guards who pretended not to be guards.

Ash ate like a wolf himself, hunched over the plate, and then pushed half his bread across the table to Sable without looking up. It was how he said things he didn't yet have words for. She took it. You didn't refuse a gift like that; you'd wound something older than language.

"He hates it here," Juniper said quietly. "The stone. The crowds. He's been counting the exits since we walked in."

"How many?"

"Nine. He says the ceiling has a tenth, but it's a lie of a door." Juniper rubbed his eyes. "He's right, actually. There's a maintenance hatch behind the eastern tapestry. I filed the work order for it four years ago."

"You filed a work order for a hidden escape route in the council chamber?"

"I file work orders for everything. That's rather the whole of my former profession." He smiled, but it didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "Vess wasn't wrong about the arithmetic, Sable. That's what frightens me. Forty thousand men is a real number. Walls are real. If I were sitting on that bench, hungry, with my city behind me—"

"You'd find another way."

"I'd *want* to find another way. Those aren't the same thing, and I've spent enough years in ledgers to know the difference between what people write and what they do." He looked at Ash, who had fallen asleep sitting up, chin on chest, the way children and soldiers sleep — instantly, defensively, ready to wake. "Sixty years ago a keeper falsified a renewal to avoid exactly this kind of hard choice. One comfortable lie, and now the gate runs on borrowed time and a boy is hunted across three provinces. Hard choices don't go away, Sable. They just accrue interest."

It was the most she'd heard him say at once since the river, and she understood it was not really about the council. It was about chapter thirteen of his own private ledger — the Thackeray name, the waystation, the proof they'd dug out of a ruined cellar that his blood had started all of this. He carried it the way Ash carried the wolf-tooth: close, silent, always touching it.

"Eat your cheese," she said. "You argue better fed."

"That's true. My best speeches have historically followed lunch."

* * *

Afternoon session, and the chamber had filled further, which meant the galleries smelled of sweat and roasted chestnuts and the particular excitement of citizens watching other citizens' misfortunes. The Speaker called the debate back to order. Councillor after councillor rose.

An old woman from Harrowgate argued for sanctuary on grounds of honor, and was applauded, and changed nothing. A merchant-prince from Saltmere argued that the free cities' prosperity rested on trade, and trade required stability, and stability required not provoking armies, and was applauded by fewer people, and changed slightly more. A young councillor from the river districts — Sable marked her face, a sharp-eyed woman named Ondile — asked the question nobody wanted asked:

"If we give up this child, what do we give up next? The usurper has learned that we negotiate. Armies that learn cities negotiate do not stop negotiating. They raise their price." Scattered applause. "I move we grant sanctuary in full."

"Seconded," said the old woman from Harrowgate, with the satisfaction of a woman who enjoyed being seconded.

And then the Speaker turned to the petitioners themselves. "The chamber will hear from Juniper Thackeray before deliberation closes."

Sable watched him stand. Watched him smooth the coat that almost fit. And she saw him do the thing she had come, over these strange weeks, to recognize as the truest thing about him: he stopped performing the respectable clerk, and simply spoke.

"When I worked for the Guild," he began, "my job was to keep accounts of impossible things. Bottled echoes. Doors that opened onto yesterday. A candle that burns grief instead of wax — we had three of those, and I want to say, as an aside, that none of them should be allowed near a funeral." A ripple of laughter; he waited it out. "And in eleven years of keeping those accounts, I learned one rule that applies to everything, including this chamber. An institution is a machine for converting individual courage into collective cowardice. It doesn't happen because the people inside are wicked. It happens because each person can always point to the rules, and the rules can always point to someone else."

He let that sit. The chamber had gone quiet in the way rooms do when a speaker stops performing and starts meaning it.

"I am not asking you to break your laws," Juniper said. "I'm asking you to notice that your laws were written by people who assumed this day would come, and wrote down, in advance, what to do about it. The Accords of Meridian, section nine: sanctuary shall not be denied to any soul fleeing a power that would use them as an instrument. Not *criminals*. Not *citizens*. Any soul. Your ancestors wrote that clause because they remembered what it was to be instruments. They knew that the day would come when protecting one inconvenient child would cost something real. They wrote it anyway. The question before you is not whether the law permits courage. It's whether you do."

From the gallery, someone began to clap — a single pair of hands, then ten, then a scatter of them, growing. The Speaker rapped for order without much conviction.

"And if we refuse?" Vess called out, silk over steel. "If the chamber weighs forty thousand lives and finds them heavier? What then, Master Thackeray? Will you call us cowards from outside our walls while the usurper burns our granaries?"

"No," Juniper said. "I'll call you honest. Cowards hide what they're doing behind procedure. If you vote to hand over a child, at least do it with the words in your mouth: *we are buying our safety with his life.* Say it plainly, and let the record show it plainly, and let every schoolchild in these cities read it plainly for the next hundred years. That's all I ask of any ledger — that it tell the truth about what was spent."

The silence afterward had weight. Sable had heard war-horns with less force behind them.

* * *

The vote came at dusk.

Not a vote, precisely — the Speaker, with the deftness of a woman who had survived thirty years of this chamber, called instead for a "sense of the assembly," which was a way of voting without the inconvenience of having voted. Four voices for sanctuary. Two against. Three abstained.

Abstention. Sable turned the word over as the chamber emptied and the candles guttered and the clerks gathered their papers with the brisk indifference of men who had seen a hundred such days. Abstention meant the council would neither shelter them nor surrender them. It meant the gates of the free cities would remain open to them in the technical sense that unlocked doors are open to wolves — technically, and until someone with a torch came along.

"They've given us nothing," she said.

"They've given us time," Juniper said. "A week, maybe two, before Vess finds a procedural way to revisit it. Before Veyra applies pressure that abstention can't withstand." He looked tired in the brazier-light, older than his years, and underneath the tiredness something stubborn and bright. "Institutions won't save him. I knew that coming in. I think some part of me needed to hear it said out loud, by nine voices in a fancy room, so I could stop waiting for rescue and start building it."

"Building it out of what? We have a boy, a falsified ledger, and—" she gestured at herself, generously, "—one extremely capable wolf."

"We have the truth," Juniper said. "Which is worth nothing in this chamber and everything everywhere else. Veyra's whole claim rests on the covenant being broken — on wolves being oath-breakers who forfeited their protections. But the renewal never happened. The keeper lied. The covenant isn't broken, Sable. It's *starving*. And starving covenants, like starving packs, get desperate." He glanced at Ash, still asleep, still guarded even in sleep, one hand curled around the wolf-tooth at his throat. "There's one account we haven't opened yet. His. What happened in his village. Who sent him, and why, and what the wolves did that night. If the First Tongue holds the original terms, he may hold them too — in his bones, if not his memory."

"He's six," Sable said.

"He's six," Juniper agreed. "And he's the last living witness to a sixty-year-old crime, and tomorrow morning I'm going to have to sit down with a child and ask him to remember the worst day of his life." He closed his eyes briefly. "I keep thinking there's a gentler order to do these things in. There never is. That's the lesson of the ledger, too."

Sable looked at the emptying chamber, at the painted doves on the ceiling who had never once landed anywhere near a battle, and felt the bitter clarity settle over her like snowfall — quiet, cold, and covering everything equally. The walls would hold or they wouldn't. The laws would bend or they wouldn't. None of it, in the end, would step between Ash and the dark. Only people did that. People, one at a time, choosing.

Outside, the evening bells rang across the free cities, and somewhere beyond the walls, patient as winter, the Guild's hunters were counting the days.

Ash woke as they gathered their things, looked from her face to Juniper's, and asked — in the careful Common Tongue Juniper had taught him, with the grave precision of a boy who understood that questions were doors — *"What happens now?"*

"Now," Juniper said, taking his hand, "you tell us a story. And it's going to be a hard one. But we'll be listening with everything we have."
