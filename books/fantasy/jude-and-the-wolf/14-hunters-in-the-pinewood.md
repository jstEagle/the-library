## 14. Hunters in the Pinewood

Sable smelled them an hour before Juniper did.

That was not a criticism — his nose was a perfectly serviceable nose for a man, flat and soft and useful for smelling bread and ink and rain coming over hills. But it was not a wolf's nose, and so while he trudged along the deer path explaining, for the third time that morning, why ledger-keepers were superior to all other forms of life, she had already catalogued eleven men, two horses, one very nervous dog, and something worse that moved without a scent at all.

"Juniper," she said.

"—and furthermore, the Guild's entire filing system rests on—"

"Hunters."

He stopped mid-sentence, which she noted with some satisfaction was the fastest way to silence him she'd yet discovered. Ash, walking between them with his hand hooked in the strap of her pack as he always did when trees grew close, went still in that uncanny way of his, head tilting, listening to things below the wind.

"How many?" Juniper asked. His voice had changed. It always did now, when danger came — the fussy clerk folding himself away like a ledger into a drawer, and someone harder looking out through his eyes. She was still deciding which of the two men she liked more. It was an uncomfortable problem. She suspected the answer was *both*, which was worse.

"Eleven behind us," she said. "Riders on our left flank, cutting for the ford. And something else." She frowned at the treeline. "Something quiet."

"The dog worries me most," Ash said solemnly, in his careful First Tongue, which by now Sable understood well enough and Juniper translated only when he wanted to stall for time. "Dogs tell other dogs about you."

"He's right," Sable said. "The dog is a traitor to its kind and must be dealt with first."

"You are both banned from tactical planning," Juniper said. "Forever. Effective immediately."

But he was already moving, already scanning the pines, and she watched him do the thing he did — the thing that had stopped surprising her somewhere around the second week of running and started feeling, alarmingly, like trust. He read the forest the way he read ledgers: entries, patterns, discrepancies. A game trail here. A deadfall there. A stream bank too soft to hold a man's weight but perfect for convincing one he'd gone another way.

"There," he said, pointing up-slope to where the pines grew old and thick around a granite spine of hill. "We make for the rocks. If they corner us anywhere, I'd rather it be where I can see their hands."

"And if we're not cornered?" Sable asked, falling into step beside him, matching her long stride to his shorter one without either of them noticing anymore that they did it.

"Then I have a plan involving the stream, three false trails, and a truly excessive amount of chalk."

Ash brightened visibly. The child adored Juniper's chalk. Sable had watched him draw ward-marks on tree bark, on stones, once memorably on the rump of a mule they'd borrowed and returned, and each time Ash had watched with the reverence other children reserved for fireworks.

They climbed. The pines closed overhead, resinous and dark, and the light went green-gold, and for perhaps twenty minutes Sable allowed herself the dangerous luxury of believing they might simply walk away.

Then the horn sounded behind them, and the something-without-a-scent stopped being subtle.

* * *

It came down the slope ahead of them, which was the wrong direction entirely, which meant it had circled wide in the night and let them run toward it. Sable got her first real look at it as it stepped between two pines, and her hackles rose under her skin where no fur existed to rise.

A warden-hound. One of the old ones — bred before the covenant, before the gate, back when men had tried to make wolves out of dogs and dogs out of nightmares. It stood the height of a horse's shoulder, gray as ash, and it made no sound at all. That was the horror of it. Wolves announced themselves; it was courtesy, it was law. This thing simply *arrived*.

"Ash," Sable said quietly. "Behind me. Now."

The boy didn't argue. That was the other thing she'd learned about him: he was brave in the specific way of children who had already survived the worst day of their lives. He pressed against Juniper's side instead, and Juniper's hand came down on his shoulder, and Sable felt something twist warmly and inconveniently in her chest at the sight of the two of them together, clerk and seal-bearer, standing in the green light like a painting of everything she'd sworn half her life ago to protect.

The hound's head turned toward Ash. Only toward Ash.

"No," Sable said, and stepped into its line of sight, and drew her blades.

What followed was not, she would later insist, a battle. It was an argument conducted at speed. The hound came for her because she put herself between it and the boy, and she met it the way her mother had taught her to meet anything larger than herself: low, inside its reach, where its size became its problem. Her left blade opened its foreleg; her right found the joint of its jaw. It screamed then — finally, *finally* making noise — and the scream was wrong, layered, almost human, and it threw her with a casual violence that sent her through a sapling and into the granite.

The world rang. She tasted copper. Somewhere above her Juniper was shouting her name in a voice cracked clean down the middle, and Ash was shouting something in the First Tongue that she was fairly sure was a curse of impressive antiquity, and the hound was coming again, limping now, furious, silent again even in its rage.

She got one blade up. It wasn't going to be enough. She knew that with the calm arithmetic of the dying, and what she thought, absurdly, in the half-second before impact, was: *I never told him. All this time on the road, all these small mornings, and I never told him.*

She did not die.

Instead the forest did something forests are not supposed to do.

Every pine within thirty pines' distance leaned. Not fell — *leaned*, groaning, roots tearing free of soil in long black fingers, and the ground itself shrugged like a dog shedding water, and the hound stumbled mid-lunge as the earth rolled beneath it. Then the deadfall Juniper had noted hours ago — a great silvered tangle of trunk and branch — lifted, creaked, and came down on the creature like the fist of a god with excellent bookkeeping.

Silence. Pine needles drifting. Sable lying on her back in the moss, bleeding, staring up at Juniper Thackeray, who stood at the crest of the rock with his arms out and chalk dust smoking off his fingers and an expression on his face like a man who has just discovered the cellar of his own house goes down much further than advertised.

"That," he said faintly, "was supposed to be a diversion."

* * *

They did not have time for awe. Eleven hunters and two riders were converging, and the horn sounded again, closer, and Juniper hauled her upright and immediately regretted it, because her left side came apart in a white sheet of pain and her knees informed her they were resigning.

"Wound," she managed.

"I can see that." His face had gone the color of oatmeal. "How bad?"

"I've had worse."

"Liar."

"Good liar, though. You taught me ledgers; I learned creative entries." She grinned at him through the blood in her teeth and was rewarded — even now, even here — by the way his mouth twitched despite everything. "Rock. Get me to the rock. I'll hold the rearguard."

"You'll hold nothing, you can barely hold yourself up—"

"Sable." Ash tugged her sleeve, urgent, and pointed downhill through the trees. Shapes moving between the trunks. Guild grey, and among them the darker leathers of the usurper's outriders, and the nervous dog, ears flat, clearly wishing it had chosen a different career. "They are talking to each other. Enemies working together. That is cheating."

"It is politics," Juniper said grimly. "Same thing."

He got her up the granite spine by main strength and stubbornness, her arm over his shoulders, his breath ragged, and every step cost her something she pretended it didn't. At the top there was a hollow between two boulders, deep enough for Ash, defensible enough for her, and beyond it a drop on the north face that Juniper eyed with the expression of a man pricing an exit.

"Here's the shape of it," he said, kneeling beside her, tearing open her jacket with hands that shook only slightly, which she counted as a triumph for both of them. "I can turn the forest twice more. Maybe three times, if the wards don't burn out. After that I'm drawing on the gate-tithe in my blood and I genuinely don't know what happens."

"So don't find out today."

"After that," he continued, wrapping the wound tight enough to make her see stars, "we go down the north face, through the fern brake, and I chalk us a road to the river. Sable. Look at me. You are not holding any rearguard."

"I'm the only one of us who fights."

"I turned a forest into a weapon an hour ago."

"You turned a forest into a *diversion*," she said, "and it killed your monster by accident. There's a difference between power and aim, clerk."

He looked at her then — really looked, the way he did over ledgers when a number refused to balance — and something passed across his face that she would spend the rest of her life trying to name. "Then teach me to aim," he said, "and stay alive long enough to criticize my form."

Below them, the hunters reached the wrecked deadfall and the ruined hound, and voices rose — confusion, fear, the particular panic of soldiers encountering guild-craft in the wild. Someone shouted an order. They began to spread out, circling the hill.

"They'll flank," Sable said. "Two parties. East and west. They think we're trapped on the rock."

"We are trapped on the rock."

"Yes, but they don't know about the north face, or the fern brake, or the fact that you are the single most dangerous accountant in the free cities." She flexed her sword hand. It worked, mostly. "So. East party comes up the easy slope. I meet them loud and angry and bleeding, which I am told is very intimidating, and I drive them west. West party meets your forest. Aim it this time."

"That's a terrible plan."

"It's a *plan*. You're the one who says a bad entry beats no entry."

"Audited and amended," he muttered, but he was already chalking, quick strokes on the granite, on the pines, muttering the old terms under his breath in a voice that made the air feel thicker. Ash crouched beside him, adding marks of his own — child-scrawled, lopsided, and yet the wards took them, flared softly blue-white, accepted them. Seal-bearer's blood recognized its own.

Sable watched the two of them work, heads bent together over the stone, and felt the wound and the morning and the whole doomed sweet length of the road since the Guildhouse compress itself into one unbearable certainty.

*Oh,* she thought. *Oh, you absolute fool. You've gone and fallen in love with a librarian.*

* * *

The east party came up the slope exactly as predicted, because soldiers are predictable; it is one of the many things Sable liked about them.

She met them loud. She met them angry. She met them, as advertised, bleeding, and she made the fight look desperate enough to be believed and controlled enough to be survivable, giving ground uphill, driving them sideways, shouting curses in three languages including two words of First Tongue that made the youngest hunter go pale and stumble, which nearly ended him right there until his sergeant hauled him back.

Six of them. She could handle six, wounded, on a slope, for approximately ninety seconds.

At second eighty-eight, the west side of the hill detonated.

Not fire — motion. The pines moved as one, a green wave rolling downhill with a sound like a ship breaking apart, and the west party's formation simply ceased to exist, men scattering, dropping weapons, one poor soul climbing a tree to escape trees. The nervous dog, displaying the finest judgment of anyone present, fled the battlefield entirely and was not seen again.

And the hound — or rather, the hound's masters had brought a second one, because usurpers believe in redundancy — came up through the chaos straight at the boulders. Straight at Ash.

Sable was there. She didn't remember deciding to be there; her body simply filed the appropriate paperwork and arrived. She took the thing's charge on her crossed blades and her boots skidding through pine duff, and it was stronger than the first, fresh, unmaimed, and she felt her left arm go numb to the shoulder on the first exchange and understood, distantly, cleanly, that this was the entry she could not amend.

So she did the arithmetic anyway. Ninety seconds. Buy ninety seconds.

"Juniper!" she roared, and heard him answer — not words, the First Tongue, the old terms pouring out of him faster now, wilder, the chalk lines on the granite blazing — and she laughed, actually laughed, because this was what she was *for*, this was the oath older than either of them, and if it ended here on a hillside smelling of pine and her own blood, at least the last thing she'd done was stand between a monster and a child, guarding a man she—

"*Sable!*"

The world tilted. The hound's second blow caught her blade and snapped it, and the backhand caught her ribs and lifted her off her feet, and she was flying, briefly, gloriously, and thinking with perfect clarity: *tell him. Tell him now, while there's still—*

Roots took her.

Great black fingers of root burst from the hillside and caught her out of the air like a mother catching a thrown child, cradling her, lowering her into the hollow between the boulders where Ash knelt with his hands pressed flat to the ward-lines and his eyes enormous. Above them, Juniper stood at the crest with both arms raised and the whole pinewood answering him — and the second hound found out what happens when a creature built of hunger meets a forest with an opinion. The pines came down on it the way judgment comes down on a falsified ledger: thoroughly, irreversibly, with interest.

Then it was over. Hunters fleeing downslope. Horns sounding retreat, confused and distant. Juniper turning, stumbling down the rock toward her, chalk-white and blood-flecked and terrified.

"You're hurt," he said, uselessly, wonderfully, dropping to his knees in the moss beside her. "You're — Sable, you—"

"You caught me," she said. Her voice came out strange. Soft. The adrenaline was draining and leaving something else behind, raw and undefended. "With the trees. You caught me."

"I — the wards, they were already shaped for you, I didn't—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I would have caught you with anything. With my hands. With my teeth, if it came to it. I've been trying to tell you for six weeks and I keep getting interrupted by armed men."

"Terrible timing, both of us."

"Consistently." His hand found hers, sticky with blood and chalk dust, and held on. "I love you. For the record. In the ledger. Entered properly, witnessed, dated." His voice cracked. "Don't you dare die and make me audit that alone."

Sable laughed, and it hurt, and she did it anyway. "Witnessed," she said. "By the seal-bearer."

"By the seal-bearer," Ash confirmed gravely, pressing his small hand over both of theirs. "It is binding. Wolves take oaths seriously. So do ledgers. You are both extremely bound now."

Above the treeline, far to the south, the light was changing — not sunset, though the hour was wrong for it, but a slow bruised gold spreading along the horizon like ink through wet paper, and the wards on Juniper's skin, the faint gate-marks he'd carried since the Guildhouse, began to glow in answer.

Juniper went very still.

"That's not Veyra," he whispered. "That's not armies. Sable — the gate knows we're coming. Something on the other side is *opening the books*."

Sable, bleeding, loved, and bound by three separate oaths, looked at the impossible gold light and said the only sensible thing left in her inventory:

"Then we'd better get there before it closes."
