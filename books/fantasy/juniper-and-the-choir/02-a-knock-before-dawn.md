## 2. A Knock Before Dawn

The knock came at the hour Casper trusted least, which was to say the hour she trusted most: that grey sliver before dawn when even the Vellum Guild's ambitions slept, and the building creaked like an old ship settling into harbor, and nothing in the world required her to be anything but a woman alone with her ledgers.

She had been awake, of course. She was always awake at this hour. Nine years of working the night audit had taught her body to rise before the bells, before the lamplighters doused their wicks, before the first carters began their arguments with the loading gates. There was a particular pleasure in being the only conscious thing in a house full of sleeping power—the bottled storms humming on their shelves in the Long Vault, the borrowed years ticking softly in their lacquered cases, the names in the auction annex whispering to themselves in voices only she could hear.

*No one knows,* she reminded herself, as she did every morning, pulling on her coat in the dark of her narrow room above the counting hall. *No one has ever known.* The gift stayed folded up inside her like a letter never sent, and as long as it stayed folded, she was safe. Invisible. A clerk with ink-stained fingers and no remarkable qualities whatsoever, which was the finest disguise ever devised, because no one looked twice at a person they believed they had already catalogued.

The knock came again. Three slow strikes, spaced too far apart, the rhythm of someone spending strength they could not afford.

Casper went down through the counting hall with a lamp held high, past the shelves where the guild's messenger-moths slept in their cedar cages—rows upon rows of grey-winged carriers who carried not letters but *intentions*, whispered into their ears by masters who paid handsomely for discretion. As she passed, the nearest cage stirred. Wings rustled against wood.

*Cold,* said a voice like dry paper tearing. *So cold. The door is cold and something is coming up through the stone.*

Casper tightened her jaw and did not answer. That was the rule, and the rule was the whole architecture of her life: hear everything, acknowledge nothing. The moths talked in their sleep sometimes—they were creatures built from borrowed intention, and intentions leaked—and a beast-speaker who answered them was a beast-speaker the Guild would find very useful indeed, and useful things got owned.

She kept walking. But the moth's words followed her down the stairs, because the cold was real. She felt it through the soles of her boots before she reached the entrance hall, a chill rising out of the flagstones like water through cloth, and beneath it—not sound exactly, but pressure. The kind of silence that has weight. The kind that presses on the eardrums the way deep water does.

The front doors of the Vellum Guild were iron-banded oak older than the city around them, carved with the Guild's motto: *ALL THINGS MAY BE HELD*. Casper lifted the bar—it took both hands, as always—and pulled one leaf open onto the steps.

A woman lay there.

She was stretched on her side on the cold stone, as if she had walked as far as she could and then simply been set down by whatever had been carrying her. Her clothes had once been fine; Casper could see the ghost of silver embroidery along a hem gone grey with road-dust, the remains of a traveler's cloak mended and remended until mending was most of what it was. Her hair was black shot through with white, unbound, tangled with burrs. One hand was pressed flat against the door itself, fingers spread, as though she had knocked and then lacked the strength to take her hand back.

Her eyes found Casper's. They were brown, ordinary, and burning.

"Please," the woman said, and the word cost her visibly, like a coin pried from a clenched fist. "I know what this house is. I know what you keep here." A wet breath. "I need you to hold something."

"We're closed," Casper said automatically, and then hated herself for it. The lamplight caught the woman's face and showed Casper what she should have seen at once: the waxy pallor, the blue at the lips, the blood-dark stain spreading slowly along the woman's side where she pressed her own arm against herself. This was not a customer. This was a woman finishing a journey with the last of her fuel.

"I'm not selling," the woman said, and almost smiled. "I'm *depositing*."

Then Casper saw the child.

The girl had been sitting against the doorframe this entire time, tucked into the shadow between the door and its frame, so still that Casper's eyes had slid over her the way eyes slide over furniture. She was perhaps six years old, small for it, wrapped in a blanket that had been dyed a green so deep it was nearly black. She had enormous dark eyes and hair cut raggedly short, as if someone had done it in haste with a knife. And she was looking at Casper—not at the Guild, not at the grand iron-banded door with its motto, not at the possibility of shelter or bread or warmth, but at Casper herself, with an attention so total it felt like being read aloud.

"Hello," Casper said to her.

The child said nothing. The child did not move. But somewhere behind Casper, inside the hall, every messenger-moth in the building woke at once—a sound like a thousand pages turning—and then went utterly silent, which was somehow worse.

"The Guild holds things," the woman said. "That's the trade. Bottled weather. Borrowed time. Names." Her breath rattled. "Hold her."

"Where is—" Casper glanced past the steps, down the empty avenue. Fog lay along the cobblestones in long grey ropes. No carriage. No escort. No one. "Who are you? What happened to you?"

"What always happens." The woman tried to push herself upright, failed, and settled for turning more fully toward Casper, one hand still splayed on the door. "They erased a country two hundred years ago and called it peace, and they have spent every year since making certain it stays erased. Maps burned. Records burned. Names struck from gravestones. And the people who remembered—" She coughed, and the cough was terrible, deep and red-flecked. "The people who remembered became inconvenient."

"You're wounded. I should fetch a physiker—"

"There's no time and we both know it." Her voice hardened, and for a moment Casper saw the shape of whoever she had been before the road wore her down: someone accustomed to being obeyed. "Listen to me. I have run for eleven days. I chose this door because your Guild is the one institution in this city that cannot afford curiosity. You people don't ask where things come from. You only ask what they're worth." Another smile flickered and died. "So don't ask. Hold her. Feed her. Keep her away from mirrors, if you can, and away from men in grey coats, absolutely. And do not let anyone make her speak."

"Make her—"

"She doesn't speak." The woman's hand left the door and found the child's shoulder, and the girl leaned into the touch with a fluid trust that undid something in Casper's chest. "Not with her mouth. Not anymore. It isn't that she *can't*." Her grip tightened. "It's that every word costs her, and she has already paid more than any child should. Do you understand? Whatever they offer you, however they test her, she must not be made to give. Promise me."

Casper had spent nine years promising nothing to anyone. Promises were inventory; promises got collected. But the woman's eyes were fixed on hers with the terrible clarity of the dying, who have no patience left for evasion, and Casper heard herself say, "I promise," before she understood she was going to say it.

The woman exhaled, and some vast tension went out of her, and Casper saw with horror that it was not relief settling over her face but simply the end of resistance. She sagged against the doorframe. Her hand slid off the child's shoulder.

"Mama?" said the girl.

One word. One small cracked word, the first sound Casper had heard from her—and the world *flinched*. Casper felt it pass through her like a bell struck inside her own ribs. Every animal within earshot stopped. In the stables across the lane, the draft horses went still mid-shift. Somewhere above, the pigeons roosting under the Guild's eaves fell silent all together. Even the fog seemed to pause, hanging in its ropes, listening.

And the moths—Casper heard them clearly now, though the cages were three rooms away and the doors between were shut—heard them all crying out in their small torn-paper voices, a single phrase repeated over and over:

*Old tongue. Old tongue. Old tongue. Run.*

The woman's eyes had closed. Casper dropped to her knees on the frozen steps and pressed her fingers to the woman's throat, and found only the slow tide going out.

"No," Casper said uselessly. "No—wait—"

But the woman had known. Of course she had known; she had chosen this door knowing, had spent her last eleven days reaching it. Her face was calm in death in a way it had not been calm in life, and her open hand had come to rest palm-up beside her, and lying across that palm was a scrap of oilcloth, folded small, tied with waxed thread.

Casper sat back on her heels in the freezing dawn light, and the child climbed carefully over her mother's body and stood on the top step, looking down at Casper, waiting.

* * *

By the time the sky went from slate to pewter, Casper had done three things, in order: covered the woman with the child's blanket (the child had removed it herself, matter-of-factly, and laid it over her mother's face, and stood shivering in her shift and dress without complaint), sent the stable boy Tallow for the watch-surgeon with instructions that would raise questions she'd deal with later, and carried the scrap of oilcloth upstairs to the counting hall, because she could not bring herself to open it on the steps where the mother could see.

The girl followed her.

Not closely—three paces back, always, a precise distance, the distance of someone who had learned exactly how much space to keep between herself and other people's tempers. She walked through the great counting hall with its thousand drawers and its chandeliers of suspended glass vials, past the Long Vault door with its nine locks, past the auction annex where the names murmured in their sleep, and she looked at none of it. She looked at Casper. Only at Casper. As though the entire impossible edifice of the Vellum Guild were scenery, and Casper were the thing worth watching.

Casper set the oilcloth packet on her desk, under the lamp. Around her the ledgers rose in their towers: acquisitions, disbursements, holdings, liens. Nine years of numbers that behaved themselves. She untied the waxed thread with fingers that were, she noticed distantly, completely steady, because her body had learned long ago that steadiness was safer than feeling.

Inside was a page torn from a book—thick paper, deckled edge, the torn margin showing the ghost of stitching—and on it, in ink gone brown with age or with something else, symbols.

Not letters. Not any script Casper knew, and she knew scripts; the Guild dealt in documents the way banks dealt in coin. Not the angular trade-cant of the eastern ports, not the flowing contract-hand of the southern courts, not the sigil-work of licensed hedge-witches. These marks were older-looking than any of it—curved and jointed like river reeds, arranged not in lines but in *spirals*, each spiral coiling inward toward a dense central knot, the way water coils toward a drain.

Except she did know them. That was the wrongness at the bottom of everything, the cold current under the ice. She did not recognize these symbols the way one recognizes a foreign alphabet—from outside, as shapes. She recognized them the way one recognizes a face across a crowded market: instantly, intimately, with a jolt of *that's mine*, before memory catches up and insists you must be mistaken.

She had never seen them before in her life.

Behind her, small bare feet shifted on the floorboards.

Casper turned. The girl was standing at the edge of the lamplight, watching the page, not Casper. And her expression—that total, unnerving stillness—had broken. What moved across her face now was grief, raw and unhidden, the grief of someone looking at the last surviving piece of a destroyed home.

"That's yours," Casper said quietly. "Isn't it."

The girl didn't nod. She stepped forward instead, into the light, and took the pencil stub that lived in the ledger's gutter—Casper hadn't offered it, hadn't gestured, and yet the girl took it as naturally as breathing—and she knelt on the floor beside Casper's desk, on the cold boards, in her thin shift, and she drew.

Casper watched the spirals come alive under the child's hand. Faster than the dead woman's careful ink, rougher, but unmistakably the same language: coil after coil, symbol nested in symbol, the pencil moving with the fluency of a native speaker thinking aloud. The girl filled the corner of the ledger page, then the margin, then, when Casper silently slid a fresh sheet of foolscap across the desk, the center of that, working with a desperate urgency, as if the words were escaping her faster than she could catch them—as if she had eleven days' worth of unsaid things backed up behind her teeth and only her hand for a mouth.

When she finished, she sat back on her heels and looked up at Casper, and pushed the page across.

Casper looked down at it.

And understood it.

Not translated it—there was no effort, no laborious matching of symbol to meaning, no scholarly work at all. She simply *knew*, the way she knew the smell of rain coming, the way she knew what the caged moths wanted at midnight, the way she knew the mood of horses in the dark. The knowledge arrived in her whole at once, uninvited, and spoke itself in a voice that was not quite her own:

***We hid the door in the counting-house of those who count all things. Find it before the grey coats do. Wake what sleeps below. Wake the choir.***

The lamp guttered, though there was no draft.

Every hair on Casper's arms stood up. She looked at the page and the meaning stayed, patient, terrible, coiled in the spirals like smoke in a bottle. *Wake what sleeps below.* Below the counting hall. Below the Long Vault. Below nine locks and two hundred years of the Guild's foundations—

Below, faint and far and unmistakable, something sang.

One note only. A single sustained tone, low, rising up through the flagstones, through the feet of her desk, through the soles of her boots, a note like a held breath, like the moment before a name is spoken. The girl tilted her head at it, listening, and her expression was not afraid.

Casper's was.

Because she knew that note. She had heard it her whole life, or rather heard *around* it, heard the gap it left in the world's noise, the way you hear the missing tooth in a comb. Every animal in this building had always avoided the cellar stairs. Every cat, every rat, every moth in its cage bent its path around that one dark staircase, and she had told herself for nine years it was nothing, old stone, bad air, the ordinary hauntings of an old house.

The note faded. The lamps steadied. Outside, the bells of the dawn hour began to ring, and the city shook itself awake, and somewhere below in the entrance hall the surgeon's boots were arriving to ask difficult questions about a dead woman on the steps.

Casper reached out and touched the doorframe of the counting hall—the old painted wood, the grain worn smooth by a century of clerks' shoulders—simply to steady herself, simply to touch something solid and ordinary while her carefully constructed life came apart in her hands.

Every animal within a mile went still.

She felt it happen. Felt it the way you feel a wave pass under a boat: the horses in the lane, the pigeons, the rats in the walls, the sparrows in the gutters, the fat carp in the fountain by the customs house—all of it stopping at once, all of it turning, all of it *listening to her*, or listening through her, or listening to whatever had just woken up and put its hand, briefly and gently, on the back of her neck.

The girl watched her from the floor, dark-eyed, and pointed one small finger—at Casper, at the floorboards, at the deep places below—and shaped, silently, with careful lips, a word Casper could not hear but understood anyway, the way she was understanding far too many things this morning:

*You.*

Above them, the first bell finished ringing. Below, the choir waited. And Casper Kavanagh, invisible woman, keeper of perfect silence, stood with her hand on a doorframe and knew that her nine years of quiet had just ended—not with a knock, but with a note.
