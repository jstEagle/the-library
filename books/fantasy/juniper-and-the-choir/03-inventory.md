## 3. Inventory

Oren Vetch believed in three things, and he kept all three the way a miser keeps coins: close, counted, and never spent.

The first was that everything could be classified. The second was that anything unclassified was merely awaiting its proper term. The third was that sentiment was a form of leakage—value draining out of an institution through cracks no ledger line could account for. He had built the Vellum Guild's reputation on sealing those cracks. Thirty-one years as guildmaster, and in that time not one asset had left his custody without a full accounting: what it was, what it cost, what it would fetch, and to whom. Bottled storms had shelf-lives; borrowed years accrued interest; names, once auctioned, were tracked through every transaction like bloodhounds follow scent. Vetch's ledgers were famous for it. Merchants from three coasts came to study them. Some came to steal them, and those men now fed the river or the worms, depending on which got there first.

So when the night-porter brought him word at dawn—a woman dead on the steps, a child alive beside her—Vetch did not think *orphan*. Orphans were a category belonging to temples and charities, entities that dealt in pity, and pity was leakage.

He thought *acquisition*.

He dressed before the porter finished speaking, because a man who moved slowly in the morning moved slowly all day, and pulled on the grey coat with the nine silver buttons, each stamped with the Guild's seal: a quill piercing a closed eye. By the time he reached the front hall, the body had already been removed—the porter knew better than to let a corpse cool where clients might see it—and only the child remained, sitting exactly where she'd been found, on the second step from the bottom, with her knees drawn up and her hands folded in her lap like a small merchant waiting for a fair price.

Vetch stopped on the landing above and looked at her for a long moment before descending.

She was perhaps seven. Dark hair cut raggedly, as if by someone shaking and hurried. A coat too large for her, good wool once, stained at the hem with something dark. No doll, no bundle, no letter of introduction clutched in her fists—which was itself informative. The dead woman had left nothing but the girl herself. Either she had owned nothing worth leaving, or she had judged the girl to be the whole of her estate.

Children abandoned at the Guild's door were rare enough that Vetch could count them on one hand across three decades. Usually they arrived by accident—a mother fleeing a debt, a father misjudging the Guild's mercy. Those children went to the temple within a day, with a receipt filed and a small donation attached, because charity performed cheaply still bought goodwill. But this child—

This child had not cried.

Vetch descended the stairs slowly, his shoes ringing on marble quarried from a mountain whose name the Guild owned outright, and the girl watched him come with an attention he found professionally interesting. Not fear. Not the blank shock of trauma. Attention. She tracked him the way the caged messenger-moths tracked lamplight—wholly, silently, with their entire bodies.

"Good morning," Vetch said, and crouched to her level, which he did rarely and only when calculation favored it. "I'm told you were left here."

The girl looked at him.

"Can you tell me your name?"

She opened her mouth. What came out was not a word. It was—Vetch would search for the right classification of it later, turning the memory over like an appraiser with a suspect gem—it was a sound made of shapes. Three syllables, none of them belonging to any language he knew, and yet arranged with such deliberate structure that they could not be babble. A toddler's nonsense scatters. This did not scatter. It landed.

Then she closed her mouth and said nothing more.

Vetch straightened. His knee protested; he ignored it, as he ignored most things that belonged to the body rather than the books.

"Bring me Kestrel-Marrow," he told the porter. "And the scribes. All of them. And have the holding room prepared—the third one, with the reinforced door." He paused. "No. The fifth. The third faces the street."

The girl's eyes followed every order as it was given. Counting, Vetch thought. She is counting us.

He approved. It showed promise.

* * *

By midmorning the hall smelled of ink and cold tea, and four scribes sat around the child at the long appraisal table while she drew.

Vetch stood at the head of the table with the acquisition ledger open before him, and he wrote as the scribes worked, because a thing unwritten was a thing unprotected, and the Guild's whole architecture rested on the axiom that writing preceded owning.

**Item:** One female child, approx. age six to eight years.
**Provenance:** Deposited at premises prior to dawn, depositor deceased (see Coroner's Receipt #4471).
**Condition:** Fed? Unclear. Uninjured visible. Coat of quality exceeding apparent means.
**Contents:** Unknown. Possibly valuable.

That last line he underlined twice.

Kestrel-Marrow, the Guild's senior scribe, a dry stick of a man who had appraised everything from a saint's severed finger to a bottled plague, knelt opposite the child with a slate and chalk and conducted the standard battery. Numbers first—children almost always gave up numbers, even mute ones, because numbers lived deeper than shame. She took his chalk, considered it, and drew instead: a circle, then a line through it, then a row of small hooked marks beneath, marching left to right with the confidence of script.

"Not numerals," Kestrel-Marrow reported, without looking up. "Directional consistency suggests writing, not drawing. Stroke order is disciplined. She writes the way scribes write, master. Left to right. Same pressure. Same sequence, repeated."

"Repeat it," Vetch said.

The scribe set a fresh slate before her. The child studied it, then reproduced the marks exactly—not approximately, *exactly*, down to the angle of each hook—and then added a fourth symbol that had not appeared before, as if correcting her own earlier omission.

Vetch felt the particular cold pleasure he reserved for assets that exceeded their initial valuation. "Language or cipher?"

"Unknown. None of our reference scripts align. I've checked High Tarnish, Low Tarnish, the mercantile pidgins, the temple hands, the old coast-runes." Kestrel-Marrow hesitated, and Vetch noted the hesitation the way he noted all hesitations—as a symptom of something being withheld or something not understood, and both required investigation. "There is one further test. The mirrors."

"No mirrors yet." Vetch closed the ledger a fraction. Mirrors were expensive instruments; they revealed aptitude, and aptitude revealed market value, and market value revealed to the scribes themselves how much temptation they were handling. He preferred his staff ignorant until he chose otherwise. "Blood-ink this afternoon. If she's marked, we'll know her lineage without troubling her tongue."

The child, throughout, did not resist. That was the detail that unsettled the scribes, Vetch could see it in their sideways glances—they expected tears, screaming, the ordinary animal panic of a small creature handled by large ones. Instead she cooperated fully and completely, performing every task asked of her with grave precision, and gave away nothing whatsoever. It was not compliance. Vetch had seen compliance; it leaked relief. This was *consent to be measured while withholding the merchandise*. 

He found himself thinking of a certain oyster the Guild had once acquired, which had held a pearl so far back in its flesh that three appraisers had declared it empty before the fourth, patient and half-blind, had found it.

"Name her provisionally," Vetch said, dipping his pen. "We don't file blanks."

"She won't say—" began the youngest scribe.

"I didn't ask what she says. I asked you to name her."

Kestrel-Marrow cleared his throat. "The porter mentioned she was found among juniper branches. Someone had cut a sprig and laid it over her. Still green, though it's the wrong season for it."

Vetch wrote: **Working designation: Juniper.** The pen scratched. Somewhere in the walls, faintly, the messenger-moths stirred against their cages—he heard the soft papery rush of wings, hundreds of them, though no message was being prepared. They did that sometimes at odd hours. He had long ago classified it as restlessness and thought no more about it.

"Hold her for full appraisal," he said. "Ten days. Feeding, sleeping, observation. No visitors. No temple. And nothing leaves this building about her—not a rumor, not a description, not a whisper. If the Crown's people hear we're holding an unclassified linguistic asset, we'll have Bureau clerks crawling through my halls inside the month, and they don't pay, they *confiscate*." He looked around the table, meeting each scribe's eyes in turn, and enjoyed the small stiffening that went through all of them. "Am I understood?"

Four heads inclined.

The child—Juniper, provisionally—watched him sign the order, and Vetch had the distinct impression, absurd and quickly dismissed, that she was reading the entry upside down.

* * *

The holding rooms occupied the east wing, behind two locked doors and a corridor that appeared on no client-facing map of the building. The fifth room had been prepared by afternoon: narrow bed, water basin, brazier, a window barred and glazed with the Guild's fog-glass that let light in but gave nothing back to look at. Adequate. Humane, even, if one cared about such classifications, which Vetch did not, except insofar as mistreatment degraded assets, and degraded assets sold poorly.

He inspected it personally at dusk, because trust was a currency he extended to no one, and found the arrangements satisfactory. The girl sat on the bed with her legs folded under her, and on the blanket before her, in chalk smuggled—note that, note *how*—from somewhere, she had covered the wool with drawings.

Vetch stood in the doorway and let himself look.

She drew doors. Doors of every proportion, some round-topped, some square, one with thirteen panels rendered in obsessive miniature. Around them she drew lines—not roads, he decided after a moment, not rivers. *Routes.* Lines that approached the doors and stopped just short of them, again and again, as though rehearsing an arrival she could not complete.

"You draw well," Vetch said.

She did not look up.

"The man who brings your supper is called Doss. If you need the privy, knock twice on the wall. The pipes carry sound." He delivered the instructions in the flat tone of a warehouse manifest, watching her the whole time. "You'll be tested tomorrow. It will not hurt. Cooperate, and things will go easily for you."

At *things*, finally, her eyes lifted to his face, and what Vetch saw in them rearranged something in his chest that he had not known was loose.

It was not hatred. Hatred he could have worked with; hatred was a transaction like any other, payable in concessions. It was not fear either, and that was the wrongness of it. She looked at Oren Vetch, guildmaster, owner of storms and years and names, keeper of thirty-one unbroken ledgers—and she looked at him with *pity*. Brief, precise, almost courteous, the way one looks at a man counting coins in a burning house. Then she returned to her chalk, and drew another line toward a door it could not reach.

Vetch left the room faster than his dignity strictly allowed, and stood a moment in the corridor composing himself, and told himself the chill along his arms was the draft from the stairwell. The east wing was always cold. Everything in the east wing was cold. There was no other explanation, and explanations were his trade.

* * *

He was crossing the ground-floor gallery an hour later, ledger under arm, when he saw the figure by the holding corridor's outer door.

Casper Kavanagh stood at the junction of the west passage with a crate-manifest in her hands and the particular stillness of a person pretending to read. Vetch knew that stillness intimately. He had cultivated it in himself for decades. Nine years she had worked his ledgers—quiet, punctual, invisible, the best auditor he had ever employed precisely because she seemed to want nothing, and wantlessness was rarer than talent—but he had not risen to guildmaster by failing to notice when an employee's feet pointed somewhere her business did not.

The holding corridor lay behind her left shoulder. Her eyes had not touched it. That was the tell. Guilty people look at what they want; Casper Kavanagh was carefully looking at a manifest she had certainly memorized an hour ago, which meant she had already looked, and was now engaged in the amateur's error of believing that not-looking erased having-looked.

"Casper," Vetch said pleasantly.

She turned with flawless composure. "Master Vetch. The storm-crate audit is finished. Eleven bottles intact, two degraded, losses itemized on page four."

"Efficient as ever." He crossed the gallery unhurried, letting his footsteps fill the space. "Tell me—did the porter wake you this morning? With all the commotion on the steps?"

"A little. I heard voices."

"Did you see the woman?"

"No, sir. Only the crowd after."

A clean answer, promptly given, with the exact degree of elaboration a truthful person offers. Vetch nodded slowly. He did not believe a word of the pattern of it—too smooth, too pre-measured, like coin weighed on a rigged scale—but belief was not yet required. Suspicion, however, was a thing he collected early and compounded daily.

"We've taken in an unusual acquisition," he said, watching her face with the same attention he'd given the child. "A ward, for now. There may be work in it for someone with a careful eye. Appraisal support. Observation." He let the word sit between them. "You have a gift for noticing what others overlook."

Something flickered behind Casper Kavanagh's grey expression—there and gone, fast as a moth's wing-beat. "I only do ledgers, Master Vetch."

"Yes," Vetch said softly. "So you do. Remarkable how much can be learned from ledgers alone."

He walked on toward his office, and did not turn his head, and counted her silence behind him the way he counted everything: precisely, and against her.

In his office he unlocked the bottom drawer, took out the private ledger—the black one, the one no scribe had ever seen—and made a new entry in his own hand:

**Item (provisional): Child, "Juniper." Origin unknown. Language unknown. Value: pending.
Note:** Auditor C. Kavanagh observed at holding corridor 21:40. Interest unexplained. Watch.

He blotted the page, considered, and added a second line beneath, in smaller characters, the kind of entry he made perhaps once a decade when an object refused to sit quietly in its category:

**Classification difficulty: high. The asset appears to be doing the appraising.**

Down in the east wing, behind fog-glass and reinforced oak, the child slept—or seemed to sleep—and on the blanket beside her, chalked in a hand older than any kingdom Vetch could name, the last door of the night had thirteen panels, and standing before it, small and unmistakable despite the crude strokes, was a figure with a ledger under one arm.

But Vetch did not know that yet. He only knew that for the first time in thirty-one years, an item in his inventory had made him feel, briefly and intolerably, like inventory himself—and Oren Vetch had never once in his life permitted a thing on his shelves to look back at him.

Tomorrow, he decided, he would order the mirrors brought out after all.

Whatever was inside that child, he intended to see it before it saw him again.
