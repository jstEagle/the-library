## 4. What the Moths Know

The moth house sat at the far end of the guild's eastern wing, a long room of slatted cages and hanging lanterns of smoked glass, and Casper hated it the way one hates the site of an old wound—not the pain itself, but the memory of when it was made.

She had drawn this duty twice a year for nine years because no one else wanted it. The messenger-moths required feeding at the turn of each season: honeyed water steeped in moonvine, a pinch of salt-ash from the guild's own hearths, and—because the Vellum Guild dealt in impossible things and priced them accordingly—a single drop of blood from whoever tended them. The moths carried letters folded into wax capsules on their backs across distances that would kill a horse and its rider both, through weather that had no business existing, guided by senses the guild's founders had bought or bred or stolen from somewhere they never wrote down. They were worth more per ounce than gold. They were treated worse than the guildhounds.

And they talked.

That was the part no one else knew. To every other keeper in the building, the moth house was silent but for the soft percussion of wings against slatted wood. But Casper heard them. She had always heard them—the small grey voices, thin as thread, that rose the moment she stepped over the threshold and did not stop until she left it.

*Warm hands. Warm hands come back.*

*Hungry. Hungry, hungry, the moonvine is sour, the water is old—*

*Flew the Salt Reach once. Flew it with my sister. Where is my sister?*

*Cold tonight. Cold tonight. Cold tonight.*

Nine years she had listened and said nothing. Nine years of standing among the cages with her jaw locked and her gift shoved down so deep it ached like a swallowed stone, because answering was the first step off a cliff she could not see the bottom of. Her grandmother's voice lived in her memory like a brand pressed into wax: *Never answer them, Casper. Not once. A door opened even a crack lets in the whole wind.* Grandmother Ilsa had died believing she had kept her granddaughter safe. Casper had spent nine years proving her right, one silence at a time.

Tonight, though, the silence felt different. Tonight it felt less like armor and more like a lid on something boiling.

She set down the feeding tray and began the rounds, cage by cage, dipping each glass spout into its bracket. The moths came to the water in a rustle of grey-and-silver wings, dozens of them, their bodies no bigger than her thumbnail, their compound eyes catching the lamplight in facets of oil-slick color. Their voices layered over one another until the room hummed with a sound only she could hear, like a seashell held to the ear by someone drowning.

*Warm hands. Warm hands.*

*New thing downstairs. New thing, new thing—*

Casper's hand stilled on the third spout.

*What new thing,* said another voice, and another took it up, and within moments the whole room was murmuring it, a tide of small grey curiosity: *new thing, new thing, the quiet child, the child who does not speak but speaks anyway—*

"Stop," Casper said under her breath, and caught herself, appalled. Not aloud. Never aloud. But the word had slipped out shaped and half-voiced, more than thought and less than speech, and several of the nearest moths lifted from their perches and beat against the cage fronts toward her, wings flashing, frantic with attention.

*She spoke. She spoke, she spoke—*

"I didn't." Her voice came out rough. She cleared her throat and kept pouring. "Drink your water."

They drank. They also kept talking, which was typical. The moths had never once obeyed her, not because they couldn't understand but because, as far as she could tell, they considered conversation a form of flight—you did it because it was what you were for.

*The child below,* said the eldest moth, the one in the iron-banded cage at the room's center, the one they called Old Grey because no keeper had ever managed a better name for it. Old Grey had been in the guild longer than Casper had been alive. It had carried messages through three wars and a plague and once, according to a ledger entry Casper had found as an apprentice, through the inside of a storm. Its voice was slower than the others, drier, like paper turning. *The child below is not silent, warm hands.*

"She doesn't speak." Casper moved to the next cage. "Believe me, the guild has tried everything short of torture to get a word out of her."

*I do not say she does not speak. I say she is not silent.* Old Grey flexed its wings once, slowly, the way an old man might unfold his hands before telling a story he'd told too many times. *You have ears built for mouths. We have ears built for older things.*

Casper poured the last spout and stood there with the empty tray against her chest, not moving toward the door. This was the trap of the moth house, the reason she drew the duty twice a year and no more often: the moths talked, and she listened, and listening was already halfway to answering, and answering was the cliff.

But the child was downstairs. The child with the ink-dark symbols that no scribe in the Vellum Guild—and the Vellum Guild employed scribes who read dead scripts the way other men read street signs—could parse. The child whose arrival had made every animal within a mile go still, a silence Casper had felt pass through her own bones like a struck bell fading.

"What do you mean, older things?" she asked the room at large, keeping her voice flat, professional, the voice of a woman discussing freight rates.

The moths surged. They loved a question; questions were open doors and they were creatures of passage. A dozen voices tumbled over each other—

*Older, older—*

*Before the walls, before the roads—*

*We remember, we were alive then, some of us, our mothers' mothers' mothers—*

"—enough," Casper snapped, and this time it came out fully voiced, sharp as a slap, and the room went still. Every wing stopped. Forty pairs of facet eyes turned to her, and the silence they made was enormous, pressing, the silence of creatures who had just realized something and were deciding what to do about it.

It was Old Grey who finally spoke.

*You answered us.*

"I corrected you. There's a difference."

*You used your mouth.* The old moth's voice had gone strange, careful, the way a man walks on ice he suspects is thin. *Nine years you have stood in this room and let us speak to you and given back nothing but your thoughts, and your thoughts are loud, warm hands, louder than you know, but thoughts are not words. Words are different. Words are doors.*

"You sound like my grandmother."

*Perhaps we sound like everyone who ever understood what a mouth is for.* Old Grey climbed the inside of its cage with slow deliberation, settling on the slats directly before her face. Up close its body was dusted silver, and its eyes held her reflection a hundred times over, tiny and fractured. *We have been trying to tell you something since you walked in. You keep talking over it.*

Casper set the tray down on the workbench. Her heart was doing something unprofessional. "Then tell me."

The other moths rustled, a wave passing through the cages like wind through wheat, and their murmur dropped to something almost reverent.

*The child's language,* Old Grey said, *is not unknown.*

"It's unknown. Three scribes—"

*Unknown to scribes. Scribes are very young. Scribes are children playing with the ashes of fires they never saw lit.* There was no malice in it, only the flat patience of great age. *The child speaks the tongue that was spoken before tongues were written. Before kingdoms put borders around breath. We know it, warm hands, because we were alive when it was alive. Our line remembers it the way your blood remembers how to bleed.*

The lamp flame leaned in a draft that wasn't there. Casper became aware that she was gripping the edge of the workbench hard enough to whiten her knuckles.

"That's impossible," she said. "Scripts change. Languages die. Nothing survives two thousand years intact—"

*Who spoke of years?* Another moth now, a young one from the western racks, its voice bright and quick. *We don't count years. We count winds. And there were winds before there were maps to complain about them.*

*The tongue has no years,* Old Grey agreed. *It has speakers. That is all it has ever had. When the last speaker stops, it does not die—it waits. It waits in anything old enough to hold a shape. In stone that remembers being mountain. In rivers. In us.* The old moth's wings opened, closed. *And in the child. The child is not speaking a dead language, warm hands. The child is speaking a waiting one.*

Casper's mouth had gone dry. "Waiting for what?"

The moths did not answer immediately, and their silence was worse than any answer, because it was the silence of creatures choosing how much truth a frightened human could carry.

*For a mouth,* said Old Grey at last. *A tongue is a river, and a speaker is a bed for it to run in. The child is a bed. Perhaps the last clean bed left.* A pause, delicate as a wing landing. *Or perhaps not the last.*

The draft again. Casper turned her head slowly and saw nothing but the lanterns, the cages, the dark windows giving back her own pale reflection—but beneath her feet, through the floorboards, through the stone, she felt it: that faint pressure she'd noticed once before and dismissed as imagination, like a note sung too low for ears and felt instead in the teeth. Something below. Something vast and patient, holding its breath.

*There are other beds,* whispered the young moth from the western racks, and its bright voice had gone small. *Deep ones. Under this house. We do not fly over the middle of the cellar floor. Have you wondered why, warm hands? Have you wondered why in ninety years no moth of ours has ever crossed that room?*

Casper hadn't. She filed the question away in the place where she kept everything she refused to think about, and it burned going down.

"The guild will want to sell her," she heard herself say. "Whatever she is. Whatever she speaks. Vetch will have her classified and appraised by week's end, and some buyer will pay a fortune for a girl who talks in a language no one can read, and she'll be crated and shipped like—" Like the bottled storms. Like the borrowed years. Like everything else in this building that had once been alive and free and was now inventory. Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated herself for it.

*Yes,* said Old Grey simply. *That is what houses like this do. It is why we are here too, warm hands. Do not mistake your kindness for our ignorance.*

"So what am I supposed to do?" The question tore out of her louder than she intended, ragged, and the moths flared toward the cage fronts in a shimmer of wings. "I'm an auditor. I audit crates. I am very good at being unnoticed—that is my single talent, that is the wall I have spent nine years building, brick by brick, and if I step outside it, if anyone learns what I can hear, what I am—" She stopped, chest heaving. "My grandmother hid me. She paid something—I never learned what—to hide me, and I am not going to undo her sacrifice because a moth told me bedtime stories about prehistoric languages."

The room was quiet. Then, gently, in a voice like ash settling:

*Your grandmother Ilsa spoke that tongue.*

The floor did not move. The lamps did not gutter. But something in Casper's chest went over a ledge.

"That's not—" Her voice failed and restarted. "Her family was from the eastern valleys. She was a wool merchant's daughter. She never—"

*She spoke it the way you speak your silences,* Old Grey said. *We flew for her once. One flight. She paid us in a memory we still carry—we do not know whose it was, only that it tasted of snow and a door. She asked us to carry a message to a woman in the capital, and the message was this:* the old moth paused, and every other voice in the room stilled to let it through, *the price is coming due for all of us, and I have hidden my granddaughter where the ledgers won't look. Forgive me.*

Casper did not remember crossing the room. She was simply suddenly there, forehead nearly touching the iron-banded cage, her breath fogging the slats, staring into the hundredfold reflections of her own stricken face.

"That's not possible. She died when I was twelve. She was—she sold wool, she mended nets, she was ordinary, she was *boring*, she was—"

*She was a speaker who chose silence,* Old Grey said, not unkindly. *As you are a speaker who chose silence. As the child below is a speaker who has not yet chosen anything, and is being watched by men with scales and prices.* The old moth's wings trembled once. *Silence hides you, warm hands. It has hidden you well. But it does not protect the child. Only a voice can do that. Only a voice can stand between her and the ones who want to own her, and there is exactly one other voice in this city that carries the same blood-tongue, and it is currently pretending to feed moths.*

Casper stepped back. Her legs felt borrowed. "I can't. You don't understand what you're asking. If I start—if I answer you, if I learn whatever it is you're offering to teach me, I can never go back to being invisible, and invisible is the only reason I've survived this place. The masters here collect things that are rare. If they find out what I am, I stop being a person to them and start being an asset."

*Yes,* said Old Grey. *All true. All known.* A pause, and then the old moth said the thing that Casper would carry up four flights of stairs that night, carry into sleep, carry out again at dawn:

*Now weigh it against what happens to the child if you stay hidden.*

* * *

She stayed until the lamps burned low, though she did not mean to. The moths talked, and for the first time in nine years she let herself truly listen—not with her guard up, cataloguing and dismissing, but openly, the way she had as a child before her grandmother's warning had taught her to close that door.

They told her about the Salt Reach and the storm-flight, about the sister who had been sold to a trade house in the south and flown until her wings gave out. They told her about winds before maps. They told her fragments of the old tongue itself—not teaching, not yet, but letting her overhear, the way one overhears music from a neighboring room: syllables like river-stones, vowels that seemed to bend light, a grammar that put the speaker inside every sentence rather than above it. She did not understand it. But some animal chamber of her, some locked room behind her sternum, recognized it. Recognized it the way an orphan recognizes a lullaby she was too young to remember learning.

Near the end, the young moth from the western racks landed on the outside of Old Grey's cage, and said, shyly:

*You answered us tonight. Twice. Truly answered, with your mouth.* Its bright voice quavered. *Do you know what that means, warm hands?*

"That I've broken a promise to a dead woman?"

*That a door is open now,* the young moth said. *Not wide. A crack. But doors do not close again once they have been opened, not really. They only pretend.* It fluttered, resettled. *We waited nine years for you to answer. The child below has been waiting since the night she was set on the steps. And the thing under the cellar floor—*

*Enough,* said Old Grey, softly, and the young moth subsided.

But the sentence hung there, unfinished, the way the worst sentences do. *And the thing under the cellar floor—* Wanted what? Had waited how long? Was patient in the manner of what?

Casper gathered the tray, trimmed the lamps, and performed every closing ritual of her twice-yearly duty with hands that were almost steady. At the door she paused, one hand on the frame, and looked back at the ranks of dim cages, the forty small watching lights.

"Thank you," she said aloud. It cost her something—she felt it leave her like a coin from a purse, small but real, irrevocable. The first payment on a debt whose size she could not yet calculate.

The moths rustled, pleased, and their murmur followed her down the corridor like a tide going out:

*Warm hands. Warm hands comes back. Comes back changed.*

In her narrow room on the fourth floor, Casper sat on the edge of her cot in the dark and did not sleep. Below her, through stone and timber and sixty feet of packed earth, she could feel it again—that low, held note, patient as sediment.

*The child is not silent,* the moths had said. *The child is a bed, waiting for a river. And perhaps you are too.*

Toward dawn, exhausted past sense, she finally slept, and dreamed of a country she had never seen, full of voices she somehow knew, all of them calling one name down a corridor of years—and the name was not Juniper's.
