## 5. The Appraisal

*Cold first. Cold is the floor. Cold is the table. Cold is the hands that turn her over like a page.*

The room has too many eyes in it.

Not people-eyes—though there are those too, four of them, five, she loses count when they move—but glass ones, brass ones, a whole shelf of mirrors angled at her like flowers turning toward a sun they intend to eat. Juniper sits on a table that is not for sitting and does not sit still inside herself. Inside herself is the only place left to sit.

They have taken her shoes. They have taken the ribbon from her hair and put it in a little tray with a label, as if a ribbon were something you could write down and thereby own. She watches it go. She lets it go. There is a list of things she will let go of before this day ends, and the ribbon is only the first, and none of them—not one—is the thing they actually want.

"Subject presents as female, approximately seven years," says a voice above her, dry as moth wings. "No response to verbal stimulus in any of the nine trade-tongues. No response to written stimulus in fourteen scripts."

Juniper looks at the ceiling instead of at them. The ceiling has a water stain shaped like a bird mid-flight, and she names the bird privately, in the old way, *sivven*, small gray singer, and the naming costs her nothing because she does not say it out loud. That is the rule. That has always been the rule, since before she had words for rules: *keep it behind your teeth, keep it warm, keep it yours.* Her mouth is a house with one door and she is the only key, and she will be dead before she hands the key across a table to men who weigh ribbons.

"Note possible deafness," says the dry voice.

She is not deaf. She hears everything. She hears the man's heart ticking too fast, like a clock someone wound out of guilt. She hears the woman with the spectacles breathing through her mouth because her nose is stopped and she is embarrassed about it. She hears, beneath the floor, far beneath the floor, down where the foundations drink from older stone—a humming. A many-throated humming, patient as roots.

She has been hearing it since they carried her inside. It is why she has not cried. Crying would be loud, and loud things get listened *to*, and if they listen closely enough—

No. Keep it behind your teeth.

* * *

The mirrors come next.

They stand her on a chalk circle and arrange the mirrors around her in a ring, seven of them, each framed in a different metal, and the woman with the spectacles explains in a bored voice that this is standard resonance testing, that Juniper should feel free to relax, that nothing here will hurt her unless she makes it hurt.

Juniper does not relax. Relaxing is how rabbits die.

In the first mirror she sees herself, ordinary, small, sleep-rumpled. In the second mirror she sees herself a half-breath late, her reflection moving after her body like a shadow that has learned to walk. In the third mirror—

In the third mirror she sees the room as it was a long time ago. Not this room. This *place*. The walls are different, higher, hung with banners the color of evening; the floor is pale wood worn soft by feet; and there are people, hundreds of them, standing where the mirrors stand, all facing inward, all singing without opening their mouths. Their song comes up through the soles of her bare feet like warmth from a hearthstone.

Her chest goes tight and hot. She knows them. She doesn't know their faces but she knows their *voices*, the way you know a lullaby you heard before you were born, and every one of them is singing the same word toward the center of the circle, toward the empty air where she stands two hundred years too late:

*Come home. Come home. Little mouth, come home.*

She slams her eyes shut. She bites the inside of her cheek until she tastes copper. When she opens her eyes the mirror shows only the room again, the chalk circle, the bored woman writing.

"Third mirror shows residual echo," the woman says. "Moderate. Possibly inherited." A pause. A pen scratch. "Possibly *significant*. Flag for second appraisal."

Juniper stands very still inside the circle and gives them nothing. Not a flinch, not a tear, not a single syllable. Let them write *residual*. Let them write *moderate*. Words are doors and she has closed hers, and what they saw in the third mirror was only light under the door, only the glow of it, never the fire.

But her hands are shaking, and she folds them together to hide it, and in the fourth mirror—the plain one, the honest one—she sees that they know she is shaking anyway.

* * *

The blood-ink is worse than the mirrors.

They tell her it won't hurt. They always say that, adults, right before the part that hurts. The man with the dry voice holds a needle-fine pen of black glass and asks her to give him three drops of blood into a shallow dish of silver liquid, and when she does not move he sighs the sigh of a man delayed over his supper, and takes her hand the way you take a parcel.

Three drops. It barely stings. But the dish *drinks* them—you can see it drinking, the silver swirling, reddening, and then shapes rise in it like smoke given bones. Letters. Or almost-letters. The alphabet of somewhere else trying to spell itself out of her blood and failing, or succeeding, and the dry-voiced man leans close and his breath stops.

"That's—" he says, and then he catches himself, smooths his face back into its ledger-blankness. "Anomalous script. Unregistered lineage markers. Note for the masters."

Juniper looks at the letters swimming in her own blood and feels, for the first time since the steps, since the cold morning and her mother's arms going loose around her, something like anger. Hot and clean and small as a struck match. Those are *hers*. Her mother bled to make her; her mother's mother before that, a long red thread of mothers going back and back and back, and every one of them paid something to pass this along, and these men in their ink-stained cuffs think a silver dish makes it inventory.

She pulls her hand away. She wipes the last bead of blood on her dress, deliberately, where they can see her do it.

The dry-voiced man writes something down. She reads it upside down, because reading upside down is one of the hundred small things her body knows that her mouth keeps locked: *"Uncooperative. Recommend name-eater."*

She does not know what a name-eater is.

She finds out within the hour.

* * *

It comes in a box. Of course it comes in a box. Everything in this building comes in boxes—she has seen the crates stacked in the corridors, labeled in a dozen hands: *bottled storm, do not tilt; borrowed year, expires; grief, compounded monthly.* The box the worm lives in is plain wood, unmarked, which frightens her more than any label could, because things that need no warning label are confident in what they are.

The creature inside is pale as a fingernail paring, long as her forearm, blind, and it moves the way syrup pours. They tip it onto a slate slab beside her and it lifts its blunt head and tastes the air with a mouth that has no teeth and needs none, because what it eats cannot be bitten.

"A name-eater," announces the woman with the spectacles, in the tone of a teacher reciting something she learned by rote and never questioned. "Harmless to the body. It consumes nomenclature—identity-residue. If the subject speaks, or even *thinks* her true name within its range, the worm will take it, and we will have the name, and the subject will simply... lack one. Very tidy. Very reversible, for our purposes, though not for hers." A small smile. "Most subjects speak within a minute. Loneliness is persuasive."

Juniper understands then, fully, what kind of place this is. Not a house. Not an orphanage. A *mouth*, a great greedy mouth disguised as a building, and everything that enters it gets tasted, sorted, swallowed, shelved. Her fear turns over and shows its other face, which is clarity.

They want her name. Fine. They may dig forever. Her name is not a word. Her name is a *country*—a small one, portable, population two—and it does not live in her mouth where worms can reach it. It lives behind her sternum, in the room with no door, and the only person who ever held the key wore it on a cord around her neck and gave it to Juniper with her dying breath and said, in the old tongue, in words that cost her the last of her blood-warmth:

*Keep it behind your teeth. Whatever they offer. Whatever they threaten. A mouth that stays shut cannot be emptied.*

So when the worm is set loose on the slate and comes flowing toward her, blind and sweet-faced and terrible, Juniper does the hardest thing she has ever done, harder than the steps, harder than the letting-go of her mother's hand: she thinks of nothing. She empties her mind the way you cup your hands to carry water—carefully, so carefully, guarding against every crack. No name. No mother. No *sivven*, no gray bird, no home. She becomes a small blank child on a table, and the worm flows up beside her, tastes the air around her head, and finds—

Nothing worth eating.

It turns away. It pours itself back toward the box as if disappointed, and the woman with the spectacles makes a low frustrated sound and writes, and the dry-voiced man says, "Remarkable discipline for her age. Or remarkable vacancy. Appraisal inconclusive. Recommend extended holding."

Extended holding. Juniper files the words away like stones for a sling.

* * *

There is one more test. She will understand later that it was not on any list.

They are carrying her back to the holding room—she walks now, having learned that walking voluntarily earns her a corridor with windows, and windows are worth more than pride—when the procession passes a wide doorway full of shelves, and on the shelves are cages, and in the cages are moths. Big ones, pale ones, wings like letters written in ash. Messenger-moths. And as she passes, every moth in the room rises at once against its cage-door, a soft thunder of wings, and they turn toward her—hundreds of small blind-seeking faces—and they do not cry out.

They go silent. All of them. A silence so complete and so sudden that the dry-voiced man stops walking and frowns at his clipboard, checking whether he has entered a dead zone.

Juniper stops too, just for a step, just for a breath. And in that breath she hears what the silence is made of. Not absence. *Attention.* The moths are listening the way the whole world listens when something old walks past—hushed, hopeful, hungry for a word they have not heard in two hundred years.

And among the cages, apart from the others, near the door, one moth is not looking at her.

It is looking at the woman holding her elbow. A tall woman, gray-cloaked, forgettable, the kind of face you lose the moment you look away—except that the moth is straining toward her with its whole small body, and the woman has gone very still, and her eyes, which have been flat as coins all day, have a crack in them now, a hairline fracture with light behind it.

The moth makes a sound. Tiny. Almost nothing. But Juniper's ears are the ears of the last mouth of Serevan, and she hears it plainly, and what the moth says—in the old tongue, in *her* tongue, broken and rusted but unmistakable—is:

*She hears us. The quiet one. She has always heard us.*

Juniper looks up at the gray woman.

The gray woman does not look back. She stares straight ahead, jaw tight, hands steady, a wall of a person, a locked door of a person. But the crack is there. Juniper has spent her whole short life learning to see cracks in walls, because cracks are how the light gets in and how the prisoners get out.

The others tug her onward, back toward the holding room, back toward the shelf where they are keeping her between appraisals, filed like the ribbon, like the storms, like the years. The door closes. The lock speaks its small iron language.

Juniper sits on the cot in the dimness and puts her hand flat against her sternum, over the country she carries, and thinks—not a name, never a name, only a shape, a hope wearing a shape:

*Gray woman. Quiet one. The moths trust you.*

*When I open my mouth—if I ever open my mouth—it will be for you.*

Down beneath the floor, far below, the many-throated humming changes key. It has noticed her noticing. It begins, very softly, very patiently, to sing a word she has not heard since the steps: her name—not her true name, but the name they will find written on the intake ledger, the bait-name, the hook-name—*Juniper.*

Over and over, like a knock. Like a promise. Like a door deciding which side of itself to open.

And somewhere above her, past the shelves and the cages and the ledgers, a gray woman who has spent nine years pretending to be furniture lies awake in the dark, hearing moths, and does not yet know that she has already been chosen.
