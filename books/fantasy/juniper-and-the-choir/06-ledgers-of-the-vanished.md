## 6. Ledgers of the Vanished

The forbidden archive lived two floors beneath the ledger hall, behind a door that had no handle on the outside and no memory of being opened on the inside.

That was how Casper thought of it, anyway—the door *remembering*. Nine years in the Vellum Guild taught her that everything here remembered something: the crates that sweated salt from the storms bottled inside them, the contracts whose ink shifted when their terms were broken, the stairwells that creaked in the pattern of footsteps no living person had made. The guild was a house built out of kept things, and kept things were never entirely quiet.

She stood before that door now with a lamp in one hand and a forged requisition slip in her apron pocket, listening to the building breathe around her the way she'd learned to listen for wardens' boots—by feel, through the soles of her shoes, through the faint tremor in the iron rail she rested her other hand upon. Third watch. Old Marlow on the stairs above, halfway through his flask, his thoughts already gone soft as wet paper. Below her, nothing but stone and the deep cold of the guild's foundations, which was not quite the same as silence.

Nothing down there was silence. She had known that for three days now, since Juniper's symbols had first appeared on the scrap of slate the child carried, and Casper had looked at them and felt something turn over in her chest like a key in a lock that had been painted shut for years.

*You know this,* something in her insisted. *You have always known this.*

Which was impossible. Which was dangerous. Which meant she had to know more.

The requisition slip was a good forgery—she'd spent nine years forging good ones, though never before for herself. It cited an audit discrepancy in Lot 114, a crate of borrowed years whose contents had been found leaking, and demanded cross-reference against pre-Consolidation holdings. Pre-Consolidation. Two hundred years and more. No one audited that far back; no one wanted to. The older the inventory, the stranger its provenance, and strange provenance attracted questions from the Bureau, and questions from the Bureau attracted erasure—not of the questions, but of the questioner. Every apprentice learned that lesson in their first year, usually secondhand, usually about someone they'd liked.

Casper pressed her palm flat against the archive door and whispered the word carved into the lintel in letters worn nearly smooth: *accounted.*

The lock answered. Not with a click—with a sensation, like a held breath released somewhere inside the wall. The door swung inward on hinges that made no sound at all, because sound was inventory too, and the guild did not waste it where no one would hear.

* * *

The air below was old in a way that had weight. It pressed on her shoulders like damp wool. Her lamp threw a small warm circle across shelves that ran back into darkness farther than the light could argue with, row after row after row, and between the rows hung tags of tin stamped with lot numbers in hands long dead. Some of the shelves were empty—holdings sold or lost or, in a few cases, she suspected, escaped—and some were so crowded with sealed jars and wrapped bundles and small iron boxes chained shut that they seemed to lean toward the aisle, straining to be chosen.

"None of you," she murmured, and heard her own voice come back flattened, absorbed, as if the archive ate even echoes. "I'm not shopping."

Something rustled anyway, three rows over, and she tightened her grip on the lamp until she identified the sound: a jar of preserved whispers settling, the way sediment settles. Bottled voices. The guild sold those too, to people who wanted to hear a dead mother say their name, or a drowned sailor sing one last verse. Casper had catalogued forty-one of them in her third year and had refused, with a discipline she still didn't fully understand, to unseal even one.

The catalogue room was where the master indices lived—a squat chamber at the archive's heart, lined with ledgers chained to their desks the way hymnals are chained in temples. Casper had seen this room exactly once, during her apprenticeship, when Master Corvel had brought the whole cohort down to show them what the guild *was*: not a shop, he'd said, but a memory with a till. Everything that exists can be owned, and everything owned can be traded, and the Vellum Guild stands at the crossing of those two truths like a tollhouse.

She had been nineteen and invisible even then, standing at the back where the lamplight gave up, and she had felt the chained ledgers looking at her—or imagined she had, which amounted to the same thing.

Now she set her lamp on the nearest desk and began pulling indices by their chains.

The system was alphabetical by region, which told her almost immediately that what she sought would be hard to find. Regions changed names; regions died; regions were, on occasion, retroactively declared never to have existed, and the guild—pragmatic to the marrow—had a shelf for those. Casper found it near the back of the room, under a tin tag reading only **STRUCK**, and the ledgers there were bound in grey leather that seemed to drink the lamplight rather than reflect it.

She counted eleven volumes. Eleven vanished countries, each with its own index, its own liquidation records, its own final inventories. She knew the names of none of them. That was the point of the shelf, presumably—that no one should know the names—but the guild had never met an object it couldn't label, and labels survived even when the things labeled did not.

She worked backward through the volumes, scanning spines, and stopped on the seventh, because its spine bore a word half-effaced by some deliberate scraping: **SER——N**.

Her mouth went dry. She hadn't expected the name to be real. She had expected, if she was honest with herself—and honesty was a currency she hoarded even more carefully than coin—to find nothing, to confirm that Juniper's symbols were merely some private childish script, a mute girl's invention, and that the turning feeling in Casper's chest was just loneliness wearing the mask of significance.

She opened the ledger.

The paper inside was the color of old ivory, soft at the edges, dense as cloth. The hand that filled it was small and exact, a merchant's hand, patient with its flourishes. And the header of the first page read:

*Inventory of the Serevan Consolidation — being the final accounting of all tradeable assets of the Kingdom of Serevan, taken in the year of its closing, by authority of the Bureau of Records and Rectification.*

Serevan. The name landed in her like a stone dropped into a well, and she listened for the splash and heard—nothing. No memory rose to meet it. No history lesson, no rumor, no grandmother's warning. There was simply a hole where knowledge should have been, a socket without its tooth, and the hole itself frightened her more than any fact could have, because holes like that did not occur naturally. Holes like that were *made*.

Two hundred years ago, every map in every atlas had simply stopped showing a country that must once have sat somewhere east of the Thornmark, in the space where schoolroom geography now showed open grassland and a footnote about poor soils. Two hundred years ago, every chronicle had skipped a beat and continued as if nothing were missing. Children had grown up, married, died, without ever wondering why the road east ended in pasture. Casper herself had studied maps—she was an auditor; maps were her bread—and she had never once noticed the absence, which meant the absence had been done *well*, which meant it had been done thoroughly, which meant whoever did it had done it before.

And now here was a ledger proving the country had existed, had traded, had *inventoried itself*—the last accounting, taken in the year of its closing, which meant someone had stood in a room very much like this one, in a city that no longer had a name, and listed everything worth selling before the end came.

Casper turned pages with fingers that shook slightly. Trade goods. Contracts. Deeds to houses that no longer existed, in streets that no longer existed, in a language that—

She stopped.

Halfway down the fourth page, in a section headed *Non-material Holdings: Scripts, Songs, and Speeches*, the clerk had abandoned his neat merchant hand and switched to columns of characters unlike anything she had ever catalogued. Curved strokes. Branching lines. Marks that hooked downward like roots and marks that lifted upward like smoke. A writing system that looked less invented than *grown*.

Casper knew it intimately.

Juniper had drawn these same shapes on her slate three nights ago—drawn them quickly, surely, the way children draw the letters of the only alphabet they trust—and had tapped the last one twice, looking up at Casper with eyes the color of river ice, waiting for an answer Casper hadn't been able to give.

But her body, apparently, had given it anyway. Because now, staring at the printed column, Casper found her lips moving, shaping sounds she had no conscious knowledge of, and the sounds were not random. They were reading.

*"Held in trust,"* her mouth said, quietly, in a voice that used her throat but wasn't quite hers, *"for the people of the speaking country, against the day of return."*

Then the voice stopped, and Casper stood alone in the lamplight with her heart hammering against her ribs like something trying to get out, and understood two things with perfect clarity.

First: she could read Serevan.

Second: she had never been taught.

* * *

She sat down on the floor. It wasn't a decision; her knees simply concluded negotiations without consulting her. The stone was cold through her skirts and she was grateful for it, grateful for anything solid, because the world had developed a distinct tilt, like a ship taking water.

*You know this. You have always known this.*

Her grandmother's face surfaced then—uninvited, unwelcome—from a summer thirty years gone: a small fierce woman in a black shawl who had lived at the edge of the Thornmark and kept bees and never, in all Casper's childhood, spoken a single word in company louder than a murmur. Who had hummed, sometimes, songs with no words, and stopped humming whenever anyone else entered the room. Who had caught five-year-old Casper talking to the stable cat and had not laughed, had not scolded, but had gone white as milk and knelt down and gripped her shoulders and said, in a whisper like a blade being drawn slowly:

*Never let them hear you. Promise me. Whatever speaks to you, whatever answers—never let them hear you answer.*

Casper had promised. Children promise anything to make the fear in a beloved face stop. And she had kept the promise for thirty years, had built her entire life inside it, had become a woman so careful, so gray, so expertly forgettable that even the guild that employed her could not have described her face under oath—and she had told herself all those years that the promise was superstition, an old woman's fear, peasant nonsense from a border province that had never mattered to anyone.

A border province east of the Thornmark.

In the space where the maps now showed open grassland and a footnote about poor soils.

"Oh," Casper said aloud, and the word fell into the dark of the archive and did not echo, and somehow that was the worst part—that even her horror was absorbed here, swallowed, kept. "Oh, you clever, terrified old woman."

Because that was what the ledger told her, in its patient merchant's hand, if you knew how to listen. The final accounting of Serevan was not a liquidation. Liquidations happened to failed businesses and conquered kingdoms, and they were loud, and they left ruins and refugees and grievances that echoed for generations. This was different. This was *tidy*. Page after page of assets transferred to Bureau custody—scripts confiscated, songs struck from the air, place-names purchased and retired—and not one entry, anywhere, marked *displaced persons* or *casualties* or *survivors*. As if the entire population had been settled amicably. As if the country had folded like a shop at the end of the trading day.

The last entry in the volume was dated two centuries past and written in a different hand—harder, bureaucratic, the hand of someone accustomed to being obeyed. Casper's gift stirred uneasily as she read it, the way a dog stirs when a stranger's tread comes up the path, and she realized the document itself was *addressed* to future readers, which no ordinary ledger is:

*Let it be recorded that the language of Serevan, having declined all offers of sale, is hereby withdrawn from circulation. A tongue that will not be owned cannot be permitted to remain free. The speakers having proved incorrigible, the speech itself shall be accounted for instead. What cannot be bought will be forgotten, and what is forgotten belongs to the Bureau.*

*This matter is closed.*

Closed. Except that it wasn't, because a ledger survived—this ledger, smuggled into the guild's deepest shelf, mislabeled, half-scraped, waiting two hundred years in the dark for someone with the wrong kind of ears to come looking. And a child survived, a mute girl with river-ice eyes who spoke in the withdrawn language as naturally as breathing, who had been left on the guild's steps four days ago by a dying woman who must have known—must have *chosen*—exactly where she was leaving her.

Not abandoned. *Delivered.* To the one house in the world where the forbidden tongue might be recognized, and the one person in the house who might recognize it back.

Casper pressed her palms against the cold floor and breathed, slowly, the way you breathe when the ground has moved and you're checking whether it will move again. Above her, muffled through two floors of stone, she could hear the guild going about its night—the creak of Marlow's rounds, the tick of the great clock, the small restless stirring of the moth cages in the atrium. Ordinary sounds. Safe sounds. The architecture of the life she had built out of not being noticed.

Except that the life was built on a foundation she'd never examined, and the foundation had just confessed to being hollow, and somewhere above her a child was locked in a holding room classified as *inventory pending appraisal*, speaking a language that an empire had once decided could not be permitted to exist.

And Casper had promised her grandmother never to let them hear her answer.

She looked at the ledger in the lamplight—at the branching, rooted, smoke-lifting letters that her lips still ached to shape—and understood, with the particular clarity of a person standing on a ledge, that the promise and the child had finally come into the same room, and only one of them was walking out.

She copied the lot number of the Serevan volume into her memory—paper evidence was a thing she did not intend to leave behind her—and closed the grey cover gently, the way you close a coffin, or a door you mean to come back through.

As she straightened, snuffing her lamp to a coal-glow, the archive around her exhaled.

It was very faint. It might have been the settling of old shelves, the shifting of preserved things in their jars. But it came from everywhere at once, from the walls and the floor and the dark beyond the lamplight, and it was not a rustle and not a groan.

It was a chord. Dozens of voices, low and blended, holding a single sustained note that no instrument Casper knew could produce—and beneath the note, barely, the ghost of words in the branching language, words she found she could almost translate, and the translation made her blood run cold:

*We know you are reading our accounts. We have been waiting two hundred years for someone to check them.*

The lamp guttered out. In the sudden dark, Casper stood absolutely still, one hand on the chained shelf, and listened as the note faded beneath the floor—downward, deeper than the archive, deeper than foundations, down into whatever lay under the guild that the guild had never put on any inventory.

Above her, faintly, the great clock began to strike the hour. And Casper Kavanagh, who had spent nine years making herself impossible to see, realized that something in the dark had been watching her for a very long time—and had just, for the first time, let itself be heard.
