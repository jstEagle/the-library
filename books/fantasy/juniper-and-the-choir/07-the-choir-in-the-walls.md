## 7. The Choir in the Walls

Casper had always believed the Vellum Guild was quiet at night.

She had built nine years of her life on that belief. The guild by day was a hive of murmured appraisals and the scratch of quills, of buyers breathing through their noses at crates that should not exist, but by night it settled into a silence so complete it felt architectural—part of the building's bones, load-bearing, essential. She had come to love that silence the way other people loved a hearth. It was the one thing she owned outright: the knowledge that when the lamps went down and the last clerk climbed the stairs, the world would stop asking things of her.

So when the singing began, three nights after Juniper arrived, it did not simply wake her. It unmade her.

* * *

It started as a sensation before it became a sound—the way an earthquake announces itself first in the soles of the feet, or grief in the throat. Casper lay in her narrow room on the third floor of the clerks' wing, staring at the ceiling she had memorized years ago, and felt the floor beneath her pallet humming. Not vibrating. Humming. A held note, low and patient, like something enormous clearing its throat on the far side of a very thick wall.

She told herself it was the pipes. The guild's plumbing was older than the guild's charters and groaned all winter. She told herself it was the wind moving through the storm-bottles in the cellar vaults, though those were triple-corked and warded twice. She told herself a great many things with the practiced fluency of a woman who had spent a decade telling herself things, and then the singing started, and every one of them died at once.

It was voices. Many voices. Underneath her, underneath the floorboards and the joists and whatever darkness lay between them, people were singing.

Not hymns. Casper knew hymns; the guild's chapel-lease required two services a year and she had sat through both with her eyes lowered and her mind elsewhere. This was nothing with words she could parse, nothing with a melody that resolved. It rose and fell in long braided lines, voices weaving over and under each other, sometimes in unison and sometimes deliberately apart, and there was a structure to it—a form, a shape—that felt less composed than *remembered*, the way a river remembers its bed. And threaded through it, unmistakable even though she had heard it only in ink and silence, was the language from Juniper's papers.

The symbols on the child's page had been flat things, marks on vellum. In the singing they were not flat at all. They were round. They turned. They meant.

Casper sat up so fast her head swam. Her hands were shaking. She pressed one palm flat against the floorboards, an instinct she didn't examine until later, and the wood was warm—not fire-warm, not fever-warm, but warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, warmth with a pulse inside it—and the humming deepened under her hand as if the floor had noticed her noticing.

Every animal within a mile had gone still the day she touched the doorframe where Juniper had stood. She remembered that stillness now with a fresh, cold clarity. Something in this building answered to the same current that ran through that child, and it had been here all along, under her feet, under nine years of ledgers, and she had never once heard it.

Or—she stopped. Went very still herself, the way Hesper the cellar-cat went still before pouncing, though the comparison came unbidden and unwelcome.

Or she had heard it all along, and had filed it away among the pipes and the wind and the settling bones of old buildings, because hearing it properly would have meant admitting the guild was not what it pretended to be, and admitting that would have meant admitting her own careful life was built on ground that sang.

The singing swelled. Under her palm, the floorboards breathed.

* * *

She did not sleep again. When the grey light came she dressed, went down early, and made herself useful in the ways that kept her invisible: she re-inked a smudged column in the intake ledger, she counted the moth-cages twice, she drank her tea standing up in the corridor where no one bothered to look at her. But her ears would not behave. All day she caught herself tilting her head, listening past the sounds of the guild for what lay beneath them, and once—only once, in the pause between one clerk's footsteps and the next—she thought she heard it again, faint as a coin dropped down a well: a single sustained note, patient, waiting for dark.

The moths noticed her listening. Of course they did. They noticed everything about her; it was their whole occupation, being noticed and reporting back. From their cages along the west wall they rustled their grey wings and sent their small voices skimming across the room like thrown stones skipping water.

*You hear it now,* said the eldest moth, the one with the torn left wing who had been in the guild longer than any living clerk. *We wondered how long you'd pretend otherwise.*

"There's nothing to pretend," Casper murmured, not looking up from her ledger. "Pipes settle."

*Pipes,* the moth repeated, with the dry contempt only a creature that has outlived four Guildmasters can produce. *Child, we fly at night. We have flown these halls since before your grandmother's grandmother sold her name to buy you people time. There are no pipes under the ledger hall. There is nothing under the ledger hall but stone, and under the stone—*

"Stop." The word came out sharper than she intended, and a passing junior clerk glanced over, and Casper bent lower over her columns until he lost interest. Her heart was going hard against her ribs. "Whatever game this is—"

*No game.* The torn-winged moth pressed itself against the bars of its cage, and its voice, which usually arrived light and papery, came heavy now, weighted with something almost like pity. *You know what we are, Kavanagh. We carry messages because we can find anything that wants finding. We found the child's language in one night, in our sleep, in the place we go when we dream. Do you know why? Because we have heard it before. Not learned. Heard. It is written into the oldest part of us, the part that was alive before kingdoms drew their little lines. That tongue is not unknown. It is remembered. By everything old enough.*

Casper's pen had stopped moving. There was a blot spreading under the nib, a slow black bloom across a column of perfectly ordinary numbers, and she watched it spread because watching it was easier than thinking.

"Remembered by what?" she asked, barely above breath.

The moths were silent a moment. All of them, the whole west wall of them, wings folded, still as paper. Then, together, in a chorus so soft it might have been the building itself speaking:

*By the ones who are still down there. Singing.*

* * *

She lasted until midnight. Then she took a lamp, told herself she was doing no such thing, and went down.

The ledger hall occupied the guild's entire ground floor: forty rows of shelving, ten thousand drawers of contracts and manifests and deeds to things that had no business existing, all of it resting on a foundation of black granite that predated the guild by centuries. Everyone knew the story the guild told about that foundation—that the stone had been quarried locally, cheaply, by the city's founders, a bargain struck in the hungry early years. Everyone repeated it. No one examined it, because examining bargains was the guild's trade and examining one's own foundations was bad for business.

Casper walked the aisles with her lamp held high, and the shadows moved, and the hall was exactly as empty as it had been every night of her working life, and none of that mattered at all, because the singing had started again.

It came up through the granite. Through her boot-soles. Through the shelf-iron, which hummed faintly in sympathy like the strings of an instrument someone was playing in another room. Up close, away from her pallet and her excuses, she could finally hear the shape of it: hundreds of voices, maybe thousands, arranged not in rows but in rings, circles within circles, and at the center of the circles a single line of melody that never ended, only passed from voice to voice like a flame handed down a line of cupped palms.

And now, this close, she could hear the words.

They were not words in any language she knew, and yet they were not foreign either. That was the vertigo of it. Each phrase arrived in her ear already half-understood, the way a face seen once in childhood is half-remembered—an inheritance sitting in her blood, waiting, and she understood with a lurch that this was what the moths had meant. The language wasn't unknown. It was *hers*, in some buried way, the way a scar is hers, and she had gone twenty-nine years without knowing her own body carried a second mother-tongue.

A phrase surfaced through the weave, clear as a bell through fog:

*The door sleeps. The door dreams. Keep the count of the small hours; keep the count of the names.*

Another:

*She is coming. The small one. The last mouth. We have waited two hundred winters and she is coming down to us.*

Casper's lamp guttered. Her hand, when she looked at it, was white around the handle.

*The last mouth.*

Juniper. They were singing about Juniper. They had been singing about her for three nights, perhaps for two hundred years, and they called her a mouth—as if the child were not a child at all but an opening, a threshold, a way for something wordless to finally speak.

The awe hit her first, and it was genuine, and it was terrible. Here beneath her feet, beneath the ledgers and the bottled storms and the auctioned names, was a country. Not the memory of a country—the country itself, or what remained of it, pressed flat under the guild's foundation like a flower in a book, still alive, still singing, still keeping count. Every history lesson of her childhood, every map in every schoolroom, every smug certainty that the world was fully drawn and fully known—all of it was wrong, and the proof was humming through the soles of her boots.

Then the fear arrived, and it was more genuine still.

Because the singing changed. She felt it change, felt the great slow wheel of voices turn toward her the way a crowd turns toward a sound, and the center melody—that endless handed-down flame—came apart from the rings and rose, alone, closer, until it seemed to be directly beneath her feet, directly beneath the spot where she stood, as if the singer had crossed a room to stand at a door.

And a single voice, no longer blended, spoke up through the granite in words she understood without wanting to:

*Clerk. You hear us. Two hundred years, and the ones above walk over our graves and hear nothing—but you hear us.*

"I—" Casper's voice cracked. She crouched, absurdly, as if proximity would help, as if politeness applied. "I'm sorry. I didn't mean to listen."

*Everyone listens,* the voice said, gentle and vast. *Few hear. Fewer answer. You are the granddaughter of Anouk, who closed our door from the outside and paid for it with her voice's home. We do not blame her. We never blamed her. Fear is a country too, and she was exiled into it.*

Her grandmother's name. Spoken up through the stone, casual as weather. Casper gripped the shelf beside her and the iron was cold and humming and the whole hall seemed suddenly very tall and very thin, a paper wall between her and an ocean.

"What do you want?"

*What we have always wanted. To be counted. To be spoken. We are not ghosts, clerk—we are the remainder. A country is not its land. A country is its words, and our words were taken from the air and locked below, and we have sung them to each other ever since so they would not die. But songs need new mouths. Old mouths close.* A pause, and in the pause the whole choir seemed to lean in. *The child hears us too. The child carries the tongue whole, unbought, unbroken—the last mouth of Serevan. When she speaks, the door will know her. And there are others above who will want that door opened for reasons that are not ours. You have already met their smell. Paper and ash. You will meet it soon.*

"Others—" Casper started. "What others? Who's coming?"

But the voice was receding, folding back into the rings, the flame being passed down the line of palms again, and the last thing that reached her, faint and clear and unbearably kind, was:

*Keep her safe, granddaughter-of-Anouk. Count the small hours. The Bureau counts too, and it counts faster.*

Then silence—real silence, architectural, load-bearing—and Casper knelt alone in the ledger hall with her guttering lamp, understanding that the quiet she had loved for nine years had never once been quiet, that she had made her home on the roof of a grave full of singers, and that somewhere in the dark above her, in the masters' wing, a man named Oren Vetch was awake too, making lists.

She just didn't yet know what was on them.
