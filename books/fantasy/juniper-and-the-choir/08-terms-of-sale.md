## 8. Terms of Sale

Oren Vetch had three rules, and he kept them the way other men kept gods.

The first: nothing enters the Vellum Guild that cannot be priced. The second: nothing leaves it that has not been priced twice. The third—and this was the rule he murmured to himself on bad nights, the one that had replaced prayer sometime in his forties—*never want anything so much that a seller can see you wanting it.*

He was thinking about all three rules on the morning the Pale Assessor came up Saltmonger's Stair, because the Assessor broke every one of them simply by existing.

Vetch watched him from the ledger hall's high window, as he watched everything. The man climbed slowly—not with the labor of an unfit body but with the deliberate economy of someone who had learned that haste revealed appetite. He wore grey. Not the grey of wool or rain or ash, but a grey that seemed to have been decided upon by committee, a grey that made the eye slide off him and settle on the stair rail, the wet cobbles, the gull screaming over the harbor roofs. Even the two escorts flanking him—Crown men in undyed cloaks, hands resting near their belts with the casualness of habit—looked more colorful than their principal. One of them carried a document case banded in black iron.

"Master Vetch." Pellam, the door-ward, appeared at his elbow, breathless from the stairs in a way that suggested he'd run. "The gentleman below says—"

"I can see what the gentleman below says." Vetch did not turn. "Has he given a name?"

"He gives a title. He calls himself the Assessor."

"*Pale* Assessor?"

Pellam blinked. "I don't know about pale, master. Ordinary-looking fellow."

That was the trouble, Vetch thought. Everyone who met the Bureau's agents described them as ordinary. There were tavern stories about the Bureau of Erasure—there always were, in a port city, where stories arrived on ships like rats—but the stories never agreed on faces. Only on the feeling afterward. The feeling of having spoken to someone and being unable, later, to say what they had looked like.

Ordinary-looking. That was worse than pale.

"Show him to the Amber Room," Vetch said. "Tea. The good leaves, not the ones I show the wool factors. And send word down to the holding floor—I want the child presentable within the hour. Washed, fed, dressed in something that isn't sackcloth. If she's marked or bruised when our guest sees her, I'll take the cost of her diminished value out of whoever's shift it was."

"Yes, master." Pellam hesitated. "Is she... is she being sold, then? So soon? She only came last week. There's talk among the clerks that—"

"There's talk among the clerks because clerks have mouths instead of minds." Vetch finally turned, and gave Pellam the look that had ended conversations for thirty years. "The Guild does not sell children, Pellam. The Guild sells *assets*. Whether this particular asset happens to be eight years old is a matter of inventory classification, and inventory classification is my business, not yours. Go."

Pellam went. Vetch remained at the window a moment longer, watching the grey man pause at the foot of the final flight of stairs. The Assessor looked up—not searching for windows, not checking sightlines the way Crown men usually did, but looking at the building itself, the way a physician looks at a patient whose illness he already knows the name of.

Then he smiled, faintly, directly up at Vetch's window, though there was no possible way he could have known which window, which shadow, which man.

Vetch stepped back from the glass before the smile finished forming.

* * *

The Amber Room was the best room in the guild and Vetch used it perhaps four times a year. Its walls were paneled in resin-gold amber through which, if one held a candle close, small ancient things could be seen suspended: insects, seeds, a fragment of feather, once—so the guild's founding charter claimed—a single drop of rain from a storm that no longer existed anywhere else. Buyers sat beneath all that preserved light and felt, without knowing why, that they were in the presence of permanence. It loosened purse strings like wine.

Vetch seated the Assessor facing the amber wall and took the chair opposite, and understood within thirty seconds that he had made a tactical error, because the Assessor wasn't looking at the wall at all. He was looking at Vetch—with the mild, bottomless attention of a man reading a ledger.

"You have a moth problem," the Assessor said.

It was not a question. Vetch kept his face still. "We keep messenger-moths. Standard practice for a house that trades in correspondence. They're valuable stock."

"They've stopped flying." The Assessor folded his hands. Grey hands, ordinary hands, with ordinary rings. "Since the night your doorkeeper found something on your steps. Every caged thing in this quarter went quiet that night—the moths, the pigeons, the fighting dogs on Corvasser Lane, the fish in the market tanks. My colleagues record such things." A small pause. "I record them personally."

"The weather was strange that night," Vetch said. "Storms unsettle animals."

"It didn't storm." The Assessor said it without accusation, the way one might correct a date. Then he let it go, visibly, deliberately, the way a fisherman lets line off the reel. "You have an item I'm told may interest the Bureau. Left at your door. Female child, approximately eight years. Vocalizes in an unclassified language. Refuses written script except for symbols matching no known alphabet in the Crown registry." He recited it flatly, a manifest. "Your own appraisers have logged her as *origin unknown, valuation pending*. Is that accurate?"

"It is accurate as far as it goes."

"And how far does it go, Master Vetch?" For the first time the Assessor leaned forward slightly, and the candlelight caught him, and Vetch saw—or thought he saw, or would swear ever after that he saw—that for just an instant the man's face was not ordinary at all, but worn smooth, featureless, like stone that had been rained on for a thousand years. Then it was a face again, forgettable, mild. "How far does it go?"

Here it was, then. Vetch had spent nine days since the child's arrival doing what he did best: waiting. He had let the clerks speculate. He had let Casper Kavanagh hover near the holding room with her ledgers and her mute, watchful ways—he had noticed that, filed it away; the woman lingered around impossible things the way rust lingers around water, and someday he would determine whether that was devotion or appetite, because both could be exploited. He had let the house hold its breath. And now the buyer had come to him, which meant the buyer needed him, which meant—for today, for this room—the advantage was his, provided he did not spend it foolishly.

Provided he did not, above all, *want anything so much that a seller could see him wanting it.*

"The child speaks a language," Vetch said, "that my scribes cannot classify. That my oldest reference works cannot classify. That the University's philologists—" he had sent copies of her symbols out under three different seals, carefully laundered, untraceable "—returned with apologies and a request never to be contacted again. Two of them returned with apologies and *fear*, which is more expensive than ignorance." He steepled his fingers. "Now. A language that frightens philologists might be merely obscure. But a language that makes animals go silent across an entire quarter of the city—that is not obscure. That is *old*. And old things, Master Assessor, are the only things this guild deals in."

"What do you believe she is?" the Assessor asked quietly.

"I believe," said Vetch, "that I don't deal in beliefs."

The Assessor laughed. It was a terrible laugh—not loud, not cruel, but empty, the laugh of a man performing a human function he had read about in a manual. It lasted exactly as long as laughter was supposed to last, and then it stopped exactly on time.

"No," he agreed. "You deal in prices. Good. Then let us speak of price." From the iron-banded case, the escort produced documents and laid them before Vetch: Bureau letterhead, Crown seal, and figures. Vetch read them with the outward patience of a man reviewing a wool contract and the inward sensation of standing at the edge of a very tall building.

The sum offered for the child exceeded the guild's entire annual revenue by a factor he had to check twice to believe.

"This is for one item," Vetch said carefully.

"That is for one item *of provenance*. The Bureau pays for certainty." The Assessor tapped the page with one grey finger. "And here is the second part of the offer, which I am instructed to make clear. Should you acquire further items of the same provenance—and the Bureau believes you are *situated* to do so—the schedule improves. Considerably. The Bureau is engaged in a long-term collection effort, Master Vetch. Certain categories of material are of exceptional interest. Anything touched by—" and here the Assessor paused, and Vetch watched the man choose, deliberately, whether to say the name, and watched him decide against it, "—by the erased country. Anything that remembers it. Anything that speaks it. Persons, texts, objects, dreams. Especially persons."

"Why?" Vetch asked. It was the question any careful merchant would ask, and he asked it in the tone of due diligence, and underneath it ran another question entirely—*how much, how much more, how far does the schedule improve*—which he strangled before it reached his voice. Never want anything so much—

"Preservation," said the Assessor.

Vetch waited. The Assessor offered nothing further, and the silence stretched, and Vetch realized with cold professional admiration that the man intended the silence to answer the question, and that its answer was: *you do not need to know why. You need to know that we pay.*

"Provenance," Vetch said at last. "You're offering a fortune for a child whose origin I cannot certify. Surely the Bureau doesn't buy on faith."

"The Bureau buys on *pattern*." The Assessor rose, unhurried, and walked to the amber wall. He studied the suspended insects with something that was almost tenderness. "Two hundred years ago, a country stood where your city now stands. It was removed. Not conquered—conquest leaves ruins, ruins leave records, records leave grief, and grief is survivable. It was *removed*. Maps redrawn. Charters burned. Names struck. Families who remembered it were recorded as mad, and then simply as dead, and then not recorded at all. The operation took eleven years and was considered the Bureau's finest work." He turned. "Do you know what the country was called?"

"Serevan," Vetch said.

Something moved behind the Assessor's eyes—the first genuine expression Vetch had seen on him, and it was satisfaction, and Vetch understood he had just failed some kind of test, or passed one, and could not tell which.

"Most people say 'what country?' Most people hear the name and it slides off them, like water off wax. You said it back." The Assessor drifted closer to Vetch's chair. "Which means either you read too much, Master Vetch—which I doubt; you strike me as a man who reads precisely enough—or you have Serevan-touched material in this house that you have *already* been studying. Before we came. Before anyone came." He smiled his committee-grey smile. "The Bureau rewards initiative. Schedule improvements are available for proactive acquisition. I mention this for your benefit."

"I found a ledger," Vetch said, because the moment had come to spend a little coin to win a fortune, and because pride is a currency too, and he had always believed in spending both wisely. "In the deep archive. Pre-Erasure binding. Trade records of a house called—" he named it, the name he had spent four nights verifying, "—and entries in a script matching the child's symbols exactly. Word for word. I have compared them myself."

The Assessor was quiet for a long moment.

Then he said, softly, with something that in a human being might have been hunger: "A ledger from inside. A *commercial* ledger. Written by merchants, in life, in the ordinary course of business—before the removal." He turned to the escort. "Note that. Priority retrieval, above the child if necessary."

"Above—" Vetch began.

"The child is one mouth, Master Vetch. The ledger is proof the mouth has a *history*. Do you understand what the Bureau collects? Not things." The Assessor's ordinary face bent close, and again the light did something wrong to it. "*Certainty.* Proof that erasure can be undone. Do you know what that means to an institution built on erasure? It means we are not eternal. It means somewhere, in a cellar, in a ledger, in a child, the country we killed is still keeping books." He straightened. "So. Terms. The sum stands for the child, delivery within the week. The sum doubles if she arrives speaking—recorded, witnessed, transcribed. The sum triples for the ledger, intact, tonight if you can manage it. And the schedule for future acquisitions will be delivered to you privately, in writing, through channels you will not remember receiving it from."

"You'll have the ledger tomorrow," Vetch heard himself say. "The child within six days."

"Six days." The Assessor nodded slowly. "Not seven?"

"She'll be ready in six."

"She had better be." All the warmth—such as it was—drained out of the room between one syllable and the next. "Because I will tell you what happens otherwise, and I tell you this as a courtesy, merchant to merchant, so that you may plan accordingly. The Bureau does not conduct failures. If the item is damaged, escaped, or *misplaced*, the Bureau's assessment does not end—it expands. We assess the seller. We assess the household. We assess everyone the item spoke to, everyone who fed her, everyone who lingered too long near her door." A beat, delicate as a knife laid flat. "Everyone. Some of those assessments become acquisitions themselves. Your guild holds many quiet assets, Master Vetch. Cages full of them, I'm told."

The threat was so smooth it took Vetch a heartbeat to feel it, and when he felt it, he felt it in his spine, cold as well-water. But his face did what it had done for thirty years across a thousand negotiating tables: nothing at all.

"The Vellum Guild," he said, "delivers what it sells."

"I know," said the Assessor, moving to the door. "That's why I came to you and not to the auction houses. They sell wonders. You sell *certainty*." At the threshold he paused. "One more courtesy. Your ledger-clerk—the thin woman, the one who audits the storms—she has been visiting the item. Unlogged visits, after hours. I note it not as accusation but as opportunity. Assets that attract caretakers command higher prices. Sentiment, properly harvested, is worth forty percent at market." He glanced back, and his eyes were the color of the space between printed lines. "But sentiment improperly harvested becomes a liability. Watch her, Master Vetch. Or better—sell her too, while she's still only *strange* rather than *proven*. The schedule covers beast-speakers. It covers them generously."

The door closed behind him with the softness of snowfall.

* * *

Vetch sat alone in the Amber Room for a long time after the escorts' boots had faded down the stair. The candles had burned low. In the amber wall, the ancient insects hung in their gold forever, mid-crawl, mid-flight, preserved at the exact moment of their capture.

He should feel fear. He recognized the shape of it, the way a man recognizes weather through a shutter. But what filled him instead, rising warm and enormous, drowning the caution of thirty years like a flood drowns fence posts, was arithmetic.

The child: a fortune. The ledger: double. The clerk Kavanagh—if half of what the Assessor hinted was true—triple again, and more, a recurring revenue, a vein. The schedule of future acquisitions, arriving through channels he wouldn't remember. The guild's stock list suddenly re-readable: every bottled wonder, every borrowed year, every impossible thing in his cellars potentially *Serevan-touched*, potentially repriced tenfold. He had spent his life trading at the edge of the impossible. Now the impossible had a sovereign patron with an open purse, and it had walked up his stairs and sat in his amber light and asked him, essentially, *name your ambition.*

Greed, he told himself, settling the terms in his mind with the clean satisfaction of a balanced column, was not greed when it was prudence. Selling the child was not cruelty; it was placement. The girl would be fed, housed, valued—valued beyond any orphanage's imagining. The Bureau preserved; the Assessor had said so. Everything Vetch did, he did for the guild's continuity, and continuity required capital, and capital required that nothing in his inventory remain unsold out of mere squeamishness. He had never once let squeamishness sign a ledger. He did not intend to begin with an item that had arrived, conveniently, silently, on his very doorstep—delivered, one might almost say, like providence.

Six days. He would move the sale to five, actually—no point letting the merchandise form attachments, or letting certain clerks grow clever. He drafted the memo in his head: appraisal concluded, classification revised, *asset designated liquid*. He would have the ledgers brought up from the deep archive personally, tonight, before anyone else went down there. He would have Kavanagh reassigned to the storm-crates where she belonged, far from the holding room.

And he would begin, quietly, compiling a list of everything else in the guild that might remember the erased country. Because if a child could carry a dead nation's tongue in her mouth, then who knew what else carried it—what slept in the cellars, what whispered in the walls, what sat in the dark of the archive pretending to be paper.

On his way out, Vetch paused at the amber wall and held his candle close, the way buyers did, and looked into the gold at the small things frozen mid-motion. An insect that had flown toward sweetness and been sealed for it.

He found, for no reason he could name, that he could not quite meet its eye.

Above him, through five floors of stone and timber, from the direction of the holding room, came a sound so faint he mistook it for the wind in the chimneys—though there was no wind, and the chimneys were cold.

It sounded almost like singing.
