## 10. The Beast-Speaker's Bargain

The cellar of the Vellum Guild smelled of cold stone and older things—tallow, mildew, the faint copper tang of ink that had been mixed with something no one would name aloud. Casper descended the stairs with a lamp held high, her free hand trailing the wall, because the dark down here was not an ordinary dark. It was the kind that remembered.

She had come for salt. That was the story she told herself, anyway. The kitchens kept their stores on the ground floor; there was no earthly reason to descend three flights into the guild's oldest bones at midnight, two days after she had read a ledger belonging to a country that did not exist, one night after hearing voices sing beneath the floorboards of the ledger hall like water under ice.

But her feet had known where to go before her mind consented. They had carried her past the holding room where Juniper slept behind a locked door, past Vetch's office with its lamp still burning, down and down, to the one place in the guild where even the masters did not like to linger.

The cellar was not empty.

A cat sat on an upturned crate in the middle of the floor, grooming one grey paw with the unhurried dignity of a creature who had never once been inconvenienced by anything, including time. She was old—that was plain even in the lamplight. Her fur had gone the color of ash and river-fog, patched with white at the muzzle, and her eyes were the trouble. Both were clouded white as skimmed milk, blind as winter glass, yet they turned toward Casper with a precision no blind eye should manage.

"Hesper," Casper said, before she could stop herself.

Everyone knew Hesper. The cellar-cat was older than the youngest guildmasters, older than some of the walls, or so the apprentices claimed in the way apprentices claim things—half legend, half dare. She came and went as she pleased. No one fed her; no one dared not feed her. Vetch himself, Casper had once seen, stepped around her in a corridor with a carefulness he showed no living person.

"You know my name," the cat said.

Casper did not drop the lamp, though it was a near thing. The voice was dry and gravelled, a stone rolled down a long hill, and it came from the cat's mouth without any of the grotesque effort she might have imagined. It sounded, absurdly, like a grandmother complaining about the weather.

"You speak," Casper managed.

"I speak," Hesper agreed. "You listen. Between us we have the beginnings of a conversation, which is more than most people manage." She finished with her paw and folded herself neatly, tail wrapped over feet, a grey question mark. "You've been talking to moths."

The words landed in Casper's chest like a dropped coin. "I don't—"

"Child. I am blind, not deaf, and this building has thin bones. You answered a moth in the ledger hall four nights ago. First words you've given any beast in nine years, by the smell of it—the whole cage nearly fainted. Moths are dramatic." Her clouded eyes held Casper's face with terrible gentleness. "You have your grandmother's trick of going quiet. I'd hoped you'd inherited more than the silence."

The lamp trembled in Casper's hand. "You knew my grandmother?"

"I knew Maren Kavanagh," Hesper said, "before she was Kavanagh, when she was Maren of Serevan and didn't know it yet either. Sit down. Your legs are about to fail you, and I dislike talking to standing people; they sway."

Casper sat. There was a stool nearby, ancient and three-legged, and she found it without looking, which told her something about how far into strangeness she had already fallen. The cellar pressed close around them: crates of things the guild had decided were too dangerous or too dull to sell, jars that shifted their own weight when observed, a wardrobe that breathed.

"My grandmother died when I was six," Casper said. "I remember almost nothing. A kitchen. A song she hummed. She told me—" The memory surfaced slowly, painfully, like something drowned. "She told me never to answer them. When the animals spoke. She said answering was how they find you."

"Maren said that?" Something moved through the old cat's body—a ripple, a grief too large for the shape wearing it. "Then she learned nothing in the end, poor woman. Or she learned exactly what she meant to learn, which amounts to the same tragedy." Hesper rose, stretched until her spine curved like a drawn bow, and jumped down from the crate. She crossed the stones toward Casper, each step deliberate, and sat again an arm's length away, close enough that Casper could feel the warmth coming off her small body like a banked fire. "Let me tell you what your grandmother never told you, because telling it cost her too much and she thought silence was cheaper. She was wrong, but she was tired, and tired people make arithmetic mistakes."

Casper waited. The lamp hissed softly between them.

"Serevan was not destroyed," Hesper said. "That is the first lie, and the largest. Countries can be struck from maps, their names unwritten, their cities pulled down—but a country is not its stones. A country is a mouth. It is everything it ever said, said together. And Serevan's mouth was sealed, not silenced. Do you understand the difference? Silencing kills the speaker. Sealing keeps him alive somewhere very small, waiting."

"The choir," Casper whispered. "Under the ledger hall."

"So you've heard them." Hesper's whiskers twitched—not surprise, Casper realized, but satisfaction, grim and old. "Two hundred years I have sat in this cellar, above their singing, listening to this guild pile its ledgers on top of their graves like a child stacking blocks on a sleeping giant. And for two hundred years I have waited for a Kavanagh to come downstairs. Your grandmother visited once. You are the second."

"Why me? Why now?"

"Because of the girl." Hesper's blind eyes did not blink; cats' eyes don't, but somehow these managed to convey blinking, a slow deliberate mercy of a blink. "Juniper. The last mouth. You touched the doorframe the morning she arrived and every animal within a mile went still—we felt it like a bell rung through water. Your gift woke up, child. Nine years of pressing it flat as a flower in a book, and one silent child walks through the door and it stands up on its hind legs." She paused. "Your gift knows what she is. It recognized her before your mind did. That is what gifts do. They are older and wiser than the people carrying them, and they are patient in a way flesh cannot afford to be."

Casper's hands had gone cold around the lamp's handle. "Vetch wants to sell her. In a few days. To someone from the Bureau—I heard him talking to himself, he does that, he thinks no one hears—and the buyer is coming to appraise her, and if I take her out the front door they will catch us before we reach the gate, and if I hide her inside the guild they will search every room, because Vetch never loses inventory, never, in nineteen years he has never once written off a loss—"

"Stop." The word cracked like a whip, and every rat in the cellar, invisible in the dark, froze mid-scuttle. Hesper waited until Casper's breathing slowed. "You are planning an escape. Good. Escapes require plans. But you are planning it with a suppressed gift, an untrained tongue, and a child whose language you cannot speak. You are bringing a knife to a war with the blade still sheathed in your own chest." She began to wash her face, fastidious, terrible. "So. Here is what I offer."

The cellar seemed to lean in. Even the breathing wardrobe went quiet.

"I will teach you," Hesper said. "What your grandmother refused to learn, what her mother paid dearly to half-know, what the speakers of Serevan carried in their mouths like live coals. I will teach you the old tongue—not the little traveler's phrases, not the market-bargain fragments, but the grammar of it, the weight of it, the way it hooks into the world and pulls. I will teach you to open your gift wide enough to hear Juniper properly, and to answer her, and to stand between her and whatever comes through that Bureau door." She lowered her paws. "And in exchange, you will pay."

"Pay what?" Casper asked, though some part of her, the part that had spent nine years watching ledgers, already knew that nothing in this building was ever free, least of all from the things that lived in its foundations.

"Every word costs," Hesper said simply. "That is the rule of Serevan speech, the rule the Bureau wanted to buy and couldn't, the reason they erased a country rather than admit it owned nothing they could hold. Every word spoken in the old tongue is a piece of the speaker given away. A memory. A warmth. A name you loved. The language feeds on its speaker the way a fire feeds on wood, and the greater the working, the greater the burning." Her blind gaze was unbearable in its kindness. "Your grandmother chose poverty of self over risk. She gave up the tongue and kept her memories, all of them, safe and useless, right up until she died with a full purse and nothing worth buying. You may do the same. Many do. Most do. It is a respectable choice."

"And the other choice?"

"The other choice is to spend yourself. Freely, badly, gloriously—to pay memory after memory for the words that save a child's life, until you reach the end of yourself and find out what remains when the spending's done." Hesper's tail curled once, uncurled. "I won't pretend it's noble. Nobility is a story survivors tell. It is simply the other price, and it comes due whether or not you believe you can pay it."

Casper looked down at the lamp. Its flame stood perfectly straight, as though listening.

She thought of her mother's face—or tried to, and found, with a lurch of horror, that she had to work at it. The shape was there, the general fact of it, but the details had gone soft as wet paper. How long since she had truly looked? Nine years of ledgers, of keeping her eyes down, of practicing invisibility until invisibility became her—had she been erasing herself all along, without any language at all, without any bargain, paying out her own face to a silence that never even thanked her?

At least the old tongue would give something back, she thought wildly. At least it would spend her on something.

"If I say yes," Casper said slowly, "when does it start?"

Hesper's whiskers lifted. In another face it would have been a smile.

"It started the moment you walked down those stairs," she said. "But formally—tomorrow night, after the guild sleeps. Bring the girl if you can manage it; she'll be your best teacher, though she doesn't know it. We'll begin with listening. Listening is free." She paused, and her voice dropped into something older, something that made the stones themselves seem to attend. "Speaking is where the bill arrives."

Casper nodded. Then, because she was her grandmother's granddaughter and honesty had always been her most expensive habit, she said: "You knew Maren. Will you tell me about her? What she was like—before?"

For a long moment the cat said nothing. The lamp flame leaned, though there was no draft.

"She hummed," Hesper said at last, "the same song you're humming now without knowing it. She was afraid of the same things you're afraid of. And she made the same face you're making—the one that says *I have already decided, I'm only waiting for someone to tell me it's allowed*." She rose, shook herself, ash-grey fur settling like snow. "It's allowed, Casper Kavanagh. Go to bed. Tomorrow we spend."

Casper climbed the stairs with the lamp guttering, and at the top step she stopped, because below her, faint and clear through the guild's old bones, Hesper began to sing—the same tune that had lived in Casper's head all her life without a name—and beneath it, deeper, the choir under the ledger hall stirred and answered, and for the first time the two songs fit together like a lock finding its key.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow we spend.

Above her, through three floors of stone and ledger and locked doors, she could just hear it: footsteps in the corridor outside the holding room. Slow. Patient. Stopping at Juniper's door.

Someone else was awake, too.
