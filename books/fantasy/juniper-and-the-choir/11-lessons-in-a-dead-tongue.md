## 11. Lessons in a Dead Tongue

The cellar beneath the Vellum Guild smelled of cold stone and older things—ink gone to vinegar, paper rotting sweet in the dark, and underneath it all a faint mineral tang like rain that had fallen centuries ago and never quite dried. Casper came down the spiral stairs at midnight, carrying a candle she had stolen from the chandler's stores, a heel of bread, and the ledger of Serevan pressed flat against her ribs where her heartbeat could feel it.

Hesper was waiting, of course. The blind cellar-cat sat on the third step from the bottom, tail wrapped over her paws like a grey scarf, her milky eyes turned precisely toward the sound of Casper's boots.

"You're late," Hesper said. Her voice was a dry rustle, a leaf dragged across slate. "Grief makes people slow. So does fear. You have both, so I forgave you an hour ago."

"I'm not grieving."

"Not yet." The cat rose, stretched until her spine curved like a drawn bow, and descended past Casper into the dark. "Come. Lessons are held below, where the walls remember better than we do."

* * *

The deepest chamber of the cellar was not on any plan of the guild. Casper had known this for years without knowing she knew it—the way she knew the moths' names, the way she had always known which crates whispered. There was a door behind the racks of preserved weather, a door that looked like a wall until Hesper brushed against it and the stones sighed apart.

Inside, the room was round, and old, and wrong for the building above it. The ceiling arched in a style no Vellum mason used; the floor was laid in tiles of greenish slate arranged in spirals, and every tile bore a carved symbol worn shallow by feet long dead. Candlelight made the symbols swim.

Juniper was already there.

She sat cross-legged in the center of the spiral, and when Casper's light touched her, the girl did not flinch. She had stopped flinching around Casper sometime in the last week—somewhere between the smuggled bread and the drawing of the door, between the sentence Casper had understood without trying and the night she had spent shaking afterward. Now Juniper simply watched her with those grave dark eyes and patted the tile beside her. An invitation. A command. With Juniper it was often hard to tell the difference.

"She insisted on coming down," Hesper said, leaping to a low shelf of stone that might once have been an altar. "She's been waiting since dusk. Children feel the pull of this room. It wants mouths."

"Don't say things like that."

"It's true, though. Truth is the only tuition I accept." The cat settled, tucking her ruined eyes away from the light. "Sit, Casper Kavanagh. Both of you. We begin."

Casper sat. The slate was cold through her skirt, and through the cold she felt something else—a faint vibration, like a plucked string still humming somewhere far below.

"Before your grandmother hid your line," Hesper began, "before the erasure, before the Bureau learned to be afraid of a country it couldn't buy—Serevan speech was taught the way rivers teach their banks. By flowing over you until you took its shape. There were schools. There were songs for children to learn the grammar by heart before they understood a single word. All of that is ash, or worse than ash—unmade, so that even the ash isn't sure what burned." Her tail flicked once. "What survives survives badly. In fragments. In cats who were old when the maps were redrawn, and in one small girl who carries the whole river inside her and cannot pour it out."

Juniper's chin lifted slightly. Stubborn. Casper was beginning to build a dictionary of the girl's silences, and this one meant *I choose what I give.*

"So we will not try to make her pour," Hesper said, watching Casper watch the child. "We will let her drip. And you—you will learn the first law tonight, or nothing else I teach you will matter."

"The law," Casper said. "You said there was a price."

"There is always a price. That is not Serevan's law; that is the world's. But most trades hide their cost behind coin and distance. Ours does not." Hesper's voice dropped, and the room seemed to lean in. "In Serevan speech, every word spoken is a piece of the speaker given away. Not effort. Not breath. *Substance.* A word takes memory, or feeling, or time out of you, and it does not come back. The old speakers were rich enough to spend lavishly—they gave away afternoons, grudges, the taste of certain fruits—and lived long and spoke much. But the poor in tongue grew quiet early. And the desperate—" the cat paused— "the desperate gave away things they could not afford, and became hollow, and the hollow ones were the first the Bureau collected, because an emptied vessel holds whatever pours into it."

The choir under the floorboards. Casper thought of them suddenly—two hundred years of voices sealed beneath the guild, singing in the dark. Had some of them been speakers who spent themselves down to nothing? Was that what remained when the giving was done?

"And if I speak anyway?" she asked. "If I pay?"

"Then the language answers you. Truly answers. It opens doors—not only stone ones." Hesper turned her blind face toward Juniper. "Show her the first word, little mouth. The one you've been saving."

Juniper looked at Casper for a long moment. Then she raised one hand, palm up, and drew a symbol in the air with one finger—a simple mark, two strokes crossing like a bird's footprint.

And Casper *heard* it.

Not with her ears. The symbol made a sound in the place behind her sternum where the beast-voices lived, where moth-whispers and dog-dreams had been arriving uninvited all her life. The sound was soft, syllabic, like water finding a crack. She knew what it meant the way she knew the meaning of a cat's flattened ears—not learned, but recognized.

"*Listen,*" she breathed. "It means listen."

"It means more than that," Hesper said. "*Listen-and-be-changed-by-what-you-hear.* Your trade-tongue has no single word for it. That is the first thing to understand about Serevan: it is not poorer than the languages you know. It is richer. Every word is a small complete thought, a seed with the whole tree folded inside. Which is why each one costs so much. You are not spending a sound. You are spending a truth."

"Now you say it," Hesper said.

Casper's mouth went dry. "It costs me something? Just that?"

"A little. The first payments are small—that is the trap. Listen costs you a moment, a color, the name of a street you half-remembered. Trifles. You'll pay and feel rich, because the word gives back more than it takes—it gives you the *hearing* of it, forever. But the ledger balances, Casper. It always balances. Speak carelessly for a year and you will find you no longer remember why you started speaking at all."

Juniper was watching her. The girl's hand still hung in the air, the birdprint symbol hovering between them, patient as moonlight.

Casper thought about her mother. She didn't know why—the thought arrived sideways, the way dread arrives. Her mother, who had died when Casper was nine, whose face Casper could still summon if she closed her eyes: the laugh lines, the ink stain on the left knuckle, the particular tilt of her head when she was pretending not to worry.

She thought of that face the way you check that your purse is still on your belt. Still there. Still hers.

"All right," Casper said. And shaped the sound.

It was harder than hearing it—her mouth was built for trade-tongue, for clipped guild-syllables and careful hedges. The word came out rough, misshapen, but it *landed*. She felt it leave her, felt the small tug behind her sternum like a thread drawn through cloth, and something went with it—something tiny, a grain of sand from an endless beach. She wouldn't even miss it. Whatever it was.

The room answered.

Every carved tile in the spiral floor brightened, just barely, the way coals breathe when stirred. Somewhere below them—far below, deeper than cellars go—the choir shifted in its sleep, and one voice among the many sang a single clear note that matched the shape of the word she'd spoken. As if she had knocked, politely, and something vast had turned toward the door.

Beside her, Juniper's eyes went wide and wet. The girl pressed both hands to her own sternum, then reached out and pressed one palm, feather-light, against Casper's—right over the place where the word had gone out.

*You paid,* the touch said. *I felt it.*

"Yes," Casper said, in trade-tongue, free of charge. "That's how it works, apparently."

* * *

They practiced until the candle had burned down to a thumb's width and Casper's head ached in a new way—not the dull throb of tiredness but a fine-grained erosion, like sand shifting in an hourglass.

Hesper taught slowly, and made them stop often. "Rest," the cat would say, after every third word. "Count what you have. Grief is tuition here, and tuition comes due whether you attend class or not. Learn to feel the difference between a cheap payment and an expensive one, or you will bankrupt yourself saying hello."

The cheap words, at least, were beautiful. Casper learned *water-that-remembers-the-sea*, and lost the smell of her childhood street in the rain. She learned *door-not-as-wood-but-as-willingness*, and lost an afternoon from her ninth summer—an afternoon she hadn't known she'd kept until it was gone, leaving a Juniper-shaped hole in the sequence of days. She learned the word Juniper offered for *small-trust-freely-given-between-strangers-on-a-dark-road*, and that one cost her the memory of her father's voice saying goodnight, which she discovered only afterward, reaching for it and finding smooth blankness, and had to sit very still for a while with her hands flat on the cold tiles while Hesper watched her with those ruined eyes and said nothing, because there was nothing to say. The ledger had balanced. The lesson had been taught.

Juniper taught differently. The girl rarely offered a word directly. Instead she would draw—on the slate floor with chalk Casper brought her, on scraps of smuggled paper—and Casper would look at the drawings and find the Serevan rising in her throat unbidden, whole sentences assembling themselves out of shapes: *the door is below and it is dreaming.* *The singers are not angry. They are waiting, which looks like anger from outside.* *You are the one who hears. That was always going to cost you.*

Each time, Casper paid. Each time, she checked her mother's face afterward, the way a miser checks a strongbox. The laugh lines. The ink stain. The tilted head.

On the fourth night—or the fifth; Casper had begun losing count of nights, another small expenditure she hadn't authorized—Juniper drew a woman. Tall, ink-stained fingers, head tilted. She pointed at the drawing, then at Casper, then made the birdprint sign for *listen* and held it, waiting.

Casper understood what was being asked. *Say her. Say your mother, in the old tongue. Give her to the language.*

"No," Casper said.

Juniper didn't argue. She simply sat, hands folded, radiating a patience that was somehow worse than argument.

"She's mine," Casper said. "Everything else—I understand, I accept, I'm paying, aren't I? Every word, a piece of me, fine, I'm learning to live as a smaller person every night. But not her. Not her face. That's not tuition. That's everything."

Hesper's tail moved once, slowly, across the altar-stone. "Do you know what your grandmother chose, when she hid your line?" the cat said quietly. "She didn't seal your gift. Gifts don't seal; they pool. She sealed your *reasons*. She made sure you'd grow up with nothing worth saying in the old tongue—no inheritance, no grief deep enough to open your mouth. Silence wasn't your armor, child. It was hers. And now you're sitting in the ruins of her spell, learning to speak, and the language is doing what languages do: taking your life as it goes." The blind eyes found her, unerring. "One day soon you will want to say something that matters. Something that saves. And the language will ask for something large, and you will have to decide, in a heartbeat, what you can live without. Practice that decision now, on your own terms, or the world will make it for you on its terms."

"I won't forget her."

"Everyone says that," Hesper said. "The ledger doesn't listen to everyone. It listens to the words."

Casper looked down at the chalk drawing. Her mother looked back at her, rendered in a child's sure strokes—more alive on the slate, suddenly, than in Casper's memory, which seemed to tremble at the edges like a candle flame in a draft.

And she understood, with a lurch, that this was the choice. Right here. She could refuse, and keep her mother's face locked away, unsaid, safe—and remain half-mute in the language that might save Juniper's life. Or she could pay.

"Grief is tuition," Hesper murmured. "So the teachers tell you. What they mean is: grief is the coin. Love is the account it's drawn from."

Casper closed her eyes. Found the face. Laugh lines. Ink stain. Tilted head.

She opened her mouth and said her mother's name in Serevan.

The word left her like blood leaving a wound—warm, immediate, irrevocable. She felt it go, felt the great ledger take its due, and braced herself—

—but what vanished was not the face. It was the sound of her mother calling her in from the rain one evening years ago, the exact pitch and warmth of *Casper, come inside, you'll catch your death*—and Casper realized, even as the grief hit, that the language had taken the truer treasure. Anyone could keep a face in a locket. The voice was the thing you couldn't draw. The voice was the thing that had made the face a mother's and not a portrait.

She was crying before she knew it, silently, the way she did everything. Juniper crawled into the circle of her arms without asking, and Casper held her, and the choir far below sang one low sustained note that might, in a kinder country, have been called comfort.

"That," Hesper said softly, into the dark, "was your first true sentence."

"It was one word."

"One word *about someone you love*. That is a sentence. Everything before tonight was finger-spelling." The cat leapt down from the altar and pressed her warm flank briefly against Casper's ankle—a gesture Casper had learned to read as embrace. "You'll grieve every time you speak now. Good. Grief keeps the price honest. The day speaking stops hurting is the day you start spending like Vetch."

* * *

They climbed back up near dawn, Casper hollowed and strangely light, Juniper's hand in hers, Hesper leading with her nose. At the top of the cellar stairs, in the grey sliver of light from the coal hatch, the cat stopped.

"What is it?" Casper whispered.

"Visitors," Hesper said. "Above us. Three sets of boots in the entry hall, and the smell of official wax and scorched paper." Her whiskers flattened. "Bureau men come before their hour. They're early, which means they're frightened, which means someone told them something they didn't want to hear."

Juniper's grip tightened on Casper's hand.

"And there's a fourth man," Hesper added, tilting her head toward sounds too faint for human ears. "He walks like the others but he stands apart from them. And he is listening—to the floor. To the walls." A pause. "To the choir. He can hear it, Casper. Barely. But he can hear it, and he doesn't yet know what to do about that."

Casper stood in the dark with a dead language burning in her chest and her mother's voice freshly spent, and understood that the lessons had just ended, and the examination was about to begin.
