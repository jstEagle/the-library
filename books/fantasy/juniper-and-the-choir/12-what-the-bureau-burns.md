## 12. What the Bureau Burns

The village of Lowmark had three hundred and eleven souls, one shrine, and a memory-records house the size of a generous barn, and by noon of the second day Imre Sol had burned all of it but the souls.

He worked methodically, because method was the whole of him. The records came out in armloads: birth-threads woven on looms that remembered every child's first cry; marriage-knots tied from hair and river-clay; death-masks pressed in beeswax so that grief would have a face to visit. He stacked them in the shrine's stone yard, doused them with lamp-oil, and fed them to the fire in the order prescribed by the Bureau's Field Manual, Ninth Revision—kinship records first, then place-records, then the deep shelves, the ones that held what a village knew about itself before it knew anything at all.

The smoke rose straight up into a windless sky, which the Manual said was auspicious. *A clean erasure ascends; a contested one scatters.*

Sol watched the smoke rise straight up and felt nothing at all.

This was not numbness. Numbness was a failure, and failures were reported. This was the opposite of failure—the smooth, frictionless operation of a man perfectly fitted to his function, the way a key is fitted to its lock. Nine years in the Bureau of Erasure's Verification Office, and Imre Sol had learned that the work was not cruelty. Cruelty required a self that could be wounded by its own actions. The work was *subtraction*. You removed a thing from the world's ledger, and the world, being lighter, went on more easily. Somewhere a village would forget why its oldest song made its grandmothers weep. Somewhere a road would lose the reason for its curve. The Bureau did not break things. It tidied.

He was thirty-four years old, thin as a filing rule, with the grey-and-white livery of a Third-Class Verifier and a face that innkeepers forgot while he was still paying them. He had burned forty-one collections before Lowmark. He could recite their names—not the villages' names, which were struck along with everything else, but the catalogue numbers, which endured, because even erasure must be accounted for. That was the Bureau's deepest joke, though no one at the Bureau laughed: they kept meticulous records of everything they unmade. Ashes, weighed. Volumes, counted. Names, entered in the Index of Removals in a hand so fine it looked like frost.

Lowmark's collection was small. Provincial. He had verified the inventory against the manifest twice, found a discrepancy of four items—a cradle-song carved on a wooden bird, missing—and noted it for retrieval. The bird would be found. It always was. People hid their memories the way mice hid grain, and people were easier to search than fields.

On the third day he interviewed the villagers, as the Manual required, to confirm the erasure had taken.

He sat in the shrine—it had been stripped of its god, who had been a minor river-saint and went onto the fire with the rest—and called them in one at a time. An old woman with flour to her elbows. A boy of perhaps ten, brought by his mother, who waited outside with her hands folded. Sol asked his questions from the printed card: *What do you remember of your grandmother's grandmother? What language did the first settlers here speak? Why does your festival fall when it falls?*

The old woman smiled pleasantly and said she'd always supposed the festival was for the harvest. The boy shrugged. His mother, when her turn came, said her family had been here forever, doing whatever families do, and wasn't that enough?

*Erasure confirmed,* Sol wrote, in his fine frost-hand. *No residual attachment detected. Subject population reports continuity without content. Recommend closure of file.*

It was textbook. It was, in fact, beautiful—cleaner than most, no riots, no weeping women clutching doorposts, none of the ugly scenes that got filed under *Complications*. And yet standing in the shrine yard that evening, watching the last embers of the deep shelves gutter out, Sol experienced an anomaly he could not classify.

Not a feeling. He checked. He was quite good at checking. But there was a hitch in the evening, the way a ledger shows a hitch when two columns refuse to sum. Three hundred and eleven souls, and not one of them had come to watch the fire. Not one had asked what burned. He had done this forty-one times, and there was *always* someone—some old man on a fence, some child throwing pebbles at the smoke. Villages came to watch their own funerals even when they didn't know whose funeral it was. Something in them leaned toward the flame.

Lowmark had turned its back. All of it. As if the village itself had decided, in the wordless way of animals before weather, that whatever was burning was better off unseen.

Sol stood in the yard until dark, and then he wrote in his private notebook—which was against regulations, all Verifiers kept one, which was also against regulations—the following sentence:

*The absence watched me back.*

Then he crossed it out, because it was the kind of sentence that got a man reassigned to the Seal Vaults, where clerks went mad among documents too dangerous to destroy and too true to file.

*

The summons came by Bureau courier on the fifth day, and it changed the shape of his errand entirely.

*By order of the Office of Acquisitions: proceed without delay to the Vellum Guild, [city withheld], there to verify and appraise certain items of interest arising from Catalogue 7-Serevan. Full authority granted. Burn nothing. Touch little. Report everything.*

He read the catalogue number three times. Serevan. Every clerk in the Bureau knew the number the way churchmen know the name of the fallen angel. Two hundred years ago there had been a country whose language was not a tool but a substance—words that *did*, speech that cost the speaker something real and bought something realer. The first Bureau had tried to buy it. The country had refused. And the Bureau, unable to own what it could not purchase, had done the only thing left in its power: it had erased the country entire. Maps, records, monuments, genealogies, the very memory of the name from the mouths of neighbors—until Serevan was a hole shaped like a country, and the hole itself was classified.

Catalogue 7 was the hole's official designation. In nine years, Sol had never once seen an item assigned to it. Items assigned to Catalogue 7 were not supposed to exist. That was rather the point of the catalogue.

He traveled fast, by post-road and Bureau warrant, and reached the city on the evening of the ninth day. The Vellum Guild rose over the merchant quarter like a paper mountain—grey stone, leaded windows, a sign of a quill piercing a cloud. Sol presented his seals at the wicket gate. A junior factor read them, went pale in the correct bureaucratic way, and led him through halls stacked with impossible inventory: jars of bottled thunder muttering in their glass; a shelf of borrowed years, each labeled with a debtor's name; cages of messenger-moths whose wings clicked like tiny typewriters.

Sol touched none of it. *Touch little.* He had burned a hundred collections; he knew how things resented being handled.

Guildmaster Oren Vetch received him in a room lined with ledgers, a broad comfortable man with ink-stained fingers and the eyes of a moneylender counting a debtors' procession. They performed the courtesies. Wine was poured and untouched, per regulation. Then Vetch slid a single sheet across the desk, and Sol felt the temperature of the conversation drop.

"An acquisition," Vetch said. "Recent. Unusual provenance. I've held it for appraisal pending Bureau interest—as the seal on your letter suggested there would be."

The sheet held a rubbing, taken in charcoal from a doorframe or a wall. Upon it were symbols: angular, dense, arranged in vertical columns like rain frozen mid-fall.

Sol knew what they were the way he knew the catalogue number—by training, by classification drills, by the Bureau's habit of teaching its clerks to recognize the shapes of forbidden things so they could report them accurately. Old Serevan script. Last attested use: two centuries past. Current attested use: none. *None* was the word the manuals used, in italics.

"Where," Sol said, "did this come from?"

"A child," said Vetch, and smiled with the satisfaction of a man holding a winning hand he hadn't yet priced. "Left on our steps nine days ago. She doesn't speak—or rather, gentlemen of my acquaintance who've heard her say otherwise insist she speaks constantly, just not in any tongue known to the guilds, the universities, or the Crown. She writes this instead." He tapped the rubbing. "My appraisers are divided. My archivist resigned. And I thought: before I sell her to the highest bidder, perhaps the Bureau would care to make an offer."

"You're selling a child."

"I'm selling *inventory*," Vetch said mildly, "that happens to have hands and feet. The distinction has never troubled my conscience, and I'd thank you not to trouble it on my behalf."

Sol looked down at the rubbing again. Regulation said: verify, appraise, report. Regulation said the correct response to Catalogue 7 material was containment and removal. Regulation said a great many things, and Sol had spent nine years inside those sayings the way a fish lives inside water, and it occurred to him now—he could not have said why—that he had never once noticed the water.

"I'll need to see the item," he said.

"Of course." Vetch rose, smoothing his waistcoat. "Though I warn you, she's not fond of visitors."

*

The holding room was on the third floor, at the end of a corridor that smelled of dust and lamp-oil, and Vetch stopped at the door and did not enter. "I'll leave you to your verification," he said, and his smile had a new edge to it, the smile of a man who has just realized his inventory might be worth more than he guessed and wants to watch the buyer's face to learn how much more.

Sol opened the door.

The room beyond was spare: a cot, a basin, a barred window, a stack of paper and charcoal stubs—Vetch, apparently, encouraged the drawing, having learned that a writing asset was a pricing asset. And in the middle of the floor sat a girl of perhaps eight years, small and straight-backed, with dark hair cut raggedly and eyes the color of deep water over stones.

She looked up at him.

And Imre Sol, Third-Class Verifier of the Bureau of Erasure, who had burned forty-one collections and felt nothing, heard singing.

It came from nowhere—or not from nowhere; that was the horror of it, the gift of it. It came from *below*. From under the floorboards, under the foundations, under the city's old bones, faint as a hymn heard through three walls of a strange church. Voices. Many voices, layered, patient, in a language he did not know and recognized instantly, because he had spent his life cataloguing the shape of the hole it had been dug out of. The choir sang low and steady, the way prisoners sing who have given up on rescue but not on the song.

The girl tilted her head, listening to something. Then she looked at Sol again—looked *into* him, the way one looks into a locked box whose lock is rusted through—and picked up a charcoal stub.

She drew, quickly, with the confidence of long practice: a vertical line, crossed by three short strokes. A column of rain. One symbol, alone on the page. She turned the paper toward him and waited.

Sol knew the symbol. It was in the recognition drills. It was in the classified glossaries, entry seven-one-one, pronunciation unknown, meaning redacted. He had copied it a thousand times in exercises, a shape without a soul, a fossil of a dead tongue.

But the choir under the floorboards surged when the girl finished drawing it, one great wave of voices, and in the surge the symbol stopped being a shape and became a *word*, and the word passed through Sol like cold water through cloth, and he understood it—understood it the way you understand a burn—with a meaning that no Bureau glossary had ever contained because no Bureau glossary could have survived containing it.

The word meant: *you hear us.*

Sol stood very still. His hand, he noticed distantly, had gone to his breast pocket, where his seals hung—the instruments of removal, of subtraction, of tidy ash. The girl watched his hand. Something moved behind her deep-water eyes that was not fear. It was older than fear. It was the look of someone watching a man decide whether to put down a knife, and knowing, with terrible patience, that the deciding will take him longer than it should.

Down in the corridor, Guildmaster Vetch coughed politely, a reminder that verification had a schedule, that appraisal had a price, that everything in this building was already sold in principle and awaited only its figure.

The girl took back the paper. Under the symbol she drew a second mark: a small circle, closed, complete. Then she set down the charcoal, folded her hands in her lap, and looked at him with the composure of a judge awaiting testimony.

Under the floorboards, the choir waited too—all of Serevan's exiled dead, sealed beneath the guildhall built upon their capital, two hundred years of patience gathered into a single held breath—and Imre Sol, who had come to verify an acquisition, stood in the holding room of the Vellum Guild and understood, with the first doubt of his life arriving like the first crack in a dam, that he had not been sent here to appraise the girl at all.

He had been sent here because the Bureau had finally found the last mouth of Catalogue 7.

And some part of him—the part that had written *the absence watched me back* and crossed it out—was already composing the false report that would keep her safe, and was horrified to find how easily the words came, and how much they sounded like a prayer.
