## 13. The Night of Open Cages

The guild kept its beasts the way misers keep coins: counted nightly, locked in the dark, and never spent unless the profit was certain.

Casper had walked past their cages for nine years with her eyes down and her gift folded up small as a pressed flower. She knew every lock on the beast-row by heart—the moth-cases with their brass grilles, the terrarium where the guild's two salamander-scribes slept coiled around inkstones, the aviary of storm-wrens whose feathers held weather the way other birds held song. She knew them, and she had never once opened a door for them. That was the arithmetic she had lived by: her silence bought her invisibility; her invisibility bought her life. Every cage she passed was another coin paid toward her own safety.

Tonight she meant to spend all of it at once.

She stood in the beast-row an hour past midnight with a ring of stolen keys in her fist and her heart going like something trying to escape through her ribs. The lamps were doused. Moonlight came through the row's high slit windows in pale blades, and in that light the messenger-moths had already begun to stir against their grilles, sensing—because they always sensed—what she had come to do.

*Open us,* they whispered, in voices like ash falling on silk. *Open us, open us, we are small, we are quick, no one counts what flies.*

"I know," Casper said. Her voice sounded strange to her, too loud in the dark, though it was barely more than breath. "I'm sorry it took so long."

The oldest of them—a grey moth the size of her palm who had carried dispatches through three wars before the Guild bought her at salvage—pressed her antennae to the grille and said, in the small grave voice Casper had been refusing to hear since she was nineteen years old: *You will not be able to take it back.*

"No," Casper agreed. "I know."

Below her feet, faint as a pulse through stone, she could feel the choir singing. It sang every night now. It had started softly, months ago—a thread of sound under the floorboards of the ledger hall that she'd told herself was water in the pipes—and grown until some nights she lay awake in her narrow room above the counting office and heard Serevan's dead singing to the child three floors down, singing *last mouth, last mouth, little last mouth*, and Casper had pressed her pillow over her ears and pretended not to understand, because understanding was a door and doors, once opened, could not be closed.

Tomorrow the Pale Assessor's wagons would come. Vetch had signed the bill of sale that afternoon; Casper had watched him do it from the gallery above the signing room, watching the Guildmaster's pen move through the word *Juniper* the way a butcher's knife moves through a joint. One week, Vetch had promised the Assessor's man. Delivery within the week.

Casper had one night.

She fitted the first key into the moth-case lock. Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Nine years of careful trembling, and now, at the moment everything could be lost, her hands went quiet—as if some part of her had been waiting for this the whole time, the way a bow waits for the string.

The lock turned with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

*Oh,* breathed the grey moth. *Oh, oh.* And then the case was open, and moths poured out into the moonlight like a handful of grey coins flung upward, wings clattering, and the noise they made—not a cry, nothing so simple—was joy, joy with terror under it, the way ice has water under it.

"Listen," Casper said, and they stilled, hanging in the air around her head like a halo of grey leaves. "I need chaos. Not harm—chaos. Every animal loose in this building by the third bell. Storm-wrens in the east corridor. Salamanders out of their tank—they'll smear ink over anything they cross, let them. Get into the kitchens, the counting rooms, the archive stairs. When the night clerks come running, I want them running the wrong way."

*And you?* asked the grey moth.

"I'm going to get Juniper."

*You will not be able to take it back,* the moth said again, gently, the way one says a thing to someone standing on a ledge. *Not the beasts. Not the girl. Not yourself. Once they see you, clerk Kavanagh, you will never be unseen again.*

Casper looked down at her own hands in the moonlight—ledger-keeper's hands, invisible hands, hands that had spent nine years learning to be furniture—and thought of Juniper in the holding room with her knees drawn up and her arms wrapped around whatever she was protecting inside herself, enduring mirrors and blood-ink and worse because she would not give the Guild one single syllable of herself.

"I know," Casper said. "That's rather the point."

She opened the wrens' aviary next, and the salamanders', and the case of glass bees that the Guild used for pollinating rare contracts, and last, with a key she had to break off in the lock because it hadn't turned in twenty years, the long cabinet where the Guild kept a sleeping thing called only, in the oldest inventory books, *the listener*. She did not open that cabinet's inner door. She wasn't fool enough. But she cracked the outer one, so that when the building woke in confusion, the listener would wake with it and hear everything—and report, in whatever way listeners reported, to whoever fed it best. She would worry about that later. Later was a country she was finally learning to live in.

By the second bell, the Vellum Guild was coming apart beautifully.

Casper heard it begin while she was still on the beast-row stairs: the storm-wrens hit the east corridor like a squall let out of a bottle, shrieking thunder in miniature, and somewhere below a night clerk began to shout, and then two more, and then the salamanders' ink was everywhere they wanted it to be, black tracks scrawled across ledgers and floors in characters that read, if you squinted, like accusations. Moths streamed through the building in ribbons, snuffing candles, tangling themselves in clerks' hair, whispering *open, open, open* into every ear they found. The Guild had spent two centuries teaching its animals that they were inventory. Inventory, tonight, was remembering otherwise.

Casper moved against the current of chaos, down the back stair to the holding rooms, with Hesper's parting words from the cellar sitting in her chest like a coal.

*They will expect the front of your mind, child,* the blind cat had told her, two nights ago, in the dark among the wine casks where she lived like a small grey empress. *Vetch is not clever the way clever men are clever. He is patient the way traps are patient. He has watched you for years—he watches everyone worth owning. So go sideways. Go through the place he thinks is beneath notice.*

"The place beneath notice is how I've survived," Casper had said.

"Yes," Hesper had said, and her clouded eyes had turned toward Casper with terrible tenderness. "And look what it's cost."

Now Casper went sideways. The holding room's main door was iron-banded oak, locked, with a night warder posted—she could hear him shifting his weight beyond it, nervous, listening to the riot above. But the holding rooms had been built into the old Serevan foundations, and the old foundations did not care about the Guild's renovations. There was a service gap behind the eastern wall, a crawl-space barely a shoulder wide, that Casper had found years ago while auditing a crate of bottled fog and had told no one about, because telling no one was the whole architecture of her life.

She squeezed into the gap with her lamp unlit, feeling her way along cold stone that hummed faintly under her palms—the choir's song moving through it, closer here than anywhere, as though the walls themselves were throat. The stones were warm. That frightened her more than anything else that night. Two hundred years dead, and the country under the Guild was still warm.

The gap ended at a ventilation grate behind the holding room's shelving. Through the slats she could see the room: one lamp, one cot, and Juniper sitting upright on the cot, awake, her eyes fixed on the grate as though she had known Casper was coming—which, Casper thought grimly, she probably had. The girl had drawn her a door, and then drawn Casper standing before it. Children who spoke in the language before kingdoms did not need to be told things twice.

"Juniper." Casper worked the grate's pins loose with a knife. "It's me. We're leaving."

Juniper slid off the cot without a sound and came to the wall, and when Casper pulled the grate free the girl climbed through the gap into her arms with the ease of long practice, as though being passed through walls between captors were simply a thing that happened to her. She was light. That was the worst of it—how light she was, after weeks of the Guild's provisioning, as though her body were slowly deciding it could manage with less.

Casper set her down in the crawl-space and put her lips close to the girl's ear.

"Can you run?"

Juniper nodded once. Then she took Casper's hand—the first time she had ever taken it, rather than merely tolerated it being taken—and pressed something into her palm: a scrap of paper, folded small. Casper knew without looking what would be on it. A door. And two figures, walking away from it together, one tall and one small, drawn with the fierce economy of a child who wasted no lines on anything untrue.

They went. Up through the gap, along the passage behind the archive stairs where the salamanders' ink glistened black on the flagstones, toward the cellar door that led to the coal chute and the alley beyond—Hesper's route, the route beneath notice. Behind them the Guild roared and stumbled, clerks shouting about wrens and moths and the listener's cabinet standing open, and Casper felt, for eleven full steps, something so foreign she nearly didn't recognize it.

Hope.

Then the lamps came up in the cellar all at once, every one of them, and Guildmaster Oren Vetch was sitting in Hesper's chair among the wine casks with his hands folded on his knee.

"There you are," he said pleasantly. "I wondered which way you'd come. You always were one for the back passages, Clerk Kavanagh. It's in your audit notes. Nine years of audit notes. Did you think I never read them?"

Later—much later, lying sleepless in a locked room with the memory replaying itself like a wound she kept touching—Casper would understand exactly how completely she had been outmaneuvered, and hate herself with a thoroughness the Guild itself could not have improved upon.

Vetch had never believed the chaos was real. He had let it happen. The moment the first moth case opened, he had gone down to his own office, taken out the file he had kept on Casper Kavanagh since her third year—thin, but damning in aggregate: the sick days after the messenger-moths' cages were cleaned; the way the kennel hounds fell silent when she passed; the incident, never explained, with the cart-horse that had refused to leave her side for a week—and he had done the arithmetic. A woman who freed the beasts was a woman taking the beasts' value out of the equation. Which meant the beasts had become a distraction, which meant she wanted something else, which meant there was exactly one something else in the building worth the trade.

He had posted no guards at the holding room's iron door, because he wanted her to succeed at the theft. He had left the cellar lit and empty, because he knew the cellar was where the rat would run—and he had sat down to wait in the chair of the only creature in the Vellum Guild whom Casper trusted, because he understood that trust, properly managed, was just another kind of trap.

"You're wondering how long I've known," Vetch said, rising from the chair. In the lamplight his face had the mild shine of good parchment, and his voice stayed level and reasonable, the voice of a man discussing freight rates. "Sit down, Clerk. Both of you. The girl can sit on that cask. No one is going to hurt anyone. I want to talk about terms."

Casper put herself between Vetch and Juniper. It was a useless gesture and she made it anyway, spreading her arms like a badly written clause. "There are no terms."

"There are always terms. That's the entire business." He took a step nearer, unhurried. "Here is mine. You will demonstrate your gift for the Pale Assessor tomorrow. Fully, publicly, on a subject of the Bureau's choosing. In exchange, the child goes free—released, unfettered, no bill of sale. I'll even write it into the ledger myself, in my own hand, witnessed. The Guild honors its contracts, Kavanagh. You know that better than anyone."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the Bureau takes both of you, and I collect the fee for two assets instead of one, and I lose nothing but the pleasure of your company." He smiled. It was not a cruel smile; that was the horror of it. It was the smile of a man balancing columns. "You've cost me a great deal tonight, you understand. The moth-lines alone. Seventy years of breeding. But I'm a fair man. Fair men don't punish—they renegotiate."

Under her hand, Casper felt Juniper go very still. Not frightened-still. Listening-still. And beneath their feet, faint but unmistakable, the choir had stopped singing.

In the sudden silence, from the shadow between two wine casks at the back of the cellar, came a sound like a small dry laugh.

"Oh, sit down, Oren," said Hesper, and stepped into the light. "You're boring the child, and she's had quite enough of boring men."

Casper's blood went to ice. *"No—"*

"Hush, dear." The old cat sat down in the middle of the cellar floor, wrapped her tail over her paws, and looked up at Vetch with her clouded eyes—looked at him, truly looked, and for the first time in the decades she had lived in his cellars she let him see that she was looking. "You wanted to know what was making your horses refuse their bridles and your hounds sing in their sleep. You've had suspicions for years; don't pretend otherwise, it's beneath us both. Well. Here is your answer, free of charge, and you may enter it in your precious ledger under whatever heading amuses you."

"What," Vetch said slowly, "are you."

"I," said Hesper, "am older than your Guild, older than your Bureau, older than the erasure you lot like to pretend was tidy. I am the reason your building stands on its foundations at all, because I argued the terms of its construction with the masons' grandmothers before your grandfather's grandfather drew his first breath. And I am—" she paused, savoring it, an empress conceding a border town, "—worth considerably more to your Pale Assessor than one small deaf-souled clerk and one mute child combined. So. New terms, Oren. Your assessor gets me. Both of them walk out of this cellar, tonight, unbought and unbound, and your ledger stays clean of perjury."

"Hesper, don't—" Casper's voice cracked in the middle of the name. "Don't you dare—"

"Someone has to pay for the escape, dear, and you haven't got anything left to pay with. You spent it all getting this far." The cat rose, stretched with obscene leisure, and trotted past Casper's ankles, brushing them once—an touch so brief and warm that Casper felt it would stay in her skin longer than most memories. "Look after the girl. Teach her the words. That's the payment I actually care about, and you'll make it whether you like it or not, so spare me the tears."

"Terms accepted?" Vetch asked, already reaching for the little notebook he carried everywhere, the one in which the whole world was, to him, line items.

"Terms accepted," said Hesper, "witnessed and sealed, and may every god your Bureau burned hold you to it."

Two night warders came at Vetch's nod and lifted the cat onto a cushioned tray as though she were crown regalia, and she went without struggle, tail curled, dignified as a deposed queen boarding the exile ship. At the cellar door she turned her blind face back toward Casper one last time.

*"Every word spoken is a piece of the speaker given away,"* she said, in the old tongue, in Serevan, so that the words rang against the warm stones and the choir below seemed to catch its breath. *"So choose, child, what you give—and to whom. That was always the whole of the lesson. I only ever taught it slowly because you were slow."*

And then the door shut, and the lamps went down to one, and Vetch looked at Casper across the cellar with the expression of a man reviewing a satisfactory quarter's accounts.

"Back to your room, Clerk Kavanagh," he said. "We'll speak in the morning. Oh—and the child returns to the holding room. Terms are terms." His eyes moved to Juniper, and lingered, and Casper saw in them, for the first time, something worse than greed: appetite with a plan behind it. "Though I confess I find myself curious about what else in this building might be worth more than I've been charging for it."

*

They let Casper carry Juniper back.

It was, she understood dimly, part of the punishment—or perhaps part of the bookkeeping; with Vetch the two had always been the same. A warder walked six paces behind them through corridors strewn with feathers and ink and the wreckage of beautiful small treasons, and Casper carried the girl up the stairs to the holding room, and at the iron door Juniper put her arms around Casper's neck and held on—not tight, but with a finality that was worse than tightness, the grip of someone who had been left before and was filing the fact away.

Casper knelt, set her down, and took the girl's face in her hands. "I'm coming back," she said. "Do you hear me? Whatever they tell you, whatever they show you—I am coming back, and next time I won't fail."

Juniper searched her face with those old, old eyes. Then she raised one finger and, on the iron door itself, in the thin layer of dust, wrote a single character of the dead tongue. Casper knew it now—knew it the way you know a scar. It was the character for *debt*. For a promise that costs.

Then the door closed between them, and Casper stood alone in the corridor with the warder's hand on her shoulder and the taste of ash in her mouth, listening—to the choir below, which had resumed its singing, low and slow and unmistakably changed. Not a lullaby any longer.

A reckoning.

Somewhere beneath the ledger hall, two hundred exiled dead were adding a new name to their song, and it was not Juniper's, and it was not Casper's.

It was the cat's.
