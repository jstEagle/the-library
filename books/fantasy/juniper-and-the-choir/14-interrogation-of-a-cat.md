## 14. Interrogation of a Cat

Oren Vetch had interrogated smugglers, forgers, a ghost that had been squatting in the guild's wine cellar for forty years, and once—memorably—a tax assessor who turned out to be three children in an enchanted coat. He considered himself something of a connoisseur of the process. Every interrogation, he had found, came down to the same arithmetic: what the subject wanted, what the subject feared, and the exchange rate between them. Torture was for amateurs. Amateurs spent their capital all at once and learned nothing worth owning.

So when the cellar-cat opened her mouth in his holding room and said, in a voice like dry leaves settling, "You'll want tea. This will take some time," Oren Vetch did not reach for the poker hanging by the door.

He reached for the bell instead, and ordered tea.

* * *

The cat sat on the table. Not on a chair; on the table, directly in front of him, her blind face angled at a spot slightly left of where his head actually was. She was old—old the way certain ledgers were old, their spines cracked, their contents suspect—and she was gray the way ash was gray, as though she had already been burned once and had not bothered to mention it. Her name, according to the guild's own records, was Hesper, and according to those same records she had been resident in the cellars since before Vetch himself had been apprenticed. The guild's records also listed her under "livestock, incidental," which, Vetch was beginning to suspect, was rather like listing a dragon under "furniture, decorative."

"You took my keeper's offer poorly," Vetch said.

"Your man offered me milk and a warm hearth if I told him where the girl sleeps." Hesper's whiskers twitched. "I have slept in every warm corner of this building for longer than your grandfather has been dead, Guildmaster. Milk is not leverage. It is a beverage."

"And what would be leverage?"

The cat turned her head with excruciating slowness until her clouded eyes pointed, more or less, at him. "That depends entirely on what you are buying."

Vetch folded his hands on the table. Around them, the holding room did its work: walls lined with lead-leaf paper to muffle scrying, a ceiling inscribed with containment script that hummed faintly, like a struck tuning fork no one could see. He had put Juniper in this room two nights ago. She had sat in the exact chair he now occupied opposite the cat, and she had looked at the containment script on the ceiling, and she had smiled—not a child's smile, not quite—and drawn one small symbol on the floor with her finger, and the script had stopped humming for eleven minutes while four scribes checked and rechecked every line.

He had doubled the warding after that. He had also, privately, tripled the asking price.

"I am buying information," Vetch said. "About the girl. About the language she speaks. About why a Bureau of Erasure is willing to pay nine years of the guild's profit for one mute child."

"Mm." Hesper began, deliberately, to wash one paw. "And here I thought you'd summoned me down from my cellar to discuss the moth cages."

"The moths are inventory."

"They are prisoners." The paw paused mid-stroke. "But you knew that when you bought them. That's rather the point of you, isn't it? You know what everything costs because you've never once paid for anything yourself."

Vetch let the insult pass through him the way rain passed through a well-built roof. Insults were currency too, and devalued ones at that. "You have a reputation," he said instead. "The staff leave offerings at the cellar door. Fish, mostly. One of my bookkeepers leaves buttons. They say the cellar-cat grants small favors—that she found Marla Tull's lost wedding ring, that she warned the night porter about the fire in the ink stores before any smoke showed. Superstition, I assumed. Guilds run on superstition the way mills run on water."

"But?"

"But I have now watched a blind, arthritic cat walk through a locked door twice." He leaned back. "Once to reach the girl's cell. Once to leave it. My wards didn't so much as flicker. So either my containment script has developed a taste for cats, or you are not, strictly speaking, a cat."

Hesper finished washing her paw and regarded him with what he could only describe as professional appraisal. "Better," she said. "Go on."

* * *

They bargained for two hours.

It was, Vetch reflected later, the finest negotiation of his career, conducted entirely with a creature who refused to say anything useful without a price attached, and who named her prices with the serene confidence of someone who had never once needed the deal more than the other party did. She wanted the moths' cage moved away from the south wall, where the dawn light woke them screaming in their small voices—she claimed she could hear them through three floors of stone, and the claim was so precisely calibrated to unsettle him that he immediately believed it. She wanted the cellar declared hers in perpetuity, in writing, with the guild's seal. She wanted, of all things, a window. A real one, east-facing, high enough that no one would think to look up at it.

"For a cat," Vetch said, "you collect real estate like a dowager duchess."

"For a guildmaster," Hesper replied, "you collect questions like a man assembling a bomb. Shall we continue pretending we don't know what the other is doing?"

In exchange, she gave him pieces. Careful, polished pieces, each one shaped exactly large enough to satisfy him and exactly small enough to keep him hungry. Yes, the child's language was Serevan. No, it was not merely a dead tongue—it had never died, only gone somewhere else. Yes, the Bureau's interest was genuine and the price they'd offered was, if anything, insultingly low. And yes—the piece that made Vetch set down his teacup with a click he hoped sounded casual—the guild itself stood on the ruin of Serevan's capital. Had always stood there. The founding masters had known. It was in the oldest charters, if one read past the seals, which no one ever did.

"The singing," Vetch said. "My night porters report singing under the ledger hall. Low. Choral. They've started refusing the shift without hazard pay."

"That would be the choir."

"The choir being?"

"Everyone the Bureau erased and couldn't fully delete." Hesper's tail curled around her paws with terrible patience. "Erasure is a sloppy craft, Guildmaster. Your Bureau can burn a country off the maps, strike its name from every treaty, pay every historian to forget—but a language lives in mouths, and mouths live in bodies, and bodies leave echoes when they die badly. Two hundred years of echoes, pressed down flat beneath your fine marble floors. They sing because song is the last thing left to them. And they have been waiting—" her blind eyes seemed, for a moment, almost to focus— "for a very long time, for someone who could answer."

"The girl."

"The girl can carry them. A vessel. A country in a jar of skin." Hesper tilted her head. "That's what your buyer wants, isn't it? Not the child. The capacity. The Pale Assessor doesn't purchase things, Master Vetch—he purchases containers. The Crown has learned that erasure doesn't hold unless you own what remains. And the girl is what remains."

Vetch turned his teacup slowly on its saucer, aligning the handle with the table's edge, then with the room's true north, then with the seam in the floorboards. He did these things while thinking, because stillness invited people to fill silences, and filling silences was how amateurs gave value away.

"And Kavanagh?" he asked.

The silence that followed had texture. Weight. He had learned, over thirty years, to feel the difference between a silence that meant *I don't know* and a silence that meant *this is the most expensive thing in the room.* This was the second kind. He kept his eyes on the teacup and waited, and made sure the waiting cost him nothing visible.

"You haven't asked about her before," Hesper said at last.

"She's a clerk. Competent. Unremarkable. She audits crates and files reports and has taken no holiday in nine years, which I initially read as ambition and have recently begun reading as camouflage." He looked up. "She spent forty minutes alone with the girl yesterday. My watcher counted fifty-three minutes, but he drinks on shift. She also freed a caged storm-moth last month and logged it as a clerical error. There is a pattern, and patterns are assets, and assets belong in portfolios."

"Clerks who free moths," Hesper said.

"Clerks who free moths," Vetch agreed, "and who, on the night the child arrived, stood in the doorway with her hand on the doorframe, at which point every animal within a mile of this building went silent at once. My stable-master thought it was an omen. I think it was a symptom."

For the first time, the cat laughed. It was not a comforting sound—it was the sound of a hinge that had not been oiled in a century, opening anyway.

"There it is," she said. "Underneath the ledger-keeper. I wondered when you'd dig down to it."

"Down to what, precisely? Name it. That is, after all, what I pay for."

"Names cost extra."

"Hesper."

"*Guildmaster.*" She drew the word out mockingly, then settled, and when she spoke again her voice had changed—lower, older, stripped of its dry amusement. What remained underneath was something Vetch recognized from auction houses and war memorials: gravity. The tone of things being sold that could not be unsold. "Your Casper Kavanagh is the granddaughter of a Serevan speaker. Her grandmother paid a great deal to hide that line—to hide the blood and the gift both—in a city that forgets on schedule. The gift runs in it regardless. Beast-speech. The oldest form of it. Not the parlour trick of charming dogs; I mean the true craft, the hearing of every voice that was never human and answering it in kind. Her grandmother could do it. Her mother could have done it, had she lived. And Casper has been strangling it in her chest for nine years, because a gift that shows gets owned, and she chose invisibility over ownership. Sound familiar?"

Vetch did not answer. His hands had gone very still on the table.

"It should," Hesper continued softly. "It's your whole creed. Hide what's valuable until the moment of sale. Except she does it out of fear, and you do it out of appetite, and the difference matters to exactly no one once the gavel falls."

* * *

The tea had gone cold. Vetch found he did not remember drinking it.

He made himself perform the arithmetic, because arithmetic was his discipline and discipline was the only altar he kept. A vessel-child, appraised at nine years' profit. A defector clerk with Bureau seals potentially within reach. A choir of an erased nation sleeping under his foundations. And threaded through all of it, hidden in plain sight behind nine years of flawless, forgettable paperwork—a beast-speaker of Serevan descent. The last living practitioner of a craft the Crown had spent two centuries trying to extinguish, currently employed by him at a salary of forty crowns a year plus lodging.

The Pale Assessor had offered a fortune for the girl. The Assessor did not know about the clerk. If the Assessor *had* known—

Vetch closed his eyes and saw, briefly and vividly, the shape of the transaction. It was not a sale anymore. Sales ended; possession compounded. A beast-speaker who could open the door the choir sang behind, who could speak to the vessel-child in the old tongue, who could give the Crown—or anyone—the thing the first Bureau had failed to buy with an entire country's annihilation. That was not inventory. That was not even treasure. That was a key cut to fit every lock in the world, currently wearing a clerk's smock and filing reports in triplicate.

He had built the Vellum Guild on a single principle: impossible things appreciate. Bottled storms. Borrowed years. Names sold at auction. And the rarest commodity of all, the one the founding masters had built their vaults above without understanding what they were standing on, was the ability to speak to what everyone else had silenced.

Casper Kavanagh had worked beneath him for nine years, invisible, and he had walked past her ten thousand times, and the sheer waste of it made his teeth ache.

"You're smiling," Hesper observed. "It's not a pleasant smile."

"I'm pricing something."

"You're pricing a person."

"I price persons daily. It's called payroll." He rose, smoothing his coat. "One final item for the ledger. Why tell me this? You've protected her, by your own account, for years. The offerings, the warnings, the—whatever you are. Why sell me the one fact that puts her in danger?"

Hesper rose also, stretching fore and aft with the boneless indifference of her kind, and for a moment she seemed less like a cat than like something wearing a cat loosely, the way a spy wore a coat.

"Because she's running out of time to be afraid," the cat said. "Fear kept her alive this long, but fear won't open that door, and the door is going to open whether she's ready or not—with her, or without her, or over her body. She needs an enemy sharp enough to cut against. Consider it a favor to her education." At the edge of the shadow beneath the door, she paused. "Oh. And Guildmaster. A word of professional advice, gratis, since you've been such a courteous host."

"Advice is never gratis. What's the invoice?"

"When you move against her—and you will, I can smell the decision forming on you like sweat—do it quickly, and do it completely, and above all do not do it halfway. Half-measures are how the last masters of this building died." Her tail flicked once. "Ask the choir sometime. They remember."

Then she walked through the locked door, and the containment script overhead hummed on, undisturbed, and Oren Vetch stood alone in the holding room with cold tea and the largest number he had ever held in his head.

He rang the bell twice—the signal that cleared his calendar—and began, in a fresh ledger, on a fresh page, to draft the acquisition of Casper Kavanagh.

Below his feet, faint but unmistakable, the choir shifted its song. As though it had heard the page turn.
