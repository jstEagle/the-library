## 16. Sol's Choice

The order came at dusk, folded twice and sealed in grey wax, the way all Bureau orders came—small, unremarkable things that ended lives. Imre Sol read it standing in his rented room above a dyer's shop, the window open to let out the stink of mordant and urine that soaked the street below, and he read it three times before the words stopped rearranging themselves into something he could pretend not to understand.

*Take delivery early. The asset is unstable. Full compensation for expedited handling. Do not wait for verification.*

Early. The Pale Assessor had agreed on a week. Vetch had agreed on a week. Sol had spent four days in this city of chimneys and ledger-houses verifying nothing in particular—counting crates of bottled weather he did not open, interviewing clerks who knew less than they pretended, establishing, in the meticulous language of Bureau procedure, that everything was exactly as it appeared. Four days of building the paper architecture that would make a child's sale legal, clean, and irreversible. And now, on the fifth evening, a courier who smelled of horse sweat had handed him an order that made all of it unnecessary.

*Unstable.* He turned the word over. In Bureau usage, *unstable* meant frightened. It meant the merchandise was crying, or refusing food, or doing something that might be witnessed by someone with a conscience. It meant take it now, before the conscience could organize itself into action.

He should go tonight. That was what the order demanded. Take the child from the Vellum Guild's holding room, bind her per protocol, deliver her to the waystation outside the walls where the covered wagon waited, and file his completion report by morning. A day's work. Less. He had done it eleven times before, and eleven times he had slept soundly afterward, because the Bureau trained its clerks well and Sol had been an excellent student.

He sat down on the narrow bed instead, and the room's single candle threw his shadow long against the plaster, a thin man shaped like a folded document.

It was not conscience. He wanted to be clear about that, wanted it the way a man wants to be clear about the difference between a fever and a chill. Conscience would have arrived weeks ago, in the village, when he burned the memory-records—the parish books, the christening rolls, the letters people write to their dead—and watched a hundred years of ordinary grief go up as smoke while the villagers stood in the road watching their own past become ash. He had felt nothing then. He had noted, with professional satisfaction, how thoroughly the fire took. Whatever was moving in him now had started later, quieter, in a corridor of the Vellum Guild, when he had heard—faintly, impossibly, through two floors of stone and timber—a sound like singing.

No. Not like singing. Like remembering.

He had told himself it was the building settling. Old houses made noises; every clerk knew that guildhalls were built over whatever the city had been too impatient to dig up. But the sound had not stopped when he moved away from it, and it had not been a noise. It had been voices, many voices, layered the way a choir is layered, except no choir Sol had ever heard sang words he could almost understand without knowing the language. For three seconds—three seconds only, before some instinct slammed shut inside him like a ledger closing—he had understood them. They were not singing to God. They were singing to be counted.

The Bureau taught its clerks that such phenomena were contamination. Residual thaumaturgy, improperly contained. The correct response was documentation followed by distance. Sol had documented. He had walked back down the corridor with his boots striking the flagstones in the approved rhythm of a man going about legitimate business, and he had returned to his room, and he had slept badly, and every night since, the hunger had grown.

That was the honest word for it. Hunger. He lay back on the bed and let himself name it, because the Bureau had also taught him that unnamed things festered into mistakes. He was hungry the way a man born deaf is hungry after hearing one bar of music through a wall. Something enormous existed, just below the floorboards of the world, and it had touched him for three seconds, and now the rest of his life was a corridor leading away from a door he could not stop thinking about.

They would put him on Juniper's transport detail. They always did—the verifying clerk rode with the asset, in case questions arose at checkpoints. He would sit across from a mute child in a locked wagon for six days, and she would look at him the way the last one had looked at him, and he would hand her over to the Assessor's people at the border, and he would receive his bonus and his commendation, and somewhere beneath a guildhall in a city full of chimneys, the singing would continue without him.

Unless.

He got up. He dressed with the care he brought to all official actions—dark coat, plain boots, the Bureau's travelling seal in his left pocket and the falsification kit in his right. He was, he reflected as he checked the wax blanks and the matrix stamps, about to commit treason. There was no softer word. Falsifying a completion report was hanging offense; the Bureau did not prosecute it, it simply erased the offender, which was faster and tidier and left no family to make noise. He knew the procedure intimately. He had drafted the procedure.

And yet his hands were steady. That interested him. In eleven years of service his hands had never once shaken—not at the burnings, not at the deliveries—and they did not shake now, and he understood that this was itself information. A man's body knew things his mind refused to file. His body had been waiting for this order the way a prisoner waits for a door left unlocked.

* * *

The Vellum Guild at night was a different animal than by day. By day it clattered with commerce: porters shouting, ledgers slamming, the caged moths rustling in their tower with the restless sound of money wanting to move. By night it was all held breath. Sol entered through the trade gate, where the night porter recognized the Bureau grey of his coat and did not ask for papers—no one ever asked the Bureau for papers; asking was how buildings burned—and crossed the counting hall with the unhurried stride of a man whose business required no explanation.

He told himself he was conducting a final inspection before removal. It was even partly true. But his feet carried him past the holding room, past the stair to the moth-tower, down the service corridor behind the ledger hall where he had first heard it, and there he stopped, one hand against the cold stone, and listened.

Nothing. Silence so complete it had texture, like felt.

Then, faintly—from deeper, lower, below the foundations—something stirred. Not song yet. A murmur. The sound a congregation makes before the liturgy begins, hundreds of throats clearing in the dark, waiting for the one voice that will start them off.

Sol pressed his palm flat to the stone. He was aware of himself with terrible clarity: a functionary in a borrowed coat, kneeling in a servant's corridor at midnight, listening at the floor of the world like a beggar at a kitchen door. Eleven years of Bureau discipline said *stand up, walk away, file your report.* The hunger said nothing at all. It simply pulled, patient and vast, the way gravity pulls, and Sol found that he had already knelt fully, both knees on the cold flags, his forehead nearly touching the stone, in a posture that had no name in any Bureau manual but had an ancient one everywhere else.

*Please,* he thought, and was astonished to find the word in him. He had not prayed since boyhood. *I don't want to own you. I only want to hear.*

The murmuring stopped.

The silence that followed was worse—or better; he could no longer sort the two—and in it he heard footsteps above, quick and light, descending. Someone else was awake in this house. Someone else had heard what he had done, or failed to do, in the dark.

He rose, smoothed his coat, and composed his face into the neutral mask the Bureau issued along with the seals. The footsteps reached the bottom of the stair and paused, and a woman came into the corridor's dim lantern-light—thin, grey-eyed, with the ink-stained fingers of a senior scribe and the wary stillness of prey animals and guilty people. He had seen her before, hovering near the holding room, catalogued her in his report as *Kavanagh, C., senior auditor; low threat; watches the asset excessively.*

She looked at his knees. At the dust on them. Then at his face, and whatever she saw there made her go very still.

"You were listening," she said. Not a question. Her voice was low and roughened, a voice used to staying under doors.

"I was conducting an inspection."

"No," she said. "You were kneeling."

Sol considered lying. He was good at lying; it was most of his trade. But the choir had heard him kneel, and this woman had seen it, and suddenly the arithmetic of the night changed—because if the dead below had heard his *please*, then perhaps they had answered it, and perhaps they had sent him this grey-eyed auditor with her ink-stained hands, or perhaps they had simply arranged for the two most dishonest creatures in the building to find each other in a corridor at midnight, which amounted to the same thing.

"The order came tonight," he said. "Expedited removal. I'm to take the girl tomorrow."

Her face did something small and catastrophic and then mastered itself. "When?"

"Dawn, per the original schedule. Tonight, per the new one." He watched her calculate, watched her begin to plan around him, around the locks, around the enforcers he had not mentioned. "Before you decide how to kill me," he continued, "you should know that I'm not going to do it."

"Going to do what?"

"Deliver her." He drew the order from his coat and held it out—not handing it to her, merely showing it, the way one shows a wound. "I'm going to file a completion report stating the asset was removed on schedule and transferred to the border waystation. Sealed, verified, countersigned. It will buy—" he calculated— "six days. Perhaps seven, before anyone checks the waystation's inbound log against mine."

Casper Kavanagh stared at him the way one stares at a dog that has begun reciting poetry. "Why."

Not *why would you risk that*. Just *why*, stripped bare, and Sol respected the economy of it. He gave her the true answer, because anything less would insult them both.

"Because I heard them," he said. "Under the floor. Three seconds. Three days ago, and I have thought of nothing else since. Your Bureau-trained clerk, mistress auditor—I've burned villages' worth of memory and felt nothing, and then a country that doesn't exist sang three bars through a floor, and now I can't—" His voice did something he did not authorize. He stopped, restarted it. "I am going to give you seals, routes, checkpoint passwords, the waystation's rotation schedule, and my personal verification matrix, which will pass any inquiry for a week. And in exchange, I want one thing."

She had gone very still again, the stillness of a person recalculating everything. "What thing."

"When you open whatever door this is—" his hand moved toward the floor, toward the murmuring dark, and he saw her eyes flick to it and back, confirming what he'd suspected for three days, that she knew exactly what lay beneath the ledger hall— "when the singing starts, I want to be there. Not as a captor. Not as a buyer. As a listener. I want them to count me, Casper Kavanagh. I've spent eleven years making sure no one was ever counted who didn't wish it, and I have never once—" he stopped again. Finished differently. "I was born in a town whose records were burned before I could walk. I don't know my grandmother's name. The Bureau keeps a list of everything it has erased, and my birthplace is on it, filed between a river and a rebellion. I have read my own absence in the archives. So yes. I'll commit treason. Not for the child, though she deserves it. Not for you, though you may deserve it more than you know. For the chance to stand in front of a door out of being forgotten, and hear what's on the other side say my name."

The corridor was silent a long moment. Somewhere far above, a moth beat against its cage—a small, frantic percussion, like a heart in a box.

Casper studied him with the flat, total attention of someone auditing a ledger line that might contain either salvation or fraud. He let her look. He had nothing left to hide that mattered; he had laid the whole fraudulent account on the stones between them.

"You understand," she said finally, "that if this is a trap, I will feed you to the things under the floor myself."

"Yes."

"And that helping us makes you nothing. No pardon, no restoration. You'll be erased right alongside the rest of us."

A strange lightness went through him at the word *us*—at being, provisionally and conditionally, an *us*. "I know," he said. "I've read the forms."

She almost smiled. It was a terrible, tired almost-smile, the kind that comes from people carrying more weight than their spines were built for, but it was real. "Dawn," she said. "There's a postern stair behind the tannery vat. If you're going to falsify a federal document in my favor, clerk, you'd best do it properly. And Sol—" She paused at the stair's foot, half-turned, the lantern catching the grey of her eyes. "The things under this floor? They heard you kneel. They heard what you asked. I don't know what that means yet. But nothing down there forgets a voice."

She climbed the stairs and was gone, her footsteps fading into the sleeping house.

Sol stood alone in the corridor, the false order warm in his pocket, treason settled into his bones like a second skeleton. Below him, soft as breath, faint as memory, the choir began to sing—and this time, when the understanding came, it did not stop at three seconds.

They were singing a route. They were singing it to him.

He took out his notebook and, by the light of the dying lantern, began to write it down.
