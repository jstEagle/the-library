## 18. Every Word a Wound

The door beneath the Ledger Hall was smaller than Casper had imagined. Nine years of secrets, two centuries of erasure, an entire country unmade—and at the bottom of it all, a door no taller than a child, set into the stone like something grown rather than built, its surface carved with symbols that Juniper had been drawing on scraps of paper for weeks.

It was breathing.

Casper could see it now that she knew how to look—the stone rising and falling in a rhythm too slow for lungs, too patient for panic. The choir sang through it, up through the soles of her boots, and this close she could almost make out words. Almost. The last of Hesper's lessons sat in her like swallowed coals: *the door does not open for power. It opens for payment.*

Behind her, Juniper made a small sound—not a word, never a word, but a note of warning—and Casper turned in time to see the lantern-light of the stairwell break apart into shadows with boots.

Vetch came first. Of course he did. He descended the way he did everything: unhurried, certain of his ownership of every room he entered, his ledger-hand tucked behind his back like a man strolling his own gardens. Four enforcers followed in Bureau grey, their faces smooth as unsold paper, and between two of them, bound in a net of silvered cord that smoked faintly where it touched her fur, hung Hesper.

They had shaved her, Casper realized. Stripped the glamour off her like bark off a living tree. What remained was not a cat. It was something old and lean and wrong-shaped in the lantern light, all shadow and joint, with eyes like coins left on a dead man's tongue.

"Master Kavanagh." Vetch's voice carried the mild disappointment of a man finding a ledger error. "You've cost me a great deal this week."

"You'll survive it," Casper said. Her voice sounded steadier than she felt. Her hands were shaking. She put them behind her back where he couldn't see, and felt Juniper's small fingers hook into her sleeve. "You always do."

"I intend to." He glanced past her at the door, and for one unguarded moment Casper saw what lived under the prudence—hunger, vast and patient as a debt accruing interest. "Two hundred years the Bureau has looked for this. Two hundred years of buying up fragments, burning records, paying fortunes for a single verified syllable. And it's been under my floor the entire time." He smiled. It did not reach anything above his mouth. "Open it, Kavanagh. You've been practicing. I can hear it in you—you speak now. Badly, but you speak."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then Imre Sol will do what he was trained to do." Vetch nodded toward the greyest of the enforcers, and Casper's stomach dropped, because the man who stepped forward was not Sol. Sol stood at the rear, half in shadow, and he was looking at her—not with the blank functionary's stare she'd braced for, but with something urgent, something almost pleading, his hand resting near the seal-case at his belt.

*Trust me,* he'd written on the scrap he'd pressed into her palm an hour ago. *When it goes wrong—and it will go wrong—look for my hands.*

The false enforcer raised a rod of black glass, and the choir screamed.

Not the singing anymore—a shriek that came up through the floor and out of the walls, out of the stones themselves, every voice of Serevan crying out at once as the rod drank the sound. The candles guttered blue. Juniper collapsed against Casper's leg, hands clamped over her ears, mouth open in a soundless wail, and Casper understood with terrible clarity: they weren't going to wait for her to open the door. They meant to crack it like an egg.

She had perhaps a minute before there was nothing left to open.

So she stopped being invisible.

"Hesper," she said, in Serevan, and paid for it—one whole afternoon vanished from her memory, an afternoon of rain and her grandmother's kitchen, gone like a coin dropped down a well—but the old creature's head snapped up, coin-eyes finding her through the smoking net.

"*Avel seren,*" Casper said—*hold the line*—and gave away her tenth birthday, cake and candles and her mother's laugh, and the net's silvered cord went slack as if the words themselves had untied it.

Hesper came off the ground like a thrown knife.

What happened next happened fast, and Casper only ever held pieces of it. The false enforcer with the rod turned; Hesper hit him at the throat and the rod shattered and the choir's scream bent back into song. Another enforcer drew steel; Sol stepped out of the shadows at last, flipped open his seal-case, and pressed a Bureau writ against the man's chest, speaking the cold formula of revocation—"asset seized, authority transferred"—and the enforcer simply stopped, mid-swing, frozen like a figure in a paused ledger, because Bureau tools obeyed Bureau seals and Sol's seals were real.

"Vetch!" Sol's voice cracked across the hall. "By writ of the Bureau of Erasure, your acquisition is hereby reclassified—"

But Vetch wasn't listening. Vetch had seen his fortune cracking and done the arithmetic, and the answer he reached was Juniper.

He moved faster than a man his age should have, ledger-hand coming out from behind his back holding a knife of pale glass—Bureau-issue, a name-eater's tooth, the same kind they'd used in the appraisal—and he crossed the floor toward the girl in four strides while Casper was still turning, still reaching, still a half-second too slow, nine years of invisibility dying hard in her legs—

Juniper turned to face him.

And spoke.

One word. Her first in months. Not Serevan—something older, the language underneath the language, the one the moths had whispered about, the one things alive before kingdoms remembered—and the word was not loud but it filled the hall the way water fills a hull. Vetch stopped as if he'd walked into glass. The knife trembled in his grip. Every candle in the Ledger Hall stood up straight and burned white.

Casper didn't understand the word. But she understood what it cost, because she'd been paying that price all week: she watched it leave the girl like blood leaving a wound. Juniper swayed. And Casper understood something else, something that arrived whole and terrible: the door was listening to *her*, yes, but it would open fastest, cleanest, cheapest—for the last mouth. For the vessel. That was what Vetch wanted. That was what the exiled dead wanted. Everyone wanted Juniper to be the door.

No.

Casper stepped between them.

"Listen to me," she said, and this time she spoke Serevan deliberately, spending herself like a woman emptying a strongbox into a fire. "*Seren vash kaelin.*" *I am the speaker here.* A memory went—her father's hands teaching hers the ledger-script, gone, just gone—and she kept talking. "*Oren Vetch, tavar sel—*" *you own nothing here*—and the summer she was fourteen tore away, some festival, someone's face she'd loved, she'd never know which. "*Kaelin seren. Kaelin seren. AVEL.*"

*I pay. I pay. OPEN.*

The door began to breathe faster.

It was the strangest agony Casper had ever known—no pain at all, that was the horror of it, just subtraction. Each sentence lifted something out of her gently, the way a pickpocket works, and she felt herself becoming lighter and more hollow and more desperate, grasping backward at memories even as she fed them away: *keep the ones of Juniper, keep those, spend anything else—* the smell of the guild's ink cellar, gone; her first day at the Vellum Guild, gone; the name of the woman who had taught her to whistle, gone, gone, gone. She was buying a door with herself and the price was climbing and she could not stop, because behind her Vetch had recovered, because Hesper was pinned under two enforcers and bleeding shadow instead of blood, because Sol was holding three men frozen with seals that were burning out one by one, because Juniper was on her knees with her hands pressed flat to the floor as if holding the world together.

"*AVEL SEREN!*" Casper screamed, and gave away the last thing she had that was large enough: her mother's face.

Not the memory of her mother—she'd spent fragments of that already—but the face itself, the image she'd carried since childhood, the thing she saw when she closed her eyes. It left her completely, cleanly, and she felt its absence the way an amputee feels weather. She knew she had loved that face. She knew it had been beautiful. She knew it the way you know a fact in a ledger, and the knowing was unbearable, and she opened her mouth to spend whatever came next anyway—

The door opened.

Not swung. *Opened*—the symbols sliding apart like a lock learning itself, the stone folding inward, and through it came not light exactly but *attention*, a vast and gentle regard pouring through the gap, and the choir rose to a pitch that was no longer grief. It was recognition. It was a country remembering it had a door.

"No—" Vetch lunged, not at Casper now, past her, knife outstretched toward the opening, toward the light, toward ownership—"it's MINE, I found it, I OWN it—"

His hand crossed the threshold.

Casper would remember this part forever, because it was the one thing the door gave back to her free of charge: Oren Vetch, master of the Vellum Guild, touching the remnant of Serevan—and Serevan, which had been erased for refusing to be owned, taking hold of the thing that tried to own it. He did not scream. That was the worst of it. His mouth opened and nothing came out, because the door took his voice first—it was, after all, the only thing he'd ever offered—and then it took the rest, slowly, thoroughly, the way he had appraised everything else in his life. He came apart like a ledger in water. Ink first, then pages, then the binding.

Hesper limped out from under the fallen enforcers, dragging one foreleg, and planted herself between the door and Juniper, hackles up, shadow-smoke rising off her back. "Hold," she rasped, in plain trade-tongue, to the light, to the choir, to everyone. "The girl chooses. Nobody else. *Nobody.*"

Sol's last seal flared and died and the final enforcer dropped, unconscious or emptied, Casper never learned which. The hall went quiet except for the breathing of the door and the singing behind it, patient now, waiting.

Waiting for a mouth.

Juniper got up off the floor. She looked at the door for a long time. Then she looked at Casper—at the hollowed-out stranger who had bought this moment with her mother's face—and reached up, and took her hand.

Together, they stepped toward the light.

And the choir, seeing them come, changed its song to something Casper had never heard it sing before: a single held note, high and clear and full of longing, like a country inhaling—about to speak.
