## 21. Exile, Chosen

They left the city before the sun did.

Casper had always imagined exile as a door slamming—some grand, terrible finality, guards at the gate, her name struck from the Vellum Guild's rolls with the same ink they used for debts. What it actually was: a milk-cart's worth of fog on the Salt Road, Hesper riding in the basket of a borrowed handcart like an old woman going to market, Juniper asleep against Casper's shoulder with her cheek pressed to the wool of a coat that still smelled faintly of the guild's ledger-hall, of dust and tallow and nine years of hiding.

The city let them go without ceremony. That was its way. It had never once looked at Casper Kavanagh when she wanted to be unseen, and now that she wanted—desperately, foolishly—to be remembered by something other than fear, it looked away again, politely, as if embarrassed by what had happened beneath its streets. Only the crows watched from the eaves of the Tannery Quarter, and only because Hesper asked them to. *Watch the road behind us,* she'd told them in that voice that was not a cat's voice at all. *And tell me if anything follows that has two legs and no shadow.*

Nothing followed. Not yet.

Casper walked with the new ledger under her arm. It was a plain thing—good paper, unruled, bound in undyed calfskin—and she had bought it from a stationer who didn't know her face, paying with coins she'd kept sewn into her coat lining for nine years against exactly this morning, though she had never once allowed herself to believe the morning would come. The ledger was blank. Every page of it. And every page of it terrified her more than the Bureau's enforcers ever had, because a blank ledger was a promise, and Casper had spent her whole life learning that promises were the most expensive things a person could carry.

She had owned ledgers before. She had filled them with other people's impossible things: bottled storms counted and recounted, borrowed years logged against their interest, names sold at auction entered in a fair hand so that no one could later claim the Guild had cheated them. She had been good at it. She had been *invisible* at it, which was better. But those ledgers had all been the same book wearing different covers—the book of things held, things owned, things kept quiet.

This one would be different. This one she would fill with words that cost.

* * *

They stopped at midmorning where the Salt Road crossed a stream gone thin with autumn, and Casper sat on the flat stone beside the water and opened the ledger to its first page and could not make herself write anything.

Juniper stood knee-deep in the shallows, boots off, trousers rolled, watching the minnows with the absolute gravity she brought to everything. She had not spoken since the door. Casper understood why—or thought she did. The girl had been the last mouth of Serevan, the vessel the dead had wanted and the living had tried to own, and she had given all of it away—every word of the choir, poured back out through the open door until the country's remnant dissolved into light. What was left in a mouth after it has given away its whole language? Casper had emptied herself of memories to speak the door open, and she woke some mornings reaching for her mother's face and finding only the shape of the absence, smooth as a river stone. Juniper had emptied herself of *everything*.

But the girl wasn't empty. Casper had learned that much already, in three days of walking. Juniper still laughed—soundlessly, shoulders shaking, mouth wide. Still scowled at bad road and worse cooking. Still drew, endlessly, in the margins of the one book they'd let her keep: doors, mostly, but lately doors standing open, with small figures walking through.

Hesper leapt from the handcart and came to sit on the flat stone beside Casper, tail curled over her blind white eyes, facing the water as though she too were considering the difficulty of first pages.

"You're staring at it like it owes you money," the cat said.

"Everything I've ever written down owed someone something," Casper said. "That's rather the point of ledgers."

"Mm." Hesper began washing one paw, unhurried. "Your grandmother had the same trouble."

Casper went still. "You said you barely knew her."

"I said I knew her *before*," Hesper corrected. "Before she paid to hide your line. There was a time when Maren Galloway wrote in a book just like that one. Words on one side, and on the other side, what each word cost her." The paw paused. "It's how we did it, in the old days. Before the Bureau made speaking a crime and everyone learned to swallow their own tongues. A speaker keeps accounts. Not of what she owns. Of what she's given."

Casper looked down at the blank page. The paper was so white it seemed to hold light, the way snow holds light, the way the door had held light when it finally swung inward and the choir came through it singing.

"She gave up her whole country," Casper said quietly. "To hide us. That's what you told me. She spoke a binding over our bloodline so the gift would sleep, so the Bureau's readers couldn't find us—and the price was that none of us would ever hear it, or speak it, or know it existed."

"And now the binding's broken," Hesper said. "Broken by you, spending yourself down to the memory of your mother's face to command a door. So here you are, first free speaker of Serevan in two hundred years, and you can't write a word in your own book." The cat's whiskers twitched—not quite a smile, but near kin to one. "Good. Means you understand the price. Your grandmother worried, at the end, that she'd hidden the language so well her line would never grieve it. Never even know there was something to grieve. Grief is tuition, Casper. You're paying it. That's how I know you'll learn."

Juniper came up out of the stream dripping, shook her feet dry one at a time with tremendous dignity, and climbed onto the stone beside them. She looked at the ledger. Then at Casper. Then she reached out and turned the book toward herself, took the pencil Casper carried behind her ear, and drew.

Not a door this time. She drew a word—one of the old symbols, the flowing hooked script that no scribe in the city had recognized, that Casper had once matched entry-for-entry against a forbidden ledger in the archive while her hands shook. *Serevan script*, in Juniper's quick sure strokes, drawn large across the first page like a title.

Then, beneath it, she drew a smaller figure—a woman, stick-thin, hair in a knot—standing with her mouth open. And from the woman's mouth she drew lines radiating outward, and at the end of each line, a tiny symbol. A word, being spoken. Being *given*.

Casper's chest ached. "I don't know what it says," she whispered. "Juniper, I'm sorry—I knew one sentence, that night, and I paid for it with—" She touched her temple, where the absence lived. "I can't read it."

Juniper tapped the symbol at the end of the first line. Then she pointed at Casper. Then she pointed at the ledger.

*Say it*, the gesture said. *Into the book.*

* * *

Hesper talked her through it, there on the stream-stone, the way she must have talked students through it two hundred years ago, when she had been something other than a cellar-cat—if she had ever been something else; Casper had stopped asking, because the answers rearranged the world.

"Start small," the cat said. "A word with no teeth in it. Not a name—names are expensive. Not a memory-word; you haven't got many left to spend. Start with something true and cheap." Her blind eyes fixed on Casper with uncanny accuracy. "What do you see?"

"Water," Casper said.

"In the trade-tongue. Now find it underneath. You heard the choir—you know the sound of it, even if you couldn't parse it. Reach for the shape of *water* the way Serevan shaped it. Don't force it. Listen for it. Your blood knows it. Your grandmother only put it to sleep, and sleep ends."

Casper closed her eyes.

It was strange, how the listening worked. Not like remembering—there was nothing there to remember, no lesson, no grandmother's voice teaching letters by a fire. It was like hearing a song from another room, muffled by walls, and leaning toward it. She leaned. Down past the trade-tongue, past the Guild's counting-cant she'd used for nine years, past even the beast-speech that came to her unbidden, the small voices of moths and cats and crows. Deeper. To a place that felt less like a room in her mind than a foundation under all of them.

And there—faint, worn smooth as the river stone where her mother's face should have been—was a sound.

She spoke it aloud.

The word was short. Three sounds, flowing one into the next the way water flows, and the moment it left her mouth she felt the cost arrive, quiet and precise, like a coin lifted from a purse. Not a memory this time—she'd braced for that—but something smaller, subtler: the exact temperature of the bathwater in the house where she'd grown up. Gone. She tested for it and found only the fact that it had existed, a smooth place on the wall of her childhood.

Her eyes stung anyway. "That's—it works. It *costs*. One word and I've lost—" She laughed, wetly. "The warmth of my own bathwater. Two hundred years of a country, and my inheritance is paying a penny at a time."

"Yes," Hesper said simply. "That's how it begins. Small coins. You'll pay larger ones when the words are larger. And someday, if you go far enough, you'll stand at a word and know what it costs and choose whether to say it—and *that*, child, is the whole of freedom. The Bureau never understood that. They thought ownership was the opposite of loss. But a thing freely given isn't lost. It's *placed*." She nodded at the ledger. "Write it down. Both sides. The word, and the price."

Casper wrote. Left column: the symbol, copied from Juniper's drawing, awkward in her merchant's hand but recognizable. Right column: *the warm water of home.* She stared at the entry for a long moment. In nine years at the Vellum Guild she had recorded ten thousand transactions, and not one of them had ever been honest like this—value stated plainly, cost stated plainly, no margin, no concealment.

"It's the first honest ledger I've ever kept," she said.

"Then you were in the wrong trade for nine years," Hesper said, "and right on time now."

Juniper, watching, did her soundless laugh—shoulders shaking—and then did something that stopped Casper's breath. She took the pencil back, and beneath Casper's entry, she wrote the same symbol again, smaller, neater. And beside it, in the right-hand column, she drew a small circle with rays around it.

Casper frowned. "What does that mean? What did it cost *her*?"

Hesper was quiet a moment. When she answered, her voice had gone soft in the way it did perhaps twice a year. "Nothing. It cost her nothing. She's not spending memories, Casper. She *is* the language. It lives in her the way blood lives in a body—she can't run out. The Bureau wanted her because of it. The dead wanted her because of it. And she gave it all away anyway, every word of the choir, freely, to a country that needed it more than she did." The cat looked at the girl, who was already drawing again, absorbed, unbothered, sunlight in her wet hair. "Which is why she'll never be a vessel again. Vessels are taken. She's a wellspring. Those can only give."

Juniper looked up then, caught them both looking at her, and rolled her eyes with the exquisite theatricality of an eight-year-old, and went back to her drawing. But before she bent her head, she tapped the ledger once—tapped the space after Casper's single entry, the vast whiteness of the remaining pages—and then tapped Casper's sternum, directly over her heart.

*Fill it. Fill yourself. We have time.*

* * *

They walked through the afternoon, and the land changed around them the way land does when you leave a city's gravity—hedgerows giving way to gorse, gorse to open heath going bronze and purple, the road narrowing until it was more suggestion than promise. Somewhere behind them, the city smoked gently against the sky. Casper did not look back. Looking back was a kind of spending, too, and she was learning to count.

In the evening they made camp in the lee of a ruined shepherd's wall, and Casper built a small fire while Hesper conducted negotiations with a nearby fox regarding territorial boundaries—the fox, upon hearing whatever Hesper actually said, departed with unusual alacrity—and Juniper lay on her stomach drawing doors in the dirt.

Casper opened the ledger again. Second page. She listened down through herself, found the word for *fire*—it cost her the smell of rain on her grandmother's street, a loss so specific and so strange that she sat with it a while, grieving a scent she hadn't known she'd kept—and wrote both columns. Then the word for *road*. Then *bread*. Then, harder, *night*, which took the sound of her mother's voice saying goodnight, and she paid it with her jaw tight and her eyes dry and Hesper pretending very hard to be asleep.

By firelight, the entries accumulated. A dozen words. Twenty. Each one a small amputation, each one written down honestly, and somewhere around the thirtieth Casper noticed that the grief had changed texture. It was still grief. But underneath it, unmistakably, was something else—the feeling she'd had only once before, touching the doorframe while every animal within a mile went still: the feeling of *answering*. Of a long silence ending. Her grandmother had hidden this language to save her line, and the hiding had worked, and the hiding had also been a wound passed down unbeknownst, and now Casper was unpicking it stitch by stitch, paying for thread.

"Some things are worth the price," she said aloud, to no one, testing the sentence in the trade-tongue because she wasn't ready to say such a thing in Serevan yet. But she wrote it in the ledger anyway, in the margin, in her own tongue, next to the word for *night*: a note to whoever might read this book someday. A note, maybe, to herself.

Juniper crawled over and read the margin. She couldn't read trade-script—Casper would teach her; there was time—but she seemed to understand anyway, the way she understood everything, and she leaned her small weight against Casper's side, and Casper put an arm around her, and the fire cracked, and above them the stars came out in numbers no city sky permitted.

* * *

It was deep in the dark, when the fire had burned to amber and Juniper slept against her ribs, that Hesper spoke without opening her eyes.

"The crows came back."

Casper's whole body went cold. "The Bureau—"

"No. Something else." The cat sat up, and in the dying light her white fur seemed almost luminous, and for a moment—just a moment—her outline flickered, the way the choir had flickered, the way light through a door flickers. "Two hundred years I've waited at that ruin of a capital, keeping what was left to keep. My work there is done. But I told you once that Serevan was not only a country. It was a *chorus*. And choruses, child, do not simply dissolve. They disperse." She turned her blind face eastward, toward the heath, toward the far hills where no maps ran anymore. "The crows say there are others on the roads. Speakers who hid as your grandmother hid. Scattered lines, sleeping blood, waking up—all at once, all over, since the night your door opened. They heard it, Casper. Whatever the choir became when it dissolved into light, it *traveled*. And it's calling its children home."

Casper's heart was pounding. "Home? Serevan is gone. We opened the door so it could *leave*—"

"Serevan the country is gone," Hesper said. "Serevan the chorus is just beginning. Somewhere out there is a road beyond the maps, and at the end of it, people who will want to meet the woman who opened the door—and the little girl who gave them back their voices." The cat rose, stretched, and settled again between Juniper and the fire, guardian-shaped. "Sleep. We walk east tomorrow. And bring your ledger."

Casper lay back against the shepherd's wall, the blank-and-not-blank book under her arm, the child breathing against her side, the stars wheeling slowly over a world remade. She was afraid, of course. She would always be afraid; fear was the tax on having something to lose, and she had more to lose now than she'd had in nine careful invisible years.

But underneath the fear, steady as a foundation, was the other thing. The answering thing.

Tomorrow she would learn the word for *east*. She wondered what it would cost.

She suspected she would pay it gladly.
