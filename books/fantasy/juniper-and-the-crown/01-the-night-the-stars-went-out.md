## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The wards had a sound, if you knew how to listen. Not a sound, exactly — more a pressure behind the ears, like standing at the edge of deep water. Elodie Quintana had spent eleven years on border watch at Hollowmere's rim, and she knew that pressure the way she knew her own heartbeat: constant, patient, older than grief.

So when it stopped, just past midnight, she felt it the way a man feels his own heart stutter.

She was crouched atop the watchstone at Raven's Tooth, the highest of the valley's sentinel rocks, with her cloak pulled tight against the autumn wind and a cup of cold chicory forgotten at her knee. Below her, Hollowmere lay in its bowl of dark pines — lamplit windows, woodsmoke rising straight as plumb lines, the millpond holding a perfect mirror of the sky.

Except the sky was wrong.

Elodie looked up and her breath went out of her in a thin white ribbon. The stars were going out. Not clouded over — there wasn't a wisp of cloud from rim to rim — but dimming, one by one, like candles pinched by an unseen hand. The Shepherd's Chain first, then the pale scatter above the northern peaks, then all the rest, until the whole black vault hung blank and starless over the valley, and the millpond below reflected nothing at all.

"No," she whispered, though there was no one to hear. "No, no—"

The god did not answer. It never answered. Eleven years ago it had gone silent mid-prayer, mid-*word*, leaving only a hollowness where its presence used to live — a silence so complete that half the valley's faithful had convinced themselves it had never been there at all. Elodie had never believed that. You didn't forget a voice like that. You didn't forget what it felt like to be *known*.

But now, as the last star died, something stirred in that hollow. Faint. Distant. Like a knock through a wall.

Elodie pressed her palm flat against the watchstone and felt it tremble under her hand — not earthquake-tremble but something finer, a shiver running up through the mountain's bones. The wards. She could feel them dying the way you feel warmth leave a room: slowly, then all at once, then gone.

For eleven years the wards had kept the world out. Now they were keeping nothing in.

* * *

By dawn the refugees came.

They came down the old pilgrim road in ones and twos at first, then in dozens, then in a ragged river of them — farmers with their livestock on rope leads, weavers with loom-weights strung around their necks like amulets, a whole family from the free city of Carrowmere carrying nothing but a grandmother in a wheelbarrow and a cage of singing finches they refused to abandon. They poured through the gap where the wards had stood for three hundred years, blinking in the grey light, staring up at the starless sky with the expressions of people who had walked out of one nightmare and into another.

Elodie stood at the valley gate with the other wardens, directing traffic she had no authority to direct, answering questions she had no answers for.

"The king," said a tanner from Marrow's Ford, his hands still black with dye. "King Aldric's army. Ten thousand strong, marching on the free cities. They say he burned Thornhollow rather than parley."

"Aldric was a good king," said an old woman, clutching a prayer-knot worn smooth as river glass. "I saw him crowned when I was a girl. He wept at the altar. That is not Aldric marching."

"Then who?" the tanner demanded. "Who wears his face?"

No one answered him. Elodie found she couldn't. Her eyes kept drifting south, past the refugee road, past the blighted midlands she'd only ever seen in maps, toward a capital she had never visited and a throne she had no reason to fear — and yet her chest felt tight as a fist, as though something in her blood already knew the way.

"You," said the old woman suddenly, catching Elodie's wrist. Her grip was bird-boned and shockingly strong. Her milky eyes went over Elodie's face, slow and searching. "You have the look of the old blood. Valley-born?"

"All my life."

The woman smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. "Then you'll want to remember this day. The day the stars went out." She released her and shuffled on into the valley, and Elodie stood in the gateway feeling as though a door had opened inside her that she very much wanted closed.

* * *

It was near dusk when the stranger fell at her feet.

She had taken the last watch again — the others said she was mad for it, two watches in one day after a night like the last, but sleep wouldn't come and the silence in her chest had grown loud enough to drown out thought. She was walking the wall-road above the eastern fields when she heard the hoofbeats: one horse, lathered, stumbling. A rider slumped forward like a sack of grain.

He made it thirty paces past the gate before the horse went down beneath him, screaming, and the rider rolled clear with the practiced grace of a man who had learned falling the hard way. He tried to stand. Failed. Tried again, got one knee under himself, and looked up at Elodie with one eye — the other was sealed shut with dried blood — and said, in a voice cracked down to the timber:

"Hollowmere. Thank every god that's left. I have to—" He coughed, wet and deep. "I have to warn—"

Then his arm gave out and he collapsed face-first in the mud at her feet.

Elodie was on her knees beside him before she'd decided to move. He was big — a head taller than her even folded up, shoulders like a barn door, the kind of man built for holding a line. His armor told its own story: good steel, once. Now scorched, gashed, one pauldron hanging by a strap, and across the breastplate a sigil half-melted beyond recognition. A knight, then. A knight of somewhere that no longer existed.

His right hand was clenched so hard around something that his knuckles had gone white as bone.

"Let go," she said, prying at his fingers. "Whatever it is, let go, I need to see the wound—"

He fought her, half-conscious, muttering — "*mustn't drop it, mustn't let them take it back*" — until finally his fingers loosened and the object tumbled into her palm.

It was a shard of gold. No bigger than a thumbnail, jagged as a broken tooth, and it was warm. Warm as a living thing, warm as a held hand, and it was *singing* — not aloud, not in any register her ears could find, but in that hollow place behind her sternum where the god's voice used to live. The silence of eleven years split open like ice on a spring river.

And the god spoke.

Not words. Not yet. Just a pressure, a presence, a vast attention turning toward her like a lighthouse beam swinging round — and underneath it, faint and fractured, something that might have been a scream, or might have been a name, or might have been both.

Elodie cried out and nearly dropped the shard. The wounded knight grabbed her wrist with sudden terrible strength, his one eye blazing open, fixed on her face with an expression she would remember for the rest of her life: recognition. Recognition and terror in equal measure.

"*You heard it,*" he rasped. "God's mercy. You *heard* it."

"What is this?" Her voice shook. "What is this thing?"

"The crown," he said. "The king's crown. This is a piece of it." He laughed, and it was the worst sound she'd ever heard, wet and broken and hopeless. "They told us it was a coronation. We rode to protect the coronation. And I watched that crown sink into his head like roots into soil, and I watched our good king's eyes go out, and I ran. First thing I've ever run from. Ser Brannoc of the Vale Guard, and I ran."

He sagged, consciousness going out of him like a tide. Elodie caught his weight against her shoulder — he weighed as much as a yearling ox — and shouted for help down the wall-road, and no one came fast enough, and all the while the shard lay in the mud between them, glowing faintly gold, humming its broken hymn.

Inside her chest, the hollow place was no longer hollow. Something was pressing against the walls of it, testing them, patient and immense and *awake*. It had been silent for eleven years. It had been silent because it had been taken. Because someone had put a god in a crown and a crown on a king's head and pointed that king at the world.

And some buried, unlooked-for part of Elodie — the part that had always known the valley was a cage dressed as a sanctuary, the part that had flinched at the old woman's words, the part that had felt the pull south since dawn — understood, with sickening clarity, that the god was not calling out to the valley.

It was calling out to *her*.

* * *

They laid Brannoc in the temple's side chamber, under the faded mosaic of the god's face that no one had repointed in a decade. The healer bound his ribs, sewed the gash above his eye, and pronounced that he would live, if fever didn't take him first. Through all of it his fist stayed curled, empty now, around the shape of something no longer there.

Elodie sat by the window with the shard wrapped in three layers of wool in her lap, watching the starless dark press against the glass. Behind her, the elders argued in low voices — seal the valley, bar the gates, pray the war passes them by. The same voices that had prayed for eleven years to a god who never answered.

The shard pulsed against her palms. Once. Twice. Patient as a heartbeat.

*You cannot stay,* it did not say, in the language of things that do not say. *You were never meant to stay.*

Elodie looked at the sleeping knight, at the melted sigil on his ruined armor, at the mosaic of a face she had loved and lost before she was old enough to mourn it properly. Home was behind these walls. It had always been behind these walls. But home had gone quiet the night the stars went out, and she was beginning to understand that quiet was not peace — quiet was a held breath.

She wrapped the shard tighter, shouldered her pack, and went to tell the elders she was leaving at first light.

The god, in the hollow of her chest, exhaled.
