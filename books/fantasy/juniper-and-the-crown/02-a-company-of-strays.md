## 2. A Company of Strays

The midlands had been dying for a long time, but it took leaving Hollowmere to see how much.

Elodie had grown up believing the world beyond the wards was like the valley — green, loud with birdsong, generous. What lay before her instead was a country the color of old ash. Fields stretched to a horizon that never quite resolved, hedgerows bleached to bone-white thorn, and the soil itself had a smell she couldn't name: not rot exactly, but absence, as if something had been drunk out of the ground and nothing had come to fill it.

Three days out from the border pass, they walked in single file along a dead riverbed, and Elodie felt the first flicker.

It started in her palms — a warmth like holding a stone pulled fresh from a fire. She stopped. Ahead of her, Ser Brannoc turned, one hand already on his sword hilt, because everything about him was readiness now, even half-healed, even limping.

"What is it?"

"I don't know." She opened her hands. Between them, faint as breath on cold glass, light gathered. Not gold, not quite — more like the color of the valley's deep water, green shot through with silver. It pulsed once, twice, and died when she flinched.

Brannoc stared at it the way men stare at ghosts. "That wasn't there at the border."

"The wards were dampening me my whole life." Her voice came out strange. "I thought I was ordinary. I thought I was *nothing*."

"You're not nothing," Brannoc said, and there was something raw under the gruffness. "I've seen what the crown does to people who are nothing. It leaves them alone."

Behind them, Wren made a small sound — not quite a laugh, not quite a sob. The court mage had been quiet since the pass, wrapped in a pilgrim's gray cloak three sizes too big, her shaved head still bearing the faded lines of the sigils they'd inked onto novices. She looked at Elodie's empty hands with an expression Elodie couldn't read yet: hunger and horror sharing a face.

"Keep walking," Wren said. "Light like that carries. Veyra's hunters can taste it for miles."

* * *

Wren

The girl's magic tasted of juniper and rain on hot stone, and Wren nearly wept the first time she felt it drift across the blighted fields.

She had spent eleven years in the Tower of the Crown, learning to feed it. That was the truth no novice ever learned until the oaths were sworn and the sigils inked into skin: the crown did not protect the realm. The crown *ate*. Every coronation, every oath of fealty, every ward laid in its name — all of it funneled down into that circlet of gold like water into a drain. And inside the gold, something screamed.

Wren had heard it her first night in the inner sanctum, pressed to the floor while the senior mages channeled the tithe. A voice like a bell struck underwater, over and over, for hours. *Let me out. Let me out. Let me out.* She had asked her mistress what it was. Her mistress had smiled with the serene exhaustion of the long-corrupted and said, "The kingdom's conscience, child. We keep it where it can do no harm."

Thirty years of kings had gone into that gold. Aldric was only the latest vessel — the crown wore him now the way a glove wears a hand. And Veyra, who called herself regent, who would call herself queen within the month, had learned to steer the glove.

So when the wards over Hollowmere collapsed — when the godless valley's ancient seal finally cracked like an egg — Wren had run. Not toward freedom. Toward the one person whose blood sang against the prison walls. The mercenary girl on the border watch, the one who carried a shard of crown-gold without knowing what it was, the one the god had stirred for after decades of silence.

Elodie didn't know yet what she was. But Wren knew, and every mile closer to the capital, the knowledge sat in her chest like a swallowed coal. Because Wren knew something else too: debts. Old ones. Veyra held her sister's life-lease, and the usurper's agents had found her before she'd found the valley. They had given her a choice, and she had told herself she could carry both loyalties at once, the way mages carried two spells in one hand.

She was beginning to learn how that lie ended.

* * *

They nearly died at the ford at Marrowbridge.

Veyra's patrols had burned the crossing, but patrols left watchers, and one of them — a lean man in ash-gray leathers with a hunter's brand on his cheek — caught the scent of Elodie's magic across the water. He came at dusk, silent as spilled sand, and would have put a knife between her shoulders if Brannoc hadn't heard the gravel shift.

The fight lasted eleven seconds. Brannoc fought like a man settling old accounts, all economy and fury, and the watcher went into the dead river and did not come up. But Elodie stood afterward shaking, not from fear — from the light pouring off her knuckles unbidden, silver-green and getting brighter, answering her terror the way a struck bell answers a hammer.

"Make it stop," she gasped. "Make it *stop*—"

"Don't," Wren said sharply, catching her wrists. "Never tell your power to stop. Tell it where to go." She pressed Elodie's palms together, folded the girl's fingers over the light like closing a book. "Breathe it down. It's not a wound, Elodie. It's a well. You've been standing next to it your whole life and calling it silence."

The light sank. Elodie breathed. And in the sudden dark, with the corpse-cold river running past and the stars still missing overhead — three days out from the valley and the sky remained a blank black lid, starless in a way that had nothing to do with clouds — she looked at the two strangers who were now the whole of her world and felt something crack open that wasn't fear.

"We need a fire," Brannoc said quietly. "And I know where the smugglers camp."

* * *

The smuggler's hollow was a fold in the earth beneath a shattered aqueduct, hidden from the starless sky by fallen arches, warm with cook-smoke and low voices. Smugglers, it turned out, were the last honest people in the midlands: they'd never pretended the crown was anything but a thief.

Around the fire, with grease and cheap wine going around, the confessions came the way confessions do — sideways, in pieces, coaxed loose by flame.

Brannoc went first, though he stared into the fire the whole time. "I was King's Shield. Eleven years. I swore my oath to Aldric's hand, not the crown's — I made sure of it, I thought I was clever." His jaw worked. "The day the crown finished eating him, my oath went with it. I felt it snap, here—" he touched his sternum, "—like a cord cut. And I stood in the throne room watching that thing wear my king's body and give orders in his voice, and I *saluted*. Because the oath was gone and habit was all that was left. Three months I served the crown wearing my king. Then I ran." He finally looked up. "The shard you're carrying, Quintana. I tore it from the crown's edge the night I fled. It screamed the whole time. I've been trying to bring it somewhere it could be heard ever since."

Elodie's hand went to the shard at her throat. It was warm. It had always been warm.

Then Wren, who had been silent so long Elodie had almost forgotten her, spoke into the firelight. "I fed it. Eleven years. Every ward I raised in the crown's name was a spoon lifting soup to its mouth. I told myself it was duty." She laughed, and it was an awful sound. "And I have a sister in Veyra's keeping, and Veyra knows I've run, and I need to tell you both something before it kills us, because you deserve to hear it from me rather than from her."

The fire popped. Somewhere above, wind moved through the broken arches.

"The crown devours kings," Wren said. "It has eaten four of them alive, and it wears Aldric now like a coat. And the thing imprisoned inside it — the god your valley was built to seal away from *it*, not the other way around — has been screaming for thirty years. Your wards didn't cage a monster, Elodie. They hid a *witness*. And the crown has been hunting that witness's bloodline since before you were born." She met Elodie's eyes, and her own were wet. "You are the key. That's why the wards kept you. That's why Veyra will tear the midlands apart to reach you. And that's why—" her voice broke, "—why I have already sent word ahead of us. To the capital. They know we're coming."

The silence afterward was total. Even the smugglers' murmur seemed to still.

Brannoc's hand had gone to his sword. Elodie's magic rose unbidden, silver-green, trembling.

But Wren didn't run. She knelt in the dirt, in the firelight, and bared her inked scalp like a woman offering her neck. "My sister dies if I don't. My soul dies if I let them take you. I chose wrong before I understood there was a right. Kill me or use me — but know that from this moment, whatever it costs, I'm yours."

Elodie looked at her a long time. At the traitor mage kneeling in the ashes of her betrayal. At Brannoc, who had not drawn his sword, who was watching Elodie with the patient ferocity of a man who had decided whose shield he was now.

Her magic sank back down, slow as a tide going out. She reached forward and lifted Wren's chin, the way you'd raise someone from their knees.

"Then you'll carry us in yourself," Elodie said. "As pilgrims. Straight through the gates. If they know we're coming, then we arrive as a gift — and gifts get close enough to touch the crown."

Wren laughed — a real one this time, cracked and disbelieving. Brannoc shook his head slowly, but he was smiling too, grim as a gravestone.

Above the broken arches, far off across the starless dark, a line of beacon fires kindled on the distant hills — one, then ten, then fifty, burning in a chain toward the capital.

Veyra was lighting the road for them.

And in Elodie's palm, the shard of crown-gold began, very softly, to sing.
