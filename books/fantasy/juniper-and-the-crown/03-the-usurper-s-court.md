## 3. The Usurper's Court

Wren had worn many faces in her years at court, but never a pilgrim's, and she found the disguise fit her more honestly than any of them. The rough grey wool scratched at her throat. The pilgrim's brand — a tin sun stamped with the sigil of the Wayfarer's Rest — hung cold against her chest. Around her, Elodie and Ser Brannoc walked in the same penitent grey, three among two hundred souls shuffling up the processional road toward the capital of Veyra's new kingdom.

The city announced itself long before they saw it. First came the smell: woodsmoke and roasting meat, fat dripping onto coals to feed a coronation feast while the poor outside the walls fought over bread crusts. Then the sound — bells, hundreds of them, ringing out of rhythm because no bell-master alive could coordinate towers that had been built by four different kings. Then the light, or rather the absence of it. Wren had warned them, but warning was nothing like seeing it: above the capital there were no stars. None. The sky was a smooth black lid, as if the heavens had been covered with a cloth, and the city glowed beneath it like an ember in a closed fist.

"The crown did that," Brannoc said quietly. He had not spoken much since the midlands. His wounds had closed but something in his bearing remained broken open, like a door left ajar in winter. "It eats everything, even the sky."

"It eats kings," Wren said. She did not look at him. "The sky is just what spills over."

Elodie walked between them with her hood up, and Wren felt the girl's magic like heat from a forge — banked, but present, humming against Wren's own trained senses. Inside Hollowmere it had been muffled. Out here, under the starless lid, it burned. Wren had been telling herself for three days that she did not know why the patrols kept missing them, why the ward-signatures along the road bent away from their little company like water around a stone. She had been lying to herself the whole way.

The gates took pilgrims freely on feast days; the Wayfarer's Rest was old law, older than Veyra, and even usurpers liked the theater of piety. A gate-sergeant glanced at their brands, waved them through with eyes already glazed by the smell of the feast. And then they were inside, in streets hung with gold bunting that looked, to Wren's eye, suspiciously like melted-down regalia, past fountains running with wine, beneath balconies where nobles who had sworn to Aldric now toasted the woman who wore his army like a glove.

Wren knew these streets. She had studied in the Collegium's shadow for six years before the court took her, and her feet remembered the way even as her stomach clenched against it: down the Tanner's Cut, through the colonnade of the old assize courts, to the service entrance of the palace where the feast-scuttle ran like a river of steam and shouting.

"You're certain about this?" Brannoc murmured. "Walking into her hall. Voluntarily."

"No," Wren said. It was the truest thing she'd said in days. "But we're certain about the crown. It has to be seen. Touched, maybe. And there's one way in tonight that isn't guarded, because no one imagines anyone would be fool enough to take it." She paused at the archway, looking back at Elodie. In the torchlight, the girl's face was pale and set, and there was something in her expression Wren couldn't name — recognition, almost, as if she'd dreamed this street before. "Stay behind me. Say nothing. If I raise my hand palm-out, you run, and you don't stop running until you're through the north postern."

"And you?" Elodie asked.

"I've escaped worse places than this," Wren lied, and smiled, and hated how easily the lie sat on her face.

* * *

They went in through the scullery, wearing stolen serving-tabards over their grey wool, carrying a tray of spiced pears neither of them would ever taste. The palace swallowed them. Wren navigated by memory and by the old servant-signs carved discreetly into doorframes — a chisel mark here, a knot in the wainscoting there — the secret grammar of people whose labor built every stone and whose names appeared in no chronicle.

The great hall opened before them like the inside of a bell.

Two hundred candles. Banners of the nine free cities, each one captured, hanging inverted from the rafters as trophies. A dais at the far end, and on it a throne that had held four kings before this year — and beside it, standing but not sitting, a man in a king's raiment who did not move unless moved.

Aldric.

Even across the length of the hall, Wren felt the wrongness of him. He stood too straight, the way furniture stands. His hands hung at his sides with fingers slightly curled, and when he turned his head, it turned all at once, smoothly, like something on a pivot. His eyes were open. That was the worst of it. They were open and brown and human and utterly empty, windows in a house where no lamps were lit. On his brow, thin as a circlet, sat the crown — crown-gold, hungry gold, and Wren could feel it *feeding*, a slow tidal pull she sensed through her teeth, through the marrow of her, the same pull she'd felt once as a child standing too close to a millrace.

Veyra sat at the high table below the dais, laughing at something a general said. Up close, without the distance of rumor, she was smaller than her legend: a compact woman in storm-grey, dark-haired, with a scholar's narrow hands. Only her eyes betrayed her. They moved constantly, cataloguing, weighing, and they never rested on anything longer than a breath.

"Keep walking," Wren breathed. "Don't look at him. Don't—"

But Elodie had stopped.

She had stopped in the middle of the hall's processional aisle, tray forgotten in her hands, staring up at the hollow king with an expression that cracked Wren's heart down its old fault lines. Because Aldric had turned his head. All at once, smoothly, pivoting — and his empty eyes had found Elodie across two hundred candles and forty feet of marble, and his mouth had opened.

"*Juniper,*" the king said.

No. Not the king. The voice came from him but it was not his voice; it was layered underneath it, beneath the floor of speech, a whisper that seemed to arrive through the stone itself, through the soles of Wren's feet, through the wine in the cups. "*Juniper. Little root. Little buried seed.*"

Every conversation in the hall died. Two hundred faces turned. Somewhere a glass fell and shattered, and the sound rang on far too long.

"What did it say?" Veyra asked. Her voice carried effortlessly, and she had not raised it. She rose from the high table and descended the steps, unhurried, a woman approaching something she had already decided to own. "Say it again," she commanded the king, but the crown had gone silent, sated, having shown its hand only once — and Wren understood with sickening clarity that it hadn't slipped. It had *called*. It wanted Elodie found. It had been bending patrols away from them the whole journey, herding them here, and it had used Wren's guilt like a leash.

Guards took Elodie's arms. She didn't resist. She was still looking at the hollow king, and tears were sliding down her face, and Wren watched her mouth shape the word *father* without sound.

Then Veyra's cataloguing eyes found Wren, and widened fractionally, and curved into a smile.

"Well," said the usurper. "And here I thought the Collegium's debts were uncollectible. Take the mage too."

* * *

They put Elodie in a room with comfortable chairs.

That was the cruelest detail, Wren thought, watching through the interrogation chamber's observation slit — a courtesy of her old rank, the last scrap of access her betrayal had purchased. No dungeon for the girl. A fire, tea untouched on a side table, a window sealed with wards so thick the glass wept condensation. Veyra sat opposite Elodie with a decanter of wine and the patience of a woman who owned the building, the guards, the night, and possibly the dawn.

"They told me about a valley," Veyra was saying. "A godless valley where the wards fell in autumn and the stars came back. Do you know what that is, girl? That is a prison failing. My prison — inherited, like the crown, like the army, like him." She gestured vaguely upward, toward the throne room, toward the hollow king. "I didn't build the cage. I'm merely the current keeper. And keepers must maintain their locks."

"I don't know what you're talking about," Elodie said, but her voice shook, and Veyra smiled like a duelist seeing a flinch.

"The crown recognized you. Before two hundred witnesses. It called you by a name nobody living should know." Veyra poured herself wine, unhurried. "So I had records searched. Old ones. Sealed ones. There was a daughter, born in the fourth year of Aldric's reign. The mother died of it — or was said to have. The child was sent somewhere the crown's hunger couldn't reach. Somewhere warded. Somewhere *godless*." She set down the cup with a small precise click. "You were bound to that valley at birth, Elodie Quintana. Or whatever your true name is. You are the key that was hidden from the lock — and keys, in my experience, always want to come home."

Through the slit, Wren saw Elodie's hands flatten against her knees, saw the girl's magic flare — visible now, a faint gold shimmer crawling up her forearms like dawn on a mountainside — and saw Veyra lean forward with the expression of someone watching a vault open.

"There she is," Veyra murmured. "There's the blood."

Wren turned away from the slit, and Brannoc stood in the corridor behind her, disarmed, flanked by guards, his face gray with fury and grief. "You did this," he said quietly. "The route through the midlands. The patrol gaps. You led us here."

"Yes." It cost less to admit than she'd expected. That was the terrible part — how little it cost. "I owed the Collegium. Veyra holds those debts now, and she holds my sister's ward-contract with them. One word from me and Wren-the-younger goes from apprentice to ash-registry. One refusal and—" She stopped. Her voice had climbed; she dragged it down. "I told myself I was bringing you here to find a way to break the crown from inside. I told myself a great many things."

Brannoc looked at her for a long moment, and what was in his face was worse than hatred. It was understanding. "And now?"

"Now," Wren said, "she'll take Elodie to the throne room at midnight. To bind her to the crown properly — a key turned in its lock. And I will be standing in that room, because Veyra wants her Collegium-trained mage to anchor the binding." She drew a slow breath, and beneath her fear something else kindled, something old and sharp and hers. "Which means she is putting me exactly where I need to be to unmake everything she's built. She taught me warding, Brannoc. Every lock she knows, I helped design. She thinks debts make chains."

From beyond the wall, faintly, through stone and iron, Wren heard it begin: the crown whispering in the throne room above, patient and sweet, calling its buried daughter home. And beneath it — so soft she might have imagined it, except that Brannoc's head lifted too — the sound of Elodie Quintana beginning to answer.
