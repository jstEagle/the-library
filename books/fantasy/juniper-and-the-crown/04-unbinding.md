## 4. Unbinding

The throne room had no windows, and so it did not know the stars were gone. That was the cruelest thing about it — a ceiling of vaulted stone painted with constellations by some dead king's flatterers, gold leaf and lapis, all of it a lie hanging over a lie. Elodie stood beneath the painted heavens in iron shackles that drank her magic like sand drinks rain, and she thought: *even the sky in this place is stolen.*

Veyra's court had emptied for the night. Only the usurper remained, seated on the steps below the throne rather than upon it, as if she could not bear to touch the seat while its true occupant still breathed. And Aldric — her father, though the word sat strange and heavy in her chest, a stone swallowed whole — stood beside the throne like a coat hung on a hook. His eyes were open. His eyes saw nothing.

The crown sat on his head. It was smaller than Elodie had imagined, a circlet of twisted gold set with stones the color of drowned things, and it *hummed*. She felt it through her shackles, through the floor, through the bones of her feet: a hunger with a voice, patient as winter.

*Elodie.* It whispered her name again, the way it had in the great hall, before half the court and all of Veyra's guard. *Elodie Quintana. Daughter of Aldric. Child of Hollowmere. You came back to me.*

"You knew," she said to Veyra. Her throat was raw from the cells below. "When you let me walk into your coronation feast dressed as a pilgrim. You knew what I was."

"I suspected." Veyra did not look up from the letter she was reading by candlelight, as though this were an accounting dispute and not the end of everything. "Wren confirmed it."

The name landed like a knife between her shoulder blades. Wren, who had taught her to hold fire in her cupped hands without burning. Wren, who had wept over the blighted midlands and sworn the crown screamed in his dreams. Somewhere above, in some tower cell of his own, he was presumably explaining himself to someone with softer hands than Veyra's.

"He had debts," Veyra said, as if reading her thoughts. "Old ones. To people who would have fed him to the crown piece by piece. I simply offered better terms." She folded the letter at last and rose, and up close her beauty was terrible the way a glacier is beautiful — vast, cold, and utterly indifferent to what it crushed. "Do you know what you are, Elodie? Not the king's bastard. That's incidental. You are the last knot in the binding. The god was sealed into the crown, and the crown sealed into the king, and the king's bloodline bound out into the valley so the seal could never be found and never be broken. Your mother carried you over the border the night you were born. The wards took you. The valley kept you safe."

"Safe," Elodie repeated. Nineteen years of exile compressed into one bitter syllable. Every night on watch at the border, every festival she'd watched from the outside, every time the mist had turned strangers away while she pressed her palms against it from within.

"Yes," Veyra said. "And now I'm going to unmake the last of it. A drop of royal blood on the crown, freely given or otherwise, and the binding dissolves. The god comes out." Her smile was thin as a blade's edge. "Into *me*."

*She doesn't understand,* said the voice inside the crown, and for the first time Elodie heard something other than hunger in it. Something almost like pity. *They never understand.*

The doors exploded inward.

Ser Brannoc came through them like a landslide given purpose, and Elodie's heart lurched — he was supposed to be two cells down, broken-handed, guarded. But the guards behind him were down, and his armor was scorched, and his left hand was wrapped in a bandage already blooming red, and none of it mattered because he was *there*, and he was roaring her name.

"Brannoc—" She surged forward; the shackles snapped her back, magic draining, knees buckling.

He reached Veyra first. What followed was ugly and fast — no duelist's choreography, just a big man who had lost too much deciding to lose nothing else. He got one arm around the usurper's throat and drove her down against the dais steps. Veyra's magic flared white, throwing him off, hurling him across the chamber into the base of the throne. He did not get up quickly. But the impact had knocked loose something from the throne's arm — a slender rod of pale metal, a scepter-thing etched with warding script, and it skittered across the marble to fetch up against Elodie's boot.

Above them, in the gallery, a door banged open, and Wren looked down.

His face was grey. His hands shook. And he was not alone — he held Veyra's own grimoire under one arm, and when their eyes met across the throne room, he mouthed three words: *forgive me. watch.*

Then he began to speak.

Elodie understood none of it and all of it — the language of wards, the grammar of bindings, the same architecture she'd felt die at the valley border on the night the stars went out. Veyra heard it too. She spun toward the gallery, and her face went through fury into something worse: recognition.

"*No*," she breathed. "Those are *mine*—"

"Every word," Wren called down, and his voice cracked but did not stop. "You made me copy the whole codex, Majesty. Twice. In my own hand. Did you never wonder why?"

The shackles around Elodie's wrists flickered. Dimmed. The script etched along their length — Veyra's work, Veyra's power — guttered like candles in wind, because Wren was unwriting them, turning the usurper's own wards inside out, feeding each binding into the next until the whole lattice groaned.

*Now,* said the god. *Child of the valley. Now, while she is blind.*

Elodie snatched up the rod. The moment her fingers closed on it, the shackles shattered — not broke, *shattered*, bursting into lightless dust — and nineteen years of dammed-up magic came through her like a river through a burst levee. She screamed. It was too much and it was glorious and it hurt, stars she had never seen blazing up inside her skin, and she planted her bare feet on the dais and reached for her father's head.

Aldric's empty eyes focused. For one heartbeat, one impossible heartbeat, the man inside looked out.

"*Run*," he rasped. "*Don't— it will take you too—*"

"No," Elodie said. "It won't. Because I'm not giving it anything. I'm taking *you* back."

She gripped the crown with both hands, and the god's voice filled the world —

*Thank you. Oh, thank you, thank you, little keeper, I have been screaming for sixty years and no one heard —*

— and she *pulled*.

The crown did not want to come free. It fought like a living thing, like a jaw locked on bone, and Veyra was rising off the steps with starved fire pouring from her hands, and Brannoc was somehow up again, limping, bleeding, putting his ruined body between Elodie and the usurper one more time. Wren's voice in the gallery climbed to a register that cracked stone. And Elodie pulled, and the crown resisted, and then —

— then Aldric lifted his hands and helped her.

Father and daughter, together, tearing the prison off his own skull. The gold came away with a sound like a bell breaking, and the world went white.

* * *

Elodie woke on cold marble with her father's head in her lap and no magic left in her at all.

She knew it instantly, the way you know a missing tooth. The well that had roared through her was simply — gone. Drained to the last drop into what came next, which was this: the crown lying in two pieces on the dais, its stones dull as river pebbles. And above the broken vault of the ceiling — which had split open like an egg, stone raining harmlessly aside as though something vast had lifted it away with two fingers — *stars*.

Thousands of them. More than the old stories promised. They poured back into the sky like water into a dry bed, and at the center of them something descended that was not descending, something that was everywhere at once choosing to be *here*, and it had no shape her eyes could hold except briefly, mercifully, the shape of a tall figure made of dusk and silver, kneeling on the air beside her.

*I am free,* it said, and its voice was the valley mist and the border-watch silence and every prayer she had ever sent into nothing. *I am free, and I am in debt to a girl who talked to me for nineteen years though I could not answer.*

"I thought you'd abandoned us," Elodie whispered.

*I was screaming,* the god said. *All this time, I was screaming. You heard anyway. That is more faith than most gods ever receive.* It bent closer, and where its face should have been there was something warm and dark and kind. *Your magic bought my freedom, child. I cannot return it — such prices are not refundable. But I can do this.*

It touched her forehead, and Elodie felt — nothing magical. Just peace. Just the certainty, sinking all the way down, that she had done the right thing and would not spend her life wondering.

Then the presence poured upward through the broken roof and scattered itself across the relit heavens, and the stars burned brighter than they had in sixty years.

Beside the throne, Veyra lay crumpled among the ruins of her own wards. Whatever she had tried to seize when the crown broke — whatever starving torrent she'd tried to swallow — had swallowed *her* instead. She was alive. Barely. Empty. Staring at the stars with an expression Elodie would spend years trying not to pity.

Aldric stirred. His eyes were his own now, and full of grief, and he looked up at his daughter and said, in a voice worn down to gravel: "You have her stubbornness. Your mother's. Gods forgive me for both of you."

Brannoc sat down heavily on the dais steps beside them, pressed his bandaged hand to his ribs, and started laughing — low, disbelieving, unstoppable. Wren came down from the gallery slowly, the grimoire clutched to his chest like a shield, and stopped at the bottom step as if he didn't dare come closer.

"I betrayed you," Wren said.

"You unbetrayed yourself," Elodie said. "That counts. Ask me again after I've slept for a week."

* * *

They walked out of the capital at dawn, four ragged figures on the west road, and no one stopped them. What remained of Veyra's court was busy tearing itself apart over succession, and the soldiers on the walls were looking up instead of out — everyone in the kingdom was looking up, at a sky that had remembered its job.

Elodie walked at the front. Her hands were empty of magic and had never felt less empty. Beside her, Aldric moved like a man relearning his own legs; behind her, Brannoc kept up a running argument with Wren about whether repentance entitled a person to the last of the bread. Ordinary sounds. Family sounds. She kept cataloguing them, hoarding them, afraid they might evaporate.

Hollowmere's border rose out of the hills nine days later, and the mist was waiting.

It parted before she reached it.

Not reluctantly, not grudgingly, the way it had turned away strangers for nineteen years — but *openly*, folding wide like arms, and Elodie stood at the threshold of the only home she'd ever known and understood that the valley had never been keeping her *in*. It had been keeping a lamp lit. It had been waiting for her to bring people home.

Behind her, the others hesitated. A disgraced king, a broken knight, a traitor mage — refugees all, certain the mist would turn them away as it always had.

"Well?" Elodie said, and stepped through. "Are you coming or not? There's room. There was always going to be room. I'm going to see to it personally."

And as her family crossed the border behind her — as the wards sang welcome instead of warning, and the valley opened its green arms to the broken and the hunted — Elodie looked back once at the road east, where the first rumors were already stirring: free cities raising banners, soldiers deserting, pilgrims walking toward Hollowmere from every direction under a sky full of returned stars.

They were coming to her. All of them. And for the first time in her life, Elodie Quintana had no intention of turning anyone away.
