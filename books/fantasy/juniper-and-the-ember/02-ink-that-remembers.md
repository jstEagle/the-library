## 2. Ink That Remembers

The trouble began with a column of numbers that refused to sum.

Paloma noticed it an hour past midnight, in the third hour of his usual shift, when the Guildhouse above him had gone quiet enough that he could hear the building settling on its foundations like an old man easing into a chair. He was reconciling the week's accounts for the Department of Borrowed Weather — a modest portfolio, mostly rain owed to orchard-keepers outside Coldharbour and one disputed fog sold twice to the same fisherman — and the totals would not agree. He checked the entries. He re-added them, finger under each figure, lips moving the way Brother Cassian had taught him to move them when the mind wanted to wander: *one line at a time, one truth at a time.*

Still they would not agree.

He did what any careful scribe would do. He turned back to the original entry, the one for the fisherman's fog, intending to find where the error had crept in — and found that the error had not crept anywhere at all. The entry had changed.

Not much. A scribe's eye might have missed it entirely. But the ink sat differently on the page now, darker, wetter, as though it had been laid down minutes ago instead of days, and the hand — his own hand, unmistakably his own, with its narrow downstrokes and the small hitch he could never train out of the letter *g* — had written something in the margin he had no memory of writing.

*Count again. It lies to you.*

Paloma set down his pen very carefully, the way you set down a sleeping animal, and looked at the words for a long time.

The lamp guttered. Somewhere above, a floorboard complained under the weight of nothing.

He told himself the obvious things first. He was tired. He had been working fourteen-hour shifts since the harvest quarter opened, balancing debts for trades that should not have been possible, and tired men wrote things without knowing it. He had certainly seen clerks drift into half-sleep at their desks and wake to find their pens had wandered off into nonsense while their hands kept moving. There was a name for it. There was probably a name for it. Cassian would know the name for it.

But tired hands did not write warnings in margins. Tired hands wrote *fisherman fog x2 4d* and then trailed off into loops. They did not write *it lies to you* in a script so exactly his own that if someone had shown him the page blind and asked whose work it was, he would have said *mine* without hesitation and signed his name beneath it.

And the numbers still didn't sum.

He counted the fog entry again, slowly, and this time he saw it — saw what the margin-note had been trying to tell him. The price had been altered. Not by him. The alteration was older than tonight; it sat under the surface of the ink like a stone under ice. Someone had adjusted the fisherman's payment downward by a copper and a half, and the adjustment had been made *in the ledger itself*, in a way that no honest amendment allowed. The Guild of Impossible Trades kept scrupulous books precisely because its trade was impossible; every entry was witnessed, initialed, countersigned by two hands. This entry bore both initials. His were among them.

Someone had used his name to shave a copper from a fisherman, and the ledger — the ledger had *noticed*, and had reached through whatever strange channel connected it to his hand to leave him a message about it.

Or. Said a smaller, colder voice, the voice that sounded like the temple dormitory at night. Or your gift is waking up, and it has started doing things while you sleep, and the first thing it chose to do was talk to you through other people's books.

Paloma pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes until he saw constellations.

He had been eleven years old when Brother Cassian caught him reading the temple's founding charter aloud — not the words on the page, but the words *underneath* them, the true names of the paper and the ink and the lamb who had died for the vellum, all of it murmured in a language Paloma had never been taught because no one alive taught it anymore. He remembered Cassian's face in that moment better than he remembered his mother's: the way the old monk had gone very still, the way his eyes had flicked to the open door, the way he had crossed the room and closed it before saying anything at all.

"You will never read aloud again," Cassian had said. "You will never tell anyone. You will copy texts for the rest of your life and be grateful for the copying, and when the gift scratches at you, you will scratch back at something else. Wood. Bread. Your own arm, if it comes to it. Do you understand me, boy?"

Paloma had understood. He had spent nineteen years understanding. And for nineteen years it had worked — or seemed to. The gift had gone quiet, or he had gotten good at not listening, and the tremor in his hands when ink moved strangely had become just another private shame, like the debt-bond scar circling his wrist, hidden under his sleeve, paid down a fraction at a time across a lifetime.

Now the ink wasn't moving strangely. Now the ink was leaving him notes.

*It lies to you.*

He took up his pen, crossed out the margin note with three clean strokes — the way one corrected an error, the way one erased evidence — and entered the correction for the fisherman's fog in the proper form, with both initials restored to honesty, and initialed it, and blotted it, and sat back.

His hands were shaking. He watched them shake with a kind of clinical detachment, as though they belonged to another clerk entirely, some poor soul at a neighboring desk whose troubles were interesting but not urgent.

Then he turned the page and began, deliberately, methodically, on the next account — the Department of Returned Years, which was always grim reading — and forced himself to work until dawn.

* * *

Brother Cassian came down to the scriptorium at midmorning, which was itself unusual. The old monk had retired from active scribal work years ago and now occupied a small room off the chapel where he repaired bindings and drank tea that smelled more of pine resin than any leaf ever harvested, and he descended into the archive perhaps once a week, usually to complain about the damp.

Today he came straight to Paloma's desk.

He did not look at Paloma. That was the first thing wrong. In twenty years Cassian had never once approached him without looking at him first, some small acknowledgment passing between them — a nod, a raised eyebrow, the particular dry glance that meant *your posture is atrocious*. Today the old man walked to the desk as though drawn by a plumb line and stood over the open ledger, and his eyes went directly — not searching, not scanning, but *directly*, the way an arrow goes — to the page where the fog account lived.

To the place where three clean strokes crossed out three impossible words.

"The Veyra requisition," Cassian said, "came up from the counting floor this morning. They're asking why the Borrowed Weather accounts ran light."

"Half a crown short," Paloma said. His voice was steady. He was proud of how steady it was. "There was an unauthorized adjustment in the fog entry. I've corrected it and flagged the page for audit."

"An unauthorized adjustment." Cassian's fingers moved — almost absently, almost carelessly — and rested on the edge of the page, beside the crossed-out margin. His thumbnail, Paloma noticed, had gone white where it pressed the vellum. "In whose hand?"

"Mine." The lie came easily because it was technically true, and lying with technical truths was the first skill the Guild taught its indentured clerks. "My initials were on the original. Whoever amended it forged around them."

"Mm." Cassian lifted his hand from the page. For a moment Paloma thought — hoped — that was the end of it, that the old man would grumble about audits and climb back to his tea and his broken spines. But Cassian stayed where he was, and after a long moment he said, quietly, without turning his head:

"How long has the ink been talking to you, Paloma?"

The scriptorium noise went on around them — the scratch of forty pens, the rustle of pages, an apprentice somewhere dropping a pot of sand and swearing under her breath — and none of it reached Paloma at all. The world had narrowed to the space between his desk and the old man's habit, to the question hanging in it like smoke.

"I don't know what you mean," he said.

"No," Cassian agreed. "You wouldn't. You'd know what I meant perfectly well; you simply don't know how to answer it. There's a difference, and it's the difference that's kept you alive this long." He pulled the second chair from the neighboring desk, the one belonging to a junior clerk who was off at the counting floor, and sat down in it with the careful economy of a man whose knees had opinions. "I'm going to say some things. You're going to keep copying while I say them, because a man hunched over his work attracts no attention, and a man staring at his superior does. Copy."

Paloma copied. The next entry concerned a widow in the Saltward district who had sold three years of her grief to the Guild and was petitioning to buy back one of them. His pen moved. His handwriting, he noted distantly, had never been steadier.

"There is a reason the temple assigns its gifted children to the archives and nowhere else," Cassian said, in the tone he used for reciting inventory. "It isn't charity. The Church believes — has believed for six hundred years — that a gift left unexercised starves. Quietly. Slowly. Like a fire in a sealed room, it consumes its own air and gutters out. Most of them do gutter out. We bury the ones that don't."

His pen scratched. The widow's grief, bought at eight coppers the year, resale value negligible.

"But a gift doesn't always die quietly," Cassian went on. "Sometimes it goes *underground*. It stops announcing itself and starts... settling. Into whatever medium is nearest. For a child raised among books, that medium is ink. Paper. The written word. The gift seeps into the tools the way water seeps into a wall, and the wall doesn't notice for years, and then one night the wall begins to speak." He paused. "You're pressing too hard. Lighter."

Paloma eased his grip. Below the desk, his knee had begun to bounce; he stilled it with an act of will.

"It happened to me at thirty-one," Cassian said. "A psalter in my keeping began annotating itself. Marginalia in my hand, correcting errors in the Latin that had survived four centuries of copyists. I did what you are doing right now. I denied it, alone, at night, to a room that had already heard everything." He almost smiled. It transformed his face briefly into the face of a much younger man, one who had perhaps once laughed easily, before decades of erasing names from reports had worn him smooth. "I burned the psalter. The annotations appeared in the next book within a fortnight. That is when I sought out the archivist who trained me — old Father Ellery, dead now — and learned that I was not damned, only inconvenient. And learned what the Church does with inconvenient gifts when it finds them."

"And what does it do?" Paloma asked. He knew he shouldn't ask. Asking was engagement; engagement was confession. He asked anyway.

"Nowadays? Nothing. Nothing at all." Cassian's eyes finally came up from the ledger, and they were not unkind, but they were very, very tired. "Which is worse. Because the Crown has started doing something, and the Church has decided that looking the other way is cheaper than objecting. You heard the criers yesterday."

Paloma's pen stopped. Everyone in Coldharbour had heard the criers. A royal proclamation, read at every gate and market cross: *By order of the Crown, all persons harboring knowledge of unnatural gifts shall render such knowledge unto the appointed assessors.* The word *assessors* was new. The word *assessors* had a sound like scales, like ledgers, like a price being set on something before anyone knew what was being sold.

"That's tax language," Paloma said. "Assessors assess. It's a census. A tithe on—"

"On what?" Cassian asked softly. "On gifts? How do you tax a thing that lives inside a person? You can't levy it. You can only take it. And a crown that takes gifts must first know where they are, and a crown that wants to know where they are will start by asking the institutions that keep records." He rose, knees cracking, and stood looking down at the crossed-out margin note for a long moment. "Every report the temple has filed on you since you were eleven years old, I wrote. Every one says the same thing: *no irregularity observed*. I have spent twenty years making you a blank page, Paloma. Blank pages don't get assessed."

The scriptorium noise rushed back in — the pens, the sand, the apprentice's distant cursing — and Paloma realized his eyes were burning and blinked it away furiously, because crying at one's desk was the sort of irregularity that got reported even by friends.

"Why?" he managed. "Why would you risk—"

"Because someone did it for me, once, and I intend to pass the debt forward until it's paid." Cassian tucked his hands into his sleeves. "But hear me, and hear me clearly. What happened to that ledger last night was not nothing. A gift that writes warnings is a gift that is *awake*, and awake things draw notice — not only the Crown's notice. Yours woke for a reason, and gifts wake for reasons, and in sixty years of keeping records I have never known one to wake gently. Whatever it saw in those numbers, it thought you needed to see it too. That frightens me more than the assessors do."

He turned to go. At the end of the aisle he paused, back still turned, and said, in a voice pitched to carry exactly as far as Paloma's desk and no farther:

"Tonight, when you balance the weather accounts, use a fresh quill. And if the ink says anything else—" his shoulders rose and fell once, "—don't cross it out. Write back."

Then he was gone up the stairs, into the smell of pine tea and binding glue, and Paloma sat alone with forty pens scratching around him and the widow's grief half-copied on his page, feeling the ground shift beneath the careful invisible life he had built, stone by patient stone, over twenty years.

* * *

He worked through the afternoon. He ate the supper brought down to the archive — barley and salt pork, eaten standing, as always. He told himself, in the steady internal voice he used for such purposes, that the world had not ended, that a conversation was not a catastrophe, that Brother Cassian had known his secret for two decades and the sky had held. He told himself the proclamation was tax language. He told himself the ink was fatigue.

At midnight, alone, he unlocked the drawer where the day's completed ledgers waited for the morning cart, and he took out the Borrowed Weather accounts, and he turned to the fisherman's fog.

The crossed-out margin was exactly as he'd left it. Three strokes through three impossible words. Honest corrections below, double-initialed. Everything in order.

Except that beneath the crossed-out warning, in his own hand, in ink that had not been there an hour ago, a new line had been added. Smaller than the first. Pressed hard enough into the vellum that he could feel the grooves with his fingertip, like scars.

*They are coming tomorrow. Hide the girl.*

Paloma read it three times.

He knew no girl. There was no girl anywhere in the ledgers, in the archive, in his life — no girl whose name he knew, whose face he could summon, whose existence touched his careful invisible world at any point whatsoever.

Outside, far off across the rooftops of Coldharbour, a bell began to toll — not the hour bell, not the alarm, but the deep slow mourning peal of the Guildhouse tower itself, the one rung only when the Guild had something to sell that it did not wish discussed aloud.

Paloma blew out his lamp and stood in the dark, holding a book that knew things he did not, and listened to the bells selling something in the night.
