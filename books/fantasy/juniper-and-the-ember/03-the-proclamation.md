## 3. The Proclamation

The bells woke the city before the criers did.

Paloma knew every bell in Coldharbour by its voice — he'd spent enough sleepless nights in the Guild's counting room to have catalogued them the way other men catalogued lovers or debts. The temple bells rang in fifths, patient and round. The harbor bell was a cracked thing that sounded like a coin dropped down a well. The Guild's own bell, which almost never rang, had a voice like a door being locked.

At dawn, all of them rang at once.

He sat up in his cot in the scribes' dormitory with his heart going hard and wrong, and for three full seconds he thought: *fire*. Then he thought: *war*. Then the third thought arrived, the one he'd been carrying under his ribs for weeks like a swallowed stone, and it said: *they know*.

He dressed in the dark. His hands were steady. He made them be steady.

By the time he reached the Guildhall steps, the fog off the harbor was turning gold at its edges and the first criers were already working their way up Sable Street, their voices pitched to carry over the bells, over the shutters banging open, over the dogs answering from every yard in the city.

*"By order of His Majesty King Aldous, blessed and long-reigning — a proclamation concerning the unnatural — let it be known that the Crown calls upon all loyal subjects to name those among them who bear gifts beyond nature's allowance —"*

Paloma stood on the cold stone step and listened to his own death warrant read out in a cheerful market voice.

*"— such persons are declared wards of the Crown, for their own protection and the protection of the realm. Those who come forward freely shall be treated with mercy. Those who are named by others shall be found. Rewards are offered for true report. The hunt begins at first light this day."*

A woman beside him — a fishwife with her basket still on her arm, gills glinting silver on her apron — crossed herself twice and then spat, which covered both possible religions. A boy of maybe ten stood on the fountain rim with his mouth open, drinking the proclamation in the way children drink anything enormous.

And everywhere, in the crowd gathering along Sable Street, Paloma watched people do the arithmetic. He could see it happening behind their eyes, quick and involuntary, the way you flinch from a flame. *Who do I know? Who owes me? Who has ever looked at me sideways?* The proclamation hadn't even finished ringing across the rooftops before Coldharbour began quietly pricing its neighbors.

He should go inside. He should go inside and ink his columns and be invisible, because invisibility was the whole architecture of his life, load-bearing wall after load-bearing wall, and the first rule of an invisible man is that you do not stand in doorways looking stricken while the world tilts.

He went inside.

* * *

Mistress Veyra was already in the counting room when he arrived, standing at the great window with her back to him, watching the crowd below disperse into its new shape. She wore mourning grey, as she always did, though no one had ever established what she mourned; Paloma privately suspected she simply found it efficient, since grey matched everything and forgave nothing.

"You heard," she said. Not a question.

"I heard."

"The bells woke me." She turned. Veyra was perhaps fifty, built like a ship's figurehead that had decided to run the ship herself, and her eyes were the pale grey of uninked paper. "Do you know how long it's been since all the bells rang together?"

"No, mistress."

"Nineteen years. The plague year." She came back to her desk and sat, and drew toward her the day's ledger — *his* ledger, the one he'd balanced until midnight, the small debts column neat as a fence. "The last time, we lost a third of our clients and half our appraisers. This will be worse. Or better." Her mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "Depending on your position."

"And what is the Guild's position, mistress?"

Veyra looked at him for a moment longer than the question required. Paloma had learned, over six years of debt-bonded service, to read those pauses. They were where she kept the things she wasn't going to say.

"The Guild's position," she said, "is that the Guild survives. Fetch me the registry of outstanding appraisals. And the client rolls. All of them."

"The client rolls?" He kept his voice level, clerk-flat. "All forty years?"

"All of them." She was already writing, fast, her pen making little biting sounds against the paper. "We're going to need to know who's been trading in impossible goods, and where they live."

It took him a moment — a bad, cold moment — to understand what she meant, and then he understood, and wished he didn't. The client rolls weren't just names and accounts. They were patterns. A widow in Tanner's Row who bought candles that never guttered. A ferryman who paid his interest in fish that tasted of whatever the eater loved best. Forty years of small impossibilities, each one logged in Paloma's own careful hand or his predecessors', each one a thread, and Veyra intended to pull every thread and see which ones screamed.

"You're going to sell them," he said. He hadn't meant to say it aloud. The words got out before the clerk in him caught them.

Veyra's pen stopped.

Paloma looked at the floorboards. There was a knot in the wood near his left foot shaped like a closed eye, and he fixed his attention on it with the desperation of a drowning man fixing his on a star.

"Mistress," he said, "I misspoke."

"You didn't." Her voice was calm, almost gentle, which was worse than anger. Anger would have meant she cared what he thought. "You observed. That's what I keep you for, Coldharbour. You observe things other clerks don't. It's why your columns balance." She resumed writing. "Yes. We'll sell them. Names, locations, habits. The Crown pays well for verified reports, and the alternative is that some other house sells them first and the Guild starves with clean hands. I've buried too many houses to romanticize clean hands."

He fetched the registries. His arms did it; something else watched from a distance.

At the door, Veyra spoke again without looking up. "Coldharbour."

"Mistress."

"If there were such a thing as a gifted person in this building — purely hypothetically — what would you advise them?"

The question hung in the counting-room air like smoke. Paloma's pulse filled his ears. He thought about Brother Cassian's hand on his shoulder yesterday, heavy and wordless. He thought about the ledger entry that had written itself, the warning in his own handwriting that he still kept folded in his shirt, against his skin, like a coal he couldn't put down.

"I would advise them," he said carefully, "that the Guild honors its contracts. All of them. In both directions."

Something flickered across Veyra's face — approval, or disappointment, or possibly both wearing the same coat. "Get to work," she said. "We open the rolls to the Crown's assessors at noon."

* * *

Noon came like a tide.

The Crown's assessors arrived in livery the color of wet slate — four of them, plus a fifth who didn't wear livery at all but a plain brown coat, and whom the others obeyed. They set up in the Guild's receiving hall at a table draped in the royal colors, and the citizens of Coldharbour came to them the way water comes to a crack.

Paloma worked the side table, verifying claims against the rolls. That was his function: a man with ink-stained fingers and a clerk's blank face, confirming that yes, the widow Maren of Tanner's Row held an account with the Guild, yes, her purchases were consistent with candle-craft of unusual quality, yes, here is her address, here is her pattern of movement, here is the day of the week she collects her pension.

Name by name by name.

He told himself the stories clerks tell themselves. *I'm only verifying records. The information already exists. If not my hand, another's.* He told himself that most of the names would prove false leads, frightened gossip, shopkeepers settling scores. He told himself that the Guild's appraisal standards were strict, that Veyra wouldn't sell a certainty, only suspicions, and suspicions bought time —

He stopped telling himself stories around the fourth hour, when a girl of nineteen came to the table to report her uncle, and gave the assessors his name, his street, and the fact that he could mend broken crockery with his bare hands, and her voice never once shook. She collected her reward — eight crowns, a month's wages — and walked out whistling.

Paloma excused himself to the privy and was sick into the channel.

Afterward he knelt on the cold flags with his forehead nearly touching the stone and breathed until the shaking stopped, and what he felt, underneath the nausea, underneath the fear, was recognition — ugly, unwelcome recognition. Because he knew exactly what lived behind that girl's steady voice. He'd lived there himself for thirty-one years. It was the country where you survive by making yourself small and everyone else smaller, where safety is a currency and someone else's body is always in circulation to buy it.

*I am not her,* he told the stone.

*You are precisely her,* answered something in him that sounded like his own handwriting. *You just haven't been paid yet.*

* * *

Brother Cassian found him at dusk on the archive stairs.

The old monk moved slowly these days, one hand on the rail, his lamp throwing his shadow huge and hunched up the stairwell ahead of him. He sat down next to Paloma without asking, which was his way, and set the lamp between them, and for a while neither of them said anything. Below them, through the archive's high windows, the city was lighting itself against the early dark — more lamps than usual, nervous lamps, whole streets lit like festival nights except nobody was celebrating.

"They rang the bells at my seminary too," Cassian said finally. "Thirty-eight years ago. Different king. Same proclamation, near enough. 'Wards of the Crown, for their own protection.'" He snorted softly. "I've never once seen the Crown protect anything it called a ward."

"What happened? Thirty-eight years ago?"

"Hunts. Bonfires, in the rural sees. Here in Coldharbour, mostly quiet disappearances — the city prefers its cruelties administrative." He rubbed his knees. "My teacher was taken. Brother Anselm. He could make bread rise by singing to it. That was all. Bread." Cassian's laugh came out bent. "They wrote in the record that he was a trafficker in unnatural leavening. As if the crime were the flour."

Paloma looked at the old man's profile, cut gold-edged by the lamplight, and felt the folded paper against his chest — the self-written warning — seem to warm.

"Cassian. Yesterday. You saw the ledger."

"I see many ledgers."

"You saw *mine*. The entry I didn't write."

Cassian was quiet long enough that the lamp flame leaned and recovered. When he spoke, his voice was lower, and very even, the way men speak when they've rehearsed a sentence for decades and hoped never to use it.

"I've seen your ledgers for six years, Paloma. And the temple's copies of them for longer than that." He turned his head, and his eyes in the lamplight were tired and absolutely clear. "Did you think it was chance that your debt-bond was transferred to the Guild instead of the cloister? Did you think the temple simply *lost* the novitiate assessment that flagged you at fourteen? Someone burned it. Someone has been burning things about you since you were a boy. Every report, every suspicion, every note in a margin." He smiled, small and crooked. "Paper burns easily, if you love someone enough."

The stairwell seemed to tilt gently, the way the whole world had tilted that morning, but this was a different direction entirely — not falling, Paloma realized. Being caught.

"Why?" His voice came out rough. "Why would you — you barely know me. I balance numbers. I'm not worth —"

"You're worth exactly what you think you're not worth, which is why you can't see it." Cassian reached over and tapped the folded edge of paper visible at Paloma's collar, the hidden coal. "That entry that wrote itself. What did it say? Exactly."

"'The hunt opens. Hide the bright ones. Count nothing for the crown.'"

Cassian nodded slowly, as if Paloma had confirmed a diagnosis. "Your gift doesn't write warnings, boy. It writes *truths*. There's a difference, and it matters. A warning is about fear. A truth is about what's coming regardless." He rose, joints cracking, and took up his lamp. "So ask yourself the real question. Not whether you're safe. Whether there's anyone brighter than you who isn't."

He started down the stairs, then paused.

"There's a gap in the archive inventory," he said, without turning around. "Third shelf, west stacks, behind the tax codices. It's been there two days. Whatever's missing, I'm sure it's nothing. I'm sure it's very good at being nothing."

His footsteps faded. Paloma sat alone on the stairs with the lamp, and the words rearranged themselves in his mind: *hide the bright ones.*

Then he was moving, taking the stairs two at a time, past the tax codices, into the cold breath of the west stacks where the archives kept their oldest silences.

The gap was there. Third shelf. A rectangle of dust-free shadow where a crate — no, not a crate, a *person*-sized absence — had waited out two days of proclamation and bells and the beginning of the end of the world.

And on the shelf's edge, small and deliberate, pressed into the dust by a finger no bigger than a robin's:

a scorch mark, no bigger than a thumbnail, still faintly warm.

* * *

Paloma stood in the dark of the west stacks with his heart hammering and did the only sensible thing a clerk could do.

He checked the borrowing register.

Nothing signed out. Nothing logged in. But the register's latest page — his own page, his own ruled columns, his own careful headings — bore a single line of text in a hand that was not his, letters formed crooked and urgent, as if written by someone who had never been taught and was teaching herself in a hurry, in ink that smelled faintly of char:

***you count everything. count me gone.***

Below it, smudged, half-erased, as though the writer had lost her nerve halfway through and then found it again:

***they are coming back at bell. i am still in the building.***

Paloma looked up from the page. Somewhere above him, muffled by floors of shelved paper and forty years of impossible trades, the Guild's evening bell began to ring — the one that sounded like a door being locked.

And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, from somewhere deep in the archive's blind corridors, came the sound of small feet running, and a candle flame roaring where no candle should roar.
