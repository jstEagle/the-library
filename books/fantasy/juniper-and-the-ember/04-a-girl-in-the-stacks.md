## 4. A Girl in the Stacks

The archive of the Guild of Impossible Trades occupied the lowest floor of the Saltstone House, three levels below the counting rooms, where the air tasted of dust and tallow and old promises that had gone sour in their bindings. Paloma Coldharbour had spent eleven years among those shelves. He knew them the way other men knew their own hands: which ladder rung was cracked, which ledger swelled in damp weather, which alcove held the contracts so ruinous they were kept under lock and prayer.

He was down there past midnight because the night ledgers would not balance.

Not in the ordinary way. The ordinary way — a transposed figure, a missing seal, a client who had traded his shadow for a debt he didn't understand — those were Tuesday. This was different. For three nights running, the columns had refused to sum. Not wrongly; *reluctantly*. The numbers sat on the page like animals pretending to sleep, and when Paloma's pen came near, they seemed to flinch.

He had told himself it was fatigue. He had told himself a great many things over eleven years, and most of them had been lies small enough to live inside.

The candle at his elbow guttered, steadied, guttered again. He trimmed the wick without looking up. That was the discipline of his life: trim the wick, balance the column, keep your head down and your name out of the margins. Brother Cassian called it *the invisible vocation*, and meant it as praise. Paloma wore it like a coat two sizes too small — serviceable, suffocating, and impossible to take off in public.

He heard the noise on his third attempt at the Veyra shipping accounts.

A soft sound. A settling sound. The sound a body makes when it has been holding itself still for hours and finally shifts its weight. Anyone else would have called it rats. Paloma had been a temple novice once, and temple novices learned the difference between rats and children, because both got into the granary and only one of them wept.

He did not call out. Calling out was a door opening, and doors opened onto things you couldn't close. Instead he set down his pen, took up the candle, and walked the aisle between the L through N folios with the unhurried tread of a man doing nothing at all.

The girl was wedged behind the O through R cases, in the gap where the shelving pulled away from the wall to accommodate the old deed-press. She could not have been more than nine or ten. Soot streaked her face in lines like rain down a window, and her hair was a black bird's nest of ash and straw. She wore a coat built for someone twice her size, sleeves rolled six times, hem dragging. Her eyes were the problem. They were wide and fixed on him with the particular ferocity of something small that has decided to die rather than be taken.

Paloma knew that look. He had worn it in a temple dormitory at age seven, the night the tithe-assessors came for the gifted and a brother named Cassian had stood in the doorway and lied with such calm authority that Paloma had lived to grow up boring.

"Rats," the girl said. Her voice was smoke-rasped. "I'm chasing rats."

"You're behind a deed-press."

"I'm *ambushing* rats."

Paloma considered this. Somewhere above them, faint through three floors of stone, the bells of Coldharbour were tolling the watch — and not the usual pattern. Four tolls, pause, four tolls. The hunt-bell. It had been ringing for two days now, and everyone in the city had developed the same trick of not hearing it.

"The catchers came through here an hour ago," he said. "Two of them. They checked the salt cellar and the coal chute and went up complaining about their boots."

Something in her posture shifted — not relaxing, nothing so generous as that. Recalculating.

"They'll come back," she said.

"Yes."

"They always come back." She said it without self-pity, the way you'd report weather. "They've been after me since Marrowgate. I burned a man's hand off."

Paloma's stomach performed a slow, cold revolution. "Did you."

"He was going to sell me." She lifted her chin. "I didn't mean to burn all of it. Just some."

"That's usually how fires work," said Paloma. "They lack restraint."

That startled a ghost of a laugh out of her — one syllable, quickly strangled, as if laughter were also something the catchers might hear. And then Paloma saw it happen. He saw it and felt the floor tilt beneath eleven years of careful, invisible life.

His candle flame leaned toward her.

Not flickered. *Leaned*. Bent like a stalk of wheat in wind, stretching toward the girl's dirty fingers as though the fire had remembered something it loved. When she noticed him noticing, she snatched her hand back into her sleeve, and the flame straightened slowly, sulking, like a dog called to heel.

"I didn't do anything," she said.

"No," Paloma agreed. "It does that on its own around you."

"It's not my fault."

"I know," he said, and found that his voice had gone quiet in a way he hadn't authorized. "Believe me. I know it's not your fault."

She studied him with those fierce eyes, running some arithmetic of her own. Children who survive on the street are never bad at sums. "You're the ink man," she said suddenly. "The one who works down here. The porters say you talk to the ledgers."

"I balance them."

"You *talk* to them. Ferro saw you. Said the pages move for you before you touch them."

Ferro. A porter with a drinker's memory and a gambler's tongue. Paloma filed him away for a conversation involving interest rates. "Porters say a great many things."

"Do they?" The girl leaned forward slightly, and the candle leaned with her, and this time she didn't hide it. There was something almost hungry in how she watched the flame bend — the way a starving person watches bread. "Do they say what happens to people like us? Now. With the bell going."

Paloma looked at her — at the soot, the rolled sleeves, the hand curled protectively around a flame's affection — and felt the recognition arrive like a debt coming due. Eleven years of trimming wicks and balancing columns and keeping his gift folded up small enough to fit inside a scribe's life, and here was everything he had hidden, aged nine, filthy, hunted, sitting behind a deed-press looking at him like he was either a door or a wall.

*They will burn the ledgers of anyone caught concealing a gift-bearer,* the proclamation had said. *And the concealer with them.*

"They say," Paloma said carefully, "that the smart ones get out of Coldharbour."

"I'm smart," she said instantly.

"I can see that."

"But I'm also hungry."

Of course she was. He should have left her there. He should have walked away, climbed the stairs, reported a rat infestation to the porter, and let the machinery of the world grind exactly as it was designed to grind. That was the bargain he had made with his life: invisibility in exchange for survival, silence in exchange for Cassian's protection, smallness in exchange for everything.

Instead he heard himself say: "There's bread in the counting room. Yesterday's. The clerks waste appalling amounts of it."

The girl's eyes narrowed. "What do you want for it?"

Nothing, he wanted to say. But he understood the question — it was the first honest transaction she'd been offered in maybe her whole life, and honesty deserved honesty. "Your name," he said. "Just for tonight. So if the catchers ask, I can truthfully say no child is hiding in my archive."

She went very still. Then, slowly, she smiled, and there was something underneath the grime and the fear that was bright and sharp and entirely unbroken.

"Juniper," she said. "My name's Juniper. And you're wrong, ink man."

"About what?"

"About the smart ones getting out." She slid from behind the deed-press, small and grimy and upright, and the candle flames along the whole aisle bowed toward her as she passed them, one after another, like courtiers to a queen nobody had crowned yet. "Nobody gets out. You just find better places to hide."

Above them, the hunt-bell tolled four and paused, tolled four and paused. And from the top of the archive stair, muffled by stone but unmistakable, came the sound of boots — several pairs, descending fast, and the flat voice of a Guild catcher saying words that turned Paloma's blood to well-water:

*"Veyra says search the lower stacks. Every shelf. She wants the little ember-girl found before the crown's appraiser arrives tomorrow."*

* * *

Paloma moved without deciding to.

Later he would try to reconstruct those ninety seconds and fail — the mind protects its own debts — but the shape of it was this: he snuffed the candle with his palm (and the dark rushed in gratefully, because darkness was the oldest friend of the concealed), he pressed the girl back behind the deed-press with a hand on her shoulder that shook only a little, and he stepped out into the aisle with a ledger open in his arms and his spectacles pushed up into his hair, wearing the face of a man interrupted at tedious work.

Three catchers came down the stair with lanterns. Paloma knew the leader by sight — Hask, broad as a door, with the burn-scarred knuckles of a man whose trade involved holding things that didn't want to be held.

"Coldharbour." Hask's lantern swept the aisle. "You're late-working."

"The Veyra accounts don't balance themselves." Paloma let just enough weariness into his voice. It wasn't difficult. "Unlike some people's evenings, mine involves arithmetic."

"We're looking for a stowaway. Small. Girl. Been seen round the docks." Another sweep of the lantern, slower now, lingering on the shadows between the shelves. "Mistress Veyra's orders. Every stack."

"In my archive?" Paloma raised his eyebrows with the mild offense of a bureaucrat. "Hask, the only things that hide in here are debts. And I assure you, none of them are small."

Hask grunted. It was almost funny — the tragedy of it, later, when Paloma had time to feel things — that the best shield he'd ever raised was the reputation for dullness he'd spent eleven years building, brick by brick, lie by lie. Boring men were invisible. Invisible men were safe. He had made himself safe the way you make anything safe: by making yourself nothing.

But then the lantern light swung past the deed-press, and Paloma saw — in the fraction of a second before it passed on — a small hand emerge from the shadow, fingers spread, reaching toward the nearest catcher's lantern flame.

And the flame *bowed*.

It bent toward the gap in the shelving like a flower toward sun, stretched, strained — and Hask stopped walking.

Paloma's heart stopped with it.

"Huh," said Hask. He raised the lantern, frowning at it, turning it as though the fault were in the glass. "Flame's drawing queer tonight. Drafty old hole, this."

"Damp," Paloma said. His voice came out level. He would think about that later, the levelness of it, and wonder whose voice he'd borrowed. "Salt air gets into everything. Ruins the bindings."

"Aye." Hask lowered the lantern. The flame straightened, cheated. "Well. Keep your door barred, scribe. Veyra wants this one found, and she doesn't want it found dead, if you take my meaning. Crown pays better for breathing gifts than cold ones." He grinned, and his teeth were yellow in the lantern light. "Appraiser comes tomorrow. Big day for the Guild."

They went up. The boots faded. The door boomed shut at the top of the stair, and the bar fell into its brackets with a sound like a full stop.

Paloma stood in the dark for a long moment, listening to his own pulse count out the seconds. Then he relit his candle from the stub in his pocket, walked back to the deed-press, and crouched down to where the girl was pressed against the wall with her hand still extended, trembling, toward a flame that was no longer there.

"You *idiot*," he whispered.

"It wanted to come to me," she whispered back. Her eyes were enormous. "I didn't even ask. It just — when I want something, things give it to me. Fire especially. I don't know why."

Paloma crouched there in the tallow-smelling dark, looking at this small, reckless, luminous creature, and understood with terrible clarity that he was already lost. Because he recognized the wanting. He recognized the thing that moves in you when the world's small magics lean your way unbidden — the terror and the sweetness of it, tangled together so tightly you can't pull them apart without tearing something.

He had spent thirty years pulling them apart anyway. He had gotten very good at it. And looking at Juniper, he saw the whole cost of that skill laid out in miniature: a child alone in the dark, teaching herself to be ashamed of the only thing about her that was true.

"Listen to me," he said. "Tomorrow an appraiser comes. If he finds you, he doesn't take you to a prison. He takes you to the palace, and the palace doesn't let things go. Do you understand?"

"Nobody lets things go," Juniper said. "That's what owning is."

From the mouth of a ten-year-old. Paloma closed his eyes briefly. "Then we're agreed on economics. Stay behind the press. Don't touch any flames. And when I bring you bread—"

"You said bread."

"—I will bring you bread. And in the morning, before the appraiser arrives, I am going to do something very stupid on your behalf, and you are going to make it necessary by being exactly this reckless. Understood?"

Juniper grinned. In the candlelight, with the soot and the fear, the grin was still somehow the brightest thing in the room.

"You're going to hide me in the inventory," she guessed.

Paloma stared at her. "How could you possibly know that?"

"Because it's what I'd do." She settled back against the wall, pulling her enormous coat around herself like a fort. "Everything's stock somewhere, ink man. Even girls. Especially girls." Her eyes drifted half-closed, exhaustion winning at last, but her voice stayed awake enough to land one more truth. "You just have to find somebody willing to do the paperwork."

Paloma rose, knees cracking, and gathered his scattered ledgers. Above, the hunt-bell tolled its four-and-four across a city that had learned not to listen. Tomorrow the appraiser would come, and Mistress Veyra would smile her accounting smile, and every hour Paloma bought for this child would accrue interest at rates he didn't dare calculate.

He carried the thought upstairs like a second candle:

*I have just become a liability. And the terrible thing is that, for the first time in eleven years, I don't mind.*

Behind him, in the dark of the stacks, a girl slept among the impossible trades, and every unattended flame in the archive leaned gently toward her, keeping watch.
