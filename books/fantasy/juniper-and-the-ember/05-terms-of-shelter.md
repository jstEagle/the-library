## 5. Terms of Shelter

The Guild of Impossible Trades kept its ledgers in the cellar, because the Guild believed that debt, like mushrooms and certain sins, thrived best in the dark.

Paloma descended the spiral stair with a girl's wrist locked in his grip and his heart performing arithmetic it did not like. Behind them, three floors up, the catchers were still turning the archive over shelf by shelf. He had perhaps a quarter hour before they thought to check below.

Juniper came willingly enough, which surprised him. She moved like a cat being carried somewhere new — not relaxed, but reserving judgment. Her eyes went everywhere: the oil lamps in their iron cages, the rows of ledger-cabinets with their brass fittings gone green, the long scarred table where Veyra conducted what she called negotiations and everyone else called weather.

"You're hurting," Juniper said.

He loosened his grip at once. "Apologies."

"You're still holding." But she didn't pull away, and he understood that this was different — she was holding back. A small hand in his, soot-grimed, unnervingly warm. Warmer than skin had any right to be. He had felt that warmth through her sleeve when he'd hauled her out from behind the geography folios, and he had told himself it was fear-sweat, fever, anything but what it was.

Above them, a shelf crashed. Voices.

"Under the table," Paloma said. "Now."

She looked at him with an expression far too old for her face — he would learn that expression well in the years to come, though he didn't know it yet — and slid beneath the negotiation table into the shadow of its hanging cloth. Paloma straightened his cuffs, smoothed his waistcoat, arranged his face into the mild, slightly bored mask of a scribe whose only concern was ink and interest, and sat down to wait for his executioner.

Veyra arrived before the catchers did. She always seemed to arrive before things did; Paloma had never once seen her approach from any direction. She was a broad woman dressed in grey silk the colour of banked coals, with rings on six fingers and none on the two that mattered — the writing fingers, kept bare, as guild law demanded. Her hair was silver shot through with black, and her eyes were the pale, unimpressed grey of a winter harbor.

"Thackeray," she said. No — wait. "Coldharbour."

"Mistress Veyra."

"You are sweating."

"It is warm in the cellars."

"It is cold in the cellars. It is always cold in the cellars. That is why we keep the ledgers here." She took her chair at the head of the table without hurrying, and beneath the tablecloth Paloma heard Juniper go very still. "You came down my stairs at speed, Coldharbour, with your collar awry and your left shoe untied. You have not had an untied shoe since novitiate. And there is soot on your cuff that matches the soot on the archive shelves, which three of my men are currently pulling apart upstairs looking for something they have not found yet."

Paloma said nothing. In his experience — and his experience was extensive, being composed almost entirely of other people's debts — silence was the cheapest currency in any negotiation, and the one most often undervalued.

Veyra opened a drawer, removed a single dried fig, and began to eat it with terrible deliberation.

"The crown," she said around it, "has posted a bounty of forty crowns for every gifted soul delivered to the appraisers. Fifty if they're delivered breathing. The Guild has already sold nine names this week. Mine men are very good at finding people who do not wish to be found." She swallowed. "So. Before you say whatever clever thing you climbed down those stairs to say — understand that I already know what it is, I already know how much it will cost you, and I am deciding whether to let you say it at all."

"I would like to register an item for appraisal," Paloma said. His voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect. "Pending valuation. Standard terms."

Silence. Somewhere above, muffled through stone, a catcher shouted something about the east stacks.

"An item," Veyra repeated.

"A found item. Unclaimed. Of unusual provenance."

"How unusual?"

"Unique," Paloma said. "In all the catalogues. In all the kingdoms' catalogues."

Veyra set down the fig. Something moved behind her grey eyes — not warmth, Paloma thought. Appetite. The same look the ledger-ink got when it crawled. The same look, though he could not yet know it, that he would one day see behind the king's eyes, and the memory of that resemblance would keep him awake whole nights.

"Show me."

He reached under the table. Juniper took his hand without being asked — which meant she had been listening, which meant she understood exactly what was happening, which meant that some part of him that was not clever at all wanted to apologize before he even finished the sentence. He drew her up beside him into the lamplight.

Juniper stood there, soot-streaked, barefoot, glaring at the mistress of the Guild of Impossible Trades with the flat defiance of something feral cornered. And then, unbidden, the lamp nearest her leaned. The flame bent toward her like a flower toward sun, stretching, straining, and for one heartbeat the cellar filled with a light that was not lamplight at all — gold shot through with red, alive, hungry — before Paloma coughed and knocked the lamp with his elbow and everything was ordinary again.

Veyra had not blinked. That was the worst of it. She watched flames lean toward children the way other women watched rain.

"Well," she said.

* * *

They negotiated for an hour. It felt like a day. It would cost him years, though he wouldn't count the full price until much later, standing in a ruined chapel counting things he no longer owned.

Veyra's terms were simple, which is how you know they are ruinous. The girl would be entered into the Guild rolls as inventory — *one (1) item, organic, self-warming, provenance disputed* — pending formal appraisal. As pending inventory, she could not legally be sold, surrendered, or seized without first completing appraisal, and appraisal required an accredited appraiser, and accredited crown appraisers, Veyra observed with something dangerously close to humor, were currently backed up until the turn of the season.

"And after the turn of the season?" Paloma asked.

"After the turn of the season, Coldharbour, the world will either have ended or changed its mind. Both happen more often than people think." She wrote as she spoke, her bare writing-fingers moving in quick precise strokes, and Paloma read the contract upside down across the table out of habit, and wished he hadn't. "Storage fees accrue weekly. Appraisal costs are borne by the registering party. Should the item prove unsellable, disposal costs are likewise borne by the registering party." She looked up. "You are the registering party, Coldharbour. Your temple bond stands as surety."

"There must be—"

"There must be nothing. There is this offer and there is no offer. Those are the two shapes of the world right now." She turned the contract around and set the pen before him. "Sign, and the girl is inventory. Decline, and in ten minutes she is bounty. Forty crowns. Fifty if breathing — and she breathes very well, doesn't she."

Beneath the table, Juniper's small hot hand found his knee and gripped it. Not pleading. Just gripping. The way you hold onto something when you've decided, provisionally and against all evidence, that it might be solid.

Paloma picked up the pen. He thought about Brother Cassian, who had told him once that every contract is a confession written in advance. He thought about the tremor in his own hands, the ink that moved for him, the careful invisible life he had built out of small balances and smaller lies. He thought about the fact that he had known this girl for less than half a day and was about to mortgage his future on her behalf, and he could find no entry in any ledger, in any catalogue, in any kingdom, under which such a transaction made sense.

He signed.

"Interest rate?" he asked, because the scribe in him needed the number spoken aloud, needed the wound precisely measured.

Veyra smiled. It did not reach anywhere near her eyes.

"Terrible," she said.

* * *

Afterward, he walked Juniper up through the Guild house to the room that would be hers — a former record closet off the third landing, twelve feet square, with a grate for warmth and a window too narrow for anything but cats and regret. She inspected it the way she inspected everything: thoroughly, silently, touching the walls as if confirming their intentions.

"They'll sell me anyway," she said. Her voice was flat. Not accusing. Worse than accusing — resigned, the way a child is resigned to winter. "People always do."

"That clause cuts both ways," Paloma said. "Inventory can't be sold while appraisal is pending. I checked the law. All of it. Twice."

"People break laws."

"Yes." He hesitated. "But I write them down. And what I write down has a way of... staying written."

Something flickered across her face — curiosity, quickly buried. She sat on the cot and pulled her knees up, making herself small, making herself a knot of soot and suspicion in the corner of the room.

"Why?" she asked finally. "You don't know me. Why spend your money? Money's real. I'm not."

And there it was, the question he had been avoiding since the archive floor, since her hand closed around his wrist, since the candle flames had bowed to her like courtiers to a queen. Because he knew why. He knew it with the sick certainty of a man recognizing his own handwriting in someone else's letter. He had spent twenty years making himself unreadable, unremarkable, a cipher in a grey coat — and here was a child who burned so brightly that lamps leaned toward her, and the world was hunting her for it, and every step he took to hide her was a step he had never taken for himself.

"Because," he said, "the accounts have to balance. Someone helped me once, when I couldn't pay. This is me paying forward."

It was a lie. Mostly. Cassian had helped him, yes — bread in the novitiate dormitory, lessons given after hours, a name quietly protected. But that wasn't why he'd signed. He'd signed because looking at her crouched behind those shelves, hunted for the crime of being what she was, he had felt something crack open in his chest that twenty years of careful bookkeeping had failed to seal.

Juniper studied him for a long moment with those ember-lit eyes.

"You're bad at lying," she said. "Your ears go red."

"So I've been told."

She almost smiled. It was a small thing, crooked and brief, gone as fast as it came — but Paloma carried it back down the stairs with him like contraband, warmer than anything else in his pockets.

At the bottom of the stairs, Veyra was waiting. She fell into step beside him without preamble, and spoke quietly, for his ears alone.

"A word of professional advice, Coldharbour. Items registered as pending appraisal attract appraisers. Word travels. The crown's men will hear, eventually, that the Guild is holding something unique." She paused at the cellar door. "When that day comes — and it will come sooner than the turn of the season — you should pray your paperwork is flawless."

"My paperwork," Paloma said, "is always flawless."

"Mm." Veyra looked at him a moment longer than necessary, the way a moneylender looks at a debtor who has just underestimated the loan. "There's a crown appraiser arriving Thursday. Routine inspection of stock. I mention it only so you can prepare."

"Thursday," Paloma repeated.

"Four days. I do hope the item can be made... presentable." She pushed open the door, letting in the noise of the Guild house above — and paused, glancing back with those pale grey eyes. "Oh, and Coldharbour? Do sleep well tonight."

He did not sleep well that night. He lay in his narrow bed above the scriptorium doing sums that refused to close, and somewhere in the dark hours he realized the trap's true shape: he had bought time for Juniper with a debt that would come due in four days — and the only coin he had to pay it with was the one secret he had spent twenty years burying.

The gift. The ink that moved. The reading of true names.

To make a child invisible to a crown appraiser's eye, he would have to make her visible to his own gift again — and the gift, he knew, always took its fee.

Outside his window, Coldharbour's bells tolled the hour, and somewhere beneath the sound, faint as a dying coal, he could have sworn he heard something answering — a second bell, deeper, hung nowhere, ringing only for him.
