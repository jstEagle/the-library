## 6. What the Ember Wants

The ember woke her before the bells did.

It always did. It didn't like the dark, or maybe it liked the dark too much — Juniper had never worked out which — but either way it pressed up under her ribs like a cat wanting to be let out, and it said, in that voice that wasn't a voice, *warm now, little one, warm and bright and full.*

She lay on the cold stone between two archive shelves with her knees pulled up to her chin and pretended to still be asleep, because pretending was easier than answering.

*You're hungry,* the ember said.

*I'm not.*

*Your belly says otherwise.*

Her belly growled, loud in the silence of the stacks, traitor that it was. Juniper scowled at her own navel as if she could glare the sound back inside. Three days since she'd eaten anything but candle ends and a heel of bread she'd found behind the refectory, and the ember knew it, and the ember was going to make this into a lesson like it always did.

*There's a man upstairs,* it murmured. *The ink-man. He smells like tallow and old paper and fear. He has food.*

*I don't trust him.*

*You don't trust anyone.*

"That's called being smart," she whispered, and then clapped both hands over her mouth because even whispering felt too loud in a building full of sleeping scribes.

The ember laughed. It didn't have a mouth either, but it laughed anyway, a low rolling warmth that spread through her chest and down her arms until her fingertips tingled. She hated when it laughed. She hated more that some small starved part of her leaned into the laugh like a plant leaning toward a window.

*You trusted your mother,* it said, gentler now, which was worse.

"Shut up."

*And look how that ended.*

Juniper rolled onto her side and faced the shelf spines instead of the dark. The ledgers smelled of dust and leather and something older underneath, something like burnt sugar. She'd been living in the archive for four days now, ever since the catchers had nearly cornered her in the fish market and she'd slipped through a gap in the Guild's wall no wider than a cat. Four days of stealing food from kitchens and sleeping between shelves and listening to the ink-man's footsteps overhead, slow and careful, like someone who'd learned early that loud people got noticed and noticed people got taken.

She should have left by now. The city was big. There were other walls, other gaps.

But the catchers were everywhere this week. Bells every morning, criers on every corner, and twice she'd seen men in grey livery walking the streets with long poles tipped in glass, holding them up to strangers' faces like they were checking fruit for rot. Checking for gift. Her mother had told her about the last hunt, before, in the low urgent voice she used for things that were true: *they put the glass to you and if you light up, they take you, and nobody comes back from where they take you.*

So Juniper stayed. And the ember purred about it, smug as a fed thing.

*You could just ask him,* it said. *The ink-man. He brought the candle down here himself. He looked right at the place where you were hiding and he didn't call out.*

"He didn't see me."

*He saw you.*

He hadn't. He couldn't have. She'd been folded into the shadow between the geography folios and the tax rolls, so still she'd barely breathed, and the man had stood there with his candle and his tired face and looked at nothing, exactly like everyone always looked at nothing, and then he'd gone away again. That was how it worked. That was how it had always worked. You made yourself small and flat and quiet and the world agreed not to see you, and that was the whole bargain, and it kept you alive.

Except.

Except the candle had done a strange thing while he stood there. She hadn't imagined it. The flame had bent — not flickered, *bent*, the way grass bends toward a warm hand — leaning toward the shelf where she hid, toward her, straining against its wick like a dog on a rope. And the man had watched his own candle lean and gone very still, and then he'd set the candle down on the reading table, deliberately, close to her shelf, and walked away without looking back.

Left burning. In an archive full of paper. At night.

Nobody did that by accident.

*He knows,* the ember sang softly, delighted. *He knows and he left you fire. Little one, he left you fire.*

"It's a trap," Juniper told it. "Traps are what people do."

*Or maybe—*

"People don't leave candles for rats in their walls. People set traps for rats in their walls." She pulled her threadbare blanket tighter around her shoulders. It smelled of the archive now, of dust and vellum, and underneath that, faintly, of the man himself — tallow and ink and something green, like crushed leaves. "I know what I am to them. I'm a rat. Rats get caught or rats stay rats. There's no third thing."

The ember went quiet for a moment, and she almost thought she'd won, and then it said, in its lowest and most dangerous register, the one that felt like standing near an open oven:

*What about warm?*

Juniper closed her eyes.

Warm was the oldest promise. Warm was the first thing it had ever offered her, three winters ago, the night her mother died, when she'd been curled in the ashes of their burned-out room with snow coming through the roof and something inside her chest had cracked open like an egg and said, *I can fix that. I can make sure you're never cold again. Just let me.* And she'd let it, because she was nine years old and freezing, and ever since then it had lived in her like a tenant who paid rent in heat and collected it in hunger.

That was the trick of the ember, the thing she'd figured out slowly and never said aloud, not even to herself in daylight: it wasn't cruel. Cruel would have been simpler. It was *lonely*. It wanted what she wanted — to not be alone, to be held, to matter to somebody — and it offered those things constantly, generously, in its soft coalsmoke voice, and every offer was true and every offer was a hook.

*I could burn the lock off the Guild door,* it whispered now. *We could walk out together, you and me, down to the harbor, onto a ship, anywhere. You'd never be cold again. You'd never be hungry. I'd eat whatever you caught me and you'd never have to steal bread from behind a refectory like a beggar-child. We'd be rich. We'd be feared. Nobody would ever put a glass pole to your face again, because they'd all be too busy running.*

"And then what?" she asked the dark. This was the game they played, the question she always asked, the one it hated. "Then what do you want?"

Silence. A long one. When the ember answered, its voice had changed — smaller, and somehow much larger at once, like a fire seen through a keyhole:

*More.*

"There it is."

*Just more. Always more. That's not wickedness, little one, that's just what fire is. Fire isn't greedy, fire is only itself. You wouldn't call the sea thirsty.*

"I'd call the sea dangerous."

*And yet you swim in it.*

"I don't," said Juniper. "I've never even seen the sea."

Another pause, and then the ember did something it rarely did. It withdrew. Not far — it couldn't go far, it lived under her ribs, it was as much her heartbeat as her heart was — but it pulled its warmth back from her arms and her fingertips and gathered itself into a small tight coal behind her breastbone, and it said, quietly, almost shyly:

*You never let me all the way in. Did you know that? All these years, and you keep a door shut. I feel it every day. I sit against it and I wonder what's behind it.*

"Nothing's behind it."

*Something's behind it. Something you won't even show yourself.*

Juniper's throat went tight. Because the ember was right, and it knew it was right, and that was the worst kind of company — the kind that could read the shape of your locked rooms without ever seeing inside them.

Behind the door was her mother's face. Behind the door was the night the soldiers came for the gifted and her mother had shoved her out the back window and said *run, don't stop, don't look back, don't ever let them see you shine* — and Juniper had run, and hadn't stopped, and hadn't looked back, and the shine behind her had been orange and growing, and she was eleven years old now and she still didn't know whether the fire that took the house had come from the soldiers' torches or from her.

She'd never asked the ember. She was afraid of the answer. She was more afraid that the ember would answer honestly.

*Little one,* it said now, tender as banked coals, *if you opened the door, I could take that memory away. Burn it clean out of you. You'd wake up tomorrow and there'd be no soldier-night, no window, no running. Just a girl with a gift and a future. Wouldn't that be kinder?*

"No," she said, and her voice cracked on it. "No. It's mine. Even the bad ones are mine."

The ember sighed, a long exhalation of heat that stirred the hair at her temples though no wind moved in the archive. *Stubborn. Good. Stubborn keeps you alive. But lonely doesn't, little one. Lonely is slower than fire and it kills just as dead.*

Above her, floorboards creaked.

Juniper went absolutely still, breath shallow, ears straining. Footsteps on the stairs — the careful ones, the ink-man's tread, she knew it now after four days of listening, each step placed like a coin on a scale. He came down into the archive and stood for a moment in the aisle, and she heard him set something on the reading table with a soft ceramic click.

A bowl. She could smell it. Barley and onion and a rind of cheese melting on top, and her stomach clenched so hard she nearly whimpered.

The man spoke, quietly, to the dark shelves, not raising his voice, not searching, not approaching. Just talking, the way you'd talk to a frightened animal you didn't want to spook.

"There's food," he said. His voice was tired and level and not unkind, which was the most suspicious combination Juniper could imagine. "It's not poisoned. I work for a Guild of accountants; we're far too cheap to waste poison on barley." A pause. "Eat or don't. But the catchers sweep this quarter at dawn, and they've started checking cellars. If you're still here when the bells ring, you'll be in a cage by noon, and I'll have written the inventory list that puts you in it. I'd rather not."

Then his footsteps retreated up the stairs, unhurried, and the door at the top clicked shut, and Juniper lay in the dark hating him with her whole body because he'd seen her, he'd known all along, and because the bowl smelled like home, and home was a burned room she couldn't look at.

*Well?* said the ember, warm and eager against her spine. *Well, little one?*

"It's a trap."

*It's soup.*

"It's a trap made of soup."

*Those are the best kind,* said the ember, laughing again, and this time she felt her own mouth twitch despite everything, and she hated that too.

She lay there for a long time. The candle he'd left the night before had burned down to a stub in its dish, and its flame sat up straight and calm now, no longer bending toward her, and somehow that was worse — as if even the fire had decided to mind its own business.

Finally she slid out from between the shelves, silent as smoke, and crossed to the table. The bowl sat there steaming gently beside a stub of pencil and a scrap of paper, and on the paper, in a cramped careful hand, was written a single line:

*If you light up when they test you, they will take you. If you light up when I test you, I will write down that you didn't. — P.C.*

Juniper stared at it. Read it again. Held it closer to the candle stub, as if the words might change in better light.

Nobody had ever written anything to her before. Notes were for people who existed on lists, in ledgers, in other people's memories. She was a rat in a wall. Rats didn't get correspondence.

*He's offering a bargain,* the ember breathed, and she could feel it pressing up against the inside of her ribs like a child at a window. *A trade. Your secret for his silence. That's how it starts, little one. That's how they all start — small trades, small kindnesses, and one day you look up and you belong to somebody.*

"I already belong to somebody," Juniper muttered, pressing one hand flat against her sternum, feeling the answering pulse of heat beneath it. "I belong to you."

*Yes,* said the ember, with a satisfaction so deep and so old it made her shiver. *Yes, you do. For now.*

She ate the soup. It was the best thing she had ever tasted, which was a lie her hunger told her, but she let it tell her, and she scraped the bowl clean with two fingers and then sat for a while in the candle-glow with the note in her hand, turning it over, finding nothing written on the back, finding nothing hidden anywhere in it except the words themselves, which were strange enough.

*If you light up when I test you, I will write down that you didn't.*

She should run. Dawn was coming; the bells would ring; the catchers would come with their glass poles; the smart move, the survivor's move, the move her mother would have made, was to slip out through the gap in the wall and vanish into Coldharbour's ten thousand shadows and never think about the tired ink-man again.

Instead Juniper folded the note into a small square and tucked it into her shirt, over her heart, next to the ember, where it warmed slowly against her skin.

"Don't get comfortable," she warned it, and wasn't sure anymore which of them she was talking to.

Upstairs, faintly, she heard the ink-man begin to move around, lighting lamps, opening shutters, starting the ordinary machinery of an ordinary morning. And beneath the bells that began to toll across Coldharbour — the same hunting-bells as yesterday, and the day before — Juniper sat very still in the archive of the Guild of Impossible Trades, a stolen bowl empty in front of her and a stranger's handwriting against her heart, and waited to find out what kind of trap a person could build out of soup and a sentence.

The ember, patient as ash, hummed one word against her ribs, over and over, soft as a lullaby:

*Soon. Soon. Soon.*
