## 7. The Appraiser Comes

The appraiser arrived on a morning that smelled of rain that never fell.

Paloma knew it before anyone told him — the Guild's whole architecture changed shape around authority the way water changed shape around a dropped stone. Porters who slouched stood straighter. Veyra's voice climbed half a register and lost its edge of boredom. Even the ink in his well seemed to sit differently in its pot, as if it too had heard the bells at the gate.

"Up," said Mistress Veyra, appearing at his elbow without sound, which was her particular talent. "Wash your hands. Wear the grey coat. You are going to be boring today, Coldharbour, and you are going to be boring *brilliantly*."

"Boring," Paloma repeated.

"The crown has sent an appraiser to inspect our stock." She said *stock* the way other people said *children*. "Every item entered since midwinter. Every appraisal, every provenance, every debt notation. If he finds one entry that smells wrong, the Guild loses its charter, I lose my position, and you lose your fingers for falsifying records. Do you understand?"

"I don't falsify records," Paloma said. It was true, in the way that most careful statements are true: technically, so far, not yet.

Veyra looked at him for a long moment. Her eyes were the color of old coins, and they did arithmetic with everything they saw. "No," she agreed. "You don't. That's why you're useful. Go and be boring."

* * *

The appraiser's name was Master Quillon, and he was not what Paloma expected.

He had imagined someone brutal — a catcher's build, a catcher's eyes, a man who smelled of rope and iron. Instead Quillon was small and dry as a winter moth, with spectacles pinched onto a nose like a knuckle, and he moved through the Guild's vaults with the gentle, terrible patience of a man reading a will aloud to heirs who were pretending not to count.

"Item forty-one," he murmured, lifting a lantern that supposedly burned without oil. "Acquired from a widow in Tallow Row. Provenance?"

"Deceased husband's estate," said the vault-keeper, a sweating man named Brack. "The widow sold it to settle debts."

"Mm." Quillon turned the lantern. The flame inside leaned toward him, then away, like a dog deciding whether it liked a stranger. "And the widow's name?"

Brack looked at Paloma. Paloma looked at the ledger.

"Grella Moss," Paloma read aloud, and the ledger helpfully supplied the name, because three days ago Paloma had written it there himself, inventing a widow out of whole cloth because the lantern had come from no widow at all but from a frightened boy of nineteen who'd traded it for bread and hadn't known what he was carrying.

That was the trouble with the Guild's stock. Half of it was stolen from people who didn't know they were gifted, and the other half was hidden from people who would burn them for it. And now all of it sat under a moth-winged man's spectacles while the crown's hunt gathered speed outside the walls.

Quillon wrote something in his own book — a slim black volume that made Paloma's skin prickle when he glanced at it, though he couldn't have said why. "Grella Moss. Address?"

"Tallow Row, the blue door past the chandler's."

"There is no blue door past the chandler's," Quillon said pleasantly. "I walked Tallow Row this morning. There is a green door, a red door, and a doorway where a house burned down two winters ago."

The silence in the vault had texture. Brack had stopped sweating and started trembling. Somewhere above them, a porter dropped a crate and swore.

Paloma felt the moment tilt, the way a table tilts when everyone leans at once.

* * *

Here is what you must understand about Paloma Coldharbour's gift, which even he understood only dimly, the way a fish might understand water:

Names were not labels. Names were *handles*. Everything that existed had one — not the name given at birth or written in a temple register, but the true one underneath, the word the thing would say if you asked it what it was. Most people went their whole lives without hearing it. Paloma had been hearing it since he was six years old, when he'd looked at a cracked bell in the temple yard and thought, unbidden, *your name is Hollowsong*, and the bell had rung though no one touched it.

He had spent twenty years learning not to listen. Listening left marks. And using it — actually reaching out and taking hold of a thing by its true name — that was different altogether. That was not listening. That was *spending*.

Because names were also debts. To speak a thing's true name was to borrow against yourself. The gift took payment. He had learned this young and painfully, and he had built his whole quiet invisible life on the principle that he would never, ever spend.

In the vault, with Quillon's pen scratching and Brack dissolving beside him, Paloma reached into his own memory and found the entry he'd written three days ago. *Grella Moss, Tallow Row.* A fiction. But fictions written in ink developed handles of their own; anything named enough became slightly real. The entry wanted to be true. It had been waiting three days for permission.

All Paloma had to do was give it.

He put his hand flat on the ledger page, as if steadying it, and spoke — barely a breath, less than a breath, a shape of sound that wasn't a word so much as the space where a word lived: *be true.*

The ledger drank it.

So did something else. Paloma felt the cost arrive the way you feel a tooth pulled — not immediately, but as a sudden strange lightness, a gap where weight used to be. He blinked. The vault was the same. Quillon was the same. But somewhere behind his eyes, a room had gone dark. Something had been in that room. He could see the shape of the absence, the dust-outline where a memory had stood, and he could not — could not —

"What was it?" he whispered, before he could stop himself.

"Pardon?" said Quillon.

"Nothing, master. Forgive me. A cough."

Quillon regarded him over the pinched spectacles. Then he wrote *Grella Moss, Tallow Row, the blue door past the chandler's* in his black book, closed it, and moved on to item forty-two.

Later, walking home through streets gone strange with dread, Paloma tried to find what he'd lost. He inventoried himself the way he inventoried stock. His mother's face — there. The smell of the temple incense — there. Brother Cassian's habit of humming when he copied psalms — there. The taste of river water in summer, the weight of his first pen, the exact words of the debt-bond he'd signed at fourteen —

There. All there. Everything accounted for.

Except that he kept returning to a gap, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth. He knew something had been taken. He simply couldn't know what. The gift had taken its payment from the one account he could never audit: the memory of what it had taken.

*That,* he thought, standing very still in the middle of Bellows Street while carts rattled past him, *is how it gets you. Not all at once. Entry by entry.*

* * *

By evening the inspection had reached the upper archive, and Paloma had spent four more times.

He hated himself for how easy it became. The third time, when Quillon questioned the provenance of a compass that always pointed at whoever held it (a gift-object, dangerous, catalogued as a mere curiosity), Paloma forged a dead merchant's history in half a breath and felt only a small cold click behind his eyes, like a coin dropping into a deep well. The fourth time he didn't even flinch. That frightened him more than the loss itself.

"You're good at this," Juniper said.

He nearly dropped his pen. She was sitting in the shadow between two archive shelves, cross-legged, soot-streaked, eating an apple she had absolutely stolen, watching him with the unblinking attention of a cat that has decided you are weather.

"You can't be up here," he hissed. "The appraiser is two rooms away. If—"

"He doesn't look at shelves," Juniper said. "He looks at books. Nobody looks at shelves." She took another bite. "I said you're good at lying."

"I'm not lying."

"You're making things true." She said it simply, without judgment, the way she'd say *you're making bread* or *you're making a mess*. "The candles do it when I'm near. They lean. They want to be closer to what I am, so they stop being quite what they were." She shrugged. "Everything costs. The candles get tired faster after. Didn't anyone tell you?"

"No," Paloma said. "No one told me. No one ever told me anything. I found all of it alone, in the dark, at six years old, thinking I was cursed."

Juniper considered him. "You're not cursed," she decided. "Cursed means it wants you dead. This just wants *paid*." She finished the apple and set the core neatly on the shelf, which was the kind of small tidy gesture that never failed to unsettle him, coming from a child who slept in rafters. "What did it take today? From you?"

"I don't know."

"That's the worst part, isn't it." Not a question. She said it with the flat certainty of someone who had paid prices herself, many times, in currencies he didn't want to imagine. "You can feel the hole but not the shape of it. Like losing a tooth in your sleep."

"Yes," Paloma said quietly. "Exactly like that."

She nodded once, satisfied, and vanished back between the shelves like smoke returning to a chimney, and Paloma sat with his ledgers and his four cold clicks behind his eyes and understood that he had just received advice about his own soul from a nine-year-old thief, and that it was better advice than the temple had given him in twenty years.

* * *

The fifth time came at dusk, and it was different.

Quillon saved it for last. The upper archive, the restricted stacks, the shelf where the Guild kept items pending appraisal — and among them, catalogued as *unremarkable hearthstone, provenance unknown*, the little lump of charred wood that Juniper had carried into the building clutched in both fists like a heart she'd been born holding.

Paloma saw Quillon reach for it and felt the world go white at the edges.

"Master Quillon." His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "That piece is scheduled for disposal. Hearth refuse. It's on tomorrow's list."

"It's on my list today." Quillon turned the stone over in his dry little hands. "Curious material. It's warm. Has been warm, I'd wager, for some time." He produced a lens from his coat — brass-rimmed, older than he was — and held it up. Through the lens, Paloma knew, the stone would show what it truly was: not hearthstone at all but a seed. An ember's shed skin, the place where a gift had rooted before finding a better host. The crown's appraisers could read such things. The crown's catchers could follow them.

If Quillon wrote that stone into his black book, by week's end there would be men with nets in the archive, and they would not be looking for hearthstones.

"Provenance," Quillon said again. "Where did this come from?"

And here was the trap inside the trap. Paloma could spend again — make the entry true, forge a history, pay another coin from an account he couldn't audit. Or he could refuse, and watch the stone enter the crown's ledger, and follow the thread backward to a girl currently sleeping in his ceiling beams.

But the stone was not a fiction. It was not an entry he had written. It had a true name already, an old one, and true names did not take kindly to being rewritten. To force a lie onto something real — that was not spending. That was counterfeiting. And counterfeiters, he suspected, paid in larger denominations.

He did it anyway.

He laid his hand on the archive shelf, and instead of borrowing, he *wrote*: he took the stone's true name — he heard it, a low crackling syllable like a fire settling — and he bent it, gently, terribly, whispering over it the shape of an ordinary life. *You were a hearthstone. You warmed a kitchen. You cooled. You mean nothing.*

The stone believed him. He felt it believe him, a small surrender traveling up his arm.

And the price came down like a hammer.

Not a click this time. A *falling*. Paloma gripped the shelf as a whole season of his life went dark — he could feel the borders of it, spring into summer, some year he couldn't name, a corridor of days simply *gone*, and worse than the days was whatever had happened in them, some event large enough that its absence left a crater. People had been in that crater. He could almost see the outlines of them, standing in the dark where his memory used to be, mouths moving, saying something important, something he had needed —

"Coldharbour?" Quillon's voice, distant. "You've gone grey."

"Bad air in the stacks, master. Happens in warm weather."

Quillon studied him a moment longer than comfort allowed. Then he wrote *hearthstone, valueless, recommend disposal* in the black book, replaced the lens, and snapped the book shut. "Your entries are unusually clean, scribe," he said, gathering his things. "Unusually clean. In thirty years of inspections I have rarely seen a ledger with so few errors." He smiled, and it was a kind smile, and somehow that was the worst thing yet. "Almost as if the record itself wanted to cooperate."

He left. The gates boomed shut behind him. The Guild exhaled.

* * *

Paloma did not exhale. He climbed the archive stairs on legs that belonged to someone else, and found Juniper exactly where he knew she'd be, wedged in the rafters above the restricted stacks, watching him with those ember-lit eyes.

"Well?" she said.

"It's done. He thinks it's hearthstone. By tomorrow it goes in the disposal bin, and we fish it out, and no one follows it here." He sat down hard against a shelf. His hands had started shaking again — the old tremor, the one he hid, back with a vengeance. "It worked."

"But."

"But I think I lost a year of my life." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "A whole year. Spring into summer. I can't even find which year. There's a hole in me shaped like something that mattered, and I'll walk past it every day for the rest of my life and never know what I'm missing."

Juniper climbed down — she moved through rafters the way other children moved through doorways — and crouched in front of him, studying his face with an intensity that should have been unnerving and was instead, horribly, comforting.

"Show me your hands," she said.

"Why?"

"Because when the candles lean toward me, they flicker first. Before they move. The flicker tells me what they're afraid of losing." She took his hands without asking, turned them palm-up, and frowned at them as though reading a ledger of her own. Whatever she saw there made her go still.

"What?" Paloma said. "What do you see?"

Juniper looked up at him, and for the first time since he'd dragged her out of the catchers' path, she looked afraid — not of him, he realized. *For* him.

"There's a hole in you," she said, "and it isn't empty. Something's standing in it. Something the crown's man left behind when he looked at you too long." Her grip tightened. "Scribe, I think you didn't just pay a memory tonight. I think you bought something instead. And I don't think it's yours."

Far below them, in the locked vault where the disposed items waited for morning, the hearthstone that was not a hearthstone began, very softly, to glow.
