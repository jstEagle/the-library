## 8. Brother Cassian's Silence

The temple of Saint Vellum-and-Ash — its true name was something older and less flattering, but the founders had paid a scribe to improve it — woke Paloma at the fifth bell with the smell of tallow and wet stone.

He had not slept. He had tried. He had lain in his narrow cell above the scriptorium with his eyes shut and his hands folded like a good debtor's hands, and all the while his mind kept returning to the ledger in the Guild archive, to the entry that had written itself in his own hand while he stood there doing nothing at all.

*The crown is hungry. Do not let them count what you love.*

He had copied it out three times since, on scraps of paper he then burned in the cell's little brazier, watching the words curl and blacken as if they resented leaving the world. Each time he burned one, he wrote it again from memory, because some part of him did not trust fire to be the end of it. Words that remembered themselves were not the kind of words you could safely destroy.

At the fifth bell he gave up, dressed, and went down to the scriptorium, because routine was the only rope he had ever trusted to hold his weight.

Brother Cassian was already there. Cassian was always already there; Paloma had privately concluded years ago that the old man did not sleep so much as pause between sentences. He sat at the head table with a pot of ink the color of dried blood and a quill worn down to a stub, correcting a novice's transcription of the Alms Rites with the patience of a man defusing something explosive.

"You look," Cassian said without looking up, "like a man who has been reading other people's mail."

"I look like a man who didn't sleep."

"That too." Cassian set down the quill. "Sit. There's bread."

There was always bread. It was stale and dense enough to serve as a doorstop, and Paloma had eaten it every morning for eleven years, since the day the temple had taken his debt and, with it, everything else. He sat. He tore the bread. He did not eat it.

Cassian watched him not eat for a while, which was a skill the old monk had perfected over decades: watching without pressure, the way water watches stone.

"The Guild sent word last night," Cassian said. "Veyra's office. They want your appraisal hours moved up. The crown's appraiser arrives within the week, apparently, and she wants her books spotless before he breathes on them."

"Of course she does."

"There was also," Cassian said, in the tone of a man setting down a second, heavier object, "a request attached. That the temple confirm your fitness for continued service. A standard formality."

Paloma's stomach performed a slow, cold revolution. "And?"

"And I have not yet answered it." Cassian rose, joints announcing themselves like a ship in weather, and crossed to the scriptorium's great cabinet — the locked one, the one Paloma had never once seen opened in eleven years. From somewhere in the folds of his habit he produced a key that looked older than the lock.

"Come here, boy. And bring your bread. This may take some chewing."

* * *

The inside of the cabinet smelled of cedar and dust and, faintly, of smoke — as though something had been burned in there long ago and the wood had never quite forgiven it. Cassian lifted out a stack of folios bound in grey leather, each spine stamped with the temple's seal and a year. Eleven of them. One for every year of Paloma's bond.

"What are these?"

"Annual reports," Cassian said. "Every bonded servant of the temple is assessed yearly. Conduct, diligence, and — this is the part nobody reads anymore, because nobody believes in it anymore — *signs*. Any indication of unregistered gift." He laid the folios on the table in a row, oldest to newest. "You've been assessed eleven times, Paloma Coldharbour. You have been found exemplary eleven times. Diligent. Obedient. Unremarkable in every particular that matters."

Something in the way he said *unremarkable* made Paloma's skin prickle. "That's — accurate. I am unremarkable."

"Yes," Cassian said mildly. "It's remarkable how well you manage it."

He opened the first folio. The page was covered in Cassian's cramped, precise hand — assessments, observations, the small bureaucratic poetry of institutional surveillance. *Subject shows unusual facility with transcription. Memorizes documents after single reading. Suggests nothing. Recommend continued observation.*

"Suggests nothing," Paloma read aloud. His voice came out strange.

"Because it suggested nothing," Cassian said. "To anyone who wasn't looking. But I was looking. I'd been looking since you were nine years old and you corrected the Abbot's recitation of the Litany of Debts by writing the true version in the margin of his copy — including the three lines the Church had paid a scribe to remove two hundred years ago."

Paloma's mouth went dry. He remembered that. He remembered being nine, and terrified, and certain he would be beaten or worse, and the Abbot had simply frowned at the margin and said *sloppy sourcing* and moved on.

"I thought —"

"You thought you got lucky."

"I thought I got lucky."

"You thought wrong." Cassian turned pages. Year by year, the reports marched past: *No signs. No signs. No indication of gift. Subject remains unremarkable.* And then, in the fourth year, a different line, in slightly smaller handwriting, as though even the ink had been asked to keep its voice down:

*Reviewed subject's transcription of the Salt Road toll records. Three figures altered from source. Alterations are correct. Source was wrong. Have destroyed the source. Recommend no further inquiry.*

"You falsified my report," Paloma said. The words felt enormous in his mouth. "You falsified eleven years of reports. That's — Brother Cassian, that's heresy. That's a hanging offense. If the diocese ever audited —"

"If the diocese ever audited, we would both hang, yes." Cassian closed the last folio with a soft, final sound, like a book deciding to be finished. "Though I confess I've always assumed they'd burn us. The Church is traditional about these things."

"Why?" It came out almost a whisper. "Why would you do that? For me?"

Cassian looked at him for a long moment. In the grey morning light through the scriptorium's high windows, the old man's face was all planes and shadows, a map of a country Paloma had lived beside for eleven years without once thinking to explore.

"Sit down properly," Cassian said. "Eat your bread. I'm going to tell you about a girl named Maren."

* * *

"Maren was a novice when I was a novitiate," Cassian said. "Which makes her older than me, which makes her dead longer than you've been alive, so mind your tone when you think about her."

They were still at the head table. The novices would arrive at the seventh bell; the light had shifted from grey to a thin, apologetic gold. Cassian had poured himself a cup of the temple's terrible wine, and after a moment's visible internal negotiation, poured a second cup and pushed it across the table to Paloma.

"She had a gift," Cassian went on. "Small one. The kind the old texts called a *hearth-gift* — she could make things last. Milk, bread, candles, medicine. Things that should have spoiled held. She was the reason our infirmary lost half as many patients as any temple in the province, though of course the credit went to Saint Ferrous and his blessed ointments." His mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "She was careful. She was careful the way you're careful. She hid it for six years."

"What happened?"

"The famine happened. Or rather, the famine ended, very suddenly, and everyone praised the King's granaries, and Maren got careless with relief." Cassian drank. "She stretched the bread ration for the whole novitiate during the worst month. Four hundred people ate bread that should have fed two hundred, and no one questioned a miracle that convenient except one junior assessor who was ambitious and attentive and noticed that Sister Maren's pantry never ran empty."

Paloma set down the cup. His hands were steady, which surprised him. "They took her."

"They took her. Quietly. There was no hunt then — the hunts came later, when the crown learned to be afraid instead of merely greedy. Then it was just an assessment. A formality. She was conveyed to the capital for *evaluation* and the temple received a letter thanking us for our cooperation in the matter of Sister Maren's *reassignment*." Cassian's voice did not change at all, which was somehow worse than if it had broken. "I was nineteen. I had known her four years. She taught me my letters' true weights — how a word sits differently on a page depending on what it costs the writer. And I did nothing. I watched them put her in the cart and I went back inside and I transcribed the Alms Rites, because that was what I was for."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below them, the temple kitchens clattered into their morning argument with itself.

"So when a nine-year-old boy corrected the Abbot's litany in his own hand," Cassian said, "and I saw what that hand could do — I made a decision. Not a noble one. Don't mistake it for nobility. I decided I was not going to watch another cart leave. That's all. It isn't courage. It's just a man refusing, year after year, to repeat himself."

Paloma found that his eyes were burning. He stared hard at the wine until it behaved.

"Eleven years," he managed. "You've been lying to the diocese for eleven years. For me. And you never said anything."

"What was there to say?" Cassian shrugged. "'I know your secret, boy, and now you know mine'? Secrets shared are just debts with better manners. You had enough debts." He tapped the row of folios. "Besides. Telling you would have changed nothing except how carefully you slept."

"It would have changed everything," Paloma said. "I have spent eleven years waiting to be found out. Every single day. I balance impossible accounts for a Guild that sells people, and I flinch when ink moves near me, and I have never once — not once — told another living soul what I can do, because I believed that if anyone knew, I would end up in a cart." He laughed, and it came out cracked. "And the whole time, the one man whose job it was to report me was standing between me and the cart, and he never — you never *said* —"

"No," Cassian agreed. "I never said."

"Why not?"

"Because you didn't need protecting from yourself, boy. You needed protecting from everyone else, and that work is done best in silence." Cassian leaned forward, and for the first time in eleven years, Paloma saw the old man look tired — truly tired, tired down to the foundation stones. "But that was before this week. Before the proclamation. Before Veyra started selling names." He paused. "Yes, I know about that too. The Guild rents our courier system; you'd be astonished what crosses this desk. Silence is a currency, Paloma, and I have been rich in it for a long time. But the market's changing. When the criers start naming the gifted street by street, my little lies won't hold. An audit is coming — a real one, crown-sanctioned, with the appraiser's seal on it. And when they open these folios, they will find either a scribe with a hidden gift, or an old monk guilty of eleven years of falsified reports. Possibly both. Almost certainly both."

Paloma's blood went cold in a new way. "Then burn them. Burn the folios, forge clean ones — you've done it before, you said —"

"I said I destroyed a *source*." Cassian shook his head. "A toll record. A scrap. These are eleven years of institutional memory under seal. If they go missing, that's not sloppiness, that's evidence of concealment, and it hangs me twice as high. No. There's exactly one clean way through this, and it's the one I've been avoiding for eleven years because it puts the weight on your shoulders instead of mine."

"Tell me."

"The appraiser comes within the week. When he comes, he'll want to assess the temple's bonded staff personally — it's in the new writ, I've read it. Standard gift-detection. Touch the subject, read the signature, move along." Cassian's eyes were steady and terrible. "Unless the subject's signature has already been assessed and cleared by a certified reader of record. Which I am. Was. My certification lapsed the year Maren was taken, for reasons I trust you can now infer."

Understanding arrived in Paloma's chest like cold water finding a crack. "You want me to use it. My gift. On the appraiser. To write myself clear."

"I want you to do what you've been doing your whole life," Cassian said, "but this time, deliberately, and knowing the price. Because there is a price — don't pretend you don't know. You've been paying it in installments since you were nine. Little memories. Little losses. The taste of your mother's cooking. The name of the village you were born in. Shall I go on?"

Paloma's throat closed. Because Cassian was right, and he had never said it aloud, not even to himself in the dark: the gift took payment. Every true name spoken, every false entry made real, and something small went missing from him — not gone, exactly, but *unowned*, like a room in his own house he could no longer enter. He had told himself for years that it was ordinary forgetting. Everyone forgot. Everyone lost things.

But he had stopped being able to remember his mother's face, and he was twenty-six.

"How do you know that?" he whispered. "How could you possibly know that?"

"Because Maren told me," Cassian said simply. "The night before the cart came. She said, 'Every miracle is a loan, Cassian, and the interest is yourself.' She smiled when she said it. She wasn't afraid. That was the worst part — she'd made her peace, and I hadn't, and I have spent fifty years not making mine." He straightened. "So. Here is what I offer, and hear all of it before you answer. I will certify your assessment myself, tonight, using my lapsed credentials, and pray the forgery holds. You will not touch the appraiser with your gift. You will not pay interest you cannot afford. And in exchange —"

"In exchange?"

"In exchange, you stop pretending you're alone in this." Cassian's voice roughened, just barely, just at the edges. "Whatever Veyra has you hiding in those archives — and don't insult us both by denying there's something; you've had the look of a man harboring a secret since Tuesday — whatever it is, when it goes wrong, and it will, you come to me. Not the Guild. Me. I'm old, I'm expendable, and I have been practicing silence for fifty years. Let me finally be loud about something."

Paloma sat with it. The bread sat untouched. The wine sat untouched. Outside, Coldharbour's bells began the quarter-hour, and beneath their familiar bronze voices he could almost hear the other bells — the ones that had tolled the proclamation, the ones that were still tolling, far off, in the parts of the city where the catchers worked.

Found family, he thought — though he didn't have the phrase yet, wouldn't have the phrase for months. What he had was this: the sudden, disorienting discovery that the floor he had stood on alone for eleven years had, the whole time, been held up by someone else's hands.

"Cassian," he said. "Her name. Maren. Is it — is it anywhere? A grave, a record, anything?"

The old man was quiet for a moment. "There's a list," he said at last. "Kept by a catcher who quit the trade. Names of everyone taken, going back decades. I've heard rumors of it. Never found it." He met Paloma's eyes. "Why?"

"Because if we survive what's coming," Paloma said slowly, "someone should read those names back. Properly. With the true weight of them. It's what I'm for."

For the first time in eleven years, Brother Cassian smiled with his whole face, and it transformed him into someone Paloma felt he should have known all along.

* * *

They were still at the table when the novice on kitchen duty came pounding up the stairs two steps at a time, breathless, holding a folded paper bearing the wax seal of the Guild of Impossible Trades.

"For Brother Paloma. The mistress says come at once." The boy swallowed. "She says — she says the inventory you flagged is asking questions. And there's a crown rider at the gate asking the same ones."

Paloma was moving before the sentence finished. At the door he stopped, turned back, and found Cassian already rising, already reaching for his coat, as though he had expected nothing else.

"Together, then," the old man said.

"Together," Paloma said, and meant it as a vow, and did not yet know what it would cost.
