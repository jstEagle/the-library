## 9. The King's Face

The summons came on a Tuesday, which Paloma would later decide was the crown's idea of a joke.

*By order of His Majesty King Aldous the Third of that name, all licensed trades of the Guild of Impossible Trades shall present their ledgers for royal inspection at the Autumn Audience, in the great square before the Palace of Bells.*

He read it three times at his desk in the Guild counting-house, while outside the window the criers' bells still rang from the morning proclamation and the city went about its business with the careful brightness of people pretending not to be afraid.

"Royal inspection," said Veyra, who had appeared at his elbow the way she always did, without sound, without warning, smelling of clove oil and ledger dust. "You know what that means."

"That we're to be robbed politely?"

"It means every scribe, every clerk, every quill-holder in this building is expected to attend. The crown likes an audience. It likes to *see* its subjects seeing it." She tapped the proclamation with one lacquered nail. "And it means the appraiser will be there. Master Quillon. He'll want to look at faces while he looks at ledgers."

Paloma kept his eyes on the page. "I'm a debt-bond scribe, mistress. My face is the least interesting thing I own."

"Mm." She looked at him a moment longer than was comfortable — Veyra had a gift for making silence into an invoice — and then swept away toward her office, calling for someone named Osk to bring her the salt-road tariffs.

Paloma sat very still until she was gone.

The trouble was that he could not refuse to go. Debt-bonded servants of the temple were property with paperwork, and paperwork was required to attend royal audiences; he had entered it himself, years ago, in the margin of some forgotten register: *attendance at civic observances, mandatory, non-negotiable.* He had written the law that now owned him. There was a lesson in that, if he'd been in the mood to learn it.

The other trouble — the one he did not let himself think about while the sun was up — was Juniper.

She was still listed in the Guild inventory as *one (1) unappraised minor, soot-affected, pending valuation*, which meant she slept in the archive loft behind the false shelf of tax records from the drowned parishes, ate from the stores Paloma skimmed with arithmetic so clean no auditor had ever caught it, and spent her days learning, under his reluctant instruction, how to be invisible. She was terrible at it. She moved like a cat burglar and breathed like a bellows, and twice this week the candles in the reading room had leaned toward her like sunflowers greeting dawn.

"You have to come," she'd said last night, perched on the edge of his desk with her heels drumming the drawer where he kept the good ink. "To the audience thing."

"I have to go. You don't."

"But what if—" She stopped. Drummed harder. The ember in her made her restless the way weather makes dogs restless; he'd learned to read it in the twitch of her fingers, the way firelight found her face even in rooms with no fire. "What if they take me while you're gone."

"They won't. You're inventory. Inventory doesn't get taken; it gets *appraised*, and the appraisal isn't scheduled till—"

"What if the schedule changes," she said, flat, in the voice of a child who had been scheduled before, and Paloma had put down his pen and looked at her properly, and hated himself a little for what he saw there: not fear exactly, but the practiced shape of it, worn smooth by use.

So here was the arrangement, made over cold plum tea at midnight: Juniper would come to the audience too, folded into the Guild's contingent as a porter's child, hood up, hands gloved — gloves were Veyra's idea, sold to him at interest, everything was sold at interest — because a crowd was the best hiding place in Coldharbour, and because Paloma could not bear, though he'd have bitten off his tongue before admitting it, to leave her alone in a building full of catchers.

*Reluctant recognition of himself in her.* That was the phrase Brother Cassian had used once, gently, and Paloma had thrown a blotter at him.

---

The Autumn Audience happened in the great square before the Palace of Bells, on a day so clear and cold that the palace's hundred spires looked cut from tin. The square held perhaps eight thousand people when it was full, and it was full by midmorning: Guild contingents in their badges of trade, temple orders in grey and gold, merchants, fishwives, children on shoulders, the whole churning body of Coldharbour pressed together and exhaling steam like one enormous patient animal.

Paloma stood in the Guild block between a tallow-factor named Hesk and a boy of maybe fourteen whose job was to hold the Guild banner and who was doing it badly. Juniper was three rows back with the porters, hood up, gloved, and every few minutes Paloma felt a small tug at the edge of his awareness — not his gift, just his nerves — confirming she was still there. Still there. Still there.

The bells began at noon.

They rang from every tower in the capital, and the sound came down like weather, and the crowd hushed the way water hushes when it freezes. Then the gates of the palace opened, and the procession came out.

Paloma had seen the king before — everyone in Coldharbour had seen the king before, at coronation anniversaries and famine thanksgivings and the annual Blessing of the Ledger, which was exactly as thrilling as it sounded. Aldous the Third was famous for looking young. Forty years on the throne and the man had the face of someone thirty-five; the ballads said it was piety, the pamphlets said it was sorcery, the taverns said it was good living, and nobody said anything too loudly either way.

The procession crossed the square. Trumpets. The Guard in white and ash-grey. The court in its winter silks. And then the king himself, riding a pale horse at walking pace, close enough to the crowd line that a child near the barrier could have touched his stirrup if the Guard hadn't been so thick.

Paloma wasn't trying to use his gift.

That mattered to him later, and he clung to it later, the way a drowning man clings to the fact that he never meant to fall in the river. He was standing in a crowd, bored and frightened in equal measure, doing what every scribe does automatically, everywhere, forever: reading. His eyes fell on the king's face the way they'd fall on any text placed in front of them.

And the true name of King Aldous rose up off him like heat off a road in summer.

It should not have worked at that distance. True-name sight needed proximity, or ink, or something of the person — a signature, a lock of hair, a debt signed in their hand. At twenty paces through a crowd, through the noise of ten thousand people, it should have been nothing but a face, a stranger's face, a famous face.

But it wasn't nothing.

What Paloma saw — what poured into him in the space between one trumpet note and the next — was a name written in the wrong direction. Every true name he had ever read ran *from* the world *into* the person: this is your mother's love, this is your craft, this is your grief, these are the threads that tie you to things. A person's name was a knot, and the threads went outward, to other people, other places, other years.

The king's name ran inward only. All thread, no knot. It went down and down into him and did not come out the other side, and at the bottom of it — Paloma's stomach turned over slowly, like a boat rolling in deep water — at the bottom of it there was no Aldous. No man. No history, no hunger for breakfast, no fondness for horses or hatred of rain. There was a shape wearing the name the way a glove wears a hand, and inside the shape there was appetite, vast and old and *patient*, coiled around itself like a tapeworm in a gut, and it was —

It was *looking back at him.*

Paloma would swear this until the day he died, and part of him did die that day, quietly, in the crowd: across twenty paces, above the heads of the Guard, the king's eyes left the middle distance where kings keep their eyes and settled on Paloma Coldharbour, debt-bonded scribe of the Guild of Impossible Trades, and the eyes were brown and mild and human and completely empty, and behind them something enormous turned over, the way a whale turns under a rowboat, considering.

The mouth smiled. The smile was perfect. It had been practiced for decades.

Then the procession moved on, the eyes slid away to the next section of crowd, and Paloma discovered that Hesk was holding him upright by the elbow and saying his name.

"—oma. Paloma. You've gone grey as porridge, lad. Breathe."

"I'm fine." His voice came out in pieces. He reassembled it. "Heat. The crowd."

It was freezing. Hesk looked at him the way men look at liars they've decided not to bother with, and released his elbow, and Paloma stood through the rest of the ceremony — the blessing, the proclamation of the harvest tithe, the announcement of the expanded *Register of Gifted Persons*, read aloud in a herald's bright cruel voice while the crowd stayed carefully silent — with his heart going like a trapped bird and one thought turning in him, smooth and terrible, over and over:

*Nothing behind the eyes. Nothing behind the eyes. Nothing behind the eyes but appetite wearing a man's shape.*

---

He didn't remember the walk back to the Guild house. He remembered fragments: Juniper's gloved hand finding his in the crowd and gripping hard, once, without looking at him, the way she did when she'd decided not to ask questions; the banner-boy fainting and the banner going down like a felled tree; the smell of roast chestnuts from a cart by the fountain, obscene and ordinary; the bells ringing the crowd out of the square, cheerful, oblivious, complicit.

Then he was in the counting-house, at his desk, with the door shut, and his hands were shaking so badly he couldn't unstopper the ink.

He tried to write it down. That was the instinct of thirty years — whatever is true, write it, because unwritten truth rots and lies ferment. But when he put pen to paper his hand wrote instead, small and neat, in his own handwriting, words he had not chosen:

*the king is a mouth*

He stared at it. Crossed it out. The crossing-out healed itself; the ink crawled back into formation.

*the king is a mouth and it has eaten the man*

"Stop," Paloma whispered, and pressed his palm flat over the page, and the trembling under his skin quieted, grudgingly, like a dog told to lie down. The ink stilled. He sat there a long time, breathing, listening to the counting-house sounds through the door — the scratch of other pens, Veyra's voice pricing somebody's misfortune, the ordinary machinery of the impossible.

A mouth. An old, patient mouth, wearing a king.

And the hunt — the Register, the catchers, the lists the Guild was selling — the hunt suddenly rearranged itself in his understanding with the horrible click of a lock accepting its key. The crown wasn't hunting the gifted out of fear. Fear didn't feed anything. But gifted souls — souls with true names, souls knotted tight to the world with strong bright thread — those would be *food*. Rich food. The hunt was a menu, and the Register was a shopping list, and every catcher in Coldharbour was a delivery boy for the thing on the throne.

Juniper. Brighter than anything the Guild had ever catalogued. A feast with legs.

He got up so fast his chair went over backward, and he was halfway to the door before he made himself stop, straighten his coat, and walk — walk, Paloma, *walk* — at the pace of a man with nothing worse than a headache.

She was in the archive loft, sitting cross-legged on the floor among the drowned-parish ledgers, feeding crumbs of biscuit to the candle flame, which leapt after each crumb like a tame fish. She looked up when he came in, and her face did the thing it did when she was pretending not to be glad to see him.

"You look bad," she said.

"I know."

"Did you see him? The king?"

"I saw him."

She waited. She was better at waiting than he was; she'd had more practice at being ignored by adults. When he didn't speak, she set down the biscuit and stood, and the candle flames in the loft all leaned toward her together, softly, like grass bending under wind, and she didn't even notice anymore, and that — that unconsciousness, that innocence about her own enormity — was the most frightening thing in the room.

"Juniper." His voice came out steadier than he deserved. "I need you to listen to me, and I need you not to ask why. From today, we don't separate in this building. Not for inventory counts, not for meals, not for anything. If I'm called somewhere, you come too. If you're summoned anywhere—" *which will happen,* said the cold voice in his head that did arithmetic, *it will happen within the month, the appraisal, the Register, the list* — "you tell me first. Always me first."

"Why?" she asked. Of course she asked why. She was feral, not obedient.

Because the king is dead, he thought. Because the thing wearing him looked at me across a crowd and considered me the way a man considers an oyster. Because you are the brightest burning thing in this kingdom and the throne is hungry, and I have read enough ledgers to know that when the accounts don't balance, someone is being robbed.

"Because I said so," he said, "and because I'm the one who signs for you," which was a lie built on a lie, and she knew it, and she looked at him for a long moment with those ember-lit eyes that saw far too much—

—and then, downstairs, the counting-house bell rang: the visitor's bell, the heavy one, rung only for official callers.

Veyra's voice floated up the stairwell, silk-smooth and pitched just loud enough to carry: "Master Quillon! What an unexpected honor. And so soon after the Audience — the Guild is, of course, entirely at the crown's disposal."

Paloma went cold to the marrow.

"The crown," said a dry voice below, "wishes to begin the appraisals early. Beginning, I think, with the newest entries in your inventory."

Juniper looked at Paloma. Paloma looked at Juniper. Above her head, every candle in the archive flared at once — a brief, furious, terrified bloom of light — and went dark.
