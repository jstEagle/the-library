## 10. Veyra's Price

The summons came at the hour when the Guild's lamps burned lowest, when even the most diligent clerks had surrendered their stools to the dark and only the ledger-moths remained, worrying at the vellum spines like small pale sins.

*Come alone. Come now. — V.*

Paloma read the note twice, then a third time, because Guildmistress Veyra did not send notes. Veyra sent invoices. Veyra sent appraisers with soft boots and harder questions. A note, in her hand, on paper that smelled faintly of salt and iron — that was not business. That was appetite.

He looked across the counting room to where Juniper slept, curled in the gap between two archive shelves like a coal banked in ash. She had learned to sleep that way in her first week here: compact, silent, one hand fisted around a stub of candle she'd stolen from his desk and refused to surrender. Even asleep, the flame nearest her leaned toward her, a slow perpetual bow, as if the light itself had chosen a queen.

Three days since the appraiser. Three days since Paloma had falsified a crown inventory with words that were almost true names, and paid for it with a memory he could no longer find. He knew he'd lost something because there was a hole in him shaped like knowing — a smooth-walled absence he kept probing with his tongue, the way one worries a missing tooth. He had asked Cassian what he'd been like at nine years old. Cassian had wept quietly into his soup and said, *You were afraid of deep water,* and Paloma felt nothing at all, which was worse than grief.

So when the note came, he did not hesitate long. Hesitation was for people whose lives still appreciated in value.

"I'll be back before the bell," he told Juniper, who opened one eye, considered him, and closed it again. It was, from her, a gesture of enormous trust. He chose to be moved by it rather than terrified.

* * *

Veyra's office sat at the top of the Guildhall, above the vaults, above the appraisal floors, above all the rooms where impossible things were weighed and priced and sold. The stairs up were old, worn concave by generations of climbing feet, and Paloma noticed — because he noticed everything, it was his curse and his trade — that the banister had been freshly polished. Someone wanted this meeting to feel ceremonial.

She was standing at the window when he entered, looking down over Coldharbour's rooftops, where the proclamation bells still tolled their slow, sick rhythm. Hunt-bells. They'd been ringing for eleven days now, and the city had begun to arrange itself around them the way water arranges itself around a stone.

"You've been busy," Veyra said, without turning.

"Ledgers don't balance themselves."

"No." She turned then. She was not a large woman, but she occupied space the way a debt occupies a life — completely, patiently, with interest accruing. Her hair was iron-grey and pinned with a quill of blackened silver, an affectation she'd kept from her clerk days, though no one alive remembered her ever being a clerk. "They balance themselves through you. Which is why I find myself in the unusual position of owing you thanks, and hating every syllable of it."

"Then don't say it."

"Oh, I won't." She gestured to a chair. He didn't take it. She smiled, thin as a ledger line. "Sit or stand, scribe, but listen. The girl is costing me. Every day she hides in my archive is a day my catchers ask questions I can't answer, a day the crown's men grow more curious about why the Guild of Impossible Trades has suddenly acquired a stock item that eats candles and leaves soot on my shelves. You understand I cannot hold her forever out of charity. Charity is not a currency I deal in."

"I know," Paloma said. "I've read your rate cards."

Something flickered behind her eyes — annoyance, or perhaps respect. With Veyra they wore the same face.

"Then you know what comes next. Sooner or later, someone sells her. It will be me or it will be someone beneath me, and I would prefer—" she paused, choosing the word with a jeweler's care, "—I would prefer it be no one at all. For reasons of my own."

"And those reasons cost?"

"Everything costs." She crossed to her desk and unlocked a drawer he had never seen opened. From it she took a single sheet of paper, unsigned, sealed with grey wax. "There is a road out of Coldharbour. Salt Road, south through the marshes, past the toll-houses, to a port where ships still sail without asking passengers their names. I can put you both on it. Clean papers. A cart. An escort who asks nothing and sees less. By week's end you're salt-smell and rumor."

Paloma's heart did something complicated and painful. Escape. The word tasted like a fruit he'd stopped letting himself want years ago, back when the temple first bound his name into its books and taught him that freedom was a line item he couldn't afford. He made himself say, carefully:

"What does it cost?"

"There it is." Veyra almost smiled. "You always ask. That's why I keep you." She set the paper on the desk between them, flat, deliberate, like a card turned face-up. "One service. One appraisal. The Guild has been offered a commission — private, discreet, enormously lucrative — to value a certain object for certain interested parties. The object resists ordinary assessment. Ordinary appraisers come back wrong. Some don't come back at all."

"What object?"

"You already know."

His mouth had gone dry. "No."

"The crown," Veyra said simply. "Appraise the crown of Coldharbour, Paloma Coldharbour. Read it. Tell us what it is, what it wants, what it's worth. Do that, and the girl walks free, you walk free, papers clean, debts dissolved — including," she added, with surgical gentleness, "the temple's claim on your name. All of it. Closed accounts. A fresh page."

The room tilted very slightly. He gripped the chair-back. *Debts dissolved.* His whole life he had been owned in installments — his labor to the temple, his gift hidden in shame, his name held as collateral against a poverty he'd been born into and hadn't chosen. And here it was, the number every debtor dreams of: zero. Balance. Freedom.

"That's insane," he said. "Nobody reads the crown. The king wears it."

"The king," said Veyra, "is not the problem. The king hasn't been the problem for some time, if half of what crosses my desk is true. Interested parties want to know what the crown is *worth*. What it would fetch. Whether it could be bought, or broken, or bargained with. There are powers in this city, scribe, older than the palace, and they have money and patience and very poor intentions. If the Guild doesn't take this commission, someone else will — someone cruder, who'll tear the answer out of the crown's keeper with hot irons instead of reading it gently off the metal."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I sell the girl to whoever pays most, and I use the proceeds to buy my way clear of this mess, and you go back to balancing accounts until the hunt reaches your door. Because it will reach your door, Paloma. Your name is in temple records. Temple records are being copied for the crown as we speak. You have perhaps a month."

The bells tolled outside, distant and slow. In the silence between strokes, Paloma heard his own heartbeat, quick and shallow, doing arithmetic.

Here is what he told himself, in that moment, and believed: *This is cleverness. This is the trade working as it should. I give them one reading — one dangerous reading, yes, but readings are what I do — and in exchange I buy Juniper's life and my own. It is terrible, but it is the least terrible thing on offer, and the least terrible thing on offer is called a bargain.*

Here is what was also true, though he did not yet have the language for it: ambition had woken in him, quiet and shameful, coiled under the fear. *Debts dissolved.* Not just escape — erasure. A clean page. And underneath even that, something hungrier: the crown was the greatest text in the kingdom, the one book no reader had ever survived, and some cold library-corner of his soul wanted to open it. Wanted to know. Wanted, God help him, to be the one who finally read it.

"I need time," he said.

"You have until tomorrow's low bell." Veyra slid the contract toward him. It was blank except for a single line at the bottom, awaiting signature. "Sign tonight, and I add a clause: the girl stays under Guild protection until your return from the capital. Not sold, not traded, not 'misplaced.' Protected. My word on it, and my word, whatever else it is, is collateral I've never defaulted on."

"And after? Once I've read it?"

"After," Veyra said, "you tell me what it's worth, and I make you rich enough to never count another man's coins. Or I make you dead enough to stop asking questions. Depends entirely on what you find." She pushed the pen across the desk. It was black-lacquered, heavy, and its nib was silver — real silver, the kind used for signing things that mattered.

Paloma looked at the blank line for a long moment.

Somewhere below them, three floors down, a soot-streaked child slept between shelves with a stolen candle in her fist, trusting him in the only language she knew, which was the absence of running away.

"Protected," he said. "In writing. Specific wording. No substitutions, no reinterpretations, no acts of God or Guild."

"In writing," Veyra agreed, and for the first time that evening she looked genuinely pleased — the pleasure of a fisher watching the line go taut. "You negotiate well, scribe. It's almost a pity."

He signed.

The ink went down clean and black, and his hand did not tremble, and later — much later, in the marsh-dark of the Salt Road, with catchers' dogs baying somewhere behind them — he would lie awake trying to remember whether the trembling had started before the signature or after, and whether it mattered, and whether anything that happened next had been written the moment he picked up the pen.

For now, there was only this: the contract drying, Veyra rolling it with brisk efficiency, and her voice, light as a closing door:

"One condition of my own, since we're trading clauses. When you read the crown — and you will read it, you can't help yourself, readers never can — whatever you see, however bad it gets, you bring it back to me first. Not the temple. Not the palace. Me. Consider it professional courtesy." She smiled, and it did not reach anywhere near her eyes. "After all, I'm paying for the truth. I should get to hear it before anyone else does."

"Professional courtesy," Paloma repeated, hollow.

"Wonderful. We understand each other." She rang a small brass bell on her desk, and a servant appeared as if from the wall. "Escort our guest out. And Paloma—" he paused at the door, "—do sleep well tonight. Tomorrow you'll want your memory sharp. Every last piece of it."

He walked down through the sleeping Guildhall, past the dark counting room, past the archive shelves where Juniper breathed slow and even among the ledgers, and stood for a while in the doorway just watching her, this child he had traded a reading of the most dangerous object in the kingdom to protect, telling himself the arithmetic worked.

It was only much later — after the chapel, after the marshes, after Wren and the letters and the throne room — that he understood what Veyra had known all along, standing at her window while the hunt-bells tolled: that a man who has spent his whole life owned will sign almost anything that promises to unown him, and that the cruelest traps are not baited with threats.

They are baited with freedom.

* * *

He did not sleep well. Of course he did not sleep well.

He lay in his narrow cot in the scribes' dormitory and listened to the Guild breathe around him — snores, creaks, the settling of old timber — and tried to inventory himself the way he'd inventory any asset before a sale. What do I remember? What am I carrying? What might I be about to lose?

Names came easily: Cassian, who wept into soup and erased reports; Juniper, who bowed flames; Veyra, who smelled of salt and iron. Places: the archive, the counting room, the chapel steps where he'd first seen a crier nailed to the proclamation post by his own voice. Skills: ledgers, false entries, true names, the careful art of being invisible.

But when he reached for the shape of his childhood — the temple dormitories, the other novitiates, the taste of the porridge, the sound of the bells that weren't hunt-bells — he found the smooth-walled hole waiting, patient as interest. Something had lived there once. Something warm, maybe. Something with a face.

*Every last piece of it,* Veyra had said, and he'd thought she meant the crown's secrets.

He got up in the dark and went to the archive, because it was the only place in the building where lying felt honest. He knelt by Juniper's shelf-nest and watched her sleep, and after a while her eyes opened — she woke the way animals wake, all at once, no grogginess, no mercy — and regarded him without surprise.

"You smell like ink and bad decisions," she said. She was nine, maybe ten, and had the conversational grace of a dock cat.

"I signed something."

"To get us out?"

"To get us out."

"Did it work?"

"It'll work."

"Then why do you look like a man who lost?" She sat up, candle stub in her fist, and the nearest lamp — three shelves away — leaned toward her like a curious dog. "My ma used to say that. About the traders who came through. They'd smile and smile and walk away lighter than they came, and she'd say, *that one lost and doesn't know it yet.*"

"Your mother sounds like she'd have gotten along with my ledger."

"She's dead," Juniper said, with the flat economy of children stating facts adults flinch from. "The catchers got her in the spring. That's why I ran. That's why I'm here." She studied him with those ember-colored eyes, and for a moment something moved behind them that wasn't anger — something younger and more frightened, quickly buried. "Is she going to sell me anyway? The iron lady."

"Not while I'm balancing the books."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I have."

Juniper considered this, then lay back down, pulling a scrap of blanket over her shoulder. "Traders always say that too," she murmured, already sliding back toward sleep. "The ones who lose."

Paloma stayed kneeling in the dark a while longer, listening to her breathing even out, and let himself imagine — just for a moment, just as a luxury — the port at the end of the Salt Road. Salt wind. A ship that didn't ask names. Juniper on the deck eating something sweet, squinting at the horizon, alive. Himself beside her, nameless by choice instead of by debt, holding a pen with nothing to sign.

Then he put the picture away, carefully, the way he put away everything he couldn't afford, and went back to his cot, and did not sleep, and in the morning the low bell rang, and the trap — patient, gilded, smelling faintly of salt and iron — began to close.
