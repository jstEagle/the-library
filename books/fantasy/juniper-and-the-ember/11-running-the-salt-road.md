## 11. Running the Salt Road

**Paloma**

The salt marshes began where the cobbles gave up pretending.

One moment they were on the last hard road out of Coldharbour, and the next the ground went soft and grey underfoot, stitched with creeks that gleamed like spilled pewter. The wind came off the flats carrying a smell of brine and old fish and something older underneath — the smell of a place that had drowned once and never quite finished the argument. Paloma's boots, made for archive floors, sank to the ankle in peat with every step, and every step made a sound like a kiss being withdrawn.

Behind them, faint but persistent as toothache, came the belling of hounds.

"They're not dogs," Juniper said. She had said it three times now, each time with more certainty. She walked ahead of him, light as a heron, barefoot on ground that would have flayed his feet raw. "Dogs bark. That's singing."

Paloma listened. The sound carried across the water in long, rising notes, almost beautiful, which was somehow worse. "Catcher-hounds," he agreed. "The Guild breeds them. They don't follow scent." He hesitated, then decided she deserved the truth; lies were heavy things to carry through a marsh. "They follow gift. It burns brighter than blood does. To them you're not a girl running. You're a bonfire on a dark night."

Juniper stopped. For a moment he thought she might cry, and he braced himself for it — he had no idea what one did with a crying child, having been one only briefly and without witnesses — but instead she looked down at her own hands as though checking them for flames.

"Can I make it smaller?" she asked. Not *can I put it out*. Smaller. As if the ember were a thing she kept, the way Paloma kept his ink-stained cuffs and his careful, invisible life.

"I don't know," he admitted. "I've never — my gift doesn't work like yours. Mine is quiet. Yours is—" He gestured at the whole horizon, which seemed fair.

"Mine is loud," she said, with something that was almost pride and almost grief.

The hounds sang again, closer. Paloma did the arithmetic he was built for: distance, rate, the tide charts he'd memorized from a shipping ledger he'd balanced three years ago for a smuggler who'd paid in oysters. The tide would turn within the hour. The causeway to the chapel ruins — if Wren's directions held — lay across flats that would be underwater by dusk.

"We need to move faster," he said, "and quieter, and those are opposite instructions."

"Then we move fast and let them hear us coming," Juniper said, "and when they come, I burn them."

"No."

"You didn't even think about it."

"I thought about it very quickly," Paloma said. "The answer is still no. Fire draws the catchers' masters, not just their dogs. And—" he searched for the words, the ones that wouldn't be a lie and wouldn't be cruelty, "—and I've seen what happens to gifted children who burn things. They end up in ledgers. In my ledgers. With a line drawn through their names."

She stared at him. Wind flattened the marsh grass around them in silver waves. Somewhere out on the flats a curlew cried its two-note question.

"Your ledgers," she said finally. "You keep books of us."

"Someone has to count what's lost," Paloma said. "Otherwise the crown gets to pretend nobody was ever there at all."

Something shifted in her face — not softening exactly, more like a door left ajar where before there'd been a wall. Then the hounds sang again, much closer, and the door shut, and she ran.

* * *

**Juniper**

The ember woke up angry.

It always did when she ran. Running meant fear, and fear fed it, and it swelled against the inside of her ribs like bread rising, like a bird battering a window, like all the words she knew for *more*. *Let me,* it whispered, in the voice that wasn't a voice, the warmth behind her breastbone that had been with her longer than memory. *Let me out and we won't run anymore. Let me out and nothing will ever chase us again.*

"Shut up," Juniper muttered, splashing through a creek that came up to her knees.

The boy — Paloma, the ledger-man, the one who smelled of ink and fear and, underneath both, something steadier — glanced back at her. "Talking to me?"

"No."

He didn't ask. That was one thing she'd noticed about him in the two days since the stacks, since she'd watched candle flames bow toward her like courtiers and known the game was up. He noticed things and then he didn't say them. It was the opposite of everyone else in her life, who said everything and noticed nothing.

The trouble was that noticing didn't help. Because the ember had heard him too. It liked him. That was the horror of it. When Paloma talked — dry and quick and quiet, counting things, always counting — the ember settled a little, the way a cat settles near a warm stove. It wanted him. Everything warm belonged to it; that was its whole philosophy, delivered wordlessly, endlessly, like rain.

And Juniper was so tired of being rained on.

The marsh opened ahead into a great flat sheet of water, shallow as a puddle, wide as a sea, mirroring a sky gone the colour of old tin. On the far side, a smudge of stone: the ruin Wren had promised. Between here and there, half a mile of nothing to hide behind.

"The causeway," Paloma panted, catching up. "Submerged path. If we—"

The ember flared.

She felt it go before she could stop it — a gasp of heat down her arms, out through her palms, and the shallow water around her lit from beneath with a soft gold glow, spreading outward in rings, beautiful, catastrophic, a lantern lit in a country of darkness. Fish scattered. Her shadow burned huge and bright on the water.

For one heartbeat she thought: *oh.* It was the prettiest thing she'd ever done.

Then the hounds stopped singing.

And started howling.

"Run," Paloma said, and there was no dryness in it at all now, just a man's voice stripped to wire. "Run *now*."

They ran. Water flying, breath tearing, the glow dying off her skin in reluctant embers while behind them the singing-notes resolved into actual barking, close, so close, and Juniper understood with terrible clarity that she had just done the one thing she'd promised herself she'd never do, which was prove him right.

A net whistled past her ear and slapped into the water.

She didn't see who threw it. She saw Paloma see it — saw him grab her collar and haul her sideways off the causeway into deeper water, both of them going under in cold brown salt, and the world became muffled roaring and stinging eyes and his hand locked around hers, dragging her along the bottom toward a tangle of dead alder roots. They surfaced inside the root-mass, in an air pocket that smelled of rot and safety, pressed together like two pages of a closed book.

Through the lattice of branches: boots. Four pairs, wading. Chain-mail whispers. A voice saying, "Glow was here. Mark the spot. Tide'll cover it, but the master wants the crossing watched."

"Not tonight," another voice said. "Tide's turning. Nobody crosses till morning."

Boots squelched away. The hounds sang their disappointment out over the water, circling, patient, and Juniper realized her teeth were chattering and Paloma's hand was still around hers and neither of them had let go.

"Incredible," Paloma whispered. His voice shook. From cold, probably. Probably entirely from cold. "You are the single most expensive thing I have ever hidden."

That was when Juniper started laughing — silent, shaking, helpless laughter that hurt her ribs and steamed in the freezing air, because it was either laugh or scream, and screaming brought back the boots.

Paloma stared at her. Then, slowly, horribly, a snort escaped him, and then he was laughing too, both of them folded double in a hollow of dead wood while the tide crept up around their sanctuary and the hunters' lights bobbed away across the darkening marsh.

* * *

**Paloma**

They crossed at full dark, when the tide withdrew and left the flats gleaming under a thin moon, and reached the ruin with numb feet and no dignity whatsoever. There was no fire — fire was unthinkable — so they sheltered in the chapel's broken apse, wrapped in Wren's oilcloth, sharing body heat with the enthusiasm of two people pretending it was purely practical.

Which was when Paloma's stomach betrayed him. It growled, long and resonant, with the unmistakable authority of a Guild auditor entering a room.

Juniper's answering stomach growled louder.

"There was a farmstead," Paloma said, "back before the causeway. I saw orchards."

"It's midnight."

"Fruit doesn't keep office hours."

Getting there involved another quarter mile of sucking mud, and climbing a drystone wall that collapsed agreeably under Juniper like it had been waiting for someone lighter than a grown man, and then they were standing in an abandoned orchard gone semi-wild, trees leaning drunkly over each other, heavy with late plums gone soft and dark as bruises in the moonlight.

They ate like animals. Paloma was dimly aware of this and found he did not care. Juice ran down his chin; plum skins piled up between them; Juniper ate six, seven, eight fruits without pausing, and then, mid-ninth, stopped.

"What?" Paloma said.

"These are somebody's plums."

"It's an abandoned croft. Look — the roof's fallen in. Nobody's tended these trees in years."

"That's not what I mean." She turned a plum over in her fingers. "Somebody planted them. Somebody stood right here and pushed a seed into the dirt and expected to eat, later. Years later. That's — " she frowned, hunting the idea the way she hunted words, which was like watching someone catch fish with bare hands, "that's trusting a future you're not in yet."

Paloma sat back against the wall, plum juice sticky on his hands, and felt the sentence land somewhere under his ribs.

"Yes," he said. "That's rather the definition of hope. The agricultural kind, anyway."

"My ma planted things." Juniper spoke to the plum, not to him. "Beans. In a pot. In the window of the room we had. She said even if we got turned out, beans don't take up much room to carry." A pause. "We got turned out. I took the pot. I don't have it anymore."

The wind moved through the orchard, and the trees creaked, and far off the hounds sang once, faint, directionless, giving up the night.

"I had a friend at the temple," Paloma said. "Brother Cassian. He used to steal honey from the infirmary stores and swear he was medicinally depressed." Juniper's mouth twitched. "He'd have liked you. He likes anyone who steals well."

"Do you miss him?"

The question was so direct it went straight through every deflection Paloma owned. He considered lying, out of habit, and found the habit had slipped loose somewhere in the cold water.

"Yes," he said. "I've known him since I was younger than you. He's the only person who ever — " *erased my name from the reports*, he did not say, because some secrets weren't his alone anymore. "He's the reason I'm careful enough to be alive."

"He's why you hide me?"

"He's why I know hiding you is worth the interest."

Juniper ate the ninth plum. Then she did something extraordinary, which was to pick the best plum remaining on the lowest branch — fat, dark, perfect — and hold it out to him with both hands, formally, like a coronation.

"For the road," she said. "Don't eat it now. Eat it tomorrow when it's horrible and wet and you want to die. That's when it helps."

Paloma took it. His throat had gone inexplicably tight. "Thank you," he managed.

"And Paloma?" She was already scrambling back over the wall, a soot-streaked silhouette against the moonlit flats, and she spoke without turning around, which was perhaps the only way she could say it. "The glow. Tonight. I tried to make it small. I couldn't. But I tried."

"I know," he said. "I saw you try."

"That's not nothing," she said. "Right? That counts?"

"It counts," Paloma said. "In my ledgers, it counts double."

She grinned — he caught it only in profile, a flash of teeth and moonlight — and dropped down onto the causeway, and they walked back toward the ruined chapel side by side, two thieves heavy with stolen fruit, while behind them the tide came in over their footprints and erased the marsh's memory of them completely.

Neither of them saw the rider on the far ridge, motionless, watching the place where the glow had bloomed.

Neither of them knew that by morning, Veyra would know exactly where they slept.
