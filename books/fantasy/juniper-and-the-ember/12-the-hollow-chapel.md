## 12. The Hollow Chapel

The rain found them an hour past dark, and the chapel found them an hour after that.

It stood at the bend of the salt road where the marsh gave up trying to be land — a grey tooth of a building, roofless, its bell tower snapped off at the shoulder like a man who'd survived a hanging. Juniper saw it first. She always saw things first; the ember in her made her eyes hungry for shapes in the dark.

"House," she said.

"Chapel," Paloma corrected, and then felt foolish for correcting a child who'd slept in gutters while he'd slept in a cell with a lock he was allowed to open from the inside.

"It's a house if we live in it tonight."

He couldn't argue with the logic of it. He hadn't been able to argue with her logic since the salt road began, since she'd stolen plums from an orchard with the calm of a tax collector and handed him three without being asked, and something in his chest had quietly rearranged itself around the fact of that.

They went in through what had once been a door and was now a suggestion.

Inside, the chapel smelled of gull droppings and old salt and, beneath all of it, something older still — stone dust and tallow, the smell of a place that remembered being used. Rain came through the missing roof in long silver threads and struck the flagstones with a sound like slow applause. In one corner, half the ceiling had survived: a rib of vaulting arched over a stretch of dry floor, and someone — smugglers, maybe, or lovers, or both — had left a rusted brazier there years ago.

Juniper went straight to it, crouched, and held out her hand.

Paloma flinched. Old habit. But she didn't call fire the way the stories said gifted children did, all shriek and blaze. She just *asked*, in some wordless way, and the wet kindling in the brazier sighed and caught — a small flame, tidy as a candle, bowing slightly toward her fingers like a dog leaning into its master's knee.

"You could warm us from across the room," he said. "You've done it before."

"She likes to be close," Juniper said. Not *I*. *She.* He'd noticed that on the road. The child spoke about the ember the way other children spoke about a sibling they didn't entirely trust.

Paloma unpacked their meagre hoard — half a loaf gone hard, the plums' stones, Wren's parting gift of smoked fish wrapped in oilcloth. Wren had stayed behind at the tinker's camp, saying she'd follow by the coast path and meet them past the toll bridge, and every hour since, Paloma had caught himself listening for a one-handed whistle in the wind that never came.

He divided the fish. Juniper took her share, then looked at his, then looked at him, and pushed the larger piece back onto his side of the oilcloth without a word.

"I'm bigger than you," he said.

"You're sadder than me," she said. "Sad people need more."

He laughed before he could stop himself — a short, rusty sound, like a hinge in a house no one visited. Juniper watched him do it with the narrow, cataloguing attention she gave everything, as though laughter were another gift she was deciding whether to catalogue or steal.

* * *

Sleep would not come for him. It rarely did, but tonight the rain kept time against the stones and his mind kept worse time against itself — Veyra's price ticking in the back of his skull like interest accruing, Cassian's face when Paloma had fled the temple archives with a child's hand in his and a lifetime of caution burning behind him like a bridge.

So he sat up, and the chapel sat up around him, and that was when he first felt it.

Not heard. Not seen. *Felt* — the way you feel a word forming in your own mouth before you speak it. A pressure in the air, faint and rhythmic, under the rain. Under the rain and under the silence and under even the small fire's crackle.

His gift woke the way it always woke: not with light but with *attention*. The world sharpened into names. The rain named itself — water, falling, returning. The stones named themselves — patience, patience, patience. And the chapel —

The chapel had a name too, and the name was *grief*.

Paloma went very still.

He had spent his whole life keeping this gift folded small, tucked into the lining of himself like contraband. Reading true names was not a party trick. It was a door, and doors opened from both sides, and the Crown's catchers had a word for people who could hear what things called themselves: *inventory*.

But the chapel was empty except for a child and a fire, and the name pressed against him anyway, insistent, and he understood with a sinking, vertiginous clarity that this place had been holding its breath for a very long time.

"Juniper," he said softly.

She was asleep. Or had been. Her eyes opened all at once, no drowsy drift, the way animals wake. "What."

"The walls are remembering something." He heard how it sounded and grimaced. "That's not — I'm not mad. Or I am, but consistently."

"I believe you." She said it flatly, without wonder, which somehow made it easier to bear than awe would have. "The fire says old things happened here. She doesn't like the floor."

"The fire has opinions about the floor."

"The fire has opinions about everything. You get used to it." She crawled over to sit beside him, wrapping herself in the blanket they'd bought with Veyra's coin, which Paloma tried not to think of as Veyra's leash. "Show me."

"I don't know if I can show anyone. I've never—" He stopped. Reconsidered. His whole life he had treated the gift as a private shame, a tremor hidden in the hands. But the hands were already trembling now, and the names were already rising, and the chapel was already speaking. "Hold my wrist. If I go somewhere strange, pull me back. Hard."

"What happens if you go somewhere strange?"

"I stop knowing which century it is." He said it lightly. It wasn't light. Every reader of true names learned early that names were doors, and that sometimes the door swung the other way, and you fell through into whatever had last been said in a place with feeling enough to leave a mark.

Juniper took his wrist. Her grip was small and shockingly strong, and warm — warmer than skin should be, the ember lending its heat through her palm like a coin left in the sun.

Paloma closed his eyes and let the chapel speak.

* * *

It came to him not as pictures but as *names spoken aloud by voices that weren't there* — and the pictures arrived anyway, because human minds are traitors and furnish empty rooms whether invited or not.

He stood in the chapel and the chapel stood in another year. Candles everywhere, hundreds, the roof intact above them, the bell tower whole and ringing. The pews were full. Not with the faithful — he knew faith's posture when he saw it, had spent his boyhood among the bowed heads of the temple — these people sat rigid, terrified, *chosen*. Farmers in their funeral clothes. A fisherman still dripping. Three children holding hands so hard their knuckles had gone white.

And at the altar, three figures.

One knelt at the altar rail, hands flat on the stone, head bowed: a woman, old, her voice moving through the air like a plough through soil. Paloma's gift caught her name and it was less a name than a *function*: **the one who holds**. She was anchoring something. Holding a door shut with nothing but her own self, the way you hold a wound closed with your palms.

One stood at the pulpit, reading — not scripture, though the cadence was scriptural, the cadence of law and ledger. An old man with a scribe's ink-stains to the elbow. **The one who speaks**. His voice was doing the work of binding, each syllable a knot pulled tight.

And the third —

The third stood before the altar with her arms open, and she was young, and she was crying, and she was *burning*. Not consumed. Burning the way a lamp burns: steadily, willingly, giving light because giving light was what she was. **The one who burns**. And the fire coming off her was not going up toward heaven. It was going *inward*, down into the stone, down beneath the altar, into something buried there that drank and drank and drank.

Paloma tried to read the thing beneath the altar and his gift slid off it like a knife off glass. It had no name. That was the horror of it. Everything had a name — he had believed that the way other men believed in weather — and this thing beneath the altar had none, only appetite wearing the shape of a hollow, and the rite was not summoning it.

The rite was *feeding* it. Sealing it. Three names woven together into a lid, pressed down over a mouth.

The old woman who holds spoke a name Paloma couldn't keep hold of — it slipped from his mind like a fish, leaving only the sense of *someone loved, someone lost, given freely*. The old scribe spoke next, and his voice cracked on his own name, and Paloma felt the terrible arithmetic of it: to bind, you must pay, and the coin is yourself.

Then the young woman who burned opened her mouth, and what came out was not a scream.

It was a *yes*.

The vision broke like ice underfoot.

* * *

Paloma came back to himself on the flagstones with Juniper's hands fisted in his collar and her face an inch from his, and the fire in the brazier standing unnaturally tall, stretched toward him like a listener leaning in.

"Breathe," she ordered. "You went somewhere strange. You said you'd go somewhere strange but you went *very* somewhere strange and your eyes were wrong."

"Were they?"

"They were looking at the altar. There isn't an altar anymore. There's just a hole where the altar was." She let him go slowly, reluctantly, the way you release something you're afraid might break. "There was a man here. Before us. He was screaming. Then he stopped, and you stopped, and I didn't like either of those things."

"There was no man here." Paloma sat up. His joints ached as though he'd carried the vision physically, as though the centuries had weight. "There was a memory. The chapel kept it. Places keep what's done in them with enough feeling — grief keeps best, grief and joy, and this place is mostly grief." He looked toward the east end of the nave, where the flagstones were broken and blackened in a pattern too deliberate to be accident. Where the altar had been, there was indeed a hole — a socket in the floor, square-cut, ancient, empty.

Empty. Which meant whatever had been sealed beneath it was sealed elsewhere now.

Or loose.

"Three of them," he said aloud, because saying things aloud made them real and real things could be fought. "A rite with three parts. One to hold. One to speak. One to burn." His voice steadied as the scribe in him took over, the part of him that had spent ten years turning chaos into columns of figures. "They bound something here. Something with no name. And the binding cost them everything — I felt it. The holder gave a name she loved. The speaker gave his own. The burner—" He stopped. Looked at Juniper.

Juniper was looking at the hole in the floor, and the firelight moved in her eyes, and her expression was the expression of someone recognizing their own handwriting in an old book.

"She burned," Paloma finished quietly. "Willingly. To seal it. And it worked, and everyone forgot, because that's what victories do — they rot into stories and the stories rot into nothing, and the thing stays buried until—"

"Until someone digs it up," Juniper said.

"Until someone puts it on their head and calls it a crown."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere out in the marsh, a bird cried once and thought better of it.

Paloma got up, legs unsteady, and crossed to the altar socket. Kneeling beside it, he let the gift rise again — carefully this time, a cupped hand rather than an open door. The stones around the socket still held the echo of the rite, worn thin as an old coin, but legible to him the way ledgers were legible: not the words themselves, those had been spent, but the *structure*. The grammar of the working.

Three names. Freely given. Woven into a single cord, and the cord drawn tight around the nameless thing, and the ends — the ends had been cut and buried, one here, and—

He frowned. The third end. The rite's grammar trailed off into absence, a sentence with its final clause torn away. Two anchors here: the holder's and the speaker's, sunk into the chapel stone like posts. But the burner's portion had gone *elsewhere*. Carried away. Planted somewhere far from this place, deliberately, so that no enemy could undo the binding by undoing it all in one spot.

A map hidden in grief. That was what the chapel was. Not a ruin — a *keyhole*, waiting centuries for someone who could read it.

"Juniper," he said. "When we reach the capital. When we do whatever it is we're going to do about the king." He heard himself say *king* and not *the thing wearing the king*, and decided he'd earn that honesty later. "There will be a rite. There's always a rite — bindings can be loosened, that's the whole point of knots. And the rite needs three."

"Three what?"

"Three people. Three names, freely given. One to hold. One to speak." He paused on the last one, and the firelight made the girl look suddenly very small and very old at once. "One to burn."

Juniper considered this with the terrifying practicality of the very young. "I burn," she said. "Everyone always gets to that part eventually. The fire told me you'd ask."

"The fire told you?"

"She tells me things." Juniper shrugged, pulling the blanket around her shoulders. "Mostly she tells me she's hungry. But lately she's been telling me about a crown. She hates the crown. She says it's her opposite. She says—" The child hesitated, and for the first time since the salt road began, Paloma saw fear move through her like weather. "She says it eats the things she loves. People like us. She says it's been eating us for a long time and getting stronger, and someday it'll be strong enough that gifts won't hide anymore, anywhere, ever."

Paloma sat back on his heels. Above them, through the broken roof, the rain had thinned, and beyond it the clouds were tearing open on stars — cold, indifferent, beautiful, each one burning exactly as much as it chose.

Three names, freely given. He turned the phrase over in his mind the way he'd turn a coin, checking for clipping, checking for fraud. He had a name. It belonged to the temple, mortgaged to the Guild, chained in clauses and covenants — but it was his to give, if giving was what saved a child. Cassian had a name. Wren had a name, and a sister's name besides, and grief makes the strongest mortar.

And Juniper had fire.

"We'll need to know more," he said at last. "Who they were. What they bound. Where the third anchor went." He touched the socket's edge, and the stone was cold, and patient, and glad — he could feel its gladness — to finally be understood. "Chapels keep records. Even ruined ones. Someone wrote this down; someone always writes it down. Scribes especially. We scribes can't help ourselves."

"Is that a joke? Are you making a joke?"

"I'm making a plan that happens to be funny." He settled back against the wall beside the brazier, beside the girl, beside the small tame fire, and for the first time since the bells had begun tolling in Coldharbour, the dread in his chest had a shape — and a shape, he knew, was halfway to a weapon. "Sleep, Juniper. Tomorrow we dig through whatever this place kept. And then we walk to the capital, and we find out what the crown remembers."

Juniper curled up by the fire. Just before sleep took her, she murmured, mostly to the flames:

"She says the man who built this chapel had a daughter. The one who burned." A pause. "She says the daughter's name started the same way mine does."

Paloma lay awake long after, staring up through the broken roof at the wheeling stars, and the chapel breathed its old grief around him, and somewhere far to the north, beneath a crown in a palace, something nameless and patient turned over in its sleep — as if it, too, had dreamed of a chapel, and woken hungrier.
