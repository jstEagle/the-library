## 13. Tallow and Thorn

The ember liked the marshes.

That was the worst part. Juniper had expected it to hate the wet, the way fire hated wet, but instead it sat under her ribs like a cat in a sunbeam, purring at all that open sky, drinking the grey light and giving nothing back. She could feel it getting heavier. Not bigger — she'd have known if it got bigger, because bigger meant hungry, and hungry meant people started looking at her with their eyes going soft and wrong — but heavier. Like a stone she'd swallowed that was learning to be a boulder.

"Stop scratching," Paloma said, without looking up from his map.

"I'm not scratching."

"You're scratching."

She was. The salt itch had gotten into everything since they'd left the road, into her sleeves and her hair and the cracks of her knuckles, and the ember made it worse by warming her skin until the itch felt like something crawling. She pulled her hand away from her arm and shoved both hands in her pockets like a lady.

"How far is this tinker person?" she asked.

"Half a day, if Wren's still where Cassian's letters said." Paloma folded the map along its worn creases, over and over, like he was trying to wear a hole in it. "A chapel ruin past the third weir. She mends things for the eel-fishers. Nets, pots, locks."

"People?"

Paloma went quiet in that particular way he went quiet, which Juniper had learned meant he was deciding how much of the truth he could afford to spend. He spent truths the way other men spent coins. Carefully. With receipts.

"She's on no list," he said finally. "Cassian was sure of it."

"You're on lists," Juniper said. "You were born onto one, probably. Debt-lists and gift-lists and now crown-lists. Maybe everybody's on some list somewhere."

"Then we'll hope hers is short." He looked at her then, really looked, the way he did when he was checking an entry for errors. "Juniper. If I say run, you run. If I say hide, you get small and dark and quiet. Can you do that?"

"I can do anything," she said, which was true, and also a lie, because there was one thing she couldn't do, which was make the ember be quiet when it wanted to sing. But Paloma didn't need to know about the singing. Nobody needed to know about the singing.

* * *

They smelled the forge before they saw it. Charcoal and hot iron and — strangest of all, so strange Juniper stopped dead in the reeds — bread.

Bread. Out here, in the middle of nowhere, where the only neighbors were eels and herons. Her stomach made a noise like a dying animal and the ember laughed inside her, a warm rolling laugh, because the ember thought hunger was funny. The ember had never been hungry in its whole long life. That was easy for it to say.

The ruin came up out of the reeds all at once: a chapel with its roof burned off years ago, walls standing to shoulder height, and in the middle of the roofless nave, absurdly, a chimney. Smoke rose from it straight and thin in the windless air. A workbench stood under an awning of oiled canvas. Tools hung from pegs in neat rows. And a woman stood at the bench with her back to them, working a file against something small and bright, and where her left forearm should have been there was a leather cap and a steel hook, glinting.

Juniper grabbed Paloma's sleeve. "She's got a claw."

"That's a tool," Paloma murmured. "Watch."

The woman didn't turn around. "I can hear you breathing from the weir," she said. Her voice was rough as a whetstone. "Two of you. One big, one small. The big one walks like he's apologizing to the ground. The small one—" she paused, and the file stopped moving, and Juniper's whole body went cold— "the small one's got a candle problem."

Juniper looked down. The stub of candle she'd taken from the chapel stash that morning was burning in her fist, and she hadn't lit it. It had lit itself. Its flame was leaning toward her like a flower toward sun, stretched thin as taffy, straining.

She closed her fist. The flame went out. Too late.

The woman set down the file and turned around. She was maybe fifty, built like a door, with a face that had been handsome once and had then decided looks were too much trouble. Her eyes went from Juniper's face to her fist to Paloma's face, and Juniper watched her do the arithmetic — catchers? bounty? trouble? — and watched the answer come back *no*, watched her shoulders drop half an inch, watched her decide.

"Huh," the woman said. "Cassian's handwriting, I'd know it anywhere. He always did fold his letters like a man hiding contraband." She jerked her chin at Paloma. "You the scribe?"

"I'm—"

"He said you'd come eventually. Said, *if a scribe and a child ever find their way to you, feed them first and ask questions never.*" She snorted. "Sentimental old fool. Well. Rules are rules." She turned back to the bench and raised her voice. "Bread's on the stone by the fire. There's stew if you want to wait for it. Don't touch the sharp things unless you want to lose something and learn from it."

Juniper looked at Paloma. Paloma looked at Juniper. Neither of them moved.

"Well?" the woman said, not turning around. "I don't say things twice. Costs too much breath."

* * *

Her name was Wren. She said it once, while ladling stew, and didn't offer a surname, and nobody asked. She ate standing up, working, filing the teeth of a lock mechanism with her right hand while her hook held the housing steady, and she did it so smoothly that after a while Juniper stopped seeing the hook at all and just saw a woman with three hands' worth of skill packed into two.

The bread was dark and dense and still warm, and Juniper ate four pieces before she remembered to be careful, and Wren watched her do it and put the whole loaf within reach without a word.

"You're staring," Wren said.

"You cook," Juniper said. "Out here. For yourself."

"And?"

"Nobody cooks for themselves. Cooks cook for somebody. Even mean cooks. Even temple cooks cooking for a god nobody's seen." She said it before she could stop herself, and then braced, because adults got strange faces when you said true things about them.

But Wren just grunted. "Used to cook for somebody. Now I cook for me and eat what I want, when I want, and nobody tells me the onions are wrong." She pointed the ladle at Juniper. "Eat your stew."

It was good stew. It had actual meat in it, or something pretending hard enough that Juniper wasn't going to sue it for impersonation. The ember purred and pushed warmth out through her chest and down her arms, and for the first time in days she wasn't cold, and she hated how much she liked it here, how much she wanted to stay, because wanting things was how you got hurt. Wanting was a debt. Everything was a debt. Paloma had taught her that without meaning to, just by being the kind of person who flinched at numbers.

"What happened to your arm?" she asked, because she was full and warm and stupid.

"Juniper," Paloma said sharply.

"It's fine," Wren said. She held up the hook and turned it in the light. "Trade secret. Catcher's gear. You grab the wrong wrist, sometimes the wrist comes off." She said it flat, like reading from a ledger, and Juniper felt the word land in her stomach like a dropped stone.

*Catcher.*

"Cassian sent us," Paloma said quickly, "to—"

"To the girl with the candle problem, who used to be a catcher." Wren set down the ladle. "Yes. I did the sums already, scribe. You think I took you in because I'm kind? I took you in because I've been waiting three years for somebody from Cassian to walk up my path, and now you have, and that means either the world's ending or it's starting to fix itself, and either way I want to watch." She looked at Juniper. "You. Candle girl. How old are you?"

"Nine," Juniper said. "Ten in autumn."

"Mm." Wren picked up her file again. "I was nine when they found mine. Mine was small. Locks. I could feel the tumblers, like little bones in a bird's chest. They put me in a catching crew because a gift that opens doors is useful to the crown, and they worked me eleven years, and then I grabbed the wrong wrist." File, scrape. File, scrape. "So. Questions."

"We don't have any," Paloma said.

"Liar," said Wren, pleasantly. "But fine. Bread first, questions never. Cassian's rule." She glanced at the sky, judging the light. "Stew pot needs scrubbing. Small one. You scrub, you earn your keep. Big one — there's a net on the east wall needs mending, and my hook's no good for knots."

And that was it. That was the whole audience. No interrogation, no terms, no interest rates, no ledger. Juniper stood there holding her empty bowl and waiting for the part where the price appeared, because the price always appeared, prices were the one law of the world that never broke — and it didn't come. Wren just went back to her bench and started filing, and the sound was small and steady as a heartbeat.

* * *

Juniper scrubbed the pot. It was a good pot, dented and blackened, and scrubbing it gave her hands something to do while the rest of her listened to the forge tick and the reeds whisper and Wren hum — actually hum, tunelessly, some fisher song — over the sound of the file.

The ember didn't like Wren. Juniper could tell, though the ember couldn't say why, because the ember didn't have words, only weather. It went sulky and dim whenever Wren came near, the way it went sulky around Paloma's ledgers, around anything with rules written on it. *She smells like doors,* the ember seemed to say. *Doors that opened for the wrong people.* Juniper told it to hush. The ember hushed, mostly, sulking under her ribs like a banked coal.

By late afternoon the net was mended and Paloma had been roped into sorting nails by size, which he did with the grim joy of a man finally allowed to alphabetize something, and Wren had taken apart a clock the size of a fist and was putting it back together wrong on purpose, muttering.

"Why wrong?" Juniper asked, watching.

"So I know which way is right." Wren held up two nearly identical gears. "Any fool can build a thing that works. Takes a real craftsman to break a thing exactly." She slotted a gear, watched the escapement stick, nodded. "There. Perfectly ruined. Tomorrow I'll fix it properly and enjoy it twice."

"That's the most stupid sensible thing I ever heard."

"Thank you." Wren squinted at her. "You talk like a forty-year-old fishwife, you know that?"

"I've been told."

"Who told you?"

"A scribe who apologizes to the ground."

Wren laughed — a short bark, surprised out of her, gone as fast as it came, but Juniper caught it and pocketed it the way she pocketed everything worth keeping. Then Wren's face went serious again, the way faces did, and Juniper braced for the question, the real one, the one everyone circled: *what can you do, how much is it worth, who does it belong to.*

Instead Wren said: "Your feet hurt?"

Juniper blinked. "What?"

"Feet. Hurt. You've been walking on them like they owe you money since you got here. There's goose fat in the tin by the fire. Rub it in, wrap them, don't use my good bandages, use the rag ones." She went back to the clock. "Can't have you going lame. Bad for travel."

"Travel where?"

Wren's file paused for exactly one heartbeat. "Wherever you're going," she said, and filed again.

* * *

Night came down on the marsh like a lid. Wren fed the forge down to coals and lit lamps — real lamps, hung on hooks, and Juniper kept her distance from them out of habit, but the flames barely leaned at all tonight, tired after the long day, and Wren pretended not to notice, which was its own kind of kindness.

They ate bread and honey. Actual honey. Juniper had a spoonful and then another spoonful and then Wren moved the jar slightly out of reach, not unkindly, and Juniper decided she'd allow it.

Paloma fell asleep sitting up, mid-sentence, a nail-sorting tally still in his lap, and Wren looked at him for a long moment with an expression Juniper couldn't read, and then threw a blanket over him with her hook, one-handed, easy as breathing.

"You love him," Wren said. It wasn't a question.

"No," Juniper said. "He's my—" She stopped. She didn't have a word. Family was a word for people who owned each other. Friends was a word for people who hadn't yet shown you the bill. "He's mine," she said finally. "Like a thing you find. Like a thing you keep."

"Mm." Wren cleaned her file with an oily rag. "I had a sister like that. Found her. Kept her." The rag went in slow circles. "Lost her anyway. World's like that. Doesn't mean you stop finding things."

Juniper waited for more. It didn't come. Wren banked the lamp, and the dark came in close and warm, and Juniper lay on a pallet of nets that smelled of tar and salt and listened to Paloma breathe and Wren not-breathe (she slept like a guard, in slices, surfacing every hour) and the ember hummed low and almost content, and she thought: *this is what it's supposed to feel like. This is the thing other people have.*

She fell asleep before she could finish distrusting it.

* * *

She woke to voices.

Low ones. Dawn-grey light through the canvas awning, the forge cold, and Paloma standing rigid by the bench while Wren stood opposite him with her arms crossed and her hook gleaming, and the air between them had the specific texture of an argument that has already happened and is now happening again in summary.

"—can't ask that of her," Paloma was saying.

"I'm not asking her anything. I'm telling you what I'm doing."

"Wren—"

"You think I patched your net because I wanted a mended net?" Wren's voice stayed level but something under it had edges. "I've been alone in this marsh for three years, scribe, mending pots for eel-fishers, waiting for a letter that finally came, and now the letter walked up my path wearing a child's face, and you want me to wave goodbye from the doorway?" She uncrossed her arms. "No. I'm coming. That's not a request and it's not a favor and you don't get a vote."

"The road we're on—"

"I know what road you're on. I dug half its graves." Wren turned, and her eyes found Juniper in the doorway, and Juniper realized she'd been listening to the whole thing and neither of them cared, and somehow that was worse and better than anything. "Pack your feet, candle girl. We leave within the hour."

Juniper looked at Paloma. Paloma looked at Wren. Wren looked at nobody, because Wren was already packing, hook and hands moving in their smooth double rhythm, wrapping tools in oilcloth, and Juniper understood suddenly that this was how Wren said yes — not with words, with packing.

"Why?" Juniper asked. It came out smaller than she meant. "Why come? You don't know us. You don't owe us. Everybody owes everybody, that's the whole world, but you don't owe *us*—"

Wren stopped packing. She looked at Juniper for a long time, and whatever she saw made her jaw work once, side to side, like she was chewing on something too big to swallow.

"Because somebody once chose me without conditions," she said finally, "and I've spent thirty years paying it forward badly, and I'm tired of doing it badly." She shouldered her pack. "Also, your scribe folds maps like a man hiding contraband, and I miss Cassian, and you burn candles without lighting them, and I've decided all of that adds up to *mine*. Found things get kept. That's the rule. My rule. First rule of my house, such as it is."

She tossed Juniper the tin of goose fat.

"For your feet. Road's long."

* * *

They left the ruin as the sun cleared the reeds, three shadows stretching long across the salt flats, and Juniper walked in the middle because Wren said the middle was safest and because walking between them felt like a thing she could get used to, which frightened her more than catchers did.

The ember woke fully with the sun and stretched and hummed, and for once Juniper let it hum, because today the humming felt less like hunger and more like company. Three of us now, she told it. Count them. Three.

Behind them, far back along the causeway, a bell began to ring.

Not a marsh bell. Not a weir bell, not a chapel bell. A city bell, deep and bronze, carrying flat across the water the way only bad news carries. Wren stopped. Paloma stopped. The bell rang nine times, paused, and rang nine again.

Wren's hook creaked in her grip.

"That's Coldharbour's alarm pattern," Paloma said quietly. "Nine and nine. That's a hunt called."

Wren spat into the reeds. "Then we'd best move faster than news," she said, and lengthened her stride, and Juniper ran to keep up between them, the ember singing low in her chest, the goose-fat tin knocking against her hip, the bell tolling behind them like a debt come due.
