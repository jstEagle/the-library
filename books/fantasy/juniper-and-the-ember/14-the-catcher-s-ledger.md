## 14. The Catcher's Ledger

Wren had one hand and a hundred habits, and the habits were older.

She woke before dawn the way she always did, in the lean-to behind her tinker's cart, and checked the perimeter the way she always didn't — because catchers didn't check perimeters, they set them, and she'd spent nine years teaching her body to forget that. Her body was a slow learner. It still slept with its back to walls. It still counted exits. It still woke at the sound of boots on gravel with its heart going like a triphammer, except now it woke afraid instead of eager, and that was the whole difference between the two lives, boiled down: fear where appetite used to be.

The girl was still asleep by the dead fire. Juniper. Soot-streaked, sharp-elbowed, curled around herself like a fist. The scribe — Paloma — slept sitting up against the cart's wheel, which told Wren he'd done time in places where lying down was a mistake. Temple-raised. She'd have guessed it even without the ink stains and the way he flinched at bells.

Wren built up the fire small. Small fires were a habit too. Catchers learned to make no smoke at all; she'd unlearned most of it, but not that.

They'd come stumbling into her camp two nights ago out of the salt marsh, the man limping and the girl burning — literally burning, sparks coming off her hair when she dreamed, little orange ghosts rising off her skin and winking out. Wren had watched them from the treeline for an hour before deciding. Then she'd cooked plums and flatbread and set them out on a stump like bait, and asked no questions, and followed them anyway when they moved on, because that was how you found out what people were running from: you made yourself easy to keep up with.

Now she took out the ledger.

It lived in a oilcloth wrap inside the false bottom of her tool chest, under the good files and the lockpicks she no longer had hands enough to use well. It wasn't much to look at. A cheap guild-press ledger, the kind sold by the dozen in Coldharbour's chandleries, its cover soft with handling. But every page of it was written in her own careful left-slanted script, and every name on it was someone she had helped take.

Two hundred and eleven names. She'd stopped counting after two hundred because counting was a way of letting yourself off. You count, you close the book, you sleep. She didn't sleep. So she kept the list instead, updated it when news came down the salt road, and carried it the way other men carried guilt-stones or saint-marks — except hers weighed more every year instead of less.

She turned to the page she always turned to first.

*Bracken, Liss. Age 14. Gift: mending — broken things knit whole under her hands. Taken 3rd of Thawmonth, Coldharbour parish of St. Vellum. Appraised "moderate utility." Disposition: palace transfer.*

Her sister.

Liss could mend anything. Kites, kettles, bones. When they were girls Wren would break things on purpose just to watch Liss fix them, and Liss would scold her and fix them anyway, and their mother would say *one of you is going to be rich and the other is going to be hanged*, and she'd been half right, which for their mother was optimism.

Wren had been nineteen and hungry and very good at finding people. That was the gift nobody put on lists — the knack for reading a street, for knowing which shuttered window held a frightened child. The Guild called it "acquisition sense." They gave her a badge and a partner and a cut, and the first job paid more than her father made in a season, and she told herself the people she found would only ever be appraised, registered, taxed. That's what the Guild said. That's what everyone said, right up until the dispositions started saying *palace transfer* and the transfers stopped having return addresses.

Liss hadn't hidden. That was the worst of it. Wren had knocked on their own mother's door with her partner Haldo behind her, and Liss had opened it, because Wren was her sister and sisters don't hide from each other, and Wren had stood there with the warrant and said, "It'll just be questions," and Liss had believed her, because love is a door that opens from the inside.

*Appraised "moderate utility."*

Moderate. As if mending were moderate. As if her sister's hands, which had knitted a sparrow's wing back onto a sparrow, were worth a line item.

Wren wrapped the ledger away and found Paloma watching her from the wheel. He was good at being quiet; she'd nearly missed him waking.

"Whose book is that?" he asked. Not accusing. Just a scribe seeing a ledger and wanting to know its accounts.

"Mine." She fed a stick to the fire. "I was a catcher. Nine years. Guild of Impossible Trades, acquisition division, before your time in the stacks maybe."

Juniper was awake too now. Of course she was — the girl slept like a cat, all ears. She sat up with a stick in her hand, holding it like a knife, which was funny and also not.

"You caught people," Juniper said. "Like the ones chasing us."

"Yes."

"And you're telling us this why?"

Because if I don't tell you, someone else will, and then you'll run, and I'm tired of following people who run. Because the scribe has ink on his fingers that moves when he isn't looking, I saw it last night writing in the dirt near his boot, and the girl sleeps in sparks, and I've decided you two are the first interesting thing to happen to me since I lost my hand, and I am not losing anything else.

What she said was: "Because you should know what I am before you decide what I can be."

Paloma came and sat across the fire. He had that look scribes got when the numbers weren't adding up — patient, worried, committed to the arithmetic anyway. "You left the Guild."

"Left is generous. I lost the hand to a boar trap on a job, and the Guild's arithmetic doesn't run to keeping cripples. They paid out my contract at four parts in ten and gave me a tinker's license as severance. Very tidy. Everything's tidy with them, right up until it's a body."

"The ledger," Paloma said. "Those are names."

"Every one I delivered. Two hundred and eleven. Some of them I've tracked. What happens after appraisal, after the palace takes them." She let that sit. "Mostly nothing comes back. Sometimes a rumor. Once, a smell."

Juniper's stick-hand tightened. "A smell?"

"Burnt sugar. From the direction of the palace, on wind days. Sweet, like something cooking that shouldn't be." She looked at the girl straight on. "Your ember. Whatever it is. I'd bet my remaining hand the crown knows about it, wants it, and has eaten better than it already."

The fire popped. Juniper didn't flinch, which Wren noted and respected. Feral children who flinch die young; this one intended to be old.

"So what do you want?" Paloma asked. "Coin? We haven't any."

"I want what I've wanted for nine years." She pulled the ledger out again, unwrapped it, and laid it open on her knee between them, turning pages so they could see — columns of names, ages, gifts, dates, dispositions, her handwriting marching down the years getting harder and harder, the letters tightening like a fist closing. "I want these back. Or I want the thing that ate them choked on its own teeth. And I've spent nine years learning that one woman with a tinker's cart can't do either."

She closed the book.

"I'm not joining you for the girl's sake, whatever she is. No offense meant, spark-child."

"None taken," Juniper said, in a tone that meant some taken.

"I'm joining for the dead. For Liss. For the two hundred and ten others. You're going wherever this goes — I can see it, the scribe reads true names and lies to ledgers about it, the girl carries a fire that makes candle flames bow like courtiers — and wherever you're going, it ends at the crown. Am I wrong?"

Paloma was quiet a long moment. He looked at the ledger the way he must have looked at every ledger of his life, and Wren watched him do the sums.

"No," he said finally. "You're not wrong."

"Then I'm in. One condition." She held up her four remaining fingers. "When we get where we're going, my names go first. Before strategy, before survival, before whatever noble thing you two are planning. My list gets read. Every name. Out loud, if it comes to that. The dead get entered into the account."

"That's not a condition," Juniper said slowly. "That's a debt."

"It's the only kind I keep."

Something passed over Paloma's face then — recognition, maybe, or relief, the look of a man finding another creature that spoke his native language. Debt. He understood debts. Probably better than anyone alive ought to.

"Then we keep it together," he said.

Wren nodded once and went to make breakfast, because grief was grief but the living needed feeding, and feeding people was the one habit from both her lives she intended to keep.

* * *

They walked the salt road north through the morning, three figures and a cart, and Wren taught them the road the way she'd once taught recruits.

"Salt flats to the east mean open sightlines — we hug the tree line. Culvert at the mile-mark, we go under not over, bridges get watched. If you hear a whistle, three notes, drop and crawl; that's Guild pattern for 'target sighted.'" She glanced at Juniper. "And you. Last night your dreams threw sparks twenty feet. We're going to work on that."

"I don't control it."

"No. But you can starve it. Wet cloth over your hair when you sleep. Ash rubbed on your skin — it eats light, don't ask me why, tinker's lore. And you sleep next to me, because I sleep light and I wake ugly."

Juniper considered her with those flat amber eyes. "You're bossy."

"I'm experienced. Bossy is what experience looks like from underneath."

The girl almost smiled. Almost. It was a start, and Wren knew about starts — she'd catalogued enough of them in other people's lives, first steps out of hiding places, first bites of offered bread. She knew exactly what a beginning looked like, and this was one, and she filed it away carefully in the place where she kept things that might still turn out well.

At noon they sheltered in the culvert while a Guild patrol passed above — six men, dogs, the particular bored swagger of a crew working a routine sweep rather than a hot trail. Wren counted them by ear, eyes shut, the way she used to. Old skills sitting up and begging.

"They're not tracking us specifically," she murmured when the sounds faded. "They're sweeping for anyone. Which means someone's paying broad fees. Which means—" she opened her eyes, "—the hunt's gone general. Proclamation money. They're buying names wholesale now."

Paloma had gone gray. "The Guild's selling them. Suspects. I know. I balance those accounts."

"Then you know the books better than I do, scribe. So here's my question, and think hard before you answer, because I've killed men for worse answers." She kept her voice level. "In those accounts. Is there a line item for disposition costs? Transport, feeding, housing of acquired assets?"

He stared at her. "There's... yes. A standing allocation. Palace transfers. It's substantial. It scales."

"Scales with intake?"

"With intake," he said slowly, "and with duration. The longer a taken person stays at the palace, the more the crown pays the Guild to keep supplying. It's been growing for years. I thought it was corruption. Stockpiling."

Wren shook her head. "Corruption hoards. This spends. Continuous payment means continuous consumption. Something over there is eating, scribe, and eating on a schedule, and the Guild is its grocer." She tapped the cart's side, where the ledger rode in its false bottom. "I've known that in my gut for nine years. Now it's in your books too. Your numbers and my names say the same thing."

Juniper looked between them. "Then what's at the palace isn't a king."

"No," Wren said. "Whatever wears that crown, it hasn't been a king for a long time."

* * *

They made eight miles by dusk and camped in a hollow of thorn and gorse off the old drover's track. Wren cooked; the other two gathered wood; the small domestic machinery of a company forming itself ran along without anyone naming it.

Afterward, Juniper came and sat beside her, close, the way cats sit near the fire they pretend not to need.

"Your sister," the girl said. "The one in the book. Tell me about her."

Wren kept her eyes on the flames. "Why?"

"Because you carry her like I carry the ember. Heavy, and close, and always lit." The girl shrugged. "I want to know what it is I'm walking beside."

So Wren told her. About a girl who mended things, who fixed the neighbor's loom and the priest's crozier and once, impossibly, a cracked bell that rang true afterward, truer than before. About a mother with a gambling problem and a father with none of the sense. About a nineteen-year-old who took Guild coin because hunger is a recruiter, and who told herself stories all the way down, and who knocked on her own mother's door with a warrant in her pocket and a lie in her mouth.

"She opened the door," Wren said. "Because it was me. That's the whole story. That's everything. She opened the door because she trusted me, and I spent the trust like it was coin, and the Guild took her, and the crown took her, and something over there took her apart for fuel." She turned her hand over, the scarred stump of her wrist catching firelight. "Trap took the hand. Deserved worse. The Guild patched me up just enough to work, and I worked, because stopping meant feeling it, and I didn't stop for four more years. Four more years, spark-child. Forty-one more names after her."

Juniper was quiet. Then, in the blunt way she had, cutting straight through to the bone of things: "You couldn't have known."

"I could have. I chose not to. There's a difference, and I've had nine years to study it."

"But you stopped."

"One day I read a disposition report that said a boy I'd taken — gifted, twelve years old, could make plants grow in bad soil — the report said his appraisal was 'declining.' Declining. Like produce." Her voice stayed flat, which took everything she had. "And I thought: declining from what? What are they doing to him that his gift runs down like a lamp? And I couldn't unthink it. Couldn't unknow it. So I got myself trapped, on purpose, on a job I could have walked clean through. Boar trap. Let it take the hand." She flexed the stump. "Cheapest price I've ever paid for anything."

The fire burned down. Somewhere in the dark a nightjar called, three notes, and all three of them flinched, and then laughed, shakily, together — the first laugh any of them had managed in days.

"Wren," Paloma said from across the embers. "The rite — the thing we're looking for, the binding — Cassian wrote that it needs three names. Willing ones. One to hold, one to speak, one to burn."

"I heard him mention it. Before." Her voice gentled at the edges; the old monk's death was still raw ash in all of them.

"If it comes to it," Paloma said carefully, "would you—"

"No."

He blinked. "You haven't heard—"

"I don't need to hear it. Whatever the role is, whatever it costs — if it's offered, I'll take it, and I won't thank you for asking nicely first." She looked at him across the coals, and let him see, for once, all the way down to the bottom of her. "Nine years, scribe, I've been waiting for a bill to come due that I could actually pay. This is it. Don't you dare spare me."

For a moment nobody spoke. Then Juniper leaned her head briefly against Wren's shoulder — a cat's gesture, quick and gone — and said, "Bossy," again, but softly this time, and Wren found that her eyes were wet and pretended they weren't, and nobody called her on it, because they were becoming the kind of company that knew which lies to leave standing.

* * *

She woke in the grey hour before dawn to the sound of hoofbeats on the drover's track.

Not one horse. Several. Moving fast, no attempt at quiet, which meant either fools or men who didn't need to be quiet, and Wren's blood went cold because she knew which one it was before she'd fully woken.

She rolled Juniper awake with her good hand and pressed fingers to lips. Across the hollow, Paloma was already up, wide-eyed, clutching his satchel.

Through the gorse, in the pre-dawn grey, she counted riders. Five. And on the lead rider's shoulder, catching the first light —

The Guild badge. Acquisition division. And below it, pinned to his cloak, a fresh writ with the crown's seal, and beneath the seal, in the Guild's tidy press:

*By appointment of Mistress Veyra, Master of Accounts: recovery of absconded inventory, one item, pending appraisal.*

Inventory. Pending appraisal. Juniper.

And riding second, squinting at a paper in the dawn light, a face Wren knew — Haldo. Her old partner. Who had stood behind her at her mother's door nine years ago, and never once, in all the years since, sent word of what became of the girl who opened it.

Haldo looked up from his paper, straight at the hollow, straight at her.

And smiled.
