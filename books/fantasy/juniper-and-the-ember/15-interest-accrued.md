## 15. Interest Accrued

They caught up with them on the third night, in the lee of a drowned mill where the salt road narrowed to a plank walk over black water.

Paloma heard them before he saw them — the particular rhythm of boots that did not care who heard them coming. Catchers announced themselves. That was the point of catchers: fear traveled faster than men, and fear did the work for free.

But these were not catchers. He knew it by the lantern light, which was green-glassed and shuttered, Guild-issue, and by the fact that they waited at both ends of the plank walk with the patience of men being paid by the hour rather than the head.

"Master Coldharbour," said the one nearest, a broad woman with a face like a closed ledger. "Mistress Veyra requests your presence."

"Requests," Paloma repeated.

The woman smiled without warmth. "Requests."

Juniper was a shadow against his side, soot-streaked and silent, her hand finding his sleeve the way she'd learned to do it — lightly, ready to let go, ready to run. Wren stood behind them both on the mill's broken threshold, her single hand resting easy near the knife at her belt, her face arranged into an expression of profound rural stupidity that Paloma had come to understand was a weapon.

"We're expected nowhere," Paloma said.

"You're expected everywhere, Master Coldharbour. That's what interest means." The woman tilted her head toward the marsh path. "There's a fire. There's food. There's a tent with a floor in it, which I'm told is more than you've had since the salt road. And there's Mistress Veyra, who has been very patient, and whose patience — like everything else in this arrangement — has been accruing."

He looked at Juniper. She looked back, ember-dark eyes catching the shuttered light, and gave him the smallest shrug: *your call, scribe-man*. Three days of running had burned through whatever fear she'd had of him and left something harder behind, something almost like partnership.

He thought about the water on either side of the plank walk. He thought about the knife under Wren's apron and the woman's four companions, two of whom had crossbows held low and casual, which was how professionals held crossbows.

"Lead on," he said.

* * *

Veyra's camp sat on a hummock of dry ground above the marsh, all canvas and iron braziers, tidy as a countinghouse. She received him seated before a folding desk with actual ledgers stacked upon it, and Paloma felt a pang of homesickness so sharp it was nearly grief — the smell of vellum, the weight of columns, the clean arithmetic of a world where everything could be entered and balanced.

She was smaller than he remembered, or perhaps he'd grown. Her hair was iron-grey and pinned with a quill through the knot, which he had always privately considered the most honest ornament in Coldharbour. She poured two cups of wine without asking whether he wanted any.

"Sit down, Paloma."

"Mistress."

"You look terrible." She pushed a cup across the desk. "Marsh fever does that. Sit."

He sat. Behind him, he knew, Juniper and Wren were being fed at a second fire, watched but not touched, because Veyra did not touch inventory until appraisal. He tried not to think of Juniper in those terms. It was harder than it should have been, because he had used exactly those terms himself, in exactly this kind of tent, to buy exactly this kind of shelter.

"How much?" he asked.

Veyra raised an eyebrow. "Direct. Good. Marshes suit you." She opened the topmost ledger and turned it toward him. His own hand stared back at him from the page — the contract of shelter, signed in ink that had moved on its own before he ever knew it could. "Principal: your debt-bond to the temple, purchased at par. Interest: compounded daily since departure. Costs incurred: two horses, one boat, four men's wages, my personal attention." She tapped the column. "You are currently worth less than nothing, Paloma. You owe me more than you exist."

"I know the arithmetic."

"Do you? Because here's mine." She closed the ledger. "I hid a gifted child in my Guild under a fiction of pending appraisal. I falsified stock records against a crown appraiser — which, incidentally, was beautifully done; I've had it copied as a training document. I then lost both the child and my best scribe out the salt gate in the same night, along with a tinker whose name was on three separate watch lists. Do you imagine the crown hasn't noticed?"

"The crown has other concerns."

"The crown has *exactly* this concern." Veyra leaned forward. "Every day you're loose, every day that girl burns unregistered somewhere in my kingdom, the price on all our heads climbs. You're not running from catchers anymore, Paloma. You're running from arithmetic."

"So I pay."

"You can't pay. That's the elegant part." She smiled, and it was the smile she wore when closing on someone's house. "But you can *work*."

* * *

Later — after the wine, after the ledgers were opened again, after the brazier had burned down twice — Paloma would try to remember at what exact moment the trap revealed its shape. He never could. With Veyra it was never a moment. It was a slope, gentle as a ledger line, and you were at the bottom before you noticed you'd been walking downhill.

"The Guild wants an appraisal," she said. "Of the crown."

The words sat between them. Somewhere outside, a marsh bird made a sound like tearing cloth.

"That's not possible," Paloma said carefully. "The crown isn't stock. It isn't tradeable. It sits on a king's head behind palace walls and forty years of law."

"Forty years of law written by whoever holds the crown." Veyra turned her cup slowly. "And here is what you don't know, because you've been balancing temple debts while I balance kingdoms. The crown is old, Paloma. Older than Aldous. Older than the dynasty. And things that old have *provenance* — and provenance can be appraised. What it's made of. What it's owed. What it will do when the man wearing it stops being able to hold it together."

"You've seen something."

"I've read something. A bill of sale, three hundred years back, in a script nobody in my archive could parse." She looked at him then, directly, and Paloma understood that this whole conversation had been built toward this look. "Nobody in my archive could parse it. But I employ a scribe who reads true names."

The marsh bird tore its cloth again. Paloma found he was holding his own hands still, deliberately, the way he did when ink wanted to move.

"You want me to read the crown."

"I want you to appraise it. Read it, value it, tell me what it's owed and what it costs and what happens if it changes hands. That's all. One reading. Then we square your account, I burn your temple bond, and you and the girl and the tinker walk away owing nothing to anyone. Clean slate. Fresh ledger." She spread her hands. "It's the only currency you have, Paloma. I'd be a fool not to ask, and you'd be a fool not to sell."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I sell your location to the crown tonight, and the girl's with it, and I take the loss on your account like any sensible merchant writing off bad debt." She said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "I told you when you came to me: everything is a trade. I meant it kindly then. I mean it kindly now."

Paloma looked into the fire. He thought of Brother Cassian erasing his name from report after report, year after year, asking nothing. He thought of Wren feeding them stew and asking no questions. He thought of Juniper asleep against a chapel wall, trusting him with the only thing she had.

And he thought: *this is what the world is. This is what it has always been. Everyone keeps a ledger, and everyone is a line in someone else's.*

"What does the Guild want with the rite?" he asked quietly.

Veyra went still. It was very slight — a half-breath's pause — but Paloma had spent ten years reading people the way he read pages, and he caught it.

"What rite?"

"The Binding of the Crown." He turned to face her fully. "The hollow chapel on the salt road. The echoes in the stone. Three names together — one to hold, one to speak, one to burn. You already know about it. You didn't send me down the salt road by accident. You sent me down it because it passes the chapel, because you hoped I'd find it, because a scribe who reads true names is the perfect instrument to dig an old rite out of ruined stone."

The fire popped. Veyra's face did something complicated and settled into honesty, or the shape of it.

"When did you work that out?"

"On the plank walk. Somewhere around 'requests.'"

"Hm." She refilled his cup. "You should have been a merchant."

"Who do you sell it to?"

"The highest bidder." She said it plainly, and he realized she'd decided to stop lying to him, and that this too was a kind of transaction — truth offered now to secure cooperation later. "Three houses want the succession. Two foreign crowns want leverage over ours. And at least one party wants the rite itself — the *unbinding*, Paloma, not the binding. Someone out there believes the thing on the throne can be taken off, and they'll pay a kingdom's ransom to know how." She shrugged. "The Guild doesn't care who wins. The Guild cares that everyone pays."

"And me?"

"You're the instrument. You're also—" she paused, choosing the word with a merchant's precision, "—collateral. If the buyer wants the scribe who performed the reading, the Guild delivers the scribe. If the buyer wants no witnesses, the Guild... negotiates. Your account balances either way. That's the beauty of it."

Paloma set down the cup. His hands were perfectly steady, which surprised him. Somewhere inside, beneath the steadiness, something was quietly closing, door after door, the way a house goes dark room by room.

"You hid Juniper," he said. "In chapter and verse. You bought me time at terrible interest. Why bother, if I was just collateral?"

"Because collateral appreciates." Veyra met his eyes without flinching. "And because I liked you, Paloma. Both things are true. That's the part you keep failing to learn about the world — it isn't kind *or* cruel. It's priced."

Outside, Juniper laughed at something Wren said — a short, startled sound, a girl discovering she could laugh. Paloma listened to it the way a drowning man listens to shore.

"One reading," he said. "The crown. Nothing else. And my account closes — truly closes, in writing, witnessed — and the girl's status as inventory is voided, and Wren's names come off the lists. All of it, in advance, in ink."

"In advance? No merchant trades in advance."

"This one does. Or there's no reading, and you write me off tonight, and good luck explaining to your buyers why their instrument burned." He nodded toward the far fire, where Juniper sat wrapped in a borrowed blanket. "She's not just inventory either, Mistress. She's the only ember the Guild has ever catalogued at that magnitude. Appraise *that* honestly and see what I'm worth."

For a long moment Veyra studied him, and Paloma watched the abacus turn behind her eyes, bead by bead.

"Witnessed terms," she said finally. "Drafted tonight, signed before dawn. You drive a hard bargain for a man who owes me his existence."

"I learned from the best."

"Don't flatter me. It accrues interest too." She rose, and paused, and for one unguarded instant looked tired — an old woman with iron hair and a kingdom of debts. "One more thing, Paloma, freely given, no charge. The party bidding for the unbinding rite. I've never met them. My contact has never met them. Payment in old coin, instructions in that same dead script your crown-bill was written in." She pulled her cloak tighter. "Whatever you think is buying you, consider who else speaks that language."

* * *

He walked back to the far fire through the cold marsh grass, and Juniper looked up at him, and Wren looked up at him, and neither asked, because they had both already read his face the way he read pages.

"It's bad," Juniper said. Not a question.

"It's a trade."

"They're the same thing," she said, "where you come from."

He sat down heavily between them. The blanket smelled of woodsmoke and someone else's life. Ahead, dawn was still hours off, and beyond dawn lay the capital, and the crown, and the reading that would cost him God knew what — he had already paid once, a memory he couldn't name, a hole shaped like something loved.

"Paloma," Wren said quietly, turning something over in her single hand — a plum, saved from the road, offered across the fire. "When we get to the city. Whatever they've got you doing. We'll be there."

"Not collateral," Juniper said firmly, taking the plum and splitting it with her thumbs, handing him half. "Family doesn't get sold."

He took the fruit. It was small and bruised and sweeter than anything he'd tasted in years, and he ate it slowly, and said nothing, because the truth was that family was exactly the kind of thing the world priced — and the only question left was who would pay.

Across the dark water, in the direction of the capital, the bells began. Distant, faint, wrong-numbered — tolling a count that no feast required.

Juniper's head came up sharply. The fire bowed toward her, every flame leaning like grass in wind.

"They know we're coming," she whispered.

Paloma counted the bells, and stopped counting, and wished he hadn't.
