## 16. Names Are Debts

The salt marsh gave way to scrubland as the moon climbed, and Wren called a halt in the lee of a fallen watchtower — a squat stone fist of a thing, half-swallowed by blackberry bramble. She built a fire small enough to hide in a cupped hand, which was the only kind of fire anyone dared build anymore, and set Juniper to sorting plums from the sack while she took first watch on the broken wall.

Paloma sat apart, as he always did, with his back against cold stone and his ledger open across his knees. Not because he had accounts to balance. Because the ledger was the only object in the world that had never lied to him, and tonight he needed something true to hold.

He had been writing the same figure in the same column for an hour.

"You're doing it wrong."

Juniper dropped down beside him without asking, the way she did everything — as if permission were a custom practiced by other people. She smelled of woodsmoke and stolen fruit. In the firelight her soot-streaked face looked younger than it had in the archive stacks, or perhaps he was simply getting used to it, which frightened him more.

"Doing what wrong?"

"The numbers." She leaned over his arm, uninvited, and jabbed a plum-sticky finger at the page. "You keep writing four hundred and twelve crowns, and then you cross it out, and then you write it again. If it's four hundred and twelve, write it once. If it isn't, stop lying to the book."

Paloma closed the ledger. "It's not a matter of arithmetic."

"It's always a matter of arithmetic." She said it with the flat certainty of a child who had learned hunger could be counted — three days since bread, two coins short, one belly empty. "Everything is. That's what grown-ups don't want children to know. They dress it up in words like *honor* and *duty*, but underneath it's all just who owes whom."

He looked at her then, really looked, and felt something turn over in his chest like a page.

"Who taught you that?"

"Nobody." She bit into a plum and spoke around it. "The streets taught me. Same as they'd teach you, if you ever got thrown off your nice shelf." She glanced at him sidelong, sharp as a fishhook. "Except you wouldn't last a week. You flinch when people raise their voices. You count things when you're scared. I've watched you."

"I do not count things when I'm scared."

"You're counting right now. Your fingers are moving under the book." She nodded at his hands, and he stilled them, and hated that she'd seen. "See? Everything's arithmetic. Even you."

* * *

The fire settled. Across the camp, Wren's silhouette didn't move against the sky, but Paloma knew she was listening. One-handed tinkerers heard everything; it was how they'd survived so long with half their tools.

Juniper finished her plum and threw the pit into the dark with a flick of her wrist, precise as a scribe sealing a letter. Then she asked the question she'd been circling for two days, the way she circled everything before striking.

"Why does Veyra want you? Really. Not the story she told us about appraisals and favors. The real one." She turned to face him fully, cross-legged, firelight catching the ember-glow deep in her eyes that no ordinary child's eyes held. "She looks at you the way merchants look at a strongbox. And you let her. Why?"

Paloma weighed the lie. It was a good lie, well-kept, oiled with years of use: *I'm useful, that's all, I'm a careful man with a careful gift.* But the girl had spent her whole life smelling out adults' false bottoms. A lie would insult her, and worse, it would fail.

So instead he opened the ledger again, but not to the accounts. He turned to the front matter, where the temple's seal sat fat and red beside a line of cramped script, and he read aloud what he had never said aloud:

"'Let it be known that the man called Paloma Coldharbour, third of that name in the temple rolls, is bound in service until such time as his debt of nine hundred marks is discharged, his name being surety for the same.'" He tapped the line. "That's what my name is. Surety. Collateral."

Juniper frowned. "It's just words."

"Just words." He laughed, and it came out wrong, dry and cracked as old vellum. "Do you know what happens if I run? Truly run, past the city walls, past the Guild's reach, somewhere the temple's writ doesn't go?"

"You'd be free."

"I'd be *nobody*. Not in the pretty way. In the literal one." He held up his hand and, after a glance to make sure Wren wasn't watching too closely, traced a character in the air — the true-name glyph for *riverstone*, small and simple, the first word his gift had ever shown him. A pebble near the fire shivered and rolled uphill, an inch, no more. "My gift works through names. The true ones, underneath. But a name only has power if it's *yours*. Mine belongs to the temple. When I speak, I speak with borrowed authority, like a clerk using someone else's seal. The further I go from the ledger where my debt is written, the thinner my name wears. Go far enough, and there's nothing left to wear."

Juniper stared at the pebble. At him. "They'd erase you?"

"They wouldn't have to do anything. Erasure would happen on its own, the way ink fades in sun. A name is a debt, Juniper. It tells the world what you owe and to whom, and the world holds you to it. My name says I owe nine hundred marks to the God-of-the-Scale, administered by His temple, and every step I take away from that contract unravels me a little more." He closed the ledger gently, the way you close a door on a sickroom. "I've been running calculations my whole life. How far can I go? How much of myself can I spend? Every kindness I've ever done, I've done inside the walls, because outside them I start to come apart."

"That's not freedom," Juniper said slowly. "That's a cage made of paper."

"A cage made of paper is still a cage if you believe the paper."

"And you believe it."

"With every bone in my body." He smiled, thin and tired. "Belief is the bars."

* * *

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, a marsh bird cried its one flat note, and Wren shifted on the wall, and the night went on being the night.

But Juniper had gone very still, in the way she went still before she struck — except this time she wasn't looking at prey. She was looking inward, at something behind her own eyes that glowed.

"The ember talks to me," she said abruptly.

Paloma kept his voice level, though his pulse jumped. He had suspected. Suspecting and hearing were different countries. "What does it say?"

"Lots of things. Promises, mostly. It says it can keep me warm forever. It says nobody will ever touch me again unless I allow it. It says—" Her jaw tightened. "It says it can burn my name clean. Make me new. No debts, no past, nothing owed to anybody." She looked up at him, and her eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "And I almost want to say yes. Isn't that stupid? It's a fire. Fires eat things. I've seen what it does when I'm angry. But it *promises*, and the promises sound like mine."

"It sounds like a lender," Paloma said quietly.

"What?"

"A lender. Warm words, easy terms, and the interest hidden in the small print." He turned to face her fully. "Juniper, listen to me, because this may be the most important thing anyone ever teaches you. There are two kinds of names in this world. The kind that's given to you — by parents, by temples, by kings, by fires — and those are all debts. Someone wrote you into their book, and now you're bound by their terms. And then there's the kind you choose. The kind you give yourself, freely, knowing the cost. That kind isn't a debt. It's a *gift*. And gifts bind differently."

"How?"

"They bind because you want them to. A freely given name holds fast no matter where you walk, because nothing external enforces it. You enforce it. It's the only anchor that travels." He hesitated, then made himself continue, because she deserved the whole weight of it. "There's an old rite. I saw its echo in the hollow chapel, three days ago. The Binding of the Crown. It needs three names, and not just any names — it needs one freely given. Given completely. Not sold, not traded, not owed. Given."

"Given to what?"

"To the working. To hold the rite together while it does what it must do." He met her eyes. "And when a name is given like that, it doesn't come back. Whoever gives it is unwritten. No past, no debts, no self. Just... space, where a person used to be."

Juniper was quiet for a long moment. The firelight moved on her face.

"That's why Veyra wants you," she said at last. "Not for appraisals. For *that*. She wants the one thing in the world that can give a name properly. A true-name reader who knows exactly what he's giving away." Her voice hardened. "She's going to feed you to the rite."

"She's going to try to sell the rite," Paloma corrected. "To whoever bids highest. The crown, a rival guild, the temple — it hardly matters. And yes. I'm the ink she means to write it with."

"Then we run. Tonight. Wake the tinker woman, take the salt road south—"

"And unravel by morning," he said gently. "You weren't listening. I *can't* run. Not far. Not anymore. The moment I left Coldharbour's jurisdiction I started spending myself. I've been counting the cost every hour — a little less memory here, a little less certainty there. Do you know I couldn't remember my mother's face yesterday? I remember that she had one. I remember loving it. The face itself is gone." He spread his hands. "I am a purse with a hole in it, Juniper, and every mile spends another coin."

Juniper's fists clenched. The nearest flame stood up straight, leaning toward her like a dog hearing its name, and for a heartbeat the whole campsite brightened — Wren's head snapped around — and then the girl breathed, slow and deliberate, and the fire remembered its manners.

"That's not fair," she said.

"No. It isn't."

"You're going to let them use you."

"I'm going to *choose* what uses me," Paloma said. "That's the difference. That's the entire difference, and it took me thirty years to learn it. Veyra thinks she owns my name because it's written in her ledgers. The temple thinks it owns my name because it's written in theirs. But there's one book neither of them can edit." He touched his own chest, over the heart. "And if I'm going to be unwritten anyway — if the hole in the purse is going to spend me regardless — then I would rather pour myself out for something I chose. A freely given name, given freely. Not as payment. As a gift."

"That's the stupidest arithmetic I've ever heard," Juniper whispered.

"It's the only sum that balances," he said. "Trust me. Balancing is the one thing I know how to do."

* * *

She didn't answer at once. She picked up another plum, considered it, and didn't eat it — just turned it over in her small grimy hands, thinking with her fingers the way she thought with everything.

Then: "When you're gone. Unwritten. Will you forget us? Like your mother's face?"

The question landed harder than any catcher's net. Paloma found he had to look at the fire before he could answer.

"Probably. Eventually. That's what unwritten means." He made himself look back at her. "But here is the part I've been turning over like a coin all day, Juniper, and I'll tell you because you've earned honesty and I rarely get to pay it. The rite demands a name freely given. Freely. Which means the giving has to be *true* — the rite will taste it, weigh it, test whether there's a debt hiding anywhere in it. And I have spent my whole life as a creature of debt. Every relationship, every kindness, every bond — all of it entered in some ledger, all of it owing or owed. Except—"

"Except us," Juniper said.

"Except you." His voice caught, and he let it. "Wren feeds us and asks nothing. Cassian erased my name for years and told no one. And you — you walked into my life like weather, wanting nothing from me but shelter, offering nothing but yourself. None of that is debt. None of it can be entered in any book. It's the first unowed thing I've ever held." He smiled, and it hurt, and it was real. "Which means that when I stand before whatever waits beneath the crown, and I give my name — I won't be giving the temple's version. Or the Guild's. I'll be giving the one written in memory of you. And maybe that's the trick of it. Maybe a name given from love weighs differently than a name given from law. Maybe it's heavy enough to break a mouth that's been eating kingdoms."

Juniper stared at him. The ember-light in her eyes had gone soft, banked, almost tender.

"You really think love is stronger than arithmetic."

"I think arithmetic is just love that's forgotten what it's counting," Paloma said. "Every ledger I've ever balanced was somebody trying to protect what they loved. Badly. Cruelly, sometimes. But that's the root of it. Debt is love with the tenderness stripped out and the fear left in."

She was quiet again. Then she scooted closer — not touching him, Juniper didn't touch, but near, nearer than she'd ever voluntarily come — and put the uneaten plum in his hand.

"Eat it," she ordered. "You didn't have any dinner. I counted."

And Paloma laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of him, rough from disuse — and ate the plum, and it was the sweetest thing he had tasted in years, and he understood suddenly that this was what a freely given name felt like even before it was given. Small. Warm. Offered without terms.

* * *

He woke before dawn to Wren's hand on his shoulder — her single hand, grip like a vice wrapped in leather.

"Riders," she murmured. "Three, maybe four, on the road behind us. Moving slow. Checking the ditches." Her eyes were flat and hard in the grey light. "Catcher pattern. Or Veyra's. These days I can't always tell the difference, and that should worry you more than it does."

Paloma rose, joints cracking, ledger already clutched to his chest. Juniper was awake too, crouched on the wall with her hair tied back, watching the road where the riders would appear.

"How long?" he asked.

"An hour. Maybe less if they find our fire-pit." Wren spat over the wall. "We need a city between us and them, and there's only one city on this road. The capital. Feast of Bells starts in three days — half the kingdom's pouring through those gates, porters and pilgrims and peddlers, every face a stranger's face."

Paloma looked north, toward the distant smudge of towers where the thing wearing Aldous's crown sat patient and hungry, and felt the hole in his purse widen by a coin.

"Then we'd better find ourselves some porter's cloaks," he said. "And pray the bells are loud enough to drown out what follows us."

Behind them, down the dark road, the first rider crested the hill.
