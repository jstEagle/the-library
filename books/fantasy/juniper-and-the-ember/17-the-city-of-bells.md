## 17. The City of Bells

**Juniper**

The bells woke her before dawn, all the way from the salt flats, a sound like a hundred bronze throats clearing themselves to sing.

She'd never heard bells before. In Coldharbour there had been the temple bell, one sour note that meant work or trouble, and she'd learned to flinch at it. These were different. These came in waves, overlapping, chasing each other across the marsh like dogs let off their leads, and Juniper sat up in the back of Wren's cart with her mouth open and forgot to be afraid.

"That's the Feast," Wren said around a mouthful of bread. She drove with her knees, her single hand steady on the reins, which still looked like magic to Juniper every time she did it. "They ring from first light to last. Every tower in Aurelmar. Been doing it since before your gran's gran."

"Why?"

"To remember the king's coronation." Wren spat over the wheel. "And to remind everyone whose city it is."

Paloma was asleep against the grain sacks, his ledger satchel hugged to his chest even now, even sleeping, like it might wander off without him. Juniper watched him for a while. He looked younger asleep. Less like a man made entirely of careful arithmetic.

The ember stirred under her ribs, warm as a hand.

*Listen,* it said. *So many hearts. So much light.*

"Shut up," she whispered, out of habit, but there was no heat in it. The ember didn't shut up. It never did. But lately its voice had changed — less like a stranger whispering through a wall, more like something curled up beside her, wanting what she wanted. She didn't know if that was better or worse.

The road climbed out of the marsh and onto the causeway, and then Aurelmar came up over the horizon all at once, and Juniper stopped breathing.

She'd imagined cities. Coldharbour had been the biggest thing she'd ever seen, and she'd hated it — the soot, the squeeze, the way people looked through you like you were weather. But this —

Aurelmar rose out of the river on seven hills, and every hill wore towers, and every tower wore gold, and the morning sun hit it all at once so the whole city blazed like a coal blown bright. Bells hung in the towers big as oxen, she could see them, swinging, and between the hills bridges arced white as bone, and down at the water a thousand boats crowded the quays with banners streaming, red and white and gold, gold, gold everywhere.

"Oh," she said. It came out very small.

Wren grinned. "Wait till you smell it."

The smell arrived an hour later: roasting meat and hot sugar and horse and river mud and something floral and cloying drifting down from the upper hills where, Paloma explained when he woke, the rich lived behind walls tall enough to keep out the view of everyone below.

They joined the queue at the Salt Gate behind forty other carts. Guild porters, all of them — Veyra's papers said the Guild of Impossible Trades was delivering festival stock to six addresses in the capital, and Veyra's papers were very good papers, because Veyra did not make bad investments. Juniper wore a porter's smock two sizes too big with the sleeves tied back. Paloma wore one too, and had spent the whole ride drilling them on it.

"You're mute," he'd said. "All three of you. Porters who talk get asked questions. Porters who don't get ignored. Ignored is the safest country in the world; I've lived there most of my life."

"And you?" Wren had asked. "You going to be mute too?"

"I'm going to be boring," Paloma said. "It's a discipline."

Now, at the gate, he was boring beautifully. A guard with a pike and a bored face flipped through the Guild manifest while Paloma stood with his hands folded and recited freight weights in a flat voice until the guard's eyes glazed over and he waved them through just to make it stop.

Juniper kept her eyes down like she was told. She lasted about nine seconds.

Then they were inside, and the city swallowed them, and she stopped looking down forever.

* * *

**Paloma**

He had been to Aurelmar twice, both times on Guild business, both times with his eyes fixed on ledgers and his mind fixed on survival. It was easier to navigate a city you refused to see.

With Juniper beside him, refusing was impossible. She turned her head like a lighthouse. Everything pulled at her — the jugglers on the fountain steps, the woman selling candied violets, the strings of paper bells strung alley to alley overhead so the whole street walked beneath a ceiling of pale ringing shapes. When the wind moved them they clicked together softly, thousands of tiny wooden tongues, and Juniper made a sound Paloma had never heard her make, low and wondering, almost a purr.

"It's paper," she said. "They're just paper. Why does it feel like —"

"Festival," Paloma said. "Paper burns clean. Afterward they sweep it into the river and by tomorrow the streets are ordinary again and everyone pretends the city isn't what it actually is."

"What is it actually?"

He didn't answer. The answer was walking past them on the corner: a beggar boy with a soldier's old coat, holding out a chipped bowl while festival crowds stepped around him like water around a stone. The answer was the baker's window stacked with small loaves priced at more than Paloma's weekly wage. The answer was the proclamation board outside the guardhouse, fresh bills pasted over older ones, and the newest bill reading HONOR THE CROWN'S VIGILANCE — REPORT UNNATURAL GIFTS TO YOUR LOCAL APPRAISER, REWARD: TEN CROWNS.

Ten crowns. Enough to eat for a season. Paloma filed that away the way he filed everything, in the cold drawer at the back of his mind, and hated himself a little for how naturally the filing came.

"Keep moving," he murmured. "Eyes down. We're boring."

"I don't want to be boring," Juniper said. "I want to see everything."

"You can see everything after we're safe."

"When's that?"

He had no answer for that either, and she knew it, and something in her face closed slightly, the wonder banking itself down to coals. He'd done that. He was getting good at doing that, and he wasn't sure whether it was protection or damage.

Their delivery address was a chandler's warehouse in the Tanners' Quarter, three streets from the palace hill. Wren took the cart there by routes only she understood — she'd been a catcher once; she knew cities the way wolves knew forests — and by midmorning they were stabled in the loft above the warehouse among crates marked CHANDLER'S WAX, FESTIVAL STOCK, DO NOT OPEN, which contained, per Veyra's arrangement, no wax whatsoever.

Paloma counted heads. Four. All present. He allowed himself one breath.

"The audience is at noon," he said. "The king shows himself on the Balcony of Bells for the Rite of Remembrance. Whole city watches. Which means the palace watch thins along the river wall — everyone wants to see him too, even guards are people, mostly." He unrolled the sketch Isolde's contact had smuggled them: the undercroft approach, the service stair, the door with the lock Wren swore she could persuade. "We move during the Rite. We're inside before the last bell."

Juniper was barely listening. She was at the loft window, looking down at the street, where festival children were running a hoop through the crowd, shrieking, chased by a dog, chased by nothing, chased by joy.

"Paloma," she said. "Did you ever have a day like this? When you were little? Where nothing hurt?"

He thought about the temple dormitory. The cold. Brother Cassian sneaking him honeyed bread on feast days, which had been the closest thing, and had been enough, then.

"Once or twice," he said.

"That's not fair," Juniper said, with the flat fury she reserved for injustices she couldn't name yet. "There's this much in the world and some people only get once or twice."

* * *

**Juniper**

She slipped out while they argued about watch rotations. Not far. Just down. Just to look. She left the smock on so she'd be boring, and kept her hands in her pockets so nothing would bow toward her, and told the ember firmly to stay quiet, and the ember — traitor, friend — hummed louder.

The street was full. Fuller than anything. People pressed shoulder to shoulder, and nobody cared who she was, because everybody was somebody else today; she saw a lord's servant dancing with a fishwife, saw a drunk priest blessing a pie stall, saw two old women sharing one pair of shoes between them, taking turns. Everyone had sugar on their fingers. The whole city smelled like a thing being celebrated, and underneath the celebration, faint as blood under bandages, something else.

She followed the something else.

It took her off the main square into a lane where the paper bells ran out, and the banners got threadbare, and the houses leaned together like conspirators. Here the festival was a rumor. Here there was a queue — a long, silent, orderly queue of people holding bowls and tickets, waiting outside a gatehouse marked CHARITY OF THE CROWN, and the something-else-under-the-bandages was coming from them. Hunger. Not stomach hunger. She knew stomach hunger like a sister. This was the other kind, the kind she'd had in the stacks of the Guild archive, the kind that ate you from the place where hope lives.

An old man near the end of the queue caught her looking and smiled with half his teeth. "Festival ration," he said, lifting his ticket. "Once a year, the crown feeds us. King's own charity." He said it proudly, and his eyes said something else entirely, and Juniper's gift — the ember's gift, her gift, whatever it was now — read the truth off him like ink off a page:

*He buried a daughter this winter. The ration is smaller than last year. He will say thank you anyway. He always says thank you. Saying thank you is how he stays alive.*

She stepped back. Her hands had come out of her pockets without her permission and the nearest lantern flame had leaned toward her like a flower toward sun, and she crushed it back down, and ran.

She found the square again just as the noon bells began.

* * *

**Paloma**

He found her absence at the same moment the bells began, and terror went through him like ice water — a child alone, a hunted child, in the capital, on the one day her face might matter least and her fire might matter most — and then he saw her, thank every silent god, pushing back through the crowd toward the warehouse lane, white-faced, fists clenched.

"Where —"

"I looked," she said. Her voice was strange. Flat and burning at once. "I looked at the city. Like you said."

"Juniper —"

"There's a queue," she said. "By the river. People with tickets. Festival ration, once a year, the crown feeds them, and they say thank you." Her eyes came up and they were wet and furious and older than she was. "He's got a whole city and he eats people, and they line up to thank him for bread."

Around them the great bells of Aurelmar crashed out the Rite of Remembrance, wave after wave, shaking dust from the eaves, and the crowd turned as one body toward the palace hill, faces lifted, and Paloma turned too, because he had to know, because he needed to see it again, confirm it, feed his certainty.

On the Balcony of Bells, small with distance, King Aldous raised one hand.

Even at this range, even through a thousand cheering bodies, Paloma's gift reached out the way it always did, unasked, unwanted, and read.

Nothing. No name. No true shape. Just appetite, vast and patient, wearing the shape of a waving hand, and beneath it — this was new, this was worse — beneath it, faintly, a sound like chewing.

Beside him Juniper had gone rigid. The ember under her ribs answered the balcony the way iron answers a lodestone, and for one terrible second every flame on the street — every lantern, every cookfire, every candle in every window — bowed toward the palace at once, a whole city's fire leaning like grass in wind.

Nobody noticed. Everyone was watching the king.

Except, somewhere, someone always notices.

A man in a gray appraiser's coat, standing apart from the crowd, lowered the glass he'd been pointing at the balcony and swept it slowly across the square instead — across the bowed flames, across the frozen girl, across the porter standing next to her whose heart had stopped and started again.

Paloma took Juniper's hand. "Walk," he said quietly. "Don't run. Boring. Be boring."

They walked. Behind them, unhurried, the gray coat began to walk too.

* * *

**Juniper**

Back in the loft, Wren listened to their report with her jaw tight, then started packing tools with the calm of a woman loading a weapon.

"Appraiser made us," Paloma said. "Or thinks he did. Either way our window's closing. We go at the Rite's last bell or not at all."

"We go," Wren said.

Juniper stood at the window again. Below, the festival roared on, oblivious, beautiful, rotten. The paper bells clicked in the wind. Somewhere under the palace hill, behind doors Wren could pick, dozens of taken people waited in the dark with their names in cages, and above them a dead king waved to a living crowd, and the crowd loved him, and the bells rang for him, and the whole golden city sang its throat raw for a mouth.

The ember pressed warm against her fear, steadying her, and she realized with a small cold shock that she was letting it. That she wanted it. That somewhere between the salt marsh and this window, the hungry lonely warmth had become the only part of her that never wavered.

*When we open those doors,* the ember murmured, *they will all lean toward me. Like the flames did. Like flowers do.*

"And then what?" she whispered.

*Then we choose,* it said, and for the first time since she'd carried it, the ember sounded uncertain too.

Below, the last bell of the Rite began to fall silent, tower by tower, and in the hush between its final strokes, Wren shouldered her kit and said, "Right. Palace's ours. Let's go steal some people back."
