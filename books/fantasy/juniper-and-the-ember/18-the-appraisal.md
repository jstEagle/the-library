## 18. The Appraisal

The crown did not sit on a cushion.

That was the first thing wrong with it. Every treasure Paloma had ever appraised for the Guild had been arranged like a bride — velvet, lamplight, a little brass plaque with a number on it. But this was kept in a locked iron cabinet in the undercroft beneath the Feast Hall, and it stood upright on a stone pedestal, alone, as if someone had once tried to make it comfortable and given up.

Veyra's man unlocked the cabinet and then left without a word, which was the second thing wrong with it. Nobody left a crown unguarded. Unless the guard wasn't for thieves.

"Ten minutes," Wren murmured from the doorway, her one hand resting easy on her belt where a knife would be if she'd been allowed to bring one. "Feast of Bells means half the palace staff are drunk and the other half wish they were. But ten minutes is still ten minutes."

"I only need two," Paloma said.

He didn't know if that was true. He'd never read anything this old. He'd read ledgers, locks, a customs seal, once a dying man's last will to find where he'd hidden the deed. Small things. Recent things. Things whose names still fit inside themselves.

A crown that had eaten a king was none of those.

Juniper crouched by the pedestal, soot-streaked even after three days of Wren's scrubbing, and stared at it the way she stared at everything — head tilted, utterly unimpressed. The ember under her skin should have been screaming. Paloma braced himself for it, for the flare that would light them up like a signal fire in the dark of the undercroft.

Instead she said, quietly, "It's not looking at me."

"What?"

"The fire in it." She frowned. "Everything burns a little. Candles, coals, people. They all look at me, kind of. This doesn't." She stood up and stepped back, and for the first time since he'd known her, Juniper looked afraid of something that wasn't a catcher. "It's not looking at anything. It's *waiting*."

Paloma pulled off his right glove.

His hand shook. It had been shaking more lately, since the salt road, since the chapel, since he'd started spending memories like a drunk spends coin. He flexed his fingers until the tremor settled to something he could live with, and reached out, and laid his bare palm against the circlet of black gold.

The world went quiet.

Not silent — quiet, the way a house is quiet when everyone in it has stopped breathing to listen. The Feast of Bells above them faded to a rumor. Wren's breathing, Juniper's heartbeat, the drip of water somewhere in the dark — all of it receded, politely, like servants withdrawing from a room.

And then the crown opened its eye.

That was how it felt. Not sight, exactly. Something older than sight turned its attention on him, patient as sediment, and Paloma's gift — his true, hidden, terrible gift — went down into the object the way a well-bucket goes down into a well, and came back up dripping.

*Gold,* he thought at first. *Alloyed, reforged, older than the dynasty.* Names layered on names: the smith who first shaped it, the priest who blessed it, the warlord whose skull it had been fitted to before there were kings in Coldharbour at all. Ordinary history. Appraisal history. He could have written the entry now and walked away clean.

Then he touched the part of it that had been Aldous.

He recoiled so hard he knocked the pedestal, and the crown rocked, and Wren swore and caught it, and for a moment the three of them stood frozen in the dark listening for boots on stairs.

Nothing came. Above them, faintly, a thousand bells rang the second hour of the Feast.

"It looked at me," Paloma whispered.

"Crowns don't look."

"This one does." His mouth was dry. "Wren, put it back exactly — no, don't touch it with your skin, use your sleeve—" She gave him a look but complied, nudging it square on the pedestal through the wool. "It's not a treasure. It's not even an object. I've read a hundred things tonight in my head, trying to find something to compare it to, and the closest I can come is—"

He stopped. Because he had found the comparison, and he didn't want to say it, because saying it made it real.

"A mouth," Juniper finished for him.

They both looked at her.

"That's why it's not looking at me," she said. Her voice had gone flat and careful, the way it went when she was holding the ember down with both hands. "Mouths don't look. Mouths wait. You had a dog, back in Coldharbour, the butcher's dog — it didn't stare at you, it just sat by the door with its mouth open, and you knew, you just *knew*, that if you got close enough—" She hugged her arms around herself. "It's been sitting here with its mouth open for thirty years."

Thirty years. The length of Aldous's reign. The length of the famine that ended the year he was crowned.

Paloma made himself step forward again. Made himself take off the other glove. Because Veyra hadn't sent him down here to feel dread; Veyra had sent him down here to put a number on the thing, and every hour he delayed was another entry in a ledger he couldn't afford, and somewhere above them a girl named Wren's-sister had died in a room like this one, and—

No. That was the ember talking, borrowing his anger. He set it aside. He needed to be cold. Cold was how he survived. Cold was how he'd survived the temple, the debt, the years of being nobody.

He laid both palms on the crown.

This time he didn't flinch away from the deep part. He went down deliberately, past the smiths and priests and skulls, past the coronation-oil and the oaths soaked into the metal like grease into a pan, down to where the king had gone, and there —

There was the mouth.

Not teeth. Not tongue. A patience. An appetite so old and so vast that it had stopped being hunger the way the sea has stopped being wet. It didn't want gold. It didn't want power. Those were cutlery. What it wanted was the thing Paloma had felt flicker through his own gift a hundred times and always refused to name: the *self*. The bright particular thread that made a person one person and no other. Names. Souls. Whatever word you used when you were being honest.

It fed on names.

And it was full. Gods, it was full. Paloma felt the weight of what it had swallowed press up against him like grain against the sides of a sack — hundreds of threads, thousands, each one a person who had knelt before this crown or been dragged before it, each one poured in and dissolved, and the crown wore their fullness the way a fat tick wears blood, gorged and patient and ready for the next.

He found condemned criminals. He expected those; Isolde's arrangement, Cassian's letters had hinted. He found courtiers who had died conveniently. He found, horribly, a whole choir — forty voices, taken together at some jubilee, and he could still hear them singing inside it, harmonies with no throats.

And then he found himself.

It was small, as these things went. One thread among thousands, thin and recent, barely a mouthful. But he knew it the way you know your own handwriting. A memory, his memory, torn out at the root — and he felt the shape of the hole it had left before he understood what the shape contained. It was from years ago. From before the proclamation, before Juniper, before any of it. From a day he had apparently once spent standing in this same undercroft, or one like it, palm against cold gold, and he had no memory of it at all.

The crown had already tasted him.

"You've read me before," Paloma said aloud. His voice came out steady, which surprised him, because nothing else about him was steady. "I've been here. I touched you. And you took a piece and let me walk away, because a piece was enough, because you're patient, because you can afford to graze."

"Paloma." Wren's hand closed on his shoulder. "Your nose is bleeding."

He touched his upper lip. Blood, warm and thin. He wiped it on his shirt, which was already ruined, everything was ruined, and kept his hands where they were because letting go meant admitting what he'd learned, and he needed one more minute.

Because there was something else in the hole where his memory had been. Edges. Fragments clinging to the wound like splinters. He gathered them carefully, the way he'd gather spilled coins, and they came up in pieces:

*A table. Candlelight. His own younger hands, writing.*

*Brother Cassian's voice, saying:* you shouldn't have come here, boy.

*And a name — not spoken, but written, in ink that moved on the page the way his ink moved, and the name was not his own, and the hand that wrote it was shaking, and the shaking hand belonged to—*

Gone. The crown noticed him noticing, and the mouth closed over the fragments like water closing over a dropped ring, and Paloma tore his hands free and staggered back into Wren, who held him up without comment, which was the kindest thing anyone had done for him in weeks.

"We're leaving," she said.

"One moment." He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "One moment. I need to think."

"You need to bleed less. Upstairs. Now."

But Juniper hadn't moved. She stood close to the pedestal now, closer than Paloma had dared, staring at the crown with her chin lifted, and the air around her had gone strange and warm, the way it did when the ember rose near the surface of her. Not flaring. *Answering.*

"It knows you've been here," she said. "Doesn't it. That's why it tasted you early. It keeps a list too."

"Juniper—"

"It's not hungry right now." She said it with the certainty of someone reporting the weather. "It ate today. There's a man upstairs wearing it, and it ate today, and it's digesting, and that's why we're still alive." She looked at Paloma, and her eyes in the dark were very bright. "But it recognized you. When you touched it. It went *oh, him again.* I felt it. Like a dog that remembers the hand that feeds it."

"That's not comforting."

"It's not supposed to be." She stepped back at last, and the warmth in the air drained away, and she was just a small grimy girl again, except for her eyes. "You said the rite needs three names. One to hold, one to speak, one to burn." She glanced at the crown. "Whatever else that thing is, it's a ledger. Biggest one in the kingdom. Everything it ever took is still in there — you said so, I saw your face when you said so. Which means everyone it ate is still in there too."

Paloma went very still.

"And if we open it," Juniper said, "we don't just break the crown. We let everybody out."

Above them, the third hour rang. Somewhere in the palace, a feast continued, and a dead man smiled, and a queen waited, and a tinker's sister's name sat folded inside black gold like a letter that had never been delivered.

Wren had gone white. "My sister," she said. "You're telling me my sister might still be—"

"Might be." Paloma steadied himself against the wall. His legs felt borrowed. "I'm telling you the crown doesn't destroy what it takes. It *keeps*. It's a miser, not a fire. And a miser—" He laughed, and it came out cracked. "A miser can be robbed."

"Don't say robbed yet." Wren pushed him toward the stairs. "Say it when we're not standing in its pantry."

They climbed. At the top of the steps, Paloma paused, one hand on the cold iron rail, and looked back down into the dark where the crown waited on its pedestal with its patient open mouth.

It had tasted him once and let him go, because it was patient, because it could afford to graze, because it had never once in thirty years needed to hurry.

Well. Patience was a strategy for creatures that expected the world to stay the same.

The world was about to change. He had a rite, a company, a feral child with a hearth in her chest, and a hole in his memory shaped like a clue. Let the crown keep its ledger. Ledgers, Paloma knew better than anything alive, could always be balanced — and he had spent his whole life learning exactly how much things cost, and exactly who had to pay.

He wiped the blood from his lip, straightened his stolen porter's coat, and followed Wren up into the noise and light of the Feast of Bells.

Behind them, in the dark, the crown lay on its stone and waited, and if crowns could smile, it was smiling — because it had recognized the taste of him, and it remembered, in its slow old way, that he was due to be finished.

* * *

They made it four corridors before the bells changed.

Not the hour — the *tune*. A single bell, high and clear, ringing over and over from the palace's highest tower, and the feast-hall music faltered, and servants froze mid-stride with trays in their hands, and somewhere ahead a door slammed and boots began to run — not toward the feast, but away from it.

Wren caught a serving girl by the elbow. "What is it? Fire?"

The girl's face was gray. "The king," she said. "They're saying the king fell down at the high table. In front of everyone." She twisted free and ran.

Juniper grabbed Paloma's sleeve. Through the tall windows at the end of the corridor, across the courtyard, the great hall blazed with light — and even from here, even at this distance, Paloma could see the crowd pouring out of it like water from a kicked bucket, and could hear the sound rising over the bells.

Not screams. Not yet.

But the sound a crowd makes when it has seen something it cannot unsee.

"He's feeding," Juniper breathed. "In public. In front of *everyone*." Her grip tightened. "Paloma. If it's hungry in public, it's hungry everywhere. We don't have weeks anymore. We might not have *days*."

Down in the undercroft, behind iron, the mouth waited. Above them, the kingdom began, note by note, to scream.

Paloma took Juniper's hand and ran.
