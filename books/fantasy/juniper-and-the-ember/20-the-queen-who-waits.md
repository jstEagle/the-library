## 20. The Queen Who Waits

The house at the end of Candlemaker's Row had no candles in its windows.

That was the first wrong thing Paloma noticed. Every other dwelling on the row burned tallow stubs against the Feast's early dark, little orange eyes winking from behind shutters, but this one sat black and blind, its windows shuttered so tight the boards had grown into the frames. Ivy climbed it with the patience of something that had been paid to wait. The brass plate beside the door read *WIDOW HALLORAN — SEAMSTRESS*, and beneath it, smaller, older, half-erased by weather: a crest Paloma recognized only because he had spent three years copying heraldry for the Guild's appraisals.

A crown above a bell.

He stopped in the street and let Wren bump into his back.

"Well?" she said. "Is it the place or not?"

"It's the place." His voice came out flat. "It's also a lie. The widow Halloran doesn't exist. This house belongs to the crown."

Juniper pressed closer to his side, hood up, ember-hidden, though nothing could truly hide her — Paloma had learned that the hard way. Even through wool and shadow he could feel her warmth like a hand resting between his shoulder blades. "Then why are we knocking on it?"

"Because Isolde's letters were signed with a seal only she would have," Cassian had said, pressing the wax impression into Paloma's palm two nights ago, blood on his sleeve, the catchers' whistles still echoing down the Salt Road behind them. "And because a woman who hides a queen's seal inside a seamstress's shop is either the queen herself or the person who decides what the queen eats for breakfast. Either way, you'll want to be polite."

So Paloma knocked.

The door opened before his knuckles fell a third time. A woman stood there, sixty if she was a day, built like a barrel and dressed in servant's grey gone soft with age, her hands folded as though she'd been standing behind that door for years waiting for someone to knock. Her eyes went over each of them in turn — Paloma, Wren, Juniper, and last, lingering longest on Juniper, with an expression Paloma couldn't name and didn't like.

"You're the scribe," she said.

"I'm a scribe."

"Don't quibble with me, boy. She said you'd come with ink-stained fingers and a face like an unpaid bill." The woman stepped back. "In. Quickly. And the child keeps her hands to herself."

*She said.* Paloma filed those two words away as they crossed the threshold into a hall that smelled of lavender laid over something else, something older and sweeter, like fruit going bad in a locked room. The shutters swallowed the light. Somewhere deeper in the house, a clock ticked with the slow reluctance of a thing counting down rather than up.

* * *

They were given a room with no fireplace and told to wait, which Paloma found insulting and Wren found funny. "We've been chased across half a kingdom," she said, settling onto a bench and propping her boots on it, "and now we're being made to sit in the parlor like tradesmen. There's a joke in there about my life."

Juniper didn't laugh. She stood at the window, prying one slat of the shutter apart with a fingernail, watching the street. She'd been doing that since the chapel. Since Veyra's men. Since the night they'd run and left Cassian's body in the mud and hadn't even been able to bury him properly, just cover him with Wren's coat and promise him things neither of them believed.

"Paloma." Her voice was small. "What if it's a trap?"

"It's a trap," he said. "Almost certainly. Everything worth walking into is."

"That's not comforting."

"I wasn't aiming for comfort. I was aiming for honesty, which is rarer." He joined her at the window. Through the gap: the empty street, the candlelit houses opposite, a cat moving along a wall with the self-importance of all cats. "But think about it from their side. If the crown wanted us dead, we'd be dead. We've been running for nine days and every catcher who's found us has been slightly too slow, slightly too few, slightly too badly armed. Someone is clearing our path."

"Veyra?"

"Veyra sells. She doesn't clear." He watched the cat vanish over the wall. "No. Whoever runs this house wants us here. Which means whoever runs this house needs something only we can do."

"And that's better than being hunted?"

"No," Paloma said. "But it's negotiable."

Juniper let the slat fall closed. In the dimness her face was all shadows and one bright point of reflected light where her eyes caught the street-glow leaking under the door. "You always talk like the ledger's balanced at the end."

"Because I write the ending I want to see," Paloma said, "and then I work backward."

She almost smiled. It was the first almost-smile in four days, and he counted it like coin.

* * *

They waited two hours. Then the clock downstairs struck a hour that didn't exist — thirteen strokes, deliberate, unmistakable — and the door opened again.

The servant woman led them down a corridor lined with portraits, all of them turned to face the wall. Paloma noticed that because noticing was his trade. Faces turned away. Names hidden. The whole house was arranged around the principle that some things must exist without being seen, and he felt a kinship with it so strong it frightened him.

At the corridor's end, a door of plain oak. The servant knocked once, paused, knocked twice more, and opened it.

The room beyond was small. Smaller than any room a queen should occupy. A bed, a desk, a window painted over so that no light came through it, and shelves of books worn soft at the spines. A fire burned low in a grate, and before the fire, in a chair that had clearly been moved from somewhere grander and more comfortable, sat a woman in a grey dress with silver at her temples and her hands folded in her lap exactly the way the servant's had been.

Queen Isolde looked up at them, and Paloma's gift rose in him unbidden, the way it always did when he was tired or afraid, reading the room before he'd decided to read anything at all.

He read: *grief, old and load-bearing*. He read: *arithmetic, endless, done in the dark*. He read: *a name spoken so often in private that it has worn smooth, like a stone carried in a pocket*.

And underneath all of it, faint as watermark: *guilt*, the way a cellar smells of damp — always present, never mentioned.

"Sit," the queen said. "All of you. You'll want to be seated for this."

Wren took the wall, arms crossed, one hand — her remaining one — resting easy near the knife at her belt. Juniper sat on the very edge of a chair as if it might be taken back. Paloma remained standing, because he had learned that standing people heard more.

"I expected the scribe," Isolde said. "I didn't expect the tinker or the girl."

"The girl comes with the scribe," Wren said. "Package arrangement."

"The girl," Isolde said, and her voice gentled in a way that made Paloma's skin crawl, "is the most dangerous thing in this kingdom, and she's sitting in my sitting room eating nothing, and everyone in Coldharbour with a ledger open is looking for her. Do you understand what you're carrying, scribe?"

"I understand what I'm protecting."

"Do you?" For the first time the queen leaned forward, and the firelight caught her face, and Paloma saw that she was beautiful the way winter is beautiful — precisely, coldly, with death implied in the structure. "Then tell me. What do you know of how the king stays alive?"

Paloma chose his words the way he chose entries: carefully, completely, leaving nothing out that mattered. "The man on the throne isn't Aldous. Aldous died — I read it off him at the public audience, months ago. What wears his shape is the crown. It feeds on the gifted. It's eaten dozens. Hundreds, maybe. And it's getting hungrier, because whatever's holding it together is failing."

"Yes." Isolde closed her eyes briefly. "Yes, that's the shape of it. Now the part you don't know." She opened her eyes. "Who do you imagine it's been feeding on? These last years. All those gifted souls your Guild catalogues and sells. Where do you think they go after the catchers take them?"

Paloma felt the floor tilt a degree. "To the palace. To the undercroft—"

"They go to the crown," Isolde said. "And the crown is fed on a schedule. Quarterly. A list arrives, sealed, from this house. Condemned prisoners, mostly — murderers, traitors, the ones the courts have already written off. Men and women whose deaths were scheduled anyway. I sign the list. I have signed the list for eleven years." She said it evenly, the way Paloma might recite a ledger line. "Eleven years of choosing which of the condemned die a week early, so that the crown doesn't reach further. So that it doesn't start taking poets. Children. Anyone whose gift might matter."

The silence in the room had weight. Juniper had gone very still, the stillness of prey that hasn't yet decided whether the thing in front of it is predator or shelter.

"You fed it," Paloma said. Not a question. His gift was reading her faster now, helplessly, and everything it returned confirmed the words: the guilt-watermark, the arithmetic, the worn-smooth name. "You kept it alive."

"I kept it *contained*," Isolde said, and for the first time her composure cracked, just at one corner, just enough to show the structure beneath. "Do you know what I inherited, scribe? A husband who walked into a binding rite to save his people from famine and never came back out — not really. A crown that wakes a little more each year. A court full of vipers who would burn the gifted in the streets if the hunt ever became honest instead of bureaucratic. And a kingdom of eight million souls who needed bread, and law, and time." Her hands tightened in her lap. "So I bought time. With prisoners. With monsters, mostly — I made sure of that, I vetted every name — until the monsters ran low and the courts started producing convenient traitors, and then—" She stopped. Breathed. "And then it was eleven years later and I was signing lists quarterly and telling myself the same sentence every time. *This buys reform. This buys the schools. This buys the year the regency council can be formed.* And the sentence kept being true, and the lists kept getting longer, and my husband's body kept sitting on that throne wearing my husband's face and smiling at me across state dinners with his mouth full of the dead."

Juniper spoke for the first time. "Did you love him?"

The question landed in the room like a dropped glass. Isolde looked at the girl for a long moment.

"Every day," she said. "That's the trap of it. If I'd loved him less, I'd have burned the crown in year one and damned the consequences. If I'd loved him less, I'd have let the thing starve and taken my chances with the famine returning. But I loved him, and he loved me enough to do the binding alone, without telling me the price, and so every prisoner I feed it is a letter I'm writing to a man who can't read it anymore." She rose, crossed to the desk, and picked up a paper — the current list, Paloma realized, forty names in a clerk's neat hand, awaiting her signature. "This quarter's. Forty names. I've been staring at it for six days trying to find forty monsters among them, and there aren't forty monsters. There's a debtor. Two poachers. A boy of nineteen who threw a stone at a tax collector." She set it down. "The arithmetic has finally failed. That's why you're here."

Wren shifted against the wall. "Why *are* we here, Majesty? Say it plainly. I've got no patience for royal preambles."

"Plainly, then." Isolde faced them. "You know the Binding of the Crown — or pieces of it. The hollow chapel showed you the rite. Three names: one to hold, one to speak, one to burn. My husband performed it alone, forcing himself into all three roles, and it cost him everything — the crown ate the sacrifice whole because there was no one to anchor him, no one to speak him back, no one to share the burning. Done properly, with three willing names, the binding can be *undone*. The crown can be unmade. And the thing inside it starves."

"And you want us to perform it," Paloma said slowly. "For you. On your schedule. Under your control."

"I want it *done*," Isolde said. "Before the next list. Before the crown grows impatient and starts taking from the living freely. Before—" her voice caught, barely, "—before what's left of Aldous is used up entirely. There's a remnant of him in there. I've seen it surface, twice, in eleven years. Twice. Like a drowning man's hand breaking water. I am not ready for it to stop surfacing."

Paloma's gift was screaming at him now, quietly, the way it did when truth was nearby. He ignored it, because he already knew what it would say.

"And the cost," he said. "You know the cost. One to hold, one to speak, one to burn. The speaker surrenders his name. The burner—" he glanced at Juniper, and couldn't finish.

"I know the cost," Isolde said. "I've had eleven years to study it. And I'll tell you what I told myself the day I signed my first list: someone pays, or everyone pays. The only choice we ever get is *who*." She sat back down, folding her hands, the queen again, the arithmetic resumed. "Now. Will you hear my plan, or will you stand there judging me while the boy of nineteen waits on my signature?"

Outside, the painted window held back the evening. The clock in the hall ticked its countdown. And Paloma thought of Cassian, erasing his name from temple reports for ten years out of love, and understood suddenly, horribly, that he was looking at a woman who had done the same thing — except her reports had been lists of the dead, and her love had fed the mouth it mourned.

"We'll hear it," he said.

And Queen Isolde began, in the level voice of a woman who had rehearsed confession for a decade, to lay out the terms of the end of the world she had managed so carefully for so long — while Juniper, beside him, went so quiet and so still that Paloma felt the warmth of her gift rise like a held breath, and knew, with ledger-certainty, that the ember inside her had heard every word too, and was interested.
