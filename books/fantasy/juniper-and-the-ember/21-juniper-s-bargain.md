## 21. Juniper's Bargain

The ember talked to her best at night.

Not in words, exactly. Words were a thing it had learned from her, borrowed like it borrowed everything — her warmth, her hunger, the shape of her wanting. What it really spoke was older than language: heat against cold skin, the memory of a hearth in a house she couldn't quite recall, the feeling of being held. It spoke in comfort. That was how she'd first trusted it, when she was small enough to sleep in doorways and the winters got into her bones and something inside her said, *I'll keep you warm if you keep me fed.*

She hadn't known then what feeding meant.

Now she sat on the roof of Wren's workshop — because that was what you called the leaning shed where Wren mended clocks and kettles and, lately, the company's boots — with her knees pulled up to her chin and the whole sleeping town of Millbrook below her. The chapel ruins were a dark shape past the orchard wall. Paloma was down there somewhere among the fallen stones, reading his old rite-echoes by candlelight, hunting for the missing piece of the Binding. Wren slept with her one hand curled around her sister's list. Queen Isolde — though nobody called her that except Paloma, who was polite about everything including treason — dozed in the chair by the fire like a woman who had spent twenty years not daring to close her eyes.

And Juniper sat on the roof with a monster in her chest, listening to it make its offer.

*You're tired,* it said, in the voice of every kind word anyone had ever said to her, stitched together. *You've been tired your whole life.*

"I'm not talking to you," she muttered. A moth blundered past her face toward the chimney smoke, drawn by nothing, confused by her. That happened sometimes now. Living things got lost around her when the ember leaned forward.

*You don't have to talk,* it said. *That's the beauty of it. You never had to carry this alone. I carried it with you. Who else did?*

The awful part was that it was right about that, or right enough to hurt. Paloma had come along later, and Wren, and even the queen with her sad careful hands. But the ember had been there in the ash-bin years, in the cellar under the tannery, in the winter she'd burned a man's hand off without meaning to and run until her feet bled. The ember had been the only thing in the world that wanted her near it.

It also wanted to eat her whole. Both things could be true. She'd learned that from watching queens.

*Here is what I know,* it murmured, settling deeper behind her ribs, warming her from the inside out the way it did when it meant business. *I know what Paloma found in those chapel echoes. Three names. One to hold, one to speak, one to burn.*

"Shut up."

*One to burn, little wick. And everyone in that house downstairs has already decided it's you. Don't they think I can feel it? Their pity comes off them like steam. They look at you and they see a candle they can spend.*

"They haven't decided anything yet." Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "We're still arguing."

*Arguing about which of you gets to die. How generous. How noble.* The ember let silence stretch, and in the silence it showed her things. It was good at showing her things — it reached into her own head and rearranged her memories like a peddler arranging fruit so the bruised sides faced away. It showed her Wren's face when she'd laughed at dinner, flour on her nose from the flatbread. It showed her Paloma asleep over his notes, ink on his cheek, looking young for once instead of hunted. It showed her the wooden bird Wren was carving in secret, badly, the wings lopsided, and Juniper felt her throat go tight because she wasn't supposed to know about the bird yet.

*If you burn in the rite,* the ember said softly, *all of that ends. Not just your light. Them too, likely — a binding gone wrong takes the room with it. But if you give yourself to me fully — not death, little wick, *merging* — no rite needs doing at all. No name spent. No three-fold price. I take the crown's hunger into myself and I feed on *you*, willingly given, and willing meat feeds clean. No prisoners. No taken. No more lists like Wren's sister's. Everyone lives.*

Juniper's breath stopped.

*Everyone lives,* it repeated, tasting the words, savoring her flinch of hope. *Wren keeps both hands' worth of tomorrows. The scribe keeps his name, which he's already halfway to losing anyway, poor moth. The queen keeps her guilt to do penance with, and she does love her penance. And you — you stop being afraid. Forever. You become the warmest thing in the world, and everyone you love sits beside you and is warm, all their lives, because of you. Isn't that what you always wanted? To matter?*

"That's not —" She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "That's not what I want."

*Isn't it? Then tell me what you want. Tell me true. Because I've been in here since before you had words, and I have never once heard you say it.*

And that was the trap inside the trap, wasn't it. Because she didn't know. She knew what she didn't want — the cellars, the catchers, the lists, the way adults looked through her like she was weather. She knew what she wanted *against*. But wanting-toward, wanting-for, some future with her own face on it — that shelf in her was empty. The ember had kept it empty on purpose, she understood suddenly, all these years. An empty girl was an easy house to move into.

"You lie," she said. She made herself say it out loud, made the words solid. "You always lie. When you said the man in the tannery yard deserved burning. When you said nobody would ever come for me. When you said you were my friend." Her hands were shaking. Heat shimmered off the roof tiles around her, faint as breath on glass. "You're lying now. Merging isn't mercy. It's just eating with better manners."

The ember went very still.

Then it laughed — a low rolling warmth, almost fond. *There she is,* it said. *There's my wick. Do you know, in four hundred years of houses, only two of you ever talked back?*

"Who was the other one?"

Silence. A long, hot, interested silence, and Juniper realized she'd asked the wrong question, or maybe exactly the right one, because the ember's stillness had a texture she recognized: the texture of a secret.

*Ask your scribe,* it said finally. *He reads old things. Ask him what burned before Aldous wore the crown, and why the Binding needed making in the first place. Ask him what happens to a gift when its vessel burns up in the binding — where does the fire go? Where does it always go?*

"What are you —"

*I am patient,* the ember said, and its warmth wrapped her like a blanket, like a shroud, like arms. *I have been patient through forty kings. I can be patient through one small girl deciding. But decide soon, little wick. The crown is hungry and getting hungrier, and hungry things get careless, and careless things start fires that spread to rooftops where children sit alone at night believing themselves safe.*

Below her, past the orchard wall, the candle in the chapel ruins guttered.

*

She climbed down and didn't go inside. She told herself it was to check on Paloma, which was true, but it was also true that she couldn't be in the warm house tonight, under the same roof as Wren's snoring and the queen's careful breathing, while the ember's offer sat in her chest like a swallowed coal.

The chapel was cold. Paloma had lit three candles and arranged his papers around him in the neat fan he thought nobody noticed was a nervous habit. He looked up when she dropped through the broken window, and his face did the thing it always did — the small unguarded brightening he probably thought he hid, followed immediately by worry, because Paloma worried the way other men breathed.

"You should be asleep," he said.

"So should you."

"Yes," he admitted. "Well." He moved his cloak off a fallen slab and patted it, an offering, and she sat, because sitting next to Paloma was one of the few things in the world that had never once cost her anything.

For a while neither of them talked. He went back to his papers, copying out the echo-fragments in his small precise hand, and she watched the candles. All three flames leaned toward her, gently, like flowers toward sun. She made herself straighten them. It took effort, holding her gift on a leash, and the effort made the ember chuckle somewhere behind her sternum.

"The rite needs three names," she said, finally, because she'd decided on the roof that she would do this, and Juniper had learned that if you didn't do the brave thing in the first minute the fear grew roots. "One to hold. One to speak. One to burn."

Paloma's pen stopped.

"Wren will hold," he said carefully. "She's already claimed it, with her sister's name as anchor. And I'll speak. Which means —"

"Which means you lose your name. Your whole self. Unwritten." She turned to look at him. His face in the candlelight was thin and stubborn and scared, and he was going to do it anyway, and that was the worst part. "And the third slot. Nobody's fighting over that one, are they?"

"Juniper."

"Because everybody's already decided. You, and Wren, and Her Majesty with her martyr eyes. Everybody's decided it's me, and nobody's *said* it, because saying it out loud would be rude, and we're all being so terribly polite about who gets fed to the fire." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it, hated the ember for hearing it, hated herself for giving it material.

Paloma set down his pen. He did that thing where he gave a sentence its full weight before answering, like weighing coins.

"You're right," he said. "That it's unfair. That we've been cowards about naming it. I'll own that." He folded his hands. "But I won't pretend there's an easy answer, because there isn't. The rite wants a burning. Something has to go into the fire. That's the mathematics of it, and I hate mathematics tonight."

"And if there was another way?" She heard her own heartbeat. Behind her ribs, the ember went silent and attentive, listening through her ears. "If there was a way where nobody burned? Where nobody lost their name?" *Where I stop being afraid forever*, whispered the coal, and she shoved the whisper down deep. "Would you take it? Even if the cost was — even if the cost was me becoming something else?"

Paloma frowned. Not suspicious — he wasn't built for suspicion; that was his flaw and his glory — but thinking, genuinely thinking, taking her question seriously the way he took everything seriously.

"Becoming something else," he repeated. "What kind of something?"

"A big something. A warm something. Something that could hold the hunger so nobody else ever had to." Her mouth was dry. "Something people could love. Sit beside. Be safe next to." The ember's promises rose in her throat wearing her own voice, and for one vertiginous second she couldn't tell which words were hers and which were its, and that frightened her more than any catcher ever had. "A hearth. Instead of a hunger."

The candle nearest her flared. Just a little. Just enough.

Paloma saw it. Of course he saw it — the man read ink that moved on its own, he missed nothing — and she watched understanding cross his face slowly, like frost forming on a window. Understanding, and then something worse than suspicion: grief.

"Juniper," he said, very quietly, "what did it offer you?"

"It doesn't offer. It *suggests* —"

"*What did it offer you?*"

The candles all bent toward her at once, three small flames straining on their wicks like dogs at a rope, and the temperature of the chapel climbed, and somewhere in the walls old mortar ticked as it warmed. Juniper stood up fast, backing toward the broken window, because if she stayed close to him while it was this loud in her chest she was going to hurt him, she'd hurt people before, she'd burned a man's —

"Don't," she said. "Don't look at me like I'm a ledger entry. Like I'm a risk you need to manage."

"I'm looking at you like you're eleven years old and carrying something that talks to you in the dark, and nobody should have to do that alone, and I am *furious* that you have been." He stood too. He was not a tall man and not a brave-looking one, ink-stained and stooped from decades over desks, but he crossed the ruined nave toward her like a man crossing a river he'd already decided to swim. "Whatever it promised you — however good it sounded — it lied. That's what it does. That's all it knows how to do. Hunger lies, Juniper. I've read the accounts. I've read what it did to the vessels before you, and none of them merged, none of them became hearths, they were all —"

"Consumed," she whispered. "I know. It showed me the pretty version anyway. And the horrible thing is—" her voice broke clean through, "—the horrible thing is I *wanted* it. For one whole minute on that roof I wanted it, because it said everyone lives, and I would rather be eaten than watch any of you burn, and it *knows* that, that's why it picked that promise, it reached into my head and found the softest spot and —"

She was crying. She was crying and the candles were roaring and Paloma did the last thing the ember expected, which was the first thing anyone had ever done.

He hugged her.

Arms around her shoulders, her cheek against his rough wool coat that smelled of ink and candle smoke and the temple soap he still used out of habit, and the ember shrieked somewhere behind her ribs — not in rage, in *disgust*, recoiling from the contact like a cat from water — and Juniper sobbed into the coat of a debt-bound temple scribe who owed the Guild his whole life and had spent it anyway, all of it, on a feral child he'd found in his archives three weeks ago.

"You don't get to disappear," he said into her hair, fierce and quiet. "Not by merging, not by burning, not by any clever bargain that trades you for us. That's not — do you hear me? We are not a transaction. None of this is a transaction. You are not collateral. You are *family*, and family doesn't get spent to settle accounts."

"You owe the Guild everything," she managed, wet and furious. "You literally owe them everything, you're the most indebted person alive, and you're telling me transactions aren't real?"

"The most indebted person alive," Paloma agreed gravely, "is exactly the right person to recognize a bad deal." He pulled back and held her at arm's length, and his eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely certain. "Tomorrow I'll show you what I found in the echoes. There's a piece missing from the standard telling of the rite — something about where the fire goes after a binding. The ember doesn't want you asking that question, which means it's the important one. Whatever it's hiding, we'll find it together. That's the deal. The only deal. Nobody vanishes, nobody merges, nobody burns alone. Agreed?"

Behind her ribs, the coal seethed and sulked and, she noticed, went quiet in a new way. A *planning* way.

"Agreed," Juniper said.

They walked back across the orchard together in the grey before-dawn, and she was so busy holding onto the warm weight of the word *family* that she didn't notice the details. She didn't notice that the lane was too quiet for morning. That no smoke rose from the bakery chimneys. That the birds had stopped.

She didn't notice the four horses tied outside Wren's gate, or the Guild badge glinting on the rider who sat waiting in the grey light with Veyra's seal on his collar, until they were thirty steps away and turning was already too late.

The rider smiled at Paloma like an old acquaintance.

"Scribe Coldharbour," he said pleasantly. "Mistress Veyra sends her regards. And her invoice."
