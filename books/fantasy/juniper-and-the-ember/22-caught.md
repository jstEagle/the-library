## 22. Caught

**WREN**

The tinker's rule was simple: never sleep in the same place twice in a week. Wren had kept that rule for eleven years, through two warrants and one hanging she'd walked away from by being three streets over when the rope went up. So when she woke in the hollow chapel for the fourth consecutive night with her back against the same cold stone and Juniper's head heavy on her shoulder, she knew exactly what kind of fool she'd become.

The kind who'd found something worth staying stupid for.

She eased out from under the girl without waking her — a trick of the old trade, moving like smoke — and climbed to the chapel's broken east window where the salt marsh gave way to the road. Dawn was coming up grey as dishwater. The reeds stood still.

Too still. No marsh birds. Wren had spent enough years listening for catchers to know that silence wasn't peace; it was payment. Something out there had bought the morning quiet, and things that bought quiet always came to collect.

She counted what she could see: four riders on the causeway, moving slow, no hurry in them at all. Men who were sure of their ground. Behind them, on foot, a dozen more fanning out into the reeds in a line so wide it could only mean one thing.

They weren't looking for tracks. They already knew where everyone slept.

Veyra.

Wren's hand closed around the wooden bird in her pocket — she'd been carving it at night, whittling juniper wood because the girl liked the smell of it, and the shape was nearly done, wings half-spread, beak open like it was about to sing. She'd meant to give it to Juniper this morning. She'd been saving the eyes for last because eyes were the part that made a thing alive.

"Paloma," she said, low, not turning. "We're sold."

Behind her, the scribe sat up too fast, ink-stained fingers scrabbling for his spectacles. He didn't ask how she knew. That was one thing she liked about him — he did arithmetic instead of arguing.

"How many?"

"Four horse, twelve or more afoot, spread wide. They'll have the causeway and both fen paths." She turned. In the grey light his face had gone the color of old paper. "Your Guild mistress wants her interest."

"She can't—" Paloma stopped himself. Wren watched him do it, watch him try to make Veyra's betrayal impossible and fail. "The appraisal. I owe her an appraisal of the crown. If she sells us before—"

"Then she sells you to the crown and collects from both ends." Wren shrugged. "That's not treachery, scribe. That's just good bookkeeping."

Juniper was awake now. Wren saw it in the way the girl went still — not the stillness of sleep but the coiled stillness of something wild deciding whether to run or bite. The candle stubs on the chapel floor leaned toward her, all six flames bending like grass in wind that wasn't there.

"They're coming for me," Juniper said. Not a question.

"They're coming for all of us," Wren said. "Difference is, they've got a price list. You're worth a fortune. Paloma's worth a fortune. Me—" she tapped the stump of her right wrist against her knee, "—I'm worth a rope. So let's not stand about admiring the arithmetic."

* * *

**PALOMA**

There was no time for a plan, so Paloma did what he always did when there was no time: he opened a ledger in his head and started cutting losses.

Cassian would not run. He understood that the moment he looked at the old monk, who had come out of the chapel's south alcove already dressed, already calm, already carrying the iron-shod walking staff he'd used on the road from Coldharbour. Brother Cassian had spent thirty years erasing names from temple reports, and a man who spends thirty years hiding other people does not start running when the bill comes due. He starts paying.

"You'll take the fen path north," Cassian said. It was not a suggestion. His voice had the flat certainty of the liturgy, the voice he used for words that had been true long before any of them were born. "It floods at high tide. The catchers know that, which means they'll leave it thin. You have perhaps an hour of water shallow enough to wade, and after that an hour where anything following you drowns."

"And you?" Paloma asked, though he already knew.

"I will be on the causeway," Cassian said, "being very visible, and very slow, and very confusing about which direction an old man went."

"No." The word came out of Paloma harder than he intended. "Brother, they killed the last two monks they caught on suspicion alone. You've been striking my name from their reports for fifteen years. When they take you, they'll find the gaps. They'll—"

"Paloma." Cassian smiled, and it was the smile from the novitiate, the one that had made a terrified boy with moving ink feel like the world had one safe room in it. "I am sixty-eight years old. I have buried every friend I started with. Do you imagine I fear the accounting? I have been balancing my ledger toward this day since before you could hold a pen."

"That's not—" Paloma's throat closed. "That's not fair."

"No," Cassian agreed gently. "Very little is. Fairness is for the next life. This one only has love, and what we do with it." He pressed something into Paloma's hand — small, cold, a lead seal stamped with the temple's broken sun. "If you reach the capital, show this to anyone wearing grey robes. There are more of us than Veyra knows. There always were."

From outside, faint across the water, came a sound that made the marsh birds finally break their silence: a horn, three notes, rising. The crown's call. Every catcher in the fen answering it like dogs to a bell.

"They're close," Wren said from the door. She had Juniper by the hand. "Scribe. Now."

Paloma looked at Cassian. Cassian looked back with an expression Paloma would spend the rest of his life trying to describe and never quite manage — pride, maybe, or permission, or simply the look of a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was heavy.

"Go," Cassian said. "And Paloma — whatever they tell you your name costs, remember it was never theirs to price."

Then the old monk turned and walked out onto the causeway, staff ringing on the stones, straight toward the riders, singing the dawn office at the top of his considerable voice.

* * *

**JUNIPER**

The fen path was water to the knees and mud to the shins and Juniper hated every step of it, because the ember hated it more. Cold water made the thing inside her shrink and sulk, and when it sulked it whispered, and its whispering had gotten louder ever since the horns started.

*Leave them,* it said, in the voice it borrowed from her mother, dead eight years. *You're fast. You're fire. Run north and none of this touches you.*

"Shut up," Juniper muttered.

"What?" Wren called back, ten paces ahead, hauling herself through the reeds with her one hand and her whole bad temper.

"Nothin'."

But the ember kept talking. It had learned, somewhere in the last weeks, that Juniper was weakest at dawn, when she was coldest and hungriest and most afraid. *They die either way,* it said. *The old one first, then the tinker, then the ink-man. Fire always wins, little wick. Be on the winning side.*

"They're not going to die," Juniper said out loud, and Wren glanced back at her, and Juniper shook her head like she was shaking off flies.

The truth was the ember wasn't wrong about everything. Juniper *was* fast. She could feel the heat waiting under her skin like a struck match, feel how easy it would be to turn around and put a wall of flame across the causeway, to burn the catchers off the face of the marsh and end the whole ugly morning. The ember showed her pictures of it. It was very generous with pictures.

It never showed her what came after. That was the trick of it. Every gift it offered had the price torn off.

Behind them, across the flat grey water, she heard the singing stop.

Not fade. Stop. Mid-verse, the way a string snaps.

Juniper stood hip-deep in the fen with the cold biting her bones and felt the exact moment Brother Cassian's voice left the world, and something in her chest answered it — the ember flaring hot and furious, steam rising off the water around her waist in a sudden white bloom, and Wren spun around with her eyes wide.

"Girl. Girl, bank it, *bank it*, they'll see the steam—"

"I can't," Juniper whispered, and it was true, and it was the most frightening sentence she'd ever spoken. "He's gone. Wren, he's *gone*, I felt it go—"

Wren crossed the water to her in four strides and did the thing nobody had ever done: she took Juniper's face in her single rough hand and held it, forehead to forehead, and said, "Then grieve later. Right now you grieve *moving*. Can you do that? Grieve moving?"

Juniper could. It turned out you could carry almost anything if you kept your legs going.

* * *

**PALOMA**

They heard the catchers behind them before they saw them — two men who had guessed the fen path after all, or who had simply been sent to sweep it, boots sucking in the mud, calling to each other in the bored voices of men doing a job.

Wren pulled them off the path into a stand of black alders, and Paloma crouched in the shallows with Juniper pressed against him and understood, with terrible clarity, that they were not going to make it. The alder stand was thin. The water was loud. Juniper was radiating heat like a forge, and the steam rose off her shoulders in a plume visible from the moon.

The first catcher came through the trees alone, ahead of his partner, sword drawn, and he was young — that was the detail Paloma's mind seized on, uselessly, horribly. Young, with a chapped face and a cheap sword, and he saw them immediately because there was nowhere to hide, and he shouted for his partner, and Wren stepped out of the reeds and hit him in the throat with the edge of her hand before the shout became a word.

He went down making a sound like a kettle. Not dead. Dying, maybe, or maybe just ruined for a while, but the partner had heard, and the partner was fast, and suddenly there were swords and screaming birds and Wren fighting with one hand and a boat-hook she'd carried since Coldharbour, holding the narrow path like it was a bridge in a story.

"We can't win this," Paloma said.

"Speak for yourself," Wren grunted, and caught a blade on the hook's shaft, and shoved the second catcher backward into deep water where his armor decided his future for him.

But there were more behind him. Paloma could hear them — five, six, spreading along the path, and the tide was turning, and the deep channels were filling, and the arithmetic was finished and the sum was ugly.

So he did the thing he had sworn on Cassian's blood he would not do.

He reached for the nearest name.

The third catcher came around the alders at a run, and Paloma read him in half a heartbeat — *Tomas Ferrow, debt-bound, mother in Coldharbour, afraid of deep water, afraid of his captain, afraid most of all of being the kind of man who does this work* — and spoke the true name aloud, softly, the way you'd speak to a spooked animal.

The man stopped as if he'd walked into glass. Stood there in the mud with his sword up and his eyes full of tears, because Paloma hadn't commanded him. He'd only shown him to himself. It was enough. Tomas Ferrow sat down in the fen and began, quietly, to sob.

"One at a time," Paloma said to Wren, and his voice sounded far away to his own ears, and somewhere inside his skull a door he couldn't see swung shut on a room he couldn't remember. "I can do one at a time."

He could. For four of them he could. On the fifth, the name came up out of him like a tooth, and with it went something small and warm he would spend years trying to identify — the memory, though he didn't know it yet, of his mother humming while she mended, gone now, gone forever, traded for ninety seconds of a stranger standing down.

The sixth didn't stand down. The sixth was Veyra's own man, guild-trained, mind like a locked strongbox, and when Paloma reached for his name the man smiled and said, "Mistress said you'd try that, scribe. She said to tell you: *interest compounds.*"

Then the world went white at the edges, because the man threw something — not a net, not a chain, but a handful of ash that burned cold wherever it touched, guild-work, anti-gift, and it caught Juniper square in the chest, and she screamed, and the scream wasn't a child's scream, it was the sound of a furnace being smothered.

The ember went dark.

Juniper dropped.

* * *

**JUNIPER**

Cold. She had never been so cold in her life. The ember wasn't gone — she could feel it, far away, behind a wall, beating like a heart in another room — but it couldn't reach her, and she couldn't reach it, and hands were dragging her up out of the water by her arms while she thrashed and bit and heard Wren roaring somewhere close, and then a sound she'd remember for the rest of her life:

A whistle. Two notes, falling.

Every catcher within a mile stopped moving.

Through the ash-haze Juniper saw why. Down on the causeway, where the riders waited, a woman had dismounted — tall, grey-cloaked, moving with the unhurried economy of someone who owned everything she surveyed. Veyra. And beside her, held on a rope like a dog, kneeling in the mud with blood in his white hair—

No. Not kneeling. Laid out. Arranged, the way you arrange goods for inspection.

"Cassian," Juniper heard Paloma say, in a voice with nothing left in it.

"He chose his ground," Veyra called across the water, and her voice carried the way money carries, smooth and final. "Frankly, scribe, he cost me three good men. I'm putting him on the invoice." She gestured, and two catchers hauled Wren back from the boat-hook she'd been swinging, wrenched it away, bound her wrists with guild-cord that smoked faintly where it touched her skin. "The girl comes with me. The scribe comes with me. The tinker goes to the crown — she has a history, and histories are owed."

"You're selling us to the thing on the throne," Paloma said. He was on his knees in the fen, ash-burned, empty-handed, and Juniper had never seen anyone look so thoroughly bankrupt. "You know what it does to us. You *appraised* what it does."

"I know precisely what it does." Veyra picked her way along the path until she stood over him, and crouched, and for one moment — one strange, unbearable moment — her voice dropped its polish entirely. "I also know what happens to a guild of impossible trades when the crown finishes hunting the gifted and starts wondering who facilitated their escape. I am not choosing the crown, scribe. I am choosing to exist next month." She stood. "You taught me that, actually. Chapter nine of your own ledger. *When every account fails, pay the creditor that kills you slowest.*"

She snapped her fingers, and the world became ropes and orders and the grey march back along the causeway, past the place where they had laid Brother Cassian, and Juniper turned her head as they passed because she refused to look away, because someone should look, because he had sung at dawn for them.

His face was peaceful. That was the worst part. He looked like a man who had finally set down his ledger and found it balanced.

* * *

**PALOMA**

They put him in a cart, of all things. A guild cart, canvas-covered, rolling east toward the capital with the tide coming in behind them and the fen birds returning to their business as if the morning had been ordinary. Wren was lashed to the tailboard where he could see her. Juniper sat beside him, ash-marked, ember-smothered, her small cold hand finding his in the dark under the canvas and gripping it hard enough to hurt.

Neither of them spoke for a long time. What was there to say that the wheels weren't already saying?

Finally Juniper shifted against his shoulder. "Paloma."

"Yes, little wick."

"The ash. It didn't kill it. The ember. I can feel it — far off, like — like a fire in the next house." Her grip tightened. "It's angry. It's never been angry before. It's always been hungry, but this is different."

"How is it different?"

She was quiet a moment. When she answered, her voice had gone strange and certain, the voice of someone reporting weather from a country only she could see.

"It's pulling *toward* the city. Toward the crown." She looked up at him, and even in the dark he could see her eyes had a coal-light in them that hadn't been there before the ash. "Paloma. I think it wants to fight."

Ahead of them, through a gap in the canvas, the towers of the capital rose out of the morning haze — and above the highest tower, above the palace, the sky had a faint bruised shimmer to it, like heat haze in winter, like air over a mouth about to speak.

The Feast of Bells was in three days.

The crown already knew they were coming. And for the first time since Coldharbour, Paloma understood that this might not be entirely a disaster — because Veyra had sold them to the king, yes.

But she had also just delivered the rite's three names directly to the throne room door.

All they had to do now was survive long enough to use them.
