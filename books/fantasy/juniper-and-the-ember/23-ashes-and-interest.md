## 23. Ashes and Interest

The safehouse smelled of tallow smoke and wet stone, and of Cassian.

That was the worst part — that a man could be dead for two days and still be everywhere in a room. His cloak hung on the peg by the door where he'd hung it the night before the catchers came. His spectacles sat folded on the table beside a half-eaten heel of bread he'd never finish, because Wren had been taken and Cassian had stepped into the street with his arms spread wide like a man greeting rain, and said, *Take me instead, I'm the one you want,* and the catchers, being practical men, had obliged him.

Paloma sat at the table and looked at the spectacles and did not touch them.

He had been sitting there since dawn. He knew this because the light through the boarded window had moved from one wall to the other, and his tea had gone cold twice, and Juniper had come down once to look at him and gone back up without saying anything, her bare feet silent on the stairs. The ember in her answered her moods now; he could feel it from across the room, a faint warmth against his cheek like standing near an oven door. Right now it was banked low. Grieving too, maybe. Or waiting.

He opened the ledger he'd brought from the Guild — one of the old ones, Veyra's own book of accounts, lifted from her office on the night everything went wrong — and he turned to a clean page and began, in his careful scribe's hand, to do arithmetic.

Not money. He was past money. Money was a language for people who still believed in tomorrow.

He did the arithmetic of trades.

*One scribe, debt-bound, gift of true names: market value, high. The crown pays forty crowns per gifted head, sixty if the gift is rare, a hundred if the gift is useful.* His pen scratched. *A name-speaker who can read the crown itself? Priceless is not a word the Guild uses. Veyra would find a number.*

*Trade offered: myself, freely given, to the crown's undercroft, in exchange for:* — and here his pen paused, hovering, dripping a small black bloom into the fibers of the page — *Wren's release. One life for one life. Clean terms. No interest.*

No interest. That was the joke, wasn't it. There was always interest. Every bargain he had ever struck in this city had come back around wearing a new face and holding out its hand. The temple owned his name at compound rates. Veyra owned his labor. And the crown — the crown owned something already, didn't it, some memory he couldn't find anymore, a hole in himself shaped like a person or a day or a word, and he only noticed the hole when he reached for things that weren't there.

He wrote: *Terms: my name, freely surrendered, in exchange for the immediate release of the prisoner known as Wren, tinker, one-handed, formerly of the Catchers' Guild.*

His hand shook. Not with fear. With something older than fear — the same tremor he'd hidden for years when ink moved on the page without him, except now the ink wasn't moving. Now it was just ink, and his hand was just a hand, and Cassian was just dead, and none of the careful invisible machinery of Paloma Coldharbour's life had ever once protected anyone he loved.

Cassian had known about the gift since novitiate. Had spent twenty years quietly striking Paloma's name from temple reports, erasing him from the record, making him small enough to slip through the world's fingers. And what had that protection bought? A funeral in an unmarked yard. A pair of spectacles going cold on a table.

Paloma set down the pen.

*Freely given,* the rite required. Queen Isolde had told them that much before she'd withdrawn behind her doors again, complicit and grieving and useful in the way of people who have made terrible bargains and intend to see them through. *Three willing names: one to hold, one to speak, one to burn.*

Wren was supposed to hold. Wren was in a cell beneath the palace, if she was alive at all, and Paloma could not stop doing the arithmetic of what happened to the taken — he'd seen the ledgers, he'd *written* the ledgers, souls fed into the crown a condemned prisoner at a time, and the crown was patient but it was also hungry, and every day Wren sat in the dark was a day the mouth got closer to her name.

So. He would go to the crown directly. Cut out the middlemen. Offer the thing it wanted most — a name-speaker, freely given, no hunt required, no catchers bleeding in the street — and ask for one tinker in return.

It was a bad trade. He knew it was a bad trade. It was the kind of trade Veyra would make, all appetite and no foresight, and he hated that he recognized it in himself, hated that grief had reduced him to the Guild's own grammar: *everything has a price, everything can be sold, love included, especially love.*

But Cassian was dead. And dead men cannot be bargained back, and living ones can, and that was the whole of the mathematics as far as Paloma could see.

He picked up the pen again.

*Witnessed and sworn,* he wrote, and then stopped, because the next line should have been his signature, and his signature was the thing he was selling, and there was a horrible circularity to it that almost made him laugh. You could not sign away your name without using your name. The temple had built that trap into him decades ago. Even his surrender would be conducted on their paper, in their language, at their interest.

"Juniper," he said quietly, to the empty room. "I need you to witness a document."

She was already on the stairs.

* * *

She came down the way she always did now — fast, quiet, one hand trailing the wall, the ember riding her shoulder like a small orange cat made of light. She was nine years old and she had been hunted by three governments and she stood at the bottom of the stairs with her chin up and her soot-streaked face set in the expression Paloma had learned to read as *I know you're about to do something stupid.*

"You've been writing since sunup," she said. "I can smell it. Ink and misery."

"I need a witness."

"For what?"

He turned the ledger toward her. She came closer, wary, the way she approached everything — sideways, ready to bolt — and read. She was getting better at reading; he'd been teaching her in the evenings, in the good days, in the days when they'd been four instead of two, and he watched her lips move slightly over the words and then stop moving entirely when she understood.

"No," she said.

"It's a fair trade—"

"It's a *stupid* trade." Her voice cracked up an octave, the way it did when the ember got agitated, and the candle on the table flared upright like a soldier called to attention. "You give them your *name*, Paloma. Your actual name. You told me — you told me what happens. You become nothing. You forget—" She stopped. Swallowed. "You forget *us*."

"Not immediately. The rite requires the speaking, and the speaking comes last—"

"You don't even know if there *is* a rite!" She slammed both palms on the table, and every flame in the room leaned toward her at once, and for a moment the shadows swung wildly like startled birds. "Wren's in the palace. The palace has guards. Guards, Paloma. Hundreds. And you're going to walk up to the front door and say *here, eat me instead*?"

"That is broadly the plan, yes."

"And then it'll be me alone again." The anger went out of her all at once, the way it did, like a snuffed wick, and what was underneath was worse. "You think I don't know what happens after? Everyone leaves. Everyone gets took. First the catchers got my mum, then the fire-people, then Wren, then Cassian, and now you want to—" Her voice did something young and ugly and honest. "You're *supposed* to be the one who does sums. Do this sum. What's left?"

Paloma looked at the ledger. At his neat columns. At the trade he'd drafted with all the care of a man balancing someone else's accounts, because that was the only way he knew how to hold anything — at arm's length, in ink, where it couldn't bleed on him.

"The sum," he said quietly, "is one life for one life. It balances."

"It doesn't balance! Nothing balances!" Juniper snatched the pen off the table — too fast for him to stop her, though honestly he hadn't tried — and dragged the ledger toward her, and began, with the furious scrawl of a child who had learned letters in a hurry and resented every one of them, to write across his careful page.

*NO,* she wrote. Underneath: *NO NO NO NO NO.* And then, slower, pressing hard enough to tear the paper: *FAMILY DOESN'T GET SOLD.*

The word landed in the room like a dropped glass.

Paloma stared at it. Family. He had used the word in ledgers — *family holdings, family debts, family obligations* — a thousand times, a dry legal term, a category. He had never once heard it pointed at himself like a finger.

"I'm not your family," he heard himself say. "I'm your— I'm the man who hid you in a stockroom and charged Veyra interest on your safety. I'm the scribe who falsified entries and let a memory get eaten to do it. I'm—"

"You're the one who gave me plums," Juniper said.

He blinked. Salt Road, weeks ago, a lifetime ago — the stolen fruit, the two of them laughing breathless in a ditch while catchers crashed around in the reeds, juice running down their wrists, the first moment either of them had felt anything like free.

"You're the one who taught me letters," she said, stepping closer, and the ember on her shoulder dimmed down to something soft, hearth-small, the way it only did when she felt safe. "You're the one who sleeps with the door open so I can find you if I wake up scared. You're the one who looked at Cassian's glasses for two days without touching them because touching them means he's really gone, and I *know*, because I haven't touched my mum's comb either." Her eyes were wet and furious. "That's family. That's exactly what family is. It's the people whose stuff you can't throw away."

"Juniper—"

"If you go to the crown," she said, and now her voice was steady, which was somehow more frightening than the shouting, "then I go with you. And I'm worth more than you. You know I am. The ember's worth more than any ten scribes. So your trade doesn't balance anyway — they'll take us both, and then who's left to remember any of us?" She jabbed a finger at the ledger. "Nobody. That's who. Nobody writes nobody down. I asked the ember once what happens to people nobody remembers." She swallowed. "Don't ask. It's bad."

The candle flames had all settled now, burning low and steady, and the room was very quiet, and somewhere above them a gull cried over the rooftops of Coldharbour like the city itself mocking them both.

Paloma looked down at the page. At his columns. At *NO NO NO NO NO* and *FAMILY DOESN'T GET SOLD* in a child's furious hand, and found, to his horror, that his eyes were stinging.

"Cassian erased my name for twenty years," he said. His voice came out rough. "To keep me safe. And it bought him a shallow grave. So tell me why your way works better. Tell me why loving people isn't just painting targets on them."

"Because Cassian didn't erase your name to keep *you* safe." Juniper climbed onto the bench across from him, kneeling, so their eyes were level, the way he'd seen her face down grown men and once a catcher with a crossbow. "He did it so you'd have something left to give *when it mattered*. That's what he told Wren. She told me. He said, *that boy will need his name someday, whole, and it'll cost him everything, and it has to be his choice.*" She reached across the table and put her small grimy hand flat on the ledger, over the trade he'd drafted. "So it has to be your choice. Not grief's. Yours."

For a long moment neither of them moved.

Then Paloma laughed — a broken, wet, unwilling sound — and dragged his sleeve across his eyes, and said, "When did you become the sensible one?"

"About the time everyone else died," she said, with a child's terrible directness, and then spoiled it by adding, "Also I'm hungry."

* * *

They ate bread and hard cheese and the last of Wren's pickled onions, because leaving food untouched was another way of agreeing that people were gone, and Juniper talked while they ate — a running account of everything the ember had shown her in the night, which was mostly nonsense and occasionally prophecy, delivered with her mouth full. Paloma listened and let the grief sit in him without trying to trade it away, and discovered that grief, unspent, was heavier but cleaner. Like carrying stones instead of owing them.

It was nearly dusk when the knock came at the door.

Three knocks. Then two. Then three again — Wren's signal, the one they'd agreed on, and Paloma was on his feet with his heart slamming before his mind caught up, because Wren was in the palace, Wren couldn't be knocking, unless—

Unless it was someone who knew the signal.

Unless it was someone who'd taken it off her.

He put Juniper behind him without thinking about it, and she let him, which frightened him more than the knock, and crossed to the door, and lifted the bar, and opened it a hand's width on the grey evening light.

Queen Isolde stood in the alley in a servant's cloak, hood up, alone, with mud to her ankles and a look on her face that Paloma had last seen on ledgers marked *FINAL DEMAND.*

"They're moving the prisoners tonight," she said, without preamble. "All of them. To the undercroft. The king is... hungrier than he's ever been, and the council thinks a great feeding will settle him before the Feast of Bells." Her jaw tightened. "It won't. Nothing settles it. But it means that in six hours, every soul the crown has taken — including your tinker — will be in one room, behind one door, under the throne."

Paloma's mind, the old ledger-mind, the arithmetic mind, was already moving: guards, shifts, gates, numbers. But Juniper pushed past him, and the ember flared up bright as a struck match, and she said:

"Then we don't have six hours. We have to go *now*."

Isolde looked at the child, and then at Paloma, and something in her face shifted — the look of a woman recalculating everything on the strength of a nine-year-old's certainty.

"There's something else," the queen said quietly. "Something I found in Aldous's private papers. About the binding. About what it actually requires." She glanced down the alley, checking the shadows. "And you're not going to like it."

Paloma closed the ledger on the table behind him — on *NO NO NO NO NO*, on *FAMILY DOESN'T GET SOLD* — and reached for his coat.

"Tell us anyway," he said.
