## 24. The Rite Requires Three

The queen's rooms smelled of lilies and dust, and Isolde had chosen both.

She had not opened the shutters in nine days. The light that came through the gaps was thin and grey, the color of old pewter, and it fell across the floor in bars like the ribs of some enormous sleeping animal. She had counted those bars more times than she could say. Counting was a kind of prayer when you had run out of honest ones.

She heard them coming up the servants' stair — four sets of feet, one small and quick, one dragging slightly on the left, one measured as a metronome, and Wren's uneven tread she knew from the tinker's brief, furious visit to the kitchens a week past. Isolde set down her needlework. She had been embroidering a bell onto a christening gown that no child would ever wear, and she folded it away with the care of a woman closing a coffin lid.

They came in without knocking. Of course they did. Paloma Coldharbour had read her true name off a palace ledger three weeks ago through two locked doors and a wall of clerks, and after that, courtesy between them had become a kind of joke neither of them laughed at.

"You shouldn't be here," she said. It came out wrong. It came out like *thank you for being here*, which was the trouble with her voice these days; everything she said curdled into its opposite before it left her mouth.

"The guards let us up," Paloma said. He looked tired in a way that went deeper than sleep. Since the undercroft — since the doors and the children and the light Juniper had carried through the dark like a lantern made of screaming — he had been fading at the edges. She had seen it happen to Aldous. She knew the shape of a man being spent.

"Your guards," Juniper said, from behind his leg, "are asleep."

All of them, presumably. Isolde did not ask how. The girl's ember had opinions about obstacles, and lately Isolde had stopped pretending she wanted to know the details.

Wren closed the door behind them and put her back against it, one hand flat on the wood, the stump of her other arm tucked into her belt the way she always kept it, like a tool she'd decided to stop apologizing for. "We need to talk about the rite," Wren said. "And we need to talk about what you've been doing in this room alone for nine days."

"I've been praying."

"No, you haven't." Wren's voice was flat as a planed board. "I was a catcher eleven years, Your Majesty. I know what a room looks like when someone's getting ready to die in it. You've got your good boots by the bed. You've written letters. There's a knife wrapped in oilcloth under the loose stone by the hearth, which was a bad hiding place even before you moved it twice."

Isolde looked at her hands. They were steady. She had trained them to be steady thirty years ago, when a queen's trembling fingers could topple treaties, and they had never once betrayed her since. It occurred to her, distantly, that this might be the loneliest thing about her life: that her body had learned obedience so thoroughly it would carry her calmly all the way into the fire.

"Sit down," she said. "All of you. If we're going to speak of the rite, then we'll do it properly, because I have studied it longer than any of you have been alive, and there are things in it you don't know. Things the chapel echoes only half-remembered."

Paloma sat. Juniper did not; she perched on the arm of his chair like a bird that had decided the chair was acceptable but might still fly off. Cassian — Brother Cassian, dead three weeks now, and yet here he was in her memory with ink on his fingers the way he'd been at twenty — Cassian would have said something gentle about grief making scholars of everyone. Isolde pushed the thought down where she kept all the others.

"The Binding of the Crown," she began, "was performed in the year 612, in the undercroft you've already seen. Three names. Not three people — three *names*, freely given, each carrying a different weight. One to hold. One to speak. One to burn."

"Hold," Wren repeated. "That's mine. We settled it."

"You settled nothing," Isolde said, "because none of you understand what settling means in a working this old. Listen to me. Please." And something in her voice must have reached them, because even Juniper went still, her eyes catching the shutter-light and holding it, twin coals banked low.

*One to hold,* Isolde thought. *One to speak. One to burn.* She had recited it so many times over the years that the words had worn smooth as river stones, and smooth stones, she had learned, are the ones that slip from your hand.

* * *

She told them about the famine first, because the rite made no sense without it.

"Winter of 611. You'll find it in the temple annals if you dig — a bad harvest stacked on a worse one, grain rotting in the granaries because the rot was in the blessing, not the wheat. Some blight of the gift, the priests called it. A curse. Whatever it was, bread stopped being food and started being a rumor. I was nineteen. I remember my mother boiling leather. I remember the carts." She paused. "You've all seen the paupers' fields outside Coldharbour. Imagine every field in the kingdom looking like that, and the snow not stopping for five months."

Juniper had gone very quiet. Hungry children recognized hunger stories the way dogs recognized whistles.

"Aldous was crown prince then. Twenty-three. He went looking for a cure the way princes do — throwing gold at temples, at hedge-witches, at anything that promised. And somewhere in that search he found the crown itself. Not the symbol. The *thing*. Older than the dynasty, older than the temple, older than the word 'king.' It had been buried under the palace since before anyone wrote it down, and it was hungry, and it made him an offer."

"Bread for a soul," Paloma said softly. His gift, she supposed, letting him fill in the gaps — or perhaps he'd already read it out of the walls.

"Bread for a soul," Isolde confirmed. "He bound himself to it willingly. That's the part the histories will never record, because I will make sure they don't, but you deserve the truth: my husband walked down into that undercroft knowing exactly what it would cost, and he paid it, and the famine broke within the season. The rot lifted. The wheat stood up straight. Forty years of plenty followed, and every loaf of it was bought with a man who slowly stopped being a man."

"And nobody noticed," Wren said. "Forty years, and nobody noticed their king was hollow."

"People notice what they're permitted to notice." Isolde rose and crossed to the writing desk, where a single key lay on a blotter gone brown with age. "Aldous held it together for fifteen years on will alone. Then the feeding started. Small, at first. Courtiers who vanished. Prisoners whose executions were moved up without explanation. And I noticed, because I am his wife, and you notice the thing wearing your husband's face the way you notice a portrait hung crooked — it takes years to admit it, and once you admit it, you can never unsee it."

She unlocked the desk drawer and took out a bundle of letters tied with black ribbon. She did not offer them around. They were hers, and she had earned them the hard way, one stolen hour at a time.

"So I made my own bargain," she said. "Not with the crown. With the machinery around it. I took control of the condemned lists. Every prisoner sentenced to die — I reviewed them personally. I commuted who I could. And the ones I couldn't save, the murderers and the monsters and the men too broken to ever leave a cell whole — I sent them down instead of out. To feed it. To keep it fed enough that it never grew bold enough to start taking from the living."

The silence in the room had a texture. Isolde had imagined this confession a hundred times, rehearsed it in the dark, and in every rehearsal someone raged at her. In this room, no one did, and that was somehow worse.

"How many?" Paloma asked. His voice was careful, the voice of a man balancing a ledger.

"Two hundred and eleven," she said. "I have their names. All of them. I wrote each one myself, in my own hand, because if I was going to feed a monster I was going to at least refuse to pretend they were inventory." She looked at Wren. "Your sister is not among them. I checked the moment I learned yours was on the catchers' list. She died on the salt road, of fever, in the spring of last year. I'm sorry. I know that's worth nothing."

Wren's face did something complicated and then went still again. "It's worth knowing," she said finally. "That's more than most families got."

Isolde nodded once, sharply, because if she let herself linger on it she would lose the thread, and the thread was the only thing keeping her upright.

* * *

"Now," she said, "the rite itself. Pay attention, because this is where your chapel echoes lied to you by telling the truth."

She laid it out the way she'd laid it out in her head a thousand nights: three names, freely given, each doing work the others could not.

"One to hold. The working needs an anchor — a name strong enough to bear the weight of everything the crown has eaten and not let go. Not strength of arm. Strength of *self*. Someone who knows exactly who they are and can stand inside that knowledge while the crown tries to drown it. When it fights — and it will fight; it has forty years of practice and a king's lifetime of stolen memories to weaponize — the holder is the rope. The one thing the working ties back to."

Wren's jaw tightened. "Still mine."

"One to speak. The unbinding must be spoken aloud, in the speaker's own true name, because a binding made by consent can only be undone by consent. The speaker gives their name *into* the working — spends it, the way a coin is spent. When it's done, the name is gone. Not hidden. Gone. The person remains — body, mind, memory — but the name, the true one, the anchor-word that the world uses to hold them in place, is unwritten. They become a kind of ghost that eats and walks and grieves."

Paloma had not moved. But Isolde saw his hands, and his hands had gone white where they gripped his knee.

"That's why you've been fading," she said gently. "You've been practicing, haven't you? Spending pieces. Little payments against the final sum."

"I appraised the crown," Paloma said. "It cost me a memory. And since then—" He stopped. Started again. "Since then I've been losing things. Small things. The name of my childhood street. What my mother's voice sounded like. I keep notes now, so I can relearn what I lose. But the notes are getting longer than the days."

"Then you already understand the price better than Aldous did, and he understood it completely." Isolde turned to the window, to the grey bars of light. "And one to burn. The third name goes into the fire itself. The crown is a hunger, and hunger cannot be destroyed — only redirected, or answered. The burning name becomes the answer. It steps into the crown's place and becomes the thing that feeds, so that the crown becomes the thing that starves. The burn does not survive as themselves. Whatever is left afterward is new. Unformed. A flame with no history, deciding what it wants to be."

Juniper slid off the chair arm and stood. She was small, soot-streaked even now, even clean — some stains went all the way through — and she looked up at Isolde with an expression far too old for her face.

"It wants me," Juniper said. "It's always wanted me. Everyone keeps saying it like it's a tragedy, but nobody's asked me what I think."

"I'm asking," Isolde said.

"Then here's what I think." The girl's chin came up. "I think the ember chose me because I was alone and it was alone and lonely things find each other. I think it's not evil, it's just *starving*, and starving makes everything cruel. And I think if I walk into that fire hating it, we both come out monsters, and if I walk in—" she faltered, just slightly, and Paloma's hand found her shoulder without either of them seeming to notice— "if I walk in some other way, maybe it doesn't have to end the way it started."

Isolde felt something crack behind her ribs. She had expected defiance, or terror, or the numb compliance of the sacrificed. She had not expected *hope*, offered up by a child like a found coin.

"That's the problem," she said quietly. "That hope. It's the reason I can't allow what you're planning."

* * *

"There it is," Wren said. "Knew it was coming."

"Your Majesty," Paloma began, rising—

"Sit down, scribe. Let me finish, because I have spent nine days alone in this room arriving at this, and you will hear all of it." Her voice cracked like a whip and she hated herself for it and used it anyway. "Three names. Hold, speak, burn. You've assigned yourselves like children dividing sweets — Wren holds, Paloma speaks, Juniper burns. And it's elegant, I grant you. It's even correct. But look at what it costs. A tinker who has already lost everything once. A scribe who will spend his remaining years unable to remember why the people who love him love him. And a child who will cease to exist as herself in a throne room full of strangers."

"And your alternative?" Wren's voice was ice over moving water.

"My alternative is me." Isolde said it simply, because simplicity was all the armor she had left. "I hold. I have held this kingdom together for forty years with my bare hands; no name in this realm is better practiced at bearing weight. I speak. I am the last person alive who heard the original binding spoken — I was there, in the undercroft, holding Aldous's hand while he gave himself away. I know the words in the old tongue. I have known them since I was twenty-four years old and too frightened to use them. And I burn." She spread her hands. "I am fifty-eight. I have no children. My husband is a mouth in a crown. My duty has eaten everything else I was, and I am *tired*, and a tired name given freely is still a name given freely. Let me be all three. Let one death close this account instead of three lives mortgaged against it."

"No," Juniper said.

"It isn't a request."

"Everything's a request." The girl stepped forward, and the candle on Isolde's desk leaned toward her like a courtier. "That's the whole point of the rite, isn't it? Freely given. You just told us. You can't give it *at* us. You can't decide we're the price and call it mercy."

"She's right," Paloma said quietly. "Consent is the mechanism. If you take our places by force of rank, the working fails, and the crown gets three names and a queen for free."

Isolde had known that. Of course she had known that — she had spent nine days turning it over, looking for the seam in it, the legal fiction, the loophole, the way a queen learns to route around every wall. There wasn't one. The rite was honest in the way that only terrible old things are honest: it wanted willingness, real willingness, and it could tell the difference.

So she played her last card, the one she'd sworn not to play, because it was cruel and it was true.

"Then hear what your willingness purchases," she said. "If Juniper burns, whatever comes out the other side will not know Paloma. Will not know Wren. Will not know the taste of stolen plums or the sound of a tinker's hammer. You will have saved the kingdom and orphaned yourselves in the same motion, and I have watched a man live forty years next to the empty shape of someone he loved, and I am telling you it is not survival. It is a slower version of the same loss."

"And if you burn," Wren said, "the kingdom loses the only person inside the palace who's been fighting it from the inside. Who inherits? Veyra's Guild? The regency council of whoever's left standing? You'd trade three flawed lives for one clean corpse and call it arithmetic."

"I'd call it duty."

"I'd call it what it is," Wren said, and her voice dropped, and it was worse than shouting. "I caught people for eleven years because I told myself it was duty. Duty is what we name the thing that eats us when we're too proud to call it grief. Your husband burned himself up for duty and look what's left. Don't you dare hand that inheritance to a child."

The words landed the way truth lands — no impact, just a sudden cold certainty spreading outward from the center of her chest. Isolde stood very still. Outside, distantly, a bell began to toll, and then another, and then a third, and the sound came through the shutters thin and wrong, arrhythmic, the bells of the capital ringing without order, the way they rang when something below the palace had begun to move.

Paloma's head came up. "The crown," he said. "It knows we're close. It's been feeling us gathering."

Juniper's candles were all leaning now, every flame in the room bent toward the girl like iron filings toward a lodestone, and her shadow on the wall had gone strange and bright-edged.

"We don't have time to settle this tonight," Paloma said. "But we don't leave this room until we agree on one thing: nobody performs this rite alone, and nobody decides for anybody else. Swear it. All of us. Queen included."

Isolde looked at the four of them — the scribe spending himself coin by coin, the catcher with her sister's name folded in her chest, the child with a sun in her belly, and herself, the woman who had fed two hundred and eleven souls into the dark to buy time she had never figured out how to spend.

Duty, Wren had called it. Grief wearing armor.

"Sworn," Isolde said, and the word cost her more than the abdication she'd drafted in the drawer beside the letters. "But understand me. The moment the rite begins, if any of you falters, I will step into the gap. That much I will not swear away."

The bells tolled on, faster now, and beneath them — faint, patient, old — something in the foundations of the palace began, very softly, to laugh.
