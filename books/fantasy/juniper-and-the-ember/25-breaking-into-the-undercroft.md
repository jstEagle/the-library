## 25. Breaking Into the Undercroft

**Paloma**

The Feast of Bells had been their cover going in; it was nearly their undoing going down.

Every bell in the capital rang at moonrise to mark the king's hundredth year of reign — a lie so old it had calcified into tradition — and under that thunder of bronze, no one heard the grate over the cistern channel give way. No one but the four of them crouched in the dark beneath the palace's east wing, and one of them wasn't a person at all.

Juniper's ember glowed through her shirt like a coal wrapped in linen.

"Dim it," Paloma whispered.

"I'm trying." Her voice was tight. "It knows they're here."

He felt it too, or something like it — not his gift reading names, but a pressure, a pull, the way water feels a hole in the dam before the dam does. Somewhere below them, dozens of small bright things were burning in cages, and the ember above them strained toward its kin like a dog at the end of a chain.

Wren would have known what to do with that. Wren was somewhere below them right now, and the thought made Paloma's hands unsteady on the crowbar.

"Channel runs straight under the undercroft," Isolde murmured. She moved through the dark like she'd been born in it, which, Paloma supposed, in a manner of speaking she had. "Thirty years ago I walked these halls as a bride. The cellars were wine then. I don't want to know what they are now."

"You don't have to come," Cassian had said, an hour before he died, and Isolde had answered him with a look that could have curdled milk. She'd worn a porter's smock over her mourning grey, and her hands — soft queen's hands — had blistered on the first grate. She hadn't complained once. That frightened Paloma more than anything else about her.

"Left at the junction," she said now. "Then down."

They went down.

* * *

**Juniper**

The dark under the palace wasn't empty dark. It was full dark, stuffed dark, dark with things inside it.

Juniper had lived in dark her whole life — under bridges, in ash-heaps, in the spaces between other people's walls — and she knew all its kinds. This kind hummed. It hummed because of them: the taken, the ones whose names were on Wren's list, packed into the wet stone below like eggs in a basket. She couldn't see them yet. She didn't need to. The ember saw for her.

*There,* it said, in the voice that wasn't quite a voice, warm against the inside of her ribs. *There, there, there.*

She'd stopped fighting the whisper weeks ago. Now she leaned into it, the way you lean into a wind that wants to carry you somewhere you already meant to go.

"Which way?" Paloma breathed beside her. He always asked her, even though he was older and cleverer and could read the true name of a stone. He asked her like her knowing mattered. Nobody had ever done that before him.

"Down and left," she said. "Where it's loudest."

"Loudest how?"

She almost laughed. "You'll hear it."

He did, three turns later. They all did. Because the humming wasn't humming anymore — it was singing, thin and cracked and terrible, dozens of voices through iron doors, and Juniper recognized the song because every gifted child in Coldharbour knew it. A counting song. A keep-away song. *One for the sorrow, two for the mirth—*

"They're children," Isolde said. Her voice came out flat and strange, like a coin dropped on stone.

"Some of them," Juniper said. The ember flared against her ribs, and this time she let it, let the light spill out through her collarbones and paint the corridor gold. "Not all."

The first door was iron, bolted from outside, with a slot for feeding trays and nothing else. Wren's lockpicks were gone with Wren, but the ember wasn't a lockpick. Juniper put her palm flat against the metal and thought *open* the way she'd think *warm* at a dead fire, and the bolt slid back with a groan like something waking up.

Inside: a girl, maybe nine, curled in straw, wrists ringed in cold-iron cuffs that had eaten the skin down to shine. When the light touched her face she screamed — then stopped screaming, because the light wasn't hurting. It was the opposite of hurting. The little flicker of gift inside her, the one the crown had been sipping at for months, stood up like a plant given water.

"Oh," the girl said. "Oh, you're *warm.*"

"Yeah," Juniper said, and her throat went thick. "We're getting you out."

* * *

**Paloma**

By the twelfth door they had a procession. By the twentieth, a crowd. Paloma walked backward through the corridors of the undercroft with his heart hammering and his ledger-mind doing what it always did when panic came: counting.

Forty-one doors opened. Thirty-eight occupied. Nine adults, twenty-nine children. Four too weak to walk, carried on the backs of strangers who an hour ago had been strangers. The cold-iron cuffs came off under Juniper's hands or under Wren's stolen pry-bar, and each time they did, Paloma felt the air change — a small bright thing rekindling, a candle relit in a room where candles weren't allowed.

His gift read them whether he wanted it or not. Names rose off the captives like heat-shimmer: *Marrow, Tansy, Bell-of-the-Lane, Osk-with-two-hands, Osk-who-is-now-one.* Each name arrived with its history attached, brief and brutal — *sold by an uncle, taken from a fair, born in the wrong parish* — and Paloma filed them the only way he knew how, in columns, in debts owed, except this ledger had no column large enough and no creditor alive who could pay it back.

Except maybe one.

"Isolde." He caught the queen's sleeve as she eased a boy onto her shoulder. "When we're out. When this is done. There will be a reckoning for whoever built this."

Isolde looked at him, and for once there was no mask, no statecraft, no careful weighing of words. What was underneath was worse. It was grief wearing fury like armor.

"I built this," she said. "I kept it fed. Whatever reckoning comes, Paloma, put my name at the top of the list."

"That's not—"

"It is exactly what it is." She shifted the boy higher. "Now stop talking and start opening doors."

He opened doors.

And then, at the far end of the last corridor, he found the door that was already open, and the cell beyond it that was already empty, and the scratch marks on the wall beside the cot that spelled out a single word in a tinker's blunt hand:

*GONE. ASKED FOR BY NAME. UP.*

* * *

**Juniper**

Up was bad. Up was where the feast was, where the bells still rang and the crown sat on its head and ate.

Juniper stood in the empty cell and read the scratches twice, and the ember went very quiet inside her, which was how she knew it was angry. Quiet-angry. Banked-angry. The way fire got just before it jumped.

"They took her up," she said. "Because of us. Because Veyra sold us and the crown wanted leverage and Wren—" Her voice cracked and she hated it. "Wren held the door so we could run, and now they're going to feed her to it."

Paloma's hand landed on her shoulder. His hands always shook, but not now. Now they were steady as a carpenter's.

"Then we go up," he said.

"There's thirty-eight of them," Isolde said from the doorway, and half the thirty-eight were crowded behind her, listening with the terrible attention of people who had been locked in the dark a long time. "Half can't fight. Half can barely stand."

"They don't have to fight," Juniper said.

The ember surged, and she let it speak through her, let it fill the corridor with light, and every freed captive turned toward her like flowers toward sun. Their gifts answered. A boy's shadow peeled off the wall and stretched toward her. An old woman's breath fogged into shapes — birds, fish, a running horse. The girl with the ruined wrists lifted her hand and the straw in her cell rose and hung there, trembling, waiting to be told.

*Calls to like,* the ember purred. *Like calls to like. We were never meant to be alone in the dark. They only ever told us we were.*

"We're the weapon," Juniper said. "All of us. If we choose to be."

Paloma was looking at her with an expression she'd learn later to call pride, though right then it just looked like fear and love fighting for the same chair.

"Up," he agreed. "But carefully. And Juniper—"

"Yeah?"

"When it gets loud in there — when the crown starts pulling — you hold on to me. Not to it. To me."

She nodded. She didn't tell him that holding on to him was the easiest instruction anyone had ever given her.

* * *

**Paloma**

The stairs to the ground floor were wide enough for six abreast, built for servants carrying feast platters, and tonight they carried something else entirely: a river of the taken, flowing upward, silent as smoke.

Paloma led with Isolde. Juniper burned behind them like a lantern at the head of a procession, and the procession behind her glowed too, forty small fires walking, and Paloma understood with sudden clarity why the crown had hoarded these souls in the dark instead of simply killing them. Together they were magnificent. Apart, in the dark, they were food.

The bells stopped.

All of them, mid-peal, across the whole city, as if a hand had closed over the throat of Coldharbour. In the ringing absence that followed, from somewhere above, came a sound Paloma had hoped never to hear again: the wet, patient sound of the mouth opening.

"It knows," Isolde whispered.

"It's hungry," Juniper corrected. "Those are different."

They came up into the kitchens, into wreckage — overturned cauldrons, a feast abandoned, spilled wine black on the flagstones like something else had been spilled. Through the servery arch, the great hall blazed with light, and silhouettes moved in it: courtiers fleeing, guards forming lines, and at the far end, on the dais, the throne, and the thing on the throne wearing the king.

It stood up.

Even at this distance, even through a wall of panicking courtiers, Paloma's gift seized him and *read*, and what it read made his knees soften. The crown had tasted him once — had eaten a memory out of him in a Guild office while he signed false entries — and it knew his flavor. Across the whole roaring hall, the thing wearing Aldous turned its head and found him in the kitchen doorway, and smiled with a mouth that was mostly the crown's.

"*The scribe,*" it said, and its voice filled the hall without effort, without courtesy. "*Come to return what you owe.*"

"He doesn't owe you anything," Juniper said, stepping forward, and the ember roared up through her like dawn through a window, and behind her, forty voices — cracked, terrified, furious — rose with hers.

Paloma had spent his whole life invisible. Careful. Small. A man who balanced other people's impossible accounts and never once collected on his own.

He stepped up beside her.

"The account," he said clearly, "is closed."

* * *

**Juniper**

What happened next, Juniper would remember in pieces for the rest of her life.

The guards charged, and the girl called Marrow threw the first wall of force any of them had ever thrown, and it wasn't pretty and it wasn't controlled and it worked. The old woman's breath-birds wheeled through the rafters and snuffed every torch. Osk-with-one-hand froze a line of soldiers' boots to the floor with frost that sang. The ember poured through Juniper and out of her hands and she didn't direct it, didn't tame it, just aimed it — at the chains of the chandelier, at the locks of the side doors, at the darkness where the crown's hunger reached for them like fishing lines and found, instead of lone frightened sparks, a net of flame.

*Together,* the ember sang. *This is what together is. This is what they were afraid of.*

They fought their way to the great doors, and the freed flowed through them into the Feast-night streets of the capital, into the crowds, into the noise, and the story went ahead of them faster than they ran: *the taken are free, the taken are free, the king's own cellar was full of the taken.*

At the threshold Juniper looked back once.

The thing on the dais had stopped smiling. It stood swaying, the king's body guttering like a badly made candle, and the crown on its head burned with a light that had nothing warm in it at all. Around it, the air bent inward, drinking.

*Soon,* it said, not aloud, straight into the marrow of everyone gifted in the hall. *You've shown me where you keep everything I want.*

Juniper took Paloma's hand and ran.

Behind them, the crown began, very softly, to laugh.
