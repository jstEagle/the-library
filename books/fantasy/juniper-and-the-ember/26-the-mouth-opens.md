## 26. The Mouth Opens

The bells went wrong on the third morning.

I had lived in Coldharbour long enough to know the city's grammar of sound — the dawn peal from the Temple of Ledgers, the noon chime that meant the Guild's markets opened, the evening toll that sent the debt-bound home to their bunks. Bells in this city did not lie. They were the one thing the crown had never thought to tax.

But on the third morning after we freed the undercroft, the bells stuttered.

They rang a phrase I had only ever read about in pre-Aldous liturgies: three strikes, silence, one strike, silence, then a long unbroken clangor like a throat being held open. The Alarm of the Body. Rung, according to the old books, when the sovereign was dying in public view and the succession could not be quietly managed.

"They know," Wren said. She stood at the chapel window with her single hand flat against the glass, watching smoke rise from three different quarters of the city. "Or the crown knows. Same thing now."

"It's not the same thing," I said. "That's rather the point."

She gave me a look over her shoulder — the look she reserved for my pedantry, which was most of her looks — and turned back to the window. Below us, Coldharbour was waking up angry. I could hear it even through glass: not the usual morning noise of carts and hawkers but something lower, a collective mutter, a city clearing its throat before it decided what to say.

Juniper sat on the floor with the wooden bird cupped in both hands. The ember inside it glowed a soft hearth-orange, steady as a candle in a windless room. She had barely put it down since the undercroft. Neither had she slept much; there were shadows under her eyes like thumbprints in ash.

"The captives," she said. "The ones we got out. Their gifts are all... loud today. I can feel them from here. Like birds all facing the same direction."

"Like calls to like," I murmured. It was the phrase she'd used in the undercroft, when forty stolen gifts had flared at once at her approach, and the crown below us had *noticed*.

That was the part none of us said aloud, though we all carried it: we had not escaped the palace unnoticed. We had rung a dinner bell in a dining room and then walked out through the kitchen.

* * *

By midmorning the criers were out, and their words were wrong too.

I heard them from the chapel steps, where I'd gone ostensibly to buy bread and actually to listen. The criers stood at the corners with the royal sash across their chests, and they shouted the same proclamation in voices pitched carefully between grief and reassurance:

*"His Majesty the King is taken ill! His Majesty requires rest! All loyal subjects are commanded to remain indoors, to refrain from assembly, to report any disturbance to the watch!"*

Taken ill. I had read the king's face at public audience months ago and found nothing behind his eyes but appetite wearing a man's shape. Whatever illness had taken him, it was the illness of a vessel cracking under what it held.

And yet the crowd around the criers did not disperse. That was the thing the proclamation hadn't accounted for. People stood in the gray drizzle and looked at one another, and I watched a whole street full of strangers arrive at the same arithmetic at the same moment: *the king has been ill for years, and the hunt for the gifted began years ago, and the prisons have been filling, and no one has ever come back.*

A woman near me said, to no one in particular, "My brother was taken in spring." Just that. And the man beside her said, "Mine too. Harvest before that." And neither of them moved toward home.

I bought my bread and walked back to the chapel with my heart going like a hammer, because I understood what I had just seen. The crown had spent decades feeding quietly, one condemned prisoner at a time, wrapped in procedure and paperwork. Quiet feeding required quiet subjects. But hunger that has been starved does not stay quiet, and a body failing in public cannot be hidden behind ceremony, and fear — the crown's oldest tool — was curdling into its opposite.

Riots do not begin with fire. They begin with two strangers comparing missing brothers in the rain.

* * *

We gathered in the chapel nave at noon, the five of us plus Queen Isolde, who arrived hooded and unannounced through the sacristy door that Wren had oiled for exactly this purpose. Isolde moved differently now than she had in her sequestered rooms. Complicity had sat on her like a fur coat; since the undercroft, since seeing the taken with her own eyes, she wore guilt like hair shirt instead, and it made her straighter, harder, faster.

"The council has been summoned," she said, pulling back her hood. Her hair had gone almost white at the temples in the past week. "Half of them think the king is dying and want to position themselves for succession. Half of them suspect something worse and want to position themselves for survival. None of them know about the rite."

"Good," Wren said.

"No," Isolde said. "Not good. Because the crown knows, and the crown will not wait politely while we prepare. You felt it in the undercroft. It has tasted freedom's possibility, and it is hungry the way a starving animal is hungry — without patience, without cunning, without anything left but appetite." She looked at each of us in turn. "It fed openly yesterday. Three prisoners from the Blackcells, taken in daylight, in front of witnesses. The palace called it 'transfer for medical care.' The witnesses saw the guards come back alone."

Juniper's hands tightened around the bird. The ember inside flared once, briefly, like a pulse.

"It's scared," she said.

Everyone looked at her. She was fourteen and soot-streaked and she spoke about an ancient devouring intelligence the way other children described a stray dog.

"How can you possibly know that?" Isolde asked.

"Because I'm the same kind of thing." Juniper lifted the bird slightly. "Only small. Only tamed. When it eats, I feel it — like swallowing someone else's meal. And what I feel isn't power. It's panic. Big things get more scared than little things, when they start to fail. Little things are used to it."

I thought about that longer than I wanted to. There was a lesson in it aimed directly at me — at the careful, invisible life I had built, at every ledger balanced to the half-penny so that nothing could ever surprise me. Little things survive failure because they practice. Big things shatter.

"So it accelerates," I said aloud. "It feeds openly because it must. Which means the king's body fails publicly, which means the city sees its god-king rotting behind the throne, which means—"

"Riots," Wren said flatly. "Already started. Fishmarket quarter burned last night. The watch fired on the crowd at Saltgate. This morning they stopped pretending it was about order and started calling it treason." She tapped the windowsill. "Clock's running. We do the rite before the city burns down around us, or after. Those are the options."

"Before," Isolde said immediately.

"After," Juniper said, at exactly the same moment.

We all turned to her. She flushed but didn't retract it.

"I mean—" She struggled. "If we do it while everything's calm, the crown still has the city to hide in. People to feed on. Guards, prisoners, all of it. But if the city's already burning, if everyone's already in the streets, if the watch is already overwhelmed — then when the crown falls, there's nothing holding anyone down. Everyone sees it happen. Nobody can pretend afterward it was a fever, or a plot, or whatever story they'll tell." She looked up. "It should die in front of everyone. That's how you make sure it stays dead."

Silence in the nave. Somewhere outside, another bell — a real one this time, a church bell ringing alarm, joining the wrong bells, and then another, until the whole soundscape of Coldharbour was discord.

"She's right," Isolde said slowly, and I could hear how much it cost her, agreeing with a feral child about the timing of regicide. "A quiet death can be rewritten. A public one cannot. If the kingdom is to believe the crown is truly ended — and it must believe it, or the next ambitious soul will go looking for it in some vault — then the ending must have ten thousand witnesses."

"And ten thousand witnesses means ten thousand chances for the crown's loyalists to cut our throats first," Wren said. "No offense to the plan, little bird, but I've seen crowds. Crowds don't check whose side you're on before they swing."

"Then we'd best be sure of our roles," I said, "before the swinging starts."

Nobody answered that. The roles were chosen — Wren to hold, me to speak, the ember to burn in its new small vessel — but *chosen* is not the same as *ready*, and we all knew the difference in our bones. I caught Juniper looking at me across the nave, quick and frightened, and I knew she was thinking the same thing I was: that my name was the hinge of the whole working, and that every hour that passed was an hour closer to the moment I would stop being Paloma Coldharbour entirely.

I smiled at her. It was the worst lie I told all week.

* * *

The riots reached our district by evening.

I was on the roof of the chapel with Wren, keeping watch, when the first fires bloomed to the west — not the orderly orange of a controlled burn but the ugly scattered constellation of a neighborhood igniting building by building. The Guild of Impossible Trades stood somewhere in that glow. I found I could not make myself wonder whether Veyra's ledgers were burning. Some debts, I decided, collect themselves.

Below us, the streets filled. Not with looters first — that came later, after dark — but with people. Families walking with bundles. Neighbors standing in doorways talking in low urgent clusters. A procession of temple novices carrying candles, marching toward the palace square, singing something old that the temples hadn't sung in my lifetime. The city was shedding its obedience the way a snake sheds skin: slowly, badly, in patches, but irreversibly.

"There," Wren said, and pointed east.

At first I thought it was another fire. Then I saw it move.

It came along the Processional Way in a loose column — watchmen first, then black-liveried catchers, then a rank of palace guards in the king's colors, and behind them something that made my gift rise in my chest like bile. A palanquin. An open palanquin, borne on the shoulders of eight men, and in it—

"In the name of the salt and the stone," Wren breathed.

The king was *displayed*. Propped upright in the palanquin, crowned, robed, his face turned toward the crowds on either side — and the face was wrong. Not the composed mask I had seen at audience. The flesh had gone gray and slack, the jaw hanging, the eyes open and fixed and dry. He looked like a man three days dead arranged by someone who had never met him. And the crown on his head gleamed wetly in the torchlight, and as the column passed beneath us I felt it — felt *it*, the mouth of it — open.

Every gifted soul in the district felt it. I knew because I heard it happen: a ripple of cries going through the streets below, here a woman dropping to her knees, there a boy's lantern-flame gift flaring wild and green, somewhere a sound like glass singing. The crown wasn't feeding. It couldn't, at distance, not properly. It was *calling*. Casting its line over a whole district of frightened, gifted, grieving people, the way a fisherman casts over a boiling shoal, letting them feel the shape of its hunger so that some fraction of them would walk toward it on their own feet.

"Get off the roof," I said. My voice came out strange. "Both of us. Now."

Wren was already moving. We came down the ladder into the nave where Juniper stood in the center of the floor with the ember-bird raised above her head like a lamp, and the light coming off it was not orange anymore but white-gold, furious, answering.

"It's calling them," she said. "Paloma, it's calling *everyone*. I can hear it in my teeth."

"I know."

"The ones we freed — the ones hiding in the cellars and the marshes — it'll find them. It'll pull them in. They'll walk to it, they won't be able to stop themselves, they'll—"

"*Juniper.*"

She stopped. The bird trembled in her grip. And I saw it then, plain as ink on a page: the crown had made its mistake, the same mistake every predator makes when it grows desperate enough to hunt in the open. It had shown itself. It had let ten thousand people see a dead man propped up in a golden chair, wearing a crown that gleamed like a wet mouth, and it had let them *feel* what it wanted from them.

Fear makes subjects. Being fed upon makes rebels.

"They're not all going to walk to it," I said. "Look."

I took her to the window. Below, in the street, the novice procession had stopped. They were facing east, toward the Processional Way, toward the palanquin and the dead king and the wet gleam of the crown. For a long moment nothing moved.

Then an old woman at the front of the procession — I recognized her distantly, a candle-seller from the temple steps, no gift I had ever sensed, nothing but age and stubbornness — raised her candle high and blew it out. One flame, extinguished. A refusal. And down the whole line, one by one, the novices did the same, until the street was dark except for the distant fires, and they stood in the dark facing the crown, and they did not kneel.

From somewhere further off, a bell began to ring again. Then another. Not the Alarm of the Body. Something older, ragged, improvised — pots and hammers and the great cracked bell of the fishmarket that hadn't swung in thirty years. The city finding its own voice, out of tune, unmistakable.

"They're not your flock," Juniper whispered, to the crown, to the east, to the thing wearing Aldous's face. "They were never your flock."

The ember in the bird pulsed gently, twice, like a heartbeat steadying.

Isolde appeared in the doorway of the nave, cloaked again, a travel bag in her hand, and the look on her face told me before she spoke.

"They've moved the rite chamber," she said. "The undercroft is emptying. The crown is gathering everything it has left — the taken, the guards, the whole apparatus — into the throne room. It's going to make its stand where the kingdom can see it." Her voice did not shake, but her hand did, on the doorframe. "Tomorrow night. The Feast of Bells. It intends to feed in front of the entire capital and call it a coronation renewal. It intends to end this tomorrow."

"Then so do we," Wren said.

I looked around the nave — at Wren flexing her remaining hand, at Isolde straightening under her guilt like a soldier under a pack, at Juniper with the small burning hearth of a tamed star held against her chest — and I felt the clock in my own ribs, ticking down the hours until I would speak my name for the last time.

One day. One night. One chance.

Outside, the bells kept ringing, and none of them belonged to the crown anymore.
