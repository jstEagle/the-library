## 27. Veyra's Reckoning

She found us at the tannery, which was the first mistake.

Not the finding — Veyra had always been able to find anything with a price tag on it, and we had a price tag now, Wren's face chalked on every catcher's board from Salt Road to the Bells. No, the mistake was that she came herself. Guild mistresses sent men to collect debts. They only came in person when they wanted something that couldn't be weighed.

I saw her before she saw me, which was the second thing that mattered. She stood at the mouth of the tannery yard in her grey wool coat, hands folded at her waist like a woman waiting for a ferry, and behind her were six of her people and no more. Six. For four of us — me, Juniper, Wren, and Isolde wrapped in a porter's cloak with her queen's posture beaten down but not out. Six was not enough to take us if it went badly. Veyra knew arithmetic better than anyone alive. She had come to talk.

"Paloma Coldharbour," she said, when I stepped into the yard alone. "You look terrible."

"I've been running."

"You've been *reckoning*," she said. "Running is just reckoning with your legs." She glanced past me at the tannery door, where I knew Juniper's eye was pressed to a knothole, and where Wren's good hand was almost certainly resting on a knife. "Your friends can come out. I didn't bring enough people for a fight because I don't want one. Fights damage inventory."

"We're not inventory."

"Everything is inventory. That's the whole trick of the world, scribe. The moment you understand that, you stop being surprised by it." She said it without cruelty. That was the worst part of Veyra; she never dealt in cruelty, only in accuracy. "Walk with me."

I walked with her. Behind us, the door opened a hand's width, and I heard Wren's low voice say something, and then boots on wet cobbles — Juniper first, always Juniper first, flanking me at a distance she'd chosen herself, close enough to burn for me and far enough that her ember wouldn't catch us both in the same blast. She'd learned tactics somewhere. It frightened me how quickly she learned things nobody taught her.

The tannery backed onto the old canal, green-scummed and slow. Rats worked the mudbanks. Veyra stopped at the water's edge and looked at the city across it — Coldharbour's skyline, temple spires and counting-house roofs, all of it grey under a sky the color of dishwater.

"Do you know what I was doing when the bells rang for Aldous's hunt?" she asked.

"Selling names."

"Counting them," she corrected. "There's a difference. Selling is what my clerks did. Counting is what I do. Two hundred and eleven suspected gift-bearers in this city, cross-referenced against debt-rolls, temple registries, and midwife records going back thirty years. Do you know how many of those two hundred and eleven ever once thought about what would happen to them?" She turned to look at me. Her eyes were pale grey, the color of ledger paper left in rain. "None. Not one. They lived their whole lives inside their own small weather and never once looked up at the sky. And then the sky came down, and they were astonished. Astonished! As if being unprepared were some kind of virtue."

"You're making a speech," I said. "You hate speeches. Speeches cost time and time is interest."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. A concession.

"Yes. All right. Here is the speech, compressed: I have sold you to the crown."

Juniper made a sound behind me, small and hot, like a struck match.

"Don't," Veyra said, without looking at her. "Don't spend your fire on words, girl. You'll want it later." Back to me. "Sold you, yes. The crown pays forty crowns a head for confirmed gift-bearers, sixty for children, and there is a standing bounty of five hundred for anyone who can appraise the regalia. I have collected on all three line items. You, the girl, and the appraisal. Wren I threw in as a courtesy discount; the crown has an old grudge against deserters."

"And Isolde?"

Veyra's composure flickered — the first true crack I'd seen in it. "The crown does not know the queen walks around in a porter's cloak. If they learn it from my report, that is between me and my conscience, and my conscience has excellent credit." She folded her hands again. "So. You are caught. The catchers are two streets away, moving slowly because I told them to move slowly. Which means we have perhaps ten minutes, you and I, and I am going to spend them asking you the only question that has kept me awake since the salt road."

"Ask it."

"Why did you read my name?"

I hadn't. Not yet. But she didn't know that, and the fact that she thought I might — that she'd crossed a city, spent her own coin, delayed her own catchers, all to stand at a canal and ask — told me everything about what was rotting underneath that grey coat.

At the salt road, weeks ago, when her men had caught us and she'd demanded the crown-appraisal, I had done something I shouldn't have. In the middle of the negotiation, furious and frightened, I had let my gift slip its leash for half a heartbeat. I hadn't spoken her name. I had only *looked* — the way I look at ledgers, the way I look at things to see what they truly are. Half a heartbeat. But Veyra Mistral, mistress of the Guild of Impossible Trades, who had bargained with archdukes and out-waited bishops, had gone white as a fresh page.

She remembered. She'd had weeks to remember.

"Because you were going to sell a child to be eaten," I said. "And I wanted you to know that I could see you. All the way down."

"That's not an answer. That's a justification. Justifications are for clerks." Her voice stayed level, but her hands had come apart, and one thumb was pressing hard into the other palm. "When you looked at me — what did you see? Tell me exactly. Don't soften it. I've never once asked a man to soften an entry in my life."

I should have lied. Every survival instinct I owned said lie: tell her she was strong, tell her she was feared, tell her whatever keeps the catchers two streets away instead of one. But lying to Veyra about accounts was like counterfeiting in front of a mint-master. She'd smell the false weight of it instantly.

"I saw a girl," I said. "On a dock. Maybe eight years old. Barefoot. Counting fish for a man who wasn't going to pay her, and knowing he wasn't going to pay her, and counting anyway, because counting was the only thing in the world that belonged to her."

The canal lapped at the mud. Somewhere behind us, Juniper breathed.

"My father sold me to a fish-factor when I was seven," Veyra said, to the water, not to me. "For eleven crowns and forgiveness of a debt. Eleven. I have bought and sold cities for less than that and I still remember the exact number, because numbers are the last things to leave." She was quiet a moment. "My name then was not Veyra. My name was a nothing-name, a dockside name, the kind of name that gets written wrong on bills of sale so often you stop correcting it. The Guild gave me Veyra. Names are tools here, scribe. You know that better than anyone — yours belongs to the temple, doesn't it? Debt-bound. Stamped. You've spent your whole life being someone else's entry."

"Yes."

"So you understand. When you looked at me and showed me that girl on the dock—" her jaw tightened, and for one unguarded instant she looked less like a guild mistress than like a woman holding a ledger open against the wind, "—you committed theft. You stole property of the Guild of Impossible Trades. That girl was written off decades ago. Depreciated. Disposed of. And you put her back on my books."

"I'm sorry."

"No, you're not. Good. Never apologize for accurate accounting." She finally turned to face me fully, and I saw that her eyes were wet and that she would deny it under torture. "Here is my problem, Paloma Coldharbour. Since the salt road, I have not slept properly. I keep the girl's books now whether I want to or not. Every contract I sign, I hear her counting fish. Every name I sell to the crown, I wonder what hers was. Do you know what that costs me? Do you know what it costs a woman like me to be *haunted by an eight-year-old*?"

"It sounds expensive."

A sound escaped her — brief, rusty, horrifyingly close to a laugh. "It's ruinous. It's the worst investment I've ever been forced to hold." She stepped closer, and lowered her voice, and for the first time since I'd known her, Veyra Mistral spoke without a price attached. "So I'm going to make you an offer, and it is the last offer I will ever make you, and then I am going to walk away and let the catchers do what catchers do, because I cannot afford to feel this anymore. Walk away. Take the girl, take Wren, take your cloaked friend, get out of Coldharbour tonight, and I will report the lot of you drowned in the canal. Forty crowns lost, sixty lost, five hundred lost. I will eat the loss. In exchange—"

"There's always an exchange."

"—in exchange, you take her off my books." Her voice cracked, actually cracked, on the word *her*. "Give her back her nothing-name. Tell me what I was called before the fish-factor. Write it in the air and let me hear it once, and then I'll sell you to the devil himself if you ask me to, but I'll sleep tonight."

Behind me, Juniper moved forward to stand at my shoulder. I felt the heat of her, faint as a candle held near the wrist. She didn't say anything. She had learned, somewhere in the marshes and the chapel and the long hungry miles, when words were mine to spend and when they weren't.

I thought about what my gift costs. Every use takes a memory; I'd paid for the appraiser's forged entries with something I still couldn't name, some room in the house of myself now standing empty. Reading Veyra's true name aloud — truly aloud, fully, not a glance but a speaking — would cost more. Perhaps much more. Perhaps another door in the house, closed and locked, its contents scattered.

But I thought about what Brother Cassian had told me, in the archive, with his hand steady on my shoulder: *a name hidden is a debt accruing; a name spoken is a debt forgiven.* He had spent twenty years erasing my name from temple reports, paying down my balance in secret, and he had died — Cassian had died — buying me minutes. I could not waste his purchase on cowardice.

And I thought about the crown. The mouth wearing Aldous's shape, patient and old, feeding on names. Everything Veyra had ever sold, everything the temple had ever stamped, everything the catchers hunted — it all flowed downhill to that appetite. Veyra was not the hunger. Veyra was a girl who had learned that the world was a ledger because the alternative was admitting the world was simply cruel, and ledgers at least balanced.

Mercy, I was beginning to understand, was not the opposite of the transactional world. Mercy was the one transaction the crown couldn't tax.

"Stand still," I said.

Veyra went very still.

I let my gift open. Not the careful slit I used on ledgers and lock-seals — the whole way, the way I'd looked at the king, the way I'd read the crown and recoiled. The canal, the rats, the dishwater sky, all of it fell away, and there she was: not the grey coat, not the pale eyes, not the empire of paper she'd built, but the true name underneath, the one the fish-factor's clerk had written crooked and she'd stopped correcting.

It was a small name. That undid me most of all. All these years, all these crowns and contracts and sold souls, and underneath it all her name was small and warm and ordinary as bread.

I spoke it aloud.

The sound of it went across the water and came back. Veyra Mistral closed her eyes, and I watched — I will watch it until I die — fifty years fall off her face at once. Her shoulders dropped. Her hands, those hands that had signed away two hundred and eleven lives, hung open at her sides, palms up, the way a child waits to be handed something.

"Oh," she said. Just that. Just *oh*, in a little girl's voice, in a dockside accent she must have spent decades sanding away.

Then she opened her eyes, and the guild mistress climbed back into place like a coat being buttoned — but not all the way. One button stayed undone forever after.

"Well," she said, roughly. "That's paid, then."

"That's paid."

"The catchers are at the chandlery on Corver Street. I'll send them to the north gate with a story about a boat. You have until dawn, and then Coldharbour closes like a fist." She straightened her cuffs. Business again, but her voice wouldn't quite obey. "One more item, scribe, free of charge, because apparently I give things away now, gods help the Guild. The crown knows you're coming. Has known for days. There's a man in the palace — grey robe, no name anyone can find — and he told the regent's council that the rite's components were gathering themselves. He used the word *components*. About people." Her pale eyes fixed on mine. "He described the girl. He described Wren. He described you. And then he smiled, and I have seen every smile in the catalogue of this kingdom, and I have never seen one like that. Whatever you're planning in that ruined chapel of yours — he wants you to plan it. Understand? The door is open because it's a mouth."

The cold that went through me had nothing to do with the wind off the canal.

"Thank you," I said.

"Don't thank me. Balance the books." She turned to go, then stopped, her back to me, and stood a moment looking at the far shore where the temple spires stood like needles waiting for thread. "The girl on the dock," she said quietly. "Her factor shorted her count every single day. And every day she counted anyway. Do you know why?"

"Why?"

"Because someday, she thought, someone would check the tally. Someone honest. Someone who'd say: *this child counted true, and everyone who cheated her owes her.*" She pulled her collar up against the wind. "No one ever did. Until today. So the debt runs the other direction now, scribe. Remember that, when you're deciding what I'm worth."

She walked away along the canal, grey coat fading into grey morning, and her six people peeled off the wall and followed her, and not one of them looked back.

* * *

Juniper took my hand as soon as Veyra was out of sight. Her fingers were hot — hotter than they should have been, hotter than skin, the ember rising toward the surface the way it did when she was frightened and refusing to show it.

"What did it cost?" she asked. She knew about the cost. She was the only one besides Cassian who had ever known, and Cassian was ash now, and the knowing sat heavy on her small shoulders. "What did you lose?"

I searched the house of myself. The rooms were mostly as I'd left them. My mother's kitchen — there. The smell of temple ink — there. Cassian's laugh, thank all mercies, still there, still there. But somewhere toward the back, where the oldest things lived, a door stood open on emptiness. I walked through it in my mind and found clean floor, swept bare, and no memory of what had stood there or who had given it to me. Something from before the temple, maybe. Something from childhood. A face, possibly. Possibly a face.

"I'll tell you when I find out," I said.

"That's not an answer."

"No," I agreed. "It's an entry pending reconciliation. It's the truest kind I have."

She scowled at me — thirteen years old, soot in her eyebrows, fire in her chest, the last person in the world with any right to lecture anyone about honesty — and then she hugged me, quick and fierce, around the ribs, the way she did when she couldn't bear to do it slowly.

Wren appeared in the doorway with Isolde behind her, and I told them all of it: the drowned-in-the-canal report, the dawn deadline, and the grey-robed man who smiled like a mouth and called us components. Wren spat over the rail. Isolde went the color of tallow and said, in a voice I'd never heard from her, small and old: *"Then he's awake. After all these years, the binding is thin enough that he's awake."*

"Then we don't have until the Feast of Bells," Wren said flatly. "We have until he decides he's hungry enough to come through it."

Isolde looked at each of us in turn — the deserter, the scribe, the burning child — and I watched duty and love fight behind her face, and for once, love won visibly.

"Then we choose our parts tonight," she said. "All three of them. Tonight, before he chooses for us."

Above the tannery, through the knothole in the door, the first light of morning came through grey and thin — and Juniper's ember answered it, glowing through the seams of her like a lamp behind paper, ready or not.
