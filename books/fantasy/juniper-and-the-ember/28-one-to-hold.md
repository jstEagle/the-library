## 28. One to Hold

The ember had opinions about the house.

Juniper felt them the moment they crossed the threshold of the tinker's loft above the salt-works — a low, grumbling warmth behind her ribs, like a cat settling into a chair it hadn't chosen. *Too many people,* it murmured. *Too many hearts. Too easy to lose count.*

"Shut up," she said under her breath.

Paloma glanced at her. "Something?"

"No." She hitched her pack higher on her shoulder and looked anywhere but at him. "Just talking to myself."

He didn't believe her — she could tell by the way his mouth went careful — but he let it go, the way he'd been letting things go ever since the undercroft. Since Wren came out of that cell with her empty sleeve and her sister's list clutched in her one hand, and since Paloma had stood in the corridor afterward looking like a man who'd read his own death warrant and found it written in a friend's handwriting.

The loft was bigger than it looked from the street. Wren's territory: benches cluttered with half-mended clocks, a wall of tools hung by size, a stove that ticked as it cooled. Isolde sat near the window with her hood down for the first time since Juniper had known her, and without the hood she was just a tired woman with grey coming in at her temples, not a queen at all. Cassian's satchel lay on the table between them, closed. Nobody had opened it since the chapel. Nobody wanted to be the one who did.

Veyra wasn't there. Veyra was "arranging things," which Juniper had learned meant either bribing someone or threatening them, and possibly both.

"Sit," Wren said. She said it the way she said everything — flat, certain, like a nail going into wood. "All of you. We're done waiting for tomorrow to decide itself."

They sat. Juniper took the spot nearest the stove because the ember liked heat even when it pretended not to, and because from there she could see the whole room, every door, every window. Old habits. The stacks of the Guild archive had taught her that much: always know where you can run.

Wren set something on the table.

It was paper — a long strip of it, torn from a ledger, covered in names. Juniper knew what it was before Wren touched it again. She'd seen it once before, on the salt road, when Wren thought everyone was asleep. The catcher's ledger. The list of the taken. Names in a neat official hand, and beside some of them, in Wren's own cramped script, little marks. Dates. Places. Last words overheard, maybe. Last kindnesses remembered.

"My sister's name is fourth from the top," Wren said. "Maren Thistle-down. Taken eleven years ago, spring. I know because I wrote the report myself. I was the one who carried her out of our mother's house in a net." Her voice didn't shake. That was the frightening thing about Wren — nothing shook, or if it did, she'd built so much scaffolding around it you couldn't see the movement. "I've spent eleven years telling myself the crown made me do it. The crown, the Guild, the law. All true. None of it useful."

She flattened the paper with her palm.

"So we're going to talk about the rite. All of it. Tonight. Because tomorrow night we walk into that throne room, and I'll be damned if any of us finds out their part in this for the first time with the crown's teeth already in them."

* * *

Isolde spoke first, because Isolde always spoke first when the speaking was hard. She'd told them about the Binding of the Crown two nights ago, in the hollow chapel, in a voice like a woman reading her own confession: three willing names. One to hold. One to speak. One to burn.

"Hold," Isolde said now, "is the anchor. The rite is a storm — it will pull at everyone in the room, and hardest at the ones doing the work. The one who holds stands at the center and does not move. They give the working something to stand on. A name, freely given, held steady from the first word to the last." She looked at her hands. "Aldous bound it alone, which is why it warped. He tried to be all three at once, and the crown ate the difference."

"And if the holder wavers?" Paloma asked quietly.

"Then the rite wavers. And the crown takes whoever's closest to breaking."

Juniper watched Paloma write that down. Of course he wrote it down. He wrote everything down; it was how he kept the world from sliding. His hand moved quick and small across the page, and the ink seemed to follow him a half-beat too eagerly, the way it always did, and she felt the ember stir against her ribs like a dog scenting another dog.

*That one,* the ember whispered. *The ink-one. He tastes of giving things away.*

"He tastes of none of your business," she muttered.

Paloma's pen paused. "Juniper."

"Nothing. Keep writing."

* * *

They argued about it for an hour.

Not loudly — that was the strange thing. Even now, with catchers in the streets and the king's body rotting in public and riots turning the harbor district into a bonfire, they argued the way people argue over dinner. Maybe because of all that. Maybe because shouting would mean admitting how close to the edge they all were.

Isolde wanted to hold. Of course she did. She laid it out plainly: she had fed the crown for years, condemned prisoners passed through her seals like coins through a miser's hands, buying time, buying reforms, buying nothing that mattered in the end. Let her stand at the center. Let her name be the anchor. It was owed.

"It isn't a debt, Isolde," Paloma said. "It has to be given. Freely. You taught us that yourself."

"Then I give it freely."

"You give it like a woman paying off a balance," Wren said. "I've watched debtors my whole life. That's not freedom. That's arithmetic wearing a nicer dress."

Isolde's jaw tightened. Juniper almost liked her for it — the queen who couldn't quite stop being an accountant.

Paloma, naturally, offered himself. Juniper saw it coming the way you see a cart tip on a hill — slow, inevitable, maddening. "I'm the one who speaks," he said. "But if holding needs a steadier—"

"No," Juniper said.

Everyone looked at her. She hadn't meant to say it out loud. But there it was, sitting in the air between them, and she found she didn't want to take it back.

"You're already giving your name," she said. "Your whole name. Everything you are. If you hold too, then there's no you left to hold anything. You'll be a hole shaped like a person standing in the middle of a spell." She heard her own voice go rough. "I've been a hole shaped like a person. It's not a job. It's just cold."

The stove ticked. Somewhere below, the salt-works groaned through its nightly settling.

"That's the most words you've spent on me all week," Paloma said softly.

"Don't get used to it."

But he smiled — that small crooked smile he thought he hid — and Juniper realized she'd just done a thing she'd never done in her life: she'd told someone no on their behalf, and meant it, and they'd listened.

*Family,* the ember observed, with something that was almost gentleness. *Strange creatures. They hold each other up.*

"For once," she muttered, "we agree."

* * *

It was Wren who ended it.

She did it the way she did everything — no speech, no wind-up. Just reached out and put her hand flat on the ledger strip, on the fourth name from the top, and said:

"I hold."

The room went still.

"Maren," she said. "My sister. Eleven years I've carried her name like a stone in my boot. Every report I filed, every gifted kid I netted and hauled to the appraisers — I told myself I was keeping her alive somewhere in the system, that if I played the game well enough they'd never need to take anyone else. Lies stack, same as anything." Her thumb moved over the inked letters, careful, like touching a scar. "Her name is the only clean thing I've got left. Not mine — hers. She gave it to me when we were girls. We used to trade it back and forth for luck. *Say Maren, say Wren, say it till the crows can't tell us apart.*"

She looked up, and for the first time since Juniper had known her, the scaffolding slipped. Just for a second. Just enough to see the grief underneath, old and enormous and load-bearing.

"The rite needs a name freely given, held steady. Hers was taken. Mine was spent. Put them together and neither one is owned anymore — not by the crown, not by the Guild, not by me." She tapped the page once. "I hold with Maren's name. That's my price for being in that room. Take it or leave it."

Nobody took it or left it. Nobody said anything at all for a long moment, and Juniper understood that silence was its own kind of yes.

Isolde bowed her head — actually bowed, the queen to the tinker, and Juniper thought: *the world really has come off its hinges.* "Then it's settled," Isolde said. "Wren holds. Paloma speaks."

"And I burn," Juniper said.

Every head turned again. She'd meant it as a statement of fact, but it came out like a challenge, and she supposed it was one.

"Not necessarily," Paloma started.

"It's fine." She pulled the word *fine* out like pulling a splinter. "I've been thinking about it. Everyone assumes the burning means me — my body, my gift, poured into the crown until it chokes. And maybe that's the old terms. But nobody's actually read the terms lately except me, and I've had a lot of time to talk to the thing living in my chest." She let the ember rise, just a little — a glow under her skin, along her wrists, warm gold light pooling in the dark loft like spilled honey. Everyone flinched except Wren. "It doesn't want to starve. It wants to be *fed*. There's a difference. Starving things take. Fed things can be asked."

"Asked to do what?" Isolde asked carefully.

Juniper smiled, and felt the ember smile with her, and for once the two smiles fit together like interlocked fingers.

"To take a new job."

* * *

Later — after Isolde had gone to write her abdication in a hand that shook only twice, after Paloma had fallen asleep sitting up over his notes like a boy at lessons, after Veyra had come and gone with a whisper and a key and a warning about the palace gates changing watch at midnight — Juniper climbed the ladder to the roof.

Coldharbour spread out below her, smoking and bell-less for once, the Feast of Bells still two days off but the city already holding its breath. Fires in the harbor district. Soldiers on the bridges. Somewhere out there, the palace, and under the palace, the mouth.

She sat on the ridge of the roof with her knees pulled up and let the ember climb into her cupped hands, where it showed itself as it truly liked to be seen: small, bright, restless. A coal with eyes it didn't have yet.

"Tomorrow," she told it.

*Tonight,* it corrected. *Time is a hunger too.*

"Everything's a hunger to you."

*Yes,* it said simply, and she laughed despite herself, because at least it was honest. Most things weren't.

Below her, faint through the floorboards, she heard Wren moving. Not sleeping — of course Wren wasn't sleeping. Juniper heard the soft scrape of a knife on wood, the sound she'd heard every night since the chapel: Wren carving. Juniper had peeked once. Wren was making a bird. A small wooden bird, wings folded, unfinished, worked on a few strokes at a time like a prayer said slowly.

*Maren liked birds,* Wren had said, the one time Juniper asked. Then she'd sharpened her knife and said nothing else, and that had been enough.

Juniper looked down at the ember glowing in her palms, and then at the dark window where the knife scraped soft and steady, and a thought began to form — small, bright, restless, exactly like the thing in her hands.

Maybe the ember didn't need a body at all.

Maybe it just needed a shape somebody had made with love, and a hearth to sit on, and people willing to keep it fed.

She grinned into the dark. "Oh," she whispered to the coal, to the city, to nobody. "Oh, I think I've got it."

And far below, in the sleeping streets of Coldharbour, the first bell of the Feast rang out early — wrong, cracked, hungry — and every gifted soul in the city woke at once, including the four in the loft, including the girl on the roof with a plan and a coal in her hands, because the crown had begun to call them home.
