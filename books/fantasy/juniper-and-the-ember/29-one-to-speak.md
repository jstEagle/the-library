## 29. One to Speak

The arithmetic was simple, and Paloma had always been good at arithmetic.

One name, freely given. One voice to speak the unbinding. Wren would hold — she had claimed it the way she claimed everything, with her one hand flat on the table and no argument permitted. Isolde had wanted all three roles and been refused, gently, by a queen's own former subjects, which was its own kind of miracle. And Juniper —

Juniper did not have to burn. That was settled. That was the whole point of everything they'd built in the ruined chapel and the tinker's loft and the long salt-stained miles between. The ember would take the crown's place, fed willingly, never starving. A hearth instead of a hunger.

Which left the speaking. And the speaking required a name. Not just any name — *his* name, the debt-bound thing stitched into him at the temple font, the ledger-entry that made him property. To speak it aloud as part of the rite was to spend it. All of it. Every syllable that Brother Cassian had protected for twenty years with his quiet erasures, every letter that made Paloma Coldharbour a person who could be found, owed, owned, remembered.

Spoken into the working, it would be unwritten. He would stand in the throne room tomorrow and say himself out loud, and when the last word left his mouth there would be nothing left to say it again.

He sat at Wren's worktable with a blank page in front of him and realized he had no idea how to prepare for becoming no one.

* * *

He tried words first. It was habit; it was who he had been.

He wrote out the rite three times in fair copy, checking each line against the echoes he'd read in the hollow chapel, against Cassian's letters, against the fragments Isolde remembered from Aldous's private devotions. The structure was sound. Three names: one to hold, one to speak, one to burn. The speaker gave his name as the key that turned the lock. The holder anchored the working so the key didn't simply vanish into the mechanism. The ember — Juniper's ember, tamed, willing — would fill the space the crown vacated, drawn thin and gentle, a flame in a lamp rather than a fire in a granary.

It was elegant. It was terrible. He checked it a fourth time because checking was easier than thinking.

Wren found him there past midnight, whittling something small and bird-shaped from a block of applewood, curls of pale wood gathering around her boots like snow that refused to melt.

"You've written it four times," she said.

"Three."

"I can count, scribe." She sat across from him, set the half-carved bird on the table between them. "You're doing the thing you do. Hiding inside the numbers."

"The numbers are what keep us alive."

"The numbers are what kept you *owned*," Wren said, not unkindly. "Twenty years of ledgers. You think if you get the accounting exactly right, the price stops being a price." She turned the bird over with her thumb. "My sister used to do that. Count things. How many steps to the well, how many coins in the jar, how many days since—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "Counting doesn't bring anyone back, Paloma. But remembering does. Remembering is the only arithmetic that matters now."

He looked at the page. His own handwriting, neat and small and careful, spelling out the shape of his disappearance.

"What am I supposed to do instead?" he asked. "Memorize the rite? I'll forget why I'm saying it. That's how it works. The name goes and everything anchored to it goes with it. My mother's face. The novitiate dormitory. The smell of the temple incense. All of it filed under *Paloma Coldharbour*, and the file burns."

"So don't memorize the rite," Wren said. She pushed the wooden bird an inch toward him. "Memorize us."

* * *

He spent the last evening doing exactly that, and it was the hardest work of his life.

Juniper first, because she was easiest and because he was a coward and wanted to start where it didn't hurt. She sat cross-legged on the floor by the hearth, teaching two of the freed children from the undercroft to fold paper boats, her soot-streaked hair tied back with a strip of red cloth Wren had given her. The ember lived in her now like a banked coal — visible only when she laughed, a faint gold flicker behind her eyes, warmth without appetite. He watched her correct a fold, patient, fierce, and he memorized the exact angle of her scowl when the boy tore his boat and she told him boats could be refolded and so could people.

*Boats can be refolded,* he thought, pressing it down deep, deeper than the rite would reach. *She said that. I want to be the kind of person who remembers she said that.*

Isolde came and went through the room like weather, carrying papers, drafting the abdication she would read before the regency council the morning after — if there was a morning after. Once she paused behind Paloma's chair and looked over his shoulder at nothing, at the air where his name used to feel solid.

"When my husband did this," she said quietly, "he told me not to watch. He said a king's sacrifice should be clean, distant, a story for the histories." Her hand rested briefly on his shoulder. "You are not allowed to be a story, scribe. You are allowed to be a man. Men are messier. Men get remembered properly."

Then Veyra, of all people, brought bread.

She stood in the doorway holding a loaf like it was an artifact from a foreign country, this woman who had sold them, betrayed them, been unmade and remade by hearing her own true name spoken back to her — the girl she'd been before the Guild, the one who'd once traded her father's compass for a meal and wept about it for years. She had let them go. She had come back. She still moved like someone waiting for the invoice.

"Eat," she said. "Tomorrow you'll need—" She stopped herself. Corrected. "Tomorrow *they'll* need your strength. Whatever's left of you."

"That's very nearly kind, mistress."

"Don't call me that." She set the loaf down hard. "I ran the arithmetic on you too, scribe. Price against return. And the sum kept coming out wrong. You were worth more than the entry said." Her eyes were dry and furious. "I hate that. Eat the bread."

He ate the bread. He memorized the way her hands shook afterward, and decided that counted too.

* * *

Near dawn, Juniper climbed onto the bench beside him.

They sat without talking for a while. Outside, Coldharbour's bells were already rehearsing for the Feast — no, not the Feast anymore, the city had renamed it, but bells don't care what you call them. They rang the same notes they'd rung when the crown was hungry, and they would ring tomorrow too, and Paloma found he wanted very much to remember what they sounded like from this window, this bench, this last hour of being someone.

"Does it hurt?" Juniper asked. "Losing it. Your name."

"I don't know. Nobody comes back to tell you."

"I would come back." She said it plainly, the way she said everything. "If it were me. I'd find my way back to wherever you were and tell you if it hurt."

He smiled. "That's not how forgetting works, little ember."

"It's how *I* work." She took his hand — both hands, hers small and warm and smelling of woodsmoke and paper boats. "Listen. Wren says the anchoring holds what matters. She's going to hold you with Aunt Sera's name, right? So the rite can't take the parts of you that we—"

"Juniper."

"—that we put in you. On purpose. All evening. All these weeks. You're full of us, Paloma. You're so full of us there's barely any room left for the temple's version of you." Her chin came up, stubborn, ember-bright. "So lose the debt-name. Lose *Coldharbour*. We'll give you a better one. We're very good at giving people things here. Wren gives birds. Isolde gives second chances. I give—" she opened her palm, and a tiny flame stood up in it, no bigger than a candle's heart, tame as a tame thing, "—I give light. You'll see."

"And if I don't? If I'm standing in that throne room and the last word leaves my mouth and there's nobody home?"

"Then I'll do what I promised." Her grip tightened. "I'll talk to you until you remember why you're speaking. However long it takes. Even mid-rite. Even if the crown screams. You'll hear my voice and you'll know: somebody loved the man saying those words, and love isn't written in ledgers, and it can't be unwritten either."

Paloma looked at her — this feral child he'd sheltered out of guilt, out of recognition, out of some debt he'd never been able to enter in any book — and understood that she had just done the one thing no temple, no Guild, no crown had ever managed.

She had made him rich.

* * *

He did not sleep. Instead he walked the house while the others slept, touching doorframes, counting nothing.

In the common room, Wren had finished the bird. It sat on the mantel beside the cold hearth, wings folded, head tilted, applewood gone golden under her knife. She'd carved it for Juniper originally — everyone knew — but sometime in the night she had scratched something small into its base, and Paloma bent close and read it in her blunt catcher's hand:

*For the one who speaks. Hold fast.*

Not his name. She was smarter than that. She'd carved the instruction without the identity, so that whatever he became, the message would still be addressed correctly.

He stood there a long time, learning the shape of the bird by heart. Learning the scratch of the letters. Learning the weight of the silence in a house full of sleeping people who had chosen him.

When the bells began the hour before dawn, Paloma Coldharbour went to the window and memorized the sky — grey going to rose, Coldharbour's rooftops stacked like ledgers, smoke rising straight from a hundred chimneys, all of it ordinary, all of it worth more than any entry he'd ever balanced.

Then he picked up the fair copy of the rite, read it once more until the words lived somewhere below memory, and burned the page in the hearth.

No drafts. No copies. Some transactions should exist in only one place.

He felt strangely light walking to the door, where Juniper waited with Wren and Isolde and Veyra, where the road to the palace ran east through streets already filling with festival crowds who didn't know their kingdom was about to be unbound by a scribe with no name to lose and everything else besides.

"Ready?" Juniper asked.

Paloma took one last breath of the name he was about to spend — felt it sit in his chest like a coin he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it wasn't part of his body — and let it go.

"No," he said honestly. "But I'm coming anyway."

And far off, beneath the bells, beneath the city, something old and patient felt three names gathering at its door — and began, for the first time in thirty years, to be afraid.
