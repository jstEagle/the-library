## 30. One to Burn

The ember woke her before dawn.

It always did. Not with words — it didn't need words anymore — but with a warmth that pressed against the inside of her ribs like a cat wanting out. *Up,* it said without saying. *Up, up, there's so much to burn.*

"Shut up," Juniper mumbled into Wren's spare blanket, which smelled of solder and tallow and, faintly, of the chapel they'd all slept in two nights ago before moving to Isolde's safehouse above the old granary. "It's not morning."

*It's always morning somewhere,* the ember said, smug as a banked fire.

She'd learned its tricks over the months. It had a voice for every mood: the coaxing voice when she was sad, the hungry voice when she was angry, the small pitiful voice — the worst one — when she tried to ignore it too long. *You'll be lonely without me,* that voice said. *No one else will ever be this warm.*

It was right about the warmth. It was wrong about everything else.

Juniper sat up in the dark of the granary loft and looked at the others sleeping around her, arranged the way they always arranged themselves: Paloma nearest the door, because he still thought he had to guard everyone even though he couldn't remember half his own childhood now; Wren on her back with her one hand curled under her chin; Cassian's letter folded in Wren's coat pocket where she kept it like a talisman; Queen Isolde apart from all of them, sitting up already, watching the window, watching the city, watching everything.

And below them, under the palace, under the streets, under everything — the crown. The mouth. The thing that ate.

Juniper hugged her knees and let herself think the thought she'd been circling for three days, the one that felt like standing at the edge of a roof.

They were going to do the rite tomorrow night. Wren would hold. Paloma would speak. And everyone — Paloma, Wren, even Isolde, even quiet practical Veyra in her grudging way — everyone assumed the third part, the burning part, belonged to her.

*One to hold, one to speak, one to burn.* She'd heard it in the hollow chapel, seen it in the echoes Paloma read off the ruined walls. Three willing names. And since the ember lived in her, since the ember was the only fire big enough to unmake a crown, then obviously, naturally, the way the story went —

Juniper was eleven years old and she had been told what she was for more times than she could count. By the catchers who chased her. By the Guild men who called her *stock*. By the ember itself, whispering at night: *you're my vessel, you're my vessel, you're mine.*

Everyone looked at her and saw the ending of the story.

Well. She'd stolen enough bread in her life to know that stories, like loaves, could be taken apart and rearranged.

* * *

She found Paloma on the granary stairs, because he never slept properly anymore and because he always sat exactly where he could see both the door and the sky, which she'd decided was a scribe habit, like the way he counted things under his breath.

He had a stub of pencil and a scrap of paper and he was writing something, crossing it out, writing it again. When she sat down next to him he didn't startle, just shifted to make room, which was one of the ten thousand small things about him that had quietly become the most important things in the world.

"You're supposed to be sleeping," he said.

"So are you."

"I'm memorizing." He tilted the paper so she could see. It wasn't words. It was little drawings — Wren's crooked smile, the exact shape of the gap in Isolde's teeth, the way Juniper's own hair stuck up on the left side no matter what. "In case tomorrow takes more than they say it will. I want to go into it full."

Juniper looked at the drawings for a long time. Her chest did something complicated.

"That's cheating," she said finally. "That's how you keep your name. You're supposed to give it all."

"I'm giving the name," Paloma said. "Nobody said anything about the love." He said it lightly, the way he said most true things, and went back to drawing, and Juniper understood that this was the moment, that if she was going to say what she'd spent three nights turning over, it was now, while the house slept and the sky was still gray and nobody could make it into a ceremony.

"Paloma." Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. She made it bigger. "Paloma. I'm not going to burn."

His pencil stopped.

"I mean — I am, a bit, probably, the rite needs fire, I get that. But not like you think. Not all of me. Not—" She pulled her knees up tighter. "Everyone keeps looking at me like I'm kindling. Like the plan is: Wren holds, you speak, and Juniper goes into the fire and that's the price and isn't it noble. Even Isolde. Even *you*, a little, I've seen your face when you think I'm not looking. You look at me like I'm already a story you're going to have to write down."

Paloma set the pencil down very carefully, the way he did with things that mattered.

"And what are you instead?" he asked. No argument in it. Just the question, open as a door. That was the other thing about him. He never told her what she was. He only ever asked.

"I've been talking to it," she said. "The ember. Really talking, not listening — there's a difference, I finally figured out the difference. And here's the thing nobody knows, because nobody's ever *asked* it anything. Everyone who ever had it just used it, or ran from it, or got eaten by it. But I asked it what it wants. Actually wants. Not the hungry stuff it says when it's trying to get its way. Under that."

"And?"

"It's cold," Juniper said. "Underneath all the burning, it's the coldest thing I've ever touched. It eats because it's starving. It's been starving since before the kingdom had a name. It doesn't want to eat us, Paloma. It wants to stop being hungry. It just doesn't know any other way to be, because every single person who ever held it treated it like a weapon or a curse, and so that's all it knows how to be." She was talking faster now, the idea pulling her up the way it always did, warm and terrifying. "And meanwhile there's this other thing that's been starving for centuries. This mouth under the palace. This crown. Eating people, eating gifts, eating whole lives, because it can't stop either. Two starving things, and everybody's plan is to feed one to the other and call it an ending."

Paloma had gone very still. Scribe-still. The stillness he got when a ledger line refused to balance and he was looking for the error that would make it true.

"The rite needs a fire to replace what the crown held," he said slowly. "The binding made the crown a vessel. Unbind it, and something has to take the weight, or the hunger just — pours out. That's why everyone assumed—"

"Assumed the vessel has to be destroyed along with the cargo." Juniper shook her head hard enough that her hair flew. "That's the old terms. That's *their* terms — the ones whoever wrote the first binding wrote, back when they thought everything alive was either a tool or a threat. But vessels don't have to be mouths. I've been thinking about hearths."

"Hearths."

"A hearth holds fire too. All night, every night. Nobody calls a hearth a monster. Nobody chains a hearth in a cellar and feeds it prisoners." She grabbed his sleeve, couldn't help it. "A hearth is fed *on purpose*. Willingly. A little at a time, by people who choose to. And in exchange it gives back warmth, and light, and a place to cook, and — Paloma, it *gives back*. The crown only ever took. What if the ember took its place but worked the other way? Fed willingly, in balance, never starving? Not a vessel chained in a cellar. A hearth in a room where people come because they want to."

The silence after she said it was so long she started to sink inside it. The ember stirred uneasily under her ribs, catching the shape of her doubt and offering, treacherously, its old bargain: *give yourself to me fully and no one else need burn, no one else need hurt, just say yes—*

"No," she told it, out loud, and Paloma glanced at her, and she shook her head: not you. Him. The other voice.

Because that was the trap, and she saw it clearly now, saw it the way she wished she'd seen it three nights ago when it had almost caught her: the ember's bargain and everyone's assumption were the same bargain wearing different clothes. *Burn whole or don't burn at all.* Feed it everything or starve it forever. All or nothing, devourer or fuel. Every choice anyone had ever offered her came in those two shapes, and she was sick to death of both of them.

"There's a third thing," she said to Paloma, and to the stairwell, and to the sleeping house, and to the ember coiled hot and startled beneath her ribs. "There's always a third thing. Nobody ever offers it because nobody ever thinks to ask."

* * *

Isolde was the first one they woke, because Isolde was already awake, because Isolde was always already awake, sitting in the window chair with her hands folded like a woman waiting for a verdict that had been coming for twenty years.

Juniper stood in front of her and made herself say all of it again — the cold underneath the ember, the hearth, the third thing — and watched the queen's face go through disbelief and dismissal and then, worse, hope, because Isolde guarded against everything except hope, hope got in under the guards every time.

"You're asking me," Isolde said at last, "to trust the kingdom's survival to a child's conversation with the very force we're trying to bind."

"I'm asking you to count," Juniper said. "You like counting. How many bindings has the old way survived? One. And it cost a king his soul and a kingdom its king and it's failing anyway, right now, tonight, while we sit here. Your way buys thirty years maybe. Mine — mine might buy forever, and nobody has to die for it. Not even me." She lifted her chin. "Especially not me."

Something moved behind Isolde's eyes — the ghost of a woman who had once fed condemned prisoners to a crown to buy time for reforms, who knew better than anyone alive the arithmetic of acceptable losses. Juniper watched her do the sum. Watched her arrive, slowly, painfully, at a number she'd never been allowed before.

"One," Isolde said. "One hearth. Fed willingly, guarded openly, answerable to — to whom?" And then, before Juniper could answer, answering herself, in the voice of a queen drafting law in real time: "To a council. To the gifted themselves. If we do this, girl, it will not be another secret. Secrets are how the last one rotted."

"Good," Juniper said. "Secrets are stupid anyway."

Wren laughed from the doorway — she'd been there long enough to hear the end, arms crossed, wooden shavings in her hair. "Hearth," she said, tasting the word. "I can build a housing for it. Brass and salt-glass, vented proper. Something you can carry, something you can *see*." Her jaw set. "Something that can't be locked in a cellar, because I'll know, and I'll come take it back."

"You'd give it hands?" Paloma asked from the stairs.

"I'd give it a *handle*," Wren said. "Different thing entirely."

* * *

They gathered the rest in the granary's main room as the sun came up, and Juniper stood on a flour barrel because if this was going to be a speech she was going to have the high ground, and laid it out one more time: the cold, the hunger, the hearth, the third thing.

She watched their faces the way she'd learned to watch fires — reading what fed them, what choked them.

Paloma's face was the easiest. He'd known on the stairs. He looked at her now the way he'd looked at the first ledger line that ever balanced after hours of trying: like something impossible had turned out to be simply true.

Wren was already sketching on a board with her charcoal stub, vents and seams and a hinged door, muttering numbers.

Isolde sat straight-backed, composing edicts in her head; you could practically hear the parchment.

But it was the ember's reaction that mattered most, and Juniper paid attention to nothing else while the humans argued logistics. Because the ember had gone quiet. Truly quiet, for the first time in her whole life of carrying it. Not sulking-quiet, not plotting-quiet. Quiet the way a fire is quiet when someone finally, finally opens the damper and lets it breathe.

*You mean it,* it said, and its voice was different, small and wondering, the voice it must have had before anyone taught it to be a weapon. *You'd feed me on purpose. Not lock me away. Not pour me out. You'd let me be — warm.*

"That's the deal," Juniper said silently. "Take the crown's place, but new terms. Fed willingly, in balance, never starving. You give warmth. They give wood. Everybody eats. Nobody gets eaten."

*A hearth,* the ember said, trying the word the way Wren had, tasting it. And then, so quietly she almost missed it, in a voice like a coal settling: *No one ever asked me what I wanted before.*

"I know," Juniper said. "That's how they got away with it."

Below her feet, far under the city, something vast and patient shifted in its sleep — the crown, sensing perhaps that somewhere above it, its replacement was being negotiated into existence on kinder terms. The granary floor hummed faintly. The candle flames all leaned toward Juniper at once, like flowers toward sun.

Paloma noticed. Of course he noticed. He crossed to her barrel and put one hand on her ankle, steadying, anchoring, the gesture that meant *I'm here, I see you, whatever comes.*

"They'll say it can't work," he said. "Veyra. The council. Half our own company, tonight, when they hear it. They'll say the old terms are the only terms, that some hungers can't be tamed, only spent."

"Let them say it." Juniper looked east, where the sun was clearing the rooftops of Coldharbour and lighting the palace spires gold — gold all the way down to the mouth beneath them. Tomorrow night they'd walk into that mouth. Tomorrow night Wren would hold and Paloma would speak and Juniper would stand in the middle of it all and offer the oldest, coldest hunger in the world a job instead of a grave.

"But first," she said, "somebody has to teach a fire what a home is."

The ember, beneath her ribs, burned steady and low and — for the first time she could remember — *warm all the way through.*

And far below the city, in the dark under the throne room, the crown began, very softly, to scream.
