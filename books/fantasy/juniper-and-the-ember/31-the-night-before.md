## 31. The Night Before

### I.

Paloma could not sleep, so he did what he had always done when sleep refused him: he worked.

The safehouse above the rebuilt chapel had once been a vestry, and it still smelled faintly of tallow and old stone. He sat at a table by candlelight with the Guild's ledger open before him — the real one, the master ledger, the one Veyra had surrendered along with everything else when she let them go. Tomorrow he would close it forever. Tonight he was checking the arithmetic one last time, because that was who he was, or who he had been, and he wanted to meet the morning as himself.

*Debt of Paloma Coldharbour to the Temple of the Quiet Hand: entered year of salt, day 114.*

He read his own name in the ledger's cramped hand and felt nothing like dread for the first time in twenty years. Only a kind of tenderness, the way you might feel toward a coat you'd outgrown. It had kept him warm. It had also kept him small.

Footsteps on the stair, soft ones. Juniper appeared in the doorway wrapped in a blanket that was really a curtain, her hair still smelling of woodsmoke from the hearth downstairs. She didn't ask why he was awake. She never asked; she just came and sat, cross-legged on the bench across from him, watching the candle flame with the proprietary calm of someone who owned fire.

"You're doing sums," she said.

"I'm closing accounts."

"Tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow."

She nodded gravely, as if this were a matter of state, which perhaps it was. Then she reached across the table and turned the candle toward herself, and the flame leaned into her palm without burning, a small orange tongue lapping at her fingers like a cat. She did it absently, the way other children twirled their hair.

"It's quiet tonight," she said. "Inside me, I mean. Usually it talks."

"What does it say usually?"

"Promises." She shrugged. "It says if I stop holding back, nobody will have to be afraid ever again. It says it can eat all the fear in the world." Her eyes came up to his, sharp and soot-dark. "It lies. But it lies beautifully."

Paloma set down his pen. "And tonight it's quiet?"

"Tonight it's listening." She looked at the flame in her hand. "I think it knows something's going to change. I think it's scared too."

He hadn't considered that. The ember, afraid. He reached across the table and covered her small fist, flame and all, and felt only warmth, the ordinary warmth of a banked hearth, of bread coming out of an oven, of a hand held in the dark.

"Tell it this," he said. "Tell it that tomorrow it doesn't have to be hungry anymore. That we're going to build it a house instead of a mouth."

Juniper studied him for a long moment. Then she closed her fist around the flame, gently, the way you'd cup water for a thirsty bird, and something in her face went soft and young.

"It heard," she said. "It says — " she paused, listening to something very far inside herself — "it says thank you. In its own language. Which is just warmth. You're feeling it right now. That's what thank-you sounds like."

Paloma looked down at their hands and realized the warmth had spread up his arm and into his chest, and that it was not the ember at all, or not only. He had spent his whole life believing love was a debt you repaid. Sitting there, he understood — finally, simply, the way you understand a sum that has balanced — that it was not.

They stayed until the candle burned low. Neither of them spoke again. There was no need. Some accounts close themselves.

* * *

### II.

Wren sat by the kitchen fire with a knife and a piece of applewood, and had been sitting there for three hours.

She carved the way she did everything: with one hand and a great deal of stubbornness. The knife was wedged against her hip; the wood turned against the blade in small, patient strokes. She had made hinges this way, and lockpicks, and once — though she never told anyone — a flute. Wood did not care how many hands you had. Wood only cared whether you were willing to listen to it.

The shape emerging under her blade was a bird. Not any particular bird. A bird the way a child draws a bird: round body, folded wings, a tail like a comma. She had started it two days ago without knowing why, and somewhere in the second hour she had known exactly why, and had kept carving anyway, because knowing a thing and saying a thing were different trades entirely.

Her sister Maren had been taken eleven years ago. Wren had been a catcher then, list in her pocket, coin in her purse, and she had told herself every night that the work was mercy — better the Guild took them than the crown, better a cell than a pyre. Then Maren's name had gone onto the list, written in Wren's own hand because someone senior had to certify the intake, and every lie she had built collapsed in a single afternoon like wet scaffolding.

She had carried the list ever since. Forty-one names. Thirty-eight dead, by her best accounting. Three unaccounted for. Tomorrow, if the rite worked, the crown that ate them would starve, and the forty-one would be avenged in the only currency that mattered: they would be the last.

The door creaked. Isolde came in, still dressed in the plain gray wool she wore now instead of mourning silks, carrying two cups of barley tea. She set one beside Wren without a word and lowered herself onto the bench with the careful stiffness of a woman whose body had learned, over years of sequestered grief, to expect nothing comfortable.

"That's the third bird you've ruined," Isolde said, nodding at the wood.

"It's not ruined. It's a wren."

"It's a potato with ambitions."

Wren snorted despite herself. Eleven days ago she had hated this woman — had hated the queen who fed condemned prisoners to her husband's crown to buy time for reforms, reforms that always seemed to need one more month, one more prisoner, one more name. She still hadn't forgiven her. But she had watched Isolde write her abdication in a steady hand, line after line, giving away a kingdom the way you'd give away a coat, and hatred needed more fuel than contempt, and contempt had run out.

"They'll want a regent," Isolde said, cradling her tea. "After tomorrow. The council will look at me first."

"And?"

"And I'll refuse. I've decided." She said it lightly, but her knuckles whitened on the cup. "Thirty years I told myself I was holding the kingdom together. What I was holding was a lid on a pot. Let someone else hold something. Let it be many someones. Wren — " she hesitated, and for a moment the queen fell away and there was only a tired woman of fifty-odd years who had lost a husband twice, once to the crown and once to the truth — "the council will need people who aren't nobles. People who know what the ledgers cost. Would you sit? When this is done?"

Wren turned the little bird over in her palm. Its wings weren't finished. They'd never be finished. That was fine. Some things you gave away mid-mending.

"I'll sit," she said. "But I'm bringing the list. Every session. Until all forty-one names are read aloud and struck through."

Isolde looked at her for a long time. "That's not how councils work."

"No," Wren agreed. "It's how they should."

The fire settled. Somewhere upstairs, floorboards creaked — Paloma, pacing, or Juniper dreaming, or the house itself turning over in its sleep. Wren picked the knife back up and made one last cut, deepening the groove where the wings folded, and thought about Maren, who had loved plums and hated mornings and once carved a wooden horse so badly it looked like a dog, and how Wren had kept it anyway, and how she would keep this bird anyway, and how keeping things badly-made was its own kind of prayer.

Tomorrow she would hold. She had chosen it, out loud, in front of all of them, and choosing had felt like setting down a weight she'd carried so long she'd forgotten it wasn't part of her body.

She blew a curl of shaving off the bird's back and watched it catch the firelight and vanish.

"One more night, Mare," she said quietly. "Then you rest."

### III.

In the room that had been the chapel's sacristy, Queen Isolde sat at a writing desk older than the kingdom's current sorrows and wrote the last sentence of her abdication for the fourth time.

*I, Isolde, by marriage Queen of Coldharbour, do relinquish—*

No. *Relinquish* sounded like surrender, and this was not surrender. This was surgery. She crossed it out.

*I, Isolde, do renounce all claim and title—*

Renounce. Better. Renounce was a word with teeth. She read the paragraph through: the renunciation, the dissolution of the hunt, the order of reparations — land, coin, and public apology, in that order, because apology without restitution was just performance, and she had performed enough for one reign. At the bottom, where the royal seal would go, she had already decided to press her signet ring into wax one final time and then melt the seal itself in the same flame, so that no one could forge her authority after she laid it down.

Aldous would have laughed at her. No — Aldous would have understood. That was the terrible thing, the thing she had carried alone for thirty years: he would have understood completely. He had bound himself to the crown to feed a starving kingdom, trading his soul for bread, and she had kept the bargain alive after him because dismantling it meant admitting the famine had never ended, only changed shape. Every prisoner fed to the thing wearing her husband's face had bought a granary, a school, a law. She had counted them. She had made herself count them, every name, because a queen who stopped counting became the crown.

Three hundred and six names. She knew them the way Wren knew her forty-one. Perhaps tomorrow they could read both lists together, and strike them through together, and let the counting end.

There was a knock. She said, "Come," and Juniper came in, blanket-caped, barefoot, trailing the smell of woodsmoke and, beneath it, something older — hot metal, summer stone, the smell of a gift at rest.

"I can't sleep," Juniper announced.

"Neither can I."

"Good." The girl climbed onto the window seat and pulled her knees up. "Can I watch you write? I like watching people write. Paloma writes like the words are afraid of him. You write like you're afraid of the words."

Isolde almost smiled. "Which is worse?"

"Yours." Juniper said it without cruelty, the way children report weather. "But yours are braver."

Isolde looked down at the page. *I, Isolde, do renounce.* Her hand hovered. Then, in the margin, in a smaller script, she added a line that would never appear in the official copy, a line for no archive and no council:

*Aldous. I am putting it down now. Forgive me for holding it so long, and forgive me for what I held it with.*

She signed the main text. Pressed the seal. Held the wax over the candle, and watched her authority pool and run and die, and felt lighter than she had in thirty years, and grieved, and was glad of the grief, because it meant the thing underneath was finally her own.

When she looked up, Juniper was asleep on the window seat, one hand curled open at her side, and in her palm — visible only for a heartbeat, a flicker, a coal remembering it had once been a hearth — a small warm light breathed in and out, in and out, like something practicing being gentle.

Isolde draped her shawl over the girl and returned to her desk, and began, in a fresh hand, the first letter of whatever came after queens.

* * *

### IV.

Veyra stood in the chapel yard below, ostensibly checking the horses, actually smoking a pipe she had sworn off eleven years ago, looking up at the lit windows of the safehouse.

She had sold them out once. She had weighed their lives against the Guild's interest and found the Guild heavier, because that was the whole of her religion: the ledger is the lord, the balance is the blessing. And then a scribe had spoken her true name aloud in a marsh-side barn — not to break her, not to bind her, just to show her — and she had seen, for about four seconds, a girl named Mereth Veyra, age nine, trading buttons for bread in a flood-year market, sharp-eyed and unbought, before the Guild had gotten hold of her and taught her that everything was inventory.

Four seconds. It had been enough. It had undone thirty years of compound interest.

She was not sorry. That was the strange part. She had expected remorse to feel like a debt called due, and instead it felt like a ledger closing — clean lines, no remainder. Tomorrow, if the scribe's mad rite worked, the Guild of Impossible Trades would lose its most profitable commodity overnight: fear of the gifted, priced per head. And Veyra found she did not care. She had already begun drafting, in her head, the charter of something new. A registry, not a hunt. An exchange where gifts were declared freely and protected by contract, not harvested by catchers. Impossible trade, yes. But she had never traded in anything else.

Above her, a window opened. Paloma leaned out — the scribe, the debtor, the man who would speak himself unwritten in the morning — and looked down at her.

"You should sleep," he said.

"So should you."

"I'm not certain I'll remember how, after tomorrow." He said it evenly, without self-pity, and that was somehow worse. "Veyra. If it works — the charter you're drafting. Leave a place in it for people who don't know what their gift is yet. Children like her. Don't appraise them first. Ask them first."

Mereth Veyra, mistress of the Guild of Impossible Trades, looked up at the man who had shown her a nine-year-old girl in a flood-year market, and inclined her head.

"Asked first," she said. "Entered into the record. Witnessed by the night and one bad pipe."

He withdrew. The window closed. The lights upstairs went out one by one — the sacristy last, where a former queen slept at a desk over a finished abdication, and a child slept on a window seat with a tame coal breathing in her hand.

Veyra knocked out her pipe against her boot, checked the horses a final time, and went inside to draft, by candlelight, the first honest contract of her life.

* * *

### V.

Last of all, in the loft above the kitchen, Juniper woke once, briefly, near the hour when even bells sleep.

She lay still and listened. Below her: Wren's slow breathing, and the tick of cooling embers. Across the yard: Isolde's pen, still moving, still brave. Upstairs: Paloma, awake, doing sums, closing accounts, being Paloma for one more night. And everywhere, under everything, the ember — quiet, listening, warm as a held hand.

Tomorrow she would carry it into the throne room. Tomorrow everyone would expect it to burn.

Instead, she thought, pressing her palm flat against the floorboards, sending warmth down through the house like a wordless goodnight — instead, it was going to come home.

The ember pulsed once against her skin. Not a promise. Not a hunger.

An answer.
