## 32. The Unbinding

The throne room of Coldharbour had not been swept in a year.

Paloma noticed it the way he noticed everything now — as a ledger entry, a line item in the account of a dying kingdom. Dust lay along the flagstones in grey drifts. The banners of Aldous's line hung faded to the color of old tea. No servants came here anymore; the thing on the throne did not require sweeping, only feeding, and its meals were brought by other hands.

They came in through the undercroft stair, thirty-one of them — freed captives still unsteady on their feet, Wren with her sleeve pinned where her hand had been, Isolde in mourning grey that was also, Paloma realized, armor. Juniper walked at his side with the ember's light bleeding faintly through her skin, a glow like a coal wrapped in paper.

Above them, behind the doors at the top of the dais steps, the Feast of Bells was ending. Ten thousand people ringing ten thousand bells to drown out the sound of their king screaming. They had heard him scream for three days now. The crown had stopped pretending.

"It will know we're coming," Isolde said quietly. "It has always known."

"Good," Wren said. "Saves us knocking."

* * *

Paloma had spent the last evening doing what he always did before an impossible trade: reading the terms until he could recite them blind.

The Binding of the Crown required three names, freely given. One to hold — an anchor, a fixed point, someone whose name meant something so solid that even a hungry god could not pull it loose. Wren had claimed it without hesitation, and she would anchor the working with her sister Maren's name, spoken and held and never let go.

One to speak — the voice of the unbinding, the one who read the true words aloud while the crown tried to eat him. That was Paloma. It had always been going to be Paloma. He was the scribe; unbinding was, in the end, a matter of striking through a line of text.

One to burn — and that was the argument they'd had for two days, and the answer Juniper had forced down all their throats: the ember itself would take the crown's place, fed willingly, in balance, never starving. Not Juniper burning. The fire choosing its own shape.

He had memorized the rite the way he memorized everything — three times through, then closed the book. But tonight, sitting in the hollow chapel with dust in his hair, he had done something different. He had put the book away and looked instead at faces.

Wren, whittling a wooden bird by candlelight, her knife moving with the economy of a woman who had learned to do everything with five fingers less than she'd been given. Cassian's absence, which sat in the room like a chair no one used. Veyra, who had let them go and then, impossibly, followed — standing watch at the chapel door with her Guild signet turned inward on her finger, saying nothing, owing nothing, present anyway. Isolde writing letters she would never send. And Juniper, asleep against his shoulder, her breath smelling of stolen plums, the ember pulsing under her collarbone slow as a sleeping heart.

He had looked at each of them and thought: *this is what I am made of.* Not the temple's ink. Not the debt-bond scarred into his wrist. This.

Because tomorrow the crown would take the rest.

* * *

Now they stood at the foot of the dais, and King Aldous raised his head.

What was left of his face had gone the color of tallow. His eyes were open, and they were not eyes anymore — they were apertures, deep and dark and patient, and Paloma felt his gift rise up unbidden, the way it always did, reading truth into the world whether he wanted it or not.

*Mouth,* his gift whispered. *Old mouth. Patient mouth. It has tasted you.*

"I know," Paloma said aloud, and his voice cracked on the second word.

The king smiled. It was a terrible smile because it was almost human — some remnant of Aldous still lived in there, some small starved thing pressed against the bars of its own body, and it smiled the way a drowning man smiles at a rope.

"You came," the king said. The voice was layered, two voices braided together, one dry and one vast. "The little reader. I remember your taste. A memory, wasn't it? Something about—"

"Don't," Paloma said.

"—a garden," the king continued softly. "A woman planting something. You don't know what she planted anymore, do you? You've been reaching for it for years. A hole shaped like a season."

Paloma's chest went cold. He had told no one about the missing memory. He hadn't known it existed until the appraiser's visit, until the falsified entry cost him something he couldn't name, and he had carried the hole ever since like a stone in his shoe — always there, never quite findable.

The crown had eaten it. The crown had been eating him since the day he first used his gift in earnest, and it wanted him to know that now, wanted him to come to the rite already grieving.

"That's the price," the king said gently. "You knew that. Everything reads true costs true. Shall I tell you what else you've lost? There are four more holes in you, little scribe. Would you like me to—"

"Wren," Paloma said. "Hold."

Wren stepped forward onto the dais. She didn't look at the king. She looked at the space just above the throne, where the crown sat on a head that barely qualified as a head anymore, and she began to speak.

"Maren Thistle-down," she said. "Born in the salt marshes, third daughter of a netmender. She could mend anything. She mended my bootlaces when I was six because I couldn't make a bow one-handed and she laughed at me and called me Lefty and she was taken on the ninth of Harvestmonth and her name is Maren Thistle-down and I am holding it."

Her voice didn't shake. Her voice was a nail driven into the world, and Paloma felt it take — felt the air around Wren go taut and still, like the moment before a bell is struck. The anchor. The fixed point.

The crown screamed.

Not from the king's throat — from everywhere. From the walls, from the floor, from the bells outside that suddenly all rang wrong, discordant, a sound like metal in pain. The candles guttered flat. Somewhere above, the Feast of Bells faltered, thousands of ringers feeling, without knowing why, that the music had turned to teeth.

"Speak," Wren said to Paloma, through her teeth, holding. "Now."

* * *

Paloma climbed the dais steps.

Each step was harder than the last, as though he were wading upstream through cold water. The crown's attention settled on him fully for the first time, and it was like being looked at by the ocean — vast, indifferent, and aware of him down to the marrow.

He stopped one step below the throne. Close enough to see that the king's lips were cracked and bleeding, close enough to see the small remnant behind the eyes pressing forward, mouthing something over and over. He read it without wanting to.

*Bread,* the remnant was saying. *I only wanted bread for them.*

Paloma's heart broke a little, and he let it, because a broken heart was still a heart, and he would need every piece.

He opened his mouth and spoke the first word of the unbinding.

He had expected it to be hard. It was not hard. It was the easiest thing he had ever said, the way the truest things are easy — the word left him like water finding a downhill grade, and the world *moved*. The dust lifted off the flagstones. The dead banners stirred. In the walls of the palace, in the foundations, in the bones of Coldharbour itself, something ancient shifted in its sleep and began, slowly, to wake.

The crown fought back.

* * *

It did not attack his body. It attacked his past.

The second word had barely left his lips when the memory hit him — not his own. A borrowed life, whole and sudden: a fisherman on the Greywater, hauling nets at dawn, the smell of brine and tar, a wife calling from the shore, forty years of mornings compressed into a single drowning instant. Paloma staggered. For a heartbeat he *was* the fisherman; he had a wife, he had a boat named after his mother, he had —

No. He had a name to speak. He gasped the third word and the fisherman dissolved, and the next life crashed in before he could breathe.

A child in the temple nursery, fevered, dying, a brother singing to her in the dark. A soldier at the northern wall, counting arrows, counting friends, running out of both. An old woman baking bread — bread again, always bread, the crown's oldest hunger wearing a thousand kitchens — and each life arrived complete, total, *true*, and each one insisted with the full weight of lived experience: *you are me, this is your life, you have always been this person, the scribe is the dream.*

"They're not yours," Wren's voice cut through, distant, iron-hard. "Paloma. They're not yours. Hold your own line."

But that was the trap, wasn't it? What was his own line? He was a man with holes in him. Four holes, the crown had said, maybe more. How do you hold your identity when parts of it have been eaten, when the crown can pour its stolen lives into the gaps and they fit *perfectly*?

The fifth life was a woman planting a garden.

Paloma went down to one knee. The word he was supposed to speak next slipped away from him like a fish. The garden was spring-green, absurdly vivid, and the woman had dirt on her cheek and she was humming, and he knew her, he *loved* her, he had loved her his whole life, who was she, who was she —

She turned and smiled at him and said a name.

His name. His real name, the one the temple had bound, the one the crown had eaten along with her face and the season and whatever she had been planting — and hearing it was like plunging his hand into ice water. He remembered. He remembered her, he remembered the garden, he remembered being seven years old and happy, and the grief of it was so enormous that for one full second he forgot the rite entirely, forgot the throne room, forgot everything but the woman in the sun.

And the crown poured itself into that second. It came flooding through the open door of his grief, life after life after life, hundreds of them, thousands, every soul it had ever eaten offered up as proof that he was no one, that he was everyone, that the self was a fiction and surrender was only honesty —

"PALOMA."

Juniper's voice. Not from the foot of the dais — from directly beside him. She had climbed the steps. The ember was blazing through her skin now, and the heat of her rolled off her in waves that made the borrowed memories flinch and curl back like paper near flame.

She took his face in both hands. Her palms were hot. Her eyes were furious and terrified and absolutely certain.

"You're the one who found me in the stacks," she said. "You're the one who lied to Veyra for me. You're the one who gave me plums when you only had three and pretended you weren't hungry. Those are yours. Nobody else's. Not the crown's. Yours."

The flood paused.

"And you still owe me a story," she added, "about how ledgers work, because you explained it once and I wasn't listening, and if you die before you explain it properly I'm going to be annoyed forever."

Paloma laughed. It came out broken and wet and it was the most human sound in the room.

He stood up. The crown threw another life at him — a queen, a beggar, a catcher, a child — and he spoke through them, around them, over them, each word of the unbinding hammered home like a nail while the storm of the stolen dead tried to tear the hammer from his hand. Wren held below, her voice steady, her sister's name a keel in deep water. Isolde had moved to the base of the dais, arms spread, facing the doors against whatever came through them. Veyra stood beside her, and Paloma heard her say, dry as arithmetic, "I never liked this palace."

Word by word. Line by line. Striking through the text.

The crown shrieked and the king's body convulsed, and Paloma saw — with the last clear corner of his gift — the remnant inside, the small starved thing that had been Aldous, pressing itself flat against the walls of its prison as the unbinding shook the bars loose.

Halfway. He was halfway. The words were getting harder now, heavier, each one costing more than the last, and the memories were no longer attacking — they were *pleading*, thousands of voices begging him to stop, to rest, to stay, to be them instead, and God, it would be so easy, it would be such a kindness, to set down the weight of being himself —

His name flickered.

He felt it go — felt the word *Paloma* lose its grip on the inside of his skull, felt it slide toward the edge of him like a book slipping from a shelf. He opened his mouth for the next word of the rite and found, with a calm and distant horror, that he no longer knew why he was speaking.

He kept speaking. Because Juniper's hands were on his face, and her voice was saying his name — the flickering, fading word — over and over, holding it the way Wren held Maren's, the way you hold a door shut against a flood.

"Paloma. Paloma. Paloma."

Below them, the great doors of the throne room burst open, and the crown's last defense came through — but that was Juniper's fight, and Juniper's fire, and the ember rose in her like a dawn deciding it had waited long enough.

Paloma spoke the next word. He did not know his name. He knew her hands.

He spoke the one after that.
