## 33. The Nameless Scribe

The throne room was full of voices that weren't voices.

Juniper felt them the way she felt heat off a forge — through her skin, through her teeth, through the ember curled behind her ribs like a second heart. Wren stood at the north pillar with both hands flat against the stone, one flesh and one carved wood, holding. Paloma stood in the center of the floor where the old kings had been crowned, and he was speaking.

He had been speaking for a long time.

His voice came out steady and low, word after word after word, names of things and names of laws and names of debts unwound backward into their first syllables, and every name he spoke made the crown on the empty throne burn a little dimmer. Juniper could see it from where she crouched by the door — a black-gold ring of light, and around it a darkness that moved like something chewing.

She was supposed to be watching the doors.

That was her job. Wren held, Paloma spoke, and Juniper burned — but not yet, not all at once, only when the crown turned to look at her. So she watched the doors and kept her flame banked small, small as a candle in a window, small as the wooden bird in her pocket, and she listened to Paloma speak the world apart.

Then his voice changed.

It didn't stop. That was the terrible thing. It went on exactly as before, same rhythm, same low music, but Juniper heard the moment it emptied out — the way you can hear when someone stops being home even if they're still talking to you. The words kept coming but nothing was behind them anymore.

"Paloma?" she said.

He didn't turn his head. His lips moved. His hands were open at his sides, palms up, the way he held them over ledgers when he was reading true names, and there was ink on them still, three days old, from the night he'd written himself ready.

Wren's head came up. "Juniper." Her voice was tight. "What's happening?"

"I don't know."

But she did know. She knew the way she'd known the smell of the catchers' dogs, the way she'd known which shelves in the archive were safe to sleep behind. The crown had found him. It had reached down into him the way roots reach into a wall, patient, splitting stone without hurry, and it had taken hold of the thing he'd offered it — his name, freely given, freely spent — and it was pulling.

*Come back,* she thought at him, uselessly, the way you think at a fire to make it smaller. *Paloma. Come back.*

He spoke on. And the words coming out of him now weren't the rite. She could hear it — hear the difference the way Wren could hear a bad gear in a good machine. He was saying other things. Names. Hundreds of names. Thousands. Names of people who had never existed, people the crown had eaten, pouring back out of him like water finding cracks, because the crown was drowning him in its pantry and using his mouth to do it.

"He's unraveling," Wren said. Her carved hand pressed harder against the pillar; the wood creaked. "I can feel it through the hold. The anchor's slipping — no. Not the anchor. *Him.* The rite is fine. He's coming apart inside it."

"What do I do?"

"You're the ember," Wren said. "You tell me."

*Thanks very much,* Juniper thought, and wanted to laugh and cry and set something on fire, all three, all at once, which was more or less her whole life in one sentence.

She crossed the floor.

Nobody had told her she could cross the floor. The plan said stay by the doors, watch the guards, keep the flame small until the moment came. But plans were things grown-ups wrote down so they'd have something to be sad about later, and Juniper had learned long ago — in the archive stacks, in the salt marsh, in every cold doorway she'd ever slept in — that the plan stops mattering the moment the person you love starts disappearing.

So she walked out onto the open stones, into the crown's line of sight, and knelt down in front of Paloma where he stood speaking his borrowed thousands of names.

Up close he looked wrong. Not hurt-wrong. *Hollow*-wrong. His eyes were open and aimed at the middle distance and there was nobody in them. His mouth moved. A dead woman's name came out. Then a dead man's. Then a child's, a child who had died two hundred years ago in a famine that King Aldous had ended by selling his soul for bread, and the child's name came out of Paloma's mouth in Paloma's careful scribe-voice, and Juniper's chest cracked open.

"You gave your name away," she said to him. "That was the deal. One to speak. You knew it would cost."

Of course he'd known. He'd spent his last evening memorizing their faces instead of words. She hadn't understood it then. She understood it now, standing in front of him while he forgot her: he had been trying to carry them somewhere the crown couldn't reach.

But the crown had longer arms than anyone guessed.

"Paloma." She put her hand on his wrist. His skin was cold. Underneath the cold, faintly, she felt his pulse, and under the pulse she felt — barely, like a coal buried in ash — the shape of him. Not his name. Names were what the crown ate. But the *shape* of him. The way he held his shoulders like he was apologizing for taking up space. The way his fingers twitched when numbers went by. The tremor he'd hidden for years, the ink moving on its own.

"Can you hear me?"

Nothing. More names. A queen's name. A tinker's name — no, wait, that one wasn't borrowed, that was *Wren*, he was saying Wren's name, saying it wrong, saying it like a stranger reading it off a list.

The crown's darkness shifted on the throne. Juniper felt it notice her, feel along her like a tongue. *There you are,* it said, without words, the way it always talked. *Little hearth. Little meal. Come closer.*

"Not yet," she whispered. "I'm busy."

And she did the only thing she could think of, the thing nobody had planned because nobody had imagined this exact shape of disaster.

She started talking.

Not the rite. She didn't know the rite. She talked the way she talked — fast, ragged, unlettered, all the words Paloma had tried to teach her spilling out crooked. She told him about the archive stacks and how the dust tasted like old bread. She told him about the plums they'd stolen on the salt road, how he'd pretended not to see her take the biggest one, how she'd split it anyway and given him half because that was the day she'd decided he was hers. She told him about Brother Cassian, who erased his name from temple reports for years just because he loved him, and about Wren carving the wooden bird by firelight with her one hand, slow, patient, shaving curls of wood that smelled like winter.

"You're Paloma Coldharbour," she said. "Debt-bound temple scribe. You balance impossible accounts. You hide the tremor in your hands. You think nobody notices you and everybody notices you. You gave me your coat in the marsh and lied that you weren't cold. You read true names and it costs you memories and you paid anyway, every time, because you'd rather lose pieces of yourself than let them take me."

His mouth kept moving. Borrowed names poured out.

"But here's the thing," she said, and her voice broke, and she let it break, because hiding things was his habit, not hers. "Here's the thing, Paloma. You don't have a name right now. Maybe you don't get it back tonight. Maybe the crown eats it all the way down and maybe tomorrow you wake up and don't know my face. But I'll know yours. Do you understand? Somebody has to remember. That's how it works. That's how it's always worked. Cassian remembered you when the temple wouldn't. Wren remembers her sister when the whole kingdom calls her a number. Somebody holds the name safe outside the thing that's eating it, and then it isn't gone, it's just — *kept.*"

She grabbed his face in both hands. Soot-streaked fingers on hollow cheeks. His eyes drifted, unfocused, past her shoulder.

"So I'm keeping you," she said. "Right now. Out loud. In front of the crown and the throne and whatever's left of King Aldous. I'm keeping Paloma Coldharbour. The man who bargained with Veyra and hated himself for it. The man who read the king's face and didn't run. The man who taught me letters and got angry when I wrote the F backwards and then showed me, patiently, eleven times, how to fix it. That man is real. I saw him. I'm the witness. And witnesses are worse than names, you stupid beautiful ledger-keeper, because names can be eaten and witnesses *can't shut up.*"

Something happened.

It was small. Later, Wren would swear she felt it through the pillar — a hitch in the hold, like a rope going taut. Juniper felt it in her hands, on his face: a warmth returning under the skin, faint as dawn through a shutter.

His eyes moved.

They didn't focus. They didn't find her. But they *moved*, tracking toward the sound of her voice the way a plant tracks light, blind, stubborn, alive.

"There you are," Juniper breathed. "Keep coming. Follow my voice. Don't think about why. Just follow it."

His mouth was still speaking. The borrowed names still poured. But underneath them now — under the flood, deep down, like a riverbed under a flooded field — she heard something else start up. A second voice, hoarse, barely there, saying the rite again. Getting the words wrong. Losing them. Finding them again.

Losing them again.

Finding them again each time because she was saying them first — no, not the rite, she was saying *plums*, she was saying *Cassian*, she was saying *you wrote the F backwards* — and somehow that was enough. Somehow the nonsense was the rope. He couldn't hold the rite by knowing it, because knowing was what the crown was eating. So he held it by holding *her*, by hooking the sacred words to a stolen plum and a backwards letter and a girl with soot on her hands shouting love across the gap where his self used to be.

"The crown's loosening!" Wren shouted from the pillar. "Juniper — whatever you're doing — *don't stop!*"

"I'm not stopping!" she shouted back, and then, softer, just for him, just for the man-shaped absence in front of her: "I will never stop. Ask anyone. I'm famous for it."

The darkness on the throne screamed.

It wasn't a sound. It was a pressure, a hunger hitting a wall, and Juniper felt it slam against her ember and slide off, because the ember wasn't afraid of hunger — the ember *was* hunger, once, and she had tamed it, and tame things know their own. The crown pulled harder at Paloma, hauling on the name it had almost swallowed whole, and found the name fraying in its grip like wet paper — because that was the trick of it, Juniper realized, the beautiful awful trick: you can't eat a man's name if the man has already given it away, and you can't keep a man empty if somebody else keeps filling him up.

Paloma's voice steadied.

Not all at once. It stuttered, caught, failed, caught again. But the borrowed names thinned. The flood receded. And the rite came up through him clean, word after word, and though his eyes stayed empty and his face stayed lost, his voice grew stronger with every line, anchored to a girl kneeling on cold stones telling him about plums.

The crown dimmed.

The black-gold ring of light guttered like a lamp out of oil. On the throne, the darkness thrashed, shrinking, folding in on itself, and beneath it — visible now, faint as a watermark — the outline of a man. Shoulders. A bowed head. Hands that had signed away a soul for bread.

"One more line," Wren called, voice cracking. "Juniper, he needs one more line and then it's the burning. Get ready. Get *ready.*"

Juniper leaned close to Paloma's ear. His lips moved through the final words of the unbinding, and she spoke against his cheek, quiet, the way you tell someone a secret you want them to keep forever:

"When this is done, you won't know me. I know that. I've known it since the night you memorized our faces instead of goodbye. So listen — listen with whatever's left, the part that followed my voice home. My name is Juniper. I'm the girl from the stacks. You hid me as inventory and it cost you everything and it was worth it. And when you wake up — whenever you wake up, however you wake up, even if you wake up as nobody at all — I'm going to be there. Because somebody kept your name safe. Me. I kept it. Right here."

She pressed his hand flat against her sternum, over the ember, over the place where she'd hidden the wooden bird.

"And I'm giving it back."

The last word of the rite left his mouth.

The crown went dark.

For one heartbeat the throne room was silent as the inside of a sealed letter — and then the darkness on the throne tore open like a seam, and something surfaced through it, blinking, bewildered, wearing a dead king's face.

And the remnant of Aldous looked straight at Juniper — past her, through her, at the hollow-eyed scribe still standing in the center of the floor — and opened his mouth to speak.

* * *

What came out was not a scream and not a curse. It was small. That was the shock of it — after all that hunger, all those centuries of appetite, what surfaced from the wreck of the crown was a voice like a tired man waking from a nap he'd meant to be short.

"Bread," the remnant said. "Is the bread — did they get the bread—"

"It's done," Wren said from the pillar, weeping, holding, her carved hand white-knuckled on the stone. "Aldous. Your people ate. Your people lived. It's finished now."

The remnant's gaze wandered the throne room like a man looking for his hat. It passed over Juniper. It passed over the dark throne. It settled, at last, on Paloma — on the empty-handed scribe who had spoken the unbinding and could not remember his own mother's face.

"You gave a name," Aldous whispered. "Freely. All of it." Something moved through the ruined face that might have been grief, or envy, or both wearing the same coat. "I only gave mine halfway. I kept a little back. That's why it hurt so long." His eyes found Juniper again. "Girl. What did you give him? When he was falling? I felt it. From inside. I felt something pull him back, and in four hundred years nothing has ever pulled anything back out of me."

Juniper's throat was tight. "Plums," she said. "And a backwards F."

The remnant of the king laughed. It was a dreadful, wonderful sound, rust and bells. "Then it's true," he said. "It was always true, and I never believed it. Love holds the place. Love holds the—" His voice snagged. His body sagged against the throne, and the last of the crown's darkness drained out of him like water out of a broken jar, and Juniper saw clearly, for one bare minute, the man he had been: young, exhausted, in love with a country that needed him to be a monster so it could go on being fed.

"Tell Isolde," he said. "The bread was real. Tell her the bread was real."

And the minute ended, and the remnant went with it, gently, like a lamp carried out of a room — and the crown sat on the cushion of the throne, black gold, silent, an empty mouth at last.

Juniper didn't move. She stayed kneeling beside Paloma, who stood in the center of the floor with his hands open and his eyes vacant and his mouth finally, mercifully still.

"Paloma?" she said. No answer. "Scribe?" Nothing. She took his cold hand. "Hey. Ledger-keeper. It's over. We won. You can come back now."

His head turned toward her voice — that blind, stubborn tracking, plant-toward-light — and he frowned, slow, like a man surfacing from deep water trying to remember which way the shore was.

"I know you," he said at last. The words came out rough, unpracticed, like a language he'd spoken as a child. "Don't I? I know you. I don't know how I know you. I don't know my own—" His breath caught. Panic flickered in the vacancy, the first real feeling she'd seen in him since the rite began. "I don't know my name. Why don't I know my name?"

"Because you gave it to save everyone," Juniper said, "and it worked, and now I'm giving it back."

She drew out the wooden bird.

Wren's carving, small enough to fit in a child's fist, smooth from handling, and inside it — banked, patient, glowing through the grain like a lantern through a shutter — the ember waited, and folded inside the ember, wrapped in warmth the way a letter is wrapped in wax, was everything she had caught of him on the night he memorized their faces instead of goodbye. Every memory she'd witnessed. Every story she'd shouted at the crown. The plums. The coat in the marsh. The F written backwards, eleven times, until it came out right.

She pressed the bird into his empty hands and closed his fingers around it.

"Here," she said. "You kept us in your head. So we kept you in ours. Take it back, Paloma Coldharbour. Take all of it. Take *home.*"

The bird began to glow.
