## 37. Rewriting the Ledger

The ledgers of the Guild of Impossible Trades were kept in a vault beneath the counting-house, and it was there, three days after the unbinding, that Paloma discovered he had become a blank page.

He had come down with Wren's key — she'd pressed it into his hand without explanation, the way she did everything, as if generosity were a transaction too obvious to discuss. The vault smelled of tallow smoke and old vellum, of forty years of debts recorded in a hand that had once been his own. He knew the shelves by touch. He knew which ledger held the salt-road bribes, which held Veyra's private accounts, which held the thin, terrible column of names sold to the crown's catchers.

What he did not know — what kept catching him like a stair that wasn't there — was who had written them.

He stood in the doorway a long time, one hand on the cold iron frame, waiting for the panic to arrive. It didn't. That was the strangest part. In the throne room, when the name had gone out of him like heat from an opened window, there had been terror — a falling sensation, a reaching for something no longer attached to him. But three days of quiet had done their work. He was not empty, he realized. He was *unwritten*. There was a difference, and the difference was everything.

An unwritten page could be written on.

He took down the master ledger — the great one, bound in green leather gone soft as cloth, its spine cracked from decades of opening. It fell open to the current page out of habit, the way a dog comes to a whistle. And there, at the top, in the entry line for the day of the unbinding, the ink had already begun to move.

Not writing itself, this time. Not the way it had in the old days, before Cassian taught him to hold still, before he learned that ink remembered what hands tried to hide. This was gentler. The letters gathered at the nib-end of nothing, faint as breath on glass, waiting.

*Name:*

It wanted his name. Of course it did. Every ledger entry began with a name; every debt required a debtor. The Guild's whole architecture rested on the belief that a person could be fixed to a page, pinned like a moth, made legible, made collectable. His whole life had rested on the same belief. Brother Cassian had found him at seven years old, already entered into the temple rolls under a name assigned by clerks who needed a column filled, and Paloma had spent thirty years believing that the name was the man — that to lose it was to dissolve.

But he had dissolved, and here he stood. Still standing. Still able to grieve Cassian, whose funeral smoke had gone up yesterday morning. Still able to feel the specific weight of Juniper asleep against his shoulder on the walk back from the pyre, her breath smelling of woodsmoke and stolen plums. A name, it turned out, was not the man. A name was only the first draft.

The ink waited.

Paloma sat down at the vault's single desk, lit the lamp, and did what he had done ten thousand times before: he considered the terms.

* * *

They had given him names, over the past three days, the way people leave food outside a shy animal's door.

Wren had offered "Thimble," delivered deadpan while she carved a new bird from scrap pine — she'd started a fresh one the morning after the rite, saying the ember's old house deserved a sibling. "Thimble," she said, "because you're small, useful, and always getting lost in the lining." He had protested that he was not small. She had looked at him for a long moment and said nothing, which from Wren was a full paragraph.

Juniper had suggested "Ember-friend," then "Ash-man," then, with the fierce seriousness she brought to naming things — she had named every rat in the undercroft, every beam in the chapel, every star she could see through the smoke-hole — "Paloma." Just that. "You can keep it," she'd said. "Names aren't owned. They're *carried*. Like the bird." She didn't understand yet, quite, what it meant that the temple had owned his, that the debt-bond had made even his name collateral, callable, forfeit. To her a name was a gift you gave because you loved someone, not a lien.

Isolde, in the brief formal hour she'd granted them before returning to the city to face the council, had addressed him as "Scribe" with such deliberate respect that it felt like an apology for a kingdom.

And Veyra — Veyra had sent a note. Of course she had. It sat now on the desk beside the lamp, sealed with the Guild's scales-and-key, and Paloma broke it open with a thumb.

*To the scribe formerly entered as Paloma Coldharbour,* it began, and he almost smiled. Even her mercy came with bookkeeping.

*The Council of Reckoning convenes in nine days. Your testimony regarding the sale of names will be required, or not, as you choose. The Guild's ledgers are yours to close or keep, per our arrangement. I have struck your debt-bond from the temple rolls myself. Consider it the last interest payment on a debt I should never have held.*

*One matter remains. The final page of the master ledger requires a signature. Unsigned accounts do not close. They merely wait.*

*— V.*

Unsigned accounts do not close. They merely wait. He turned the phrase over like a coin of uncertain mint. It was true — he had seen it in the archives a hundred times, estates left in limbo for generations because some ancestor's hand had failed at the last line. An account unsigned was an account still hungry. The Guild itself was that: a vast unsigned account, all its cruelties still accruing, waiting for someone to pick up the pen and either settle them or let them run forever.

He looked at the moving ink. *Name:* it whispered, patient as the crown had been patient, but without the hunger. Ink only wanted what you gave it. That had always been the difference between ink and crowns.

Here was the problem, and he turned it slowly, savoring the turning: whatever name he wrote would be true. That was the nature of his gift, the gift he had hidden since childhood, the gift that had cost him a memory at the appraiser's desk and a name in the throne room. When he wrote a thing, the thing became what he wrote. He had falsified Juniper's entry once and the lie had cost him the taste of his mother's bread — a memory he still could not find, though he knew the shape of the hole where it had been. So he could not write carelessly. Whatever he set down about himself, he would *be*.

And the temple's name — Paloma Coldharbour, dove of the cold harbour, assigned by clerks, bonded to an abbey, mortgaged against thirty years of copying — that name was ash now. Struck from the rolls. He felt around the edges of it the way you probe a missing tooth and found nothing, no pull, no claim. If he wrote it, it would simply be a word he chose, free of the debt for the first time. But it would still be their word. The clerks' word. A name designed to fit a column.

Wren's voice, in memory: *you're small, useful, and always getting lost in the lining.* Juniper's: *names are carried, like the bird.*

He thought about what a name was for. Not identification — any mark would serve that. A name was a handle by which love could find you. That was all it had ever been, in the end. Cassian calling him across the novitiate garden. Juniper shouting it from rooftops during the escape from the undercroft, ragged with fear. Wren saying it quietly over the wooden bird, the night they'd thought he might not come back from speaking the unbinding. The name had mattered because of who was doing the calling.

So let the name come from the callers.

* * *

He climbed out of the vault near dusk and found them where he knew he would: the chapel kitchen, which was not a kitchen so much as a hearth with ambitions, where Wren was teaching Juniper to solder and Isolde's abdication draft lay abandoned on the table while the queen herself, sleeves rolled, attempted bread.

"You're doing it wrong," Juniper said. "You have to *want* it to rise."

"Bread does not respond to intent, Juniper."

"Everything responds to intent. You just have to be loud inside."

Isolde caught sight of him and straightened, flour to her elbows, and for a moment the queen and the baker occupied the same body without contradiction. "Scribe. Good. We kept you a bowl."

They had kept him a bowl. Something in his chest went loose and warm at that, at the plain domestic fact of it: a bowl, kept, for a man with no name, by people who had decided he was theirs anyway.

"I'm going to write myself tonight," he said. No preamble. With these three, preamble was wasted breath. "In the master ledger. The closing signature. And I want—" he hesitated, and found the hesitation itself instructive; the old Paloma would have bargained alone, drafted alone, presented a finished document for approval. "I want you to give me the name. All of you. Whatever you call me when you're not thinking. That's the name I'll sign."

Silence. Then Wren set down her soldering iron. "You understand what you're asking. Whatever we say, you'll *be*. Permanently."

"I know the terms."

"Then I withdraw Thimble." A pause, perfectly weighted. "Mostly."

"It has to be honest," Juniper said, with the gravity of a magistrate. "Not a joke-name. Jokes are for middle names."

"Are they?"

"Everything has layers. Ask the onion."

Isolde wiped her hands and regarded him for a long moment, and Paloma watched the queen recede and the woman surface — the woman who had fed prisoners to a crown to buy time for reforms, who had chosen duty until duty curdled, who was now, page by page, learning to choose otherwise. "When my husband bound himself," she said quietly, "he did it alone. In the dark, with lawyers. He believed sacrifice must be solitary to count. I have come to believe that is the single worst idea this kingdom ever produced." She looked at the others, then back. "You are asking us to do together what Aldous did alone. Yes. I'll give you a name."

They gave it to him that evening, by lamplight, each in turn, and none of them knew what the others had said.

Wren went first, gruff, not looking up from her carving: "Keeper. Because you kept her alive, and you kept the list of the taken safe, and somebody in this circus has to keep things." She blew a curl of pine off the blade. "Also it annoys Veyra, which is a bonus."

Juniper went second, solemn as an oath: "Emberkin. Because you're not fire, but you live next door to it, and you never once got burned." She frowned. "Or maybe that's two names. Can you have two? I have four. Five if you count Rat-Queen."

Isolde went last, and hers was the longest coming. She stood at the window a while, watching the city bells — silent now, truly silent, for the first time in years, the Feast of Bells having become simply bells again, rung by hand, for joy. "My husband's name meant *noble counsel*," she said finally. "It was a title wearing a name's clothes. So I would give you the opposite. I would call you *Plainword* — the man who says the true thing simply, when everyone else is managing. It is a rarer gift than reading true names, and I have watched you do it since the day you walked into my seclusion and told me my bargain was murder." A small, tired smile. "Plainword. If you'll have it."

Three names. Keeper, Emberkin, Plainword. He turned them over, tested their weight, and found — this was the miracle, the small domestic miracle he would remember longer than the throne room — that they were not in conflict. They were facets. A keeper of things, kin to fire, plain of speech. A man his friends could find in the dark by any of three handles.

He thanked them. He ate the bread — risen, barely, defiantly — and he went down alone into the vault.

* * *

The lamp burned low. The master ledger lay open. The ink still waited at the head of the final entry, and Paloma — Keeper, Emberkin, Plainword — picked up the pen.

He did not write quickly. He was, whatever else had changed, still a scribe, and a scribe's last entry deserves care. He wrote the date. He wrote the account's summary in brief, unflinching lines: the sales of names, the interest taken on children, the appraisal of the crown, the betrayal and the settling of it, Veyra's reckoning rendered in a single sentence — *the Guild's mistress was shown her own first page, and chose to begin again.* He wrote the disposition of assets: the archive to the regency council, open to any gifted soul who wished to read it; the vault funds to reparations, per the council's decree; the building itself—

He paused at the building. Then he wrote: *to be a school.* Juniper would need somewhere with good light and thick walls, and the look on Veyra's face when she read it would be worth the postage.

Last, the signature line. The cursor of ink pulsed there, eager, and he held it still with an effort of will that once would have left him shaking and now felt like breathing.

*Name of closer:* the ledger prompted.

He wrote: *Keeper Emberkin Plainword, called by his friends, formerly entered as Paloma Coldharbour, debt discharged, bond void, self-authored.*

The ink drank it in. And Paloma felt it settle onto him — not like a collar, not like a chain, but like a coat cut to fit, warm at the shoulders. Three names, freely given, freely taken. He tested each one silently. *Keeper*, and he felt the list of the taken in his breast pocket, the names Wren had carried twenty years, now his to carry forward. *Emberkin*, and somewhere above him the hearth-glow in the wooden bird flickered, acknowledging a neighbor. *Plainword*, and he understood suddenly that he would never again be able to lie comfortably, which was, he suspected, exactly what Isolde had intended.

Then, because an account unsigned waits forever, he signed.

The pen moved. The ink dried. And the master ledger of the Guild of Impossible Trades closed with a sound like a long-held breath released — a soft sigh of vellum settling, forty years of impossible trades balanced at last, not forgiven but *finished*, every debt converted into its lesson and shelved.

He sat back. The lamp guttered. And in the quiet he heard, distantly, from the chapel above, Juniper's voice raised in argument with a rat, Wren's knife whispering through pine, Isolde laughing — actually laughing — at something. His family, keeping themselves, while he kept the books.

He blew out the lamp and climbed toward the light.

At the top of the stairs, Wren was waiting, leaning against the doorframe with the fresh-carved bird in her palm — unpainted, unfinished, its wings half-open. She held it out.

"For the mantel," she said. "Next to the other one. A pair keeps better than a single." Then, squinting at him with the expression she used for measuring timber: "Well, Keeper. How does it fit?"

"Like it was made for me," he said. "Which, technically—"

"Don't ruin it."

He was still smiling when the knock came at the chapel door — three sharp raps, official, the kind that preceded proclamations. Through the window they saw a rider in council colors, dismounting, a scroll in his fist, and behind him on the road, dust rising from more riders than one messenger requires.

Juniper reached the door first. She read the seal through the wood — Paloma saw her do it, the ember-sense flaring gold in her eyes — and her face went strange.

"It's from the north," she said. "There's another crown."
