## 38. Coronation of Ash

The crown had not been worn in a year, and Isolde intended that it never would be again.

She kept it in a locked case now, on a table in the small audience chamber where Aldous had once taken his breakfast. Not the great hall — she refused to give it the great hall. The case was plain glass, the table plain oak, and the crown sat inside like something dead that hadn't finished deciding to be dead. Some mornings it seemed heavier than gold had any right to be. Some mornings she thought she could hear it breathing.

She did not look at it as she dressed.

Her maid — no, her *dresser*, the title mattered less than the woman's name, Sera, who had held her position through three decades of court intrigue by the simple method of being uninteresting to everyone — laced the back of a grey gown. Grey. Isolde had chosen it deliberately. There would be those who read mourning into it, and they would be wrong, and they would be right, and both readings would serve.

"You'll want the pearls," Sera said.

"I will want nothing that glitters."

Sera, wisely, said nothing else.

* * *

The Hall of Records had been chosen over the throne room for exactly one reason: the throne room still smelled of ash. No amount of scrubbing had lifted it. The undercroft beneath had been sealed, catalogued, and consecrated against return, but the stone above remembered, and so did the people. When the regency council convened, it would convene among paper and ink and the long shelves where the kingdom kept its accounts — because if this new thing was to be anything, Isolde had decided, it would be an accounting.

She arrived early. She wanted to see the room before it filled.

Wren was already there.

The tinker stood at the head table with her single hand flat on the surface, reading the grain of the wood the way other people read documents. She'd dressed for the occasion, which meant she had brushed the sawdust off her coat and put on a shirt with buttons. Her wooden bird — no, the bird was Juniper's now; Wren had carved a second, smaller one, which hung at her throat on a leather cord — caught the light from the high windows.

"You're staring," Wren said without turning.

"I am observing a member of my council arriving before I do."

"Then observe this." She turned. Her face was thinner than it had been on the salt road, harder in some places and softer in others, the way faces got when they'd been to the undercroft and come back up the stairs. "There are forty-one names on the list I carried as a catcher. Thirty-three of them were in that hole under this palace. Nine came out alive. I've spent every morning since writing letters to the families of the twenty-four, and every afternoon being told by clerks that reparations require *forms*."

Isolde held her gaze. "And what do you intend to do about it?"

"Burn the forms, probably. Figuratively." A pause. "Mostly figuratively."

"Wren." Isolde crossed the floor until they stood close enough that the tinker had to tilt her chin up. "I did not put you on this council to be polite. I put you on this council because you are the only person in this kingdom who has been on both sides of the hunt — the list and the listed — and because you do not forgive easily. If you begin forgiving me today, I will know this council has failed."

Something moved behind Wren's eyes — not warmth, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of one debt-holder meeting another.

"Then don't expect forgiveness," Wren said. "Expect arithmetic. Every name, every coin, every year. I'll be counting."

"Good," said Isolde. "So will I."

* * *

Veyra arrived last, which was itself a performance, and Isolde permitted it because understanding Veyra meant understanding that everything she did was a performance, including sincerity, including remorse, including — on rare and expensive occasions — the truth.

The Guild mistress wore merchant black trimmed in silver, and she paused in the doorway just long enough to let the room notice her, then swept down the aisle between the record-shelves as though she owned them. In a sense, she had tried to. Three separate times over the past year, Guild agents had attempted to purchase the Hall of Records' western annex. Isolde had denied all three petitions personally, with increasingly terse letters.

"Majesty." Veyra bowed precisely the correct depth. "Councilor. Tinker."

"Guildmistress." Isolde gestured to the chair at the council table — third from the left, deliberately neither beside nor across from her own. "You received the summons."

"I did. I confess I read it twice. *Reparations* is not a word the Guild uses. We prefer *settlements*. Reparations implies fault."

"It implies more than fault. It implies repair." Isolde sat. "Sit down, Veyra."

Veyra sat. And here was the thing Isolde had learned watching Paloma confront her in the Guildhall a year ago — the thing that had convinced her this woman belonged in the room despite everything, or perhaps because of everything: Veyra sat like someone listening. Whatever masks she wore, beneath them was a mind that took account of everything, and a mind that takes account can be made to balance.

Paloma had shown her the girl she'd been before the Guild. Isolde had never asked him what name he'd spoken aloud that day. Some ledgers were not hers to open.

"The council is called to order," she said. "First item: the abdication."

* * *

It took four hours.

Not the abdication itself — the document was three sentences long and Isolde had written it herself, in her own hand, in ink she had mixed that morning from ash and gum arabic, because symbolism was a currency even cheaper than gold and she intended to spend freely. The four hours went to the argument.

The Lord Chancellor, a soft man named Pellinore who had served the thing wearing Aldous's face for eleven years without ever quite noticing it had no soul, objected first to the word *abdication*.

"Your Majesty cannot simply—"

"I can. Watch me." She slid the document across the table. "I married into this crown. I never held it by blood, and after what it fed on, I will not hold it by consent either. Read the second sentence aloud, Chancellor, since you seem to have trouble with it."

Pellinore read, his voice thinning as he went: "*I, Isolde, Queen of Coldharbour, do renounce all claim and title, and do hereby transfer the authority of the crown to a Regency Council of Nine, to govern until such time as the people of this kingdom choose their own form of rule.*"

"'Choose their own form of rule,'" repeated the Chancellor. "That is — Majesty, that is anarchy."

"That is a *question*," Isolde said. "Anarchy is what happens when you refuse to ask it for two hundred years. Ask it now, while there is still a kingdom left to answer."

It was Wren who broke the deadlock, in the way Wren broke most things: directly, and slightly off-topic. "The Chancellor's worried about who holds power after you step down. Fine. Here's who. Nine seats. Three elected from the city wards, three from the provinces, three appointed — one gifted, one Guild, one—" she glanced at Isolde, "—one who remembers how the old machine worked, so we know which gears to strip out. That's the council. That's the succession. Nobody's born into anything ever again."

"And when the provinces refuse to send representatives?" Pellinore asked.

"Then we go ask them in person," said Veyra, "and the Guild's caravans happen to be traveling to every province twice a year already. It would be a shame if civic duty couldn't ride along at standard freight rates." She smiled her merchant's smile. "Consider it a settlement."

Isolde watched the Chancellor do the arithmetic — the Guild's reach, the tinker's ferocity, the queen's patience — and arrive, reluctantly, at surrender. It was the same arithmetic she'd done herself, months ago, alone in the small audience chamber with the caged crown breathing at her elbow. Power did not dissolve. It only changed hands, and the only question worth asking was whether the next hands had ever been burned by holding it.

These hands had. All nine of them, by the time the list was final.

* * *

They broke at midday, and Isolde walked the colonnade alone, as she did every day, past the windows that looked down on the square where the Feast of Bells had become the Feast of Ash, and then the Riot of Bells, and then — after the crown fell silent and the king's body finally, mercifully stopped — simply the Square, which the people had begun calling Liberation Square in the way people name things: without asking permission.

Below, workmen were raising a monument. It had been commissioned by public subscription, which meant it was ugly, which meant it was honest. A column of dark stone, and on it, the thirty-three names from Wren's ledger, cut deep enough to run a finger along. Twenty-four of them marked with a small carved flame. The workmen had argued about the flames — some said grief, some said honor — and Wren had settled it in one sentence: *They burned. Let the stone say so.*

Isolde stopped at the window and let herself feel it, the way she let herself feel it once a day, briefly, on schedule, because grief that is scheduled does not leak.

Aldous.

Forty years ago — no, forty-one; she had counted — a young king had stood in a famine-winter and made a trade that bought a nation its bread. She had found his letters in the spring, in Cassian's old cell at the temple, wrapped in oilcloth against damp and time. *If the crown must eat,* he had written, in a hand that shook only at the end, *let it eat me slowly rather than my people quickly.* He had thought he was buying a bounded thing. He had not known it would learn to reach.

She had loved him. That was the part no chronicler would write correctly. They would write that she was complicit — and she was; she had signed the warrants, she had told herself each condemned prisoner was a year purchased for reform, and reform had needed decades and gotten one — but beneath the complicity, uncomplicated by any excuse, was love, and love was why she had stayed, and staying was the crime. She understood that now. Love that makes you silent is not love. It is collateral.

The bells rang noon. She counted them out of habit — twelve, and the thirteenth half-strike that the bell-founders had never fixed because the flaw gave Coldharbour's voice its character — and turned back toward the hall.

* * *

The afternoon session was the hard one.

"The Hunt," Isolde said, when the council reassembled, "is formally ended. I want the ending to have teeth. Chancellor, read the draft."

Pellinore read. The Edict of Unbinding: the Crown's warrant for the seizure of gifted persons revoked in perpetuity; all registry lists destroyed publicly; every person taken under the hunt to be located, released if living, named if dead; and to each survivor and each bereaved family, restitution — land where land was wanted, coin where coin, and for those whose gifts had been drained in the undercroft, access to the temple healers and the Guild's own appraisers, free of charge, in perpetuity.

"In perpetuity," Veyra murmured. "Majesty, do you know what the Guild charges for appraisal?"

"I know exactly what the Guild charges. I have read your rate cards since I was nineteen. Which is why the edict says *free of charge* — and why the crown will reimburse the Guild at fair market value for every appraisal performed, audited quarterly by the council." She folded her hands. "You will not profit from the wounded, Veyra. But you will not lose money healing them, either. I am buying your cooperation, not your charity. Charity from the Guild would frighten me."

For a moment — a real moment, one of the ones Paloma must have seen under all the lacquer — Veyra's smile faltered into something almost like respect.

"Quarterly audits," she said. "You've been talking to the scribe."

"I talk to everyone. It's the only intelligence gathering that works."

The vote passed eight to one. The one dissent was Pellinore, who objected to the cost, and whose objection Isolde entered into the record verbatim, because she wanted history to see exactly what mercy had cost and exactly who had begrudged it.

Then came the part she had saved for last, because it required her to stand.

* * *

"The registries," she said, "are in the vault beneath this building. Eleven years of lists. Names collected by catchers, by informers, by neighbors — neighbors, gentlemen; the hunt ran on neighbors — compiled by the Office of Appraisal and countersigned by me."

No one spoke. Wren's hand had gone to the little wooden bird at her throat.

"I could order them burned privately. Quietly. It would be cleaner." Isolde met each set of eyes around the table. "Instead, in one week, on the anniversary of the unbinding, the vault will be opened and the lists carried to Liberation Square, page by page, and burned in a public pyre — and before each page burns, a reader will stand and speak the names on it aloud, so that the last thing these lists ever do is give testimony instead of taking it."

"Who reads?" Wren asked quietly.

"Whoever comes. Any citizen may take a page." Isolde's voice held, mostly. "I will take the first. My own signatures. I will read them myself, all of them, however long it takes, and then I will feed them to the fire with these hands, and the kingdom can watch its queen burn her own complicity in front of God and the wards."

Silence, of the kind that is not empty but full — the silence of a room recalculating.

It was broken, as it had been broken all day, by Wren, who stood, crossed to the window, and looked down at the column of names.

"My sister's on page one," she said. "Of the master list. First name taken under the first warrant. I'll read that page." She turned around, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "And then I'm going to carve her name into that monument myself, with my own chisel, because the stonemasons keep making the serifs too pretty."

"Noted for the record," said the clerk, in a voice that wobbled.

* * *

They adjourned at dusk. Isolde remained.

Alone in the Hall of Records, with the lamps lit low and the shelves rising around her like patient witnesses, she unlocked the drawer in the council table and took out the abdication document one more time. Three sentences. Forty-one years compressed into them, and a famine, and a bargain, and a slow horror, and a girl with an ember in a wooden bird, and a nameless scribe who had taught her the difference between a signature and a self.

Tomorrow she would sign it. Tomorrow the title would leave her like a tooth leaving the jaw — a strange lightness, a tongue returning to a gap.

But tonight, one last piece of business, and it was not on the agenda.

She drew a fresh sheet toward her, dipped her pen, and wrote:

*To the scribe called formerly Paloma, now called whatever his friends call him —*

*I am told you have taken a new name and that you will not tell anyone what it is until it is written in a book of stories rather than a book of law. I respect this more than I can say, and I envy it entirely.*

*Two requests, then, from a woman who is about to become no one official:*

*First — when you write the story of this year, write Aldous whole. Write the famine and the choice and the man, not only the mouth. He was my husband and he was a good king for exactly as long as it took the crown to teach him otherwise, and someone must remember the difference.*

*Second — the council has voted funds for a school. Not a temple school. A house, actually, above a rebuilt chapel on Bellwether Lane, though I suspect you already know the address. Juniper will teach. Wren has promised benches. The Guild has, through channels I chose not to examine, donated the roof.*

*What it lacks is a keeper of accounts. Someone to make the impossible trades balance.*

*The position pays nothing. The interest, I am told, is paid in family.*

*Come sign your name in my book, whatever it is, and I will sign mine out of yours.*

She signed it — *Isolde, Queen of Coldharbour, for one more day* — blotted it, and sealed it with plain wax, no crest, because tomorrow there would be no crest to press.

As she set the seal, the lamp guttered, and in the glass case across the room — she saw it plainly, and did not flinch — the crown shifted, very slightly, in its cage.

Not breathing anymore. Settling. The way old houses settle. The way debts settle when they're finally, truly paid.

Or — and this was the thought she carried out of the hall and down the dark colonnade and into the last night of her reign — the way a mouth settles when it has learned that somewhere in this city, a small warm ember sits tame on a mantelpiece, and a child teaches children, and a nameless man is learning to write.

Patience, the crown had always said, was its oldest virtue.

Isolde blew out the lamp, and went to find out what it was waiting for.
