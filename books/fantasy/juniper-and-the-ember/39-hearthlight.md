## 39. Hearthlight

The house above the chapel had been a granary once, then a smuggler's loft, then nothing at all for eleven years — and now it was home, which was the strangest entry in any ledger Paloma had ever kept.

He wrote that thought down because it was true, and because he had learned in the last year that true things deserved ink even when they were small. Especially when they were small. The Guild of Impossible Trades had taught him that everything was measured, weighed, appraised, priced. It had taken an unbinding, a dead king's last breath, and a girl with a hearth in her chest to teach him the opposite lesson: that some things were only worth what they were because no one could price them.

His new name sat at the top of the page in his own careful hand. *Ember Thackeray* — no. He smiled and crossed it out. Wren had suggested it as a joke, months ago, over plum wine: "Call yourself Ember, since you two are attached at the soul." Juniper had thrown a crust of bread at her.

He settled instead on what the others had given him on the night the ledgers closed. They had sat around this very table, all five of them plus a queen who insisted she was no longer one, and Isolde had said, "You spent your whole life writing other people down. Let us write you." And they had passed the pen around like a communion cup, each adding a word, arguing, laughing, until the name was done:

*Aldous Hearthwright.*

Cassian's name in it, though Cassian would never see it. The king's name in it too, redeemed from the thing that had worn him. *Hearthwright* because Wren said a man should be named for what he builds, and Paloma built stories now, and warm ones.

He looked at the name a long moment, the way he still did every morning, checking that it held. It always held. Names given in love, Juniper said, were load-bearing.

Below it he wrote:

*One year since the Unbinding.*

*Item: the chapel roof, repaired (Wren grumbling about every slate).*
*Item: six gifted children resident, fed, taught, and impossible.*
*Item: one tinker's bird, carved of ash wood, glowing gently on the mantel, requiring neither feeding nor fear.*
*Item: one scribe, formerly debt-bound, currently happy, which took some getting used to.*

He underlined the last item twice.

Downstairs, the day was already assembling itself with the particular chaos of a household that contained Juniper Ash — she had chosen her own surname, naturally, and refused all suggestions with the imperiousness of someone who had once nearly become a vessel of primordial fire and found the experience instructive.

"Paloma!" her voice rang up through the floorboards. "Paloma, the Kettlewick boy is doing it again!"

"Doing what again?"

"The *thing!*"

Which told him nothing, but told him everything. He set down the pen and went down to see.

* * *

The schoolroom had been the chapel nave, back when the chapel had a congregation. Now the pews served as desks, the altar was a shelf for slates and ink pots, and the stained glass — half-shattered by a catcher's torch during the bad year — had been replaced with plain panes that turned the morning light into something honest and unadorned.

Six children sat in a crooked semicircle. Tam Kettlewick, eight years old, stood at the front with his eyes squeezed shut and his fists clenched, and above his open palm, a spoon was bending itself into a shape like a wilting lily.

"It won't stop," Tam said through his teeth. "Miss Juniper, it won't—"

"Breathe," Juniper said. She crouched to his level, and Paloma paused in the doorway to watch her work, because he never quite got tired of watching it. "You're not bending the spoon. You're asking it. There's a difference. The first way breaks things. The second way makes friends."

"I don't want a friend that's a spoon."

"You want a friend that's breakfast." She tapped his clenched fist gently. "Open your hand. Gifts aren't cages, Tam. They're doors. You get to choose which ones you walk through."

Tam opened his hand. The spoon straightened, wobbled, and then — deliberately, almost smugly — curled itself into a neat hook and hung there.

Juniper laughed, bright and unguarded, the laugh of a girl who had spent her first ten years feral in the archive stacks and had decided, somewhere along the way, that joy was also a door worth walking through. "There. Now hang your coat on it and sit down."

Paloma slipped in and took his place by the window, where he always sat during lessons — not teaching, exactly, but listening, and occasionally writing down the things the children said, because children with gifts said astonishing things when they forgot an adult was present.

Mira, nine, could hear the names of lost objects calling from wherever they'd been mislaid; she spent most lessons with her head cocked like a bird's, cataloguing the chapel's buried history of dropped buttons. Dell, the eldest at twelve, grew small green things from his fingertips when he slept, so that moss crept across his slate by afternoon and had to be peeled off before arithmetic. The twins, Sorrel and Sage, moved heat between their palms like passing a secret. And little Oskar, six, whose gift nobody had identified yet except that candles leaned toward him the way they had once leaned toward Juniper, watched everything with the solemn patience of a cat deciding whether to trust a room.

"Today," Juniper announced, climbing onto the altar shelf to sit cross-legged, "we're going to talk about the ember."

Every child went still. Even after a year, the story of the Unbinding was the story they asked for at bedtime, the way other children asked for dragons.

"But we talked about it Tuesday," Mira protested.

"We're going to talk about the part I never tell right." Juniper's face did something complicated — pride and grief sharing a chair. "Everyone always asks how we beat the crown. Nobody asks what happened after. What it's like to carry something old and hungry and teach it manners." She held out her hand toward the mantel, where the wooden bird sat in its place of honor, its grain faintly luminous, like sunlight trapped in honey. "Come here, then."

The bird lifted from the mantel and drifted across the room, unhurried, and settled in her palm. The candle flames bowed toward it — all of them, gently, the way flowers turn — and the children made the small collective sound they always made, half gasp, half sigh.

"This used to be a mouth," Juniper said. "It ate kings. It ate a whole kingdom's worth of gifted souls, and it was still hungry, because that's what hunger does when you feed it instead of answering it." She stroked the bird's carved wing with one finger. "Now it's a hearth. Do you know the difference?"

Oskar raised his hand, solemn. "A mouth takes. A hearth gives."

"A hearth gives," Juniper agreed. "And here's the part I never say right: it didn't change because we beat it. It changed because somebody *asked* it." Her eyes found Paloma's across the room, and held them. "Somebody offered it a choice instead of a war. That's the whole lesson. That's the only lesson. Everything else is details."

She sent the bird back to the mantel and clapped her hands. "Arithmetic. Yes, groan, get it out of your systems. Moss off the slates, Dell, honestly, it's like teaching in a bog."

Paloma opened his notebook against his knee and wrote, in the margin of a page otherwise full of figures: *Somebody offered it a choice instead of a war.* Then, beneath it: *Remember this on the hard days.*

Because there were still hard days. That was the thing the ballads got wrong, and there were ballads now — three of them, one of which was criminally inaccurate about the salt road and another of which gave Wren two hands, which she found funnier than any joke she'd ever told herself. The ballads ended at the throne room door. They didn't show the nights afterward when Paloma woke reaching for a name he no longer owned, or the mornings when Juniper stared at the bird and wondered if she'd caged something that had only pretended to be tame, or the way Wren sometimes went silent mid-sentence, her missing hand aching around a sister twenty years gone.

Home wasn't the absence of hard days. Home was having somewhere to put them down.

* * *

At midday, Wren arrived from the market with a handcart of salvaged bell-bronze and a scowl of tremendous satisfaction.

"Regency council's finally releasing the old cathedral bells," she announced, dumping a verdigrised lump on the workbench that served as the kitchen table between meals. "Melting them down for the reparations fund. Official decree and everything. Isolde signed it herself." She grinned at Paloma. "Our queen. Still can't say it without snorting."

"She's not our queen anymore," Paloma said. "That's rather the point."

"She's our Isolde, then, which is worse, because now I have to be polite to her at dinner." Wren wiped her hands on her apron — the one with the pocket sewn where a left hand used to be, a joke she'd made with her own needle and defended fiercely — and surveyed the room with the proprietary eye of a woman who had rebuilt half the chapel roof herself. "Where is His Former Majesty?"

"Garden. Arguing with the roses."

"Good. Keeps her humble."

Isolde had taken the ground-floor room that faced east, and she had filled it with seed catalogs and soil-stained gloves and absolutely nothing regal, which was, everyone understood without saying, the entire point. She abdicated formally within the month after the Unbinding — the regency council had been her own design, drafted in her own hand, with Wren seated on it over the objections of every hereditary peer in Coldharbour and Veyra seated beside her over Wren's objections, which Isolde had called "a healthy tension" and Wren had called "an ulcer."

The hunt for the gifted was ended. The reparations were flowing — slow as winter syrup, but flowing — to the families of the taken, each payment accompanied by a copy of the Catcher's Ledger, restored, corrected, and read aloud in the great hall every quarter so that no name on it would ever again be a number. Wren attended every reading. She stood at the back with her cap in her hand and her chin up, and when they reached her sister's name — *Lark, of the Marshside Wrens, taken in the year of the Gray Frost* — she never wept, and everyone in the hall knew better than to look at her, and somehow that was its own kind of tenderness.

Veyra, for her part, had proven the year's strangest harvest. Freed by mercy she had never budgeted for, the Guild mistress had done what transactional creatures do when the transaction changes: she had renegotiated. The Guild of Impossible Trades now traded exclusively in possible ones — apprenticeships for the gifted, restitution brokerage, the quiet buying-back of debts that predators had written in blood. She ran it with the same ruthless precision she'd once used to sell children to catchers, and if her ledgers were cleaner now, well. Paloma had audited them personally. Twice. She'd let him, and charged him nothing, and that frightened him more than her old cruelty ever had.

"People don't change," he'd said to Isolde once, watching Veyra across a council dinner.

"No," Isolde had agreed. "But they can be shown a different price list."

* * *

Evening came early in autumn, and the household gathered the way it always did — without arrangement, by gravity. Bread and stew and plum wine (Wren's orchard had finally forgiven her). The bird on the mantel glowed its low honey light, and the flames didn't bow to it anymore; they simply burned alongside it, colleagues now, comfortable.

"So," Wren said, feet on the bench, carving knife moving through a fresh block of ash wood. She carved a bird every season now — none of them vessels, just birds, gifts for the children, for strangers, for the mantel. "Council meeting Thursday. They want to formalize the school. Charter it. Make it official."

Juniper looked up sharply. "Official how?"

"Crown-chartered. Inspectors. Curriculum standards." Wren's knife paused. "Their hearts are in the right place. Mostly."

"And their ledgers?" Paloma asked quietly.

"That's the question, isn't it." Wren resumed carving. Shavings curled to the table. "Charter means protection. Funding. It also means a register. Names of the gifted, written down. In a crown archive. Again."

The silence at the table had a familiar texture. Every one of them had lived inside that silence before.

"They'd never—" Dell started.

"They said that last time too," Wren said. Not cruelly. Just truly, which was her way. "Not the same people. Not the same crown. But paper has a long memory, and archives burn forward as well as back."

Juniper set down her spoon. "Then we don't charter."

"Then we lose the funding, and the funding feeds six kids."

"Then we charter and lie," Sorrel said.

"We don't lie to children," Isolde said, from the doorway, where she had appeared with garden dirt still on her knuckles and the expression of a woman who had heard the last three minutes through the wall and come anyway. "I spent fifteen years lying to a kingdom to protect it. I can tell you precisely what it costs." She came to the table and sat, and looked around at them — her strange family, her penance and her reward. "But I'll tell you what I know about institutions. They bend. Slowly, badly, with terrible noise — but they bend toward whatever weight is consistently applied. We bent the crown once. We can bend a charter."

"How?" Paloma asked.

Isolde smiled, and it was the old queen's smile, but aimed kindly for once. "We write the terms ourselves. Before they do. Tomorrow, we draft a charter with a clause no inspector can stomach and no child can fear: *no names recorded, ever, in any archive, under penalty of the rite.*" She glanced at the bird on the mantel. "Let them try to argue with the people who unbound a king."

Wren laughed — a real one, deep in the chest. "Threatening the government with folklore. You've been in the garden too long."

"I've been in the garden exactly long enough," Isolde said, "to learn that things grow where you plant them, not where you're told."

Later, when the children were asleep and the dishes were done, Paloma climbed back up to his desk and found his morning page waiting. He added the evening beneath the morning's items:

*Item: a charter to be drafted, terms ours, names unwritten.*
*Item: the work is not finished. The work is never finished. This is, I am told, what living is.*

He sat with the pen hovering, and then, because it was the anniversary eve and because some entries deserve making, he wrote the last line slowly, the way you speak to someone across a great distance:

*Brother Cassian — the children ask about you. I tell them you were the first person who chose me before I could pay for it. I tell them that erasing my name from the temple reports was the bravest act of accounting in the history of the kingdom. I tell them your silence was the loudest love I ever heard. The house is warm. The ledgers are closed. Rest.*

He blotted the page. Downstairs, faintly, he heard Juniper's door open and close, her footsteps crossing to the mantel, the small ritual he knew by heart: her whisper to the bird, goodnight, old hungry thing, sleep soft. The glow in the parlor dimmed by a fraction, contented, like an ember settling into ash for the night.

Paloma blew out his candle and, in the dark, felt the truth of the year settle over him the way snow settles on a roof — heavy, but holding.

Home, earned. Home, kept. Home, tomorrow, to be defended again — starting with a charter, a clause, and a pen.

He was already asleep when, downstairs, the wooden bird flickered — once, twice — bright as a struck match, and dimmed again, as if something very old and very patient had dreamed, briefly, of being needed.
