## 40. Chapter 40

The house above the rebuilt chapel had a leak in the roof that no one ever fixed properly.

Paloma had come to think of it as a feature rather than a flaw. On nights when rain came in off the harbor, it fell in three places: into the copper pan Wren had wedged between the rafters over Juniper's room, into the clay pot on the landing that someone — he suspected Isolde, though she'd deny it under oath — had planted with mint, and onto the corner of his own desk, which was why his ledgers lived in a crate and his stories lived in his head until they were ready to be written down all at once, fast, before the damp could get ideas.

Tonight the rain was doing its work. Drip, drip, drip against the copper pan, a sound like a small patient clock. Paloma sat at his desk with a candle burning low and a blank page in front of him, and for the first time in longer than he could measure — longer than his name had been his own, longer than the temple, longer than the debt — the page did not frighten him.

He was writing the last line of a story about a scribe who could read the true names of things.

It was not, strictly speaking, true. The scribe in the story was braver than Paloma had been, and funnier, and he lost fewer memories along the way. But Wren said that was what stories were for — the mending of things that couldn't be mended any other way — and Wren had one hand and forty years of hard miles behind her, so Paloma had learned to take her word on matters of repair.

He wrote: *And when they asked the scribe what his name was, he told them the truth.*

He stopped. Read it back. It wasn't right yet. It was close, the way a key is close to fitting a lock, but there was a burr on it somewhere, something that would catch if you turned it too fast.

The candle flame leaned sideways. Not toward the window draft — toward the door.

Paloma smiled without looking up. He'd learned to read the candle the way other people read weather. When Juniper came down the hall, fire bent toward her like wheat toward sun, even now, even with the ember tamed and sleeping in its wooden bird upstairs. She carried warmth the way some people carried debt: everywhere she went, everything near her felt a little lighter.

"You're still awake," she said from the doorway. She was eleven now by nobody's best guess, tall as a fence post and growing like spring, her hair cut short since summer because she'd decided long hair was "a thing people can grab." Soot still found her no matter how clean she started the day. Some children attracted mud; Juniper attracted ash. Paloma had stopped fighting it.

"So are you," he said.

"I heard you writing." She came in and sat on the floor beside the desk, cross-legged, the way she sat everywhere, as if chairs were a formality other people needed. "You do this thing with your breath when you're stuck. You hold it. Like the words might hear you."

"Do I?"

"Like a thief holding still in a pantry." She picked at a splinter in the floorboard. "Is it the last line?"

"It's the last line."

"Read it to me?"

Paloma looked at the page. Then at her. In the first weeks, back when she'd been a feral shape in the archive stacks with candle flames bowing around her like courtiers, she had never asked him for anything. Asking was weakness; asking got you caught. He remembered the night that changed — stolen plums on the salt road, juice running down their chins, her laughing for the first time with her whole body — and he remembered thinking: *there she is.* Whoever she'd been before the streets, before the ember, before the running. There she was, waiting under all that soot.

Now she asked for things freely. Bedtime stories. Second helpings. To be read to. Every request was a small miracle he never got used to.

"Not yet," he said. "It's not finished. Listen—" and he read her the whole final passage instead, the unbinding rewritten as fable, the crown become a mouth become a door become, at the end, simply a circle of light on a mantel. Juniper listened with her chin on her knees, and when he finished she was quiet for a while, in the way of people who are deciding whether to say the true thing or the easy thing.

"The bird glowed last night," she said finally. "While I was asleep. I woke up and it was bright as a coal and I could feel it — not hungry. *Listening.* Like it was dreaming about us."

"Dreaming about us how?"

"About everyone. Wren's sister. Cassian." Her voice went careful around Cassian's name; it always did. They had buried him under the chapel's new east wall, in ground that had been salt marsh and was now garden, because Wren had declared that Cassian would have hated a fancy plot and loved being useful. Things grew well there. "About the king, maybe. About Aldous. All the people it ate, I mean. It keeps them somehow. Not — not *them*, exactly. More like the shape of them. The warm part."

Paloma set down his pen. This was the conversation they'd been circling for months, the one that arrived every few weeks like the tide, and each time it came a little further up the shore.

"Juniper—"

"I'm not going to do anything stupid." She held up both hands. "I know what you'll say. I know what we all agreed. The ember stays in the bird, the bird stays on the mantel, nobody feeds it souls, everybody goes home happy. That's the story." She scowled at the floor. "But it's growing, Paloma. Slowly. A little more light every month. And I'm the only one who can feel what it wants, and what it wants isn't to be fed. It wants to be *given*. There's a difference. Hunger takes. Giving is chosen. That's the whole lesson, isn't it? That's what the rite was."

The rain filled the pause, patient on the copper pan.

"Tell me what you're actually afraid of," Paloma said. It was a trick he'd learned from her, oddly enough — cutting straight through to the soft place, because feral children have no patience for circling.

Juniper pulled her knees tighter. "That someday it'll be big enough that keeping it small will hurt someone. That I'll have to choose again. And that next time I won't be quick enough to find the third option." She looked up. "You found yours. You rewrote your name out of love instead of law. I want to believe there's always a third option. But believing it and knowing it aren't the same thing."

"No," Paloma said. "They're not."

Downstairs, faintly, the front door opened and closed. Footsteps — two sets, one heavy and deliberate, one lighter with a hitch in it, the gait of a woman who'd spent years learning to balance what was missing. Voices murmured. Then Wren's laugh, low and rough, the laugh she only made when Veyra said something outrageous, which meant Veyra was here, which meant—

"Veyra's here," Juniper said, at the same moment Paloma realized it. "At this hour? She never comes at this hour unless—"

"—unless something's wrong," Paloma finished.

They were both moving before the sentence ended, Juniper snatching the candle, Paloma marking his page with a ribbon out of pure habit even though the world could be ending. Old scribe reflexes died hard.

* * *

Veyra stood in the kitchen in a traveling cloak still beaded with rain, and she looked — Paloma searched for the word and settled on it reluctantly — *unarmored.* Guild mistress Veyra, who had sold names to catchers and smuggled children out of cities and once, memorably, let herself be defeated by the sound of her own childhood spoken aloud, stood in their kitchen with her hood down and her hands visible, the old negotiator's habit of showing she held nothing.

Wren leaned against the counter beside her, arms crossed, one sleeve pinned and empty. Isolde sat at the table with a half-mended shirt in her lap that she had clearly abandoned mid-stitch. The former queen mended clothes the way other people prayed.

"There's tea," Isolde offered, in the voice she used for defusing things.

"Later, perhaps." Veyra's eyes found Paloma. "I'll be brief, because brief is kinder. Three days ago, a man came to the Guild asking after a scribe who could read true names. Not a catcher. Not crown. He paid in old coin — pre-Aldous coin, coin that hasn't circulated since the famine years. And he left this." She set something on the table.

A ledger. Small, travel-worn, its cover cracked leather gone soft as cloth. Paloma knew that binding. He knew that binding the way you know your own handwriting.

"That's a temple ledger," he said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "Novitiate issue. We all kept them."

"It's marked with your novitiate sigil." Veyra turned it around and slid it across the wood. "And it's full."

Paloma didn't touch it. Not yet. Around it the kitchen seemed to contract, the lamplight pulling inward, the rain on the roof suddenly very loud. Juniper had gone still beside him in the way she went still when the ember stirred — but the wooden bird was upstairs on the mantel, and whatever had stilled her now was older than the ember. It was the stillness of a person hearing their own name called from a great distance.

"What's in it?" Wren asked quietly.

"Entries," Veyra said. "In Brother Cassian's hand. Dates going back twenty years. And on every page, the same entry repeated, over and over, in slightly different words." She recited it from memory, flat and precise, the way she delivered prices: "*Erased one name from the report. The boy shows the gift. Let the record say otherwise. Let the record say nothing. Let him be free.*"

Nobody spoke. Isolde's hands had stopped on the torn shirt. Outside, the rain kept its time against the copper pan, drip, drip, drip, like a heart deciding.

"He erased me," Paloma said. "From everything. For twenty years."

"He erased you from *their* records," Veyra corrected gently. "This book is what he kept instead. Every lie he told on your behalf, written down so he wouldn't lose count of his sins. He was a meticulous man. It's why you two understood each other." She paused. "There's more. The last entry is dated four days before he died. And it isn't in his hand."

Paloma touched the ledger then. The leather was cold. He opened it to the final page, and there it was — the last entry, in a hand he almost recognized, a hand that trembled the way his own had once trembled when ink moved on its own:

*If anyone finds this: the name he gave away was never the temple's to keep. It is written here, in the margin, where no law can reach it. I am sorry I could not give it back sooner. I was a coward for most of a life, and then, briefly, I was not.*

And beneath it, in the margin, in faded brown ink that had waited twenty years for an eye that could truly read it:

*a name.*

Not *Paloma Coldharbour* — that name had unraveled in the throne room, spent like a coin, given freely to the rite. Not the name the temple had bound him with, either. Something else. Something older, from before the debt, before the orphanage, before the first ledger — a name he had not known he possessed, because he had been taken young, and the ones who took him had renamed him like inventory.

Cassian had found it. Cassian had dug through the oldest records, the pre-temple registers, the parish books of a village that no longer existed, and he had found the name of a boy who became an orphan who became a scribe who became a debt — and he had hidden it in a margin, where no law could reach it, and he had died before he could give it back.

Paloma read it. His gift rose in him the way it always did now, quiet and warm, less a power than a sense, like hearing — and the name on the page resolved itself, became real, became *his*, in a way that made his chest ache like a healed bone remembering the break.

"Oh," he said. Just that. Just *oh.*

"Papa?" Juniper's hand found his. She'd started calling him that eight months ago, casually, catastrophically, in the middle of breakfast, and the word had rearranged his entire life. "What is it?"

"It's my name," Paloma said. "My first one. The one I had before anyone owned me." He laughed, and it came out wet. "Cassian found it. Before he died. He's been carrying it all this time."

Wren swore softly, reverently, the way she swore at beautiful joinery. Isolde pressed her stitched shirt to her mouth. Even Veyra looked away, giving the moment privacy the way she gave everything privacy: transactionally, but completely.

"And the man who brought it?" Paloma asked, when he could speak. "Old coin, asking after a name-reader. Who was he?"

"That's the second thing." Veyra's composure returned, but underneath it Paloma's gift caught something new — fear, banked and controlled, the fear of a woman who had bet her life on worse odds and knew exactly what these odds looked like. "He wouldn't give a name. He wore a traveler's cloak and gloves, indoors, in summer. But when I told him the scribe he sought was dead — that's the official record, you understand, the nameless scribe of the Unbinding, may he rest — he smiled. And he said, *'Then tell whoever holds his ledger that the crown was only the first mouth. There are others. Older. And one of them has woken up hungry.'*" She met Paloma's eyes. "Then he paid for his ale with a coin from the famine years, and by the time my porter reached the street, there was no one there. Just wet footprints. Going toward the harbor."

The kitchen was silent. Above them, faint through the ceiling, the wooden bird on the mantel gave off a glow that none of them could see and all of them, somehow, felt — warmth spreading through the joists like sunlight through water.

Juniper's grip tightened on Paloma's hand. "It knows," she whispered. "The ember. It heard. It's — it's scared."

Paloma looked down at the open ledger, at the name in the margin that was his and had always been his, at the last gift of a cowardly, courageous man who had spent twenty years committing small mercies in ink. Then he looked at his family — the tinker, the queen, the guildmistress, the girl with embers in her blood — gathered in a leaky kitchen at midnight because the world, it appeared, intended to keep requiring them.

"Well," Paloma said, and closed the ledger, and felt the name settle into him like a bone setting right. "I suppose we'd better find out what else is hungry."

Outside, far off across the dark harbor, a bell began to toll — though no bell in Coldharbour rang at this hour, and the sound of it was old, and deep, and patient, and glad.
