## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

There are three rules to keeping accounts at the Gilded Root, and I break two of them before breakfast.

Rule one: never count by candlelight. The flame makes the numbers swim, and swimming numbers in this trade have a way of becoming true. Rule two: never read an entry aloud. Rule three—the one I actually keep—is that the ledger is always right, even when it's lying.

I sat in the counting room with a candle burning anyway, because the morning fog off the canal had come up through the floor grates like it paid rent, and because rules are for people whose ledgers don't whisper.

The counting room sits at the heart of the guildhouse, a long stone belly of a room with shelves rising three stories on every side. Not books—vessels. Jars of bottled grief ranked by vintage. Corked storms from the Sundering Coast, still sulking behind their glass. Borrowed luck in thimble-sized vials, each one labeled with the name of the fool who'd sold it and the date it was due back. Somewhere on the third shelf of the east wall, if you knew where to look, there was a jar of somebody's last good summer, and somewhere darker, a drawer of things the guild didn't sell so much as *keep*.

My desk sat in the middle of it all like an island nobody visited on purpose. Ledger open. Quill trimmed. Ink the color of a bruise. Around me, the guild slept off its earnings, and I did what I do best: I made the impossible add up.

"Bottled grief," I murmured, running my finger down the column. "Twelve jars out, nine returned, three outstanding." The grief-trade was steady work. People came in weeping, paid half their savings, and walked out lighter—which is to say emptier—and the guild corked what they left behind and resold it to poets. Everyone won except everyone.

I made my marks. Totaled the column. Checked it twice, because the ledger punishes confidence.

Then I moved to luck.

Borrowed luck is trickier arithmetic. Luck isn't a thing you sell; it's a thing you *lend*, and lending means interest, and interest means the ledger has opinions about when it comes home. I traced the entries with my quill tip. *Marrowick, borrowed seven years' fortune against his daughter's wedding. Due: winter. Ossory, borrowed one lucky day, repaid in full, plus a hangover he didn't know he'd agreed to.* The guild's contracts were famous for fine print. I wrote some of that fine print myself. I was good at it. That was the trouble with being good at something rotten—you got asked to do it again.

And underneath all of it, quiet as a coin dropped in a well, I heard the debts.

Not metaphorically. Not the way any accountant hears debt, which is to say constantly, in the voice of their own anxiety. I heard them the way you hear a roomful of people talking through a wall—murmur, murmur, murmur—except the wall was everything. Every contract in this room spoke. Every jar hummed its terms. Every vial of borrowed luck ticked down its days like a clock with a grudge. When Marrowick's daughter married, I would hear it. When Ossory's hangover bloomed, I'd hear that too, faint and smug.

Nobody at the Gilded Root knew this about me. Nobody could. The guild traded in gifts the way other merchants traded in silk—if they found one growing wild on the premises, they didn't marvel at it. They clipped it, potted it, and put it to work. Corvane, our guild master, had a saying about it: *Everything in this house is inventory.* He'd said it looking at me once, smiling, and I'd spent six years pretending I hadn't noticed.

So I kept my gift folded up small, like a knife in a boot, and I let it whisper to me anyway, because a ledger-keeper who can hear which debts are about to go bad is worth her weight in gold, and I liked being worth my weight in gold. It bought the room I lived in. It bought Juniper's—

It bought nothing for Juniper. Juniper had been gone since we were children. Thirteen years, give or take a fever and a flood and whatever else the world had used to lose her. I kept her name out of my working thoughts the way you keep a cracked tooth away from your tongue. Mostly.

The candle guttered. I turned the page.

* * *

The soul-ledger lives in a locked drawer inside a locked cabinet inside a locked room, and I had keys to all three, which tells you either how much Corvane trusted me or how little he thought anyone would dare. Souls are the Gilded Root's rarest stock. We didn't sell them whole—that was vulgar, and besides, whole souls fetch poor prices compared to pieces. A memory here. A first love there. An entire childhood, if the seller was desperate enough and the buyer particular.

The entries were written in Corvane's own hand, which meant the ink was black and the margins were bare and the cruelty was all in the arithmetic.

I worked down the page with my quill hovering, not touching, because even I had a superstition or two left. *One soul, portioned: courage, sold to a general. One soul, portioned: the ability to sleep, sold to a woman who'd wronged too many people to rest.* Standard stock. Grim, tidy, profitable.

Then the quill stopped moving, because my ears did what they do.

Every entry in the soul-ledger whispered. They couldn't help it—a debt spoken into a contract doesn't ever quite stop speaking. Usually the voices were strangers': flat, transactional, tired. *Sold. Sold. Sold.* Like rain on a roof.

This one said, clear as a bell over the harbor:

*"August."*

I sat very still. The candle leaned. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard settled, and the house went back to breathing.

I told myself it was the fog. Fog does things to sound in the counting room; the jars amplify, the stone carries, and a body alone among ten thousand whispering debts will start hearing names in the static. Sailors hear their mothers in the wind. Accountants hear ghosts in the columns. It's a known condition. There's probably a jar for it somewhere in this building.

I looked at the entry anyway.

The hand was Corvane's. The date was thirteen moons past. The item line read, in his neat, bloodless script: *One soul, entire. Seller: consigned. Buyer: private party, below.*

*Below* meant under the mountain. Everyone at the Gilded Root knew what *below* meant, and everyone agreed, in the wordless way of people employed by something frightening, never to wonder about it aloud. The court beneath the mountain was where bad contracts went to be honored forever. The moon above it counted things. That was all I knew, and knowing even that much felt like holding a lit match in a paper house.

*Consigned,* though. Consigned meant the seller hadn't come in weeping and desperate. Consigned meant someone had brought the soul *in*—the way you'd bring in a bolt of cloth, a crate of storm, a jar of somebody's last good summer.

Consigned meant inventory.

I read the entry again, slower, letting my gift lean against it the way you lean your ear against a door. The whisper came again, and this time it wasn't just my name. It was humming. Off-key, stubborn, the tune she used to hum when she thought I was asleep—some market song about a fisherman and a foolish wife, four notes and then a drop, four notes and then a drop.

Juniper hummed like that. Had hummed like that. Thirteen years ago, before the fever season took the low quarter and took her with it—or so the story went, the story I'd been handed at eleven years old by people who were very sorry and very busy and very much in possession of a ledger I hadn't been allowed to read.

The candle flame stood perfectly straight, as if it, too, was listening.

"Say it again," I whispered to the page.

The page said nothing. Pages are cowards when you want them to talk. But the whisper was still there underneath, patient as interest accruing, and now that I'd heard it I couldn't unhear it, the way you can't unhear your own name across a crowded room. Four notes and then a drop. My sister's soul, entire, consigned thirteen moons ago to the court beneath the mountain, and it had been sitting in this drawer, humming to itself, for—how long? How many nights had I sat at this desk with my boots on the cabinet, close enough to touch the lock?

Thirteen moons. I did the arithmetic without wanting to. The moon overhead was fat and nearly round, one turn from completing its cycle—one turn from whatever *completion* meant in a contract signed below, where moons were counted on bone calendars by pale hands that never hurried, because nothing below ever ended, it only finished.

I closed the ledger. I opened it again. The entry hadn't changed. Entries don't. That's rule three, and rule three has never once been wrong: the ledger is always right, even when it's lying. And it wasn't lying now. That was the horror of it. For once, the thing was telling the truth in plain ink, and the truth was that the Gilded Root—the guild that fed me, housed me, taught me my letters and my numbers and my fine print—had sold my sister's soul while I balanced its books and thanked it for the privilege.

I am, by profession, a calm woman. Calm is a tool like any other; I keep mine sharpened. I closed the ledger, squared it on the desk, aligned the quill beside it, and blew out the candle, and my hands did not shake, because shaking is for people who have time for it.

Then I sat in the dark with ten thousand whispering debts and listened to one of them hum.

Four notes and then a drop.

* * *

Corvane found me there at midmorning, which he had a talent for—arriving exactly when a person had just decided something and not yet decided what. He filled the doorway the way he filled most doorways, silver-haired and silver-tongued, wearing kindness like a coat cut slightly too well.

"August." My name in his mouth always sounded like an appraisal. "You've the look of a woman who's found an error."

"No errors." I kept my voice level, my hands folded on the closed ledger. "Balanced everything but the soul-page. Thought I'd leave that for daylight."

"The soul-page balances itself." He came into the room, trailing the smell of clove oil and money. His eyes went to the drawer, then to me, then back, quick as a pickpocket. "You slept here again."

"I slept *enough* here."

"Mm." He touched the cabinet—not opening it, just resting his fingers on the wood, proprietary, the way a man touches a horse he's already sold. "The moon turns soon. End of the month. Big settlements coming due, and the crown's making noise, as crowns do. I'll need you sharp."

"I'm always sharp."

"You're always *tired*," he corrected gently, "and you've started talking to the inventory." He smiled to show it was a joke. It was not a joke. Nothing Corvane said was ever entirely a joke; that was practically the guild motto. "Go home. Sleep in a bed. Come back tomorrow and be brilliant."

He left the way tide leaves—slowly, taking something with him that you only notice later.

I stayed until my shift ended, because leaving immediately would have been a confession, and then I walked home through streets gone strange with early dusk, and the whole way the city whispered its debts at me. The baker owed his landlord and his late wife an apology. The lamplighter owed a debt to a man with iron teeth. The river owed the sea, the sea owed the sky, the whole world was a ledger with no honest clerk, and threaded through all of it, faint and off-key and getting no farther away no matter how fast I walked:

Four notes and then a drop.

At my door, I stopped. Someone had chalked a mark on the frame—a circle with a slash through it, fresh, the chalk still bright. I stared at it for a long moment, my breath fogging, my gift straining after it the way a dog strains at a lead.

I didn't know the mark. But I knew what kind of thing gets marked on doors in the night, in a city where the crown has started asking questions about gifts, and none of those kinds of things end with a knock and a friendly chat.

I went inside. I barred the door. I sat down at my little table with the soul-entry copied out from memory onto scrap paper—thirteen moons, consigned, buyer below—and I looked at my own handwriting until the candle burned low.

One turn of the moon. That's what the contract had left. One turn, and whatever *permanent* means below the mountain, it would mean it about Juniper.

I had spent six years being the Gilded Root's best clerk. I knew exactly how much my competence was worth, to the copper.

Tomorrow, I decided, I would find out what it cost.

The mark on my door, meanwhile, could wait until morning. Some debts you choose. Some get chalked onto your frame while you sleep. Either way, the moon was turning, and it wasn't waiting for me.
