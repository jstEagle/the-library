## 2. Thirteen Moons

I did not sleep. This is not a boast. Sleep is a skill I have never mastered, and the counting room at three in the morning is where insomniacs like me go to be useful about it.

The Gilded Root keeps its ledgers in a vault below the counting room, behind a door that requires three keys and a drop of blood from someone the guild trusts. The blood-lock is the guild's little joke. Trust, they say, is the only currency we never trade in. Ha ha. Very droll. Give me back my sister.

I'm getting ahead of myself.

The entry had whispered to me all evening while I balanced the rest of the book—bottled grief, two units, consigned by a widow in Salt Row; borrowed luck, returned slightly used; a storm in a jar that kept rattling its shelf like a dog wanting out. Ordinary inventory. Impossible things are only impossible until you've invoiced them, after which they're just stock. That's the first lesson Corvane ever taught me: *sentiment is a leak in the ledger, August. Find it and plug it.*

I was plugging leaks when the whisper came again, threading up through the floorboards of the vault like smoke through a keyhole.

*Thirteen moons,* it said. *One turn remaining.*

And beneath it, faint as a name called across water, a voice I knew.

I want to tell you I went straight down to the vault. I didn't. I sat with my pen hovering over the widow's grief for a full quarter hour, telling myself the voice was tiredness, or the wind, or one of the bottled sorrows leaking its contents into the air. The Root's cellar is full of things that murmur. You learn to ignore them the way you learn to ignore your own heartbeat—mostly, until something goes wrong.

But the voice said my name. Not August. *Gus.* Nobody has called me Gus since I was nine years old.

* * *

The vault took my blood without comment. It always does. There's something insulting about a lock that accepts you so easily, like a lover who's stopped checking whether it's really you at the door.

The soul-ledgers live on the lowest shelf, wrapped in oilcloth because paper souls sweat. Souls, it turns out, are the simplest entries in the whole guild's books. No haggling over condition, no complaints about vintage. A soul is a soul. Debit: one. Credit: whatever the buyer paid. The ledger doesn't record what the buyer wanted it *for*. That's not our business, Corvane says. Our business is the price.

There were eleven entries under the current moon-cycle. I read them by candlelight, and my gift—the thing I keep folded up small inside me like a knife wrapped in a stocking—read them louder.

You should understand how the hearing works, since you'll be stuck with me a while. Most people see a debt as ink on vellum. I hear it. Every obligation ever written into a Gilded Root ledger speaks, if you have ears for it, and mine have been open since before I knew what they were hearing. Debts sound different depending on their weight. A borrowed year sounds like a coin dropped on wood. A bottled grief sounds like crying through a wall. And a soul—

A soul sounds like a person deciding, very quietly, to stop being a person.

Eleven whispers. I made myself listen to each one. A ferryman who'd sold his passage home. A child too young to know what her parents had signed—I skipped past that one fast, because there are limits to what I can do at three in the morning and stay functional. An old man's soul sold against a debt of six coppers and a chicken, which tells you everything you need to know about interest rates at the Gilded Root.

Then the twelfth line. Thirteenth moon. One turn remaining.

*Gus,* it said. *It's cold down here.*

Juniper.

* * *

Let me tell you about Juniper Montrose, because the ledger won't, and someone should.

She was born fourteen months before me, which she maintained made her the elder and therefore in charge, an argument she won for eleven years through sheer refusal to lose it. She had a gap in her teeth she could whistle through in two notes, and she could name every bird in the city by its shadow alone, and she once convinced an entire street of neighbors that the moon had been repossessed and would be visible again once payments resumed. People believed her. People always believed Juniper. She had that kind of face, that kind of voice—a voice like a hand on your shoulder saying *of course it will be all right*, and then, infuriatingly, it would be.

She disappeared when I was nine. The official story—the story I was given, the story I swallowed because children swallow what they're fed—was plague fever. Bramble the hedge-witch sat with her for three nights, and on the fourth morning there was no fever and no Juniper, and Corvane told me the guild had done everything, and he put his hand on my head the way he did when he wanted me to stop asking questions, and I stopped asking questions.

I was nine. Forgive me. I was nine, and the man who fed me put his hand on my head, and I mistook the weight of it for comfort instead of a lid.

For twenty years I have carried her the way you carry a stone in your boot—not crippling, just present, just enough to change how you walk. I lit candles for her at Vesper's shrine every month on the dark of the moon. I wrote her name in the margins of ledgers where no one would look. I built a small, load-bearing grief and hung my whole life on it, and I told myself that was devotion.

It was not devotion. It was ignorance wearing devotion's coat. Because here is what the ledger told me at three in the morning, in ink that has been dry for thirteen years:

*Sold: one soul, female, age 11. Buyer: the Court Beneath the Mountain. Seller: the Gilded Root, per standing arrangement. Term: thirteen moons of the seller's choosing, renewable at completion of cycle. Consideration: waived. Consideration: waived. Consideration: waived.*

Waived. Three times. Which meant the guild gave her away. Sold her for nothing—or rather, sold her for something so valuable it couldn't be written down, and my gift, my cursed honest ears, heard what was written between the lines, because debts always speak their true shape even when the ink lies:

*A favor owed upward. A silence bought. A girl traded for a guild's continued permission to exist.*

They hadn't sold her soul to pay a debt. They'd sold her soul to buy their own safety. My sister was not a payment. She was a bribe.

* * *

I don't remember leaving the vault. I remember being in the stairwell with my hands shaking so badly I had to press them flat against the wall, and the wall was cold, and I thought, absurdly, *she said it's cold down here, she can feel the cold, souls in lanterns can feel the cold, what kind of god lets that be true—*

Vesper. Yes. I'll get to him. He and I have an arrangement too, of sorts: he went silent when I was young, and I stopped speaking to him, and we have maintained this mutual silence with the discipline of two people who both know exactly whose fault it is and have agreed never to say so. But old habits die hard, and in the stairwell I caught myself praying. Not words. Just the shape of a prayer, the way you reach for a handrail that isn't there.

*Tell me I misread it.*

He said nothing. He is very good at that. Twenty years of practice.

* * *

Here is the arithmetic, because arithmetic is what I have instead of tears, most days.

The court beneath the mountain buys souls the way merchants buy wine—for laying down, for maturing, for drinking later at some occasion we aren't invited to. But souls spoil slowly, and the courts are patient, and so the contracts run on moon-cycles. Thirteen moons is the standard term. During those thirteen moons, the sale is provisional. The soul sits in its lantern, aging, but the contract remains—what's the word—*reversible*. After the thirteenth turn completes, the sale settles. Permanent. Irrevocable. The soul stops belonging to anyone at all, including itself, and becomes simply part of the court's holdings, like a chair.

Juniper's entry said thirteen moons. It also said, in smaller script, the phrase that turned my stomach from dread into something with edges: *term renewed annually at seller's option.*

Annually. For thirteen years. Every year, on the same night, someone at the Gilded Root walked to this shelf, opened this book, and chose to leave my sister in the dark one more time. It wasn't one decision. It was thirteen. Thirteen separate mornings when some clerk—you, August, it was always going to be you eventually—could have read the line and asked a question, and thirteen times the guild chose not to.

And now the moon hangs one turn from full cycle. I checked the almanac twice, as if the moon might have changed its mind since the first check. Nine nights. In nine nights the sale settles, and Juniper Montrose stops being a girl in a lantern and becomes furniture in the dark.

Nine nights to find a way to un-sell a soul from a court that does not issue refunds.

* * *

Dawn came gray through the counting-room shutters, and with it came Tam, the junior clerk, yawning, carrying the morning's post. He's a good boy, Tam. Eager. Writes a fair hand. Believes everything Corvane tells him, which is either innocence or survival, and in this building the two look identical.

"Late night, miss?" he asked, eyeing the candle stubs.

"Inventory," I said. "The cellar needed a full count."

"Anything amiss?"

Everything. Everything is amiss, Tam. The foundation of my entire life is amiss. "A discrepancy," I said. "In the soul-ledger."

His face did something quick and complicated—fear, mostly, quickly filed away—and he said, "Those're Master Corvane's books, miss. Best left to him."

"I balance every book in this guild," I said, and heard my own voice come out flat and strange. "That's my work."

"Yes, miss." He set down the post and fled, and I noted, distantly, that I had frightened him, and noted, more distantly still, that I didn't care, and understood that something in me had come loose overnight—some beam that had been holding up a wall, quietly cracked through, waiting twenty years for exactly this weight.

Corvane found me an hour later. Of course he did. The man has a sense for trouble the way sharks have a sense for blood, except Corvane prefers his blood diluted, aged, served at room temperature with paperwork.

"You were in the vault," he said. Not a question. He stood in the doorway of my office with the morning light behind him, so his face was in shadow, which I used to think was accident and now recognize as technique.

"Full count," I said. "As the books require."

"Mm." He let the silence do some work. It's his favorite employee; never asks for wages. "August. These are delicate times. With the crown's proclamations coming, I need to know my people are steady. Are you steady?"

Steady. I thought of Juniper at eleven years old, signing nothing, agreeing to nothing, a consideration waived three times over. I thought of thirteen annual renewals. I thought of the hand on my head, the lid on the questions, the stone in my boot.

"I'm steady," I said.

Because here is the thing about betrayal: it works best when nobody knows it's happened. Corvane thought he was looking at his reliable ledger-keeper, the orphan he raised, the instrument he'd tuned. And I looked back at him with the same face I've worn for twenty years, and inside that face I was doing arithmetic.

Nine nights. One soul. One guild that had better hope I never learn the full shape of what it owes.

"One more thing," Corvane said, turning to go. "The soul-ledgers. I'll be handling those personally from now on. No need to trouble yourself with them again."

"No need," I agreed pleasantly.

He smiled, satisfied, and left, and I sat in the gray light with my hands flat on the desk, listening to the ledger whisper up through the floorboards one last time before I sealed my ears against it.

*Gus,* it said. *Are you coming?*

"Yes," I told the empty room. "Hold on. I'm coming."

Nine nights. And the first thing I needed was to know exactly what I was up against—which meant going back to the beginning, to the shrine of the god I stopped talking to, where all of this, I suspect, actually started.
