## 3. The Crown's Proclamation

The criers came at dawn, which was rude of them. Dawn is a time for coffee and lies of omission, not for the crown to start shouting about who it intends to disappear.

I heard them from my window above the counting room—three voices, spaced along Copperlane like bells with grudges.

"By order of Her Radiant Majesty! A hunt is declared for the gifted, the marked, and the debt-touched! Let all citizens give ear: those who harbor such persons harbor treason! Those who conceal such persons share their chains!"

Chains. Not even a euphemism. The crown had stopped pretending this was anything but what it was, and there was something almost refreshing in that. The Gilded Root could learn a lesson from such honesty. We'd have charged extra for it.

Below me, Copperlane woke up wrong. You could hear it—the particular quality of silence that falls over a street when everyone on it is suddenly doing arithmetic about their neighbors. Who talks in their sleep. Whose garden grows too fast. Whose child never cries, whose bread never burns, whose luck runs suspiciously sweet. I stood at the glass with my coffee going cold and listened to a whole street decide, quietly and all at once, that trust was an expense they could no longer afford.

And then, because my gift is a curse with excellent hearing, I listened to the debts.

They came up through the floorboards the way they always did—not sound exactly, more like the memory of sound, spoken in the voice of whoever owed them. *I owe Mrs. Halloway two silver for the eggs.* *I owe the crown my taxes and I owe my brother an apology eleven years old.* *I owe God a candle I promised when my daughter had the fever.* Ordinary debts, small and human, the kind that make a city hold together like stitching. But underneath them, new threads, taut and ugly:

*I owe the crier a copper if I report old Tam and his glowing hands.*

*I owe my neighbor nothing, and thank every saint for it, because her boy marks doors.*

That last one made my stomach drop through the floor and keep going. I looked across the lane. On the door of the cobbler's shop—good woman, decent awls, once fixed my boot free of charge when Juniper was sick and I couldn't pay—someone had chalked a mark. A circle with a line struck through it. The hunt-mark. It meant *gifted here* or *suspected*, and either way it meant the iron hounds would come sniffing before week's end, and the cobbler would vanish overnight the way people had been vanishing all month, leaving behind cold ovens and unpaid debts that rang in my ears for weeks after.

The mark was fresh. The chalk still glittered.

I closed my curtains like a coward and told myself it was prudence.

* * *

At the Gilded Root, business went on, because business always goes on; that's the guild's one true faith. Bottled grief moved briskly in times like these—a widow bought three vials' worth of a stranger's calm mourning, because her own was too loud. Someone traded six borrowed years against a storm in a jar, and I entered it in the ledger without comment, though privately I thought anyone who wants someone else's years has never actually had to live inside their own.

I kept my head down. I balanced columns. I did not think about the entry I'd found two nights past, the soul sold thirteen moons ago, the voice that whispered out of the ink in a register I knew better than my own heartbeat. *Juniper.* I hadn't slept so much as arranged my sleeplessness into shifts.

It was nearly noon when Corvane sent for me.

Corvane does not send for people. Corvane *arrives*, the way weather arrives, or tax season. So when a junior clerk appeared at my elbow, gray-faced, saying the master would see me in the amber room, I felt the day tilt beneath me like a deck in rough water.

The amber room is where the Gilded Root conducts its gentler business. Everything in it is warm-colored and soft-voiced—the honeyed paneling, the lamps with their smoked-glass shades, the carpet engineered to swallow footsteps and, presumably, confessions. Corvane sat behind a desk that was really more of an altar, with a decanter of plum brandy and two glasses, which was the first alarming thing. Corvane pours brandy the way other men draw knives: only when something is about to be cut.

"August." He said my name like he was tasting it. "Sit. Drink."

"I'm working."

"You're always working. It's one of your virtues. Sit anyway."

I sat. I didn't drink. He poured for himself, unhurried, and outside the shuttered windows I could hear the criers again, farther off now, circling toward the river districts. *A hunt is declared. A hunt is declared.* Like a song you can't get out of your head, if songs could ruin your life.

"You've seen the proclamation," he said.

"Hard to miss it. They've woken half the city twice."

"Mm." He turned his glass by the stem, watching the brandy move. "Then you'll know the situation is becoming inconvenient. Three of our suppliers gone this month. The storm-broker vanished Tuesday night—hounds at his door before midnight, and no one in Copperlane heard a thing, which tells you everything about how much Copperlane wanted to hear a thing." He smiled faintly. "The crown has developed a taste for impossible things, August. And we are, as ever, in the business of supplying them."

"The crown doesn't want to buy," I said. "It wants to confiscate."

"The crown wants what it's always wanted. Control of things it doesn't understand." He set the glass down. "Which brings me to you."

Here it came. I felt it coming the way I feel debts coming—before the words, a pressure, a weight shifting onto the scales.

"You're gifted," Corvane said.

Not a question. My blood went cold and then hot and then very, very quiet, the way it does when survival takes over the books entirely. "I'm an accountant."

"You hear them." His voice stayed gentle, which from Corvane was worse than shouting. "Debts. Spoken aloud, in the debtor's own voice. Don't insult us both by denying it—I've watched you work for nine years. No one balances a ledger that well by arithmetic alone. You don't read the numbers, August. You *listen* to them. It's why I hired you. It's why I've kept you close." He leaned back. "It's why you're still alive."

Every instinct I had said *deny, deflect, die on that hill if necessary*. But Corvane had just done something far more dangerous than accuse me. He'd told me he'd known. For nine years. Which meant every day I'd spent hiding my gift behind competent bookkeeping, I'd been hiding it behind a door he'd already opened.

"What do you want," I said flatly.

"Protection." He let the word sit between us like a coin on a counter. "The hunt is real, and it is thorough, and the hounds are bred to scent gift the way they scent blood. A woman who hears debts would fetch a fine bounty—and a finer interrogation. But a woman under guild protection, sworn into the inner circle, registered in our private rolls as guild property—" he spread his hands, magnanimous, "—is untouchable. The crown may hunt the gifted. It does not yet dare hunt *us*. Not openly. Not while we hold certain accounts of certain courtiers that would empty the palace faster than plague."

"And the price?"

"Surrender your ledger access to the general stacks. Swear the inner oath—binding, witnessed, irrevocable. Your gift becomes a guild instrument, used at guild direction, for guild purposes. In exchange, the guild becomes your shield." He poured himself more brandy. "You'd answer to me alone. Think of it as employment, elevated."

Think of it as a leash, elevated. I looked at him—at the warm lamplight caught in the amber paneling, at the soft carpet, at the man who had signed off, thirteen moons ago, on a ledger entry that whispered in my sister's voice—and I understood, with the cold clarity I usually reserved for other people's debts, exactly what was being built here.

He wasn't offering me shelter. He was offering me complicity with a roof over it. If the crown ever came asking who among the Gilded Root was gifted, Corvane wouldn't have to hide me. He'd simply produce the oath, the registration, the binding—and I would stand there, sworn and sealed, while the guild's business continued uninterrupted. Whatever that business included. Souls. Sisters. Whatever else lived in the ledgers I'd been balancing for nine years without letting myself truly listen.

And if I refused? Then I was unsworn, unregistered, unshielded—a debt-touched woman with a hunt-mark's worth of suspicion hanging over her, and no one to blame but herself. The cage wasn't closing. The cage had been built around me years ago, furnished, warmed, stocked with plum brandy, and today Corvane was simply showing me the door and informing me it locked from the inside.

"You've thought about this a long time," I said.

"Since the night I first heard you humming to the ledgers," he said. "You hum when you listen. Did you know?"

I hadn't. Somewhere in nine years, my tell had become his comfort.

"I'll need time," I said.

"You have until the moon turns." He smiled again, and this time it reached his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "Thirteen days. Fitting number, isn't it? The city loves its omens." He rose, signaling dismissal, then paused, one hand on the honeyed paneling. "August. One more thing. There's a page missing from the vault registry. Last moon's soul transactions, folio seven. You balance those stacks—you'd notice a gap."

Would I ever.

"If you happen to recall anything about its removal," Corvane said lightly, "you'll bring it to me. Not because I suspect you." The lamplight made his eyes the color of the brandy. "Because I protect what's mine. And I do hope, for both our sakes, that you are still mine."

* * *

I walked home the long way, through streets that were learning a new language. Chalk marks bloomed on doorframes like a blight—some accurate, some spiteful, some, I suspected, settling old scores under cover of patriotism. An old man was scrubbing frantically at his own threshold, trying to erase a mark someone else had drawn, and the debt ringing off him was small and terrible: *I owe my son the truth that I can't do magic, I never could, please, please believe me.*

By the time I reached my stairs, the light was going coppery and the criers had gone home, and I stood a long moment looking at my own door.

No mark on it. Not yet.

But I found myself thinking about the vault, about folio seven, about the page I had taken—folded flat against my ribs right now, Juniper's name in faded ink, thirteen moons of interest accruing—and about the fact that Corvane already knew it was gone. He hadn't accused me. He'd *invited* me. Which meant either he didn't know who took it, or he knew exactly who, and wanted me to come to him carrying it, swearing oaths, handing over the last piece of myself that wasn't already his.

Either way, the trap had two doors, and both of them locked behind me.

Inside, I lit no lamp. I sat in the dark with the stolen page and listened to the city's debts rising through my floorboards like groundwater—fear, denial, small betrayals already being planned—and beneath all of it, steady as a pulse, the whisper I'd carried since the counting room:

*Juniper. Thirteen moons. One turn left.*

Twelve, now.

Corvane had given me until the moon turned to swear myself into his cage. The contract under the mountain gave me the same span to get my sister back. He thought he'd set the clock.

He had no idea he'd handed me the deadline I needed—or that I'd already decided which cage I intended to break.

I folded the page away, and in the dark, I started making a list.
