## 4. What the God Left Behind

There are three kinds of silence in this city. The kind that comes before a debt collector knocks. The kind that lives in the counting room after Corvane says *we'll discuss it later* and means *I've already decided and you won't like it*. And then there's the silence of Vesper — the god who stopped speaking when I was nine years old, mid-sentence, mid-blessing, mid-something I've spent twenty years pretending not to remember.

The first two silences are temporary. The third one has been my whole life.

I told myself I wasn't going to the shrine.

I told myself that on Tuesday, while balancing the ledger of bottled griefs, three jars short and no explanation for where they'd gone. I told myself that again on Wednesday, when I traced Juniper's name in the soul-ledger until my finger left an ink stain shaped like a small accusing moon. And I told myself Thursday morning, standing in front of my door with its fresh-painted hunt-mark — a black circle, the crown's little way of saying *we know what you are* — before walking in exactly the wrong direction from the guildhouse, downhill, toward the river district, toward the shrine I hadn't visited since I was old enough to know better.

Self-deception, I've found, works best if you commit to it fully. Half-hearted lies collapse under their own weight. Wholehearted ones can carry you all the way to the bottom of Lantern Row before you admit you've been lying at all.

Lantern Row used to be a pilgrimage street. That was before the war with the salt principalities, before the plague of borrowed years, before the crown decided gods were bad for tax revenue. Now it's a row of shuttered devotional shops and one very persistent woman who sells fried dough to people who don't know they're grieving yet. She waved at me as I passed. I bought a dough because refusing felt rude, and ate it because it would have been ruder still not to, and by the time I'd finished licking sugar off my thumb I was standing at the foot of the shrine steps trying to remember how I'd gotten there.

The steps were marble once. Now they're marble the way an old soldier is still technically a man — structurally, nostalgically, with gaps. Moss had claimed the lower third. Weeds grew in the cracks with the confidence of squatters who'd been there long enough to claim legal title. At the top, the shrine itself: a low stone building with a domed roof, its silver leaf long since peeled away by weather and thieves, its doors hanging open like a mouth that had forgotten how to close.

Vesper's shrine. Built for the god of endings-that-are-beginnings, which is a pretentious thing to be god of, but he was always a bit much. He'd have wanted the moss, I think. He liked things that grew in neglected places.

I stood at the bottom of the steps for a long time, doing sums.

Here is what I knew: Vesper went silent when I was nine. Not dead — gods don't die, whatever the theologians say; they just stop answering, the way rich relatives do when you ask about money. One night he was there, a voice like cold water in the back of my skull, patient and faintly amused, and the next night nothing. I screamed at him for a year. Then I bargained for two more. Then I stopped, because the guild found me — found the girl who heard debts spoken aloud, every unpaid thing in the city whispering its balance as its debtor walked past — and the Gilded Root, whatever else it is, knows what to do with a strange child. It fed me. Taught me numbers. Gave me a ledger instead of a god.

Twenty years of ledgers. Twenty years of telling myself the silence was his choice, his abandonment, his great cosmic shrug. *Gods leave*, I wrote once, in the margin of an account book, in ink so faint only I could read it. *That's the whole of their nature.*

And here is what I didn't know: why I was climbing these steps now, thirteen moons after my sister's soul was sold beneath my nose, one turn of the moon from losing her forever, when every rational bone in my body said the shrine would be empty, cold, useless, a tourist attraction for ghosts.

Because hope, I've learned, doesn't consult the rational bones. Hope is the fried-dough woman of the soul. It waves at you whether you want it or not.

* * *

Inside, the shrine smelled the way abandoned holy places smell: dust and pigeon and old incense gone bitter, the ghost of a thousand prayers soaked into stone. Light came through the dome's broken oculus in a single slanted column, full of drifting motes, illuminating absolutely nothing worth illuminating. The pews had rotted. The offering boxes had been pried open decades ago. Someone had carved *GODS ARE DEBTORS TOO* into the third pew, which was either profound vandalism or vandalized profundity, depending on your mood.

I was in a mood for profundity. It didn't help.

The altar stood at the far end, under the dome. Cold gray stone, bare, swept clean by wind through the broken doors. No idol — Vesper never kept idols; he said they were portraits painted by strangers. Just the altar, and above it, carved into the wall in letters deep enough to survive the weather, his one commandment, the whole of his theology:

*ALL ACCOUNTS SETTLE.*

Comforting fellow, our god. No love thy neighbor, no mercy clauses. Just the promise that everything evens out eventually, which sounds like justice until you're the one holding the debts.

I walked up the aisle, my boots loud in the quiet, and put my hand on the altar stone.

Cold. Of course it was cold. What had I expected — warmth? A voice? Twenty years of silence broken because August Montrose finally deigned to visit? Gods don't work like that. Gods don't work at all, maybe. Maybe they never did, and the voice in my childhood head was a fever, a gift running wild, a debt-collector's hallucination from a girl who heard too much and understood too little.

"Fine," I said aloud, because the shrine was empty and empty places deserve honesty. "Fine. I came. You're not here. Noted in the ledger."

My voice echoed off the dome and came back sounding smaller than it went out.

I turned to go. And that's when I saw it.

Tucked against the altar's base, half-hidden in the shadow of the stone, sat a candle. Small. White wax gone soft and lopsided with melting and re-melting, pooled over itself in layers like geological strata. A votive candle. The cheap kind, tallow-and-salt, sold by the dozen in the devotional shops that used to line Lantern Row.

It was burning.

Not guttering. Not dying. Burning — steady, upright, a flame with good posture, the flame of a candle that has been lit recently and tended properly. The wick was trimmed. The wax pool around it was clean, free of debris, free of the dead moths that littered every other surface in this building.

I stared at it for what felt like a full turn of the hourglass.

Then I did what any sensible person does when confronted with an impossible thing in an abandoned shrine: I checked the math. Candles burn down. This candle was perhaps four inches tall, and votives of that size last six hours, eight if the wax is good. For it to be burning now, someone had lit it today. Within hours. In a shrine everyone in the city knew was abandoned, on a day when the crown was nailing hunt-marks to doors and the streets were full of iron hounds and men with nets.

Someone had walked past all of that, up those rotten steps, into this dusty ruin, to light a candle for a silent god.

And not just today. I crouched, careful, and looked closer. The layers of melted wax told the story the way ledgers tell stories, if you know how to read them. Melted, cooled, melted again, dozens of times. New candles set atop the stubs of old ones, wick to wick, flame passed forward like a relay runner's baton. This wasn't one candle. This was one candle continued — a chain of them, weeks of them, months, each new taper pressed onto the frozen pool of the last.

Somebody had been keeping this flame alive for a very long time.

I sat down on the cold floor, right there in the dust, because my legs had opinions about standing.

Here's the thing you need to understand about me: I don't trust gifts. Gifts are just debts wearing perfume. Every kindness in the Gilded Root came with a hook in it — Corvane's protection offered yesterday was a leash with a velvet lining. When someone gives you something, they're writing themselves into your ledger, and most people who write themselves into your ledger intend to collect.

But a candle. A trimmed wick. Wax layered on wax in the dark of an abandoned shrine, month after month, with nobody watching, nobody collecting, nobody even knowing.

That wasn't a debt. Debts announce themselves. This was — I reached for the word and hated myself for reaching — this was devotion. Quiet, stubborn, unglamorous devotion, the kind that shows up as maintenance instead of miracle. Somebody loved this god enough to keep his light lit when he wouldn't even speak. Somebody thought he was worth it.

And underneath that thought, soft as a moth's wing, came another one, the one I'd been carrying up the steps without admitting it:

*What if he isn't gone?*

What if the silence wasn't abandonment? What if it was something else — exile, injury, imprisonment, some ending that hadn't finished becoming a beginning? He was the god of endings-that-are-beginnings, after all. Maybe silence was just an ending he hadn't worked his way out of yet. Maybe somewhere behind it, he was —

No. Stop. I pressed my palms against my eyes until I saw stars.

This was how it started. This was exactly how it started, twenty years ago, the bargaining, the *maybe he'll answer tonight*, the girl kneeling on cold stone making deals with a silence that never broke. I'd sworn off that particular flavor of heartbreak. I had a sister to save and a guild to betray and a moon turning overhead like a clock winding down, and I did not have time to sit in a ruin hoping a god would notice me.

Except.

Except my hand had moved without my permission, and my fingers were hovering over the candle flame, close enough to feel the heat, and I was remembering — not the screaming years, but before them. Before the silence. The voice like cold water. The way it never commanded, only asked. *Will you carry this? Will you count this? Will you tell me what you hear?* A god who treated a nine-year-old girl's terrible gift as a job offer rather than a curse. The only adult in my childhood who ever asked permission.

And then the night it stopped. The night I —

I flinched away from that memory like it was a hot stove. There was something there, at the edge of it, something I couldn't look at directly, the way you can't look at the sun or your own funeral. A shape. A weight. Something I had done, or said, or failed to do, on the last night Vesper spoke.

The memory slid sideways and was gone, the way those memories always go, leaving only the bruise of having almost remembered.

"All accounts settle," I told the empty shrine. My voice cracked on the second word, which was undignified, and I chose to pretend it hadn't happened. "You wrote that. So settle. Answer me. Tell me what happened that night. Tell me if you're in there. Tell me anything, you silent, stubborn, absent—"

The flame flickered.

Just a draft, I told myself. Broken doors, river wind, of course it flickered. But it flickered *toward* me, bent against the airflow, leaned in my direction like a dog hearing its name, and for one absurd heartbeat the whole shrine felt less empty, the column of light less accidental, the silence less like absence and more like —

Like held breath.

Then it was just a candle again, burning its steady, well-tended burn, and I was a grown woman sitting in the dust arguing with wax.

I laughed. It came out wrong, wet at the edges, and I wiped my face with my sleeve and laughed again, properly this time, at the sheer ridiculousness of it. Thirteen moons. One turn left. My sister's soul in a cage beneath the mountain, sold by the family that raised me, signed by the man whose ledgers I balanced, and here I was, having a theological crisis with a votive candle.

But I didn't leave. That was the thing. I gathered my dignity, such as it was, and I stood, and I brushed off my skirts, and I did not leave.

Instead I did the only sensible thing left. I took out my coin purse, found the stub of white candle I'd bought three days ago from the fried-dough woman's neighbor — don't ask why I'd bought it; I'd told myself it was for the shop, in case of power failures, and I'd almost believed it — and I crouched at the altar's base and touched its wick to the living flame.

The new candle caught. Two flames now, side by side, the old tended one and mine, leaning together in the draft.

"There," I said quietly. "Now it's two of us being stupid."

I stayed a while longer. I won't pretend otherwise. I sat on the rotting steps inside the door, out of the wind, and watched the candle burn, and let myself feel the thing I'd been not-feeling since I found Juniper's name in the ledger: not dread, not rage, but the smaller, sharper pain underneath both. Grief with the wound reopened. Twenty years of telling myself she was dead, because dead was bearable and *sold* was not, and now she was neither, she was *waiting*, thirteen turns deep in a court that counted souls like coins, and every day I'd spent not looking for her was a day the interest compounded.

"I'm coming for her," I told the candle. "Whoever's tending you — whoever taught you that trick with the flame — tell them too. Tell anyone who's listening. I'm coming, and I'm bringing the whole ledger."

The flame stood straight. The dust drifted through the light. The shrine said nothing, in the way it had said nothing for twenty years.

But when I finally rose to go, stiff-kneed, dusk pooling blue in the doorway, I noticed something I'd missed on the way in. Low on the altar's side, hidden in shadow, scratched into the stone in a hurried hand — not the old carved commandment, but something newer, fresher, done within the last few days by someone with a knife-point and no time:

Three words, small as a confession:

*SHE REMEMBERS YOU.*

I read them twice. Three times. The letters didn't rearrange themselves into sense. They stayed exactly as impossible as they were.

She. Not *he*. Not the god. *She remembers you* — and there was only one she the world could mean, one person whose memory of me mattered enough for someone to carve into a holy place in secret, in haste, in the dark.

Juniper.

Someone knew I'd come here. Someone knew about my sister. Someone was leaving messages in abandoned shrines like breadcrumbs, and had been tending a dead god's candle long enough to layer wax on wax on wax, waiting — for what? For me? For this exact evening?

Hope, I've said, doesn't consult the rational bones. But fear does, and fear was tapping its foot, reminding me that in a city where the crown hunts the gifted and the guild feeds names to hunters, a message that convenient is also a message that could be bait.

I looked at the candle. It burned on, indifferent, patient, tended.

"Whoever you are," I said to the empty air, "you'd better hope I find you before the hunters do. Because I have questions, and unlike the crown, I don't use iron."

Outside, the first stars were out, and the moon hung low over the mountain — fat, pale, one turn from full, one turn from done. Thirteen of thirteen, closing like a ledger at month's end.

Behind me, in the dark shrine, the candle kept burning. Kept by unseen hands. Waiting.

So was I.
