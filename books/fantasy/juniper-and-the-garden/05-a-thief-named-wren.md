## 5. A Thief Named Wren

There are three ways into the Gilded Root's vault, and I know all of them because I helped design two of them and bribed my way past the third.

The first is the front door, which requires Corvane's seal and a smile you don't mean. The second is the coal chute, which requires a tolerance for soot and a body that forgives you for it. The third is a servant's stair behind the wine cellar that doesn't appear on any blueprint because I paid the architect's widow to forget she'd ever drawn it. She'd owed me for a coat I'd mended in winter and never asked payment for until I needed a door instead of coin. That's how debts work, if you're careful with them. You don't collect. You wait.

I chose the third way, at the hour when even the guild's night clerks have gone home to wives they lie to, carrying my father's old lockpicks and a candle stub I stole from Vesper's shrine without asking his forgiveness. Gods who've gone silent can't refuse you anything. It's the one perk of abandonment.

The lock on the cellar door was new. That was my first warning.

The Gilded Root does not change its locks. Locks cost money, and Corvane hoards money the way dragons hoard gold—jealously, stupidly, with no plan beyond the having of it. A new lock meant someone expected a visitor. Someone had counted the candles, or noticed the missing page before I did, or simply looked at me across the counting room table yesterday and seen what I'd spent eleven years teaching myself to hide: that I hear things other people only think.

I picked it anyway. I'd like to tell you I hesitated. I didn't. Juniper's name had been sitting in that ledger for thirteen moons, written in ink I'd mixed myself, filed under a category I'd invented—*long-term holdings, subterranean*—and every day I'd walked past it was another day I'd balanced the books of my sister's cage. The moon had one turn left. Hesitation was a luxury priced beyond me.

The vault breathed cold when the door swung open. It always does. Impossible things keep their own weather. Bottled storms sweat on their shelves; borrowed years tick faintly, like insects in a wall; the souls—the souls are quietest, which is somehow worse. They sit in their lanterns and jars and lead-lined boxes with the patience of people who have learned that screaming changes nothing.

I found the ledger shelf by memory. Volume nine. Thirteenth year. My own handwriting marched down the page in its tidy columns, and there it was:

*One (1) soul, female, age 7 at acquisition. Seller: Gilded Root, per arrangement. Buyer: Court Beneath the Mountain. Term: thirteen lunar turns, after which title passes absolutely.*

*Name: Juniper Sandoval.*

*Acquired in settlement of debt incurred by A. Sandoval, mercenary, to Vesper, god of dusk-tolls.*

I had read that last line forty times since finding it, and it still didn't make sense. What debt could a seven-year-old girl incur? Against whom? Against *me*? I'd been a child too. Children don't owe gods. Except apparently this one did, and the guild had collected, and I had signed the receipt.

I copied the entry onto scrap paper with shaking hands, and that is when the luck went out of the room.

You'll laugh, but I felt it happen. The air changed texture, the way silk changes when you run your hand against the nap. The candle guttered though there was no draft. Somewhere above us, a bottle of borrowed fortune slipped its shelf and shattered, and I heard the years inside it spill out and evaporate—all of it wasted, all of it *wrong*, the specific wrongness of a thing that has been made unlucky on purpose.

"Down here," said a voice from the rafters, "you want to be very still."

* * *

She dropped from the shelving like a falling star that had decided, at the last moment, to be a person instead. Small. Maybe nineteen. Dark hair cut short and practical, a face built for grinning and currently doing it, dressed in the kind of clothes that exist in three cities under three different names because their owner keeps stealing better ones. Her eyes were quick and brown and entirely too amused for a woman standing in a vault full of souls at an hour when being caught means the river.

"You tripped something," she said. "Not a lock. A *luck*. There's a charm wired over the door—cheap work, honestly, I'd have done it better—and it dumped about six months' bad fortune on whoever came through last." She peered at me. "That was you, wasn't it. You came through maybe ten minutes ago."

"I came through the wine cellar."

"Mm. And the charm's anchored to the vault door itself, so—" She shrugged. "Congratulations. For the next few months, birds will hate you, bread will stale early, and every coin you flip will land on the side that ruins your evening."

"That explains the candle," I said, mostly to myself.

"Explains a lot of things, probably." She hopped down off a crate of bottled grief—I recognized the shipment; I'd appraised it Tuesday—and stuck out her hand. "Wren."

"August."

"August," she repeated, tasting it. "Like the month. Boring month. Everything's dying and everyone pretends it's still summer." Then her gaze snagged somewhere around my collarbone, where my scarf had slipped, and the grin fell off her face like a plate off a table.

I knew what she was looking at. Three days ago, someone had chalked the crown's mark on my door—a circle with a slash through it, the hunter's sign meaning *gifted, unclaimed, watch*. I'd scrubbed at it until my knuckles bled and the wood underneath stayed marked anyway, because hunter's chalk isn't chalk, it's a covenant, and covenants don't wash off. They just move house. Apparently mine had moved to my skin, or my luck had gotten so bad it was showing through.

"Huh," said Wren.

"Don't," I said.

"Don't what?"

"Scream. Run. Offer me to the hunters for the bounty. Whatever you were about to do."

"I wasn't going to do any of those." She tilted her head. "I was going to ask why a marked woman is breaking into the vault of the guild that's been selling names to the crown."

The vault got very quiet. Even the borrowed years stopped ticking.

"You know about that," I said.

"I know lots of things. It's my whole personality." She sat down on the crate of grief like it was a park bench, which, given what was inside it, took either courage or a profound lack of imagination. "I steal from the Gilded Root about once a season. Not for profit—well, some for profit—but mostly because they deserve it. And lately, when I'm in their walls, I hear things. Clerks talk. Guards talk worse. And three weeks ago I heard a runner come in from the palace district with a list, and I heard Master Corvane read it, and I heard him add names to it." Her eyes came up to mine. "Your name was fourth."

The floor seemed to tilt. Or maybe that was the bad luck settling in, rearranging my sense of which way was down.

"Fourth," I said.

"After a hedge-witch who grows things she shouldn't, a blind singer who remembers songs that haven't been written yet, and—" Wren paused, and for the first time the amusement drained out of her voice entirely, "—me. I'm third. Which is flattering, actually. Ahead of a mercenary with a god's mark on her door. Though I suppose they haven't figured out yet that the mark's not the crown's."

My hand went to my collarbone before I could stop it. "What do you mean?"

"I mean I've seen that mark before, and it wasn't on a hunter's notice." She leaned forward. "It was chalked on the shrine up on Lantern Hill. The dead one. The silent god's. Somebody drew it over the altar years ago and never scrubbed it off, and it looks exactly like the thing on your door except older, and softer, like it's been rained on by somebody crying." She shrugged. "I used to sleep up there sometimes, before the hunt. Free roof. Good acoustics. Creepy altar. Anyway."

Anyway. As if she'd said *anyway* about the weather and not about the fact that my god's sigil and the crown's hunting mark were the same shape.

"They're not the same symbol," I said. "The crown's mark is a circle slashed through. Vesper's is—" I stopped, because I was doing the geometry in my head, and the geometry was betraying me. A circle. A line drawn through it. Dusk-toll: the moment the sun's disk is cut by the horizon. The crown hadn't invented their mark. They'd *stolen* it, or inherited it, or dug it up out of some archive and sharpened it into a weapon, and now they were using my god's oldest sign to hunt his children.

Or his child. Singular. Me.

"You didn't know," Wren said. It wasn't a question, but I shook my head anyway, and something in her face shifted—not softening exactly, more like a door unlatching. "Right. Well. Here's my situation, August-like-the-month. In three weeks, hunters with iron dogs are going to work through that list, top to bottom, and I'm third on it, and I am *not* going to be caught, because I have a gift, and my gift is that bad luck follows me around like a loyal dog of my own, and I can throw it. At people. Doors. Bridges. Once a wedding, but he deserved it." She stood up. "But throwing bad luck at iron dogs is like throwing soup at a flood. So I need allies. Real ones. The kind who don't sell each other's names."

"And you're asking me."

"I'm asking the woman who broke into a vault she had keys to." She gestured at the ledger open on the shelf, at my scrawled copy, at the entry I hadn't thought to hide because hiding it hadn't occurred to me until now—too late, her eyes already reading upside-down the way thieves' eyes do. Her expression did something complicated. "Juniper Sandoval," she read. "Age seven at acquisition. Thirteen turns." She looked up. "Who's Juniper?"

"My sister."

The word came out of me sounding like something exhumed. I hadn't said it aloud in years. In the guild we don't say names we've entered in ledgers; saying them makes them heavier, and heavy entries take two clerks to carry. But it was out now, hanging in the cold air between the bottled storms and the borrowed luck, and it wouldn't go back in.

"She died," I said. "When we were children. Fever. That's what I was told. That's what I *grieved*." I put my hand flat on the ledger page, over the neat column of my own handwriting. "She didn't die. She was sold. By this guild. To the court under the mountain. And the sale completes in—" I did the arithmetic I'd been doing every morning since I found it, the arithmetic that sits in my chest like a stone, "—nineteen days. After that, it's permanent. No terms. No recourse. Title absolute."

Wren was quiet for a long moment. In the vault's cold, our breath hung between us like two small ghosts negotiating.

"Nineteen days," she said finally. "To break into a court that's been under the mountain since before the city had a name, get past nobles who trade in souls the way fishmongers trade in herring, and steal back a soul that's been legally purchased with paperwork in triplicate." She rubbed her jaw. "I've done some bad jobs. This is the worst job I've ever heard of."

"Yes," I said.

"And you're going to do it anyway."

"Yes."

"Alone?"

"I was planning on it."

"Well, that plan's garbage." She said it cheerfully, the way you'd tell someone their hat was on fire. "August. Look at me. You've got a mark on your skin that says *hunt me*, a god who's gone quiet, a guild master who's feeding your name to the crown, and nineteen days. You need a thief. You specifically need *this* thief, because I have been in and out of half the buildings in this city and under a third of them, and I know things about the mountain that aren't in any ledger." She held out her hand again, the one I'd failed to shake the first time. "Alliance. Fifty-fifty. You get your sister, I don't get caught, and we both get to burn Corvane's ledgers on the way out. I find that deeply motivating. Don't you find that deeply motivating?"

I should have said no.

Let me be clear about that. I should have said no, because alliances are debts with extra steps, and I could hear hers already—that faint whisper every debt carries for me, the sound only I seem cursed to hear. Hers was loud. Louder than most. Underneath the grin and the swagger, Wren owed somebody something enormous, something with teeth in it, and the debt had a sound like a trapdoor opening slowly.

But then, I owed plenty myself. I owed a silent god a childhood of unanswered prayers. I owed a dead-not-dead sister thirteen years of birthdays. I owed the guild my signature on a sale I couldn't remember making. Everyone in this city was drowning; the only question was whose hand you grabbed on the way down.

And beneath everything—beneath the fear and the arithmetic and the stone in my chest—there was a smaller, stupider feeling. The feeling of hearing someone say *you need allies* and realizing that for eleven years, no one had. The guild had given me a desk and a salary and a room with a lock on the outside, and I had called it family because the alternative word was too frightening to hold.

"Alliance," I said, and shook her hand.

Her grip was warm and calloused and quick, and the moment our skin touched, I heard her luck brush against mine—two currents of misfortune tangling, recognizing each other, doing whatever it is that unlucky things do when they stop fighting and start cooperating. Somewhere above us, another bottle fell off a shelf. Neither of us flinched.

"So," Wren said, releasing my hand and cracking her knuckles like a woman settling in for a long story. "Nineteen days. We'll need three things minimum: a way under the mountain, a way to void a soul-contract, and a miracle. I can maybe get us the first one. There's a witch on Candlemaker's Row—Bramble, they call her—who grows doors where doors shouldn't be, and she owes me for a job I did last spring, and also she's second on the hunter's list, so she'll be motivated."

"Bramble," I repeated. The name rang a bell somewhere deep, in the place where old fevers live. "I think—I think my sister knew her. Before."

"Small city," Wren said. "Everyone knows everyone, especially the people the crown wants dead." She was already moving toward the vault door, light on her feet, pausing only to liberate a jar of something labeled *LUCK, FORTUNATE, MILD* from a low shelf and tuck it into her coat with the unembarrassed ease of long practice. "Come on. We shouldn't be here when the night clerk does his rounds, and you especially shouldn't, because your luck is currently a live animal with opinions."

I took one last look at the ledger. Volume nine, thirteenth year. *Juniper Sandoval.* Nineteen days.

Then I closed it, and followed the thief out through the door I'd bribed into existence, and felt—for the first time since I'd read my sister's name in my own handwriting—something that wasn't dread.

It might have been hope. It might have been the mild fortunate luck leaking through Wren's coat. With her, I suspected, I'd spend a long time learning to tell the difference.

At the mouth of the wine cellar, she stopped, sniffed the air, and frowned.

"Huh," she said. "Do you smell smoke?"

Behind us, deep in the guildhouse, a bell began to ring.
