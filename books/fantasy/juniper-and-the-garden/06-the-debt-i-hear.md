## 6. The Debt I Hear

Corvane's counting room smelled of cedar oil and old money, which is to say it smelled like a lie that had been dusted regularly.

I sat in the chair across from his desk—the visitor's chair, the one with the shorter legs so that anyone sitting in it looked slightly up at him. I'd noticed the trick years ago and had taken to wearing heels on the days I expected to be summoned. Today I'd worn my tallest boots. Small rebellions. You take them where you can find them when you work for a guild that trades in souls.

"August." He said my name the way he said everything—warmly, and as if he were pricing it.

"Guildmaster."

"You know why you're here."

"I have a suspicion," I said. "But you pay me by the hour, and I've learned not to do free work."

The corner of his mouth moved. Not a smile. Corvane didn't smile; he deployed expressions the way he deployed people, each one costed against its return. "Page forty-one of the soul-ledger is missing."

"Is it."

"It is."

"That's unfortunate," I said. "Ledgers are sacred things. Whoever removed that page should be flayed, drawn, and made to do inventory of the bottled griefs without gloves."

He studied me across the desk. Behind him, the great window overlooked the Gilded Root's courtyard, where the fountain murmured its perpetual counterfeit of innocence. The morning light came through gilded glass and turned everything the color of a coin—everything except Corvane himself, who remained whatever color he decided to be, which was usually the color of trustworthiness, which was usually a lie.

"You're the last person to sign out the ledger," he said.

"I'm the only person who *can* sign out the ledger. That's rather the point of me."

"And yet."

"And yet," I agreed pleasantly.

Here is what you need to understand about being interrogated by Corvane: it does not feel like an interrogation. It feels like a conversation between colleagues, conducted over tea that never gets offered, in a room where the door has quietly been locked by someone whose job description includes locking doors. The trap is always already sprung. The only question is whether you knew you were walking into it, and whether you can make him believe you didn't.

I had known. I had walked in anyway, because refusing the summons would have told him more than any answer could.

"The missing page," he said, folding his hands, "recorded a sale. Thirteen moons past. A soul, sold to a buyer below the mountain. Do you remember the entry?"

My heart did something complicated and traitorous behind my ribs. I kept my face doing something simple and boring instead. Thirteen moons ago. Juniper. The entry I had found three nights ago in the archive copy, the name written in the ledger's careful hand: *Juniper Sandoval — sold entire — court beneath the mountain — thirteen turns to permanence.*

One turn left now. Twenty-eight days, give or take what the moon felt like doing.

"I process hundreds of entries," I said. "Souls, storms, luck, years. Last month alone I catalogued a jar containing someone's grandmother's talent for whistling. You'll forgive me if one page doesn't sing itself back to me."

"One page," Corvane said softly, "always sings. That's why we keep them locked."

And there it was—the thing beneath the thing, surfacing for air. He wasn't asking whether I remembered. He was telling me he knew I'd read it. The question was how much else he knew, and whether the answer was *everything*, in which case this room was not an office but a throat, and I was already partway down.

I heard the debts before he spoke again. I always hear them—that is my gift, or my affliction, or my invoice from the universe, depending on the day. Every debt a person carries has a sound. Most are small. A neighbor's unpaid kindness, a friend's unkept promise, a merchant's fudged weights—they whisper, they rustle, they tick like beetles in a wall. I have spent my whole life pretending not to hear them, because a bookkeeper who hears everyone's hidden debts is worth more than gold and more dangerous than plague, and either way, someone eventually decides to own her outright.

Corvane's debts did not whisper.

They never had. From my first week in the counting room, twelve years ago, his debts had sounded like weather—a low pressure system of obligation rolling somewhere offshore, vast and patient. I'd learned to work around it the way sailors work around storms. You don't ask what the storm wants. You batten down and you count your days.

But today, as he leaned forward into the gilded light, the storm had a shape.

"—and of course," he was saying, in the tone of a man offering a generous settlement, "there remains the matter of your protection. The crown's hunt widens daily. They took the cooper's daughter on Tallow Street two nights ago. Iron hounds. She was gifted, they say—could taste lies. The hounds found her by the flavor of her own fear." He let that sit. "You are gifted, August. We both know it, though neither of us has ever been so gauche as to say it aloud. The Gilded Root can shield you. The Gilded Root has *always* shielded you. All I require is your ledger access surrendered to my direct oversight, and a renewed oath of service. Sworn properly. Before witnesses."

"A new oath," I repeated.

"The old one has grown... imprecise. Oaths do that. Like maps." He smiled his priced smile. "The new one will be very precise. It will specify, among other things, that you report directly to me. That you disclose all irregularities immediately. That you hold no knowledge of guild transactions outside your sanctioned duties."

In other words: stop reading pages you shouldn't. In other words: forget Juniper's name. In other words: be mine, entirely, and call it rescue.

I should note here—for honesty's sake, since honesty was about to become the most expensive commodity in the room—that some part of me wanted to say yes. That's the genius of a gilded cage; the gilding isn't decoration, it's argument. Warm rooms. Regular wages. Protection from iron hounds that tasted fear on the air. I was twenty-six years old and tired, and the cage door was open, and all I had to do was walk in and stop listening to the voice that had whispered to me from a ledger page in a dead girl's cadence.

*Dead girl.* No. Not dead. Sold. There was a difference, and the difference had twenty-eight days left to live in it.

"May I ask a question?" I said.

"You may always ask. Answers are a different economy."

"When you renewed the vault wards last spring—who paid for the under-mountain salt?"

Something flickered across his face. Small. Priced. Filed away.

"Routine expenditure," he said.

"Mm. Only, under-mountain salt doesn't come from any quarry I can find in the trade rolls. It comes from below. And things from below are never routine, and they are never *only* expenditures. They're installments."

The room went quiet in the way rooms do when they realize they've been hosting a different conversation than the one they thought.

"August." My name again, repriced. Lower now. "What exactly are you suggesting?"

And here I made my mistake, or my choice—I've never fully settled which, and the difference may not exist. I stopped performing. I stopped being the competent, slightly cheeky bookkeeper who notices nothing and costs little. I let myself listen. Fully. Deliberately. For the first time in twelve years, I turned my gift toward Corvane like a lamp turned into a dark corner, and I let myself *hear*.

Gods forgive me. Gods, or Vesper, who hadn't spoken to me since I was nine, who had gone silent mid-prayer one autumn night and left me alone with a head full of other people's debts and no god to explain why.

His debts rose around him like water finding its level.

The small ones first, almost tender: a debt to a woman named Maren, thirty years old, shaped like a promise broken in a garden. A debt to his dead partner, unpaid, compounding. A debt to the crown—not coin but names, a running tally of gifted folk fed to the hunters like grain to geese, and the interest on that debt was accruing in a currency I didn't want to imagine.

And beneath all of it, holding all of it up, the way bedrock holds up a city:

A debt to the court beneath the mountain. Measured in souls. Not one soul. Not ten. A *quota*—a rolling, renewing quota, due in full when the moon completed its thirteenth turn, and the moon, I remind you, had one turn left. And against that great stone weight of obligation, payments already made, entries already entered in a hand I recognized because it was the same hand that wrote every ledger in the Gilded Root, including mine:

*Juniper Sandoval. Sold entire.*

She wasn't an aberration. She wasn't a single terrible transaction buried in thirteen years of honest trade. She was a *payment*. An installment on a debt Corvane had been servicing since before I could write. The guild hadn't betrayed me once. The guild had been betraying the whole city, slowly, seasonally, the way a farmer rotates crops—and my sister had simply come due.

The cold that went through me then was not fear. Fear is hot; fear runs and shouts and breaks dishes. This was the other thing, the quiet thing, the thing that comes after fear has spent itself and leaves behind a clarity like frost on glass. I looked at Corvane—at his warm eyes and his gilded light and his priced smile—and I understood that I had spent twelve years balancing the books of a man who was selling my city one soul at a time, and that my competence had been load-bearing.

That's the thing about cold clarity. It doesn't rage. It takes notes.

"You've gone pale," Corvane observed. "I'll take that as distress at the crown's hunt. Sensible. All the more reason to accept my offer."

"Yes," I heard myself say. "All the more reason."

He blinked. I watched him reprice me in real time—recalculating, adjusting, deciding that the fish had taken the hook. "August—"

"I accept," I said. "Your protection. Your terms. Ledger access surrendered, oath renewed, disclosures immediate. When do you want me to swear it?"

The smile he deployed then was the most genuine thing I'd ever seen him produce, which tells you something about the man, and something about me, for recognizing genuineness chiefly in the moment I weaponized it.

"Tomorrow night," he said. "Before witnesses. The full rite."

"Tomorrow night," I agreed, and stood, and my legs held, which I counted as the day's second miracle. At the door I paused, hand on the frame, and gave him one last piece of the performance—one last coin tossed to the dog that might otherwise follow me home.

"For what it's worth," I said, "whoever took that page did the guild a disservice. Ledgers should balance. Even the ugly ones. *Especially* the ugly ones."

"Especially the ugly ones," he echoed, warm as banked coals, and I walked out through the cedar-and-money smell of the only home I'd ever known, knowing that in twenty-nine hours I would swear an oath I intended to break before the wax cooled, and that the man across the desk owed a debt measured in souls, and that the moon was coming to collect.

* * *

I made it four streets before my hands started shaking.

Not during. Never during. During, you are marble, you are ledger-crisp, you are the competent woman with the tall boots. Afterward, the bill arrives. Everything bills afterward. I am, professionally speaking, very familiar with this principle.

I ducked into the shrine-row off Cooper's Lane—the little alcoves where the city's forgotten gods rented space between a chandler and a pie-shop—and pressed my palm flat against cool stone until the shaking worked itself out through my fingers. Somewhere above, the moon hung invisible in the morning sky, one turn from done. Below my feet, somewhere far under the mountain's roots, Juniper was alive. Alive, and caged, and an installment payment, and she had been for thirteen years while I alphabetized bottled storms and called it a career.

*Thirteen moons.* I'd thought the number was cruelty. Now I understood it was accounting. Permanence wasn't a curse; it was a fiscal quarter closing.

One turn left. One oath to falsely swear. One guildmaster to betray, carefully, completely, and—if there was any mercy in the arithmetic—in a way that also settled what he owed.

Because here was the thought that arrived while my hands shook, arriving cold and clear as a bell struck once: I could run. Wren and I could run—we had a thief's alliance now, fragile and new, sealed in a vault by lamplight and mutual blackmail potential. Bramble's shop was a sanctuary, if sanctuaries still existed by week's end. We could go east, over the mountains, away from hounds and hunts and hollow-eyed guildmasters.

But the debt would still exist. The quota would still come due. Corvane would simply collect other names—other sisters, other sons, other gifted children who heard too much or grew too much or tasted lies—and the ledger would keep balancing, and I would keep being the kind of person who lets ledgers balance.

No. Cold clarity has rules. First rule: the enemy has a face. Second rule: once you've seen the face, you can't unsee it, and a debt you can see is a debt you can collect.

Third rule, which I invented on the spot, standing in a shrine alcove with my heart hammering out terms: *you don't pay his debt for him. You make the court collect it from him.*

I didn't yet know how. I knew only that somewhere in the contract language of that soul-sale—somewhere in the clauses I'd half-read by candlelight before fleeing the archive—was a loophole, because there is always a loophole; contracts are written by people, and people are inveterate loopholers. I needed the full text. I needed the original. Which meant the vault, which meant Wren, which meant explaining to a pickpocket who flung bad luck like knives that tomorrow night I was going to swear a false oath to the most dangerous man in the city, and that after that, there was no version of events in which any of us lived small, quiet lives.

Somewhere across the rooftops, a bell rang noon. Twenty-eight days. Give or take what the moon felt like doing.

I straightened my boots, wiped my face with the heel of my hand, and went to find a thief.
