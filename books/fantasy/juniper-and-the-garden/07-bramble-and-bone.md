## 7. Bramble and Bone

There is a particular art to fleeing a place that raised you. You cannot run, because running invites pursuit. You cannot dawdle, because dawdling invites reflection. What you must do is walk—briskly, purposefully, with the expression of a woman who has somewhere entirely legitimate to be—and you must not look back at the guildhouse doors even when every debt in your chest is screaming at you to do exactly that.

I walked. I did not look back. I am very proud of this.

The city of Cinderhollow at dusk is a thing of chimneys and grudges, smoke curling up from a thousand hearths like the city itself trying to remember what warmth was for. I moved through the Tanner's Quarter with my collar up and Wren's hand clamped around my wrist like a shackle she'd picked out herself.

"Left," she hissed.

"There's nothing left but a dead-end."

"Exactly. Nobody watches a dead-end." She tugged me into it anyway, into the shadow between a tannery wall and a stack of hides that smelled like regret given form. "Now stand there and look unlucky."

"I don't have to look."

"Perfect. Method acting." She peered back the way we'd come, one eye showing beneath her hood. "Two hunters on the corner. Iron hounds. They're not following us yet, which means they're following someone else, which means we have maybe four minutes before they follow us instead, because that's how my luck works. It's not bad luck, exactly. It's *scheduling*."

"That's a horrifying way to describe a gift."

"You should hear how I describe yours." She turned back to me. In the dying light her face was all sharp angles and sharper eyes. "So. The hedge-witch. Bramble. You're sure she'll take us in?"

I wasn't sure of anything anymore. That was rather the theme of my week. But some things you carry longer than sense, and Bramble was one of them—a name from childhood, from fevers and poultices and a small round woman who smelled of thyme and spoke to plants as if they were difficult relatives. When Juniper burned with fever, it was Bramble's garden that pulled her back. I remembered sitting on her step, nine years old, watching her press her palms into the dirt and *ask*, and the foxglove leaning toward her like it had opinions.

"She owes me nothing," I said. "But she loved my sister once. That's worth more than an account."

"Mm. Sentiment. My favorite currency—it spends terribly." Wren pushed off the wall. "Dead-end's done its job. Come on. And if she turns us in, I want it noted that I objected."

"Noted," I said. "In your ledger or mine?"

"Yours. Mine's just a list of people who owe me apologies, and it's getting long."

* * *

Bramble's house sat at the crooked end of Nettle Row, where the cobbles gave up and let the weeds win. It looked like the house had lost an argument with a garden and made peace with the terms: ivy over the door, moss on the roof, a window box of something flowering out of season because no one had told it not to.

Wren knocked twice, then, after a pause, three times more in a rhythm I didn't recognize. Bramble answered before the third knock finished falling.

She was smaller than memory made her, rounder, greyer at the temples, but her hands were the same—earth-stained, quick, already reaching for us as though she'd been expecting trouble and had set out cups for it. Her eyes went from Wren to me, and something in them went still and old.

"August Montrose," she said. "You've got your sister's chin and your mother's stubbornness, and both of those are going to be problems. Get inside before the row sees you."

Inside smelled of drying herbs and woodsmoke and something green underneath it all, alive, like the house itself was breathing. Shelves crowded the walls, jars labeled in a handwriting that fought its own legibility. A pot of rosemary by the hearth turned its leaves toward Bramble as she shut the door, the way a dog turns toward its person.

"You knew I'd come," I said.

"I knew *someone* would come." She busied herself with the kettle, which was already hot, which meant she'd been waiting. "When the crown posts hunt-notices on every corner and the Gilded Root starts burning pages, a woman who keeps a garden learns to count the days. Sit. Both of you. You look like a funeral that got rained on."

We sat. The tea was chamomile and something bitter underneath, and I drank it without asking what the bitter thing was, because asking felt like a luxury I couldn't afford.

Bramble settled across from me, hands folded over her apron, and studied me the way she must have studied sick children: thoroughly, kindly, without flinching from what she found.

"Now," she said. "Tell me why you're here, and tell me true, because the plants will know if you lie and they gossip worse than the fishwives."

So I told her. Not everything—I kept Vesper's shrine folded away in the private room of my chest—but enough. The ledger entry. Juniper's name in the soul-accounts. Thirteen moons sold to the court beneath the mountain, one turn remaining before the sale set permanent like a bone knitting wrong. Corvane's offered protection, which was a cage with better upholstery. The hunters in the street.

Bramble listened without interrupting, which is a rarer skill than healing. When I finished, she was quiet a long moment, and the rosemary by the fire drooped, just slightly, as if it too had heard.

"Thirteen moons," she said at last. "A lunar year and a turn. That's the standard term for a court sale. After the thirteenth turning, the contract doesn't expire. It *roots*." She said the word like it tasted of iron. "The soul grows into the new soil. You can dig it up after that, but you won't get the same plant."

My tea had gone cold in my hands. I hadn't noticed drinking any of it. "You know the terms."

"I know the terms." Her mouth went thin. "Because twelve years ago, a man came to this house with a contract and asked me to witness it. He said it was a debt settlement. A soul, sold to settle a guild obligation. He needed a healer's mark because the court requires proof the vessel is sound—the body healthy, the mind whole. A soul in a broken vessel fetches less, you see. They're practical down there."

The room tilted, gently, the way a floor tilts when you realize it's been tilting all along.

"The vessel," I said. "Was it—"

"It was a girl of six years old, fevered half out of her mind, and I signed because he showed me guild seals and I was a fool who trusted the Gilded Root the way everyone in this city trusts it—because it's always been there, because it's gilded." Bramble's hands had gone white where they gripped each other. "I checked her lungs. I checked her heart. I pronounced her sound. And then I went home and was sick in my own garden, and the garden grew thorns for a year afterward, and I never asked another question because asking questions is how people in this city disappear."

"Who was the man?" Wren asked quietly. She'd gone very still, the stillness of a pickpocket cataloguing exits.

"He wore grey and he smelled of ink." Bramble looked at me. "But he carried Corvane's seal. Second ring. The one only his inner circle holds."

*Corvane.* The name landed in my chest like a coin dropped down a well—you wait for it to hit bottom and it never does. I thought of him offering me protection with that soft voice, that patient ledger-keeper's smile. Protection. He'd sold my sister and then offered to shelter me from the consequences of a world he'd helped make hungry.

"He knew," I said. "He's known the whole time. He watched me balance the accounts for ten years and never said a word."

"Or he was waiting," Bramble said, "to see if you'd find it yourself. There are two kinds of dangerous men, August. The kind who hide things, and the kind who leave things where you'll trip over them, so the blame lands on your foot and not his hand."

Wren snorted. "That's the most depressing wisdom I've ever heard, and I once heard a drunk philosopher argue that hope is just greed with better manners."

"Hope is exactly that," Bramble said, "and I've never seen the problem." She stood, joints cracking like green wood in a fire, and crossed to the window, checking the street through a gap in the ivy. "You can't stay here. You understand that. If the hunters come to Nettle Row, I can't protect you—not with charms, not with anything. My gift grows things. It doesn't grow walls."

"I'm not asking you to hide us," I said. "I'm asking—" And here was the hard part, the part I'd been rehearsing on the walk and fumbling now that it mattered. "I need to know about the court. The terms. How sales work, how they break. You witnessed one. You know more than any book will tell me, and books lie anyway—they're written by people with something to sell."

Bramble turned from the window. In the firelight her face was all creases and contradiction, fear and something fiercer wrestling behind her eyes.

"And what will you do with it? Knowledge is a seed, girl. It grows whatever you plant it in. Revenge grows one crop. Rescue grows another."

"Rescue," I said. It came out steadier than I expected. Maybe because it was the first true thing I'd said all day. "Juniper's been down there thirteen moons because adults kept deciding things for her. The guild decided. Corvane decided. I'm not going to be one more adult making decisions over her head. But I am going to get her back, and I'd like to do it without feeding anyone else to the court's ledgers."

The rosemary perked up. I swear it did. Leaned toward me like it was listening.

Bramble saw it too. Something in her face eased—not softened, exactly; eased, like a fist unclenching into an open hand.

"Well," she said. "The plant likes you. That's not nothing. Plants are honest in a way people manage to avoid being." She came back to her chair but didn't sit; she hovered over it, vibrating with decision. "Here's what I know. Court contracts are written in debt-language, older than the guild's. Every clause is a scale, and every scale wants balancing. Nothing down there is given and nothing is taken—everything is *traded*. Which means every sale has a seam. A place where the scales could tip the other way, if you brought the right weight."

"A loophole," Wren said, with the reverence other people reserve for scripture.

"A seam," Bramble corrected. "And seams are dangerous to go looking for, because the court writes its contracts the way spiders write webs. Touch the wrong thread and you're in the silk up to your neck." She looked at me, and her eyes were wet and furious and resolved, all at once, the exact expression I imagine I wore looking at that ledger entry. "I signed my name to that sale twelve years ago. I've owed that girl a debt ever since, and I hear you're the sort who takes debts seriously."

"I'm the sort who hears them," I said. "Whether I want to or not."

"Then hear this one." She thrust out her hand, earth-stained palm up. "I'm coming with you. To the mountain, if it comes to that. I've spent twelve years watering thorns. I'd like to grow something else."

I took her hand. Her grip was stronger than her size promised, and warm as turned soil, and something in my chest that had been clenched since the counting room loosened by a single degree. Not safety. Nothing was safe. But company—that rarest commodity, the one no ledger lists.

Wren groaned theatrically from her corner. "Wonderful. A fugitive accountant and a grandmother. We'll be the most feared crew in Cinderhollow. Hunters will flee from our sensible footwear."

"I'm fifty-eight," Bramble said, "not ancient, and I once grew a bramble hedge through a tax collector's house in under an hour. Ask the tax collector. If you can find him. He lives in the woods now and screams at birds."

Wren blinked. "I take it back. I'm terrified. This is the best day of my life."

* * *

We spent the night in Bramble's cellar, which was less a cellar than an underground second garden—root vegetables in dark beds, mushrooms glowing faintly blue along one wall like a constellation that had gotten lost, and a smell of deep earth that felt older than the city above it. Bramble fed us nettle soup and bread with honey, and while Wren slept curled against a sack of turnips like she'd been born to it, Bramble talked.

She talked about the court in the careful voice of someone defusing something. About the pale nobles who counted moons on calendars of bone. About time pooling down there like water in a cellar, slow and cold and drinkable, if you didn't mind what it did to you. About souls kept in lanterns, and how the light changed color depending on what the soul missed most.

"What color was hers?" I asked. "If you know. If witnesses ever—"

"I don't know hers." Bramble's voice gentled. "But I know the lore. Blue for home. Gold for love. White for a self, for the person they used to be." She looked at me across the mushroom-light. "Thirteen moons is a long time to burn, August. Whatever you bring her back to, make sure it's worth the color she's been."

I didn't sleep much after that. I lay on the cellar floor listening to the house breathe and the garden whisper—because Bramble's garden did whisper, all night, a low green murmur like a congregation murmuring amen—and I made plans and unmade them and made them again.

Near dawn, footsteps on the cellar stairs woke me properly. Bramble, descending with a lamp, her face tight.

"Up," she whispered. "Now. There's a hunter at the end of the row. With a hound. And August—" she hesitated, and I learned in that moment that Bramble's face had never once lied to a child with a fever, and was not going to start lying now, "—he's knocking on doors. Working up the row. Door by door."

Wren was awake instantly, no groaning now, all business. "Back way?"

"There's a back way," Bramble said, "but it goes through my garden, and my garden—" she paused, and the ghost of something wilder than a hedge-witch crossed her face, "—my garden doesn't like strangers. Hold my hands. Both of you. And whatever touches you in there, don't pull away. Pulling away is how people lose fingers."

We went up through the kitchen, past the rosemary—which had grown overnight, I noticed, tall enough to brush the window, as if it had been standing guard—and out the back door into a wall of green.

It wasn't a garden. It was a decision Bramble had made over thirty years and never explained to anyone, a tangle of hawthorn and briar and climbing beans and things I had no names for, growing dense as a fortress and twice as hostile. Bramble took my hand and Wren's and stepped in, and the plants *moved*. Branches drew aside like curtains held by servants. Thorns turned outward, away from us, toward whatever might follow.

Behind us, faint through the green, I heard the front knocker. Then voices. Then, cutting through it all, a sound that raised every hair I owned: the low iron rattle of a hound's chain, and the hound's voice, a baying that sounded less like a dog and more like a bell tolling for something.

And beneath it—because I always hear it, God help me, I always hear it—I heard the hunter's debt. Faint, at the edge of my gift's reach, but clear in shape: an obligation owed, and the creditor's name attached like a scent.

Corvane. The hunter owed Corvane. Or Corvane owed the hunter—or they owed each other, tangled like briars, the guild and the crown wound together so tightly you couldn't tell which vine was strangling which.

"They're not hunting the gifted," I said aloud, as understanding settled over me cold as well-water. "They're collecting. The crown gets its scapegoats, and Corvane gets—" I stopped, because the rest of the thought wouldn't come yet, only its shape, and its shape was terrible.

"Gets what?" Wren demanded, stumbling beside me through the breathing green.

"I don't know yet. But he's keeping a ledger inside the ledger, and I'm going to read it if it's the last accounting I ever do."

Bramble squeezed my hand hard. Ahead, the briars parted onto a lane behind the row, grey with early light, empty—for now.

"Then we'd better move," she said, "because I know a blind singer who remembers everything this city's forgotten, and if anyone knows what Corvane's been hiding, it's the woman who keeps the songs."

The hound bayed again behind us, closer, and we ran.
