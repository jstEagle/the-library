## 8. The Hunt Begins

The hounds arrived on a Tuesday, which felt rude. Even catastrophe should have the decency to wait for the weekend.

I heard them before I saw them—the particular clatter of iron paws on cobblestone, because of course the crown's hunters had iron hounds. Not flesh-and-blood dogs with warm breath and dumb loyalty. No, these were cast in one piece, jointed at the neck and hips like puppets for a god who'd lost interest halfway through, and they made a sound when they moved that I will be hearing in my nightmares until I die: a low, ringing toll, like a bell struck underwater.

They were hunting us. Hunting anyone whose blood carried a gift. And they had come to Bramble's door.

"They're three streets out," I said, pressing my palm flat against the window frame of Bramble's back room. "Six hunters. Four hounds. They're stopping at every house, checking doors."

Bramble looked up from her mortar, where she'd been grinding something that smelled like a meadow having a bad day. "Checking doors how?"

"Marking them." I'd seen it through the gap in the shutters as the sweep crawled up Copperlane—each door touched with a smear of white chalk, a sigil like an eye squeezed shut. Doors marked clean got left alone. Doors unmarked got opened. "It's some kind of ward-test. The chalk reacts to gifted blood inside."

"Chalk," Wren said from the rafters, where she'd been sleeping upside down like a bat with opinions. "They're finding us with *chalk*."

"It's not just chalk." Sable sat by the cold hearth, her blind eyes turned toward the window as if she could hear the hunt coming—which, to be fair, she probably could, three days ahead of schedule. "It's grave-chalk. Ground bone and consecrated lime. It remembers what it was."

"Wonderful," Wren said. "So we're being hunted by stationery."

"We're being hunted by the crown," Bramble said quietly, "and the chalk is the least of it. Those hounds don't track scent. They track *debt*."

Everyone looked at me. I hated that. I was getting used to hating it.

"They can't," I said. But even as I said it, I was listening—and beneath the iron toll of their approach, beneath the boots and the creak of leather harness, I caught it. A hum. The same hum I heard from every ledger entry, every unpaid account, every promise waiting to be kept or broken. The hounds were full of it. They rang with debt the way a struck cup rings with water.

"Oh," I said. "Oh, that's clever. That's *vile*. Someone taught them to smell what people owe."

"And who do you owe, August?" Sable asked softly. "All of you. Think. Everyone owes something—to a neighbor, a god, a dead parent, themselves. The hounds don't care about guilt. They care about the *weight*."

I thought about my own ledger of obligations, the one no guild ever recorded. I owed Vesper thirteen years of silence I didn't understand. I owed Juniper a childhood I'd failed to protect her from. I owed Bramble my life twice over since the guildhouse, and Wren—I owed Wren for the vault, for the alliance, for the way she'd looked at the hunt-mark on my door and chosen *us* instead of the reward money.

By the hounds' arithmetic, I must have glowed like a festival lantern.

"Back door," Bramble said, already moving, sweeping tinctures into a satchel with the brisk economy of a woman who had evacuated before. "Through the garden. There's a drainage culvert under the wall that comes up behind the tannery. It smells like sin, but it works."

"What about the shop?" Wren dropped from the rafters, landing soft as an apology. "Your whole life is in this shop."

"My whole life," Bramble said, pulling the satchel strap across her chest, "is standing in this room arguing about a shop. The rest is replaceable."

But her hands, I noticed, lingered one extra second on the doorframe as we left—on the little carved sprig of rowan above the lintel, worn smooth by years of her thumb touching it for luck on her way out. She didn't touch it for luck now. She touched it goodbye.

* * *

The culvert smelled exactly as promised. I have been in the Gilded Root's vaults where bottled grief sweats through its glass, and I would take that over tannery runoff again without hesitation. Wren made it four steps before starting a running commentary that none of us asked for and all of us needed.

"When this is over," she said, hunched double in the dark, "I'm opening a bathhouse. A nice one. Marble floors. Free towels. And absolutely no drainage culverts, iron dogs, or crowns anywhere within a day's ride."

"You've never worked an honest day," I said.

"I'm working one right now. This counts. Crawling through murder-water for a woman I met four days ago while committing burglary together—that's labor, August. That's practically a trade."

"Guild," Bramble corrected absently, ahead of us in the dark. "A shared illegal enterprise is a guild."

"See? She gets it. Hedge-witches know things."

Sable brought up the rear, walking the culvert's center line without a stumble, her hand trailing the wall not for balance but, I suspected, for reading. She'd told us once that stone held echoes the way wax held seals—press it right and you could lift the shape of everything that had passed. I tried not to think about what the culvert walls knew about us.

We came up behind the tannery into grey afternoon light and the sound of the hunt, closer now, working its way along Copperlane toward Bramble's street. From the roofline of the tannery's drying sheds we could see it: six hunters in crown grey moving door to door, chalk in hand, and behind them the hounds.

Up close—or as close as I wanted—they were worse than the sound suggested. Each one stood the height of a pony, cast iron gone black with oil, their heads smooth except for two slits where eyes should be. They didn't pant. They didn't sniff. They simply stopped at each door, tilted their heads, and *listened*, the way I listened to ledgers. Then the hunter beside them chalked the door, or didn't.

And then I heard the leader.

He stood apart from his men, tall, grey-gloved, face like a closed ledger himself. And when my gift reached for him—because it always reaches, I can't help it, hearing debts is like hearing your own name called across a market square—what came back nearly put me on my knees.

Not a debt *to* him. A debt *from* him. A great coiled weight of obligation, and threaded through it, unmistakable, a signature I knew better than my own handwriting.

Corvane. The guild master's mark was woven through the man's debt like a colored thread through grey wool. He *owed Corvane*. Owed him enormously, recently, and the terms of it sang out to my ear with horrible clarity: *names for names.* The Gilded Root was feeding the crown its list of the gifted, and this man was the collection agent.

The guild hadn't just abandoned its own to the hunt. The guild was *running* it.

"August?" Bramble's hand on my arm. "You've gone white as the chalk."

"Their leader," I managed. "He's carrying a debt to Corvane. A big one. Paid in names." I watched the hunters work down the street, door by door, and understood with sick certainty that the list in that man's gloved hand had been written in a counting room I used to balance books in. My entries. My columns. Had my own handwriting helped point the hounds?

Below us, the sweep reached a door I recognized—a cobbler's, where a boy lived who could mend anything broken, crockery and bones alike, if you didn't ask how. The hound stopped. Tilted its head. Listened.

Then it screamed.

There's no other word for it. The iron jaw split open—not hinged, *split*, like the whole head was a bell cracking—and out came a sound that wasn't sound so much as a hole in sound, and every debt the boy had ever owed came pouring off him like smoke drawn through a chimney. I heard them from the rooftop. His mother's medicine, unpaid. His master's tools, borrowed. Three years of small kindnesses he'd never returned. The hound drank it all down and the hunters went through the door.

"No," Bramble breathed.

"Hold." Sable's voice cut like wire. "If you go down there, you die with him, and he'll still be taken. That's the design of it. That's why they hunt loud."

She was right, and I hated her, and I loved her, and I held the edge of the roof so hard the old wood groaned. Below, the cobbler's boy came out between two hunters, wrists bound with the same grave-chalk rope, and he wasn't screaming. He was looking back at his house. At his mother in the doorway, who was doing the hardest thing any parent ever does, which is nothing.

"Remember his face," Sable said quietly. "All of you. Remember it. Rage fades unless you give it a face to hold onto."

As if we could forget. As if my gift would let me forget—I could still hear the boy's debts fading down the street, a diminishing chime, an account being carried away to collections.

Wren spoke first, and there was no joke in it. "So what do we do?"

"We get off this roof," I said, "before someone hears *my* debts ringing."

* * *

We didn't make it off clean. Of course we didn't. The city had other plans.

The culvert route was compromised—crown men at the tannery end, chalk marks already blooming on the walls like mold—so we went overland, roof to roof through the ragged spine of Copperlane, Bramble moving better than a woman her age had any right to, Sable guided by Wren's hand and her own impossible ears. We were two streets from the shrine district, from the abandoned chapel of Vesper where we'd been sheltering, when the hounds changed direction.

All four of them. At once. Like compass needles swinging north.

Toward us.

"They've caught our scent," I gasped, hauling Bramble over a gap between roofs. "Or our *debts*. They've heard us."

"Then we give them something louder to listen to." Wren had stopped at the ridge of a dyer's roof, and she was grinning, and I want it noted for posterity that I have never in my life seen anyone grin at four iron hounds converging on their position. She pulled something from around her neck—a charm, a knotted thing of black thread and a bent nail and what might have been a rabbit's foot if rabbits had feet like that. Her gift. Her curse. The unluckiness she'd been born drowning in, learned to hold like a held breath, and could throw.

"Wren—" I started.

"Count of three," she said. "One."

"Wren, whatever you're about to do—"

"Two."

"—at least tell us where to *stand*—"

"Three."

She flung the charm straight up into the air between us and the hounds, and the world broke.

I don't have a better word. Bad luck isn't dramatic, not really—it's small, and relentless, and everywhere at once. The tile under my feet chose that moment to slide. The chimney beside Wren shed a brick. A washing line snapped somewhere below and a sheet billowed up into the path of the lead hunter, blinding him at exactly the wrong step. The lead hound's front leg seized mid-stride—some flaw in the casting, some bubble in the iron finally giving way—and it went down in a shriek of metal, ploughing a furrow through the cobbles, and the hound behind it tripped over the first one, and the third collided with a fruit cart that had no business being on that street, and the fourth—

The fourth made it through. Head down, jaw splitting, that bell-crack scream building, aimed at Bramble.

Bramble did what Bramble does.

She didn't run. She dropped to one knee on the rooftop, slapped her bare palm against the gutter, and *grew*. I felt it more than saw it—the whole street shivering with green pressure—and then the ivy that had spent thirty years politely decorating the dyer's wall decided, at its mistress's request, to stop being polite. It came off the wall like a wave, took the fourth hound around the legs, around the throat, around the eye-slits, and *held*, vines thickening as they tightened, wood creaking against iron.

Iron doesn't strangle. But iron anchored in forty feet of living ivy doesn't chase, either.

"Move," Bramble said, in a voice I hadn't heard from her before—low, rooted, ancient. "Now. While my garden has it busy."

We ran. Over the last roofs, down through a cooper's yard, through the shrine district's crooked lanes, until we collapsed in the shadow of Vesper's ruined chapel with our lungs full of knives.

And that's when we saw the smoke.

Not from the direction of the hounds. From behind us. From Copperlane. From a thin, black column rising over the rooftops with the unhurried confidence of a fire that knows it has time.

Bramble went very still.

"That's not the shrine district," Wren said uselessly. "That's—"

"Copperlane," Bramble said. "Third street. Where the shops are."

Nobody said the rest. Nobody had to. We ran anyway, because hope is stupid and we were all stupid with it, and we came around the corner onto Bramble's street in time to see her shop—the shop, her life, thirty years of tinctures and salves and the rowan sprig over the door—guttering like a candle in a windstorm.

No hunters stood watching it. That was the part that told me everything. Hunters arrest; arsonists leave. Two figures in plain cloaks had done this and walked away, and as they turned the far corner, one of them glanced back, and my gift caught him the way a hook catches a fish.

Debt to Corvane. Fresh. Small, compared to the hunter-leader's, but the same thread, the same signature. *Burn the hedge-witch's shop. Leave nothing she can regrow.*

They weren't hunting us randomly. They were following a list, and the list was written by the man who raised me, and he had just crossed out Bramble's name the only way he knew how.

Bramble didn't cry. She stood in the street with ash drifting down around her like terrible snow, and she watched her life burn, and her face did something worse than crying. It did arithmetic. I could see her counting what was lost, item by item, and filing each loss somewhere deep and green and patient, the way soil files a seed through winter.

"The rowan over the door," she said at last, conversationally, to no one. "My grandmother planted that tree. They burned the cutting."

"Bramble—" I started.

"Don't." She held up a hand. Ash settled on her knuckles. "Don't apologize. Don't comfort me. Just tell me what you heard, August. All of it. Who gave the order. What they were paid. Every name, every debt, every term." Her eyes, when they found mine, were the eyes of something that had decided to stop being a herb garden and start being a forest fire. "Because I have spent fifty years growing things, child. I am *very* good at patience. And I have just been given a reason to practice the other kind."

So I told her. The hunter-leader's debt, vast and coiled, signed in Corvane's thread. The arsonists' smaller payments. The list. *Names for names.* The guild that raised me, feeding the crown's hunt one gifted soul at a time—buying its own safety with ours, and paying down older debts besides, debts I was only beginning to guess the size of.

When I finished, Wren spat into the ash. Sable turned her blind face toward the burning shop and began, very softly, to hum—a low dirge-note, memorizing it, adding it to whatever archive she carried behind those clouded eyes.

And I stood in the falling ash of a woman's life and felt my rage do what rage does when it finally finds its floor: it stopped burning wild and started burning *downward*, into something dense and black and structural, like coal, like a seam of it running under everything I was.

Corvane had my sister's contract in his vault. He had the crown's hunters on his string. He had a debt due beneath the mountain when the moon completed its thirteenth turn, and I had eleven nights to reach the court before he did.

Eleven nights to learn how to walk down a stair that costs memories. Eleven nights to find a singer who remembered too much and a god who'd forgotten how to speak.

Eleven nights.

"Come on," I said, and my voice came out flat and hard and new. "We have a stair to find."

Behind us, Bramble's shop folded inward with a sigh, and the rowan sprig over the door—blackened, cracked, but not destroyed—dropped into the ash at her feet.

She picked it up and put it in her pocket, and we went.
