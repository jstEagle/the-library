## 9. Songs for the Silent

The candle led us through the city like a rumor.

It had no business burning. I'd found it in the ruined shrine of Vesper three days ago, a stub of votive wax that should have guttered out decades ago, and it had been burning ever since—patiently, stubbornly, the way old debts burn. And wherever we carried it, it leaned. Not flickered. *Leaned*, like a dog on a scent, its flame bending toward something only it could see.

"It's doing that thing again," Wren said, walking backward in front of me so she could watch the flame without watching where she was going. This was, I'd learned, an accurate summary of Wren's entire approach to life. "Left. It wants left."

"The candle wants left," Bramble repeated from behind me. She said things like that sometimes—repeating them slowly, as if tasting them for rot. "A candle with opinions."

"Everything in this city has opinions," Wren said. "Most of them are about me, and most of them are bad."

"That's not an opinion," I said. "That's your gift. You're a walking consensus."

Wren grinned at me over her shoulder, and I felt the small, dangerous warmth of it—the warmth of people who had, in the space of four days, become the sort of people you check the exits for. Bramble's shop was ash. The hunters' iron hounds had our scents now, and somewhere in the Gilded Root's counting room, Corvane was feeding my name to the crown one careful spoonful at a time. We were fugitives with a heretical candle, a hedge-witch whose garden grew out of a wheelbarrow, and a pickpocket who could make a bridge collapse under the right tax collector.

And still the candle leaned left.

We went left.

* * *

The leaning got worse as we climbed into the Weft—the old district above the tanneries, where the houses leaned against each other like drunkards keeping each other upright. Nobody rich lived here. Nobody official came here. Which meant, historically, that everything interesting happened here.

The flame bent so hard toward a doorway that wax began to run sideways down the stub, pooling against my knuckles, warm as a held hand.

"This one," I said.

The doorway belonged to a building that had once been a shrine—smaller than Vesper's, humbler, its carved lintel worn to suggestion. A sign hung beside it on a leather cord: no god here, just songs. Someone had a sense of humor. Or a sense of honesty. In this city those were often the same thing.

Bramble touched the lintel and frowned. "I know this place. There used to be singing every seventh night. When I first came to the city." She paused. "Twenty years ago, maybe more."

"And then?" Wren asked.

"And then nothing. It stopped. Everything stops, eventually." Bramble said it without bitterness, which somehow made it worse.

Inside, the shrine was one room, high-ceilinged, lit by a hundred candles that were all, I noted with a lurch, the same kind as mine. Votive stubs. Old ones. Burning without any visible means of support, arranged along the walls in rows like a congregation that had decided to stay after the service ended forever.

In the center, on a plain wooden stool, sat a woman.

She was older than Bramble, younger than the walls, wrapped in gray wool despite the summer heat. Her eyes were closed, and they stayed closed when we entered—which is how I knew, before anything else, that they had been closed for a very long time. There's a difference between someone not looking at you and someone who has made peace with never looking again.

"You brought it back," she said. Her voice did something strange in the room. It didn't echo. It *lingered*, the way a bell lingers, the way a name lingers in a house after the person has gone. "Three days ago you took it from Vesper's altar, and tonight you brought it home. Thank you. It was getting anxious."

"Candles get anxious," Wren whispered to Bramble.

"Mine does," I said, and stepped forward. "You knew I took it?"

"I heard it leave." She turned her face toward me—not quite toward me; slightly off, the way the blind do, or maybe the way she did, and I couldn't tell which. "Every candle in this room heard it leave. They've been waiting twenty years for someone to carry fire out of that shrine instead of into it."

"My name is August Montrose."

"I know what your name costs," she said, and my skin went cold. "Sit down, debt-hearer. This will take a while, and standing makes people brave, and brave is the wrong shape for what you're about to hear."

I didn't sit. But I stopped standing quite so bravely.

"Wren," Bramble murmured, "the door."

"Already thinking it," Wren murmured back, and I heard her settle against it, casual as furniture, one thumb hooked in her belt near the little bone charm she wore. Our whole arsenal, such as it was.

The woman smiled faintly, as if she'd heard the whole negotiation. Perhaps she had. "My name is Sable. I sing what happened. That's the gift, if you want to call it that—I keep memories the way other people keep coins, and I can give them back. Sing them back. Yours included."

"I don't want mine sung," I said. "I want answers. About Vesper. About why he went silent."

"They're the same question." She folded her hands in her lap. "But fine. Ask your questions first. People always need to ask their questions before they can hear their answers. It's a flaw in the design."

I pulled up a stool—there were several, stacked against the wall, dustless, as if recently used—and sat across from her. Up close she smelled of beeswax and something older underneath, stone and rain.

"Who are you to Vesper?"

"A singer. Once, his singers filled this room. We sang the year's debts to him so he wouldn't have to listen to them alone. A god of accounts needs choir, August. Even silence gets lonely." Her voice caught on the last word, smoothed itself. "When he went silent, we kept singing anyway. For years. Then one by one we stopped, because there's only so long you can perform for a wall. I'm the last one who still comes. I sing to the candles. They remember better than people do."

"Why did he go silent?"

Sable tilted her head. "You've asked everyone, haven't you. The priests. The archives. Corvane, even—he'd have told you anything you wanted, back when you were useful."

Heat crawled up my neck. "He told me gods go silent when they're done with the world."

"Mm. And you believed him, because believing it hurt less than the alternative." She unfolded her hands. "Here is what I know, and I'll warn you now—it isn't the whole of it. What I have is half a song. The other half isn't mine to give. It's yours. It's been yours for twenty years, locked up tight, and you've walked past it every day of your life like a door you've decided isn't there."

"Then sing me your half," I said. "And stop talking about doors."

For a moment she just breathed. Then she reached beside her stool and lifted a small drum—not a drum, exactly; a shallow bowl of dark wood with a skin stretched across it, scarred with use—and set it in her lap. She didn't strike it. She just rested her palms on it, and began to hum.

The humming was wrong. Or rather, it was too *right*. It slid under my ribs and found the place where I keep the things I don't look at directly, and it started, gently, horribly, to unpack them.

And then she sang.

* * *

I have heard the guild's memory-brokers describe what a stolen memory feels like from the outside: cold, weightless, secondhand. This was none of those things. This was *mine*. The stone of the shrine floor under my knees. The smell of incense and wet wool. I was nine years old again, and I was kneeling at Vesper's altar, and I was screaming.

Not crying. Screaming. The way children scream when the world has broken in a place adults insist is unbreakable.

*I couldn't see myself. I could feel myself—the small fists, the raw throat—but the vision gave me no mirror. Only the altar, and the cold, and the voice of the priest trying to pull me back, and beyond it all the presence at the heart of the shrine: vast, patient, attentive. Listening.*

*Because listening was what Vesper did. He was the god of what is owed. He heard every debt spoken in the world, every promise, every betrayal, every coin that changed hands carrying its little freight of obligation. The priests said he never slept because the world never stopped owing.*

*And I knelt there with my sister's fever burning her alive two streets away, and I screamed my offer at him:*

*"Take it. Take the debt. Take the pain—hers, mine, all of it, I'll pay anything, I'll pay YOU—"*

*And the presence listened. And listened. And did not answer.*

*"You HEAR everything!" My fists on the altar stone. Blood, maybe; I remember the sting. "You hear every debt in the world, you hear pennies changing hands, and you won't hear ME? She's DYING. Take it! TAKE IT OR—"*

*And here the song slowed, and Sable's voice dropped into a register that made the candles lean, and the child's voice on the altar said the words I have spent twenty years not remembering:*

*"—or I vow you silent. I vow it. No one will owe you anything ever again. No one will speak a debt in your hearing. I'll spend my whole life making sure. I'll be your silence, Vesper. I swear it on my own name."*

*A child's oath. A child's vow, sworn in grief and fury, on the one currency a god of debts cannot refuse—because a freely given vow is the one coin in the world that must be honored, no matter how small the hand that pays it.*

*The presence at the heart of the shrine went still.*

*And then, softly, like a great door closing in a far room, the listening stopped.*

*The fever broke that same night. Juniper lived. And Vesper never spoke again.*

* * *

I came back to myself on the floor of the shrine, which was undignified, and with Bramble's hands on my shoulders, which was worse, because it meant I'd needed catching.

Wren was crouched in front of me, uncharacteristically quiet, holding out a water flask. I took it. My hands shook enough to make the water slosh audibly, which in the silence sounded like applause.

"No," I said.

"Yes," Sable said gently.

"No. That's not—that's not how it works. Gods don't break because a *child* throws a tantrum. He's a god. He hears every debt in the world. One vow from one nine-year-old doesn't—"

"He heard every debt in the world," Sable agreed. "Including yours. Especially yours. Do you understand what you swore, August? You didn't curse him. You *paid* him. You paid him your whole life's voice, given freely, in exchange for your sister's life—and a freely given payment must be honored. He didn't abandon you. He accepted your terms. All of them. Including the part where the one who owed him would grow up hating him for the silence she bought."

The room tilted. I gripped the flask.

"But that's insane," I whispered. "He could have refused. He refuses things all the time—people pray for terrible things, people offer—"

"People offer what they don't mean," Sable said. "You meant it. Every word. You would have spent your life making the world deaf to him, and he knew it, and he loved you too much to let you become that. So he took the smaller price. He let you believe he'd abandoned you, because the alternative was letting a grieving child grow into a woman who'd burned down a god out of spite." She paused. "Though I'll grant you, the arrangement has had its costs on his side too. Twenty years of silence. Twenty years of watching you hate him. He tended your candle every night, debt-hearer. It was the only conversation you'd allow."

I thought of the votive stub burning impossibly in a cold shrine, and something in my chest cracked along a seam I hadn't known was load-bearing.

"Juniper," I said. My voice came out flat and strange. "Her fever broke. Because of me. Because I—"

"Half-truths," Sable said, and her voice hardened, the bell-tone gaining an edge. "That's what I warned you of. Listen carefully now, because this is the part I cannot sing whole, and half-songs have killed better people than either of us."

She set the bowl-drum aside and leaned forward.

"Your sister lived. Yes. But the vow was heard by more than Vesper. A freely given vow rings through every court that trades in souls—and there is a court beneath this mountain that heard a child offer *anything* for her sister, and filed it away. Thirteen moons ago, when the Gilded Root sold a soul in their direction, someone remembered that old ringing. Someone recognized the shape of the family. Your vow saved Juniper once, August. I think—I *think*—it painted the mark on her that made her worth taking twice."

The bottom fell out of the room.

Bramble made a sound low in her throat. Wren said, very quietly, "Oh, that's *filthy*."

"So it's my fault." The words tasted like copper. "Thirteen moons ago. The sale. It traced back to—"

"No." Sable's voice cracked like a whip, and the candles all flared at once. "Don't you dare finish that sentence. A child's grief is not a contract. Whatever court twisted it, whatever guild brokered it, whatever man signed it—the sin is theirs. But you need to know the shape of the thread, because you're going to meet the ones who pulled it, and they will tell you it was fate, or justice, or your own doing. It was none of those. It was *appetite*. Remember that."

I remembered it. I wrote it somewhere behind my eyes in letters I suspected would outlast most of my other memories.

Outside, somewhere below the Weft, a bell began to ring—not the hour bell; the wrong rhythm, the quick triple-strike the criers used. Hunt movement. Wren was at the window before the third strike, flattened against the wall, peering down through the gap in the shutters.

"Torches," she reported. "Four streets down, moving toward the tannery road. Iron hounds, two of them. They're not searching, they're *sweeping*." She looked back at me. "August. They're sweeping toward us. How?"

Nobody answered, because we all arrived at it together, and the arriving felt like ice water.

The candle. My candle—Vesper's candle, burning twenty years of tended devotion, a beacon of the old god's fire carried openly through a city hunting the gifted. To hunters with iron hounds and crown-sanctioned senses, we might as well have been dragging a bonfire.

"Can you put it out?" Bramble asked.

I looked at the stub in my hand. Small. Stubborn. Burning the way it had burned for twenty years in an empty shrine, faithful past all reason.

"No," I said. "But I know someone who might tell us where it *wants* to go."

Sable was already rising from her stool, wrapping her gray wool tighter, her blind eyes bright and aimed at nothing and everything. She picked up her bowl-drum and slung its cord over her shoulder.

"Well," she said. "Twenty years I've sung to candles. I suppose it's time one of them sang back. Lead on, debt-hearer. And August—"

She turned her face to me, and for a moment the lingering quality of her voice filled the whole shrine, and every candle flame stood perfectly straight, as if at attention.

"When we find the stair—and we will find the stair—remember that the toll is memories. Choose beforehand what you can afford to lose. Because the mountain takes what you hesitate over longest."

The candles guttered, all together, like a held breath released.

"And I already know," I said, "which memory I can't afford to lose."

"Yes," Sable said softly. "That's why you'll lose it first."
