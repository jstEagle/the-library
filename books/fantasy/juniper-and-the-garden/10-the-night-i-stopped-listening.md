## 10. The Night I Stopped Listening

Sable's safehouse was a cellar beneath a shuttered dyeworks, which meant it smelled like a fight between a tannery and a flower shop. Wren had complained about the smell for the entire walk down. Bramble had pointed out that if Sable could hide from crown hunters for six years under a dye house, she'd earned whatever smell she liked. Wren had then complained about Bramble.

I stood in the doorway and listened to them bicker, and I thought: this is what a family sounds like when it's still deciding whether to happen.

Sable herself sat in the middle of the room on a stool that had been mended so many times it was more rope than wood. She was older than I'd expected—silver shot through her dark braids, her hands folded in her lap like a woman waiting for tea that would never come. Her eyes were closed. They stayed closed even when she turned her face toward me with the unerring accuracy of a compass needle finding north.

"August Montrose," she said. "You walk like someone counting steps."

"I count everything," I said. "It's a professional hazard."

"Ledger-keeper." She smiled without much humor. "The Gilded Root taught you to hear debts. But they never taught you to hear yourself, did they."

Wren, sprawled against a barrel, went very still. Bramble set down the kettle she'd been fussing with. Even the candle flames seemed to lean in.

I kept my voice level, because level was all I had left. "You said you remembered something about Vesper. About the night he went silent. I came to hear it."

"You came to hear it," Sable agreed. "But you didn't come to *listen*. There's a difference. One is weather. The other is standing in the rain and letting it soak you through." She patted the floor across from her. "Sit down, girl. This is going to take a while, and my knees aren't what they were, but yours will be."

I sat. It seemed safer than arguing with a blind woman who could apparently read posture.

* * *

"Before I sing anything," Sable said, "you should know how memory-song works. I don't tell stories. Stories are lies with good posture. What I do is give back what was taken—or buried, or burned, or hidden behind a wall a child built so she could keep walking." Her head tilted. "You built a very good wall, August Montrose. Masonry work. I'm almost impressed."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"No," she said gently. "That's rather the point."

Bramble crouched beside me. "August. When I treated your fevers—when you were small—you talked in your sleep sometimes. About a garden. About a voice. And once, only once, you woke up screaming at something no one else could see, saying *take it back, I didn't mean it, take it back.*" She touched my shoulder. "I've wondered for twenty years what you wanted taken back."

"And now we find out," Wren said quietly. All the flippancy had gone out of her voice, which frightened me more than the hunters had.

Sable began to hum.

It wasn't a melody I recognized, and yet every hair on my arms rose like it had been waiting years for exactly these notes. The sound filled the cellar the way water fills a cup—not rushing, just rising, patient, inevitable. And then, somewhere inside the humming, I stopped hearing Sable's voice and started hearing mine.

A child's voice. My voice.

* * *

*I am seven years old, and my sister is dying.*

*Not dying the way people say it in front of children—sleeping, resting, going on a long journey. Dying the way the fever actually is: Juniper small and burning in the bed we share, our mother wringing a cloth in water gone gray, our father gone three days to fetch a physician who will cost more than we have. The room smells of vinegar and smoke and the sweet-rot smell underneath that I am not supposed to recognize but do, because I hear things. I have always heard things.*

*I hear what people owe. It started before I can remember—the whispers that follow strangers down the street, the ledger-voices that murmur from coins and contracts and promises. Mother says it's a blessing. Mother says it quietly, in the tone she uses for things that are also curses.*

*Tonight the whole city owes something, and none of it matters, because the debt I care about is the one I can't collect. Juniper owes nothing. She is seven years old and kind to stray cats and she owes the world nothing, and the world is taking her anyway.*

*So I go where children go when the world takes things.*

*There is a shrine two streets over, tucked between a cooper's yard and a wall nobody claims. It isn't grand. Vesper was never a grand god—he's the god of dusk, of endings done gently, of accounts settled fair. His shrine is a stone alcove with a worn bench and an altar where anyone might leave a candle, and his silence is famous. He doesn't speak to priests. He doesn't send omens. He listens, the old women say. He is the god who hears debts, and who never lets one go unpaid unfairly.*

*A god who hears debts. Like me.*

*I have been coming here since I was five. I talk to him the way other children talk to dogs—confiding, one-sided, certain of being understood. Tonight I kneel on the cold stone and I put both hands flat on the altar and I say:*

*"Take it."*

*The words come out ragged. "Take it from her. Take the fever. Take the pain. You're the god of fair accounts—she doesn't owe this. Nobody could owe this. So take it, or take it from me instead, I'll carry it, I'll carry anything, just—"*

*And here is the thing about gods, the thing no hymn tells you: sometimes they answer.*

*Not with thunder. With weight. The air above the altar thickens, and I feel him arrive the way you feel someone enter a room behind you—presence, attention, vast and quiet and *listening*. And into my chest, soft as a moth landing, comes an answer I understand without language:*

***No.***

*Not cruelty. I feel that too, the way you feel warmth through a wall. It is grief-shaped, his refusal. It is the shape of every healer who has ever had to say the fever must run its course. Some debts cannot be transferred. Some pains belong where they fall. If he takes hers, the account of the world itself goes crooked, and crooked accounts unravel everything—this is what pours into me in that single wordless syllable, the whole terrible arithmetic of a god who keeps honest books.*

***No. And I am sorry.***

*I am seven years old. My sister is burning. Every prayer I have ever whispered into this stone has been heard by something vast and gentle, and now, when it matters most, it says no.*

*Something in me does not break. Something in me decides.*

*"Then I take it back," I whisper. "Everything. Every prayer. Every secret I told you. I un-give them. I curse the listening. Let your voice be silent as you let my sister burn—let no one ever hear you again, let your shrines go cold, let your name go out of mouths, until—"*

*I don't finish the curse. I can't. Because the moment the vow leaves me—a true vow, spoken by a gifted tongue, sealed with the whole furious weight of a child's love—I feel it land.*

*I feel it land on him.*

*The presence behind me makes a sound. Gods should not make sounds like that. It is not thunder; it is smaller and infinitely worse, the sound of a great voice being strangled mid-word, a bell wrapped in wool, a man struck dumb mid-apology. The air shudders. The candle gutters. And through the last thread of connection before my vow severs it forever, I hear—not words, never words—but meaning, arriving broken:*

***Wait. That is not— you misunderstand— there is another way, if you would only listen—***

*But I have stopped listening. That is the whole of it. That is the exact and entire shape of my sin. A god offered me a harder road and I refused to hear the second half of his sentence, and my gift, my cursed blessing, my ledger-ear, drove the door shut with a vow I was too young and too heartbroken to know I had no right to make.*

*The presence fades. Not leaving. Silenced. Sealed behind the wall of my own words.*

*In the morning, Juniper's fever breaks. The physician arrives to find her asking for bread. Our mother weeps and calls it a miracle, and I stand in the doorway knowing exactly what it was: coincidence, or mercy from somewhere else entirely, or simply the fever running its course as a patient god knew it would.*

*I never go back to the shrine. I build the wall brick by brick—I forget the garden, I forget the voice, I fold the night up small and bury it under ledgers and ledgers and ledgers, because numbers never ask you what you did when you were seven.*

*Only the candle stays. Once a year, without knowing why, I light a votive to a god whose name I pretend means nothing to me.*

*Old habits. Old guilt. Old love, wearing its disguise.*

* * *

The song ended.

I was on the floor. I don't remember getting there—knees on cold flagstone, palms flat, exactly the posture of a seven-year-old at an altar, and I understood with sick clarity that my body had been holding this memory's shape for twenty years, waiting for me to catch up.

Nobody spoke. Wren had stopped pretending to lounge; she sat forward with her elbows on her knees, watching me with an expression I'd have paid money never to see directed at me: pity, carefully leashed.

"So," I said. My voice came out sanded flat. "Let me make sure I have the accounting right. The god didn't abandon me. I abandoned him. He tried to refuse my bargain kindly, and I—what, threw a tantrum with cosmological consequences? Cursed the poor bastard into a twenty-year muteness because he wouldn't cheat the books for me?" I laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "He's the god of fair accounts. And I made him pay for my sister's fever with his own voice."

"August—" Bramble started.

"Don't." I held up a hand. "Don't soften it. I've spent my whole life reading other people's debts. Every contract, every hidden obligation, every soul sold thirteen moons ago—and the whole time the largest unpaid debt in the city was mine, and I couldn't hear it because I'd deafened myself to it on purpose." I looked at my hands. Ledger-keeper's hands. Ink-stained, steady, complicit. "Do you want to know the worst part? The truly exquisite part?"

Wren raised an eyebrow, which in Wren-language meant *proceed, disaster child.*

"The candle." I pushed myself upright. "Every year. I told myself it was habit. Tradition. Respect for an old local god. And some part of me—the part that built the wall—knew exactly what it was. An apology payment. Interest on a principal I refused to acknowledge. I've been servicing that debt annually for two decades like the cowardly bookkeeper I am, paying the minimum, never touching the balance."

"That's not cowardice," Bramble said firmly. "That's a child surviving something unbearable."

"It's both," I said. "That's the thing about shame. It doesn't excuse the act. It just explains why the act was committed by someone who then had to live with it." I dragged a hand down my face. "He answered me, you know. In the shrine, the other day. The candle was still burning. Someone tended it. And I felt—hope. Hope that he'd forgiven me, that he'd waited, that the silence was his choice and his grace." My laugh came out hollow this time. "And it turns out the silence was my sentence, served by him. Twenty years of a god gagged by a seven-year-old's grief, and I walked around feeling *abandoned.*"

Sable reached out and found my wrist with uncanny accuracy. Her grip was surprisingly strong. "Girl. Listen to me, because this is the part everyone gets wrong." Her blind eyes aimed at my face with terrible precision. "A vow made by a child in agony is still a vow—that's the cruelty of gifts, they don't check maturity. But you were seven. You were offered a hard truth by a god who loved you, and you were seven, and hearts that age don't have rooms big enough for 'no' said kindly. He knows that better than anyone. He's the god of fair accounts, yes?" Her mouth curved. "Fair accounts include context."

"He hasn't spoken in twenty years," I said.

"Hasn't been *heard* in twenty years," Sable corrected. "Those are different entries in the ledger. Ask yourself—if your voice was stolen by someone you loved, and you could not speak, could not explain, could do nothing but tend their candle when they weren't looking and wait for them to grow strong enough to hear the truth—how long would you wait?"

The question went through me like a blade finding the gap in armor.

Twenty years. He had waited twenty years, mute, misjudged, abandoned by the one worshipper whose prayers he'd answered every single time, and his response upon my return to his shrine had been... a candle, kept lit. A warmth in the cold. An open door with no words available to hang above it.

"He wasn't punishing me," I whispered. "He was waiting for me to come back and *listen.*"

"Now you're doing arithmetic," Sable said approvingly.

I sat with it. Shame is a strange substance—it doesn't dissolve, it settles, like sediment in wine, and the only thing to do is let it sit until you can pour off what's clear. What was clear, after a long moment, was this: I had spent my life believing myself the abandoned one, the orphan twice over, the girl the silent god forgot. And the truth was inverted, upside-down, a ledger entry posted to the wrong column for two decades.

Vesper had never gone anywhere. I had locked the door from the inside and called it a locked door from the outside.

"There's a stair," I said suddenly.

Everyone looked at me.

"Sable—your song. The memory. When the vow landed, when the connection severed—I felt the shrine. The altar. The stone under my hands." My heart was going fast now, the way it did when the numbers finally balanced. "There's old blood in the Gilded Root archives about the under-mountain court, about roads that aren't roads. And Vesper is the god of thresholds and fair passage. If any god kept a back door to that court—a stair that isn't there—it would be behind his own altar. Under his own shrine. Where his most faithful—where *I*—" My voice caught. "Where the child who broke his heart used to pray."

Wren was already on her feet. "The shrine we visited. Cold altar, burning candle, mysterious tending hands. You're saying the way down to save your sister has been sitting under our feet the whole time, guarded by the god you accidentally muted."

"When you phrase it like that I sound like a cautionary tale."

"You *are* a cautionary tale," Wren said, grinning. "You're my favorite cautionary tale."

Bramble squeezed my shoulder. Sable inclined her head, already reaching for her cloak. And I stood there in the dye-smelling dark, carrying the heaviest thing I'd ever held: the knowledge that the god I'd blamed for silence had been silenced by me, and had spent every year since keeping a light on anyway.

One debt remained unpaid. And unlike Corvane's, unlike the court's, this one I intended to settle in person—with an apology twenty years overdue, and a request I had no right to make:

*Teach me to listen. Then show me the way down.*

Sable found the door in the dark without touching the walls. "Come on, then," she said. "The moon won't wait for us to finish feeling things."

She was right. We had six nights left—and a god to un-silence, a stair to find, and a sister to steal back from the pale nobles below the mountain.

Starting, I supposed, with an apology.
