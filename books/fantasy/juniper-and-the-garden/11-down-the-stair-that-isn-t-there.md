## 11. Down the Stair That Isn't There

Sable sang us down into the dark, which is the sort of sentence I never expected to write in a ledger and now can't stop hearing in my own head.

The shrine of Vesper sat where it had always sat, at the crook of the river road where the city forgot to keep building—cracked steps, a door that hadn't opened in years, moss eating the carvings of hands that once held up the lintel. I'd walked past it a hundred times since I was small enough to believe gods answered letters. I'd never once noticed that the building was too shallow.

"There's no room in there for anything but the altar," Bramble said, frowning at it the way she frowned at blighted leaves. "I've been inside. It's one room. Where's the rest of it?"

"That's the point," Sable said. She stood with her face tipped toward the doorway, listening to something none of us could hear—or rather, listening to something only she could hear, because she'd heard it before, in a memory that wasn't hers until she made it a song. "There isn't any rest of it. Not from out here. The stair isn't *in* the shrine. The shrine is just the lid."

Wren crouched on the bottom step, turning her bad-luck charm over in her fingers—a knucklebone on a cord, polished smooth by other people's misfortunes. "So we're going to open a lid," she said. "On a god's house. And go down the stairs under it. Toward the court that eats souls." She looked up at me. "Just checking the shopping list before we commit."

"You could stay topside," I said.

"And miss this? Absolutely not. If you're all going to die stupidly, someone has to survive to make it a good story, and Sable will make it a *song*, and songs are honest in ways stories aren't." She pocketed the charm and stood. "Besides, I owe you. You didn't turn me in when you caught me in the vault, and nobody's ever not turned me in before. It's disorienting. I need to work through it."

Bramble touched my elbow—she had a way of touching people like she was taking a pulse even when she wasn't—and said nothing, which from Bramble was a whole paragraph of support.

Sable began to sing.

It wasn't a song with words, not at first. It was the sound of the night Vesper went silent, the one she'd pulled out of me two days ago like a splinter: rain on the shrine roof, my child-voice cracking with rage, the terrible quiet afterward. But as she sang, she bent it, threaded it through with something older, and the doorway of the shrine began to listen.

I don't know how else to say it. Stone listened. The moss on the lintel shivered though there was no wind. And where the back wall of the shrine should have been—if there had ever been a back wall—the air went thin as paper over water.

"Memory is a key," Sable said, letting the last note fall away. "That's what the old songs say about Vesper. He doesn't lock doors. He locks *forgetting*. You have to remember your way in." She turned her blind eyes toward me, and I felt them on me anyway; Sable's gaze had weight, like a hand on the back of your neck. "You remembered the worst night of your life so I could sing it. That's why it opens for you. That's why it'll only open for you."

"Lucky me," I said, and pushed through the wall that wasn't a wall.

* * *

The stair went down farther than the mountain had any right to be deep, and every step took something from me.

I want to be precise about that, because precision was my trade for eleven years, and if I'm precise maybe it won't feel like what it actually felt like, which was being pickpocketed by architecture.

The toll announced itself on the fourth step. A warmth behind my eyes, a sensation like a page lifting in wind—and then the taste of cinnamon was simply gone from my memory. I knew I'd loved it. I knew Juniper used to sneak cinnamon buns from the market tray and we'd split them on the shrine steps. But the *taste* itself, the warm brown bite of it, had been lifted out and left a clean hole where it lived.

I stopped. I put my hand on the wall. The stone was cool and patient.

"It takes memories," I said. My voice came out flat. Ledger-flat. "Each step. That's the toll."

"How do you know?" Wren asked.

"Because I just lost the taste of cinnamon, and I'm furious about it in a way that doesn't match losing a taste." I looked back up the stairs, at the sliver of grey evening light above. "Corvane's contract said the court trades in borrowed years and bottled grief. Of course the road there is paved with the same currency. Everything down there is paid for. Even arriving."

Bramble caught my arm. "August. We can turn around."

"We can't." I hated how steady I sounded. "Juniper has one moon-turn left. Thirteen turns and the sale goes permanent—her soul belongs to that court past any contract, past any loophole, past any clever reading of terms. We're past the twelfth turn, Bramble. There is no turning around that costs less than going forward."

Sable had gone very still on the step above me. When she spoke, her voice was gentle in the way that made me want to flinch. "Then we choose what we lose. That's the trick of it. The stair will take whatever's nearest the surface unless you hold something back. Singers know this. Grief takes the freshest things first unless you give it the oldest ones on purpose." She smiled, small and sad. "Feed it deliberately. Don't let it graze."

"So we ration our own minds," Wren said. "Wonderful. I've rationed bread, coin, and luck, but never *self*." She rubbed the knucklebone. "Fine. Rules. Everyone says out loud what they're willing to spend before each flight of stairs, so if someone comes out the bottom missing something important, the rest of us know what it was and can—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Can remind them. Later. If there is a later."

"That's grim," Bramble said.

"That's bookkeeping," I said, and something in my chest hurt and steadied at the same time. "Wren's right. It's a ledger. We write down what we pay as we pay it. So that when we climb back out, we can read ourselves back into existence." I dug out the little notebook I'd carried since I was nine years old, the one Corvane never knew about, the one with Juniper's name written in it thirteen times, one for each moon. "I'll keep the accounts."

Nobody laughed. But Wren put her hand briefly on my shoulder, and Bramble nodded, and Sable tilted her head as if she were listening to the stair itself decide whether we were worth the trouble.

We went down.

* * *

By the hundredth step I had lost the smell of rain, the name of my first teacher, and the entire summer I was twelve.

I know the shape of that loss better than the contents, which is its own particular cruelty. There's a smooth place in me where that summer used to sit. I know it was warm. I know Juniper was in it. I know that when I try to reach for it, my mind slides off like a boot on wet stone, and I am left holding the fact of happiness without a single drop of the happiness itself.

Wren gave up her memory of her mother's laugh somewhere around step sixty and refused to talk about it, which meant we all talked around it carefully for an hour, like walking around a broken floorboard.

Bramble lost the name of the flower she'd grown for her mother's grave. She cried quietly for three steps and then wiped her face and said, "Well. I'll grow a new one. A new name for a new flower. Gardens forgive," and I have never admired anyone more or told her so, because telling her would have cost both of us more than the stair already had.

Sable lost almost nothing, and that frightened me more than anything else on that stair.

"You're not paying," I said finally, when the silence got too heavy. "Everyone's bleeding and you're walking through untouched."

"I'm paying," she said. "You just can't hear it. I pay in other people's memories. Every song I carry, I carry at cost—I've sung away whole villages' worth of remembering to keep the important ones alive. The stair and I have an arrangement." Her mouth curved. "It knows I'm mostly memory already. It's not greedy for what little I've kept for myself." She reached out and found my sleeve with two fingers, steering by touch. "But here's what I hear, August, since you asked. The stair isn't taking randomly. It's taking what you offer without knowing you're offering. And you—" her fingers tightened slightly, "—you keep offering everything except what you came here to save."

"What do you mean?"

"Thirteen moons," she said softly. "One for each step of the year. You wrote her name thirteen times. And yet the stair hasn't touched a single memory of Juniper. Not one. Do you know why?"

I did know why. I knew it the way you know a debt is coming due.

"Because I'm holding them too tight," I said. "And the stair is saving them for last. For the moment I'm weakest. Because that's what debts do—they wait."

Sable said nothing. She didn't have to. The stair went on down beneath us, patient as interest accruing.

* * *

Somewhere past the two-hundredth step, the walls began to change.

The rough-cut stone smoothed. Then it gleamed. Then, faintly, it became reflective—not mirror-bright, but the dull shine of a pond at dusk, and in that shine I started seeing things that weren't reflections.

Faces. Small ones, flickering, like fish under ice. Memories that belonged to the stair itself, I suppose, or to everyone who'd ever paid it. A woman laughing in a kitchen. A boy running through wheat. An old man's hands folding a paper boat. They drifted along beside us as we descended, keeping pace, and I understood with cold clarity that these were the tolls of everyone who had come this way before us, swimming in the walls like coins at the bottom of a well.

"Does anyone else see—" Wren started.

"Yes," said Bramble.

"Don't look at them long," Sable said sharply, the sharpest I'd ever heard her. "They're not decorations. Some of them are still *aware*. The court doesn't waste what it collects. If you look too long, they'll look back, and then you're having a conversation, and conversations down here have prices."

But one of them looked at me anyway.

A girl, maybe ten, with flour on her cheek, pressing her palms against the inside of the stone as if the wall were a window. Her mouth moved. No sound came through—but I heard it anyway, because hearing is my curse and my gift and apparently my job description even on a haunted staircase:

*I just wanted to sell one bad winter. One. Nobody told me winters compound.*

I shut my eyes and kept walking. Behind me, faintly, I heard Wren whisper, "Who was that?" and Bramble answer, "Someone who came before," and neither of them said the obvious thing, which was: *someone who never came back.*

* * *

We rested on a landing that might have been a landing or might have been the stair's idea of mercy. There was a niche in the wall, and in the niche, impossibly, a candle burned. Small, white, guttering in air that shouldn't have moved.

Votive. I knew that candle. I'd lit its twin a thousand times as a child, back when I still talked to Vesper like he was a grumpy uncle instead of a silent god.

"He's down here," I said. The realization landed in my stomach like a dropped ledger. "Or his attention is. The shrine upstairs, the candle that never went out, and now this. He's been marking the path. He's been *waiting*."

"For thirteen years?" Wren scoffed, then caught my expression. "For thirteen years?"

"The silent ones wait longest," Sable said. "Silence isn't absence, August. Sometimes it's a held breath."

"Well, he can exhale after we get my sister," I said, harsher than I meant to. I stared at the candle flame. "I don't want his help. I don't want his forgiveness or his guidance or whatever this votive-light hospitality is supposed to mean. He went quiet when I needed him. Whatever reasons he had, they weren't good enough, and I'm not going to stand here being grateful for a candle."

Bramble said, gently, "No one asked you to be grateful."

"No," I agreed. "But everyone keeps looking at me like I should be getting ready to be."

The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft. Just slightly. Toward the downward stair. Like a nod. Like *this way.*

I blew it out.

The dark that followed was total for exactly one heartbeat—and then the walls' drowned faces brightened, hundreds of them, watching us with the patience of the collected, and Wren said, "Okay, that was worse, please relight the creepy god-candle," and I did, and we pretended none of it had happened, and we went on.

* * *

Near the bottom, the stair tried to take Juniper.

I felt it happen the way you feel a hand slide into your pocket. We were maybe fifty steps from whatever waited below—the air had gone strange and sweet, like fruit rotting into wine, and the bone-cold of the deep mountain seeped up through our boots—and the warmth behind my eyes rose all at once, hungry, and it went straight for the center of me.

Her laugh. The real one, the snorting, undignified one she'd deny to her grave. I felt it lift. Felt it start to peel away like bark from a branch—

"No," I said.

Not loudly. That was the terrible part. I said it the way I said numbers in the counting room, quiet and final, and I planted my feet on the step and I *held*. I gathered every memory of her—her laugh, her scowl, the gap in her teeth, the way she said my name like it was a joke only she got—and I held them the way you hold a door shut against a flood, with your whole body, with your teeth, with everything.

The stair pushed. I pushed back.

"August." Bramble's hand on my arm. "August, let me see your eyes."

"She's trying to take her," I ground out. "It's saving her for last, like I said, it's—it's waiting until I'm tired—"

"Then we're not tired," Wren said, and stepped onto the step below me, facing down the dark, arms crossed like she was daring the architecture to try something. "Take mine instead. Take another summer, take my whole useless childhood, I've got plenty. But you don't touch hers."

"Nor mine," Bramble said, and planted herself on my other side.

Sable began to sing. Low, wordless, a song with no memory in it at all—just structure, just a frame, a scaffold of sound thrown up around me like a fence. *Hold what's yours inside the song,* she'd told me once. *A memory set to music weighs more. It's harder to carry off.* She was fencing my sister's laugh with notes, and the stair pushed once more, twice, and then—like a creditor deciding a debtor wasn't worth the lawyers—it subsided.

The pressure eased. I sagged. Bramble caught most of my weight, which was generous, since I'm taller.

"It backed off," I panted. "Why would it back off? Nothing down here backs off. Everything collects."

"Maybe," Sable said slowly, still singing softly, "because it already knows how this ends. Maybe the court doesn't need to take her memories on the way down." Her voice went very quiet. "Maybe it's planning to let you offer them yourself. Freely given. That's the currency that matters most down there, August. I've sung about this court. Everything else is theft. But a gift—" she let the note die, "—a gift binds forever."

The silence after that was long, and full of dripping water, and full of four people not looking at each other while they thought about what freely given might cost.

Then Wren, because Wren was Wren, broke it: "Well. Good thing none of us plans to give this court a single damn thing for free. We're thieves, witches, singers, and accountants. Free is the one price we don't know how to pay."

I laughed. It came out cracked, but it came out.

Below us, the stair ended. Not in a door—in a threshold of pale light, the color of moonlight through bone, spilling up the last few steps like water finding its level. From beyond it came music, distant and elegant, the sound of a court that had all the time in the world because it had taken everyone else's.

And standing in that light, silhouetted, waiting as if she'd known the exact moment we'd arrive, was a figure in servant's grey with a lantern hanging from one hand—and even across the distance, even after thirteen moons, I knew the set of those shoulders, the tilt of that head, the way she stood like she was bored by eternity itself.

"Juniper," I breathed.

She raised the lantern slightly. And in its glow I saw her face—older, harder, furious, alive—and I saw, wrapped around the lantern's cage of black iron, a faint golden shimmer that pulsed like a heartbeat.

Like a soul, knocking to get out.

"About time," my sister called down the stair, in a voice that had learned sarcasm in the dark and sharpened it there. "Do you have any idea how long a moon-turn is when you're counting?"
