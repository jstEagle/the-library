## 12. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The stair stopped pretending to be a stair about two hundred steps down.

It became something else—wider, smoother, worn in the middle like a road that had been walked by a great many people who never came back up. The walls stopped being stone and started being *bone*, though I want it noted for the record that I didn't say this out loud. Wren said it. Bramble said it louder. Sable just put her hand against the wall and hummed a note of recognition, which was somehow worse than either.

"Nobody panic," I said, mostly to myself.

"I'm not panicking," Wren said. "I'm inventorying. Do you know how much a femur that size would fetch? Because I do, and the answer is 'a house.'"

"You are not going to rob the architecture."

"Not rob. *Reallocate.*"

Sable's hand found my sleeve. "Listen," she whispered, and I did, because listening is the one thing I have always been good at.

Beneath our footsteps there was music. Not the kind you'd choose—a slow, wet, ceremonial sound, like a heartbeat played on a cello underwater. And threaded through it, faint as smoke: voices. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Saying names.

I knew what they were before I let myself know. Debts. Souls being counted, called, catalogued. The same whisper I'd heard in the ledger thirteen moons ago, when a routine entry had spoken in my sister's voice.

*Juniper.*

My hands were steady. I made them be steady. There's a trick to it—you grip something and pretend the trembling belongs to the thing instead of you. I gripped the forged letter of credentials in my pocket, the one Sable had helped me write from memory of Corvane's own correspondence, and I pretended my hands belonged to someone competent.

"Remember your parts," I said. "I'm a collector for the Gilded Root. You three are my retinue. Wren, you're my clerk."

Wren's grin could have opened locks. "I've always wanted to embezzle for a guild."

"You're not embezzling. You're *decorating* an embezzlement."

"Bramble's the apothecary," Sable murmured. "And I'm the archivist."

"And if anyone asks why the archivist is blind, tell them the Gilded Root values memory over eyesight." I took a breath that tasted like old pennies and cold water. "They'll believe it. It's exactly the sort of thing the Gilded Root would say."

* * *

The stair ended at a door with no doorframe—just a threshold drawn in silver across the bone, and beyond it, light.

Not torchlight. Not moonlight. Something between candle-flame and dawn, sourceless, coming from everywhere and nowhere, the way light behaves in dreams. We stepped through it together, and the world underneath the mountain opened like a book nobody should have written.

I had imagined a cavern. What I got was a *city*.

Streets of pale marble wound upward into dark that might have been sky or might have been more mountain; there was no telling where one ended and the other began. Buildings rose in tiers, their windows glowing warm gold, and between them ran canals of what I first took for water until I saw it move wrong—too slow, too thick, catching the light like honey or like years.

Time. The canals were time. I don't know how I knew. I just did, the way you know a debt when you hear it.

And the people—

"Pale," Bramble breathed beside me. "Gods above, look at them."

The nobles of the under-mountain court drifted along the streets like figures in a procession that had forgotten its destination. Their skin was the white of things kept from the sun for generations—mushrooms, pearls, the bellies of cave fish. They wore clothes of black silk and silver thread, and every single one of them wore jewelry that moved.

Rings that turned slowly on their own. Necklaces whose beads orbited their throats like tiny planets. A woman whose earrings were two small moons, waxing and waning in alternation.

"They're wearing time," Sable said softly. "Or it's wearing them."

A noble passed close enough to touch. He glanced at us with eyes like coins left in a fountain—shiny, sunk, and seeing nothing—and then his gaze snagged on me and sharpened all at once, and I felt it like a hook behind my ribs.

He could hear me. Or rather, he could hear what I heard. My gift, my curse, my little internal ledger of everyone's debts—it rang out in this place like a struck bell. Every soul here was a debt. I was standing in a room where the whole room was owed.

*Collector,* the noble's mouth didn't say, but his eyes did. *One of ours, then.*

I bowed. Low, smooth, the way Corvane had drilled into me at fifteen: *When in doubt among the powerful, August, bow as if you're reaching for their purse.* "Gilded Root business," I said. "Debt collection."

Something flickered across his face—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would go if smiles were currency here, and apparently courtesy was. He inclined his head and drifted on.

I exhaled through my teeth. "That went well."

"That went *terrifyingly* well," Wren said. "Did you see his rings? Three of those alone—"

"Wren."

"I'm just saying the retirement plan writes itself."

* * *

We followed the canals inward, toward the sound of the counting.

The heart of the court was a plaza—or a hall; the ceiling was so far above it might have been weather—and in its center stood the calendars.

Bone calendars. That was the phrase Sable used, and it was exact. Great spoked wheels of yellowed bone rose from the marble like enormous, patient flowers, each ring carved with marks, each mark a moon. Courtiers gathered at them the way merchants gather at an exchange, reading the turns, placing wagers, adjusting accounts. At the base of the largest wheel, clerks in gray scratched entries into ledgers bound in leather so dark it was nearly black, and I recognized the binding because I'd balanced accounts in its twin for eleven years.

Calfskin from the herds below. Of course. Everything down here was accounted for. Everything down here *was* an account.

"The thirteenth wheel," Sable said, tilting her blind face toward the largest calendar. "Feel it. The whole city leans on it."

I felt it. Everyone felt it. The air itself pulled toward that wheel the way water pulls toward a drain, and on its outermost ring, twelve marks glowed faintly silver, and the thirteenth—the last—was bare.

One turn left. Thirteen days, maybe less, depending on how a moon turned when the only moon it answered was carved on bone.

"August." Bramble's hand closed around my wrist, her grip strong as roots. "Your face. Fix your face."

I fixed my face. It's a skill, like the steady-hands trick. You take everything screaming inside you and you fold it small and you put it somewhere no one can see, and then you walk toward the clerks like a woman with an appointment.

Which, as it happened, I now had.

* * *

The chief clerk was named Vellum, because of course he was. He was pale as his masters but smaller, sharper, with ink-stained fingers and spectacles made of two lenses so thin they were barely there—glass worn by a man who wanted to seem like he needed help seeing, when really he saw everything.

"Gilded Root," he said, when I presented the forged letter. His voice was dry as the paper. "We haven't had collectors down in some years. Master Corvane found the journey... unprofitable."

"Master Corvane finds most things unprofitable," I said. "That's why he sends other people."

Vellum's quill paused. For one horrible instant I thought I'd overplayed it—but then the corner of his mouth twitched, the tiniest movement, a man enjoying a joke he wasn't allowed to laugh at. Clerks, I thought. Clerks are the same everywhere. Give them one honest grievance about their employer and they'll forgive you anything else.

"And the nature of your collection?" he asked.

"A defaulted account." I slid a second paper across his desk—one Sable had composed from half-remembered court forms, full of language that sounded official and meant almost nothing. "A soul, sold on contract, payment terms lapsed. The Root exercises its right of audit and recovery."

"Soul-recovery requires a court seal." His eyes came up over the useless spectacles. "You'll need to petition. Petitions are heard at the turn of the tide—" he gestured vaguely at a canal, where the honey-slow current had begun, impossibly, to reverse— "which is now, conveniently. The court loves convenience. It's the only mercy they stock."

"Lead on," I said grandly, while inside me a clock I couldn't see began ticking very fast.

Because here was the problem, the one I'd been carrying since the stair: I had planned to pose my way to Juniper. Find her, get her out, run. I had not planned for the fact that to free a soul in this place, I might have to stand in front of the entire pale nobility and *argue accounting at them*, using my gift, in front of creatures who could hear me hearing them.

Every debt in this city sang to me. If I listened too hard, they'd feel it. If I listened too well, they'd know exactly what I was—and hunted things that flee to the under-mountain do not come here to collect debts. They come here to hide from them.

So I did the hardest thing I have ever done, harder than the stair, harder than the lie: I stopped listening.

I folded my gift shut like a ledger slammed closed, and I walked through a city made entirely of debt deaf, and it hurt. It hurt like holding your breath. By the time we reached the petitioners' gallery, sweat had soaked through my shirt despite the cold, and Bramble was watching me with the expression of a woman deciding whether her patient needed herbs or a slap.

* * *

The gallery was a crescent of benches facing a dais where three nobles sat beneath a canopy of black silk. They were older than the others—older in a way that had nothing to do with years. Their skin was translucent at the edges. When they moved, I could see the faint glow of something beneath, like lanterns wrapped in skin, and I understood with a lurch that these were souls worn on the outside, or nearly. Nobles who had spent so much of their currency that it showed.

The court called cases in order. Most were brief. A noble wished to extend a loan against a granddaughter's remaining decades; granted. A merchant sought to redeem his brother's soul and lacked sufficient collateral; denied, and the man was escorted away making a sound I will not write down even now.

Then: "The Gilded Root," Vellum announced, "petitions for audit and recovery."

We stood. All four of us, in our borrowed dignity, in the light of the soul-lanterns, before creatures who had been trading in impossible things since before my guild learned to spell *profit*.

The center noble regarded me. Her eyes were the worst yet—not coin-eyes but scale-eyes, weighing, and I felt her attention pass over me like a hand checking a purse for false bottoms.

"August Montrose," she said.

My blood stopped. Then restarted, badly.

"You know my name," I managed.

"We know all names that owe us." She smiled, and her teeth were very white and very even, like piano keys. "The Gilded Root owes us a great deal. Tell me, little collector—when your master's account comes due at the thirteenth turn, will you be the one balancing it?"

Behind me, Wren had gone very still. Bramble's hand hovered near her satchel. Sable's head tilted, listening to something in the noble's voice that I had locked myself out of hearing.

"I collect what I'm sent to collect," I said. "Nothing more."

"Mm." The noble unfolded a document—bone-pale parchment, sealed in silver. "Then perhaps you can explain why the Gilded Root's own records show a *second* signature on the Montrose sale. One we were never told about."

The word *Montrose* went through me like a blade finding the gap in armor.

Thirteen moons ago, a soul was sold. I had spent weeks believing Corvane sold my sister. But the noble held a paper with two signatures, and her smile said she had known I would come the moment I walked through the silver threshold, and the thirteenth mark on the bone calendar hung empty above us all, waiting for its turn.

"Read it carefully, collector," she said, sliding the document toward the edge of the dais. "Before you argue terms you don't understand. The second name may surprise you."

I stepped forward to take it, and my hands, traitorously, finally began to shake.
