## 13. Juniper

The court beneath the mountain kept its servants the way a miser keeps coins: counted, polished, and never spent on kindness.

I learned this in the three days I spent posing as a debt-collector for the Gilded Root, which is to say I learned it the way you learn anything in that place—slowly, at cost, and with the constant sensation of being weighed. The pale nobles did not eat. They did not sleep, as far as I could tell. They drifted through their bone-white halls trailing centuries like cheap perfume, and when they looked at you, you felt your whole life laid out on a table and appraised by someone deciding what they'd pay for it.

Wren lasted about half a day before she started picking pockets. Bramble lasted less than that before she started glaring. Sable, bless her blind eyes, simply stood very still and memorized everything, which was somehow the most unsettling behavior of the three.

Me, I smiled and carried my ledger and said things like *the Gilded Root extends its regards* in a voice I didn't recognize as my own, and every hour of it felt like wearing someone else's skin inside out.

But I hadn't come for the nobles. I'd come for a name, and on the third evening, following the thread of a debt I'd been hearing since we descended—a small, stubborn whisper threaded through the court's great grinding chorus of obligations—I found her.

She was carrying candles.

That's how it started, anyway. A servant's corridor off the eastern gallery, lit by lanterns that burned with a light I recognized even before I understood it: a warm gold, slightly wrong, like sunlight remembered rather than seen. Soul-light. I'd inventoried enough bottled souls in the counting room to know its color the way you know your own handwriting.

The woman walking ahead of me had Juniper's shoulders. She had Juniper's walk, too—that particular stride of someone who grew up running through orchards and never quite stopped moving like the ground might reward her for it. Thirteen years had passed since I'd last seen those shoulders. She'd been nine. I'd been twelve, and I had let go of her hand in a market crowd for exactly as long as it took for the world to end.

"Excuse me," I said, because apparently thirteen years of grief and guilt distilled down to the manners Corvane beat into us. *Always be polite to assets.*

She turned.

And there she was. Older. Sharper. Her hair cut short and practical, her hands nicked with old work, her face wearing an expression I knew intimately because I saw it in mirrors whenever I balanced the guild's cruelest accounts: fury held in perfect, functional check.

Her eyes went to my ledger. To my guild badge. To my face.

Something moved behind her expression—something enormous—but she didn't drop the candles, and she didn't cry out, and she didn't run to me. What she did was look me over from boots to crown with the flat assessment of someone pricing a horse, and then she said:

"You're not one of them."

It wasn't a question. It also wasn't *August*, or *sister*, or any of the ten thousand things I'd rehearsed hearing in the dark over thirteen years.

"No," I managed. "I'm not."

"You're wearing their colors, though." Her chin lifted. "Gilded Root. You collect for them?"

"I—" My throat had closed to the width of a candlewick. "Yes."

"Hm." She set the candles down on a niche shelf with deliberate care, one by one, as if giving herself something to do with her hands that wasn't throwing them at me. "Then you can tell whoever sent you that the eastern galleries are fully staffed, the kitchens have no vacancies, and if they try to move me to the counting floors again I will put hemlock in the pale lady Vessarine's wine, and I don't care what it costs me."

"I'm not here to—"

"Everyone here is here to do something to me," she said. "Move me. Count me. Spend me. So." She crossed her arms. "What are you here to do?"

And that was when I saw it. Hanging at her hip, small as a purse, was a lantern of green glass, and inside it burned a flame the color of her—the exact warm gold of soul-light, but shaped somehow, flickering in a rhythm I felt in my chest like a second heartbeat. Her soul. Caged in glass no bigger than a fist, and she carried it herself, obediently, everywhere she went, because the alternative was letting them carry it for her.

The debt-whisper I'd been following for three days came from that lantern. Faint. Stubborn. *Owed, owed, owed.* Not her debt. The debt *of* her. The court's claim, still outstanding, still ticking toward permanence with every turn of the moon above.

One turn left.

"Juniper," I said.

Her arms uncrossed. Just slightly. Like a door opening a crack onto a room where something had been screaming for thirteen years.

"How do you know that name?" she asked quietly.

*Because I've written it in ledgers. Because I've carved it into doorframes. Because I named every stray cat in the guildhouse after you until Corvane made me stop.* "Because I've been looking for you," I said. "Since the market. Since I lost your hand in the crowd. I looked for thirteen years, Juni, and then I found the entry, and I—"

"Stop." The word cracked like a whip. "Don't. Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"That." Her voice shook, and she hated it shaking, I could see her hating it. "The speech. The reunion speech. I've had thirteen years to imagine it and every version ends with someone sweeping in to fix me, and I am *not broken*, and I am *not* being fixed. Do you understand? Whatever you think you're doing here—rescuing me, avenging me, whatever—it doesn't happen unless I say it happens."

The candles sat between us. The lantern burned at her hip. Somewhere deeper in the court, a bell made of bone rang a hour that didn't exist above ground.

"Okay," I said.

Her eyes narrowed. "Okay?"

"Okay. You're right. I'm sorry." I set my ledger down on the floor—deliberately, visibly, the way you'd set down a weapon—and stepped back from it. "I'm not here to fix you. I'm here because I heard your name in a ledger entry and I couldn't breathe until I saw your face. That's all. That's the whole truth. What happens next is yours to decide."

She stared at me for a long moment. Then, slowly, suspiciously, like a cat accepting an apology it intended to hold against you forever:

"Who *are* you?"

"August," I said. "August Montrose. Your sister."

*

Here is what nobody tells you about finding the person you've grieved for thirteen years: the grief doesn't leave. It just changes jobs.

For thirteen years my grief had been a searcher. It had gone through ledgers and graveyards and gutters looking for her, turning over stones, checking names against names. Now it stood in front of me holding candles, alive and furious and twenty-two years old, and the grief didn't know what to do with its hands. It kept reaching for the shape of loss and finding a person instead.

We talked in the servant's corridor for two hours, in low voices, while she pretended to restock candle niches and I pretended to inspect inventory. She told me about the market—what she remembered of it, which was almost nothing except cold hands and a man who smelled like cloves. She told me about the first year below, when she'd cried herself sick and the pale nobles had found her tears *interesting*. She told me about the lantern, and how they'd shown it to her once, early, so she'd understand exactly what she was worth to them, and how she'd decided that day that if she was going to be owned she would be the most inconvenient piece of property the court had ever acquired.

"They call me 'the resilient asset,'" she said, with a smile that could have opened a vein. "Vessarine finds my defiance charming. Charming. Like I'm a dancing bear."

"What do you actually do here?"

"Serve. Carry things. Be displayed at gatherings as proof of the court's reach." She shrugged. "And listen. That's the real work. Servants are furniture to them. They talk in front of furniture. I know which noble owes which noble what. I know where they keep the contracts. I know that Vessarine has been skimming from the treasury of years for two hundred years and thinks no one's noticed." Her eyes glittered. "Furniture pays attention."

God, I loved her. It hit me sideways, that thought—thirteen years of loving a memory, and now here was the actual person, sharp and scheming and alive, and the love wasn't smaller for being aimed at something real. It was bigger. It was terrifying.

"You could escape," I said. "With us. There are three others—good ones. We have a way out."

"There's no way out of the stair," she said flatly. "You paid tolls coming down. You'll pay worse going up, and the court watches the stairmouth like a hawk watches a rabbit hole. Besides." She touched the lantern at her hip, and her jaw tightened. "This goes where I go. If I run, they hunt me, and a soul-lantern leaves a trail you can follow across the world. No. Running isn't the play."

"Then what is?"

"The contract." She said it like a blade coming out of a sheath. "My sale. I've spent years getting close to the archive where they keep it. I know which shelf. I know the lock. I know that Vessarine checks it every full moon, which means I have—" she glanced up, as if she could see through a thousand feet of mountain to the sky, "—days. And when I get it, I burn it, and then let them explain to themselves why their asset stopped existing."

"It won't work," I said, before I could stop myself.

Her head snapped around. "*Excuse me?*"

"Burning the contract—" I winced. "Juni, I've read soul-contracts. Professionally. Burning the copy doesn't void the debt; the debt lives in the terms, not the paper. The paper's just... a receipt."

"A receipt," she repeated, dangerously.

"For a sale. And sales have terms, and terms have loopholes, and—" I took a breath. "If I can read the contract, I can find the flaw. That's what I do. That's the only thing I've ever been good at. Reading debts."

"And then what? You wave the contract around and declare me free?" Her voice climbed, and she wrestled it back down. "No. Listen to me. I have watched people decide things for me my entire life down here. The man who took me decided. The court decided. Every noble who's ever smiled at me has already decided what I'm for. I am not adding my long-lost sister to that list. Whatever happens next, I decide it. Me. Do you hear me?"

"I hear you."

"Say it back."

"You decide," I said. "Whatever happens next, you decide it."

Something in her shoulders loosened—a single degree, maybe two. She picked up her candles again, and for a moment we just worked side by side in silence, and it was so absurdly domestic, stocking candle niches in a court of the deathless dead, that I almost laughed.

Almost.

"Why didn't you come sooner?"

The question landed soft, which made it worse. Soft questions always did.

"I didn't know," I said. "Until a month ago, I didn't know you were sold. I thought—I looked, Juni. For years. But the guild buried the entry, and I didn't find it until—" I stopped. Told the truth instead. "Until I heard it. I have a gift. I hear debts. And your entry whispered to me in a voice I knew, and I traced it, and I found your name, and I have not slept properly since."

She was quiet a long time. "A gift," she said finally. "Like theirs."

"Nothing like theirs."

"Everything like theirs, probably. That's usually how it works." She slotted a candle into a niche with unnecessary force. "The guild raised you, then. After."

"After," I agreed.

"Were they good to you?"

"They were useful to me. It took me twenty-five years to learn the difference."

That earned me something—not a smile, exactly, but the ghost of one, the family resemblance to our mother's mouth showing through for half a heartbeat. "Corvane still running the Root?"

"You know him?"

"He brokered my sale." She said it without inflection, the way you'd report the weather. "He stood in this corridor, actually. Right about there. He looked at me like I was a column of numbers, and he said, 'She'll keep.' Then he took his payment and went home to his gilded house." Her eyes found mine. "Why? Is he someone to you?"

"He raised me," I said. "And in four days, I'm going to destroy him."

"*Four days?*"

"The moon turns in four days. When it does, your sale becomes permanent. Permanent means unbreakable, and unbreakable means no loophole in any contract anywhere saves you. So we have four days to get that contract, find the flaw, and break the sale before the thirteenth turn completes."

She stared at me. "You said I decide."

"You do. This is me laying out the options. Deciding's still yours."

Another ghost-smile. God, I was going to get myself killed for that mouth someday, and I already knew it.

"Fine," she said. "Here's what I decide. Tomorrow night, Vessarine holds a gathering—a moon-counting. All the senior nobles attend, which empties the east archive. That's our window. Your friends, you, me. We take the contract, we read it, and if there's a flaw, we use it." She held up one finger. "But we do it my way. Nobody sweeps in. Nobody decides for anybody. And if it goes wrong, everyone runs, and nobody stays behind for anyone. Agreed?"

"Agreed," I said. "Except the last part. I'm terrible at that one."

"August—"

"I'll *try*," I said. "That's the best honest offer you're getting from me tonight."

She looked at me for a long moment, and I watched her weigh it—watched her do the same grim accounting I did, tallying what a person promised against what a person was, and finding both columns short. Family. We couldn't help it.

"Fine," she said. "Try."

Then, quieter, almost against her will, she reached out and touched my sleeve. Just the sleeve. Just for a second. Her fingers were cold from the corridors and steady as a surgeon's.

"You got tall," she said.

"You got scary."

"I had practice." She pulled her hand back and picked up her candles, businesslike again, the moment folded away somewhere safe. "Tomorrow night. East archive, after the third bone-bell. Wear something that looks expensive and stupid. And August?" She was already walking away, lantern swinging at her hip, its little flame burning steady as a held breath. "When this is over—whatever happens—you and I are going to have a very long conversation about thirteen years of silence. And you're going to stand there and take it."

"Yes," I said. "I know."

She turned the corner and was gone, and I stood alone in the candlelit corridor with my heart doing something complicated and painful in my chest, and that was when Sable's voice found me through the dark, soft and certain:

"You found her."

"I found her," I said. "And she's magnificent, and furious, and she wants to rob an archive tomorrow night with a plan she built alone in the dark, and I've never been prouder or more frightened in my life."

Sable tilted her head, listening to something beyond the walls. "There's more," she said. "Isn't there. Something you're not telling her."

I looked down at my ledger, and at the page I'd torn from it three nights ago—the page with the sale's terms copied in my own hand, and beneath them, in the margin, the thing I'd noticed and told no one. The clause that made my blood run cold.

The loophole existed. A greater debt, paid in the seller's name, voided the sale entirely.

And the seller's name on Juniper's contract—the hand that had signed her away, thirteen moons and thirteen years ago—

Was mine.

"Not yet," I said. "Come on. We've got an archive to rob."
