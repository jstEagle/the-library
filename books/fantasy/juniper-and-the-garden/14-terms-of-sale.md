## 14. Terms of Sale

The contract was written on skin.

I should have known. The Gilded Root wrote everything important on skin—our own ledgers were calfskin, our oaths inked on the inside of the wrist—but this was different. This was *old* skin, gone the color of candle smoke, soft as a moth's wing where it had been handled by too many pale hands over too many years. It hung from an iron stand in the court's archive like a trophy, or a warning, and when I unrolled it the letters moved.

Not metaphorically. They crawled. Slow as beetles under bark, rearranging themselves whenever I blinked, so that no two readings of the contract could ever match. That was the point, of course. A contract that couldn't be read twice couldn't be contested. The court beneath the mountain had been in the business of souls since before my city had a name, and they had learned long ago that the best way to keep a debtor from arguing terms was to make the terms unreadable.

But they hadn't counted on me.

I pressed my palm flat against the skin and listened.

Every debt has a sound. Most people never hear it—a coin owed whispers, a favor promised hums, a soul sold *sings*, low and terrible, like a hymn played backward in an empty church. Corvane thought my gift was hearing what debtors said aloud. He'd spent thirteen years making sure I only ever heard half of anything. But debts speak even when their owners are silent. Debts speak *especially* then.

The contract sang.

It was a foul, knotted song, all counterpoint and no melody, clause tangled into clause like briars grown through a fence. I closed my eyes and followed the threads. *One soul, freely surrendered, being the girl called Juniper Sandoval, sold by the hand of Corvane, master of the Gilded Root, in the fourteenth year of her life.* My sister had been fourteen. Thirteen moons ago she'd been fourteen years old and someone had put her name on skin and signed it with wax and blood, and I had been upstairs balancing accounts for bottled grief while it happened.

I let that hurt pass through me and kept listening. Grief is a luxury down here. The court pools time like water; you can drown in your own feelings if you're not careful, standing in one spot while a decade seeps past your ankles.

*Terms:* the song went on. *The soul shall serve the court until the moon completes its thirteenth turn from the sealing, at which hour the sale becomes permanent and irrevocable, world without end, amen.*

Thirteen turns. Tonight would be the twelfth's last breath. We had one day. Maybe less—the way time pooled in this place, "one day" might mean an hour or a week, and there was no way to know which until it happened.

*Conditions of voidance:* and here the song changed. Here it went quiet, almost shy, the way people go quiet around the thing they're most ashamed of. I leaned closer. The crawling letters stilled under my fingers, as if they knew they'd been caught.

*The sale shall be rendered void and null if, before the thirteenth turn completes, a greater debt be paid in full in the name of the seller.*

A greater debt. Paid in the seller's name. In *Corvane's* name.

I read it three more times to be sure. The letters held still for me, sullen, like a child who knows the game is up. It was real. It was a loophole wide enough to drive a funeral cart through, sitting right there in the middle of the oldest contract in the archive, and I understood immediately why: the court didn't consider it a loophole at all. To the nobles of the under-mountain, it was a feature. They *wanted* debts to be contestable—contested debts meant greater payments, and greater payments meant richer courts. They simply assumed no one would ever be able to read the terms, much less meet them.

They had assumed wrong. They had assumed wrong about me the moment they let a debt-hearer walk through their door wearing a merchant's smile.

*A greater debt,* I thought. *Paid in Corvane's name.*

And Corvane owed more than anyone I had ever listened to. I'd heard his debts for years without understanding what I was hearing—that low grinding undertone beneath his voice, like millstones turning, like something enormous rolling slowly toward him across the years. He owed the crown favors he'd never admit. He owed the guild's founders oaths he'd broken. And he owed the court itself—I'd learned that in the counting room, cold clarity settling over me like frost—he owed the court beneath the mountain a debt measured in souls, due when the moon turned. Juniper hadn't been the payment. She'd been the *down payment*. An appetizer. Corvane had sold my sister to buy himself time, and now the bill was coming due, and he intended to settle it with a whole ledger of hunted names.

Which meant Corvane's debt was bigger than Juniper's price. Which meant if that debt got paid—in his name, by his hand or his ruin—the contract would tear itself apart, and my sister would walk out of here free.

All I had to do was find a way to make the court collect everything Corvane owed. All at once. Before the moon finished turning.

Simple. Clean. Completely impossible.

I laughed, quietly, alone in the archive among the hanging skins, and the sound came out strange in that pooled-time silence, and somewhere far above us the twelfth moon began its last slow slide toward the horizon.

* * *

We regrouped in the alcove behind the bone calendars, where the nobles' counting chambers gave way to servant passages and the air smelled less of lilies and more of stone. Bramble had found us a niche behind a curtain of hanging moss—actual moss, growing down here in the dark, fed by whatever wrong light the court used instead of sun—and she'd coaxed it thick enough to hide three people and a lantern holding a caged soul that wasn't my sister's but still made my teeth ache to look at.

Wren sat cross-legged on the floor, flipping a stolen courtier's ring over her knuckles. Sable had her blind eyes closed and her head tilted, listening to the court the way I listened to debts—which is to say, entirely, and with visible pain.

"Twelve hours," Sable said. "Maybe. The moons down here don't move right. They stutter."

"Stuttering moons," Wren said. "Of course. Why would anything be simple."

"Tell me about the contract," Bramble said. Her hands were dirty to the elbow—she'd been grafting something into the moss, some hedge-witch trick to give us early warning if anyone came down the passage. Little white flowers opened along the curtain now, one every few minutes, blinking like slow eyes.

So I told them. The terms, the thirteenth turn, the loophole. When I said *a greater debt paid in the seller's name*, Sable's head came up like a hound scenting rain.

"That's why you can hear him," she said softly. "Corvane. That grinding sound you describe. A debt that size doesn't sit quiet. It wants to be paid. It's been trying to sing itself into the world for years, and you're the only ear it's ever had."

"So we pay it," Wren said. "How hard can it be? Man owes a bunch of horrible monsters a pile of souls. We steal the souls, we hand them over, we say 'with compliments of Corvane,' everybody goes home."

"The court doesn't want the souls," Sable said. "Souls are currency, not dinner. The court wants the *debt*. Debt is the only thing down here that's alive. You don't pay a debt like this by handing over goods." She turned her face toward me, those milk-pale eyes finding mine with unnerving accuracy. "You pay it by giving them the debtor."

Bramble went very still. "You mean kill him."

"I mean," Sable said, "that the court collects people the way my mother collected songs. Whole. With interest. If Corvane's debt came due—if it were *called*, formally, in this court, in front of the nobles—they wouldn't take coins from him. They'd take *him*. Everything he is. And a collection that size, claimed in his name—" she smiled, thin and terrible, "—would outweigh one stolen girl a hundred times over."

"And the contract tears," I said.

"And the contract tears."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Down the passage, one of Bramble's white flowers opened with a sound like a sigh.

"There's a problem," Wren said, in the tone of someone pointing out that the ship is on fire. "Calling a debt 'formally' requires the court to *acknowledge* it. These people have spent centuries not acknowledging things. That's basically their whole culture. How do we make a room full of immortal accountants look at a number they've been pretending isn't there?"

"We don't," I said. "We make *him* say it. Corvane comes tonight—or sent someone, but it'll be him, because men like Corvane never trust the important betrayals to underlings. He comes to deliver the rest of his payment before the moon turns. And when he stands in front of the court and presents his ledger of souls, I'm going to be standing next to him. As the Gilded Root's debt-collector. Officially. With credentials." I tapped the seal I'd forged in the archive, wax still warm in my pocket. "And I'm going to do what I do best. I'm going to balance his books. Out loud. Every hidden debt, every broken oath, every soul he's skimmed off the top of his own payments—read aloud, itemized, in front of the very creditors he's been cheating. The court won't ignore that. Courts like this live for nothing else. They'll call his debt. They'll claim him. And they'll do it in his name."

Sable nodded slowly. Bramble looked sick. Wren grinned like a knife.

"I love it," Wren said. "It's terrible and I love it. One flaw."

"One?"

"You'll be standing next to him when the court decides to take everything he is. These are not creatures known for careful aim."

"I've considered that."

"Considered it how? From what angle? While screaming?"

"Wren," Bramble said gently.

"No, I want to know the plan for the screaming part—"

That's when the flowers screamed first.

All of them, at once, along the whole length of the moss curtain—white petals snapping open and then *shrieking*, a high glassy sound like a kettle dying, and Bramble was on her feet with her hands raised and Sable was saying, "Someone's coming, someone's coming *fast*," and I snuffed the lantern because a caged soul glows like a confession, and we pressed back into the niche just as the servant passage filled with light.

Not torchlight. Court-light, pale and sourceless, the color of the inside of a shell. And walking in it, unhurried, dusting ash from the sleeve of a coat worth more than our street, came Corvane.

He looked good. That was the obscene part. Thirteen moons of selling souls and feeding names to crown hunters and he looked *good*—rested, silver at the temples, a man at the height of his powers. Two guild enforcers flanked him, and between them, held by the arm—

My whole body went cold.

"—and mind the hem," Corvane was saying pleasantly, in the voice he used for clients, "the stairs here are older than manners."

They were holding Wren.

Not our Wren. Our Wren was six feet behind me, clamped under Bramble's arm, both of them pressed into the moss, breathing through their mouths. The girl between the enforcers was small and quick-eyed and wore a coat cut from the same cloth, and when she lifted her chin I saw the hunt-mark scarred onto her jaw—the mark the crown burned into the gifted, the mark that had been on *my* door two weeks ago.

Another unlucky girl. Another thief. The crown hunters must have swept her up in the last sweep, and Corvane—resourceful, thrifty Corvane—had recognized the value of spare inventory.

"Here's the thing about the gifted," Corvane said to the empty passage, conversational, as though giving a tour. "Everyone thinks they're rare. They're not. They're *seasonal*. Like fruit. And the crown's hunt has made this a bumper year." He stopped at the iron stand where the great contract hung, and laid one gloved hand flat against the ancient skin, almost tenderly. "I've brought the remainder of my payment. Eleven souls, plus one spare, in case of spoilage. I'd like to conclude our business tonight, ahead of schedule. The moon is nearly done turning, and I do hate deadlines."

The enforcers shoved the girl forward. She stumbled, caught herself, and spat at Corvane's boots with remarkable accuracy.

Behind the moss, Wren had gone rigid against Bramble's side. I felt her shaking—not fear. Fury. Because the girl they were holding might have been her, in another street, another year. Might have been any of us.

And I understood, with the cold clarity of the counting room, exactly what had happened. This wasn't a trap for me. Corvane didn't know I was down here. This was worse: it was nothing personal at all. It was just business, arriving early, the way business does when the debtor is nervous. He'd come to pay off his soul-debt ahead of the deadline so the court could never call it. So the loophole I'd found would close forever, tonight, while I stood ten feet away behind a curtain of screaming flowers.

If he paid in full, Juniper stayed in her lantern until the world ended.

If I stepped out, I lost my ambush—my one chance to read his debts aloud in front of the court, to turn his own accounting against him. He'd see me, and a man like Corvane improvises fast, and I'd get one glimpse of his face before the enforcers took me, and then we'd both be inventory.

Stay hidden and lose my sister. Step out and lose everything.

The girl with the hunt-mark looked around the passage—quick, professional, the way Wren looked at exits—and her eyes passed over the moss curtain and stopped. Just for a heartbeat. She saw *something*: the flowers, maybe, still trembling from their scream. Or maybe just the shape of shelter. And she did the most reckless, generous thing I have ever seen a stranger do.

She opened her mouth and started to whistle. Badly. Deliberately badly, a shrill tuneless racket, dragging the enforcers' attention to her, buying whoever hid behind the moss a few more seconds of being nobody.

Corvane sighed. "Yes, thank you, that will be quite enough of—"

I stepped out.

Later I would try to remember making the decision and fail, because there wasn't one. There was just the space between one heartbeat and the next, and my boots moving, and my voice—my counting-room voice, level and pleasant and absolutely steady—saying:

"Master Corvane. You're early."

He turned. And I watched thirteen years of patronage, of protection, of the gilded cage he'd built me brick by patient brick, reorganize itself behind his eyes into something new. Not surprise. Worse. *Recognition*—the look of a man confirming an old suspicion he'd been wise enough to keep to himself.

"August," he said warmly. "There you are. I wondered when you'd find your way home."

"I'm not home," I said. "I'm working. Gilded Root, collections division." I produced the forged seal and held it up to the sourceless light, and beside me, silent as a held breath, Bramble and Sable emerged from the moss with Wren between them, and Corvane's gaze flicked over each of them—cataloguing, pricing, filing—and came back to me with a smile that had never once reached his eyes in all the years I'd known him.

"Well," he said. "Isn't this convenient. I was going to need a witness."

Behind him, the enforcers tightened their grip on the whistling girl, and above us, far above, the twelfth moon touched the horizon and began, at last, to set.

"Then witness this," I said, and put my hand on the great contract's skin, and *listened*—to Corvane's debts rising around him like floodwater, years of them, decades, a grinding chorus that only I could hear and that I was about to teach the whole court to sing.
