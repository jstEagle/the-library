## 15. Corvane's Ledger

Corvane arrived the way debt always arrives: too early, and wearing better clothes than you.

He came through the court's great doors with two guild enforcers at his shoulders and Wren slung between them like a sack of turnips—alive, I noted with a lurch of relief, but bound at wrist and ankle with iron thread that made her flinch every time it shifted. Her bad luck had clearly run out somewhere on the stair. Or been spent. Knowing Wren, she'd spent it protecting someone and would never admit it.

"Well," Corvane said, and his voice filled the pale hall the way oil fills water. "August. Still playing bookkeeper, I see."

The court had gone still around us. The pale nobles sat in their alcoves like moths pinned to velvet, watching with the particular hunger of creditors who smell a default. Behind me, Bramble's hand found my shoulder—a hedge-witch's grip, earthy and immovable—and Sable stood with her sightless eyes turned toward the sound of Wren's breathing, her fingers already moving against her thigh, counting out some song she hadn't sung yet.

I had planned for this moment in the way I plan everything: obsessively, at night, with lists. But plans are just ledgers written in advance, and I have learned—thirteen moons late—that the ledger is never finished until someone pays.

"Master Corvane," I said, and gave him the bow I'd given him every morning for eleven years. Low enough for respect. Shallow enough to keep my face hidden while I arranged it into something he'd believe. "You're expected."

"Am I." He stepped forward, boots ringing on bone-white floor. The enforcers dragged Wren after him. "I've come to collect what's mine before the moon turns. The court will forgive the haste. Business has been—" his gaze slid over Juniper, standing rigid beside the lantern that held her soul, "—complicated."

"You mean your ledger's been leaking," I said.

Something flickered behind his eyes. Not surprise. Confirmation. He'd suspected the missing page was in my hands, and here I was, confirming it, which meant either I was very stupid or very dangerous, and Corvane had built an empire on never assuming stupid.

"August." He said my name the way he said *asset*. "Give me the page. Swear the new oaths. The crown's hunters are tearing the city apart looking for the gifted, and I am the only shelter left standing. You know this. You've balanced my books long enough to know exactly how much protection costs."

"I do," I said. "That's rather the problem."

I opened the ledger.

Not the physical one—the one in my hands, though I held that too, the stolen pages pressed flat beneath my palm. I meant the other ledger. The one I'd been hearing since I was six years old, the gift I'd hidden from him for eleven years behind a mask of competent silence: every debt spoken aloud, every unpaid weight whispering its terms into my ears.

And Corvane—Corvane was *loud*.

He had always been loud. That was the thing I'd never let myself understand until Sable sang my own memory back to me: I hadn't stopped listening because the debts were unbearable. I'd stopped listening because *his* were. Because the man who raised me hummed with obligations the way a drowned man hums with riverwater, and a child cannot reconcile that with the hand that feeds her, so the child does the only arithmetic available. She subtracts herself. She goes quiet. She calls it loyalty.

I was done being quiet.

"Do you want to hear something funny?" I said, walking toward him across the bone floor, my voice carrying in that hushed court the way a struck bell carries underwater. "In eleven years, you never once asked why I kept the ledgers so well. Why the accounts never drifted. Why every entry balanced to the copper."

"Because you were thorough," Corvane said. His enforcers had gone uncertain; even they could feel the temperature of the room changing. "Because I trained you."

"Because I *hear* them." I stopped three paces away. Close enough to see the sweat at his temple despite the cold. "Every debt. Spoken aloud. Like a voice reading over my shoulder. Bottled grief whispers its interest rates. Borrowed years mutter their due dates. And souls—" I looked at Juniper's lantern, and let him watch me look. "Souls sing."

The court leaned in. I felt it the way you feel weather change.

"You knew," Corvane said quietly. "All these years."

"I hid it from you for eleven years. Which, if you think about it, means I'm better at your business than you are."

Wren made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled by iron thread. Good. Stay awake, thief. I need you insolent.

"But here's the part that isn't funny," I went on, and lifted the stolen pages. "Here's the part where we stop negotiating and start accounting."

* * *

The court beneath the mountain runs on one law older than its pale nobles, older than its bone calendars and pooled time: *a debt spoken truly must be answered.* They'd told me themselves, in their rustling voices, when I arrived posing as a collector. It was the only reason my disguise held. A collector who can name a debt precisely is a collector worth fearing.

So I named them.

I turned to the nearest alcove, where a noble with a face like frost on a window watched me with ancient patience, and I read.

"Entry one." My voice didn't shake. Eleven years of practice reading aloud in a silent counting room had seen to that. "Corvane, master of the Gilded Root, owes the crown of Vell two hundred crowns in bribes unpaid since the winter of the border war—debt inherited, not incurred, but *inherited knowingly*, which under your law makes it his. Interest accrued: seventeen years' compounding. Current value: a district."

The noble's mouth curved. Somewhere in the hall, a sound like ice settling in a lake.

"Entry two. Corvane owes the widow Maren of Copperlane the true price of her husband's bottled courage, sold twice—once to her, once to a duelist—the same vial, emptied by sleight of hand. He owes her not coin but *truth*, and truth owed compounds faster than gold."

"Entry three. Corvane owes the guild's foundlings eleven years of wages withheld as 'room and board,' board being bread and the room being a cot shared three to a bed, while he dined on pheasant above their heads."

"August." Corvane's voice had dropped into the register he used on clients who were about to become problems. "Stop."

"Entry four." I turned the page. My hands were steady. God help me, my hands were steady, and some distant part of me understood that this steadiness was the most frightening thing in the room. "Corvane owes the crown's hunters a standing fee for names delivered—gifted children, mostly, sold to the pyre or the pit—and that debt is denominated in *souls*, payable when the thirteenth moon completes its turn. Which is tonight."

The hall went absolutely silent. Even the pooled time seemed to stop pooling.

"And entry five," I said, and now I did turn to face him fully, and I let him see my eyes, and I did not blink. "Corvane owes one Juniper Sandoval her freedom. Sold thirteen moons ago without consent, without knowledge, without so much as a signature forged in her favor. He owes her a life. And there is no interest rate in any ledger, mortal or otherwise, that covers what he owes for that."

"You little—" Corvane moved. Of course he moved. He was not a man who stood still while his empire was read aloud. He crossed those three paces fast, hand rising, and I saw the blow coming the way I'd seen a thousand entries come due: clearly, and knowing no one would stop it.

No one human, anyway.

He never reached me. The air thickened around his wrist like cooling wax, and the nearest noble rose from her alcove—unfolded, really, the way a spider unfolds—and said, in a voice like pages turning:

"*A debt spoken truly must be answered.*"

"He's my creature," Corvane snarled, straining. "Eleven years. I bought his silence fair and square—"

"No," said the noble. "You *bought* many things. But this one—" the pale head tilted toward me, and I felt the weight of that attention like a hand on my crown, "—this one was never yours to purchase. She listened freely. And she has spoken freely. And by our oldest law, Master of the Gilded Root, the accounting stands."

* * *

What happened next took less time than balancing a morning's books, and I will remember it—whatever else I lose—for the rest of whatever life I have.

The nobles came down from their alcoves. All of them. They moved without walking, the way frost moves across glass, and they gathered around Corvane in a ring that had no gaps in it. His enforcers dropped Wren and ran—I don't blame them; I've read enough ledgers to know when an account is closed. Bramble gathered Wren up with both arms, iron thread and all, and Sable began to sing, low and steady, something old that made the bone floor hum in sympathy.

Corvane fought. I'll give him that. He fought the way he did everything—efficiently, ruthlessly, with contingency plans peeling off him like burning paper. He offered them money. He offered them territory. He offered them *me*, actually, mid-struggle, voice cracking on the word, offering August Montrose and her listening ears as if I were still his to give.

"That account is settled," the lead noble said, almost gently. "She spoke. You did not pay. There is nothing left to trade but yourself."

"They'll take everything," Corvane said—to me, now, the ring closing, his composure finally, *finally* cracking open to show the frightened clerk underneath, the small greedy boy who'd grown into a large greedy man. "August. Everything I built. Everything I *taught* you—"

"You taught me the numbers," I said. "You never taught me what they meant."

And then I did the last thing, the thing I'd saved, the thing that wasn't justice so much as bookkeeping, clean and cold and final. I opened the ledger to the last page—the one with his name at the top of every column, the sum of sums, thirteen years of hidden debts tallied in my own careful hand—and I read the total aloud.

Every figure. Every name. Every soul.

The court received it the way a bank receives a foreclosure.

They claimed him. That's the only word for it. They didn't kill him—death, I suspect, is too simple an instrument for creatures who deal in time. They *claimed* him, folded him into their accounting the way you fold a bad debt into a year-end balance, and the last I saw of Corvane, master of the Gilded Root, keeper of impossible things, was his face turning toward me over the shoulder of a pale noble, mouth still moving—*August, August*—as the alcoves swallowed him down into whatever column they file such men under.

The lantern light in the hall dimmed by one shade. Somewhere, a bone calendar ticked.

* * *

Afterward, the hall was very quiet, and Wren broke it, because of course she did.

"So," she said, spitting out a strand of hair, still trussed in Bramble's arms like an indignant cat. "Are you going to untie me, or was watching me dangle part of the plan?"

"It was contingency," I said, working at the iron thread with a knife Bramble passed me. "You're lucky I didn't leave you hanging."

"I'm *never* lucky. That's the whole—" She stopped. Blinked at me. "Wait. Did you just make a joke? While I was hostage? You absolute—"

"Wren." Sable's voice, warm as candlewax. "Let her have it. She's earned one terrible joke."

Bramble said nothing. She was looking at me with an expression I couldn't parse—pride and grief tangled together, the way roots tangle in dark soil—and then she simply pulled me into her arms, smelling of green things and woodsmoke, and held on until my knees remembered they were tired.

It was Juniper who brought me back.

She stood beside her lantern—her caged soul, guttering softly in its glass prison—and she was watching me with an expression I'd spent thirteen moons imagining and gotten wrong in every particular. Not gratitude. Not yet. Something harder and more honest.

"You read him like a ledger," she said.

"That's what I am. What I was. What—" I gestured vaguely at the ruins of my former employer. "Apparently what I'll always be."

"The court's going to want payment for this," Juniper said. "For the disruption. For the claim. Nothing in that place is free, August. You know that better than anyone."

I did know it. I'd known it since the moment I walked down the stair that isn't there, paying memory-tolls with every step, trading pieces of myself for passage. And I could feel it now, gathering in the hall like pressure before a storm: the nobles resettling in their alcoves, the pooled time beginning to move again, the whole vast machinery of the court beneath the mountain turning its attention toward the loose end standing in its midst.

Me. And the contract still glowing on its pedestal, unbroken, binding Juniper's soul until the moon completed its turn—or until a greater debt was paid in the seller's name.

Corvane's death-by-accounting had voided nothing. The sale stood. The moon was rising toward its thirteenth turn, and the court's oldest law hung in the air between us, patient as interest:

*A debt spoken truly must be answered.*

The lead noble unfolded from her alcove one more time and regarded me with those frost-window eyes.

"You have done the court a service," she said. "And the court does not permit its servants to leave owing nothing. The contract remains. The moon turns within the hour. Tell me, little listener—what will you pay?"

Juniper opened her mouth. I saw her draw breath to argue, to offer, to do the same reckless arithmetic I'd been doing since I found her name in that ledger—and I stepped forward before she could speak, because there was one lesson Corvane taught me that I intended to keep:

Never let family sign first.

"I already know the terms," I said. "I read the fine print years ago."
