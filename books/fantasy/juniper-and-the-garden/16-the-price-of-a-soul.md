## 16. The Price of a Soul

The court beneath the mountain did not have a throne room so much as a counting room that had grown ambitions. The same was true, I suppose, of the Gilded Root, and of me, and possibly of every institution that had ever handled money it hadn't earned honestly. Pale nobles sat in tiers along the walls like ledgers bound in human skin, which, knowing this court, some of them were. Their faces were the color of candle wax left too long in the sun. Their eyes were the color of interest accruing.

At the center of it all stood the Bone Calendar, and on it, the thirteenth moon hung by a thread of silver no wider than a hair.

I had been performing debt-collector for the Gilded Root for what felt like three hours and what my knees insisted was considerably longer. Beside me, Wren kept her hands very still, which for Wren was the equivalent of screaming. Bramble's fingers were buried in the pouch at her belt where she kept seeds that grew into things with opinions. And Sable—blind Sable, who saw more than any of us—had gone quiet in the way that meant she was listening to something underneath the room.

Juniper stood ten feet away, flanked by two nobles whose smiles suggested they found us charming the way one finds a mouse charming when it has wandered into the pantry. My sister. Thirteen moons caged, and still standing like the floor owed her an apology.

I should have known it would come to this moment. In my experience, every contract eventually arrives at the part where someone pays.

"The matter," said the noble nearest the Calendar—a tall one, with a voice like coins being counted slowly, "is one of precedence."

"Precedence," Wren muttered under her breath. "They've got us in a hole under a mountain and they're worried about *precedence*."

The tall noble continued as though she hadn't heard, or as though hearing were beneath her. "The Gilded Root sold us a soul. A fine soul. Thirteen turns of the moon it has served this court faithfully." Juniper's jaw tightened. Faithfully. As if she'd had a choice. "But the contract contains a clause. An old clause. A clause we honor because we are, above all things, honorable." Here several of the wax-faced nobles made a sound that might have been laughter in a healthier throat. "A sale is voided if a greater debt is paid in the seller's name. Not against the seller. Not *by* the seller. *In his name.* Corvane sold your sister. Therefore the payment must be made as if from his hand, freely given, without coercion, and it must be greater than what he took."

"And what," I said, "does the court consider greater than a soul?"

Every face in the room turned toward me with the slow, synchronized attention of a field of flowers deciding to be menacing.

"A soul," said the tall noble, "is everything a person is. It follows that only one thing can outweigh it." She smiled, and her teeth were small and even and terrible. "Everything a person *was*. A life's worth of memory. Freely given. Every recollection surrendered—not destroyed, we are not barbarians, memories keep beautifully down here—but given over, entire, until nothing remains of the giver but the giving."

The silence afterward had weight. It pressed down on the room like snowfall.

I did the arithmetic before anyone else could speak, because arithmetic is what I'm for. A life's worth of memory. Everything. Not a toll per stair, not a moon's turn here and there—the whole ledger, burned. Whoever paid would walk out of this court a stranger wearing their own face.

And whoever paid had to do it *in Corvane's name*, freely, which meant—

"No," said Juniper.

She said it before I'd even finished the thought, which told me she'd done the same arithmetic faster. My sister had always been quicker than me at sums that mattered.

"No," she said again, louder, stepping forward, and the nobles flanking her moved to let her pass with the bored courtesy of guards who knew exactly how much danger a caged soul could pose, which was none. "You don't get to stand there doing that face, August. I know that face. That's the face you made when you gave me the last of the bread in the winter of the gray fever. That's the face you make right before you decide something stupid and generous and irreversible."

"It's a good face," said Wren. "Very symmetrical."

"*Wren.*"

"I'm just saying, if someone's going to sacrifice themselves nobly, at least they're easy to look at—"

"*Wren.*"

Bramble put a hand on Juniper's shoulder. Juniper shook it off, then immediately looked sorry, then didn't, because thirteen moons of being owned will sand the apology right out of a person.

"You don't even have anything to pay *with*," Juniper said, turning back to me, and her voice cracked in the middle like old ice. "August. You came down a stair that eats memory. You've already paid half yourself to get here. What do you think is left?"

I thought about the stair. I thought about what I'd already fed it: my mother's lullaby, gone; the name of the street where we'd lived before the guild took us in, gone; the taste of the honey cakes Bramble used to bribe sick children with, gone, though Bramble's face when she'd realized I couldn't remember them had been payment enough of a different kind. I thought about how much of me was already missing, and how the holes didn't hurt yet—they just echoed.

What was left? Enough. Enough to matter. Enough to lose.

"That's rather the point," I said. "It doesn't have to be much. It has to be *everything*. And I happen to have everything. Well—" I glanced at Wren. "Everything except whatever dignity I had left after the vault incident."

"This isn't funny."

"It's a little funny."

"It is *not*—" Juniper stopped. Breathed. When she spoke again her voice had dropped to the register she used for talking me out of trees and trouble, the big-sister voice, the one that had kept us both alive through years when nobody else bothered. "August Montrose. Listen to me. I have spent thirteen moons in a lantern watching these creatures count time like it belongs to them. Do you know what kept me sane? Not hope. Hope is for people with options. What kept me sane was knowing that somewhere above ground, my sister was being insufferably competent at a job she hated, and that someday—*someday*—she'd come marching down here with her ledger and her righteous fury and her absolutely infuriating habit of paying other people's debts, and I would finally get to tell her—"

Her voice broke again. She pushed through it.

"—that she never once, not *once*, had to buy me back. Because I was never for sale. Not really. Not in any way that counts. They can hold my soul in glass, August, but they can't hold what I'd give for you, and what I'd give for you is *this*. So step aside. Let me pay it. I'm the one they bought. Let me be the one who voids the bill."

And there it was. The trap inside the trap, the beautiful, awful symmetry of it. She wanted to pay for me because I'd come to pay for her, and if either of us succeeded, the other would spend the rest of a hollowed life not remembering why it mattered.

I understood, suddenly, why the court honored this clause. It wasn't mercy. It was sport. This was what they farmed down here, in the dark, in the pooled time: not souls exactly, but the *choosing*. The moment when love stands up and volunteers. They collected that the way Corvane collected interest.

Fine. Then let them collect mine.

"Sable," I said quietly.

Sable's head turned toward me. Her milky eyes found my face with unnerving precision. "I hear you, August."

"Is she going to talk me out of it?"

Sable was quiet a moment. Listening—to the room, to the debts humming in it, to whatever songs memory leaves behind when it thinks no one is keeping score. "No one talks anyone out of anything down here," she said at last. "There's only what people choose, and what they choose it *for*." A pause. "But August. Once you say it, it's said. These ones don't do refunds."

"I work in accounting, Sable. I know exactly how final terms work."

Wren grabbed my sleeve. For once she had nothing clever to say. Her bad-luck charm, the little iron nail she wore on a cord, was trembling against her collarbone, throwing off tiny hexes of misfortune that made the nearest noble's wineglass crack down the middle. "August," she said. "August, you absolute ledger-keeping lunatic. We didn't come all this way—we didn't burn Bramble's shop, we didn't walk the stair, we didn't listen to Sable's horrible sad singing—"

"My singing is *lovely*," said Sable.

"—so you could delete yourself. There has to be another way. There's always another way. I'm a thief. Finding another way is literally the whole job."

"There isn't," said the tall noble, pleasantly, "but please, take your time. We have so much of it down here."

And that—that smug, patient, interest-bearing voice—decided it for me. Not the logic. Not even the love, though the love was there, enormous, a fact as fixed as gravity. What decided it was the sound of a creature that had watched thirteen hundred years of desperate people bargain, and had never once been surprised.

I stepped forward.

"Then witness," I said, and my voice carried further than it had any right to, the way voices do in courts and churches and other places built to make small people feel large. "I am August Montrose, keeper of ledgers for the Gilded Root, and I speak in the name of Corvane, master of that house, who sold this soul—" I pointed at Juniper, and my hand did not shake, which surprised me; I'd have bet money on the shaking. "He took one soul. I offer its equal and better: a life's worth of memory, entire, freely given, without coercion, without regret—" *a lie, that last part, but contracts run on intent, not honesty* "—and without condition. Void the sale. Free my sister. Take everything I am and leave the rest of them alone."

The words left me and the room *changed*. I felt it the way you feel a door close in a distant part of a house—a pressure shift, a settling. Every noble leaned forward in their tiers. The Bone Calendar's silver thread began, very slowly, to glow.

"August." Juniper's voice, small now. Small in a way I'd never heard it, not even when we were children and the fever had her and she'd whispered that she was scared of the dark inside her own head. "August, please."

"Hey." I turned to her, and I let myself look at her properly, because I had a suspicion I ought to bank the image while the banking was good. Red hair, cropped short by the court's indifferent scissors. Freckles the fever had never managed to fade. Eyes that had spent thirteen moons in a cage and come out furious instead of broken, which was the most Juniper thing about her. "Whatever happens next," I said, "you were worth it. Every stair. Every page of every ledger. Every year I spent pretending I didn't miss you. You were worth all of it, and if I forget, then some stranger is going to meet you and think, *who is this incredible person*, and fall halfway in love in the first five minutes, and honestly? Good for her. She's going to love you. I guarantee it. It's the easiest thing I'll ever predict."

"You can't—"

"I can. I'm excellent at closing accounts."

Bramble was crying, silently, the way hedge-witches cry, efficiently, without wasting water. Wren had both fists in my coat now, holding on, and her iron nail was shrieking faintly with bad luck aimed at the universe itself. Sable had begun, very softly, to hum—the memory-song, the one that held the shape of a person—and I understood she was building me a container, something to pour myself into, so that maybe, *maybe*, not all of me would spill.

"Terms," said the tall noble, rising at last from her seat, and the whole court rose with her, wax faces gleaming. "Speak the acceptance. Speak it whole. Speak it *freely*."

The silver thread on the Bone Calendar stretched taut.

Behind me, at the edge of the chamber, a door I hadn't noticed opened, and cold air came through it carrying the smell of ink and coin-grease and cologne applied by someone with more money than taste.

Corvane. Of course. He'd come to collect early—he'd come to watch, to gloat, to make sure his investment closed properly—and he was about to arrive precisely in time to learn what his name had purchased.

I didn't look at him. I looked at my sister.

"I accept," I said. "Everything I am, freely given. Begin."

The thirteenth moon completed its turn.

* * *

It started, strangely, with the smell of rain.

Not the mountain's dead mineral air—rain, real rain, the summer storm when I was six and Juniper was four and we'd stood in the doorway of a house I could no longer name, counting the seconds between lightning and thunder because our mother taught us that the gap was the storm telling you how far away it was. I felt that memory lift out of me like a leaf off wet stone. Gently. Almost kindly. The court took it, and somewhere in the tiers a noble sighed with pleasure, the way Corvane sighed over a well-balanced column.

Then the rest came, and it was not gentle.

My first day at the Gilded Root, peeling potatoes in a kitchen the size of a cathedral. Gone. The night Vesper went silent, and the vow I'd screamed at a god who wouldn't answer. Gone—and oh, I felt that one go, felt it tear free like a hook from deep flesh, and I almost laughed, because whatever terrible thing I'd done at that shrine, I was losing the chance to ever properly repent it. The names of every debtor I'd ever recorded. The sound of Corvane's voice saying *you're one of us now, girl*, and the shameful warmth I'd felt at being chosen. The weight of my own name. *August.* It went last of the first wave, and as it left I heard myself think, distantly, *so that's what it feels like to be unnamed*, and then I didn't hear anything, because the thinking-it was gone too.

Hands held mine. Two of them, calloused and stubborn—one with dirt under the nails, one with a thief's quick grip. Someone was humming. Someone was singing. Someone was saying my name over and over, *August, August, August,* like they were stitching it into me fast enough to survive the unraveling, and I clung to the sound of it without knowing whose mouth it came from, without knowing why it mattered, knowing only—it was the last thing I had, and I was not going to drop it.

The court drank. The lantern on its chain flickered. Somewhere above us, impossibly far away, a god who had been silent for twenty years leaned close to the place where I had been, and waited to see what was left.
