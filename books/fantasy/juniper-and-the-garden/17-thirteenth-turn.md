## 17. Thirteenth Turn

The court beneath the mountain does not have a sky, so it makes do with a ceiling. Somewhere above the pale vaults and bone-white colonnades, the moon is turning its thirteenth and final revolution, and I can feel it in my teeth like a debt coming due.

Which, of course, it is.

"State the terms," says the Lady of the Long Count, and her voice sounds like coins being counted in another room.

I stand at the center of the court with Juniper's lantern-soul burning cold at my hip, Wren's hand fisted in my sleeve, Bramble's garden-smell clinging to me like a blessing, and Sable's song still ringing in the hollow places where my memories used to live comfortably. Corvane is gone—claimed by the nobles as payment, dragged down into the counting dark while he screamed about ledgers and loyalty and other things that had never once been true. The pale nobles have turned their attention to me now, all those faces like unfinished portraits, all those eyes like scales waiting for a weight.

"The contract," I say, and my voice comes out steadier than I have any right to expect. "Terms of sale. A soul, sold thirteen moons past by one Corvane, acting for the Gilded Root. The contract voids if a greater debt is paid in the seller's name."

"And what will you pay, little collector?" The Lady tilts her head. "You carry nothing. You own nothing. You are owed nothing."

"I am owed everything," I say. "That's rather the point."

* * *

Here is what they don't tell you about memory: it isn't a thing you keep. It's a thing that keeps you.

Every piece of August Montrose—the name, the vows, the first time she heard a debt spoken aloud and thought she was going mad, the taste of stolen plums on a summer afternoon, the exact weight of her sister asleep against her shoulder—every piece is a brick in the house of herself. And the court beneath the mountain wants the whole house, brick by brick, until there's nothing left standing but an empty lot where a person used to be.

They call it payment. They're very polite about it.

"Begin," says the Lady of the Long Count.

And I begin.

* * *

The first thing to go is small, which feels like a mercy and isn't.

My mother's voice, humming. Just the hum, just three notes of a lullaby she never finished because she died before she could. It lifts out of me like smoke off a candle, and I feel the shape of it go, feel the hole it leaves behind, smooth-edged and clean as if it had never been filled.

"That's not—" Wren starts.

"It's fine," I say. "It's fine. Keep holding on."

Wren has both my hands now, her grip too tight, her bad-luck charm rattling against my knuckles like a tiny furious heartbeat. She's supposed to be flinging misfortune at the nobles, but there's nothing here to curse that isn't already cursed, so she's spending her gift the only way left: holding on to me like luck itself might be pried loose if she lets go.

"You come back from this," she says. Not a question. An order. "You hear me? You come back, or I'll haunt you myself, and I'm very annoying."

"Noted," I say, and lose the smell of rain on hot stone.

* * *

Bramble stands at my left with her hands full of living things—a sprig of rosemary, a curling vine, a handful of seeds that shouldn't germinate this far underground but are pushing green anyway, stubborn as she is. Every time something leaves me, she presses one of them into my palm and makes me name it.

"Rosemary," she says. "For remembrance. Say it."

"Rosemary."

"For remembrance," she repeats. "Say it again."

"For remembrance." My tongue is thick. The word tastes borrowed. "Bramble, I don't know how much longer I'll know why that matters."

"That's all right," she says, and her voice cracks only a little. "That's what I'm for. I'll remember for you until you can remember for yourself. That's what gardens do. They hold things over winter."

I lose my seventh winter. I lose the name of the street I grew up on. I lose the feeling of sunburn and the cure for it, which Bramble taught me twice, which she will apparently teach me again.

* * *

Sable sings.

She can't see the court, doesn't need to. She stands behind me with her blind eyes lifted toward a ceiling that holds no moon, and she sings the songs of memory—her gift, her terrible beautiful gift—and every verse is a rope thrown across a chasm. When my name starts to slip, she sings the song of August Montrose, mercenary, ledger-keeper, sister, liar, coward, friend. When my vows dissolve, she sings the vow back into the air around me so I can hear it even after I can't remember making it.

"August," she sings. "Who heard debts and did not look away. Who was silent when silence was a sin. Who came down the stair that isn't there."

"Was I brave?" I ask. It seems important to know.

"You were terrified," Sable says, "and you came anyway. That's better than brave. Brave doesn't know what it's risking."

I lose the sound of my own laughter. I lose the guildhouse kitchen, the burnt porridge, the argument about card games. I lose Vesper's silence—not the god himself, but the specific texture of thirteen years of unanswered prayer, which turns out to be a kind of companionship, and losing it is lonelier than I expected.

* * *

Juniper.

Juniper's lantern hangs at my hip, and inside it her soul burns steady and cold, and through the lattice of the cage I can feel her watching me pay. She's been shouting—I can't make out words anymore, just fury, just love shaped like fury—but somewhere in the last hour she went quiet, and quiet from Juniper is worse than screaming.

"AUGUST." Her voice finally cuts through, raw. "Stop. Take my soul out of the lantern instead. Give them the lantern. Give them anything else—"

"There isn't anything else," I say. "There's only me. There was always only me. I just didn't know it until now."

"You promised you'd stop deciding for me!"

"I did," I agree. "This one's mine. This one was always mine."

I lose her face.

I want to tell you I fought for it. I want to tell you I gripped it with both hands and refused. But memory doesn't work that way down here—it lifts out gently, like a leaf letting go of a branch, and the worst part is the gentleness. One moment I know my sister's face the way I know my own heartbeat, and the next moment there is a girl in a lantern whose voice I love and whose face is a blank where a face should be, and I am smiling at her anyway because some part of me, deeper than memory, deeper than payment, knows her.

"I don't—" My voice breaks. "I don't remember your face. I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I don't—"

"It's all right." Juniper's voice comes through the lattice, wrecked and fierce. "It's all right. I'll show you. I'll show you every day until you're sick of it. Do you hear me? You don't get to be sorry. You get to come back and look."

"Deal," I whisper, and lose the word *deal*, and lose the word *whisper*, and lose—

* * *

The moon completes its turn.

I know this because the ceiling of the court begins to glow, bone-white light bleeding down through stone, and the pale nobles rise from their seats like a tide coming in. The Lady of the Long Count opens a book that wasn't there before, and the book is my life, and page by page it is going blank.

"Paid," she says. "In full. Freely given."

Freely given. Those four words echo strangely. Somewhere far away, a cold altar and a burning candle. Somewhere far away, a voice I gave up on. *An invitation, not a command.* Did someone say that? Did I dream it?

"August." Hands on my face. Wren's hands, small and grimy and shaking. "August, stay with us. Say your name."

"My—" I reach for it and find a hole. "I had one. Didn't I?"

"You did. It was a good one. Bit pompous, honestly. Full name was a mouthful." Wren is crying without seeming to notice. "We'll get you a new one. Something shorter. Something with fewer syllables and more style."

"Bramble?" I turn—or try to; my body feels like a coat someone else is wearing. "Do I know you?"

"You will," Bramble says fiercely, and pushes a seedling into my palm, and closes my fingers around it. "Green things. You like green things. Remember that much. Just that much."

"Sable—"

But Sable is already singing, low and endless, the song of a woman paying a debt she chose, and the song goes into the hole where my name used to be and fills it, not with memory but with music, which is nearly as good and lasts longer.

* * *

Here is the last of it:

A soul, freed. A lantern, gone dark. A contract, ash. The pale nobles bowing their unfinished heads as the greater debt settles over the court like snowfall, and the Lady of the Long Count closing her book with a sound like a door shutting on a room I've never seen.

And four people—one hollowed, one freed, one lucky-unlucky, one growing, one singing—standing in the light of a moon they cannot see, holding on to each other in the dark.

"Up," someone says. "We're going up. Can you walk?"

"I don't know who's asking."

"Everyone," says the voice, which might be Wren, might be Juniper, might be all of them braided together. "We're all asking. Come on, whoever-you-are. Let's go home."

Home. The word lands in me like a seed in soil, and I don't know why, but I hold on to it.

I hold on to it all the way up the stair that isn't there, one step at a time, toward a shrine I don't remember and a candle I've never seen and a god who owes me nothing and is waiting anyway.

The dark takes the rest.

But the hands don't let go.
