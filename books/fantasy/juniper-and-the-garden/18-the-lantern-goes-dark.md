## 18. The Lantern Goes Dark

Here is the thing nobody tells you about getting your soul back: it's heavy.

Thirteen moons I'd carried a lantern with my name scratched into the glass, and the whole time I'd assumed the weight was the cage. It wasn't. The cage was light as breath. The weight was me—all of me, every fevered childhood night and every furious thought I'd ever had about the court's pale nobility—poured back in at once the moment the contract burned to ash between August's hands.

I came back to myself like a flood through a broken door, and the first thing I did was fall over.

The second thing I did was look for my sister.

She was standing where she'd stood when the court took its price, arms still half-raised from the offering, and there was nothing behind her eyes. Nothing is a strong word, I know. But I'd spent thirteen moons learning what souls looked like from the inside of a glass box, and I knew an empty house when I saw one. The lamps were out. The hearth was cold. August Montrose, who could hear a debt whispered three streets away, who had once told our mother exactly how many coins the baker shorted us and been right to the copper—August looked at me with the polite blankness of a stranger waiting for directions.

"Juniper?" said Wren, behind me. The little thief's voice cracked on the second syllable. "Is she—"

"She's here," I said. My voice came out rough, rusted from disuse. "Mostly."

Bramble pushed past me, hedge-witch hands already moving, pressing two fingers beneath August's jaw, peeling back her eyelid, muttering something green and growing under her breath that made the moss on the cavern wall lean toward my sister like it wanted to help. Sable stood very still, her blind face tilted, listening to something none of us could hear.

"There's nothing wrong with her," Bramble said at last, and her voice was gentle in the way voices are gentle when they're delivering terrible news. "No wound. No curse. She paid it all, freely given, just like the contract demanded. There's nothing to heal because nothing's broken. It's just—" She swallowed. "It's just gone."

Around us, the Court Beneath the Mountain went on with its evening. Pale nobles drifted between bone calendars, counting moons, counting debts, counting each other. They had not stopped their revelry when August emptied herself. Why would they? A payment received is not a tragedy; it's a line item. One of them—a tall creature with a face like a closed book—glanced our way and inclined its head, almost courteously, as if to say: lovely transaction, do come again.

I picked up the lantern. My lantern, dark now, its glass cool for the first time in thirteen months. And then I picked up my sister, one arm over my shoulders, and I turned us all toward the stair that isn't there.

"Walk," I told the others. "Before they think of a reason we owe them something else."

* * *

The stair remembers you going down. That's the cruelty of it. Every step you climbed into the mountain, every memory the toll-keeper's darkness drank from you on the way in—the stair keeps those, the way a tavern keeps your coat. And it wants them back before it lets you leave.

I felt it the moment my foot touched the first step: a soft tug behind my sternum, patient as interest accruing.

*One toll per soul,* the stair seemed to say, in the voice stairs have, which is no voice at all but a pressure, a suggestion, a door slightly ajar. *You may pass. But passage costs.*

"I don't have anything left," Wren said. She was ahead of me, one hand on the wall, and I heard the fear in her. On the way down, the stair had taken things from her too—I never asked what, and she never offered—but whatever she'd paid, she'd paid down to the quick. "I'm empty. I'm a purse turned inside out. Take a look, there's lint."

"You have plenty," Sable said quietly. She walked last, one hand on Bramble's shoulder, her sightless eyes aimed at nothing and seeing everything. "Everyone has plenty. The stair isn't greedy. It only wants what you can spare without dying." A pause. "Though it doesn't mind if 'without dying' is generous."

"And August?" Bramble asked. "She has nothing. Less than nothing. How does she climb?"

I answered by answering the stair.

The tug came again as I lifted August's foot over the threshold-step, and I opened myself to it the way you open a window to let out smoke. *Take this,* I thought, and gave it the morning Juniper-and-August were seven and nine, stealing plums off Madge Tullery's tree, and Madge caught us, and instead of shouting she laughed so hard she had to sit down in the dirt, and she gave us the plums anyway, and we ate them on her fence until our fingers were sticky and purple.

The stair took it. The step held firm beneath August's boot.

And I felt the hole where the memory had been—not painful, exactly. More like a tooth missing. Your tongue keeps going back to check.

"Juniper," Sable said softly, behind me. "What did you give?"

"A plum tree," I said. "Doesn't matter. Keep climbing."

It mattered. Of course it mattered. But here is the other thing nobody tells you: paying a memory is easy the way bleeding is easy. You just open, and it leaves, and afterward you're lighter, and everyone knows lighter is better, right? Everyone knows the feather floats and the stone sinks. Nobody asks the feather whether it misses being a bird.

We climbed. August leaned on me, breathing, blinking, obedient as furniture. At the fourth landing she spoke her first words since the payment.

"Are we going somewhere?"

My heart did something complicated and unhelpful. "Yes," I said. "Up."

"Good," she said. "Up sounds correct." And she kept walking, trusting me absolutely, the way you trust a staircase not to be a lie.

I gave the stair another memory at the seventh step. The winter our mother died, and August—eleven years old, all elbows and fury—stood in the snow outside the Gilded Root's door and swore she would learn every number in the world so that nothing would ever be taken from us uncounted again. I gave that away and felt it go, and I hated the stair, and I hated the court, and I hated Corvane most of all, wherever his coward soul was hiding, because he had written the contract that put my sister on this stair in the first place, and he had done it with a fountain pen and a smile.

But I kept climbing. Because someone had to carry her up, and I was the only one strong enough, and I don't mean my arms.

* * *

On the twelfth step, Wren collapsed.

Not dramatically—Wren didn't have the energy for drama anymore, which for her was saying something. Her knees simply folded, and she sat down on the stair like a puppet with cut strings, and she started laughing, which is always worse than crying.

"It wants another one," she said. "From me. And I keep thinking, fine, take it, take anything, except—" The laugh curdled. "Except I can't remember what I've already given. I've been counting on my fingers and I've run out of fingers. What if the next one is the one that matters? What if I pay the memory of why I'm doing this and then I'm just some girl on some stairs for no reason at all?"

Sable knelt beside her. Blind, she found Wren's face anyway, the way water finds a channel.

"Then we'd tell you," Sable said. "That's what the rest of us are for. Memory isn't meant to live in one skull, Wren. It's meant to be passed around like bread. Here." She took Wren's hand and pressed it flat against her own chest. "You're the girl who flung bad luck at a crown hunter and made his own hound bite him. You're the girl who stole a soul-ledger page out of the vault of the Gilded Root while the alarm bells rang, and got out through a chimney. You're the girl who held August's hand while she paid everything, and didn't let go even when her grip went slack. That's who you are. Now stand up and let the stair have something small, and I'll hold the rest of you until we're out."

Wren stood up. She gave the stair something—it must have been small, because she barely flinched—and we climbed on.

At the fifteenth step it was Bramble's turn, and she gave away, I learned later, the memory of her shop. Not the burning of it. The whole of it, forty years of seedlings and tinctures and the smell of rosemary on wet mornings, surrendered in one lump sum, and she climbed the next hundred steps with tears running silently into her beard-stubbled chin, humming a growing-song to fill the space where her life had been.

At the eighteenth step, Sable paid. She wouldn't say what. She only smiled after, a strange, unburdened smile, and said, "Well. Now someone else will have to remember my songs for me," and Wren took her hand and said, "Deal," and I understood that I was watching something being built on that stair, out of holes and handholds, and it was sturdier than anything the Gilded Root ever raised.

And at every step, I paid for August.

A memory of rain on the guildhouse roof. A memory of the taste of burnt porridge. A memory of learning to whistle. A memory of our father's hands, which I hadn't known I still had until I felt it leaving, and I nearly snatched it back, and didn't, because August's boot needed the step to hold.

The cruel mathematics of it: she had paid everything to free me, and now I was paying myself hollow to walk her home, and neither of us had asked the other's permission, and both of us would have refused it, and that, I think, is what love actually is. Not the grand offering on the altar. The stubborn refusal to let the other person be the only one who bleeds.

* * *

Near the top, where the air began to taste of rain and ash instead of candle-smoke and old stone, August stopped walking.

Not fell. Stopped. Turned her head and looked at me with those empty-lantern eyes, and frowned, the first expression I'd seen on her face since the contract burned.

"You keep giving things away," she said. "Every few steps. I can feel it. Like drafts through a wall."

I blinked. "You can feel that?"

"I feel lots of things." She said it without wonder, without fear, just reporting weather. "Debts. Mostly debts. Everyone on these stairs owes somebody something. Except—" her frown deepened, working at a problem with tools she no longer owned, "—except I don't know why I know that. I don't know what a debt is. I just know that the man behind us owes a woman named Marta eleven coppers and an apology, and the little one owes herself forgiveness, and the singing one—" she tilted her head toward Sable, "—the singing one is carrying a song that isn't hers yet. She's holding it for someone."

"That's you," I said. My voice came out strange. "She's holding it for you."

"Oh," said August. And then, after a moment, with the grave courtesy of a stranger: "Is it good? The song?"

"It's the best one I've ever heard," I said, which was true, though I hadn't heard it yet either.

She nodded slowly, accepting this the way she'd accepted *up*, and we climbed on. But something had shifted in my chest, cold and bright. The gift hadn't gone with the memories. The gift lived deeper than memory—in the bone, in the blood, in whatever part of a person the courts and gods and guilds couldn't write contracts against. They had taken her ledger of days and left her the arithmetic. They had emptied the house and left the walls standing.

Which meant, I thought, gripping her tighter around the waist, that whoever she became on the other side of this stair, she would still be August. Some August. An August I might have to introduce myself to.

An August who might not remember me.

I paid the stair another memory rather than finish that thought. This one was recent—only a year old—and it was of the two of us as children, before everything, dancing on our mother's grave because she'd once said she wanted lilies planted there and neither of us could afford lilies, so we danced instead, hard enough to churn the earth, on the theory that joy was also a kind of planting. The stair took it gently, almost kindly, and I felt it go, and I thought: *she'll never know we danced.*

Unless someone told her. Unless someone sang it, or grew it, or pickpocketed it back into her pocket piece by piece over years, the way you re-teach a body a language.

That was the job now. That was mine. Rescue, it turns out, is not a door you open once. It's a stair you climb forever, taking turns carrying each other, and the toll is everything, and you pay it gladly, and you pay it twice.

* * *

The last step was the worst.

Of course it was. The stair saved its best trick for the exit, the way any decent extortionist does. As the gray light of the ruined shrine above touched our faces—as I smelled rain and char and, faintly, impossibly, roses—as Bramble actually sobbed with relief and Wren whooped and Sable began, softly, to sing the song she was holding—

—the stair pulled hard. Not a tug. A yank. A landlord's fist at the collar.

*Last toll,* it said. *The biggest. From the carrier.*

And I knew, the way you know things on stairs, what it wanted. It wanted the newest thing. The heaviest thing. Thirteen moons of rage and grief and longing, the whole architecture of my rescue, the memory of finding her name in the ledger, the memory of the lantern, the memory of this very night—everything that had made me walk down and climb back up. It wanted to leave me standing in the shrine with a hollow sister and no idea why I'd bothered.

"No," I said.

The pressure increased. Behind me, Wren cried out—she felt it too, felt the stair leaning on all of us now, hungry, closing its account.

"No," I said again, and I did the only thing I could think of, the thing no one in the court or the guild or the god-haunted world above would ever think to do, because everyone pays debts and no one renegotiates them.

I bargained.

"You've had thirty-one memories off me tonight," I said to the dark. "Thirty-one, and I counted, because unlike some people I keep a ledger. That's more than anyone's paid you in a century, maybe ever. So here's my counteroffer: you let all five of us walk out of here owing you nothing, and in exchange, I'll come back. Once a year. Midsummer. I'll bring you a memory freely given, a good one, a fat one, and you'll have a harvest instead of a theft. Interest, stair. Ever heard of it? No? Because you're a stair, and stairs don't understand time. But I do. And I'm offering you a better deal than you've ever gotten, and if you squeeze me dry right now you'll get one big meal and then never see me again, because I don't revisit places that rob me."

Silence. The kind of silence that is actually listening.

Then the pressure lifted, all at once, like a hand withdrawn, and the last step was just a step, and we stumbled out of the dark and into the shrine of Vesper with the rain coming down through the broken roof and the votive candle still burning—still burning, after everything, small and stubborn as a heartbeat.

I set August down on the cold altar steps. She looked around at the shrine, at the candle, at the four strangers breathing hard around her, and then at me, and she said, politely:

"Do I know you?"

And that should have been the end of me. That should have been the moment I broke.

But before I could answer—before the grief could finish arriving—the candle flame stood up straight, taller than a candle has any right to burn, and the air in the shrine went warm and vast and attentive, like a room inhaling, and a voice that was not a sound but a presence, low and old and achingly familiar, filled the stone from floor to broken rafters.

"*She is not lost,*" said the silent god Vesper, speaking for the first time in thirteen years. "*And neither are you. Come. Both of you. We have much to return, and longer than you think to return it.*"

August turned toward the candle, her empty eyes wide, and I saw it cross her hollow face like weather: recognition without memory. The feeling of a name without the name itself.

"Vesper," she whispered. Then, frowning at her own mouth, at the word she shouldn't have known: "Why do I know that?"

I took her hand. Empty ledger, full ledger, it didn't matter. Hand was hand.

"Sit down," I told her. "Someone's about to tell us a story. And this time, sister, we get to hear the ending."
