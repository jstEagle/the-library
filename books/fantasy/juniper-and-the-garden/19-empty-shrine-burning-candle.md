## 19. Empty Shrine, Burning Candle

Here is what nobody tells you about carrying your sister up a stair that eats memories: it's the arms that give out first. Not the heart. The heart, apparently, can run on spite and moonlight indefinitely. But the arms—my arms, which have hauled feverish children and stolen chickens and one memorable drunk priest—are shaking by the two-hundredth step, and August is lighter than she should be, because so much of her has been paid away.

I counted the tolls as we climbed. I had nothing else to do with my mouth except keep talking.

"Your name is August Montrose," I said, somewhere around step three hundred, feeding her my memories like a woman feeding coins to a ferryman who never blinks. "You snore. You deny that you snore. You once fought a goose over a bread roll and lost."

The stair took its cut of each one. That was the arrangement—I'd made it at the bottom, when the lantern in my hands had gone dark and cold and I'd understood, finally, what my sister had done. The stair wanted memories to let us pass. It didn't care whose. So I gave it Juniper-things: the taste of the plum tree in the yard of the house we lost, the sound of our mother humming off-key, the exact color of the ribbon I wore the day the guild took me. Every memory I surrendered was a memory of myself, and every one I spent on her was a memory of her, and I could not always tell the difference anymore, which is perhaps the point of sisters.

Wren climbed behind me, muttering curses at the dark and flinging bad luck ahead of us like a woman scattering seed for hostile birds. Bramble carried Sable, who had sung the whole way up—not a song I knew, but a song that knew *us*, stitching our names together note by note so the stair couldn't sort us into separate debts.

And August walked. Mostly. When she couldn't walk, I held her up. When she couldn't hold her own name in her head, I said it for her, over and over, like a prayer to a god who owed me.

We came out through the floor of the ruined shrine just before dawn, into air that tasted like rain and woodsmoke and being alive, which is a flavor you stop noticing until you've spent thirteen moons underground trading pleasantries with nobles whose skin is the color of candle tallow.

* * *

I want to be honest about the first hour, because honesty is the only thing I brought back from under the mountain intact.

The first hour, I was furious.

August sat on the broken altar steps with Bramble's cloak around her shoulders and looked at me the way you look at a stranger who's been very kind to you at an inn—grateful, polite, and utterly blank. She didn't know me. She'd paid me away. All of me, down to the last argument, the last shared joke, the last time I'd made her laugh until she wheezed. She'd handed over thirteen years of having a sister like a merchant settling an account, and she hadn't even hesitated, and now she was sitting there asking Wren, gently, "Have we met? You seem familiar," and Wren was crying and pretending not to, and I—

I wanted to shake her.

I wanted to say: *You don't get to do that. You don't get to decide what I'm worth. You don't get to buy me back with yourself and leave me holding the change.* I had spent thirteen moons in a lantern learning exactly how it feels when someone else decides your fate for you, and here was my sister, my stupid brave terrible sister, doing the same thing with a smile on her face and no soul left to argue with.

Bramble caught my elbow before I could say any of it. She's small and round and smells like chamomile and she has hands that know when not to touch things.

"Not yet," she murmured.

"She erased herself."

"Yes." Bramble watched August with the look she gives seedlings on a late frost—worried, but unwilling to declare anything dead. "And grief makes people cruel to the ones they love. Give it till sundown."

So I gave it till sundown. I cleaned the shrine instead, because my hands needed work and the shrine needed it worse. Thirteen years of dust on the flagstones. Bird nests in the rafters where a god used to live. The great stone basin of the altar dry as old bone, the votive niches empty except—

Except one.

One candle, still burning. Small, white, half-melted into a puddle of itself, in the niche where I would later learn August had set it when she was nine years old. It should have burned out decades ago. It hadn't. It had a flame the color of late evening, violet at the edges, and it did not gutter when the door opened or the wind came through the broken wall. It just burned, patient as debt.

Sable found me staring at it. Her blind eyes turned toward the flame anyway, the way they do, like she's listening with her whole face.

"It's been lit since before I was born," she said. "Someone tends it."

"Who?"

"I asked the city that question for thirty years, Juniper. The city doesn't answer. Cities only gossip." She smiled, thin and tired. "Gods answer. Eventually. When they're ready."

"And if they're never ready?"

"Then you learn to live in the silence." She touched the niche's edge with two fingers, careful as touching a wound. "But this one—" her voice went strange, soft, "—this one isn't silent anymore. Can't you hear it?"

I couldn't. I heard wind. I heard Wren outside teaching August how to cheat at cards, which was either an act of friendship or reconnaissance, possibly both. I heard Bramble singing to the weeds in the courtyard, coaxing them into flowers because she couldn't help herself. Ordinary sounds. Human sounds.

But the candle kept burning.

* * *

August slept that night in the shrine, because she had nowhere else to go and, I think, because some part of her that no longer remembered anything still knew this place. She lay down on the altar steps like a child going to bed in a house she's lived in forever, and she slept without dreaming—or if she dreamed, nothing crossed her face.

I kept watch. Old habit. Under the mountain you learn to sleep light or not at all, and I hadn't quite unlearned it.

Sometime past midnight, the temperature changed.

Not colder. Warmer—the way a room gets warmer when someone who loves you enters it. The air thickened. The shadows in the corners leaned inward. And the candle in the ninth niche flared from a thumb-height flame to something tall and steady and unmistakably *attentive*.

I stood up so fast I knocked over Bramble's water jug.

"August," I whispered. Then, louder, because whispering felt absurd in the presence of whatever had just filled the room: "August. Wake up."

She woke the way she used to when we were children and I'd shake her out of sleep to show her a comet or a fox or trouble—instantly, completely, no fog. She sat up. She looked at the candle. And her face did something I will remember for the rest of my life, which is convenient, since I paid most of the rest of my life to a staircase: her face went young. Nine years old. Standing in this same shrine, setting down a candle for a god who had already stopped answering.

"Is someone there?" she asked. Her voice cracked in the middle. "I don't— I don't remember you. I don't remember anything. But I know you're sad. Why are you sad? Why am I sad?" She pressed a hand flat against her sternum. "There's a hole here. There's a hole shaped like something I chose."

Oh, you absolute idiot, I thought, with all the love in my ruined chest. You paid everything and left yourself the guilt.

The candle flame bent toward her. Not like wind. Like attention.

And then Vesper spoke.

I need you to understand what that was like, because I grew up on stories of the silent god—the mercenary's god, the god of debts and dusk, who stopped speaking to his faithful a generation ago and never said why. Pilgrims used to come to this shrine and press their ears to the stones. Whole theologies had been built on his silence. Entire orders of priests had taken vows of quiet in solidarity, which always struck me as deeply missing the point.

He spoke, and it wasn't a voice. It was more like remembering something you never knew—a warmth behind the breastbone, a meaning arriving fully formed, wearing sound the way a traveler wears borrowed clothes.

*I am here.*

August made a sound I've only heard from animals and from people at funerals.

"You left," she said. "Everyone says you left. Everyone says you went silent and abandoned—"

*I never left.*

The words settled into the room like snow.

*I waited. Every night. In the dark, in the dust, while the world decided I had died of indifference. I waited because I could not speak—and I could not speak because of you.*

"Because of me?" August's hand was still on her chest, over the hole. "I don't even know you."

*You knew me better than anyone ever has,* the god said, and there was something in it that wasn't blame. Something rawer. *You knew me well enough to break my heart, little ledger-keeper. And a god's heart, once broken, stops the mouth along with everything else.*

I looked at Bramble, asleep against the far wall—when had she fallen asleep?—and at Sable, who was not asleep at all, whose blind eyes were wide open and shining, listening the way she listened to memories, drinking it in.

August stood up. Barefoot on cold stone, wrapped in a dead woman's cloak, with nothing inside her but the shape of what she'd given away, and she faced her god.

"What did I do?"

The flame flickered. For a moment I thought he wouldn't answer—that the silence would close over him again like water. Sable's breath caught. My hands found each other and held on.

*You were in pain,* Vesper said. *And you asked me to take it. And I refused—because pain freely suffered is the only coin the living truly own, and I would not rob you of it. And you were nine years old, and nine-year-olds do not accept theology. So you cursed me. You told me that if you had to hurt alone, then I would speak alone, forever, to no one. And a curse spoken in perfect faith, by a heart that meant every word—*

He paused. Gods, it turns out, can grieve.

*—that is a debt even I must honor. Your vow silenced me. Not my will. Never my will. I have been trying to reach you for twenty years, August Montrose, and tonight you finally have room to hear me.*

The candle burned steady. Violet-edged. Patient.

August sat back down on the altar steps, slowly, like her knees had filed formal notice. "I broke you," she said. "A child broke a god."

*A child broke a god's silence,* he corrected. *The god broke himself, staying to keep faith with a curse rather than abandon the child who spoke it. We are both, I think, guilty of loving badly.*

"That's the stupidest thing I've ever heard," August said, and laughed, and the laugh broke in the middle and became something else, and I understood that she was crying, and that she didn't know why, because the reasons had been sold to a staircase, and I had to turn away and look at the wall for a while.

When I turned back, the flame had moved.

It had left the niche. It hung in the air in front of August at the height of a standing man, tall and calm, and the light it cast wasn't light exactly—it was *recognition*. Everything it touched looked more itself. The cracks in the flagstones looked honestly cracked. The dust looked honestly dusty. Even August, hollowed and hollow-eyed, looked like August—like the truest possible August, the essential one, the part that couldn't be paid away because it was never hers to sell.

*Here is what I can offer,* Vesper said. *What you paid on the stair, I kept. A god holds what passes through his house. Your name. Your vows. Your years. Your sister's face. All of it, stored like coin in a vault, waiting for a claimant. I can restore it. All of it. Tonight. You would walk out of here whole—you would remember the guild, the hunt, the betrayal, the price, all of it, every wound intact.*

The room went very quiet.

"And the cost?" August asked. Of course she asked. Whatever else had been scraped out of her, that instinct remained: nothing is free, check the ledger, read the fine print. I could have kissed her.

*No cost,* said the god. *That is precisely what I cannot make you believe, so I will say it plainly and let you sit with your suspicion, which I confess I have earned. No cost. No debt. No exchange. Restoration, offered freely, to a woman who has spent her whole life paying for other people's bargains.*

"Why?" August's voice was thin. "Why would you—after everything—"

*Because I watched you give everything you were to save your sister,* Vesper said, *and I recognized the gesture. It was mine, twenty years ago, when I stayed silent to honor a furious child's curse. Love that pays without counting. You learned it from somewhere, August. I would like, very much, to spend eternity finding out if it was from me.*

August looked at the flame for a long time.

Then she looked at me.

She didn't know my name. She didn't know my face belonged to her. But something under the emptiness—the load-bearing part, the part you can't sell because it isn't inventory—looked at me, and her brow furrowed, and she said:

"Do I know you?"

And I said, "You're about to."

Because here's the thing I decided sometime between the two-hundredth step and the ninth niche: I am done letting other people decide things for my sister. Not gods. Not guilds. Not August herself, not if it means erasing her. If Vesper offers her a whole self on a silver platter, fine—but she's going to choose it knowing what she's choosing, and the first thing she chooses back is going to be *me*.

So I sat down next to her on the altar steps, in the light of a god's first words in twenty years, and I said:

"Ask him to wait one minute. Then ask me to tell you a story. It starts with a plum tree, a goose, and the worst bread roll theft in the history of the Gilded Root—and it ends with you owing me, sister, and I collect all my debts."

The candle flame leaned toward us both, and Vesper—who had waited twenty years in the dark—waited one minute more.
