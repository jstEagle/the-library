## 20. What the God Asks

I woke in a body I did not recognize and a world I did not remember.

That is not quite true. It is worse than that. I woke in a body that recognized everything and knew nothing—the hands flexed when the cold came, the feet found the stairs without my asking, the throat made a sound of grief at a doorway I had never seen before. My body remembered. I did not. I was a stranger wearing myself like a borrowed coat, sleeves too long, pockets full of things whose names I'd lost.

The shrine was small and dark and smelled of wax and old stone. There was an altar, and on the altar a candle burning with a flame that leaned toward me like a curious dog. And there was a voice.

It was not a voice like other voices. Other voices come from outside you and stop at your ears. This one arrived already inside, the way warmth arrives, the way the knowledge of your own name arrives—except I did not know my own name, so perhaps it arrived the way a name arrives to someone who has never needed one.

*You are awake,* it said. *Good. You have been asleep a long time, even when you were walking.*

"I don't—" My voice cracked like dry clay. "Who am I?"

The silence that followed was not empty. It was the silence of someone choosing their words with great care, which is its own kind of speech.

*You are the one who paid,* the voice said at last. *You are the one who climbed down thirteen steps and gave away everything she carried, one piece at a time, so that another could climb up carrying her.*

"Another." The word meant nothing. A shape without a picture. I said it again, tasting it. "Another."

*Your sister,* the voice said gently. *Juniper. She carried you up the stair when your legs forgot how. She is sleeping now, upstairs, in the house above the shrine, with three others who refused to leave you. They will tell you who you were. But they cannot tell you who you are. That part is yours.*

I sat on the cold floor because standing seemed like a skill I might not have. The candle flame watched me. Somewhere above, floorboards creaked—people moving, alive, waiting—and every creak made my chest ache in a way I had no story for. Grief without a cause. Love without a face. My body full of rooms whose doors were locked from the inside.

"Are you a god?" I asked.

*I am Vesper.*

The name landed somewhere deep and rang like a bell in an empty house. I waited for it to mean something. It didn't. It just rang.

"They say you went silent," I said. "I don't know who *they* are. But the words came with me, like stones in my shoe. *The silent god.* Why would a god go silent?"

Another careful silence. Longer this time. When he spoke, his voice had a texture I can only call old—not tired, but weathered, like wood that has been held by many hands.

*Because someone asked him to.*

"That's a strange reason for a god to lose his voice."

*It is a strange story,* he agreed. *And it is yours. I could give it back to you. That is why we are here, you and I, in the dark, with the candle between us. I have kept what you paid. All of it. Every memory, every name, every vow, every scar's history, every laugh you ever laughed at something Wren said—*

"Wren." The name lit up like a struck match and then, cruelly, went out. I grabbed for it. "Wren. Who is—who was—"

*A thief,* Vesper said, and there was something in his voice that might have been amusement, if gods are allowed such things. *A very good one. Yours. One of them. They are all upstairs, and they have been taking turns watching you sleep, arguing about who loves you most, and losing.*

I laughed. It startled both of us—I saw the candle flame jump—and then the laugh turned into crying, because I was laughing at a joke told by a stranger about people I loved and could not find. The grief had no address. It just filled the whole house of me.

*This is the part I cannot do for you,* Vesper said quietly, when the crying had spent itself. *So listen. I will say it once, plainly, because you once told me—no. Because someone once told me that plainness was the only honesty left when everything else has been traded away.*

The candle burned straighter. Even the dark seemed to lean in.

*I can give you back everything. Every memory. The debt you paid can be returned, with interest, tonight, before the moon finishes setting. You would wake tomorrow as the woman who walked down the stair—whole, complete, carrying Juniper's face and Corvane's crimes and the sound of Sable singing and the exact weight of Bramble's hand on your shoulder. You would know why you wept just now. You would know why the word "sister" makes your ribs ache. You would be her—the one who did all of it, who betrayed a guild and out-bargained a court and paid a price no one should pay.*

"And?" I said, because his pause had the shape of a door not yet opened.

*And you would also be her in another way,* he said. *You would carry it all forward. Every grief, every betrayal, every debt-shaped scar. You would be rebuilt from the wreckage, and the wreckage would always be in you, load-bearing. Some people live that way. Many do. It is not a small thing. It is not nothing. But it is one life, and it is the one already written.*

The candle flickered. Outside, far off, a nightbird called, and the sound passed through me like wind through an empty sleeve.

*Or,* Vesper said, *you begin again.*

The words hung there. I made myself ask.

"What does 'begin again' mean? For someone like me. I'm nobody. I woke up an hour ago on a shrine floor."

*You woke up an hour ago having given away a lifetime to save someone you love,* he said. *That is not nobody. That is the opposite of nobody. It is the purest ledger entry I have ever witnessed, and I have been keeping accounts since before your mountains had names.*

He let that sit. Then:

*Here is what beginning again means. Not restoration. Not erasure, either—you will keep the body's knowing, the hands' habits, the love that lives below memory in the marrow. Your friends will re-teach you the rest: Wren's schemes, Bramble's garden, Sable's songs, Juniper's laugh. Piece by piece, freely given, the way all the best things are given. You will be built new out of the same materials—but chosen this time. Chosen by you.*

"Why would I choose less?" I asked. It seemed a fair question. "Why would anyone choose half a life over a whole one?"

*Because,* said the god, *the whole one was bought with everything you had, and you would spend the rest of it paying interest on yourself. Because the woman who went down the stair made her choice in desperation and debt, and desperation and debt were the only languages she had ever been taught. Because—*

He stopped. And when he resumed, his voice had changed—smaller, almost human, almost shy, which is a strange thing to hear from something older than mountains.

*Because I owe you an apology, and I would rather make it to someone new than to someone who already knows to expect nothing from me.*

I looked at the candle. Really looked. The flame stood very still, the way a person stands still when they have finally said the true thing.

"Tell me," I said.

*Thirteen years ago—or twenty-two, or however you count it—a child knelt where you are kneeling now. She was in pain. The kind of pain children should never be handed, and the guild handed it to her early, as they hand everything, wrapped in gold ribbon. And she prayed to me. She prayed the way children pray, absolutely, with her whole self, certain that love and power were the same thing. Take it from me, she said. Take the pain. You're a god. Taking pain is what gods are for.*

The dark pressed close. I found I was holding my own hands, though I didn't know why, only that it felt like something I had done before, many times, in exactly this posture.

*And I refused. Because her pain was tied to her sister's safety, and to take one was to lose the other, and I thought—I believed—that refusing was the loving act. That she would understand someday. That understanding was worth more than relief.* A pause, thin as paper. *She did not understand. She cursed me instead. She used the last of her faith like a knife and she cut my voice out of the world, and I let her, because a god who will not answer a child's prayer has no business answering anything. Her vow bound me. Her despair was the binding. I have been silent for every year since, listening to her grow up alone in a guildhouse that sold her sister, hearing every debt in the city through her ears because I could speak to her nowhere else.*

My breath stopped. The pieces didn't fit together—they weren't mine to fit—but they lay near each other on the floor, and I could see the shapes.

"The child," I said carefully. "Was she—"

*You,* Vesper said. *You were the child. You are the child. Both are true. The woman who cursed me and the woman who freed my sister-servant and the woman sitting on my floor with no memories are all the same soul, and I have wronged all of them, and I am sorry.*

The apology sat in the air between us, enormous and unadorned. No conditions attached to it. No *but*, no *however*, no fine print. I searched for the trick in it the way you search your pockets for a hole, and found none, and that undid me more than any trick could have.

"You could have taken the pain," I whispered. "You could have found another way."

*Yes,* he said simply. *I have had thirteen years beneath your silence to find it. I was not wise enough, then. I am asking to be wiser now. Which brings me—to the ask.*

He had called it an invitation before, in the phrasing of things, but now he named it properly, and the naming changed the weight of it.

*Here is my ask, and hear all of it before you answer. I will not restore you against your will. I will not restore you partially, or secretly, or with strings. If you choose to begin again, you choose it freely—not out of fear of what the memories hold, not out of shame, not because the price of wholeness frightens you. And in exchange—for this is the exchange, and I will not pretend otherwise—I ask that you choose me. Not as a debt repaid. Not as a god you owe. As a companion, freely taken, the way one takes a friend. Someone to walk beside while you learn your own name. Nothing more than that. Nothing less.*

"And if I refuse?"

*Then I light your way to the door, and I wish you well, and I mean it,* he said. *Grace that requires acceptance is not grace. It is salesmanship. I have watched the Gilded Root practice salesmanship for a very long time. I find I have no talent for it.*

Something in my chest turned over—an old reflex, reaching for a laugh I couldn't source. *Gilded Root*. The words were stones in my shoe again, heavy, familiar, wrong. I set them aside. There would be time. Apparently there was going to be time, whole unwritten acres of it, and that was the strangest gift of all.

I sat with the choice. I want to be honest about how long I sat with it, because the honest version is: not very long. The poets would have you believe these decisions take nights of anguish, and maybe for some souls they do. But I had just spent—I gathered—a lifetime learning the difference between a gift and a transaction. I knew the smell of each. Restoration smelled like the Gilded Root. Like ledgers, like interest accruing, like a gilded cage with the door painted on. Beginning again smelled like this shrine: wax, cold stone, and a candle someone had tended faithfully for thirteen years without being asked.

But I made myself ask the hard question anyway, because whoever I had been, I could feel her discipline in my spine like a splint.

"If I begin again," I said, "and let them teach me—who decides what they teach me? What if they give me back only the kind parts? What if I never learn what I paid, or why, and I become someone soft who'd flinch from doing it again?"

*Then you will be someone soft,* Vesper said, *who flinches. Good. Flinching is underrated. But no—they will not curate you. I know them. The thief will teach you every scheme including the ones that failed, especially those. The witch will teach you the garden and the fevers and the night the shop burned. The singer will sing you the truth even when it costs her, because that is her gift and her affliction. And your sister—*

His voice did something I had not heard it do. It gentled all the way through.

*Your sister will tell you everything. The childhood. The sale. The lantern. The stair. She will not spare you, because sparing people is what was done to her, and she has sworn off it. You will get all of it, August. Chosen, but whole.*

*August.*

The name went through me like dawn through a window. Not recognition—nothing so dramatic. Just a settling. A key turning in a lock I hadn't known was mine. So that was the shape of me. *August.* A month at the end of summer, when the light goes golden and everything ripens or rots, sometimes both in the same week.

"That's my name," I said, stupidly, wonderfully.

*It was,* Vesper said. *Whether it still is—that is the question I am asking you to answer.*

I looked at my hands. Strangers' hands, my hands. I thought about the creaking floorboards above, the people up there who loved a woman I couldn't remember being, who had argued about who loved her most and lost. I thought about a word—*sister*—that ached like a tooth.

Here is what I decided, and I decided it the way you decide to breathe, which is to say the decision made itself and merely informed me:

I did not want the old life back. I wanted the people in it. Those were different wants; I understood that now, with the clarity of someone who had finally read the contract all the way to the bottom. Memory was the guild's currency. Debt was the court's. Desperation had been mine, once, and look where it had gotten everyone. I could start a new ledger. Everyone downstairs and upstairs and under the mountain had taught me—at least my bones believed they had—that accounts could be balanced, forgiven, burned, begun.

"Yes," I said. "I'll begin again. And yes—I choose you. Not because I owe you. Because anyone who keeps a candle lit for thirteen years for someone who cursed him is either a fool or a friend, and I'd like to find out which."

The candle flame bowed. If a god can laugh, he did—it moved through the shrine like warmth through a cold room, and every stone in the walls seemed to relax by a hair's width, as if they had been holding their breath since before I was born.

*Fool,* he said. *Definitely fool. Welcome to the arrangement.*

*One more thing. They are coming down the stairs right now—all four of them, because none of them could bear to wait their turn, and Wren picked the lock on the argument. They are afraid you will look at them like strangers. You will. Let them introduce themselves anyway. Let your sister say her name and watch whether your eyes fill, because they will, and she will pretend not to notice, and it will be the first kindness between you of the new count.*

Above us, the shrine door banged open. Footsteps—many, fast, tangled together—and voices tumbling down the stairwell, and one voice cutting through all the rest, rough and furious and shaking:

"August—*August*, are you—do you know me? Please, please tell me you know me—"

I stood up. My legs remembered how. And I turned toward the light spilling down the stairs, toward the four shapes frozen on the steps, toward the girl in front with a lantern swinging wild in her grip and tears cutting clean lines down her furious face, and I said the only true thing I owned:

"I don't know you. But I think I chose you. Will that do for a start?"

* * *

She crossed the room like a falling tree. Her arms closed around me and her lantern clattered to the stone and the candle on the altar leaned toward us both, and over her shoulder I saw a wiry young woman punch the air and mouth *yes*, a broad gentle one press her hands to her mouth, and a blind singer tilt her head as if listening to something beautiful being written down.

Behind us all, quiet as a banked fire, the god settled into the walls to watch.

"Start anywhere," Juniper whispered into my hair. "We've got time. We've got *all* the time now. And Aug—there's something we have to show you. Something we took from the guildhouse before it burned. You're going to want to see what's written in it."

She pulled back, wiped her face with her sleeve, and grinned at me with a grin I didn't remember and somehow already loved.

"How do you feel about burning books?"
