## 21. New Ledgers

The ledgers came out of Juniper's pack wrapped in oilcloth and smelling of smoke and secrets, and I have to admit my first thought was not *how noble* or even *how dangerous* but *I wonder what the interest rates were.*

Old habits. The guild raised me on arithmetic before it ever taught me kindness, and kindness was mostly a footnote.

There were seven of them. Seven fat volumes bound in green leather gone soft with handling, their spines cracked like old knuckles. The Gilded Root's soul-ledgers. Every sale, every transfer, every collateral arrangement the guild had made in souls since before Corvane's grandfather learned to smile with his teeth. Juniper and Wren had carried them out of the burning guildhouse like midwives delivering something ugly into the world one last time.

"They were in the vault," Wren said, dropping onto Bramble's kitchen bench and putting her boots up on it in a way that suggested she'd been told not to at least once already. "Third shelf, behind the bottled griefs. Corvane kept them close because he knew exactly what they were worth."

"And what are they worth?" I asked.

Wren looked at me for a long moment—the look people kept giving me that week, that careful, measuring look, checking how much of me had come back. "To whom?" she said finally. "That's the question, isn't it. To a collector, they're priceless. To the families whose names are written in them—" She shrugged, and her shrug didn't land as carelessly as she wanted it to. "They're worth everything. And nothing you can buy."

Bramble set a pot of tea down hard enough to slosh. "Then we'd best decide what we're doing with them before someone else decides for us. That's how the Root worked. That's how every rotten thing works—somebody else decides while you're still deciding."

I put my hand flat on the top ledger's cover. The leather was warm from the fire it had been carried through, or maybe I imagined that. Maybe I just expected it to be warm, the way you expect a snake to be.

My gift was mostly quiet these days. Vesper had offered me back everything I'd paid on that stair—every memory, every year of myself—and I'd said no. Not all of it. What I have now is what my family gave back: Wren teaching me her cons over stolen afternoons, Bramble walking me through her garden naming things like godparents, Sable singing me whole evenings until the songs stuck where memories should go, Juniper laughing across tables until my chest ached with a hurt I couldn't source and didn't need to. It's an odd way to hold a self. Like a house rebuilt from salvaged timber—you know which beams are new because everyone tells you the story of where they came from, over and over, until the story becomes part of the beam.

But some things came back on their own. Debts, for instance. Debts I never lost. When I touched that ledger cover, I heard it—not words, exactly, but weight. Hundreds of small weights stacked inside, each one a name, each one humming its own low note of *owed*.

"It's all in here," I said. "Every soul the Root ever sold. Every contract. Every loophole." I looked up. Four faces around Bramble's kitchen table, lit by a lamp with a crooked wick, and I thought: this is what I paid everything for, and it turns out *everything* bought quite a lot. "Every debt has terms. And every term can be read aloud."

Sable tilted her head, listening to something beneath my voice the way she always did. "You want to unwrite them."

"I want to void them," I said. "Chapter and verse. The court under the mountain runs on accounting—it doesn't run on cruelty, cruelty is just its favorite currency. If a contract is read wrong, it fails. If a debt is paid in the seller's name, it dies. Corvane taught me that without meaning to. He spent thirty years hiding his debts inside other people's handwriting." My hand curled on the ledger. "I spent ten years learning to hear handwriting. Let's see whose arithmetic holds up better."

Juniper laughed—that laugh, the one I keep in the pocket where my childhood used to be—and said, "There she is."

* * *

We worked for nine days.

I wish I could tell you it was dramatic. Mostly it was tea, and ink-stained fingers, and Wren falling asleep face-first in volume four and waking up claiming she'd been "resting her eyes strategically." Mostly it was me reading line after line in a voice that got steadier as it went, hearing each contract's hidden architecture the way a locksmith hears tumblers: *here, the clause that lets the court renew the term silently; here, the clause that names no witness; here, the clause that transfers liability to the seller's bloodline forever.* Here, page after page, the specific shape of how the Gilded Root ate people.

Some entries I read aloud and they simply—unraveled. Ink fading like breath off glass. Somewhere in the city, I liked to imagine, a soul-lightened person paused mid-chore feeling inexplicably less heavy, and went on with their day.

Others needed more than reading. They needed payment, or substitution, or a name spoken by the right throat. For those we made a list.

The list had forty-one names on it by the end. Forty-one souls still held somewhere—most in the court's deep vaults, a few sold onward to collectors whose addresses Wren produced from a memory she claimed was "purely professional" and whose expression suggested otherwise.

"We can't leave those," Bramble said, when I explained. She was repotting something fierce-looking while she talked; her hands always needed a plant in them the way mine needed a pen. "Forty-one people caged in lanterns under a mountain while we sit up here feeling righteous about burned books."

"I'm not suggesting we feel righteous," I said. "I'm suggesting we do this in order. You don't storm the vault before you've cut the contracts. We void everything voidable first. Then whatever's left holding those forty-one—" I tapped the last ledger, the one with the living debts, "—has nothing left to stand on. A court that trades in souls needs souls to be *ownable*. We're about to make ownership itself the thing that fails."

Wren grinned at me sideways. "Look at her. Ten days ago she didn't know her own name, and now she's talking about collapsing the metaphysical property market."

"You taught me confidence scams," I said. "This is a confidence scam with extra steps."

"The best kind," said Wren, and Sable laughed her low laugh, and outside Bramble's window the garden grew a little faster than gardens should, leaning toward the kitchen like eavesdroppers.

* * *

On the tenth day we burned them.

Not in secret. That was the point, actually—Corvane would have done it in secret, at midnight, with the ashes buried. We built the fire in Bramble's front garden at noon, in a copper cauldron big enough to bathe a child in, and we let the neighbors watch.

They came, too. Word travels fast in a city where people have been vanishing overnight for a season. First a few faces at fences. Then more. By the time Juniper carried out the first ledger, there must have been sixty people crowded along Bramble's wall and spilling into the lane, drawn by rumor and hope and the particular hunger of the frightened for anything that looks like an ending.

I stood at the cauldron and I read.

Not the whole books—nobody's patience survives that—but the shape of them. I read the guild's founding charter aloud, and heard it groan as its oldest clauses failed one by one, because half of them depended on oaths sworn by people now dead or disgraced or, in one satisfying case, currently being held as payment by pale nobles under a mountain. I read the standard soul-sale contract, the template used hundreds of times, and named every hidden tooth in it so the crowd could hear what had been done in their city's name. I read the names of the guild officers—not just Corvane but the quiet ones, the accountants, the friendly woman who'd signed for my own sister's purchase with a neat, unbothered hand.

And then, one by one, we fed the ledgers to the fire.

Here is what I learned about burning seven volumes of stolen souls: it takes longer than you think, and it smells worse than you fear, and when the crowd started singing—Sable started it, some old street song about morning coming whether you invite it or not—I had to stop reading for a minute because my eyes had stopped cooperating.

Juniper stood beside me the whole time. She didn't sing. She watched the pages curl and blacken with an expression I couldn't read, and later, much later, she told me why: "I kept waiting to feel free. And then I realized I felt free already, and the burning was just for everyone watching. Aug—they didn't need us to destroy it. They needed to see it destroyed. There's a difference, and it matters."

That's my sister. Thirteen moons in a lantern, and she comes out teaching *me* things.

When the last ledger went in—volume seven, the thin one, the one with only three entries left in it, all marked PAID IN FULL in my own handwriting from another life—the fire flared green for a heartbeat. Vesper, settling into the flames to watch, or approving, or maybe just warming himself. Gods are hard to read even when they talk. Mine talks now, in a voice like a door opening in an empty house, and most of what he says is short.

He said, that afternoon, as the ash rose: *"Well begun."*

High praise, from him.

* * *

The shop opened a month later.

It wasn't my idea. Or rather, it was everyone's idea, delivered to me separately and with great confidence that the others wouldn't approve, which is how you know a found family is working properly.

Bramble wanted the space above the old shrine turned into a healing garden—she'd already started, of course; there were seedlings in the shrine's cold font before anyone agreed to anything. Wren wanted a fence-and-fortune office, "discreet inquiries, reasonable rates, no job too morally complicated." Sable wanted a school, quietly, for kids who heard things they shouldn't and sang things they couldn't explain—hunted children, in other words, the kind the crown's proclamation had made of so many, before the proclamation died with the regime that wrote it. Juniper wanted, of all things, a bakery, because apparently thirteen moons of dreaming about food leaves a person with ambitions.

What we actually opened was one long room with a counter, a garden out back, a stove in the corner that Juniper ruled like a small benevolent monarchy, and a sign painted by Wren that read:

**THE NEW LEDGER — Debts Heard Honestly. Trades Considered. Nothing Sold That Isn't Ours to Sell.**

Underneath, smaller, in different handwriting: *Tea's on. Ask for August.*

People came. At first they came to stare at the woman who'd burned the Gilded Root's books—my reputation arrived ahead of me wearing clothes I didn't recognize, which felt fitting. Then they came because their neighbor's curse lifted and they wanted to know if I could do that for their brother. Then they came with impossible things to trade, because a city that lived a century next to the Gilded Root doesn't stop needing impossible things; it just stops wanting to be robbed for them.

A bottled storm, traded for a widow's safe passage home. A borrowed year, returned by the man who'd taken it, because he heard we took returns without penalty. A memory of a dead son, brought in by his mother, who wanted it copied before hers faded—Sable did that one herself, sang it into permanence, and refused payment, and the mother left bread anyway, because that's how the neighborhood decided to run its economy.

And debts. People still bring me debts. They sit across the counter, embarrassed, twisting their hats, and say *they say you can hear them,* and I listen, and I tell them the truth: what they owe, to whom, and—this is the part that matters—what it will actually cost, and whether there's a kinder road out. Half the time there is. The Gilded Root survived on people not knowing they had options. Options turn out to be cheap to manufacture and expensive to suppress.

Vesper approves. I can feel it, a warmth along the walls when a contract gets unwound honestly, like a cat settling. He asks for almost nothing. Some evenings he asks for one candle, and I light it on the old altar out back where Bramble's roses have climbed right over the stone, and we sit together in the dark, the god who waited and the woman who forgot him, and neither of us says sorry, because we've both decided beginning again counts as the apology.

Last week a girl came in, maybe fourteen, hunted-eyed, with a gift she didn't have a name for yet and a mark chalked on her door that she'd scrubbed at until her fingers bled. Wren fed her. Juniper got her laughing inside an hour, which I consider the true test of any rescue operation. Sable's going to take her on.

I'm telling you this because it's the ledger I keep now. Not names and prices—a running count of doors opened. It's slower arithmetic. It compounds strangely. Some nights I balance it and find I've come out ahead in ways I have no column for.

Tonight the shop is closed, the lamp is low, and Juniper is in the garden planting something she won't tell me the name of, digging with the fierce concentration she brings to everything. Wren's asleep on the bench inside, having lost a card game to a sleeping position. Sable's humming through the open window, working out a new song. Bramble is threatening her tomatoes with encouragement.

I should go help. I mean to, in a minute.

But there's a knock at the door—late, hesitant, the particular knock of someone carrying something heavy—and when I open it, there's a courier standing on the step, travel-worn, holding out a letter sealed not with wax but with a pressed sprig of rowan.

"From the north," she says. "From the mountain folk. They say—" she glances at the sign, then at me, and something in her tired face goes hopeful, "—they say you're the one who reads debts honestly. They say there's a debt up there even the court was afraid of. And they say the moon's turning again."

I take the letter. Under my fingers, faint as a pulse, it hums.
