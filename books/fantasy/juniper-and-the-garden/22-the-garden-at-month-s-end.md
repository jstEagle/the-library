## 22. The Garden at Month's End

The first thing I learned to do with my new life was weed.

Not the most dramatic of occupations for a woman who once balanced ledgers for the Gilded Root, who had stood in a court beneath a mountain and read a man's soul into his own grave. But there is something honest about weeds. They don't lie about what they are. They don't hide their debts in the margins. A weed is a plant that made one bad decision about where to put down roots, and I have never been in a position to judge anyone for that.

"Left," Juniper said from two rows over, not looking up. "You're missing the left ones."

"I'm leaving them on purpose. Diversity."

"You're leaving them because you can't tell them apart from the chamomile."

I could not tell them apart from the chamomile. This was one of the many things my sister had re-taught me over the past year, along with her laugh — which she demonstrated now, sharp and delighted, the way it sounded when she caught me being wrong, which was often, which was, I had come to understand, one of her great joys in life.

Sisters. You pay a lifetime of memories to free one, and the first thing she does with you is make fun of your gardening.

I wouldn't have traded it. That was the strange arithmetic of it all, the sum that refused to balance no matter how I turned it: I had given away everything, and somehow ended up with more than I'd started with. The Gilded Root taught me that every gift creates a debt. Vesper taught me that some gifts are meant to be simply received. And this garden — this stubborn, weedy, half-wild patch of earth above a shrine that no longer answered to anyone's grief — taught me that some things grow back even when you're certain they won't.

The morning was doing that thing autumn mornings do, all gold light and cool air, the kind of weather that makes you believe the world might be fundamentally well-intentioned after all. Below us, through the trellis gate Bramble had grown out of living hawthorn, the old shrine sat quiet. Not abandoned anymore. Just quiet, the way a house is quiet when the people in it are content.

*Good morning,* said Vesper.

His voice came the way warmth comes through a window — present, unhurried, asking nothing. A year ago I would have flinched at the sound of any god in my head. Now I just brushed dirt off my knees and answered the way you answer an old friend who has stopped by without announcing himself.

"You're up early."

*I am always up early. I am a god. We keep terrible hours.*

"That explains the dark circles under the moon."

A pause. Then, faintly, unmistakably: amusement. It still startled me sometimes, how much personality fits inside a god who spent twenty years silent. Though as Sable had explained it to me — twice, patiently, with her sightless eyes turned toward my face like she was reading large print — he hadn't been silent so much as silenced, and the difference mattered. A locked door is not an empty room.

Juniper straightened up, wiped her hands on her apron, and looked at the bare patch of ground at the garden's center like it owed her money.

"Well," she said. "Today's the day."

"So you've said. Every morning. For a week."

"And today I mean it." She planted her fists on her hips, which was her negotiating posture, her arguing posture, and apparently also her tree-planting posture. "You're stalling too. Don't think I haven't noticed you've been circling that hole since dawn."

I had been circling the hole since dawn. In my defense, it was a very significant hole. We had dug it together three days ago — me, Juniper, Wren complaining theatrically the entire time about manual labor while secretly digging faster than either of us, Bramble murmuring blessings into the soil until the dirt practically purred. The hole waited there now, patient as an open ledger line, waiting for its entry.

At the edge of the garden, wrapped in burlap where it had traveled with us from the nursery down in Lowmarket, stood a sapling. Rowan. For protection, Bramble said, though I suspected she mostly liked rowan berries in her tea. Its leaves shivered in the breeze like it knew it was about to be asked to do something important.

"It's just a tree," I said.

"It is absolutely not just a tree." Juniper crouched beside it and peeled back the burlap with the tenderness she usually reserved for stolen pastries and nothing else. "It's the tree we plant on the spot where everything changed. Every story needs one. You told me that yourself."

"I did?"

"You did. Last spring, over the fence, to Mrs. Halloway, whose memory of the conversation is unfortunately better than yours." She grinned. "You said stories need landmarks. Places where the ground shifts. Otherwise people forget where they were standing when their lives turned over."

I had no memory of saying it. I had no memory of last spring at all, or of Mrs. Halloway, or of the fence, or of most of the year that had passed since a court of pale nobles took my memories one by one and called it payment. What I had instead was stranger: other people's recollections of me, handed back like borrowed books. *You laughed at that. You cried at that. You said this thing once that I've never forgotten.* I was built now out of secondhand timber and my own new growth, and I had decided — after a long winter of deciding — that this was not a tragedy. It was just a different kind of architecture.

Some of the borrowed pieces fit better than others. Wren swore I once threw a man off a bridge for insulting Bramble's soup, which seemed excessive, but Wren told it with such relish that I'd started half-believing it, and honestly? I hoped it was true. I wanted to be someone who threw men off bridges for soup-related slander.

"August." Juniper's voice had gone quieter. "Hey. Where'd you go?"

"Nowhere. Here." I knelt beside her at the hole. The soil smelled like rain and turned earth and, faintly, of the shrine below — stone and candle smoke, the scent of a promise kept for a very long time. "Tell me again why the center of the garden?"

"Because." She lifted the sapling out of its wrapping, cradling the root ball against her chest like a sleeping child. "When they cut us open, this is the spot. Directly above the stair. Everything we paid went down through here, and everything we got back came up through here. If anything in this ground deserves a tree standing guard over it, it's this."

She was right, of course. She was usually right, and she knew it, and she wore that knowledge the way Wren wore other people's purses — casually, and with total commitment.

We lowered the sapling together. Her hands and mine, tangled around the trunk, guiding roots into the dark. The rowan settled into its hole with a soft sound, almost a sigh, and Bramble would have said the tree was grateful, and Wren would have said Bramble was projecting, and both of them would have been right.

*Well planted,* Vesper observed.

"You didn't help."

*I held the wind off it. You're welcome.*

"You held the wind off it."

*A god's work is mostly invisible. It's how we maintain our mystique.*

I pressed soil around the roots with my palms, and Juniper did the same, and for a while neither of us spoke. This was another thing I'd learned: silence isn't always a wound. Sometimes it's just two people kneeling in the dirt, agreeing without words that this moment is worth staying inside of.

"There," Juniper said finally. She sat back on her heels. Dirt streaked her face from hairline to chin, and she looked, for the thousandth time that year, exactly like the sister I remembered and exactly like a stranger and exactly like herself, all at once. "One year ago tonight, the moon finished turning."

"One year ago tonight, I couldn't have told you my own name."

"And look at you now. Weeding. Terribly."

"Terribly," I agreed. "But with tremendous confidence."

She laughed — the real laugh, the one I'd paid a fortune to get back, the one Sable had sung into my ears note by note until it lived in me again. Around us the garden answered: bees waking in the comfrey, the hawthorn gate rustling, the new rowan giving its first small shiver of established residence.

Footsteps on the path — two sets, one quick and light, one slow and deliberate. Wren arrived first, as Wren arrived everywhere first, vaulting the fence rather than using the gate on principle. She had a paper bag under one arm and a suspicious expression, which was her resting face.

"Is it done? Is it in? Did I miss it?" She skidded to a stop at the rowan, examined it, and nodded with the gravity of a guild assessor. "Hm. Crooked."

"It is not crooked," Juniper said.

"It's leaning left."

"It's reaching toward the light, you absolute goblin."

"I'm just saying, if I stole a tree, I'd steal a straighter one." Wren dropped onto the low wall beside the bed and opened the paper bag, releasing the smell of fresh rolls from the bakery on Cooper's Lane. She'd been buying from them every week for months, paying full price, in coin, visibly, where everyone could see — which for Wren was less honesty than performance, but I'd learned not to look a reformed thief's redemption in the mouth. "Also, news. Mild news. Possibly interesting news. Depends how bored you are."

"We're planting a tree," I said. "Nothing you say can compete."

"A crown surveyor came through Lowmarket yesterday. Asking questions about the old shrine."

The warmth of the morning dimmed by a degree. Juniper's head came up sharply. Even Vesper's presence shifted, attentive, like a cat lifting its ears.

"What kind of questions?" I asked.

"The boring kind, mostly. Who owns the land. Whether the structure's occupied. Whether anyone's claimed the deed since the guild—" she waved a hand, meaning *since we burned every soul-ledger in the Gilded Root and Corvane's empire collapsed into the courts' hungry mouths*, a gesture we all used now, shorthand for the worst year of our lives, "—well. Since all that." She tore a roll in half and offered me a piece, which was how Wren apologized for bringing up bad things: with carbohydrates. "But here's the part. He wasn't wearing crown colors. Gray coat, no badge, and he paid for his information with a coin I've only seen once before."

"Where?"

"In the counting room of the Gilded Root. On Corvane's desk. Under-mountain mint." She let that sit. Wren was good at letting things sit; she'd learned it from watching me wait out debtors. "Old currency. From before the court closed its doors. Somebody down there — or somebody who used to be down there — is asking about us."

Beneath us, faint as a heartbeat, I felt the stair. The one that isn't there. Sealed now, collapsed by Bramble's brambles and Vesper's will and a great deal of very motivated masonry, but still present the way a scar is present. The court beneath the mountain had gone quiet since the night the nobles claimed Corvane and his mountain of hidden debts. Quiet, but not empty. Nothing that old is ever empty.

"They're just questions," Juniper said, but her hand had found mine, grip tight.

"For now," Wren said. "Could be nothing. Could be a clerk tidying old records. Could be—" she shrugged, "—could be the start of book two."

"There's no book two," I said. "This is a garden. Gardens don't have sequels."

"Everything has sequels. That's what makes it a story instead of an ending." She pointed the remaining half-roll at me. "Your words. Fence. Mrs. Halloway. Last spring."

I sighed, took the roll, and let it go. Some arguments you win by losing.

*They are only questions,* Vesper said, low and steady, a hand on the shoulder of my mind. *And whatever comes asking — you will not face it alone. That was true a year ago. It is truer now.*

"I know," I said aloud, and Juniper squeezed my hand because she heard it too, or felt it, or just knew me — which amounted to the same thing.

By midday the whole family had gathered, the way they did on month's end, which had become our holiday without anyone officially declaring it. Sable arrived singing something old and slow, guided by Bramble's arm, her white eyes catching the sun. She'd taken to calling the garden "the listening place," because — she said — the ground here remembered everything that had been poured into it, and if you sat quietly enough, it hummed the memories back. She'd written three songs about this garden already. In one of them, I was portrayed as significantly more graceful than I am. I have never corrected her.

We ate rolls and late-summer plums and Bramble's honey cakes, and Wren retold the bridge story — third time this month — adding a fourth man and a wider river, and nobody stopped her, because the truth of a story matters less than what it keeps alive. That was the trade we ran now, here above the old shrine. Not bottled storms. Not borrowed years. Small honest commerce in possible things: seedlings and songs, charms against nightmares, remedies Bramble brewed, the occasional memory carefully re-gifted — *here, this one's yours, I kept it safe* — passed hand to hand among people learning that not everything precious must be owned to be shared.

In the late afternoon, Juniper and I walked the rows together, checking the beds the way we once checked ledgers, and she told me the story again. Our story. The thirteenth moon, the lantern, the stair that cost me everything. She tells it differently each time — adds details, drops others, lets Wren be braver and Bramble wiser and me more frightened than I probably was. At first this troubled me, hearing my own life drift like a boat unmoored. Now I understand: she isn't changing the story. She's keeping it. A story that never changes is dead; a story tended grows like the rowan, rings upon rings, each year's telling wrapped around the last.

"And then," she said, at the part where the contract burned, "the lantern went dark, and I picked you up, and you weighed nothing at all. Like carrying a bundle of laundry. All those years of being the strong one, and it turns out my big sister weighs about as much as regret."

"You're a terrible eulogist."

"You weren't dead, you were just extremely empty." She bumped her shoulder against mine. "Best trade you ever made."

"The only trade I ever made for free."

We stopped at the rowan. It had settled in already, impossibly — Bramble swore she'd done nothing, but the sapling stood a hand taller than at dawn, leaves glossy, leaning slightly left, toward the light, exactly as Wren had accused. Beneath it, the ground hummed its low remembering hum. Above it, the evening was assembling itself out of gold and violet, and the moon was rising — thin now, a fingernail paring, beginning its next long count from one.

Thirteen moons ago, a moon like this one had marked the start of everything terrible. Thirteen moons later, another turn completed, and here we stood: not unscarred, not unpaid, but upright. Growing.

"Do you ever miss it?" Juniper asked quietly. "What you gave them? Being able to remember yourself?"

I considered it honestly, the way I now tried to consider everything — no margins, no hidden entries. Somewhere in the dark below us was a version of me complete and entire, filed away in a court's cold accounting, and some nights I dreamed in a voice I didn't recognize and woke with words in my mouth I'd never learned. Those were the missing pieces, knocking.

"Sometimes," I said. "But then I remember—" I stopped. Smiled. "Well. Then someone reminds me. That's rather the point, isn't it? I don't have to carry it alone. That was the whole lesson. Took a god going silent, a guild going rotten, and a year of my life to learn it, but I'm told I'm a slow student."

"The slowest," Juniper said fondly. "Vesper says so constantly."

"He does not."

*He does,* Vesper said warmly, from everywhere and nowhere, from the shrine below and the rowan above and the space between my sister's hand and mine. *Constantly. With great affection. Slow students make the deepest roots.*

The first star came out over the mountain — the mountain, still there, still keeping its secrets, still humming its faint under-mountain hum that only Sable and I could hear. Something stirred in it tonight, faint and far off, like a page turning in a sealed room. Old questions, Wren had said. Gray coats and old coins.

Tomorrow, maybe, we'd talk about it. Tomorrow we'd sharpen our small honest weapons — songs and charms and stubborn hands — the way we'd learned to. Tonight, the moon began its count from one, and the rowan leaned into the light, and my sister stood beside me in the garden we'd grown out of the ruins of everything, and Vesper's voice wrapped around us like a candle flame against the coming dark:

*Begin again,* he said, as he said every month's end, every ending, every opening line. *Begin again.*

And we did.
