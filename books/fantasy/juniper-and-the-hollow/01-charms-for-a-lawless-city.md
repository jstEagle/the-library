## 1. Charms for a Lawless City

The fog came off the Greyharrow harbor in the hour before dawn, thick as spilled milk and smelling of tar, brine, and the particular rot of fish left too long in the sun. Ivo Thackeray had learned to read that fog the way other people read weather. It told her when the night-market would be slow, when the harbormaster's men would be drunk enough to look the other way, and — most importantly — when the wardens were out.

This morning the fog said *stay small*.

She set up her stall anyway, because staying small was a thing you did with your voice and your posture, not with absence. A witch who vanished entirely was a witch someone went looking for. A witch who sold dried lavender and knotted thread at the edge of Fishgut Row was furniture. Furniture didn't get hanged.

Her stall was three planks on two barrels, wedged between a chandler's shop and a shuttered pawnhouse at the crook of the row where the cobblestones gave way to mud. She arranged her wares with the care of a woman laying out an argument: bundles of rosemary for memory, salt in twist of paper for thresholds, river-stones worn smooth and drilled through with holes — *worry stones*, she called them, though they did nothing but give anxious hands something to rub. And beneath the table's lip, wrapped in oilcloth where no honest eye would fall: the knots.

Luck-knots. Seven-strand braids of red cord and grey wool, each one closed with a stitch only she knew how to make, each one carrying a whisper of working folded into its heart like a moth in amber. They were small magics. Humble magics. The kind Juniper had called *kitchen-craft* back when kitchen-craft was still legal, before the Wardens' Edict turned every hedge-witch in the borderlands into kindling for their own pyres.

Twelve years since she'd last spoken his name aloud. Twelve years since she'd walked out of that cottage on the moor with her satchel full of stolen patterns and her heart full of something she refused, even now, in the privacy of her own skull, to call by its true name.

She'd been selling luck-knots ever since. It was either penance or habit. She'd stopped trying to tell the difference.

"Oi. Thackeray."

Brann Odo came down the row like a ship coming into harbor — all momentum, no grace. He was a dockworker built like a capstan, forearms scarred white with rope-burn, and he'd been buying charms from her for three years without once asking what was inside them. That was why she liked him. That, and he paid in real coin.

"You've got the look of a man who needs more than rosemary," Ivo said, not looking up from her arrangement.

"I've got the look of a man who's been promised the *Merrilee*'s run to Vellmark and doesn't trust the promise." He planted both fists on her table, making the worry stones jump. "Captain says the sea-lanes are cursed this season. Says ships are going dark past the shoals. Whole crews." He lowered his voice, which for Brann meant it dropped to roughly the volume of normal speech. "You know anything about that?"

Ivo kept her hands moving, folding paper, sorting cord. "I know the sea's always hungry and captains are always looking for something to blame besides their own navigation."

"It's not just the sea." He glanced over his shoulder, down the row toward the water, where the masts stood up out of the fog like the ribs of drowned things. "My cousin's girl works the tide-registry. She says the ledgers have gone strange. Ships logged in that never come in. Ships come in that were never logged out." He turned back, and there was something under the bluster now, something that looked, uncomfortably, like fear. "I want a knot. The strong one. Whatever you charge the rich merchants, double it, and I'll pay it."

"The strong one" took four hours to braid and cost her a headache like a nail behind the eye. She reached beneath the table for the oilcloth bundle, and her fingers hesitated there a moment longer than they should have.

Here was the arithmetic she never let herself finish: every knot she sold was a small theft against the world. Not a big one — nothing like what she'd done, nothing that mattered in any ledger worth keeping — but a theft nonetheless. Magic taken from the weave without asking, worked into cord, sold for copper. Juniper had taught her that magic was a conversation. *You ask*, he used to say, palms open over the hearth-flame, *and sometimes the world answers, and you say thank you, and that is the whole of the craft.* She had learned instead that you could take without asking if your hands were quick and your conscience was quiet, and hers had been very quiet for a very long time.

She unwrapped the bundle. The knots lay in their rows like sleeping eels.

"For the *Merrilee*," she said, selecting one, "you want this. Tie it to the mast-foot, not your neck — neck-charms drown with the man, mast-charms drown with the ship, and I've only ever known one man who could swim in armor."

Brann laughed, a sound like a crate dropping. He counted coins onto the table — real ones, Greyharrow mint, the wolf-and-tower stamp worn soft — and then, because he was Brann, he lingered.

"You hear about the wards?" he said.

Ivo's hands did not stop moving. This was a skill she had cultivated over twelve years: the ability to keep her hands busy while everything behind her face went still and cold as well-water.

"What about them?"

"Border wards. Up along Serle way." He scratched under his jaw. "Fisherfolk say the light on the ward-stones has been going strange. Flickering. Like a candle in a draft. Old Man Corrick swears he saw one go out altogether, up past the salt-marsh, and when he went to look at it the stone was cold as a corpse." He shook his head. "Wardens say it's nothing. Wardens say a lot of things."

"Wardens hang people," Ivo said mildly, "so I try not to have opinions they might disagree with."

That earned another laugh, but Brann's eyes stayed on her a beat too long, and Ivo felt the familiar prickle between her shoulder blades — the sense of being seen a half-inch deeper than she allowed anyone. He was a good man. Good men noticed things. It was why she kept her transactions short and her smiles professional and her history locked in a box at the bottom of her chest, weighted with stones, sunk deep.

He touched two fingers to his brow — a dockman's salute — and lumbered off down the row with his luck-knot coiled in his fist like a rescued snake.

Ivo exhaled and began restacking her wares, and that was when the child appeared.

* * *

Greyharrow children were gulls: they arrived in shrieking flocks or not at all, and they descended on anything shiny. This one came alone, which was the first odd thing. A girl, maybe eight, maybe nine, thin as a whittled stick, dressed in clothes that had been handed down through at least three owners and none of them kind. Her hair was the color of wet sand and her eyes were the color of nothing much at all — grey, flat, the grey of the harbor in November.

She stood at the edge of the stall and stared at the worry stones.

"Those are a penny," Ivo said. "And before you ask, no, they don't do anything. They're for holding. Some folk find that helps."

"I don't want those." The girl's voice was oddly level. Children in Greyharrow learned early to bargain; this one bargained like a customs officer. "I want a knot. A red one."

"Luck-knots are five pennies."

The girl dug in her pocket and held out her hand, palm up, fingers closed. When she opened them, a single coin sat in the center of her grubby palm.

It was a silver piece. Too much change for a five-penny charm, for a start. But that wasn't what made Ivo's stomach drop.

The coin was wrong. Not clipped, not counterfeit-worn, not any of the dozen frauds Ivo had learned to spot in twelve years of market work. It was *wrong* the way a note played on a broken instrument is wrong — the shape right, the pitch off. It sat in the child's palm and it *hummed*. Not a sound, exactly. Something below sound. Ivo felt it in her molars, in the bones of her wrists, in the place behind her sternum where her craft lived. The hum climbed into her like water climbing a wick.

Every knot on her table stirred. Just slightly. Just enough that the red cords lifted their ends like sleeping snakes tasting air.

Ivo did not reach for the coin. Reaching for it felt, in some wordless and animal way, like reaching into a snare.

"Where did you get this?" she asked. She kept her voice light. Market-light. The voice of a woman discussing lavender.

"Found it," the girl said.

"Found it where?"

The girl's flat grey eyes came up and fixed on Ivo's face, and for a moment — one long, cold moment — Ivo had the vertiginous certainty that she was not looking at a child at all, but at something wearing a child the way fog wears a streetlamp. Then the girl blinked, and she was a girl again, shivering in her dead men's clothes, holding out a coin she shouldn't have.

"At the church," she said. "Under the floor. There's a hole under the floor and things come up out of it at night and Mama says don't look but I looked." She shrugged, a small, terrible shrug. "There's lots of them down there. They hum."

Ivo's mouth had gone dry. Down the row, the fog was thickening rather than lifting, though the sun should have been burning it off an hour ago. The light had a bruised quality to it, yellow-grey, like old paper. Somewhere out on the water a bell buoy rang, and the sound carried strangely, arriving late and doubled, as if the air itself had grown slow.

"Take a knot," Ivo heard herself say. "Any one you like. Keep the coin."

Something flickered across the girl's face — disappointment? hunger? — and then smoothed away. She selected a luck-knot with surprising delicacy, wound it twice around her wrist, and tucked the coin back into her pocket after all, despite the offer, despite everything, and Ivo found she could not bring herself to insist.

"Thank you," the girl said. And then, turning to go, pausing at the corner of the stall like an afterthought: "You knew him, didn't you? The one who taught you the knots."

The harbor went very quiet. Even the gulls stopped.

"I don't—" Ivo began.

"He hums too," the girl said. "Down under the floor. All of them do. But his hum is different. It knows your name."

Then she was gone, down the row and around the chandler's corner, small feet slapping mud, and the ordinary noise of Greyharrow crashed back in — cart wheels, gull-cries, a fishwife swearing at her son — as if it had only been waiting politely for the strangeness to pass.

Ivo stood behind her stall with her hands flat on the planks and her heart going like a drum in a procession.

*His hum is different. It knows your name.*

There was exactly one person alive or otherwise who had taught her the knots. There was exactly one grave she had never visited, because she had never been sure there was a body in it. Twelve years ago she had done a thing — one thing, one necessary, unforgivable thing — and run south to Greyharrow and buried the doing so deep that some mornings she could almost believe the life she'd built here was real: the stall, the lavender, the honest coppers, the woman named Thackeray who had never been anyone's apprentice, never betrayed anyone, never—

A knot on the table twitched.

Not stirred this time. Twitched — deliberately, unmistakably, the way a finger twitches. The nearest luck-knot, seven strands of red cord, rotated slowly on the planks until its trailing end pointed north. Toward the mountain. Toward Serle, forty leagues up the coast road, where the border wards stood cold as corpses and the fog was rolling in from somewhere that was not the sea.

Ivo looked at it for a long time.

Around her, Greyharrow went about its morning, blind and loud and blessedly ignorant. Out on the water, the bell buoy rang again, late and doubled. Under the floor of a church somewhere in this city, things hummed in the dark, and one of them, apparently, remembered her name.

"Well," Ivo Thackeray said to no one, to the fog, to the ghost of a man she had spent twelve years not mourning. She picked up the pointing knot and felt its hum crawl up her arm like a hand closing around her wrist. "Well. You always did hold a grudge."

Above the harbor, unseen through the murk, the first star of the evening sky flickered — and went out.
