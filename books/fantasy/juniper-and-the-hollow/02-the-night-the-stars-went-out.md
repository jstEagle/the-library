## 2. The Night the Stars Went Out

The luck-knot had barely left Ivo's hands when the first ward-stone screamed.

She felt it before she heard it — a shudder through the soles of her boots, up through her shinbones, into the roots of her teeth, as though Greyharrow itself were a struck bell and something enormous had just rung it wrong. The coin in her apron pocket went cold against her hip. Every charm on her person — the salt-thread at her wrist, the iron nail wrapped in red wool, the little knotted cord she wore under her collar like a scar — went silent all at once.

That was the thing about charms that no one who didn't make them ever understood: they were never quiet. A working charm hummed. It was a small warmth in the fingers, a faint sweetness in the nose, a sense of *company*, of thread pulled taut between the maker and the made. Ivo had lived with that hum for twenty years, and for twelve of them she had hated how much she missed it whenever she forced herself to stop working. Now it stopped all at once, everywhere, and the silence was so total it was less like quiet and more like deafness.

Then the sky went out.

Not clouded. Not dimmed. *Out* — the way a lamp goes out when someone pinches the wick with wet fingers. One moment there were stars over Greyharrow, the ragged scatter of them the city always got between the chimney smoke and the sea haze, and the next there was nothing. No moon either; the moon had been three days from full, fat and yellow over the harbor cranes, and now there was only a hole where it should have been, a absence shaped exactly like a moon, which was somehow worse than if the whole sky had been taken uniformly. The dark came down like a lid.

Ivo stood in the mouth of Cooper's Lane with a dockworker's payment still warm in her palm and watched the world lose its ceiling.

For three heartbeats nobody moved. Then Greyharrow began to scream.

* * *

It is strange, she thought later, what a city does when the stars die. She had imagined — in the abstract way one imagines disasters — that panic would look like running. It did not, mostly. It looked like standing very still in doorways with lamps held up uselessly, as though light could be thrown at the sky like a stone. It looked like the fishwives on Gull Street falling to their knees in their aprons, not praying so much as *bracing*, the way you brace when a wave comes. It looked like old Tammas Wren — no relation to anyone, just an old man who sold rope — climbing his own roof with a lantern because he could not bear to be under anything anymore.

And underneath all of it, threading through it, the sound Ivo would remember longest: bells. Every temple bell in Greyharrow ringing at once, unstruck, which meant the wards were ringing them, which meant the wards were still doing *something* — dying loudly, the way a drowning man shouts.

"The border wards," said the dockworker beside her. He was a big man, Brannick, with hands like mooring line, and he had bought his luck-knot not ten minutes ago for the safe passage of his brother's boat. He was staring up into the starless dark with his mouth open. "The border wards have gone, haven't they. That's what that is."

"That's what that is," Ivo said.

"Nothing's gone over the border in six hundred years," Brannick said, in the tone of a man reciting scripture to keep himself from weeping. "My gran used to say. Nothing crosses. Nothing comes down off Serle. Six hundred years."

"Yes," Ivo said. Her voice sounded far away to herself, flat and small. "Well."

Because here was the thing she could not say to Brannick or to the screaming fishwives or to the wardens already lighting their signal-fires along the seawall: the border wards had not simply failed. Charms did not fail like that. Wards did not fail like that. She had spent twelve years making small, honest, careful magic — knots and patience, Juniper used to say, knots and patience — and everything she knew told her that a working did not *stop*. It frayed. It thinned. It leaked at the edges like an old roof. It did not go out all at once, everywhere, like a snuffed candle, unless something had eaten it.

Something had eaten it.

And beneath the dread, beneath the cold spreading through her chest like water finding the seams of a hull, there was the other thing. The worse thing. The thought she could not stop thinking, which arrived fully formed and terrible:

*This has found me.*

* * *

She made herself walk home. That was the discipline of it, the part of the craft no one wrote songs about: when the world ends, finish your errands, count your stock, bar your door. Panic killed hedge-witches faster than wardens did. So she walked through the chaos of Greyharrow with her head down and her hood up, past temples disgorging their congregations into the streets, past a warden patrol moving with the grim purpose of men who suddenly had nothing left to guard, past a child crying not with fear but with the particular outraged grief of the very young, who know viscerally that the world is supposed to hold together and have just been shown otherwise.

Her rooms above the chandler's shop on Ropewalk were dark, of course. Everything was dark. She lit her one candle with hands that shook only slightly, and that small steadiness almost made her laugh. Twelve years of hiding her craft, of selling charms out of a basket like a peddler sells ribbons, of flinching every time a warden's boots rang on cobbles — and now the sky itself had committed the greater crime, and no one was looking at her at all.

She took down her stock box.

It was a plain thing, cedar, iron-cornered, older than her trade in Greyharrow. Inside, nested in wool, lay the charms she kept for herself: the ones too strong to sell, the ones with pieces of her actual self knotted into them, because that was the price of real work and she had long ago stopped pretending otherwise. She lifted them out one by one and laid them on the table, and one by one confirmed what she already knew.

Dead. All dead.

The storm-knot her mother's pattern, gray as ash. The fever-charm she'd made during the sweating sickness, still smelling faintly of juniper berries — she'd named the habit after her master and had never once let herself think about why. The traveler's cord, the hearth-blessing, the little woven eye that was supposed to watch for lies. Dead, dead, dead. She touched each one and felt nothing come back, the way you touch a cheek for breath.

Twelve years of work, gone in a single night. And not just hers — every charm in Greyharrow, every charm on the coast, every knot and sigil and whispered blessing between here and the border, all of it fed by the same deep current, the same slow pulse of power that came up out of the earth and was caught and carried and *regulated* by the great border wards on Mount Serle. Cut the wards and you cut the river. Everything downstream went dry at once.

She sat back on her stool and looked at her dead craft spread across the table like a funeral, and she thought, with a clarity that felt almost like relief: *Someone unmade the wards. On purpose. From inside.*

And then she saw the last charm.

It had been at the bottom of the box, wrapped in its own cloth, and she had almost not unwrapped it because she had almost forgotten it was there — which was impossible, and she knew it was impossible, because she remembered the night she'd made it, eleven years ago, drunk on cheap wine and grief on the anniversary of a death she had caused, and she remembered deciding to forget it, and forgetting being a kind of spell all its own.

She unwrapped it anyway.

It was a simple thing. A knot, really — a single length of blue thread worked into a pattern she had learned at nineteen from a woman with ink-stained fingers and a laugh like a crow, a pattern called the north-seeking hitch, which hedge-witches used to find their way home when they'd wandered too far. She had tied it around a sliver of something — wood, old wood, silvered with age, from a doorframe she had once leaned against while learning her letters — and she had spoken no words over it, because words would have made it real, and she had wanted it to stay a secret even from herself.

Every charm on the table was dead. This one was burning.

Not alight — glowing, a soft steady blue-white like foxfire, like the last coal in a banked fire, like — and her stomach turned over — like starlight. It pulsed gently in her cupped palms, patient as a heartbeat, and when she tilted it, the glow leaned. North. Always north. Toward the mountains.

Toward Serle.

"No," Ivo said aloud, to the charm, to the empty room, to the dead sky through her window. Her voice cracked on it. "No, you don't. You're thread and patience and a piece of a doorframe, and you don't get to—"

The charm pulsed again, warm against her fingers, and it felt — this was the unbearable part, this was the part that made her set it down on the table and wipe her hands on her apron as though it had burned her — it felt *familiar*. It felt like the kitchen of the apprenticeship cottage on a winter evening. It felt like ink stains and crow-laughter and the smell of juniper berries. It felt like being nineteen and loved and utterly unaware of what she was about to do.

It felt like Juniper.

Ivo put both hands flat on the table and breathed until the shaking stopped, mostly.

Because here was the arithmetic she had been refusing to do for twelve years, and the night had finally forced it: the wards had been eaten from inside. The Hollow Court — the story every child on the coast knew, the court beneath the mountain that traded in souls, that the wards had been built to wall away forever — the Hollow Court was awake, or stirring, or hungry. And Juniper Thackeray had died twelve years ago in a way that had never quite made sense, a wasting, a fading, a going-out, and Ivo had signed the papers that sent her to the mountain infirmary and had never once asked the questions that mattered, because asking them would have led back to the thing Ivo herself had done, the bargain she had made, the name she had given—

She had never let herself finish that thought. Not once in twelve years. It always turned aside at the last moment, like a path overgrown.

Tonight, with the stars gone and a dead woman's knot glowing on her table like a compass needle, the path lay open and bare all the way to its end.

*I sold her,* Ivo thought. *Whatever she was to the Court, whatever they took — it started with me. I gave them the thread and they pulled.*

The charm glowed. North. Patient. Insistent, in the way of things that have waited a long time.

Outside, Greyharrow rang with bells and fear. Somewhere a temple choir had begun to sing the old warding hymn, badly, voices cracking. Somewhere the wardens were already saying the word *witchcraft* in the low satisfied tones of men handed a solution to a problem they didn't understand. By morning there would be pyres. There were always pyres, after. Hedge-witches made convenient sacrifices to frightened gods.

Ivo looked at the charm a long time.

She thought about staying. She was good at staying; she had made an art of it, twelve years of small rooms and small magics and looking carefully away. Staying was safe. Staying was the whole shape of her life, the walls she had built out of guilt and called prudence.

But the charm was warm, and the sky was empty, and somewhere under Mount Serle something was wearing the dark like a crown, and somewhere in that dark — she knew it the way you know a scar aches before rain — was a debt with her name on both sides of it.

"Oh, Juniper," she said quietly. "What did you leave in the walls?"

The charm answered the only way a knot can: it glowed, and leaned north, and waited.

Ivo took down her traveling cloak.
