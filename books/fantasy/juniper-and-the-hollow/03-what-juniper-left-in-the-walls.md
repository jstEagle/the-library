## 3. What Juniper Left in the Walls

The charm pulled north like a fish on a line.

Ivo had made it eleven years ago, in the last autumn of her apprenticeship — a simple wayfinding knot, three strands of red wool braided over a splinter of rowan, the kind of thing Juniper used to sell at market for two coppers and a kind word. It had hung forgotten among the dead charms in her satchel through twelve years of running, and now it glowed against her ribs like a coal someone had breathed on, tugging her up out of Greyharrow's drowned streets toward the hills.

She did not want to go. She went anyway.

There was no arguing with a thing like that. She had spent half her life learning what charms could do and the other half pretending she'd learned nothing at all, but some knowledge sat too deep to unlearn: you did not fight a working that had finally found its purpose. You followed it, and you hated yourself a little for how much easier it was to follow than to stop.

The road out of the city was a river of the frightened. Carts piled with hens and children and grandmothers' portraits; a man leading a horse that had gone blind with terror; women walking barefoot because they'd given their shoes to their daughters. Nobody spoke above a murmur, as if the dark overhead were an ear pressed close, listening. Ivo kept her hood up and her hands in her pockets, one of them closed around the burning knot, and let the crowd carry her as far as the north gate before the charm's pull diverged from the exodus and she had to strike off alone, up the drover's track where the gorse grew black and shoulder-high.

Behind her, Greyharrow burned lamps all along its walls, a scatter of defiant orange under a sky with nothing in it. Ahead there was only the mountain, Serle, a shape darker than the dark, wearing what remained of the night like a stolen cloak.

*You knew,* she told herself, climbing. *Some part of you always knew.* The stars hadn't simply gone out. Stars didn't go out. Wards didn't fail. Things were taken, or given away, and either way there was a hand involved, and hands could be found.

And the charm in her pocket was hers — her own work, her own knots, her own clumsy apprentice stitching — and it had not died with everything else. Which meant it was anchored to something the crown's hunger couldn't eat. Or something it refused to touch. Or something it was saving.

She walked until her legs shook, and near dawn — though *dawn* meant nothing now except a grey bruise on the eastern edge of the world — the charm stopped pulling and began instead to burn steady, like a held breath, and Ivo looked up and saw the cottage.

* * *

It stood in a fold of the hillside where the drover's track bent away from the sea, low-roofed, turf-sodded, its chimney leaning like an old man telling a long story. Juniper's cottage. Twelve years since Ivo had seen it, and it looked as if the years had simply walked around it, unwilling to come in.

The garden had gone wild, but wild in Juniper's way: feverfew crowding the doorstone, wormwood grown into a grey-green hedge, rows of something white-flowering that Ivo didn't recognize and suspected Juniper had bred on purpose just to be mysterious. The beehive by the wall was empty, its straw collapsed into a soft mound, but a single bee — one bee, in this ruined season, under this starless sky — circled the doorway twice and vanished inside.

Ivo stood at the gate for a long time.

The last time she had stood here she had been nineteen and running, with a satchel of stolen charms and a lie in her mouth, and she had not looked back because looking back was a weakness Juniper had warned her about and also, Ivo understood now, the whole of what Juniper herself had done wrong: she had never once stopped looking back at the girl she'd taken in, hoping to see her turn around.

*I'm sorry* was a coin so worn it wouldn't spend. Ivo pushed open the gate anyway.

Inside, the air was cold and sweet with dried herbs and dust and old smoke, and underneath it all, faintly, impossibly, juniper berries. The main room was exactly as she remembered it and nothing like it at all: the great hearth swept clean, the table scrubbed pale, shelves of jars ranked in careful order, each labeled in Juniper's slanted hand — *mugwort, elderflower, salt from the grey shore* — labels that should have faded decades ago and hadn't, because Juniper had written them into the wood with more than ink.

On the table lay a book, half-burned.

Ivo's heart did something complicated and painful. She crossed the room without deciding to, and picked it up, and knew the weight of it in her hands the way you know a face. Juniper's journal. Leather covers, brass corners, the spine cracked from years of being carried in a coat pocket. But the right-hand third of it was charred black, pages fused into a brittle fan, edges crumbling at a breath. Burned deliberately — the burn started clean at a torn margin, someone holding pages into a fire and then changing their mind, or running out of courage, or running out of time.

She sat down at the table, in Juniper's chair, which groaned under her exactly as it always had, and opened the journal to the first unburned page.

* * *

*Third of Harrowmonth.*

*The girl broke my best luck-knot today trying to see how it worked. I told her a charm is not a clock; you do not open it to find the mechanism. She said if she can't take it apart she'll never make her own. I said perhaps. I said perhaps is the most dangerous word in craft.*

*She has her mother's hands. Quick, and always reaching.*

Ivo read it twice. Her eyes stung. She turned the page with exaggerated care, as if the paper might bruise.

*Twelfth of Harrowmonth.*

*A man came from over the mountain today. Fine coat, wrong boots — city boots, polished, on a man who claimed to walk the high passes. He asked strange questions about the wards on Serle. Who keeps them. How old they are. Whether anyone still remembers the older wards beneath them. I told him ward-keeping is Vey family business and none of mine. He smiled like a door closing and bought a fever-charm he did not need.*

*Tam Vey came by after, chasing his father's lost dog. Good boy. Watches everything, says little. His father built the border wards and it has made him proud and afraid in equal measure, the way builders are.*

Ivo frowned. The handwriting had changed here — smaller, pressed harder, the pen digging furrows. She turned pages faster. Most of the middle of the journal survived, though the burn had eaten deep into the later entries, and she read with the particular dread of someone descending stairs in a house they thought they knew.

*Last of Harrowmonth.*

*He came again. Corvane Ash. He does not pretend now. He says the Court under Serle is waking hungry, that the crown it stole needs feeding, that the border wards are a meal waiting to be plated. He says there is a price for silence and a greater price for noise, and asks me which I can afford.*

*I asked him what he wanted. He said: not me. He looked at the door where your coat hangs, girl, though you were out gathering yarrow and don't know any of this is happening. He said: the young ones burn brighter. The Court pays better for bright souls.*

*He left a token. A black coin that hums when held. I have buried it under the rowan and I am writing this down so that if anything happens to me, someone will know it was not accident, not age, not weather. It was a sale. Everything with that man is a sale.*

Ivo's hands had gone cold. The black coin. The child in Greyharrow had paid her with a coin that hummed wrong — three days ago, a lifetime ago, before the sky emptied. She had thrown it in the harbor. Hadn't she? She tried to remember, and couldn't, and the not-knowing sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone.

She kept reading. The entries grew shorter, the penmanship deteriorating, and then came the entry that stopped her breath.

*They have named a debt against me. Ash came at moonset with papers, real papers, sealed with the Court's hollow sigil — a ring within a ring within nothing. He says the debt is old. Older than his office. He says it was incurred the day I took in a foundling child whose name was already written in the Court's ledger, whose soul was promised before she could walk, and that the debt has been accruing interest for sixteen years.*

*He says there are two ways to settle it. The child's soul, or mine.*

*She is nineteen. She has her mother's hands and her father's temper and no idea what she is worth, thank every merciful star. I have been saving for this without knowing it — every charm sold, every winter laid in, every kindness banked. A soul is not spent all at once. Mine is large and slow-burning, like good peat. Hers would go up like straw.*

*I have signed. Do not look for me to regret it. Regret is for people who had a choice they could live with.*

*But I have done one thing he does not know about. One small thing, hedge-craft, beneath the Court's notice precisely because it is small. When she was six and fevered nearly to death, I kept a piece of her back — a splinter of her soul, no bigger than a knot of wool, worked into a charm to hold her life together. She recovered. She never knew. And when she ran away she took my satchel by mistake, the old one, the one with the early charms in it, the ones I taught her to make with her own quick reaching hands.*

*Somewhere in that satchel is a piece of Juniper. And somewhere in the world is a girl carrying it, who thinks she abandoned me, and who must never, ever come looking — because if the Court learns what she carries, they will want it, and through it they will want her.*

*So: stay away, stubborn girl. Stay lost. Be safe and small and ordinary and hate me if it helps. Hate is a fine warm coat for winter.*

*— J.*

The journal slipped from Ivo's fingers onto the table.

The room tilted. She gripped the table's edge and put her head down and breathed, and the smell of juniper berries rose around her, and twelve years rearranged themselves in the dark behind her eyes.

She hadn't taken the satchel by mistake. She remembered now — remembering hurt, remembering was like pulling a splinter from a wound too long healed over — Juniper pressing it into her hands at the gate, saying *take the old one, love, the new one's got a broken strap*, and Ivo, nineteen and furious and already lying, had believed it was carelessness. Had believed everything was carelessness. Had built twelve years of guilt on the foundation of a deliberate kindness.

A piece of her own soul. In a charm. In the satchel. In the satchel she still carried, because she had never been able to bring herself to throw away the last things Juniper taught her to make.

Her hands were shaking as she unbuckled it. She spilled the contents across the table — the dead luck-knots, the gray fever-tassels, the wayfinder that had led her home. And there, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, a charm she had never been able to identify: a knot of many colors, dense as a fist, humming faintly with a warmth that had never faded while every other working died.

It was glowing now. Softly. Steadily. Like a coal breathing.

Like a heartbeat.

"Oh," Ivo said aloud, to the empty room, to the woman who wasn't in it. "Oh, you clever, stubborn, impossible old woman."

The grief came then, all of it, twelve years of it, and she let it — forehead on the table, shoulders shaking, the journal's burnt edge crumbling beside her cheek like black snow. She cried for the girl who ran and the woman who stayed and the piece of a soul that had waited patiently in a satchel for its owner to stop being ashamed and come home.

When it was spent, she wiped her face on her sleeve, the way Juniper used to tell her not to, and read the journal again, properly this time, hunting.

Ash. The Hollow Court. A debt of souls, sold and brokered, the wards a meal. If Juniper's soul had been payment, it would not have been destroyed — souls were currency here, Ash had said so himself in his fine-coated way. Currency was kept. Shelved. *Filed.*

Which meant Juniper might not be gone. Might be *held*. Dim, maybe, the way a lamp is dim under a bushel — but intact, recoverable, if someone was fool enough to walk into the Court beneath Mount Serle and ask for her back.

Someone would have to be that fool.

Ivo looked at the glowing knot in her palm — the piece of herself she'd carried unknowing, the piece that had kept her alive at six and might keep her sane in whatever dark was coming. The Court would want it. Through it, they would want her. Juniper had written *stay away* in letters dug deep enough to bleed.

Juniper had also, Ivo thought, spent her whole life teaching one lesson above all others: that power is not taken. That it is made, slowly, in small stitches, by people patient enough to keep working when the grand magics fail. That hedge-craft — humble, stubborn, made of wool and rowan and attention — outlasts crowns.

"You hid yourself in the smallest thing you could find," Ivo murmured. "So that's where I'll look."

Outside, the lightless sky pressed down on the wild garden. Somewhere on the slopes of Serle, in a twilight market she had heard whispered of only in smugglers' songs, souls changed hands like coins, and one of them was Juniper's.

Ivo buckled the satchel. She took the journal, wrapped it in oilcloth, and tucked it against her heart next to the knot. At the door she paused, and — feeling foolish and doing it anyway — straightened the jar of mugwort that had drifted a finger's width out of alignment on its shelf.

"I'm coming," she said. "And I'm bringing everything you left in the walls."

Behind her, in the empty cottage, every label on every jar turned very slightly, all at once, as if the house itself had nodded — and far up the black flank of the mountain, something answered: a sound like a bell rung underwater, and the sense, sudden and intimate, of being *seen*.

The road to Serle was open. Something at the top of it already knew she was coming.
