## 4. The Ward-Keeper's Son

The road north out of Greyharrow was called the Pilgrim's Stair, though no pilgrim had climbed it in living memory and the only souls who used it now were smugglers, tax-fleeers, and the occasional desperate lover running toward a marriage the families had forbidden. Ivo had taken it twice before in her life: once at nineteen, fleeing south with a satchel of stolen charms and a heart full of ash, and once at twenty-three, when she'd gotten as far as the second switchback before turning back, unable to make her feet carry her any closer to the cottage she had abandoned.

Now she climbed it a third time, and the mountain did not want her.

She felt it in the way the path seemed to lengthen under her boots, each mile taking two to cross. She felt it in the wind, which came down off Serle's shoulders carrying a smell like struck flint and old copper — the smell of wards burning out, though there were no wards left to burn. And she felt it most of all in the charm at her throat, Juniper's luck-knot, which had not stopped its slow, insistent pull since the night the stars went out. It tugged against its cord like a hound on a leash, northward, always northward, patient as grief.

Three days she had walked. The lowlands behind her had been all panic: refugees clogging the roads with carts and crying children, wardens bellowing orders that no one obeyed, priests of every stripe declaring the darkness a punishment for someone else's sins. But up here the world had gone quiet in a way that was worse than noise. The villages she passed stood shuttered even at noon. In one, every door had been marked with chalk — small, careful sigils, hedge-craft, the kind of protection people made when they no longer believed anyone official would protect them. Someone had tried to ward their whole village with kitchen salt and good intentions.

It hadn't worked. Of course it hadn't. Nothing worked anymore. The sky above Serle was a lid of black, starless even at midday, as if the mountain had swallowed that whole quarter of heaven and not yet digested it.

Ivo kept her hood up and her hands in her pockets and told herself she was walking toward answers, not toward the thing she had run from. Twelve years of practice at that particular lie, and still it sat poorly on her tongue.

* * *

She heard the horses before she saw them — or rather, she heard the absence of them, the way the birds along the roadside went silent all at once, in a wave, like a curtain being drawn. Then hoofbeats, muffled by pine duff, and the jingle of tack, and a voice saying something low and sharp that she couldn't make out.

There was no cover worth the name. The pines grew thick but their trunks rose clean and bare for ten feet, and Ivo was forty-one years old and three days tired. So she did what she had learned to do instead of fighting or fleeing, the lesson Corvane Ash had beaten into her in another life: she arranged her face into something unremarkable and kept walking, a hedge-wife going nowhere in particular.

They came around the bend at a trot. Two riders, then a third leading a packhorse. Not wardens — no livery, no warden-white armbands, and wardens this far from Greyharrow traveled in companies of twelve, not three. These wore oiled leather and travel-stained wool, and they moved like men who had been riding hard and sleeping badly.

The lead rider reined in when he saw her. He was young — younger than her, certainly, though the dark circles under his eyes argued otherwise — with a long face and hair the color of wet slate cut short and practical. He looked at her the way one looks at a door that might be locked.

"Off the road," he said. "Please."

"I'm just traveling," Ivo said. "Same as you."

"No one's just traveling anymore." He said it without heat, almost wearily, as if he'd said it a hundred times in three days. "Roads are closed by order of the Interim Council. You'll turn around."

"The Interim Council," Ivo repeated. "That's new."

"It's four days old." Something flickered across his face — bitterness, maybe, or grief wearing bitterness's clothes. "Formed after the wards fell. After my father—" He stopped himself. His hand tightened on the reins until the horse sidestepped. "Turn around. Go back to the city. It isn't safe up here."

"Neither is down there, from what I saw."

"That's different." He said it too quickly, too firmly, the way people say things they need to be true. Behind him, one of his companions — a heavyset man with a crossbow slung across his back — shifted in his saddle, and Ivo marked the movement without appearing to mark it.

"Who are you?" she asked. "Soldiers? Wardens?"

"We're nobody." The word came out flat and final. "Go home."

But she didn't go home, because home was a rented room over a dyer's shop with her charms packed in a false-bottomed trunk, and because the knot at her throat chose that exact moment to pull — hard, sudden, unmistakable, yanking her half a step toward the mountain like a fish on a line.

The young rider saw it. Of course he saw it; there was nothing subtle about it. Her collar dragged, her chin dipped, and for one naked moment she stood on the road visibly tethered to something invisible, straining north.

His eyes went from her throat to her face, and everything in them changed.

"You're a witch," he said.

"I'm a charm-seller," Ivo said. "Knots and knots and nothing else. Half superstition, half string."

"Knots don't pull." He dismounted in one motion, landing light despite his exhaustion, and crossed the distance between them before the crossbowman could say anything. Up close she could see how young he really was — nineteen, twenty at most — and how badly frightened, fear worn so long it had become a kind of armor. "Knots don't glow. Knots don't point. That's working craft, working craft is banned, and banned craft is exactly what I—" He caught himself again, jaw tightening. "What the law says I should arrest you for."

"But?" Ivo said.

He hesitated. And in the hesitation she read him, quickly and completely, the way she read everyone whose help she might someday need: he was desperate. Desperate enough to have ridden out with two hired swords and a packhorse into a country gone dark, looking for something, and the something was standing right in front of him wearing a hood.

"You're looking for a witch," she said quietly. "Aren't you."

* * *

They made camp an hour later in a hollow below the road, out of the wind, and the young man — Tam Vey, he finally admitted, saying the name like it cost him — laid out the shape of it over a fire built small and smokeless, the way soldiers build fires.

His father was Aldous Vey. Chief Ward-Keeper of the Northern Reach. The man who had designed and raised the border wards that had stood for sixty years, the great lattice of stone and sigil-work that kept the Hollow Court asleep beneath Mount Serle and kept the old hungers penned behind the world's skin. Every child in Greyharrow learned his name alongside their letters.

Four nights ago, the wards had failed. Not broken — failed, all of them, in the same hour, from the sea-cliffs to the high passes, like a hundred lamps guttering on the same draft. And the same night, Aldous Vey had vanished from his tower above the pass, leaving behind an unlocked door, a cold supper, and a ledger-room with one drawer forced open.

"They're saying he sold them," Tam said. He stared into the fire. His two hired men had withdrawn to the edge of the light, pretending not to listen, listening anyway. "The wards. That he took Court silver to open the way. There are already songs about it, filthy little street songs. They hanged a man in Greyharrow two days ago for singing one."

"Do you believe it?"

Tam's hands curled into fists on his knees. "My father built those wards when he was younger than me. He spent thirty years maintaining them. He missed my mother's funeral vigil because a ward-stone on the coast was singing wrong and he wouldn't leave it until it was quiet." His voice cracked and he mastered it, brutally, the way young men master such things. "So no. I don't believe it. But I also don't believe a man can vanish through a locked door, and I don't believe the stars can be eaten, and both of those happened. So I've stopped believing things and started looking instead."

"For what?"

"For whoever can tell me what actually happened that night." He looked up at her, and the firelight showed her the truth underneath all his armor: he was not looking for his father. He was too clear-eyed for that. He was looking for the reason. "You felt it, didn't you. When the wards fell. Everyone with a drop of craft felt it — the city's full of stories. Charms dying all at once, scrying bowls cracking, hedge-witches screaming in their sleep. And yours didn't die." His gaze dropped to her throat, where the luck-knot lay glowing faintly under her collar like a coal banked in cloth. "Yours woke up. And it's pointing at my father's mountain."

Ivo said nothing. The knot pulsed gently against her skin, patient, certain, and she hated it a little.

"Where did you get it?" Tam asked. "That charm."

For a long moment she considered lying. She was very good at lying; it was one of the few crafts she'd ever fully mastered. But lies were debts too, and she had spent twelve years paying interest on one already, and something about this boy's raw, unsentimental grief made the old lie feel heavier than usual on her tongue.

"A woman named Juniper made it," she said. "Twenty years ago. She was — my teacher. My master, in the hedge-craft. Before I left."

"Left?"

"Ran." The word came out before she could dress it up. "I ran away. Twelve years ago. I never went back." She turned the cord over in her fingers. "This was hers. One of the last things she gave me. It died with all the others the night the stars went out — every charm I own went dark at once, everything I've made since, everything I bought. Except this one. This one started pulling me north."

Tam had gone very still. "Toward Serle."

"Toward Serle."

"And you followed it." He wasn't accusing her. He sounded almost awed, and that was worse. "Alone. On foot. Because a dead woman's charm told you to."

"She's not dead," Ivo said, too fast, and then, because the night was dark and the boy was grieving and some rotten part of her wanted company in the pit: "Or she wasn't, twelve years ago. The journal I found in her cottage — what was left of it — said she owed a debt. Sold to the Hollow Court. I think..." She stopped. Started again. "I think whatever took the stars took her too. Or she's down there. Or part of her is. I don't know. I know the charm wants me to go, and I know Juniper never made a charm in her life that didn't mean exactly what it said."

The fire popped. Somewhere upslope, a nightbird cried once and thought better of it.

"My father's ledgers are in that mountain too," Tam said at last. "If they're anywhere. The real ones — the working records, the ward-maintenance logs, thirty years of his handwriting. If he sold the wards, it'll be in the ledgers. If he was made to sell them, or tricked, or if someone else used his credentials — it'll be there. Everything's in the ledgers. He used to say a ward without records is just a wall."

"So we want the same hole in the ground," Ivo said.

Something that was almost a laugh escaped him, brief and startled. "That's the most honest recruitment speech I've ever given."

"I haven't agreed to anything."

"You will." He said it without arrogance, which somehow made it more convincing. "Because you need what I have. You think you're going to walk up to Mount Serle and knock? The Underway has nine entrances and six of them are death and two of them are watched, and the last one — the miners' stair — hasn't opened since my grandfather's day, and the mechanism that opens it is keyed to ward-keeper's credentials. To *my family's* credentials." He tapped his own chest. "Blood-keyed. My blood opens doors in that mountain that no amount of hedge-craft will. And I know the tunnels. I grew up in them. My father used to take me down on inspection rounds — I could draw you the first three galleries blindfolded."

"And in exchange you want—"

"The truth. Whatever it is." His voice dropped. "Even if it's the song. Even if he sold them. I'd rather bury a traitor than worship a ghost."

Ivo looked at him across the small fire — this furious, exhausted boy with his dead-or-damned father and his blood-keyed veins — and felt the old machinery of self-preservation turn over inside her, cataloguing exits, weighing costs. He was a liability. He was grief walking upright, and grief made people reckless, and reckless people got witches hanged. Every instinct she had honed in twelve years of hiding said: walk away, climb alone, trust the knot and your own two feet.

But the knot pulled north, and the tunnels were real, and she was forty-one years old and tired of running from a mountain.

"One condition," she said.

Tam's chin came up, wary. "Name it."

"When this is done — whatever we find, whichever way it breaks — you forget my face. No ledgers, no testimony, no 'there was a witch with us.' As far as the Council and the wardens ever learn, you climbed alone."

"That's it? Not gold? Not a share of—"

"There's no share of anything down there but trouble." She fed a twig to the fire and watched it catch. "I've spent twelve years making sure no one remembers my name. I'd like to keep it that way."

He studied her for a long moment, and she watched him decide — watched suspicion and desperation wrestle openly across his young face until desperation won, as it always does, as it had won for her, once, in a cottage that smelled of juniper berries and woodsmoke.

"All right," he said. "Forget your face. Though I'll warn you — I'm bad at forgetting. My father says—"

He stopped. The sentence died in his mouth, and Ivo understood suddenly how many times a day that must happen to him now, how many small graves of unfinished sentences he walked past.

"Rest," she said, not unkindly. "We climb at first light."

* * *

She did not sleep. She sat at the hollow's edge with her back against a pine and the luck-knot warm in her palm, watching the black scar where the stars should have been, and turned the boy's words over like stones.

*A ward without records is just a wall.* What did that make a soul without its body? A door without its key?

The camp settled around her — the hired men snoring in shifts, Tam breathing at last in the deep rhythm of true exhaustion, the packhorse stamping and sighing. The mountain loomed against the starless sky, a darker darkness, and the knot in her hand pulsed its slow patient pulse, and Ivo Thackeray, who had spent twelve years perfecting the art of being no one, sat beneath it and let herself, for one unguarded minute, be someone's apprentice again.

Then the cold came.

It came up out of the ground, not the air — a specific cold, localized, deliberate, pooling around her boots like water finding its level. The fire did not gutter. The horses did not stir. Only Ivo felt it, and only Ivo heard the voice that came with it, thin as breath on a window:

*Ivo.*

Not Juniper's voice. Not quite. But shaped like Juniper's voice the way a footprint is shaped like a foot — the same soft vowels, the same upward lilt at the end of a name, as if the speaker found the name itself faintly amusing, fondly familiar.

*Ivo Thackeray. You brought the knot. Good girl.*

She was on her feet with a warding sign half-traced before she knew she'd moved, her heart slamming, her breath smoking in air that had no business being cold. Around the dead campfire, nothing moved. The hired men slept on. Tam slept on.

Only the darkness at the treeline was not entirely dark. There was a paleness there, at the edge of seeing, the suggestion of a figure — small, slight, bird-boned — that resolved when she looked directly at it and dissolved when she looked away, like a face in smoke.

"You know my name," Ivo whispered.

The paleness rippled. It might have been laughter.

*Everyone down here knows your name,* said the ghost. *You'd be surprised how often it comes up.*

The luck-knot burned hot against her palm, and far above, on the peak of Mount Serle, something in the starless dark turned over — slowly, like a sleeper remembering an appointment — and began, very quietly, to pay attention.
