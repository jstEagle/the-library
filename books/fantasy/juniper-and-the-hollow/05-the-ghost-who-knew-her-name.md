## 5. The Ghost Who Knew Her Name

The fire Tam built was too neat. That was the first thing Ivo noticed about him — he laid his kindling like a man setting a table for company, each stick crossed and considered, as if the mountain might inspect his work. She had said nothing about it. She had said very little at all since they'd struck their bargain on the switchback above Greyharrow: her charms against his road-knowledge, his father's name held between them like a knife neither wanted to pick up.

They were camped in the lee of a fallen fir, halfway up Serle's shoulder, where the pines grew crooked with wind and the air tasted of iron and old snow. The wards had been down four days now. Four days of a sky like a closed lid. Ivo kept catching herself looking up, expecting stars the way you expect your own reflection in a window, and finding nothing — not cloud, not haze, but an absence so complete it felt less like night than like being inside something's mouth.

She was mending the strap of her charm-bag by firelight when she felt it.

Not heard. Not seen. *Felt* — a coldness at the base of her skull, precise as a needle, the way frost finds the gap under a door. Her hands went still. The thread slipped from her fingers.

"Tam," she said quietly.

He looked up from the pot he was nursing over the flames. "What?"

"Don't move."

The coldness moved instead. It came around the edge of the fallen fir the way smoke comes around a door — without feet, without weight, without any of the courtesies a body owes a body — and it resolved, slowly, into a shape. A girl. Or the memory of one. She stood just past the firelight's reach, where the warmth gave up and the dark began, and she was made entirely of that absence: no starlight to catch in her hair because there was no starlight anywhere; no shadow to anchor her because shadows need a light to be cast from. Where her eyes should have held some glimmer, there were only two small holes in the world.

Ivo had seen ghosts before. Hedge-craft taught you that much — that the dead lingered like stains, like damp, like the smell of smoke in cloth, and that most of them wanted nothing but to be acknowledged and released. This was not that. This ghost did not drift or moan or repeat herself. She *stood*. She watched. And when she spoke, her voice was clear and dry and unhurried, like someone reading aloud from a book she knew by heart.

"You still bite your thread," the ghost said. "Left-handed. You never learned to do it right."

Ivo's blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the dead.

Because that was not a stranger's observation. That was not even a witch's observation. That was the particular, useless, intimate knowledge of someone who had sat across a worktable from her for three years — who had told her, again and again, *left hand steady, Ivo, the knot knows a trembling hand* — in a cottage below this very mountain, in a life Ivo had buried so deep she sometimes convinced herself she'd imagined it.

"Who are you?" she said. Her voice came out flat. She was proud of that, later. At the time it cost her everything she had.

The ghost tilted her head, considering, and there was something almost amused in the tilt. "Wren," she said. "You may call me Wren."

"May I." Ivo set down the strap. Slowly. The way you set things down when you're deciding whether to reach for a knife. "And what are you doing on my mountain, Wren?"

"Your mountain." The ghost smiled, and the smile did not move the rest of her face at all. "That's new. Twelve years away and it's *your* mountain."

Beside the fire, Tam had gone rigid. He was young — younger than Ivo, barely twenty, with his father's broad jaw and none of his father's steadiness — and he stared at the ghost with the naked wonder of someone seeing his first dead thing. "Is she... is she one of the taken? From the wards?" His voice cracked on *wards*, and Ivo saw him flinch at himself. "Did the Court send her?"

"I'm not sent," Wren said. "I'm *attached*." She pronounced the word carefully, tasting it. "There's a difference. Sent things can be called back. Attached things have found where they belong."

"I don't belong to you."

"No," Wren agreed, pleasantly. "But something of yours belongs to me. Or perhaps I belong to it. We're still negotiating."

Ivo's hands had begun, without her permission, to do what her hands always did under threat: to work. A thumb worrying the scarred callus at the base of her finger, tracing the small hidden knots she wore braided into her own wrist-band — the witch's rosary, the ones she'd sworn off making for anyone but herself. Protection. Warding. *Go-away.* She could feel the charms' shapes in her mind, could feel how few of them would mean anything to a thing that had no body to repel and no mind to cloud.

"What are you?" she asked again, and hated how much the question cost her, how many times she'd already asked it.

"I told you. Wren." The ghost drifted a half-step closer to the fire — not into the light, but to the exact border of it, so that her edges smoked faintly where warmth met absence. "A wren is a small bird. Unremarkable. Loud for its size. It builds in walls and hedges and forgotten places, and it sings the same few notes its whole life, and if you grew up hearing those notes you will know them anywhere, won't you, Ivo? Even twelve years later. Even in the dark. Even out of another bird's throat."

The fire popped. Somewhere up-slope, ice cracked in a way that sounded almost like speech.

"That's enough," Ivo said.

"It isn't," Wren said, "but go on."

Tam looked between them, lost. "Do you know her?"

"No," Ivo said.

"Yes," Wren said, at exactly the same moment, and the two words lay together in the cold air like a coin showing both faces at once.

* * *

She did not leave. That was the worst of it. Ghosts in the stories departed at dawn, or dissolved when named, or faded once their business was spoken. Wren settled against the darkness beyond the fire like a cat claiming a windowsill, and when they broke camp in the grey morning — a morning the same color as every hour now, since the sky had stopped distinguishing — she came with them, drifting at Ivo's left shoulder, precisely where a companion would walk, precisely where no footfall sounded.

Tam kept glancing at the empty air beside Ivo and then quickly away, the way men look at a wound.

"She's following us," he murmured, an hour up the trail.

"She's following *me*," Ivo corrected. "You're incidental."

"Why you, though?" He ducked under a low branch, breath steaming. "If the Court sent her — if she's one of the soul-taken — why attach to a hedge-charmer from Greyharrow? No offense."

"None taken," Ivo said, meaning all of it.

"Because she asked so nicely," Wren said.

Ivo stopped walking. The trail here was a shelf of rock above a ravine filled with fog, and her boots skidded slightly on rime, and she used the moment — the small stumble, the arm thrown out for balance — to cover the fact that her heart had lurched. Because Wren hadn't answered Tam. Wren had answered *her*, in a tone Ivo recognized, a tone of patient correction, of a teacher drawing a slow student back to the lesson:

*Because she asked so nicely.*

Three years. Three years of asking. Of standing in Juniper's kitchen with her hat in her hands, asking to be taught, asking to be trusted, asking — in the end, in the last week, in the way that mattered more than all the others combined — asking to be forgiven. Asking so nicely. And being refused, in the end, in the way that mattered most.

"You knew her," Ivo said. Not a question. She made it not a question because questions could be denied; statements had to be argued with, and she wanted to watch Wren argue.

"I know *you*," Wren said.

"You speak like her."

"Do I?" The ghost drifted ahead along the shelf, through the fog, and for a moment her outline thinned until Ivo could see the rock through her chest, and then thickened again on the far side, whole. "Voices are cheap. Anyone can wear a voice. Ask me something a stranger couldn't know. Go on. Test me. You're dying to."

Ivo should not have. Every craft-lesson she'd ever learned said don't engage the uncanny thing, don't feed it attention, attention is food. But there was a hunger in her too, twelve years old and never fed, and it leaned toward this ghost the way a cold hand leans toward a stove.

"The kitchen floor," Ivo said. "In the cottage. What was wrong with it?"

Wren's smile returned — that unmoving smile, the smile of a painted saint. "Loose stone by the hearth. Third from the corner. Juniper kept her true ledger under it, wrapped in oilcloth, because she didn't trust banks or magistrates or apprentices who asked too many questions about the wrong shelves." A pause, delicate as a held breath. "You found it anyway. Second winter. You read the whole thing while she was at market, and you put it back exactly right, and you never told her, and you have wondered ever since whether she knew."

The ravine fog seemed to press closer. Tam had gone pale. "Ivo," he said carefully, "who's Juniper?"

"My former master," Ivo said, and her voice was steady, and somewhere beneath the steadiness a door she had nailed shut twelve years ago rattled on its hinges.

"And where is Juniper now?" Wren asked, softly, and the softness was the cruelest part, because it was *kind*, and kindness from this creature was like warmth from a corpse — evidence of something that used to be alive.

"Gone," Ivo said.

"Mm," said Wren. "Gone. Sold. Taken. There are so many words for the same hole, aren't there? Which word do you use? The one that puts the blame farthest from yourself, I'd guess. You were always good at that. Better than good at the craft, worse than honest with yourself. *The knot knows a trembling hand,*" the ghost added, almost gently, "and yours has trembled for twelve years."

"Stop." It came out louder than Ivo intended. A raven went up off the shelf-edge, screaming into the soundless sky.

"I'm not the one who started," Wren said. "I'm only the one who stayed."

* * *

They walked the rest of the day, and Wren talked, and that was its own kind of siege.

She did not menace. She did not wail. She simply *knew*, and she dispensed her knowing in small unhurried doses, timed like a cook salting a broth: the name of Ivo's first failed charm (a fever-knot, botched, given free to a ferryman's daughter who died anyway); the color of the ribbon Ivo had worn at her binding-day ceremony (blue, though blue flattered her ill, because Juniper had chosen it and Ivo had lacked the spine to say so); the exact wording of the last thing Juniper ever said to her — *"You take, Ivo. You've always taken. One day you'll meet the thing that takes back."*

Ivo did not remember telling anyone that. She did not remember telling *anyone* that. She had carried it alone the way you carry a swallowed stone, and here it was, recited back to her in Juniper's cadence, in Juniper's dry unhurried music, by a dead girl with holes for eyes who claimed to be nobody.

At the noon halt, while Tam dozed against his pack, Ivo finally turned on her.

"You deny being her."

"I do."

"But you won't say what you are."

"I've said. Wren."

"A name isn't an answer."

"No," Wren agreed. "It's a *handle*. Answers are heavier. You lift them when you're ready." The ghost settled onto a boulder that could not possibly have borne weight, and yet seemed to sit, and the sitting was somehow worse than the floating. "Ask me the real question, Ivo. The one under the others. You've been circling it since the fire last night."

Ivo's jaw tightened. Below them, the fog in the ravine had begun to move uphill — wrong, all of it wrong, the weather itself seeming to lean toward the mountain's crown as if summoned. "How do you know these things?"

"There it is." Wren folded her insubstantial hands. "Two answers. Choose whichever lets you sleep. First: I am a ghost of this mountain, and the mountain remembers everything that happens on it, and I have been reading you since the moment you set your boot on Serle's stone."

"And second?"

The ghost was quiet a long moment. When she spoke again, the dry music had gone out of her voice, and what remained was simpler and infinitely more frightening.

"Second: some pieces of a person stay behind when the rest is taken. They stay where they were happiest. Or where they were left." Those eyeless hollows fixed on Ivo with terrible patience. "Think hard, witch. Think about the night you ran. Think about what you took with you that wasn't yours. Think about what she pressed into your hand at the door, and what you told yourself it was."

Ivo's hand rose, unbidden, to the charm-bag at her hip — to the oldest charm in it, the one she never sold, never showed, the small knotted thing she'd carried twelve years without once examining why. Juniper's knot. Given at the door, the last night. A protection, she'd always told herself. A master's farewell blessing.

Her fingers found it through the leather, and for the first time in twelve years, it hummed against her skin — faintly, wrongly, in time with something far beneath the mountain, the way a tuning fork answers a note struck in another room.

Wren watched her touch it, and something moved across the ghost's absent face that was almost grief.

"Not yet," Wren said quietly — to Ivo, or to the bag, or to whatever listened through both. "But soon. Keep walking, Ivo. The market opens at dusk, and souls are traded at dusk, and yours has been on somebody's list for a very long time."

Behind them, far below, the road they had climbed went dark — not shadowed, *dark*, a seam of absolute black unspooling up the mountainside like ink poured along a thread, and from within it came a sound like a great many voices all inhaling at once.

"They know you're coming," Wren said, rising from her impossible seat. "Walk faster."
