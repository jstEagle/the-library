## 6. Serle's Twilight Market

The market announced itself before they saw it — by smell first, then by sound.

The smell was the strangest part. Ivo had expected rot, or ash, or the cold mineral breath of deep places. Instead the air above the last switchback of the road smelled like a fair: hot sugar and woodsmoke and cut apples, underlaid with something sweeter still, something that made her mouth water and her hindbrain recoil all at once. It took her a hundred paces to place it. It was the smell of a candle just snuffed — of something bright, recently alive, put out.

"That's what souls smell like," Wren said, walking beside her without disturbing so much as a pebble. "When they're fresh off a person. The brokers burn tallow scented with it. Old trick. Makes people hungry without knowing why."

"And you know this how?"

"I know a great many things." Wren's voice was Juniper's voice; Ivo had stopped flinching at it somewhere on the third day and hated herself for that too. "None of them help."

The sound came next: not music exactly, but the memory of music, played slightly out of true — fiddles and hand-drums and a bell that rang in a key no bell should ring in, all of it arriving at Ivo's ears a half-beat after she felt it in her teeth. Tam heard nothing at all, he said. He walked with his father's ward-ledger strapped under his coat and his hand resting on it the way other men rest a hand on a knife.

Then the road crested a shoulder of black stone, and there it was.

Serle's Twilight Market filled a bowl-shaped hollow on the mountain's flank like water filling a cup. Tents of grey silk. Lanterns that burned with light the color of dusk held forever at its final minute. A thousand figures moving through the lanes — some merely strange, robed and masked and bargaining loudly, and some wrong in ways the eye refused to hold: too tall, too jointed, faces like unfinished sketches. And everywhere, threaded through everything, the trade. Ivo saw a woman pay for a bolt of starless silk with a small glass vial that glowed faintly gold from within, and saw the silk-merchant hold it up to his lantern and squint into it like a jeweler judging a stone.

"Souls are coin here," Tam said quietly. "Whole ones, splinters, shavings. My father's ledgers list exchange rates." He said *my father* the way a man says a word that has turned to glass in his mouth.

Ivo adjusted the strap of her charm-bag and did not look at the vials. She looked at the exits instead. There were three, and two of them were guarded by things wearing the shapes of men.

"Remember," she said. "We're traders out of Greyharrow. I sell charms. You're my porter. Wren—" She stopped. Wren was already fading, going thin as smoke against the darkening sky, and even thinned she drew stares from the lanterns themselves, which bent their light toward her like flowers toward sun.

"Wren is nobody's problem," Wren said. "Wren is a rumor. Try not to sell me."

They went down into the market.

* * *

The lanes swallowed them. That was the only honest word for it. One moment Ivo was walking between tents; the next the market had arranged itself around her, lanes curving where lanes should have run straight, stalls appearing ahead that she was almost certain she'd already passed behind. The dusk-light never changed. Somewhere above, real night must have been falling on the world, but down here the hour stood still, held open like a door propped with a chair.

She set up her pitch in a lane of charm-sellers — because of course the Court had a lane of charm-sellers, hedge-craft gathered like moths, and every one of them selling variations on the knots and bindings Ivo had spent twelve years pretending were beneath her. A woman with silver rings on every finger sold luck-knots braided from hair. A man whose eyes were the exact gray of old ice sold bottled courage. At the end of the row, a stall hung with hundreds of paper birds sold forgetfulness, one folded wing at a time.

Ivo laid out her wares on a cloth of undyed wool: knotted cords, salt-charms, a fever-bag, three luck-knots made in Greyharrow from honest hemp. Humble craft. Knots and patience. Her hands knew the work so well they ached with it.

Within a quarter hour, she had customers.

That was the bait working, or the hunger working, or both. Word moved through the market the way blood moves through a body, and by the time the false dusk had aged not at all, a small crowd stood at her cloth, and coins were changing hands — real coins, mostly, though one man paid with a tooth and another with a lock of hair wrapped around a splinter of bone, and Ivo took both because refusing payment in the Twilight Market was, according to Tam's urgent whisper, a declaration of war.

A child bought a knot against nightmares. Ivo tied it herself while the crowd watched, her fingers moving through the old patterns — over, under, name the thread, breathe on the crossing — and felt the small warm click of a charm taking hold, the only magic left in the world that had ever felt like anything other than theft.

"You're good," said a voice at her shoulder. "Better than this row deserves."

She knew before she turned. The whole market had known since she'd set foot in it; she could feel it now, the attention, the slow converging interest, like fish gathering beneath a boat. But she turned anyway, because running was not among her options anymore.

Corvane Ash had not aged a day.

Twelve years, and the magistrate who had taught her everything worth regretting stood at her stall in robes the color of a starless sky, his gray beard trimmed neat, his hands clasped behind his back like a schoolmaster strolling between desks. His eyes were the same as she remembered: patient, amused, and utterly without floor. Looking into them was like looking down a well and hearing your own pebble never land.

"Ash," she said.

"Ivo Thackeray." He smiled, and the market seemed to lean in. "Charm-seller. Of all the trades in all the world. Do you know, when the ledgers flagged your name at the mountain's edge, I told them it must be an error? *That girl would rather die than come back.* And yet." He gestured at her cloth, her knots, her honest hemp. "Here you are. Selling charms. In my market."

"It's not your market."

"Everything under Serle is mine to broker, child. That is what a magistrate *is*." He picked up one of her luck-knots, turned it over with genuine, infuriating appreciation. "Juniper's patterns. I'd know the tension of these knots anywhere. She had the finest hands I ever trained. Second-finest." His gaze came up and rested on Ivo's fingers. "You've been hiding this craft for twelve years. What a waste. What a *waste*. All that talent, buried in dockside trinkets, while—"

"While you broker souls by the vial."

"While I keep the accounts balanced," Ash said mildly. "Someone must. Souls do not stop existing because the stars went out, Ivo. They pool. They circulate. Without a broker, the strong simply eat the weak, and there is no ledger, no price, no recourse. I am the *recourse*." He set the luck-knot down and straightened it, precise as a surgeon. "You always did mistake me for the villain of your story. I was your best teacher."

"You taught me that power is taken."

"And look how well you learned. You took yourself away. Twelve years of running — and do you know what running is, Ivo? It is power taken *from yourself*." He leaned close, and his voice dropped to the register he'd used across a dozen lesson-desks, the one that made knowledge feel like a secret being granted. "You want something here. Everyone who comes down the mountain wants something. And everything in this market can be bought. Not stolen. Not fought for. *Bought.* There is a price for whatever you came for, and I promise you, child, you can afford it. People always can. That's the tragedy and the mercy of it."

Behind Ash, at the edge of the lane, Ivo saw Tam frozen mid-step, staring at the magistrate with an expression she couldn't read — recognition, yes, but something older underneath it, something that looked like a boy hearing a story he'd been told all his life turn out to be about himself.

"I'm just selling charms," Ivo said.

"Of course you are." Ash set a coin on her cloth — heavy gold, stamped with a crown rendered in negative space, a crown made of absence — and stepped back. "For the nightmare-knot. For the child. Keep the change; buy yourself something pretty." He began to move away through the crowd, which parted for him like water, and then paused, and spoke without turning. "The vault opens at full dark, Ivo. Whatever you're hunting, it will be easier with a friend inside. Ask any lamp in the market where to find me. They'll tell you. Everything here tells me everything."

Then he was gone, and the crowd closed over the space where he'd been, and the false dusk went on holding its breath.

* * *

Tam found her three lanes away, in the shadow of a tent that sold silence by the pound.

"He knows your name."

"He's known my name for twelve years. He wrote it in his ledger the day I ran." Ivo kept walking. Her heart was doing something complicated and shameful — hammering, but not only with fear. Some traitorous chamber of it was still sitting in that classroom twenty years ago, drinking in Ash's lessons, feeling the world's machinery lay itself bare and thinking: *yes. This is how it works. This is how it has always worked.*

"The coin," Tam said. "Look at the coin."

She looked. Crown of absence. Court mint. And along the rim, in letters so fine they were nearly a texture: *REDEEMABLE AT THE BROKER'S DISCRETION.*

"He's inviting you," Tam said. "He's not threatening. He's *inviting*."

"I know."

"Do you?" Tam caught her arm — the ward-keeper's son, the boy who'd ambushed her on the mountain road with a crossbow and a grudge, gripping her sleeve like she might blow away. "Because my father used to say the Court doesn't take people, Ivo. It *hires* them. Every soul in those vials signed something. Every single one thought they were making a bargain." His voice cracked, and he hated it, and pushed on. "You looked at him like — like he was offering you a seat at a table you'd been hungry for your whole life."

Ivo stopped walking.

The market flowed around them. Somewhere near, a broker weighed a soul on brass scales, calling out the weight in a language Ivo didn't know and understood perfectly: *light, light, this one is very light, worn thin by wanting.* The dusk-lanterns burned their held-breath color. And under the sugar-smell and the snuffed-candle smell, Ivo could smell the thing Ash had named without naming: the comfortable cage, the warm ledger-line, the place where a clever girl's debts could all be paid with one signature.

"I was sixteen," she said finally, "when he told me power is taken. And I believed him, and I took, and the taking cost—" Her throat closed on Juniper's name. It had been closing on it for twelve years. "It cost more than I had. So don't ask me what I looked like. Ask me what I *want*."

"What do you want?"

"To walk out of this market with what I came for," Ivo said, "and owe this place nothing. Not a coin. Not a favor. Not a signature." She closed her hand around Ash's gold until the stamped crown bit her palm. "He thinks everyone has a price because *he* was cheap. Let him wait at his vault until full dark. We won't be coming through the front."

Tam studied her for a long moment. Then, slowly, the hard line of his shoulders eased half an inch — not trust, not yet, but the shape trust leaves in a person before it arrives.

"The vault's under the market," he said. "My father's ledgers map the old service ways. Ward-keeper's son, remember. If anyone knows how to get beneath this place without asking a lamp, it's me."

* * *

They found Wren at the market's edge, standing where the grey silk tents gave way to bare black stone, looking up at the sky that wasn't there.

Up here, past the lantern-light, the heavens over Mount Serle were a smooth and absolute dark — no stars, no moon, no clouds, just a ceiling of nothing, and Ivo felt again the vertigo of it, the animal conviction that the world had been beheaded.

"You met him," Wren said, without turning. Her voice had gone strange — thinner, and yet somehow closer, as if the market's attention had pressed her more solid rather than less. "Ash."

"You *knew* he'd find us."

"I knew the market would hand you to him. It hands everyone to him eventually. That's what markets are for." Now Wren turned, and her absent face was angled toward Ivo with an intensity that made the hair rise on her arms. "Listen to me, and listen carefully, because the next hours will try to make you stupid. Ash will offer you a bargain. He will make it sound like mercy. He will make it sound like arithmetic — a simple trade, a fair price, everyone signing, everyone consenting. And the worst thing, Ivo, the most dangerous thing, is that it *will* be fair. By his lights it will be scrupulously fair. That is how the Court eats. It does not steal. It lets you *sell*."

Ivo's hand went, without her deciding it, to the inner pocket of her coat — to the one charm that had survived the night the stars went out, the one that glowed and pulled north, the one that had led her home to a burned journal and a debt sold to the Hollow Court. It was warm against her ribs. Warmer than it had been an hour ago. Warmer, she realized with a cold sliding feeling, than it had been since the night it woke.

"Wren," she said. "Why does my charm glow brighter the deeper we go?"

The ghost was quiet for a moment. Around them the market murmured and traded and ate, and overhead the starless ceiling pressed down like a lid on a pot.

"Because it isn't pulling you north anymore," Wren said at last. "It's pulling you *down*. And it isn't leading you to Juniper, Ivo. It's leading you to the piece of Juniper that's been riding in your pocket for twelve years — and the crown knows its own, and it's *reaching for her*." Wren lifted one translucent hand, and Ivo saw that her fingers had gone from smoke to something almost like skin, almost like warmth, and that she was looking at them with an expression Ivo couldn't bear to name.

"Full dark is coming," Wren said. "And something down there is counting the minutes until it gets to hold me again."
