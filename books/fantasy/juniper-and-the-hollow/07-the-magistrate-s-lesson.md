## 7. The Magistrate's Lesson

The twilight market did not keep hours. It kept moods.

Ivo learned this on her third day behind the stall she'd rented with the last of her honest coin — a crooked table in the shadow of a buttress that wept pale lichen, beneath a lantern that burned without flame and gave light the color of drowned things. When the Court was hungry, the crowd came thick and quick, spending souls like copper. When it was sated, the lanes emptied as if the mountain had swallowed them whole, and the vendors sat among their wares listening to the stone breathe.

She was learning to read its breathing. That frightened her more than anything else so far.

"Charm-seller," said a woman with riverweed braided into her hair, stopping at the stall. "What have you got?"

"Luck-knots." Ivo held one up, red thread worked through grey, tied in the pattern Juniper had taught her when she was nine years old and all her fingers were thumbs. "Honest work. Thread and patience."

The woman laughed — a bright, wrong sound, like glass remembering being sand. "Thread and patience. Child, this is Serle's market. Nobody here buys patience."

She bought three luck-knots anyway, paying with a soul-chit that hummed against Ivo's palm like a trapped moth. The chits were how the Court paid for everything: little slivers of pressed soul-light, each one stamped with a sigil that shifted when you weren't looking directly at it. Ivo had spent two days teaching herself not to flinch when they touched her skin. She was still teaching herself.

The woman moved on. Tam appeared at the stall's edge a moment later, wearing the flat cap of a market porter and the expression of a boy who hated every second of the disguise.

"Nothing," he said quietly, arranging a tray of knot-charms that were, mostly, just knots. "Third circuit of the lantern-row. No sign of Ash. No sign of anyone who looks like they run this place. It's all middlemen and haggling. Where does it *go*, Ivo? All these souls?"

"Down," Wren said.

Tam jumped. He still hadn't gotten used to the ghost, who could go unnoticed by everyone but Ivo until she chose otherwise. She stood now at the corner of the stall, translucent as breath on cold glass, watching the crowd with an expression Ivo couldn't read — or couldn't bear to read, because every day it looked a little more like someone she remembered.

"Everything goes down," Wren continued. "The market is the mouth. The Court is the stomach. Your father built the wards to keep the stomach closed, and someone opened them, and now everything flows downhill." Her eyes found Ivo's. "Including us."

"I know why we're here," Tam muttered. "I'm asking what we're actually *doing*. We've been selling knots for three days. If Ash is the broker, shouldn't he have — "

"He'll come," Ivo said.

"How do you know?"

Because power always comes to see what's new, she thought. Because Corvane Ash never let a strange face pass through his jurisdiction unexamined, not in twelve years of magistracy, not when examining it might yield something useful. Because she had been his student for four years and she knew the shape of his attention the way she knew the shape of her own hands.

"Because I'm bait," she said instead, "and bait doesn't ask questions."

Tam's jaw tightened, but he took the tray and went back to his circuits, shoulders stiff under the porter's coat. Wren lingered.

"You're afraid of him," the ghost said. Not a question either.

"I'm afraid of what I was, when he was done with me."

"And what was that?"

Ivo tied a knot. Red thread over grey, over red. "Efficient."

* * *

He came at the change of the mood, when the market thinned and the lantern-light deepened from drowned-green to something nearer violet, and the vendors began folding their awnings with the slow, dreamlike movements of people half-asleep. Ivo was counting her chits — forty-one, enough to buy a rumor, nowhere near enough to buy a soul — when the temperature of the lane changed.

Not colder. *Ordered.* As though the air itself had been straightened, squared, made to stand at attention. She knew that feeling. She had felt it in a magistrate's study in Greyharrow, twelve years ago, sitting across a desk of black oak while a man with kind eyes and a voice like a closing ledger explained to her exactly how much her life was worth and to whom.

"Thackeray."

Her hands did not shake. She had trained them out of it long ago, and besides — she had rehearsed this. Every version of it. She turned, and arranged her face into the wary blankness of a hedge-witch out of her depth, and looked up at Corvane Ash.

He had aged well, which was somehow worse. Silver at the temples now, threaded through dark hair; the same lean frame wrapped in robes that made no concession to the market around them — charcoal-grey, severe, fastened with a clasp shaped like a set of scales. His eyes were the same. That was the worst part. Warm brown, patient, attentive. Eyes that had once looked at a frightened nineteen-year-old girl and seen a tool worth sharpening.

"You know me," he said, pleased.

"Everyone knows the magistrate of Greyharrow." Keep it flat. Keep it small. A charm-seller who fled south when the wards fell, hiding her craft, nothing more. "Or — knew. Before."

"Before." He smiled and gestured at the dying market, the violet light, the mountain looming above them with its crown of no-stars. "Yes. Before. May I?" He didn't wait for permission; he never had. He picked up one of her luck-knots and turned it over in long, careful fingers. "Red thread. Juniper's pattern, isn't it? The crossing at the fourth turn. I remember her work. I confiscated enough of it."

The name landed like a hook behind her sternum. Ivo let her breath out slowly. "You knew Juniper?"

"I appraised her." He set the knot down with something almost like tenderness. "Every hedge-craft in the borderlands passed across my desk eventually, Thackeray. Yours included. Do you remember what I told you, the last time we spoke?"

*I can't protect you from this, girl. But I can teach you how the world actually works. Magic isn't given. It's taken. The only question is whether you're the one taking.*

"I remember you telling me to inform on my own master," Ivo said. "And me refusing. And you letting me walk away anyway, which I always thought was strange."

Ash's smile widened by a degree. "Did you? Strange." He turned the luck-knot over again. "Walk with me, charm-seller. Indulge an old man's nostalgia. You can afford to close early — I'll make up your losses."

It wasn't a request, and they both knew it. Ivo lifted the latch on her stall and stepped into the lane beside him. Behind her, Wren drifted after like a shadow that had opinions.

They walked the lantern-row as the market slept around them. Vendors nodded to Ash with the careful deference of men nodding to weather. He returned each nod precisely, equitably, a magistrate to his bones even here — especially here.

"Do you know what my title is now?" he asked. "In the Court?"

"They don't hand out titles at the surface market."

"No. They don't." He stopped before a fountain that had run dry a century ago, its basin filled instead with drifting motes of soul-light, rising and falling like snow that couldn't decide which way was down. "I am the Broker of Debts. Everything owed in the Hollow Court passes through my hands. Every soul pledged, pawned, sold, or seized. I keep the ledgers." He watched the motes rise and fall. "Do you know how many souls are held down there, Thackeray?"

"No."

"Neither do I. That's rather the point of a crown of night — it eats its own accounting." He said it lightly, but something moved behind the warmth of his eyes, some flicker she might once have called conscience and now suspected was only fatigue. "But I know where every *named* soul sits. And there are perhaps thirty names in all the world I could fetch for you in an hour, if you had the price."

The lane was very quiet. Somewhere above, the mountain groaned, settling its weight.

"Juniper," Ivo said.

There it was. She hadn't meant to say it — the plan had been to make him say it first, to give away nothing — but the name tore free of her like a knot cut with a knife, and she saw him register it, saw the pleasure move through him subtle as water finding a crack.

"Ah," Ash said softly. "So that's the shape of you."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Twelve years ago a girl refuses to sell her master to me. Flees the borderlands the same season Juniper Thackeray is taken by the Court — a debt called in, very properly, very legally, through channels I did not then understand. And now the wards fall, the stars go out, and a charm-seller arrives in Serle's market selling *Juniper's own knot-pattern* and asking, in her sleep, for a name she thinks no one hears her say." He turned to face her fully, and the kindness in his eyes was the kindness of a trap sprung clean. "You were never bait, Ivo. You were a letter arriving. I've simply been waiting for you to finish reading yourself aloud."

Wren had gone rigid at Ivo's shoulder. *Say nothing,* the ghost breathed, a sound like frost forming. *He's counting your pulse.*

Ivo counted her own pulse instead, and slowed it, the way Juniper had taught her — *fear is just blood, child, and blood obeys the breath* — and met her old teacher's gaze with nothing in her face at all.

"What do you want, Corvane?"

"To offer you a trade." He reached into his robe and produced a ledger — small, black-bound, its pages edged in silver that hurt to look at directly. "Your master's debt sits in my vault. Shelf nine, row three. Dim — souls fade when they're unclaimed, did you know that? Twelve years in the dark, and what's left of Juniper Thackeray is about the size of a candle-flame. But intact. I checked this morning." He smiled at whatever crossed her face. "Intact, because I wanted it intact. Sentimental of me, perhaps. Or prudent. One keeps good stock against the day a buyer comes."

"The price," Ivo said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears.

"Service." Ash closed the ledger. "The Court requires a charm-maker. A real one. The hollow things down there can copy craft but they can't originate it — that's the curse of them, they can only take, never make. And I require—" the pause was surgical "—an apprentice. Again. Someone who understands the surface, who can move through Greyharrow and the border towns without raising alarms. Someone whose loyalty I can hold in my hand like a coin." He extended the ledger toward her. "Serve for a year. One year, Ivo. At the end of it, shelf nine, row three, and you both walk out of this mountain alive. That is more than anyone else in this market will ever be offered. That is more than *deserves* to be offered."

The motes rose and fell. The mountain breathed. And Ivo stood in the violet light and felt the terrible seduction of it settle over her shoulders like a well-cut coat.

One year. One year of pretending — and she was so *good* at pretending, she had done it for twelve years, she had worn guilt like a second skin until it fit better than her own — and Juniper would be whole. Alive. In her arms. All she had to do was become, briefly and deliberately, the thing she had spent twelve years running from having become by accident.

That was the cage, and she saw its bars clearly, and the bars were gilded, and the gilding was *comfortable*, and Corvane Ash had built it to her exact measure because he had measured her himself, years ago, with those warm patient eyes.

"You taught me magic is taken," she said. "Not given."

"I did. It's the truest thing I know."

"Then take the deal myself," she said. "If it's so simple. Take the Court's power. Take the crown. Why broker for it? Why carry ledgers for something that ate the sky?"

For one heartbeat — one — something raw surfaced in Ash's face, something old and drowned, and then it was gone, smoothed away like a wrinkle pressed from silk.

"Because I lost my chance to refuse," he said quietly, "and I would spare you the same lesson, if you're wise enough to take the cheaper one." He set the black ledger on the dry fountain's rim between them. It lay there like a door lying open. "You needn't answer tonight. The offer stands for three days. After that, shelf nine, row three goes to auction — and the Hollow Court bids in ways you cannot imagine and I will not describe."

He bowed — precisely, courteously, a magistrate to his bones — and walked away down the sleeping lane, and the air behind him slowly forgot how to stand at attention.

Ivo stood alone with the ledger. Wren hovered at her elbow, and when Ivo finally looked at her, the ghost's face was unreadable — except that it wasn't, except that it was the exact face Juniper had worn the day she caught young Ivo stealing charms from the till and said nothing, only waited, only *waited*, until the confession came on its own.

"Don't touch it," Wren said.

"I'm not going to touch it."

"You want to."

"That's not the same as touching it." But her hand was already rising, traitor fingers reaching for the black binding, and she forced them back down, curled them into a fist against her thigh. "Three days. He gives me three days because he knows I'll spend them arguing with myself, and every argument ends the same way, because he built the arguments too."

Wren drifted closer to the ledger, studying it without touching. Then her translucence flickered — and for an instant, just an instant, Ivo saw her solid: real hands, real sleeves, a burn-scar on the left wrist that Ivo had kissed once, bandaging, when she was fifteen and clumsy with the iron.

"Wren—"

"Shelf nine," the ghost said, and her voice had gone strange and distant, like something heard through water. "Row three. Ivo — I know that shelf. I've *seen* that shelf. In dreams, since the night the stars went out. There's something else on it." Her eyes lifted, and they were full of a fear that had nothing to do with ghosts. "Something that's been waiting for you longer than Ash has."

Behind them, in the dry fountain, every mote of soul-light suddenly sank at once — all together, all downward, like a held breath — and somewhere beneath the market, deep in the mountain, something vast turned over in its sleep and began, softly, to count.
