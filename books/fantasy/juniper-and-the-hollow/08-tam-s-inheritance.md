## 8. Tam's Inheritance

The ward-house at the foot of Mount Serle had always smelled of chalk and cold iron, of the tallow candles his father burned down to stubs because new ones cost coin better spent on salt for the boundary stones. Tam had grown up breathing that smell the way other children grew up breathing bread or river water. He had hated it, once. He would have given anything, in those last hours before everything ended, to smell it again.

He let himself in through the kitchen door, because the front door was where grief lived and he wasn't ready for it.

Inside, nothing had changed. That was the cruelty of it. The kettle sat on its hook. His father's coat hung on its peg by the door, shoulders still holding the shape of the man who'd worn it, as though Aldous Vey had only stepped out to check the eastern stones and would come back stamping snow off his boots, complaining about the price of lamp-oil, ruffling Tam's hair with a hand rough as bark. The night the stars went out, the ward-keeper of Greyharrow had simply ceased to be — no body, no blood, no farewell. Just an empty coat and a mountain that had swallowed its keeper whole.

Tam lit the lamp with hands that wouldn't quite steady. He'd told Ivo Thackeray and her ghost that he needed a day in the city before they climbed. That much was true. What he hadn't said was why.

*Because you're hoping it's all a mistake,* he thought, setting the lamp on his father's worktable. *You're hoping you'll find the ledgers clean, the columns straight, your father's hand steady and honest from first page to last.*

He pulled out the ledger chest.

It was iron-banded oak, older than Tam, older maybe than the house, and it sat beneath the window where the light fell at midday because his father had been particular about reading conditions the way some men were particular about their boots. The key lived on a cord around Aldous Vey's neck, which meant it should have gone into the ground with him, except there had been no ground to put him in. Instead Tam had found it, three days after the sky went black, tucked inside his own pack between a spare shirt and a coil of copper wire, wrapped in a scrap of paper bearing four words in his father's blunt hand:

*Read them. All of them.*

Tam had carried the key for two days without using it. Two days of walking beside a hedge-witch who watched him like she was weighing what he'd fetch at market, and a ghost who spoke in someone else's voice, and both of them asking questions about the wards — how they'd failed, whether there'd been warning, whether Aldous Vey had sent any last report up the mountain before the end. And Tam had answered honestly, because he knew almost nothing, because his father had kept the deep workings of the craft behind a locked lid and a locked door and a lifetime of *not yet, boy, when you're older.*

*When you're older.* Well. He was older now, and the lesson had arrived anyway, wearing a crown of absence.

The lock turned with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

* * *

There were eleven ledgers.

Tam laid them out on the worktable in order, oldest to newest, and the sight of them arranged so plainly made his throat tighten. Thirty years of his father's life in ruled columns: the boundary stones and their maintenance, the salt-cycles, the re-scribing of the great ward-matrices that ringed Greyharrow and the borderlands beyond, the small repairs, the seasonal renewals, the endless patient labor of keeping one thin line of light between the living world and everything that wanted in. His father's handwriting marched across every page — cramped, precise, unlovely, honest. Tam knew that hand the way he knew his own heartbeat. He had learned his letters copying it.

He started with the oldest volume and read.

Hours went past the window. The light shifted from grey to gold to the bruised violet of a sky that still had no stars in it, and Tam read, and the world he had built his life upon came apart page by quiet page.

The early ledgers were exactly what he'd expected. Costs and counts. A stone re-set here, a sigil renewed there. Notes in the margins — *east line humming again, will check at solstice; bought too little silver, do better next year* — the marginalia of a careful man doing careful work. Tam found himself smiling at one entry, twelve years old, recording the purchase of a child's practice-kit: *T. broke the third chisel already. Charge it against his allowance. He'll thank me someday.* He did not feel like thanking anyone. He felt like a boy standing in a house where the walls had begun, very softly, to lie.

The change came in the ninth ledger.

At first it was nothing — a gap in the records, a month where the entries thinned, where the usual meticulous notations gave way to single lines: *Renewal deferred. See private account.* Tam frowned. His father kept no private accounts. His father believed, with the fervor of the converted, that a ward-keeper's books should be legible to God and magistrate alike. *Secrecy is where rot moves in,* Aldous used to say, tapping the open ledger with one blunt finger. *Show your work, Tam. Always show your work.*

But here was a gap. And then another. And then, in the tenth ledger, gaps that weren't gaps at all.

Tam turned back a page, then forward, certain he'd misread. He had not misread.

Under the heading *Border Matrix — Annual Renewal*, in his father's unmistakable hand, the entry read: *Renewal performed at reduced strength per agreement. Balance of power diverted as contracted. Payment received in full.*

*Per agreement.* *As contracted.* *Payment received.*

Tam sat very still. The lamp hissed. Somewhere outside, a dog began to howl at the starless dark and did not stop.

His father had written those words. Not forged — Tam knew forgery when he saw it, had been taught to see it, and this was no forgery. The ink aged true. The pressure of the pen was his father's pressure, heavier on the downstrokes, impatient at line's end. Aldous Vey had sat at this very table, in this very chair, and recorded in his own honest book that he had weakened the wards that protected ten thousand people, and that he had been paid for it.

Tam's first thought was that his father had been threatened. His second was worse: that he'd been bribed. His third thought arrived slowly, the way cold arrives through a floor, and it stopped his breath entirely — because in the margin beside the entry, in smaller script, almost hidden, his father had written a date and a name.

The date was twelve years past. The name was *T.*

* * *

He read the rest of the night.

The story assembled itself out of margins and deferrals and payments received in full, and it was not the story Tam had carried out of that house on the morning the sky died. It was not even close.

Twelve years ago, something had come to Greyharrow. Something from under the mountain. Tam could not learn what form it wore — his father never wrote it plainly, only circled phrases like *the broker below* and *the one who buys* — but it had made Aldous Vey an offer, and the offer had concerned Tam. There was a page near the middle of the tenth ledger where his father's composure finally cracked, where the neat columns dissolved into lines crossed out and rewritten, and at the bottom of the page, pressed hard enough to tear the paper, stood a single sentence:

*They asked for the boy. I told them the wards instead. God forgive me, I told them the wards.*

Tam read it six times. The words did not soften with repetition. They got sharper, like a blade honed on each pass.

His father had traded the border wards — thirty years of maintenance, renewal by renewal, strength siphoned away season after season, a wall dismantled brick by brick over a decade so gradually no one noticed the mortar going soft — to buy his son free of whatever claim the Hollow Court had tried to lay on him. Every deferred renewal, every *reduced strength per agreement*, every payment received in full, was a year of Tam's life purchased with other people's safety. The stars had not gone out because the wards failed. The wards had failed because they had been sold, patiently, lovingly, by the most honest man Tam had ever known.

And the final ledger — the eleventh, the current one — held only a handful of entries. The last was dated the night the sky went black. It read:

*Balance due. They have come to collect the remainder. I go up the mountain to settle it myself. If the boy reads this, tell him the wards were never his inheritance. Tell him I am sorry they were mine.*

Below that, in ink of a different color — darker, almost brown, and Tam did not let himself think about what kind of brown ink dries that way — a second hand had added a single line:

*Paid in full. Account closed. The son remains ours to call.*

* * *

Dawn came grey and without comfort. Tam became aware, distantly, that he was sitting in his father's chair with the eleventh ledger open in his lap and that his face was wet and that he had been crying without any memory of starting. The dog outside had finally gone quiet, or perhaps Tam had simply stopped hearing it.

He should burn them. That was his first coherent thought. Burn the ledgers, bury the truth, walk away, let the dead keep their secrets. If no one ever read these pages, then his father remained what Tam had always believed him to be: a stubborn, decent, overworked man who died keeping his watch. The wards broke. Tragedy. Accident. No one need ever know that the tragedy had been scheduled, invoiced, and *paid in full*.

And then he thought of the eastern villages. Mirefen, where the drowned things walked since the wards thinned. The farmsteads along the border road where people had nailed horseshoes over every door and still lost children in the night. Ten thousand people who had trusted the name Vey the way they trusted stone and rain, and whose trust had been spent, year by year, to save one boy who had never even been asked.

*I told them the wards instead.*

His father had chosen. That was the thing Tam could not get around, the splinter driven too deep to draw. Given the choice between his son and everyone else, Aldous Vey had chosen his son, and had done it slowly enough to pretend, right up until the last page, that he wasn't choosing at all. And now Tam understood something else too, something that sat in his stomach like swallowed ice: the Court had accepted the trade. It had taken thirty years of wards and one vanished ward-keeper, and it had left the boy alive and marked — *the son remains ours to call* — because a debt unpaid is worth more than a soul collected. Because someday, the Court had known, the son would climb the mountain himself, carrying his father's guilt like an heirloom, and offer to settle accounts.

That was why the ghost had looked at him strangely. That was why Ivo Thackeray watched him the way a moneylender watches a debtor. Everyone on this road, Tam realized, was a creditor or collateral, and he had just discovered which one he was.

He closed the ledger gently, the way you close a coffin.

Then he opened it again, because his father had taught him better than to flinch from a record. *Show your work.* Fine. Then he would show his. He took a fresh sheet of paper, dipped his pen, and began to copy out the entries that mattered — the deferrals, the diversions, the dates, the terrible arithmetic of a decade — in his own hand, which was his father's hand, which was the point. If the Vey name was going to mean anything after this, it would mean a man willing to write down what his family had done. The ledgers would go up the mountain with him. Not as a blueprint. As a bill.

Outside, the violet dawn was lifting over Greyharrow, and far off across the rooftops Tam could see the twilight market's glow bleeding up from the slopes of Serle like a wound in the world that refused to close. Somewhere under that glow, his father's account stood open. Somewhere under that glow, a court of the soul-drained waited for the son to come and call.

He packed the ledgers, banked the lamp, and paused at the door with his hand on his father's coat — just touching the wool, just once — before stepping out into the starless morning to find the hedge-witch, and tell her the wards had an heir, and the heir intended to collect.
