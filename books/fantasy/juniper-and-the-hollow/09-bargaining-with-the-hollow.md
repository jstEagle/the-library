## 9. Bargaining with the Hollow

The Underway did not begin so much as it swallowed.

One moment Ivo was walking the old pilgrim road beneath Mount Serle's shoulder, where the pines grew crooked and the wind smelled of snow and iron, and the next there was a doorway in the rock that had not been there a heartbeat before — a seam of darkness shaped exactly like a door, patient as a held breath. Tam stopped so abruptly she walked into his back.

"That wasn't here yesterday," he said. "I've mapped this road. My father's maps. There's no door."

"There is now," Wren said.

She stood at the threshold, and Ivo watched her again — the watching had become a habit she could not break, an itch behind the eyes. Wren was more solid than she'd been three days ago on the mountain road, when she had first stepped out of starless dark and spoken Juniper's cadence like a knife slid between Ivo's ribs. Then, Ivo could have counted her ribs through her shift. Now the shift hung on shoulders that cast shadow. Her hair had color in it now, a brown so dark it was nearly black, and when she turned her head Ivo saw the light catch the edge of her jaw.

Ghosts did not gain flesh. Ghosts faded, thinned, went to smoke and memory. Whatever Wren was doing, she was doing it backwards.

"You first," Tam said to Wren, because he was not a fool.

Wren smiled — and it was Juniper's smile, the one-sided one, the one that had meant *I know something you don't yet*, and Ivo's chest tightened around twelve years of guilt. "It doesn't eat ghosts," Wren said. "That's rather the point of me."

"And what's the point of us?" Tam asked.

"Passage," Wren said simply. "The Court opened this for me. It knows I'm coming home." She glanced back at them both, and her eyes — grey, grey as winter water, grey as eyes Ivo had once trusted with her life — lingered on Ivo a half-second too long. "And it knows you're coming too. It's been waiting for you, Ivo Thackeray. That should frighten you more than the dark does."

It did. But Ivo had spent twelve years running from things that frightened her, and all it had bought her was a satchel of dead charms and a name she couldn't say aloud. She adjusted the strap across her chest, touched the one charm that still lived — the small knot of juniper wood and red thread that had pulled her north since the night the stars died, warm as a sleeping bird against her sternum — and stepped through the door.

* * *

The dark inside was not empty. That was the first thing she understood, and the thing she would spend the rest of the descent trying to forget.

The Underway breathed. She felt it in the walls — a slow, vast rhythm, in and out, in and out, like the flank of some animal too large to have a name. The stone under her boots was warm. Not sun-warm; nothing down here had ever seen the sun. Warm the way a body is warm, the way a fevered brow is warm, and twice an hour the passage contracted around them by a finger's width, so gently that if she hadn't been counting she might have missed it.

"It's digesting," Wren said, when Ivo finally asked.

"Don't," Tam said.

"Somebody has to say it." Wren walked ahead with the easy stride of someone who knew every turn before it came, which was its own horror. Her voice carried strangely in the tunnel, arriving a half-beat before her lips moved, or after — Ivo could never decide which, and the not-deciding made her teeth ache. "Souls go in. The crown drinks. What's left goes into the stone. You're walking through what's left."

Ivo ran her hand along the wall without meaning to and snatched it back. The stone had been smooth as glass there, and faintly veined, and for one lurching moment she had thought she saw a face in the grain — mouths, dozens of them, open. She told herself it was mineral patterning. She told herself a great many things.

They walked. The tunnel descended in long spirals, sometimes wide enough for six abreast, sometimes narrowing until Tam had to turn sideways and his breath came fast and shallow — he hated the closeness, she could see it, the ward-keeper's son who had grown up under open skies, and she hated it differently: the dark reminded her of the cellar under Juniper's cottage, where she had hidden while men in magistrate's colors took her master away, and where she had done nothing, said nothing, sold nothing except her silence, and called it survival ever after.

"How much farther?" she asked, the fourth or fifth time.

"However far it needs to be," Wren said. "The Underway isn't a place, Ivo. It's an appetite. We're not walking to the Court. We're being swallowed toward it."

* * *

They camped when they could walk no further, though camp was a generous word for sitting pressed together in a niche while the walls pulsed around them. Tam produced hard bread and harder cheese from his pack, and they ate by the light of the living charm, which Ivo had unstrung and set in her palm like a coal. Its glow was wrong — not gold but a deep violet, the color of the sky just before stars appear, and it threw their shadows up the walls in shapes that moved even when they didn't.

"The wards my father built," Tam said suddenly, into the silence. He'd been turning something over in his hands — a scrap of lead, a ward-seal stamped with the Serle sigil. "He checked them every season. Thirty years. He used to take me up the peak when I was small and make me hold the sounding-rod while he read the harmonics. He loved those wards like children." His voice went flat. "And then one night they just... weren't. And he was gone. And everyone says he sold them, and maybe he did, but I keep thinking — thirty years, Ivo. You don't love something thirty years and sell it in one."

"People do worse for better reasons," Ivo said.

"Is that what you tell yourself?"

She looked at him. In the violet light his face was all angles and exhaustion, seventeen years old and carrying his father's disgrace like a yoke. "I don't tell myself anything anymore," she said. "That's the trouble with running. Eventually you run out of lies, and then all that's left is the truth, and the truth is heavy, and you've got to carry it the rest of the way."

Wren, sitting apart, said nothing. But she was looking at Ivo with an expression Ivo couldn't parse — grief, or recognition, or something older than either — and when their eyes met, Wren looked away first.

* * *

On the second day — if days meant anything where no dawn had ever reached — the tunnel began to change.

First came the sound. A murmur, far off, like a market heard through several walls: hundreds of voices, none of them words. Ivo stopped. "What is that?"

"The Court," Wren said. "Or the souls in it. Or both. Down here the distinction stops mattering."

Then came the doors. Little ones, set into the tunnel walls at intervals — human-sized, wooden, iron-hinged, absurdly domestic in all that living stone. Some stood ajar. Through one, Ivo glimpsed a kitchen, a cold hearth, a chair facing the wall. Through another, a child's bedroom, the bed neatly made, dust on everything, and hanging from a nail on the door, a luck-knot. Her own work. Her own knots — she knew the tension of them the way she knew her own handwriting. Knots she had sold in Greyharrow over the years, hanging on doors deep inside a mountain.

Her stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

"They come back," Wren said quietly, following her gaze. "Charms are promises, Ivo. When the promiser dies, or is taken, the promise comes home to be collected. Every charm you ever sold to someone the Court took — it's down here somewhere. Waiting for you."

"I never sold to anyone who—"

"You can't know who," Wren said. "That's the cruelty of hedge-craft. You sell luck to strangers. Luck has to come from somewhere."

Ivo thought of the dockworker with the luck-knot, three nights before the stars went out. She thought of the coin that hummed wrong. She had given it back to him. She had told herself that was enough.

* * *

The third descent brought them to the river.

There was no water. That was the horror of it — a riverbed the width of a street, stones worn round and pale as knucklebones, banks of silt, the whole architecture of a river without a drop in it. And moving along the dry channel, slow as fog, low as mist, came something that gleamed. Not light. Gleam. The way a fish gleams, the way a dead eye gleams.

Tam grabbed Ivo's arm. "What is that?"

"Payment," Wren said. "Going upstream. Souls flow downhill to the Court. This—" she tilted her head, listening to something they couldn't hear, "—this is going the other way. Refunds. Exchanges. Someone above is buying."

"Who buys souls?" Tam whispered.

"Whoever can pay," Wren said. "Ask yourself what they're paying with."

Ivo watched the gleam pass them, close enough that she felt it — a pressure against her skin like standing near a struck bell, and inside her satchel, impossibly, one of her dead charms twitched. Just once. Like a sleeper dreaming of waking.

When it had gone, Wren was taller.

Not much. An inch, perhaps less. But her hands were solid now, knuckles and nails, and when she crouched to touch the dry stones, Ivo heard the small grit-sound of skin on rock. Three days ago she had been smoke wearing a shape. Now she left faint prints in the silt.

"You're growing," Ivo said. It came out accusatory. She hadn't meant it to.

Wren rose and looked at her own hands, flexing them, and there was something terrible in her face — wonder and dread in equal measure, a person watching themselves become real and knowing the price. "The crown is close," she said. "It made me out of what it's missing. Every mile we descend, it spends a little more of itself on me. It's feeding me to get me back." She smiled that one-sided smile, and it shook at the corner. "I'm bait, Ivo. So are you. So is he. The Court doesn't need to hunt us. It only needs to want us, and we'll walk the whole way down on our own legs."

"Then why guide us at all?" Tam demanded. "If you're its creature, why help?"

"Because," Wren said, and for a moment her voice was not Juniper's cadence but something rawer, younger, hers alone, "whatever the crown wants me to be, I remember being something else. And I would rather choose wrongly than be chosen rightly." She turned and walked on. After a moment, they followed.

* * *

Near the end — though Ivo would not know it was near until it was behind her — the tunnel opened into a gallery, and the gallery was full of doors.

Hundreds. Thousands. Standing free without walls, hinged on nothing, ranked in rows into the dark until the violet glow of Ivo's charm gave out and the dark kept going anyway. Doors of pine, of oak, of painted harbor-blue, of black iron. Each one bore a mark scratched into the wood: a name, or part of one. She found *MERRIC HALLOW* on a fisherman's door, salt-stained. She found *BESS T.* half-finished, as if the carver had been interrupted. She found, on a plain grey door near the aisle's center, in a hand she knew better than her own — the careful, looping, teacher's hand —

*JUNIPER.*

The word hit her like a fall. Ivo put her palm flat against the wood and the wood was warm, and beneath her hand, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, something knocked. Once. Twice. Patient. Not desperate — patient, the way Juniper had been patient with her through four years of apprenticeship, through every botched charm and stolen glance at the advanced grimoires, patient as stone, patient as love.

"She's in there?" Tam asked softly. "Behind the door?"

"No one is behind the doors," Wren said. "The doors are what's left of the having-a-door. The Court takes everything, even the way out." She paused. "But knock, if you need to. Sometimes they knock back."

Ivo knocked. Two raps, the way Juniper had always answered her childhood signal — two knocks meaning *yes, come in, the kettle's on*. And from the other side of a door that led nowhere, in a mountain that ate souls, came two knocks back.

Ivo Thackeray had not wept in twelve years. She had built a life out of not weeping, plank by plank, and she had believed the structure sound. It took her a long moment to understand the wet heat on her face, and longer to forgive it.

"We keep moving," Wren said gently. "Before it learns your knock."

* * *

The last stretch narrowed, and warmed, and the murmur became a music — courtly music, distant strings and a slow waltz, absurd and obscene down here in the dark — and the walls began to glitter. Veins of something bright ran through the stone, and Ivo made herself look closely and immediately wished she hadn't: the veins were threads. Silver-fine, countless, all running the same direction, down, converging ahead like rivers to a sea. Soul-stuff. Twelve years of taken souls, spun into the mountain's warp.

And threaded among them, distinct, a single strand of a different color — green-gold, herbal, stubborn — running parallel to the others but separate, apart, the way Juniper had always stood slightly apart from every crowd she'd ever entered.

Ivo's charm flared against her sternum, hot enough to sting, and pulled downward like a compass needle finding true north at last.

"There," she said. Her voice cracked and she let it. "That's her. That's the debt. I'm done bargaining."

"No," Wren said, and took her arm — took it with a solid, warm, undeniably living hand, and that was the most frightening thing yet, because the hand fit Ivo's arm the way another hand had once fit it, guiding her through her first charm-work, steadying her first spell. "You're not done bargaining. You've just arrived at the market. And Ivo — listen to me, whatever happens — when they offer you a price, and they will offer, and it will be fair, that's how you'll know it's a trap. Nothing down here is fairly priced. Fairness is the lure."

Ahead, the tunnel ended in light — twilight-colored, sourceless, the exact amber-grey of the hour when hedge-witches lit their lamps — and through it came the smell of hot sugar and cut grass and something underneath, older, sweeter, rotten at the heart.

Music. Laughter. The clink of coin.

The Hollow Court's market, opening before them like a mouth.

Ivo straightened her collar, strung her dead charms where a peddler would hang them, and stepped out of the dark to sell.
