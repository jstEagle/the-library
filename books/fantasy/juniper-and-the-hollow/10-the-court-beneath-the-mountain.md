## 10. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The Underway ended the way a held breath ends — not with a sound but with a release, a sudden opening in the dark that let them through.

Ivo had expected a door. What she got was a threshold of bone-white stone worn smooth as river glass, and beyond it a light that did not come from any torch or lamp but from the air itself, a pale gold shimmer like sunlight remembered rather than seen. It fell across her face and she flinched, twelve years of night-craft rising in her like a startled bird.

"Steady," Wren murmured at her shoulder. The ghost had grown so solid on the last mile that Ivo could see the weave of her grey dress, the fine lines at the corners of her eyes. "It won't hurt you. It doesn't know how."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be." Wren's mouth curved — Juniper's crooked half-smile, exactly, unbearably, Juniper's — and she stepped past Ivo onto the smooth stone. "It was meant to be true. Come and look at what they've built with all those souls they swallowed."

Ivo looked.

And for one long, unforgivable moment, she forgot to be afraid.

* * *

The Hollow Court lay in a cavern so vast it had weather. Not wind, exactly — but movement, a slow tidal stirring of air that carried scents: hot wax, cold iron, overripe plums, something sweeter underneath that Ivo refused to name because naming it would mean admitting it smelled like the inside of a charm when it worked, like power settling into its shape.

Above, where a sky should have been, the cavern's ceiling rose beyond sight into a darkness that glittered. At first she thought it was ore, some vein of mica catching the golden light. Then she saw that the glints moved. Drifted. Swirled in slow constellations that almost — *almost* — formed patterns she knew. The Ploughman. The Sisters. The Weeping Star.

"They're wearing the stars," Tam said hoarsely beside her. He had gone the color of tallow. "Or what's left of them. Look — they're not shining. They're *reflecting*. Something down there is giving off light and the fragments are just... catching it."

"The crown," Ivo said. Her voice came out flat and strange, as if someone else were using her mouth. "It ate them. It's wearing them."

Below the false sky, the Court spread itself in terraces down the curve of the cavern like frost ferns growing across a window. Pale stairways. Bridges of black stone strung with lanterns that burned without flame. Gardens where the flowers were made of wire and glass and moved anyway, turning their faces toward passersby with an attention no flower should have. And everywhere, everywhere, people.

That was the word her mind offered first, and it was a lie, and she knew it was a lie, and she used it anyway because the truth was worse.

They were beautiful. That was the first obscenity of the place. Every soul-drained courtier wore beauty like a uniform — skin lit faintly from within, hair moving in the tide-air as though underwater, garments that shifted color with their moods. They drifted along the terraces in slow processions, gathered at fountains that poured light instead of water, danced in long unhurried lines to music Ivo could not hear but could feel in her back teeth.

And none of them were *there*.

She saw it when one turned its face toward her as she descended the first stair. The eyes were lovely and utterly empty, twin lamps burning behind frosted glass. The mouth smiled at precisely the right moment. But nothing behind the smile reached the surface. It was like watching a puppet operated by someone who had only ever had happiness described to them.

"They're still in there," Wren said quietly. "That's the cruelty of it. The crown doesn't destroy what it takes. It wears it. Keeps the shape, keeps the smile, keeps the dance — and takes everything that made it worth keeping."

"How many?" Tam whispered.

Wren didn't answer. She didn't have to. Ivo counted the terraces, the bridges, the gardens, the slow bright rivers of the drained, and stopped counting somewhere past the number of people who lived in Greyharrow entire.

* * *

They went down among them, because going down was the only direction left, and because standing apart would mark them faster than anything else. Ivo pulled her charm-seller's shawl tight and became, by long practice, nobody: a hedge-witch come to market, unremarkable, unremarked.

It nearly worked.

The trouble was that the Court *noticed* craft. Within a hundred paces, heads began to turn — not all at once, nothing so crude as that, but in ones and twos, empty beautiful faces tilting toward her like sunflowers after a sun that wasn't there. The luck-knots in her pocket grew warm. The little warding stitch she kept sewn into her cuff began to itch.

"They can feel your charms," Wren murmured. "You're carrying living magic into a room full of things that are starving for it. Walk slower. Breathe slower. Be boring."

"I am extremely boring," Ivo hissed. "I have been boring professionally for twelve years."

"And yet." Wren touched her elbow — and Ivo felt it, actual pressure, five cool points of contact, and hated how much that small solidity undid her. "There. A fountain ahead. Sit. Courtiers sit at fountains. Do it."

They sat on the rim of a fountain that poured pale gold light into a basin, where it pooled and swirled and never overflowed. Up close Ivo could see that the light was threaded with movement — tiny shapes turning in the flow, too small to resolve, and every few moments one of them would press against the surface from inside, briefly, like a moth against a window.

She looked away first.

Across the basin, a courtier in silver-grey watched her. Or faced her; watching implied intent. Its lips moved.

"New," it said. The voice was pleasant and hollow, a bell with the clapper removed. "New-smelling. Sweet. Where do you keep it?"

"Keep what?" Ivo asked, mild as milk.

The courtier smiled its perfect secondhand smile. "Your *name*," it said. "Everyone who comes down here whole keeps their name somewhere. In a pocket. Behind the teeth. May I—"

"No," said Wren, and her voice cracked across the fountain like ice on a pond. The courtier went still. For a heartbeat something moved behind its empty eyes — recognition, or fear, or memory — and then it rose, bowed with flawless grace, and drifted away into the bright crowd.

Tam exhaled. "What did you—"

"I don't know," Wren said. She sounded shaken, and looked it, and Ivo realized with a lurch that the ghost's hands were trembling. "I don't know what I did. It heard me the way things hear each other down here. Ivo — I'm getting *stronger*. Every mile we've walked, I've been more here. That isn't how ghosts work. Ghosts spend. I'm being *spent on*."

Ivo looked at her — at the solid wrists, the shadow she now cast on the pale stone, the breath that fogged faintly in the cavern's chill — and felt the shape of an answer pressing against the back of her mind, an answer she was not ready to have.

"Not here," she said. "Not now."

"Now might be all we get."

"*Not now.*"

* * *

They followed the terraces down toward the heart of the Court, and the heart revealed itself gradually, the way a wound reveals itself under bandages — first a glow, then a glare, then the thing itself, too large and too bright to look at directly.

At the bottom of the cavern stood a palace. It had not been built so much as *grown*, walls of black stone ribbed like the inside of a shell, and every surface of it was crusted with light — gems, yes, but also coins, rings, lockets, wedding bands, spectacles, thimbles, a child's tin whistle, ten thousand small bright treasures fused into the stone like barnacles. Things people had loved. Things people had been holding when they were taken, or things taken *instead* of them, payment rendered to the Court by the desperate above.

And set into the palace's highest tower, visible from every terrace in the cavern because everything in the cavern was arranged to make it visible, hung the Crown of Night.

Ivo had imagined a crown. Gold, perhaps, or iron, studded with stolen starlight.

It was none of those things. It was a *hole*. A circlet-shaped absence hanging in the air above the tower, a ring of pure dark against the golden haze, and around its edge the stolen stars orbited slowly — fragments of the Ploughman, slivers of the Sisters, ground up and set spinning like flies caught in amber. Light went into it and did not come out. Even from this distance Ivo could feel it pulling, a faint tug behind her sternum, gentle as a hook through wool.

Her charms answered it. All of them at once, the whole humble inventory of her pockets warming like stones from a fire, straining toward the tower.

"Stop that," she breathed at them, absurdly, and pressed her hand flat over her pocket.

"It feels you," Wren said. "The way the courtiers felt you. Only bigger."

"Then we're fools to have come."

"We were always coming." Wren's voice was very quiet. "From the night the sky went out. Maybe from twelve years ago. Maybe from before that. Some debts walk you to them, Ivo. You just keep your feet moving until you arrive."

On the lowest terrace, before the shell-palace's gates, a procession was forming. Courtiers arranging themselves in ranks, the music Ivo couldn't hear swelling into something felt in the ribs. And down the great stair came figures that even the drained made way for — tall, robed in colors that hurt to look at, their faces hidden behind masks of polished bone. Servants of the crown. Or its hands.

Behind them, borne on a litter of black silk, came a woman whose beauty was of a different order entirely. Not borrowed. Not hollow. She was old in the way mountains are old, and her eyes, when the litter passed close enough for Ivo to see them, were the eyes of someone who had once been kind and had found kindness expensive.

The Regent, Wren had called her, in the whisper-talk of the Underway. The crown's keeper. Its wearer, some said. Its prisoner, others said.

As the litter drew level with the fountain, the woman turned her head. Slowly. Deliberately. Across forty feet of crowded terrace, past a hundred drifting courtiers, her gaze found Ivo Thackeray crouched among the drained like a sparrow among glass birds.

And she smiled.

Not the courtiers' secondhand smile. A real one. Warm, even. Welcoming.

"*There* you are," she said, and though the words were pitched for her alone, they crossed the space between them with terrible ease, the way a needle crosses cloth. "We've been keeping your seat."

* * *

The procession swept on toward the palace gates, and the crowd closed behind it like water, and Ivo stayed crouched at the fountain with her heart slamming and her charms burning in her pockets, doing the arithmetic of survival.

"She knows me," she said. "How does she know me? I've never been down here. I'd remember."

"Would you?" Wren asked softly.

"Don't. Don't do that riddle-ghost thing with me right now."

"I'm not being a riddle." Wren took her hand — solid, cool, real — and turned it palm-up, and nodded at the old scar across Ivo's lifeline, the one from the binding-cut every apprentice took, the one she'd told herself for twelve years was just a scar. "Ash didn't find you in Greyharrow by accident. The wards didn't fail by accident. The coin that hummed wrong, the charm that led you north — Ivo, you weren't running from this place anymore than a river runs from the sea. Everything flows downhill. Including you."

Tam had gone very still. "Then why bring us in alive? Why the market, the disguises, all of it? If the crown wanted her, it could have—"

"Because taking is cheap," Ivo said, and heard Corvane Ash's voice in her own mouth, twelve years old and warm as brandy, *power is simply taken, girl, the only sin is hesitating*, "and this place doesn't want to take her. It wants her to *give*. Freely. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point."

She looked up at the tower, at the ring of hungry dark wearing its necklace of murdered stars, and felt the pull again behind her sternum, patient as tide.

Somewhere in that shell-black palace was a vault, and in the vault a shelf, and on the shelf a soul gone dim but not out — a soul she had sold with her silence, or that had been sold for her, the ledger of it waiting to be read. Somewhere in that palace too, if Wren's whispers held, was the man who had taught her everything worth regretting.

"Right," Ivo said, and stood, and shook out her shawl, and put on the face she wore for customers — open, honest, a little foolish, utterly forgettable. "If they're expecting me, then I'll arrive properly. Charm-seller, come to trade. Let's go give the Hollow Court exactly what it asked for."

"And what's that?" Tam asked.

Ivo looked at the gates, at the bone-masked honor guard, at the smiling Regent already receding into the glare.

"A witch," she said, "who thinks she wants what they have."

And she walked down the last stair into the light.
