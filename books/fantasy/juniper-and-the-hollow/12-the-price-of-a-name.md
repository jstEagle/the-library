## 12. The Price of a Name

I had been dead for twelve years before I knew it.

That is not quite true. I had been *something* for twelve years — a warmth in a charm-bag, a hum in a knot of red thread, a voice that spoke only when no one was listening. I had been the reason Ivo Thackeray's luck held when every other hedge-witch's luck ran out. I had been the small kindness she never noticed, the way a lamp does not notice the oil. And I had been, all that while, a lie I told myself so often it wore smooth as river stone: that I was simply what remained of a woman called Wren, some gutter-ghost with no grave and no name but the one Ivo gave me.

The mountain took the lie apart piece by piece, the way water takes apart a wall.

It began in the Underway, where the dark was old enough to have opinions. We walked by the light of Ivo's charms — her humble knots and knuckles of thread, which burned low and steady where grander magics would have guttered — and with every mile the crown's hunger reached up through the stone like roots through a coffin lid, and with every mile I felt myself grow *more*. More solid. More warm. More burdened with the terrible weight of having hands.

I looked at my hands often now. In Greyharrow they had been suggestions, smudges at the edge of lamplight. On the mountain road they had gained edges. Here beneath Serle, in the breathless dark between the ward-stones, they were hands entire: knuckles, nails, a scar across the left palm I did not remember earning and could not stop touching.

"You keep doing that," Ivo said. She did not look back. She was counting paces, marking turns, doing the small competent arithmetic that kept us alive. "Touching your hand."

"Do you remember giving me this scar?"

That stopped her. Her lantern-charm swung, and its light caught the hollows of her face — she had grown thinner since the market, thinner since Ash's offer, as though the Court's attention itself fed on her and left her less. "No," she said carefully. "Should I?"

"Someone should." I flexed the fingers. The scar pulled, faintly, sweetly, like an old ache remembered in a dream. "It isn't mine. I know that much now. It's borrowed. Everything about me is borrowed."

* * *

Here is what I remembered, in the deep places, when the crown's hunger pressed close enough to whisper through my bones:

A cottage with rosemary growing wild along the south wall. A kettle that whistled three notes because the spout was dented. Rain on a roof I had mended myself, badly, laughing about it. And a girl — sixteen, furious, brilliant, her magic coming up in her like floodwater through floorboards — standing in the doorway with a bag already packed, saying words I had spent twelve years not hearing:

*I can't stay, Juniper. Don't you understand? He said if I stayed I'd be nothing. He said power is taken. I'm going to take mine.*

And me — older, tired, my own craft worn smooth and small as a hearthstone — saying the only thing I had left to say, which was not *stay*, because you cannot chain a storm to a kitchen:

*Then take this too.*

And pressing into her palm a luck-knot, red thread, seven turns, my blood in the dye and something more than blood in the tying. Something I did not tell her I was giving. Something I could not take back once the knot closed.

*A piece of me,* I whispered to the dark of the Underway, walking behind the woman that girl had become. *I tied a piece of myself into your pocket and sent you out into the world, and I let you believe it was only luck.*

Because that was the truth under all my truths. Not that I was Juniper. That I was the part of Juniper who loved Ivo Thackeray enough to be cut away for her sake — and the part that stayed, that followed, that kept her alive through twelve years of running from a debt she didn't know she carried. The Court had not made me. The Court had only *fed* me. Its hunger reached through the mountain like a bellows, and everything it breathed toward Ivo — every stolen scrap of soul that drifted near her, every echo of the woman whose soul sat dim on a shelf in Ash's vault — had pooled in the vessel Juniper left behind. Filled it. Gave it weight and warmth and, at the last, a face.

Wren's face. The face Ivo dreamed for me, one night in a ditch outside Greyharrow, half-asleep and grieving, murmuring *someone should walk with me* — and the fragment in her charm-bag had answered, shaping itself out of starless air into the companion she needed.

I had thought I woke into being. I had been woken *into use.*

* * *

We camped in a side gallery where the stone sweated cold water and the walls were carved with ward-sigils so ancient their meanings had worn away, leaving only the shapes of protection, like footprints of a god long gone. Tam slept badly, curled around his father's ledger as if it might still bite him. Ivo sat sharpening a knife she would never use against anything that mattered.

I sat across from her and watched the firelight move in her face, and I understood that I was going to have to say it, and that saying it would be the second death — the real one, the one without return.

"Ivo."

She looked up. Twelve years of guilt lived in the lines around her eyes, and I hated that I had helped put them there, and I hated more that I was about to deepen them.

"When we get to the vault," I said, "when you take Juniper's soul back — it won't be whole."

Her knife stopped. "What do you mean, won't be whole? You said—"

"I said dim. Intact. I lied by omission, which is apparently a family trait." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "There's a piece missing. A piece that's been missing for twelve years. A piece that's been sleeping in your charm-bag, keeping your luck warm, learning to love you the way the whole of her loved you — which is to say stubbornly, and against all sense, and past all deserving."

The knife-point lowered. I watched her not understand, and then watch herself begin to understand, and I watched the understanding arrive in her face the way frost arrives on a window — slowly, then all at once, in patterns too intricate to undo.

"No," she said.

"Wren isn't my name."

"Don't." The word cracked down the middle. "Don't you dare—"

"Juniper named a wren once," I went on, because someone had to say it and it could not be her, not yet, maybe not ever. "In the journal you found — page forty-one, the entry half-burned. *A wren built in the eaves again. Small bird, enormous voice. Ivo used to say it sang like it was owed money.* You read that passage four times before we left the cottage. You thought you were reading it for the debt. You weren't. You were reading it because some part of you has always known what walks beside you."

She stood up. The firelight bent away from her, or she bent away from it; her magic stirred, the stolen, hungry, crown-touched thing Ash had promised her a throne beside, and for one moment the shadows leaned toward her like courtiers.

"You're lying," she said. "Or the crown is using you to lie. It wants me to give things back. It wants me weak. This is—" Her voice failed and restarted, lower. "This is what it does. It takes what you love and wears it."

"Yes," I said gently. "It does. And I am what it's wearing. Both things are true, Ivo. That's the cruelty of it. I am a ghost the crown dressed, and I am also hers — the piece of Juniper that chose you over wholeness, twelve years ago, with a knot of red thread and no goodbye. The crown didn't make that love. It only gave it somewhere to stand."

Tam had woken. He lay very still, watching us the way he watched everything now — a boy who had learned his inheritance was a wound and could not yet decide whether to cauterize or cherish it. I was glad he heard. Someone should witness. Grief unwatched curdles.

"How?" Ivo's voice was small now, stripped of the magistrate's lessons, of Corvane Ash's silken arithmetic. Just a girl in the dark asking how. "How can you be her? She sold her soul — the journal said—"

"She sold it to buy your escape. All of it, or so the ledger says. But souls aren't coins, Ivo. They don't come off in clean weights. When she tied that knot — when she put her blood and her intent and a piece of her own continuing into your keeping — that piece passed out of the sale. Out of reach of the Court. Out of reach of time." I spread my borrowed hands. "They couldn't sell what she'd already given away. So the vault holds the greater part of her, dim and waiting, and the lesser part has been walking at your heel for twelve years, complaining about your cooking, saving your life eleven times — yes, I counted — and telling you stories about a witch you thought you'd murdered."

"I didn't—"

"You sold her. Yes. I know. I've known since the road, since you talk in your sleep, since you flinch every time someone says the word *debt*." I stood, and my body — my beautiful, temporary, stolen body — felt every inch of its borrowed weight. "And here is the thing you cannot forgive yourself for, Ivo, so I will say it plainly, because I am the only creature in creation with the authority to absolve you and I intend to spend that authority before I spend everything else: *she forgave you before you finished betraying her.* She saw you going. She saw the shape of what Ash had taught you to want. And rather than let you be caught, rather than let you be broken into something the Court could wear — she paid. She paid with herself. Your betrayal was the price; her sacrifice was the coin. You have spent twelve years drowning in a debt that was discharged the moment you walked out that door."

The fire popped. Somewhere far above, through miles of stone, the crown turned in its sleep, and the dark leaned closer to listen.

"Why are you telling me this now?" Ivo whispered.

"Because in the vault you'll find her, and she will be missing a piece, and you will see the shape of the absence and know it the way you know your own handwriting. And I will not let you learn it there, in that place, with Ash smiling. You deserve to learn it under open stone with a friend beside you." I swallowed. Borrowed throats ache when they carry true things. "And because there is a price, Ivo. There is always a price. To make her whole, the piece must go back. All of it. Every memory, every scar, every hour of loving you."

"And the piece goes back," she said slowly, "and Wren—"

"There is no Wren. There never was. There is only what you needed, wearing what she gave."

* * *

She did not cry. I had known she wouldn't; Ivo Thackeray cried the way mountains erode, invisibly, over years. She sat back down across the fire and looked at me for a long time, and I let her look, because whatever else I was, I was the last hours of a woman who had waited twelve years to be seen.

"The scar," she said finally. "On your hand. Where's it from?"

"A kettle," I said. "Dented spout. Whistled three notes. I was showing a furious sixteen-year-old how to hold it steady while her magic tried to boil the water from the inside." I smiled, and felt the smile fit my face the way a key fits a lock it was cut for. "You laughed. First time in weeks. You said, *if I ever have a shop, you're banned from the kettle.*"

Ivo made a sound that was not quite a laugh and not quite anything else. "Juniper's kettle."

"Juniper's kettle. Juniper's rosemary. Juniper's terrible mended roof." I folded my hands in my lap, the scar upward, an offering. "I am not asking you to decide tonight. But I am asking you to decide *before* the vault. Because when you see her — the greater part of her, dim on that shelf — you will want to bargain. With Ash. With the crown. With God and the dark under the dark. And every bargain you strike will cost either her wholeness or my existence, and I need to know, before we descend another mile into this hungry mountain, which of those you can live with. Because I know which one *she* would choose. She chose it once already."

Silence. Tam sat up slowly, the ledger in his lap, and said, in the careful voice of a boy learning to speak in a house of ghosts: "There might be a third way. The ledgers — my father's ledgers — debts can be transferred. Reassigned. If the crown considers Wren an asset, a holding—"

"It doesn't consider me anything," I said softly. "It considers me *ripe.*"

And even as I said it, I felt it — the crown's attention turning toward me through the stone like a great eye opening, patient, pleased, recognizing its own work. It had fed me flesh mile by mile. It had ripened Juniper's cast-off love into something whole enough to harvest. Whether Ivo gave me back to the soul in the vault or the crown took me for itself, the fragment would return to the mountain.

The only question left in the world was whose hands it returned in.

Ivo looked at me across the dying fire, and her eyes were wet after all — erosion, sudden as a landslide — and she said, "Then we go down tomorrow. And we go down fast. Before it finishes deciding what you are."

Far below us, deeper than the vault, deeper than the Court, something vast and crowned began — very quietly, very patiently — to sing. Three notes. Like a kettle.

Like a dented spout, calling someone home.
