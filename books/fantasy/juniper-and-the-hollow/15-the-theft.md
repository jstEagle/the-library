## 15. The Theft

The vault door was not a door.

That was the first thing Ivo understood about it, standing in the cold gallery beneath Ash's counting-house while Tam's borrowed credentials still hummed warm against her collarbone. Doors could be picked. Doors could be charmed, coaxed, bribed with a knot of red thread and the right whispered word. This was a seam — a place where the mountain had been persuaded to close over its own mouth, and the stone had been paid well to forget there was ever an opening at all.

She pressed her palm flat against it and felt the payment still moving in there. Old magic, deep magic, the kind that came from crowns and courts and men who believed power was simply taken. It moved like blood under skin.

"Tell me you have something," Tam breathed behind her. He had wedged himself into the alcove's shadow, his father's ledger open across his knees, and he kept glancing back down the corridor as though the dark itself might grow eyes. "Because I got us this far on paper and paper doesn't open mountains."

"I have knots." Ivo flexed her fingers. They were stiff with cold and older fear. "I have thread. I have patience, which is more than this place has."

"That's not —"

"It's enough." She said it the way Juniper used to say it, back when Ivo had been too young and too proud to hear what the words actually meant: *enough* was not a consolation. *Enough* was a discipline.

Wren drifted up beside her, and where she passed, the seam of stone seemed to flinch. The ghost had been growing more solid all the way down through the Underway — her edges no longer bled into the air but held it, and when she turned her head Ivo could see the small scar at her hairline that Juniper had carried since a kettle exploded in the winter of Ivo's fourteenth year. Looking at it made something in Ivo's chest grind like a millstone with nothing left to crush.

"The Court put its hunger in the stone," Wren said. Her voice was quiet, and it was Juniper's voice, and Ivo had stopped pretending that didn't hurt. "Hunger opens for food. Not for force."

"So we don't knock." Ivo drew the coil of charm-thread from inside her coat — plain brown wool, waxed at the ends, the same thread she'd sold by the luck-knot in Greyharrow markets for coppers. Twelve years she had despised this work. Twelve years she had told herself that hedge-craft was a child's alphabet, that real power lived in wards and ledgers and crowns, in things taken rather than things tied. Her hands, traitorous and faithful, already knew the first knot.

She began to work.

* * *

The first charm was a listening-knot, because you did not fight a lock; you eavesdropped on it.

She tied three loops and fed them a whisper of her own warmth — not magic, not really, just attention, the way you'd give attention to a frightened animal. The wool warmed against her fingertips and began, very faintly, to tick. Like a clock. Like a heart counting something down.

"What's it doing?" Tam whispered.

"Listening to the stone's memory." Ivo closed her eyes. Through the knot she could feel it: the seam remembered being open. It remembered the day the masons had sealed it, remembered the song they'd sung to make the rock agreeable, remembered — here the knot shivered — the taste of the mortar, which had been mixed with something sweet. Something stolen. A pinch of soul-work in the mortar, the Court's own currency spent on keeping its treasures shut.

"They paid the door with souls," she said, and her voice came out flat and cold. "To keep other souls in."

"That's obscene," Tam said.

"That's accounting," said Wren.

But it told Ivo what she needed. A lock paid in souls would always be hungry, and hunger had a shape, and shapes could be matched. She cut a length of thread, tied a simple open loop — the kind of knot a fisherman's daughter learned before she learned letters — and breathed into it the only thing she had that was worth anything in this place: a memory of wanting. Greyharrow's market square at dusk. The coin that hummed wrong in a child's palm. Twelve years of running with her hand closed around a guilt she wouldn't name.

The loop drank it and went taut, quivering, a small hungry thing itself.

Then she pressed it to the seam, and the seam opened — not wide, not all at once, but the way a mouth opens when it smells supper. A crack of darkness. Cold air breathing out, smelling of dust and lamp-oil and something sweeter underneath, like fruit going soft.

"Inside," she whispered. "Quickly. Before it remembers it isn't hungry anymore."

They slipped through, and behind them the stone sighed shut to within a finger's width, holding itself ajar the way a dog holds a door with its nose. Ivo patted the wall once, absurdly, the way you'd thank a horse.

* * *

Ash's vault was not a dungeon. That was the horror of it.

It was a library. Shelves of pale wood rose three times a man's height into gloom, and on every shelf, in rows as neat as ledgers, sat vessels of glass and silver and clay — jars, phials, lanterns, locket-boxes, teacups, one entire birdcage — and each one held a light. Not flame-light. Soul-light: slow, dim, the color of a candle seen through a curtain, the color of a face remembered instead of seen. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. The whole room breathed with their faint collective glow, and the glow pulsed, very slowly, all together, like the chest of some enormous sleeping animal.

Tam made a sound low in his throat. "His ledger," he said hoarsely. "In my father's records, the transfers were numbers. Just numbers. I thought — I don't know what I thought. Casks. Coins." His hands were shaking around the ward-ledger. "This is what the wards were *for*. This is what he sold them to fill."

"Don't look at them too long," Wren said softly. "Some of them look back."

Ivo didn't need the warning. She could feel them noticing her — the way a field of wheat notices wind. Little attentions turning toward the warmth of her living body, pressing shyly against the insides of their glass, and beneath her feet the floor was warm, warmer than stone should be, because the Court's hunger ran through this room like a root system and everything here was being slowly, patiently drunk.

"Juniper," she said aloud, to steady herself. "Where would he file her?"

"Ash files by debt," Wren said. "Not by name. Names are for the living."

So they walked the aisles, and Ivo read the shelf-labels in Ash's beautiful, terrible hand: *Paid in full. Held against future service. Forfeit, unclaimed. Interest accruing.* Shelf after shelf after shelf, and every label a life reduced to its balance, and somewhere in these rows a soul that had once taught a furious orphan girl to tie her first knot, saying *the thread forgives you, Ivo, that's the whole secret, the thread always forgives.*

There. Third aisle, seventh shelf, near the end where the light grew thin.

The vessel was a preserving jar — a kitchen jar, chipped at the lip, the kind Juniper had kept plums in. Of course it was. Ash hadn't even given her a fine cage. Inside, the soul-light was dimmer than the others, guttering, but it moved when Ivo came near. It leaned toward her the way a plant leans toward a window.

"Oh," Ivo said. It was not a word so much as a wound opening. "Oh, you stupid, stubborn old man. You're still in there. You've been in here *waiting*."

The light pressed against the glass toward her, and though it had no voice, Ivo heard it anyway — or heard what twelve years of memory supplied: *took you long enough, girl.*

"We can't just lift it off the shelf," Tam whispered. He had found the shelf-label and gone gray. "*Interest accruing.* There's a lien on it. Look — there's a thread running from the jar up into the shelving, into the wall. It's attached."

He was right. From the jar's neck ran a single filament of darkness, fine as spider silk, rising into the wood and away into the mountain's bones. The crown's taproot. Even here, even sleeping, the Crown of Night was drinking.

"If we pull it free," Tam said, "we pull the thread, and the thread pulls —"

"Everything," Ivo finished. "Yes. I know." And some cold practical part of her noted, with shame, how easily the sentence came: she had been raised by a magistrate who thought in exactly these terms. Consequence as architecture.

But she was not raised only by him.

She knelt in the aisle with her thread and her patience, and she did the humblest work of her life.

* * *

You could not cut the crown's claim. She knew that much now; Wren had told them on the road down, and the ghost's knowledge had the flat certainty of someone reporting weather. What was taken could only be returned. But a claim, a lien, a debt — those were agreements, and agreements were made of attention, and attention could be *occupied*.

So she built a decoy.

It took her forty minutes, kneeling on cold stone while Tam counted footsteps in the corridor above and Wren stood watch over the jar like a dog over a cradle. Forty minutes of the smallest charms she knew, the ones she'd sneered at, the ones she'd sold for coppers in lawless markets while wardens sharpened their hooks for hedge-witches.

A warmth-knot, filled with the memory of her own body heat, so the thread would taste a living soul nearby. A pulse-charm, two beads of waxed wool tied in a figure that beat against her palm like a second heart — she set it against the jar so the drinking-root would feel a heartbeat where it drank. A name-knot, hardest of all: she tied it around her own spoken name, *Ivo*, and hid the knot inside the jar's shadow so that anything reading the vessel's label would read, for a few precious minutes, not *Forfeit, unclaimed*, but *Ivo Thackeray, held against future service*.

Let the crown drink a decoy. Let it spend its hunger on a knot of wool and a lie.

"And now," she murmured, "the thread forgives me."

She untied the claim.

Not cut — untied. Her fingers worked along the filament of darkness the way Juniper had taught her to work a snarl out of fishing line: gently, without pulling, finding the turn of it, the agreement in it, the place where it had said *yes* and asking it, knot by patient knot, to unsay. The dark fought her. It tasted her decoy-heartbeat and drank, and while it drank it went slack, and in its slackness she turned it, and turned it again, and the last loop slipped free like a ring off a finger.

The jar came off the shelf light as bread.

The soul inside flared — one bright, astonished pulse, like a struck match — and every other light in the vault turned toward it. Ten thousand dim faces pressing toward the glass. The floor's warmth surged. Somewhere far above, deep in the mountain's heart, Ivo felt something enormous shift in its sleep and begin, slowly, to wake.

"Go," Wren said. "Now. It felt that."

They ran. Ivo clutched the jar to her chest and it was warm, warmer than glass had any right to be, and it pulsed against her palms in time with her own hammering heart, and she thought absurdly: *this is what it feels like to carry someone.* She had carried the weight of selling him for twelve years. This was lighter. This was unbearable.

They reached the seam. The stone had begun to close — the decoy's warmth fading, the door remembering its price — and Tam threw himself at the gap and shouldered it, screaming through his teeth, holding a mountain's mouth open with his back while Ivo and Wren slid through into the corridor beyond.

"Come *on*—"

He came. The seam snapped shut behind him like a jaw.

And then the alarms began.

Not bells. Nothing so kind. The walls themselves lit — veins of cold white fire crawling up through the stone, racing away in every direction, toward the market, toward the Court, toward whatever slept beneath the crown. And with the light came a sound, vast and subterranean, a note below hearing that Ivo felt in her teeth and her ribs and the roots of her hair: the sound of ten thousand stolen souls being called to account, and one of them missing.

Above them, boots. Many boots, on many stairs.

Tam grabbed her arm. "The Underway's flooded with wardens by now — we'll never —"

"There's another way." Wren was already moving, more solid than Ivo had ever seen her, almost casting shadow in the vault-fire's glare. She pointed down a narrow stair Ivo hadn't known was there, descending deeper — *deeper*, when every instinct screamed *up*. "Through the drinking galleries. Past the crown's root. It's the one road they won't think to guard, because no one goes *toward* it."

Ivo looked down the stair, into the dark, toward the thing that had eaten the stars.

Then she looked at the jar in her arms, at the small dim light pressing trustfully against the glass, waiting on her the way it had waited twelve years.

"Hold onto me," she said to both of them, and stepped down.
