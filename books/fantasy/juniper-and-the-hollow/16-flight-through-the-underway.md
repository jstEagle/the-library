## 16. Flight Through the Underway

The alarm of the Hollow Court was not a bell. It was a hunger.

Ivo felt it before she heard it — a low vibration in the soles of her boots, in the bones of her jaw, as though the mountain itself had opened its throat and begun to swallow. Behind them, far below and yet everywhere at once, the Court's lights guttered and flared like a thing inhaling. Then came the sound: not shouting, not horns, but a vast wet shifting, the sound of a thousand silk garments moving at once, of a court rising from its cushions all together because it smelled something stolen.

"Run," Wren said.

Ivo ran.

The Underway swallowed them three steps past the vault door — a corridor of raw stone that had never known daylight, its walls sweating a cold that was older than cold. She clutched Juniper's soul against her chest with both arms, wrapped in the wool scarf she'd wound around it, and even through the wool she could feel it: a faint warmth, a pulse out of rhythm with her own heart, slow as a sleeper's breath. Twelve years dim. Still intact. Still *her*.

Wren ran ahead, and Ivo ran watching her, because Wren was coming apart.

It began at her fingertips. The ghost who had walked beside her for two hundred miles — solid enough to carry a pack, solid enough to argue, solid enough to press a hand to Ivo's shoulder when the dark pressed back — was thinning at the edges like smoke off a candle. Her left hand had gone translucent to the wrist. When she lifted it toward the wall to steady herself, Ivo could see the stone through her fingers, could see the veins of pale mineral light running through the rock like frost on a windowpane.

"Wren—"

"Don't." Wren didn't turn around. Her voice was steady, and that steadiness frightened Ivo more than screaming would have. "Don't waste breath on it. Save it for running."

"It's taking you back."

"The crown is reaching for what's its own." Wren said it flatly, the way a person names a weather. "Every mile we climb, it pulls harder. It knows I'm a piece of the ledger now. It wants me shelved."

Behind them, deeper in the mountain, something answered her words. Not a voice. A *drawing-in*, vast and patient, the sound the sea makes when it decides to take back a sandcastle. The walls shivered. Somewhere far below, a door of green glass slammed, and the slam traveled up through the stone and into their teeth.

They ran.

* * *

The Underway did not forgive runners. It had been carved by no one Ivo could name — ward-keepers' ancestors, perhaps, or the mountain itself dreaming — and it changed as they climbed, the way a dream changes. One stretch was a stair cut so steeply they climbed it on hands and knees, Ivo hugging the soul-scarl against her chest, feeling each step jolt through the little warm weight like a heartbeat being knocked loose. Another stretch widened into a gallery where the ceiling hung with stalactites like the ribs of some drowned cathedral, and the mineral-light ran so thick in the walls that Wren cast no shadow at all — because there was nothing to cast it on; because there was almost nothing left of her to cast.

At the top of the stair, Ivo stopped, bent double, lungs full of stone-dust and old air.

"We can't keep this pace," she gasped. "You're fading faster every hour."

"I'm aware."

"You said the crown couldn't reach you outside the Court. You *said*—"

"I said it would be harder." Wren stood at the mouth of the next passage, and the light behind her — such as it was — came through her shoulder like lamplight through a curtain. She turned, and Ivo saw that her face had gone fine-grained and faint, the way chalk goes under a thumb. But her eyes were still Wren's eyes: sharp, dry, unbearably knowing. "I also said I'd get you to the surface, and I don't intend to be wrong about that either. So we stop wasting time arguing about my hands and start moving."

Ivo wanted to shake her. She wanted to sit down in the dark and refuse. She did neither, because behind them, down the long throat of the stairway, the darkness made a sound like a held breath being slowly released, and both of them understood it: the Court was climbing too.

She shifted the bundle higher against her chest. Through the wool, faint as a moth's wing, the soul pulsed. And for a moment — just a moment — the pulse quickened, as if the sleeper inside had dreamed of running too.

"Hold on," Ivo whispered to it, to her, to the woman she'd betrayed and been betrayed by and loved across twelve years of silence. "Hold on, Juniper. I'm bringing you home."

Then she ran again.

* * *

They lost the pursuit at the Weeping Gallery, or thought they did.

It was a chamber where water fell from cracks in the ceiling in a hundred silver threads, pooling in black basins worn smooth by centuries, and the sound of it filled everything — covered their footsteps, covered their breathing, covered, Ivo prayed, the sound of the soul's small pulse. They waded the pools hip-deep, cold biting through her clothes to the marrow, and came out the far side into a passage so narrow they had to turn sideways, the rock pressing her shoulder blades, the soul-bundle scraping gently along the stone ahead of her like a child's hand being led.

It was in the narrow place that Wren finally spoke of it.

"You should know what happens," she said. Her voice came strangely in the tight stone, flattened, intimate, right against Ivo's ear though she was three body-lengths ahead. "If we make the surface. What has to happen."

"Don't."

"You'll want to argue. Better to argue now than up there, under the sky, with the Court coming out of the mountain after us."

Ivo squeezed sideways another step. The rock scraped her cheek. "I'm not arguing about this, Wren. I'm not having the conversation."

"You gave your word to Tam you'd bring him home. You gave your word to yourself you'd bring *her* home." A pause, filled only with dripping water somewhere behind them. "You've never once given your word to me. So let me give mine instead. If it comes to choosing — if it comes down to my piece or hers — you choose her. Swear it."

"No."

"*Ivo.*"

"I know what you are." The words tore out of her, ragged, echoing down the narrow throat of stone. "I've known since the market, since you said *you always hummed when you worked*, since you knew the way she took her tea, since—" Her voice cracked and she hated it, hated the crack, hated the tears that scalded in the cold. "You're the piece of her that stayed. The piece I kept without knowing. Every charm I ever sold, every knot I tied, I was carrying you, and I didn't — I didn't *know*—"

"And she went hollow for it." Wren's voice was gentle now, which was worse. "Twelve years in Ash's vault so you could run free. That's the shape of it. That's the debt. And I am the interest, Ivo. I am the part that got to live. Don't you dare spend her second chance keeping me alive out of guilt."

Ahead, the narrow place opened. Grey light — real light, starved and grey, but real — bled down from somewhere above, and Ivo saw Wren silhouetted in it, and saw, with a lurch, that she could see the rock of the doorway *through* Wren's whole body now, see it plain as a figure drawn on glass.

"How long?" Ivo whispered.

Wren looked at her own hand, turning it in the grey light, watching the stone show through. "Till morning," she said. "Perhaps a little past."

* * *

They rested because they had to, in a side-chamber off the main climb — a hollow like the inside of a cupped hand, floored with soft grey dust. Ivo built no fire; fire drew eyes, and eyes were what the Court had too many of already. Instead she sat with the soul-bundle in her lap and unwound the scarf just enough to look at it.

It lay in her palms like a coal gone out but not cold: a soft grey-gold shimmer, formless and yet not formless, because when she looked at it sidelong — the way you look at stars — she could almost see a shape inside it. A curve of shoulder. The suggestion of a jaw. And threaded through it, brighter than the rest, a single filament of light that pulsed in time with nothing else in the world.

"She's in there," Ivo said. It wasn't a question. She wasn't sure she'd meant to say it aloud.

Wren sat across from her, knees drawn up, more shadow than woman now. "All of her but me."

"All of her but me," Ivo corrected quietly. "That's what you are. You're the piece that stayed with *me*. Which means—" She stopped. Started again. "Which means when I give you back, I'm giving back the last of her I had. The twelve years' worth. Everything I kept."

"Yes."

"And then she wakes up remembering all of it. Remembering what I did. Remembering selling herself to buy my escape. Remembering—" Ivo's hands closed around the bundle, careful, careful, the way you hold a bird. "Remembering that I ran, and stayed run, and built a whole life out of not looking back."

Wren was quiet a long moment. The dust around them seemed to listen.

"Do you know what I remember?" she said at last. "I'm only a fragment. A splinter. But I remember the night you left. I remember her standing in the cottage doorway after you'd gone, holding the charm you'd left on the table — a luck-knot, badly tied, you were always careless with the finishing work — and she didn't cry. She untied it. Knot by knot. All night. Like she was taking apart the twelve years to see how they'd been made." Wren's faded face tilted. "And do you know what she found at the center? At the heart of the knot?"

Ivo couldn't speak.

"A thread of herself. Deliberately worked in. She'd put a piece of her own soul into every charm she ever taught you to make — hidden, deep, so you'd never run quite beyond her reach. So you'd never be quite alone." Wren smiled, and it was Juniper's smile, and it broke Ivo open. "You didn't steal a piece of her when you fled, Ivo. She *gave* it. She's been giving it your whole life. The only question left is whether you're brave enough to give it back."

Above them, faint through fathoms of stone, the mountain groaned.

* * *

They climbed through the small hours, and the Court climbed with them.

It was not always behind them. That was the horror of it. Twice the hunger came *through* the walls — Ivo felt it first as a wrongness in the stone, a softening, the rock going pliant as bread dough, and then faces pressing through, half-formed, the soul-drained ones, the Court's servants sent out like hounds. Their eyes were the worst: not empty, but *wanting*, wide and wet with appetite, mouths opening in faces that had forgotten mouths were for speaking.

The first time, Wren drove them back. She stepped between Ivo and the wall and *flared* — blazed white as a struck match, blazing with borrowed light, with the crown's own fire turned inside out — and the faces recoiled with a sound like steam, shrinking back into solid stone. When it was done, Wren swayed. Her right arm was gone to the elbow. Just gone, the sleeve of her hanging empty.

The second time, Ivo did it herself. Small charms. Humble craft. She had despised them once — knots and patience, hedge-magic, the work of women who hung for it — and now she knelt in the dark of the Underway with soul-drained faces pressing through the rock around her and tied a quenching-knot in her bootlace, whispering the old words Juniper had taught her, *what is bound is bounded, what is bounded is blind*, and the faces went blind, went still, sank back like drowned things settling. It cost her almost nothing. That was the joke of it, the bitter joke she laughed at, gasping, alone in the dark while Wren watched her with Juniper's eyes: the grand magic of the Court, the crown that ate stars, and here was bootlace and patience holding it at bay.

"What is bound is bounded," Wren murmured, when Ivo sagged back against the wall, shaking. "She'd be proud. She'd also say your finishing work is still sloppy."

Ivo laughed — a cracked, wet sound — and got up, and climbed.

* * *

Near dawn, by the measure of her aching body rather than any sky, the passage ended.

It ended at a door.

Not a door of the Court's making — no green glass, no silver filigree, none of that glittering starving beauty. This was an old door, an honest door, oak gone black with age, set into the stone in a frame of granite, and above it, cut deep and worn shallow by centuries of weathering that had somehow reached even here:

**SERLE STANDS. THE KEEPER WATCHES.**

Tam's family mark was beneath it — the ward-keeper's sigil, the circle crossed four ways — and someone had scratched newer words underneath, in a hurried hand Ivo recognized from his ledgers: *the wards were sold, not broken. ask the ledger.*

"He knew," Ivo breathed. "His father knew, even then. He left it here — a warning, for anyone who ever came back down."

"Or a message for his son," Wren said softly. "Fathers do that. Leave messages in the places they hope their children will never have to read them."

Beyond the door, Ivo could feel it: air moving. Real air, mountain air, cold and clean and smelling of rain and pine and the high bare world. The surface. An hour's climb, maybe less. Freedom laid out before them like a road.

And behind them, filling the passage from wall to wall, floor to ceiling, the darkness breathed.

It had stopped sending faces. It had stopped pretending to be anything but what it was. The whole length of the Underway below them had gone soft and dark and *attentive*, and Ivo understood, with the clarity of the nearly-dead, that the Court itself was climbing — that the mountain's hollow places were its throat, and it had decided, at last, simply to swallow.

The soul in her arms pulsed, fast now, fluttering. Frightened. Awake enough to be frightened.

Wren stood before the door with her one hand flat against the oak, and she was nearly transparent altogether now, the grey dawn-light of the surface showing through her like the moon through cloud. She looked back at Ivo, and her expression was calm, and tired, and absolutely certain.

"It won't fit through the door," she said. "The Court. It's too large, too hungry — it needs the wards broken to come up, and the wards are broken, but the breaking takes time to widen. We have until the summit. Until the ward-stone." She paused. "It will try to stop us there. And it will use the surest tool it has."

"Which is?"

Wren smiled that broken-open smile. "Me," she said. "Come on, Ivo Thackeray. Let's go stand under the sky one last time."

She pushed the door open onto the grey and rushing light of the coming dawn — and Ivo, clutching Juniper's soul to her chest, stepped through into the wind, and heard, far below, the mountain begin to rise.
