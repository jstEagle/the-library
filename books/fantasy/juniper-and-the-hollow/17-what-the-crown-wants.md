## 17. What the Crown Wants

The dark behind them had a sound now.

Ivo had heard hunger before — the low complaint of an empty belly, the rasp of a beggar's breath outside a shuttered window, the particular silence of a man deciding whether to steal. But she had never heard hunger with a voice like this: vast and patient and wet, rolling up the Underway behind them like a tide that had learned to want.

"Keep moving." Wren's hand found her wrist in the black. The ghost's fingers were almost warm now. Almost solid. That was the worst part — every mile they fled deeper from the Court, Wren grew more real, as though the crown's attention itself fed her, as though being hunted was a kind of meal. "There's a fork ahead. Old drainage cut. It climbs."

"How do you know?"

"I remember these tunnels." A pause, thin as paper. "I remember them because *she* walked them. Because some of what Juniper knew stayed where I stayed." The fingers tightened. "Left, Ivo. Take the left."

Ivo took the left. The soul-jar bumped against her ribs inside her coat, wrapped in three layers of charm-cloth, and each time it touched her it hummed — a small, dim note, like a struck bell heard through a wall. Juniper's soul. Dim but alive. She had carried it out of Ash's vault past two sleeping wardens and a door that screamed, and now she carried it through the mountain's bowels while the mountain itself began to wake.

Behind them, far back along the passage they'd come by, something answered the humming. A second note. Deeper. Older. The crown, calling to what it had lost.

"It knows we have it," Ivo said.

"It has always known." Wren's voice did something complicated — fear braided through relief. "That's what I've been trying to tell you. It isn't chasing us to punish us. It's chasing us because we're carrying part of it home."

Ivo stopped so abruptly that Tam, limping ten paces ahead with his father's ledger strapped across his chest like a bandolier, nearly walked into her back. His face was grey in the witchlight — the little knot-charm she'd made him burned green at his collar, guttering, spending itself faster than any charm should.

"Don't stop," he said. "Please don't stop. I can hear it."

"I need to hear *her*." Ivo turned to Wren. "What do you mean, home? The crown doesn't want the soul back. The crown *ate* the souls. Why would it—"

"Because it isn't eating them," Wren said. "It's holding them. There's a difference, and you of all people should understand it. You kept a piece of Juniper for twelve years without knowing. Did you eat her?"

The words went into Ivo like a hook going into a fish — no pain at first, just the sudden terrible knowledge of being caught.

"I didn't know," she said.

"No. You didn't." Wren's eyes were Juniper's eyes, and that had stopped being uncanny somewhere on the third day and become simply unbearable, the way a wound stops hurting and starts aching. "But you kept her anyway. You couldn't help it. Some things hold on to what they love even when they don't know they're doing it. The crown is like that. Only it loved everything. It loved the stars so much it swallowed them, and it loved the souls so much it took them all, and now it is a crown full of everything it loves and nothing it can feel, and it is *starving*, Ivo. Hunger isn't what it does. Hunger is what it *is*."

Tam made a small sound. When Ivo looked at him, he had the ledger open, one finger pressed flat against a page, reading by the dying green light.

"This is my father's hand," he said quietly. "This entry. Dated the night the wards fell. He wrote—" His voice caught, steadied. He was his father's son; he could read a ledger aloud even while the world ended. "*'The Crown cannot be broken. Corvane asked me to break it when he first understood what he'd made. I tried. We tried, the whole college, forty ward-keepers working nine nights. Every strike against it only taught it the shape of the blow.'*" He looked up. "Every strike taught it the shape of the blow. Do you understand? Everything we do to fight it, it learns. It's been learning since before either of you were born."

"Then what did your father write next?" Ivo asked. "There's always a next line. Ledger-men don't write laments. They write balances."

Tam looked down again, and Ivo watched the color leave his face entirely, watched him go the grey of old bone, and knew the answer before he said it.

"*'It cannot be destroyed,'*" he read. "*'It can only be unmade. And unmaking is not taking apart. Unmaking is giving back. Piece by piece, to its proper place, freely given — until there is nothing left of the theft for the crown to be. The stars must be returned to the sky. The souls must be returned to their bodies or their rest. And the piece that holds the whole together — the piece it keeps closest, the piece it wears at its heart — must be returned last, by the one who gave it, or the one who kept it.'*"

The tunnel was very quiet. Even the hunger behind them seemed to lean in and listen.

"The piece it wears at its heart," Ivo repeated.

Wren smiled. It was the saddest thing Ivo had ever seen, and she had seen her master's soul in a jar, and she had seen her own name written in a debt-book twelve years ago, and she had seen the sky go out.

"You already know what it is," Wren said gently. "You've known since the market. You felt it pull. That's why you ran so fast, and why you ran *toward* the mountain instead of away — you thought you were following Juniper's soul, but half of you knew better. What did the charm do, Ivo? The one that glowed when all the others died? Where did it point?"

Ivo's mouth was dry. "North."

"To the mountain. To the crown." Wren spread her hands — solid hands now, callused hands, hands Ivo had watched knot charms for six days without once letting herself wonder why the knots came so naturally to those fingers. "I'm the piece it wears at its heart. I'm the fragment of Juniper that stayed with you — the piece you kept without knowing, the piece the crown has been reaching for ever since it learned I existed. I'm not a ghost. I'm collateral. I'm the keystone. And the crown wants me back."

* * *

*(Wren)*

She had not meant to tell her like this. She had planned it a hundred times in the dark miles of the Underway — gently, by firelight, with the soul-jar open between them so that Juniper's other half could witness. She had rehearsed sentences. She had, in a moment of weakness she was not proud of, rehearsed arguments: *you owe me, you kept me, I am yours, give me back.*

But there was no firelight here, and no time, and the hunger was coming up the tunnel behind them like water finding its level, and so the truth came out the way truths do in the end — plainly, badly, all at once.

She watched Ivo take it. Watched the woman stand very still in the witchlight with her jaw set the way Juniper's used to set when the medicine tasted foul but the drinking was necessary. Twelve years ago Wren had been the part of Juniper that loved this girl enough to stay. Now she was the part that had to ask the girl to let go, and she found that the fragment of a soul is large enough to hold both of those things and be torn between them.

"I won't pretend I'm not afraid," Wren said. "I got solid. I got a body, or near enough. I got to taste bread again in Greyharrow — you bought it without knowing why I cried over it. I got six days of being a person. Do you know what that's worth, after years of being an echo?" Her voice cracked and she let it. "It's worth everything. And I would keep it. If there were any other way — any way at all — I would keep it, and I would hate myself quietly forever, and I think you would too, and I think that's exactly what the crown is counting on."

"What do you mean?"

"I mean it doesn't just want me back. It wants me *kept*. It wants you to hesitate. It wants you to stand on that peak with the whole sky waiting and find one reason — love, guilt, cowardice, it doesn't care which — to hold on to me one more day. Because a crown of night that is refused is still a crown. But a crown that is given back, freely, completely—" She shook her head. "Your magistrate built a trap out of your own heart, Ivo Thackeray. He's been building it for twelve years. The runaway wasn't an escape. It was ripening."

Ivo flinched as if struck. Behind her, Tam said, very quietly, "My father's ledger says the same. Page after page. *'Corvane does not want the crown served. He wants it completed.'*"

"And if we complete it instead?" Ivo's voice was rough. "If we give everything back — the stars, the souls, all of it — what happens to the Court? To Ash? To the thousands of people down there wearing their own hollowed faces?"

"They wake," Wren said. "Or they rest. Whatever was owed them comes back to them. That's what unmaking means. Not destruction — *return*." She stepped closer, close enough that Ivo could see the faint starlight-colored shimmer under her skin, the borrowed substance the crown's hunger had lent her. "And whatever was kept comes back too."

"I can't." It came out of Ivo like a stone dropping down a well — long fall, long silence, then the flat knock of bottom. "Twelve years ago someone sold a soul to save me and I ran and I let it happen and I told myself I didn't know. I'm not doing it again. I'm not standing in front of another sacrifice and calling it fate. Find me another way, Wren. Find me *any* other way."

"There isn't one." Wren said it softly, and hated how much it sounded like mercy. "You asked the question. This is the answer. The crown cannot be destroyed. It can only be emptied. And it cannot be emptied unless the last piece walks back into it willingly — and the last piece is me."

* * *

*(Ivo)*

They climbed.

The drainage cut rose in switchbacks, slick underfoot, the air growing colder and thinner and tasting of iron and old snow. Somewhere above them was the summit of Serle, and the broken ward-stone Tam spoke of like a man speaks of his father's grave. Somewhere below and behind, the hunger climbed too, patient as arithmetic.

Ivo counted her charms as she climbed, because counting was what she did instead of screaming. Three luck-knots, fraying. One salt-thread, spent. The green light at Tam's collar, down to its last ember. And in her coat, against her heart, the jar with its dim humming soul — and beneath the jar, sewn into the lining where she had carried it unknowing for twelve years, the small hard weight of the original charm. The one Juniper had made her. The one that held the piece. The keystone.

*Piece by piece,* the ledger said. *Freely given.*

Her hands remembered knots. That was the joke of it, the bitter joke she suspected she'd be laughing about for the rest of a short life: she had despised hedge-craft her whole career, the humble knotting and salting and patience of it, while Corvane taught her that power was taken and grandeur was real. And now the fate of the sky hung on whether a hedge-witch could tie one true knot — the knot of giving back, which no one had ever taught her because no one had ever needed it before. Every craft she knew was a keeping-craft. Hold the luck. Bind the fever. Keep the door shut. Nobody taught witches how to let go.

"Ivo." Tam had stopped at a bend where the cut opened onto a ledge, and beyond the ledge was night — true night, starless, the whole ruined sky laid out like an open mouth. Wind came off it, smelling of the peak. "Look."

Far below and behind, down the throat of the Underway, the darkness was moving. Not the ordinary dark of an unlit tunnel. A darkness with texture, with appetite, pouring upward through the mountain's veins toward them — slow, unhurried, certain. And threaded through it, points of cold light, hundreds of them, drifting like plankton in a rising tide: the stolen souls, the crown's hoard, coming up to see what had been taken from it.

Among them, near the front, moved a figure in a magistrate's coat, walking on the dark as if it were a road.

"He's not even hurrying," Tam whispered.

"He doesn't need to." Ivo's mouth tasted of copper. "We're going where he wants us. We've been going where he wants us since Greyharrow." She looked at the sky, at the enormous emptiness of it, and felt the choice stand up inside her at last, fully grown, unmistakable — not a door opening but a door that had been open all along, that she had been standing in the frame of for twelve years pretending it was a wall.

Give back the stars. Give back the souls. Give back Wren.

Or keep her. Keep the last bright piece, and rule a starless world beside a starving crown, comfortable and powerful and hollow, and call it survival.

"Wren," she said.

"I know." The ghost stood beside her at the ledge, solid in the wind, her hair moving like a living woman's. "I know what you're going to say. Don't say it yet. Say it at the top, where the sky can hear it. If I'm to be given back, I want to be given back properly — freely, completely, to its proper place." She smiled, and it was Juniper's smile, and it broke Ivo's heart cleanly, like a knot cut with a sharp knife. "Besides. We're not there yet. And I didn't get solid just to die in a drainage ditch."

Above them, the broken ward-stone waited on the peak, humming faintly with everything it had failed to hold. Below them, the tide of the taken climbed, and the magistrate rode its crest with his hands folded, patient as arithmetic.

Ivo put her hand on the charm in her coat lining — the keystone, the kept piece, the whole of it balanced on one knot she had never tied.

Then she started climbing.
