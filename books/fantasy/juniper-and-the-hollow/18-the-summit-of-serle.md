## 18. The Summit of Serle

The mountain had a throat, and they had climbed out of it.

Ivo felt it behind her still — the Underway's long dark swallowing their echoes, the Court's hunger waking mile by mile beneath their heels like a beast turning over in its sleep. Now there was only stone under her boots and wind off the peak of Serle, and above her, where the sky should have been, nothing at all.

No stars. No moon. Not even cloud — clouds would have been something, a texture, a lie with shape to it. This was a black so complete it seemed less like night than like an eye pressed shut over the world. She had lived twelve years under that blackness and told herself she was used to it. Standing here, at the top of everything, with the broken ward-stone rising before her like a snapped tooth, she understood she had never once been used to it. She had only learned not to look up.

The ward-stone stood taller than a man and twice as wide, or it had once. Now it was split from crown to root, the two halves leaning apart like lovers who had stopped speaking. Frost clung to the fracture in patterns that were not frost at all — Tam had gone pale when he saw them, back down the mountain where he could still see them, and said, *Those are ledger-marks. Someone wrote debts into the stone itself.* His father's handwriting, he'd meant. His father's sin, worn openly on the highest rock in Greyharrow for twelve years while everyone below looked up and wondered why the sky had gone out.

Tam wasn't with them now.

* * *

They had made him stay below. That was the argument, three hours ago, in the last sheltered hollow before the scree: Tam Vey, jaw set like his father's ledgers, insisting he was coming; Wren standing between him and the path with that terrible calm she wore like borrowed clothing; Ivo saying nothing, because saying nothing was the oldest skill she had.

"The Court knows your name," Wren had told him. "It has your father's signature in its books. You walk up that mountain and every soul in it turns to look at you."

"Then let them look."

"They'll take you," Ivo had said finally, and hated how much her voice sounded like Ash's when she said it — practical, cold, arithmetic dressed as mercy. "You're the son of the man who sold the wards. Do you know what you are to them? You're collateral. You're the receipt." She had gripped his shoulder then, felt him shaking under the wool of his coat, young and furious and so much braver than she had ever been at his age. "Stay alive. If we fail, someone has to be alive who knows what happened here. That's not cowardice, Tam. That's the whole job."

He had given her his father's knife — a ward-keeper's knife, its blade scored with tiny true names — and refused to say goodbye, which was its own kind of honesty. Then he had sat down against the rock with the stolen ledger-pages spread across his knees, and begun, methodically, terribly, to memorize them.

Now, alone at the summit with Wren, Ivo unwrapped the bundle she had carried through the dark for what felt like a year and had been four days.

Juniper's soul lay in her hands like a coal that had forgotten fire.

It was small. That was the thing no one warned you about souls — how small they were when you finally held one. All those years of Juniper filling a cottage with her voice, her laugh, her terrible singing, her hands steady over a charm-knot while Ivo's shook; all of it, and here it was, a dim grey-gold light scarcely bigger than a sparrow's egg, pulsing slow as a sleeping heart. It should have frightened her, how little of a person there was. Instead it made her furious — furious at the Court, at Ash, at the crown that ate stars, and most of all at herself, twelve years running from a debt this size because she had mistaken it for a shame instead of what it was.

A gift. Juniper had sold herself to buy Ivo's escape. Every step of Ivo's flight, every border crossed, every year of safe anonymous charm-selling in Greyharrow — purchased. Paid for in advance by the woman she had betrayed.

"I keep expecting it to be bigger," Wren said beside her.

Ivo didn't jump anymore. She'd stopped jumping at Wren somewhere on the third day of the Underway, which was either trust or surrender; she hadn't decided. "So do I."

"It isn't smaller, though." Wren crouched, and the starless dark did something strange around her — bent toward her, the way water bends toward a drain. She had grown more solid every mile since they'd entered the mountain, as if proximity to the crown fed her, and now in the lightless air she cast a faint grey luminescence of her own, like the memory of moonlight. Her face was Juniper's face, younger, unlined, and her voice was Juniper's voice, and every hour Ivo spent beside her was a small quiet torture she had stopped pretending she didn't feel. "It's just folded. Souls fold when they're frightened. Like moths."

"You would know."

"Yes," Wren said simply. "I would."

The wind came up the peak in long slow breaths, carrying the smell of deep stone and old ice and, underneath, faintly, sweetly, wrong — the smell of the market below, of honey and rot, of the twilight stalls where souls changed hands like copper coins. The Court was climbing. She couldn't see it yet, but she could feel it in the mountain's bones, a pressure rising through the rock like sap through a tree. They had perhaps an hour. Perhaps less.

She knelt at the base of the broken ward-stone and began to work.

* * *

It was humble work. That was the joke of it, the bitter punchline of her whole life: after everything — the Court, the crown, the vault, the theft, the flight through screaming dark with alarms chasing them like hounds — it came down to knots.

Her fingers knew the patterns without her. The first knot was a patience-knot, the kind she sold dockworkers for two coppers, the kind that held a fraying rope one more season. She tied it in the air above the ward-stone's fractured heart, using thread pulled from her own hem, and spoke the words Juniper had taught her when she was nine years old and impatient and certain she deserved grander magic than string:

*"Hold what is breaking. Ask nothing of what is broken."*

The knot took. A thin gold line of light ran along the ward-stone's crack, stitching, and the mountain shuddered — not with anger, Ivo thought, but with relief, the way a body shudders when a splinter comes out.

"Again," Wren said. "Faster. It feels you."

The second knot was a truth-knot. Harder. Truth-knots required you to mean what you said, and Ivo had spent twelve years practicing the opposite. She tied it anyway, in red thread this time, salvaged from the market stall where she'd played bait and nearly lost herself to the seduction of shelves full of bottled power. She tied it and said, aloud, to the stone, to the dark, to whatever of Juniper was listening inside the dim coal in her pocket:

"My name is Ivo Thackeray. Twelve years ago I betrayed my master to Corvane Ash. I told myself it was survival. It was ambition. I wanted what he had and I took the shortcut he offered and I let her pay the toll." Her voice cracked on the last of it and she let it crack, because a truth-knot with a smooth voice in it was worth nothing. "That's the truth. Take it. Use it."

The red thread flared and sank into the stone beside the gold, and high above — impossibly high, higher than any star had hung — something flickered. One point of light. Faint as a dying ember, but there.

Wren made a sound Ivo had never heard from her: a small, wounded gasp, both hands flying to her mouth.

"That's—" Wren lowered her hands slowly. "I remember that one. That's the Shepherd's Eye. Juniper used to point it out to you when you complained about the walk home. You said it looked like it was following you."

"It was," Ivo said, throat tight. "Everything was. I just never looked."

"Keep going."

The third knot was the hard one. The giving-back knot. There was no pattern for it in any book Juniper had owned, no charm-seller in any market who sold it, because no one bought it and no one taught it. It had come to Ivo in the vault, in the moment she'd understood what Juniper had done, and it had been growing in her since like a seed in wet ground: the knowledge that the crown of night could not be fought, could not be broken, could only be *unmade* — returned, piece by piece, to everything it had taken. And that the last piece, the piece that had walked beside her for four days wearing Juniper's face —

"Ivo." Wren's voice, very quiet. "You're stalling."

"I'm tying a knot."

"You're stalling with string." Wren sat down across from her, cross-legged, solid enough now that her weight pressed real impressions into the frost. In the stitched light of the ward-stone her face was calm and unbearably dear. "We agreed on the mountain road. We agreed in the Underway. We agreed in the vault, when you cried on me, which you'll deny later, and I'll allow it because I'm generous."

"This isn't funny."

"No," Wren said. "It isn't. But it's simple, and simple things frighten clever people worst." She held out her hand, palm up, and in it — where had she gotten it? From herself, from the substance of her own fading ghost-flesh — she held a single length of thread, silver-grey, glowing faintly. "Tie it. Before the Court gets here and makes it complicated."

Ivo's hands would not move.

Here was the shape of it, plain as a knot diagram: give Wren back, and Juniper woke whole — remembering everything, including the fragment that had kept Ivo company, including the sacrifice, including the betrayal. Refuse, and she kept Wren — kept the voice, the company, the second chance, the do-over she'd wanted for twelve years — and Juniper stayed a dim coal on a shelf forever, and the stars stayed dead, and the Court went on eating.

Every debt comes due. She had known that since the market. She just hadn't known they'd all arrive at once, on a frozen peak, with the mountain rising against her.

Because the mountain *was* rising. She felt it now unmistakably — a groan through the bedrock, deep as a whale-song, and far below them the slope of Serle began to move. Not avalanche. Worse. The twilight market's lights were climbing, hundreds of them, thousands, pouring up the switchback trails like a river of drowned lanterns, and among them moved shapes — the soul-drained courtiers in their glittering rags, the hollow-eyed brokers, and behind them all something vast that wore the mountain itself like a cloak.

And at the head of the climb, unhurried, immaculate, a figure in magistrate's grey with a crown-shaped shadow where his shadow should have been.

Corvane Ash.

"He brought the whole Court," Ivo breathed.

"He brought the whole Court," Wren agreed, "because you have something it can't replace, and it's terrified. Look at them, Ivo. Really look." Her grey eyes — Juniper's eyes — were fierce now. "A starving thing always runs fastest when the meal gets up off the plate."

Ivo looked. Ash climbing at the head of that glittering horde, patient as winter, and she understood with sudden sick clarity that he wasn't coming to fight her. He was coming to *collect*. The crown had chosen her long ago — he'd told her that in the vault, told her her runaway had been permitted, that she'd been left to ripen — and everything since, the escape, the chase, even this summit, might be the last turn of a trap twelve years in the closing.

Or it might not. Or it might be both at once, because Ash had never taught her lies. He'd taught her something more useful and more poisonous: that power is simply taken, and the only question is whether you're the hand or the throat.

She looked down at Wren's outstretched palm. At the silver thread.

"I can't," Ivo said. "Not yet. If I tie it now, before the end, and Ash takes me — if the crown takes me — then Juniper wakes into a world where it wins. She'd wake knowing. She'd wake *remembering*, Wren, everything, including losing. I won't hand her that. Not as a gift. Not after—" Her voice failed and she hated it and pushed through. "After everything I already handed her."

For a long moment Wren said nothing. The lights of the Court climbed. The single star overhead — the Shepherd's Eye — burned its faint stubborn ember, and Ivo thought: *one. We got one back. Let that be enough to start.*

Then Wren smiled, and it was so exactly Juniper's smile, the one that meant *you've finally said something true, you impossible girl*, that Ivo's chest hurt.

"All right," Wren said. "Not yet. But soon, and you don't get to decide alone — that's the whole lesson, isn't it? Nobody decides alone. That's what you never learned." She closed her fingers over the silver thread and tucked it away inside herself, somewhere no vault could reach. "Finish your knots, witch. I'll watch the stairs."

There were no stairs. There was only the mountain, and the lights coming up it, and Ash's grey figure steadily closer, close enough now that Ivo could see the crown-light reflected in his eyes like distant windows burning.

She turned back to the ward-stone and reached for her thread.

The fourth knot was the last one she knew how to tie, and it was the one Juniper had never taught her, because Juniper had never known it either. Ivo understood it suddenly, wholly, the way you understand a thing at the exact moment you can no longer afford to misunderstand it: the final knot was not tied in thread at all. It was tied in magic — hers, all of it, the talent she'd hoarded and hidden and run with for twelve years, given away freely into the stone, into the sky, into the returning dark, until the crown had nothing left of her to want.

Power is simply taken, Ash had taught her.

He had never once considered that it could be *given*.

Below her, the first rank of the Hollow Court crested the ridge, lantern-souls swinging, and Ash lifted one white hand, and the mountain's hunger opened like a mouth.

Ivo Thackeray began to untie herself.
