## 19. Juniper and the Hollow

The ward-stone at the summit of Serle was older than the mountain's name. It had been a single pillar once, tall as three men, carved with the first wards ever laid on this border — Tam had told her that on the climb, his voice thin with cold and grief — and now it lay in pieces like a broken tooth, its runes gouged out by hands that had wanted the sky dark.

Ivo knelt among the shards with Juniper's soul cupped against her chest.

It was lighter than she had imagined. All those years of imagining — the weight of a stolen thing, the drag of guilt made solid — and it weighed no more than a sparrow's heart. It pulsed faintly through the cloth she'd wrapped it in, warm as bread from an oven, and every pulse said the same wordless word: *here, here, here.*

Above her, the sky was a wound.

No stars. No moon. Just black, and beneath the black a pressure like the inside of a held breath, and beneath *that* — rising through the broken stone, through the scree, through the soles of her boots — the Court itself, climbing. She could feel it the way you feel a flood coming: not water but hunger, a tide of the soul-drained pouring up the mountain's throat, wearing their borrowed finery, their faces slack with that terrible glittering contentment.

Wren stood at the edge of the shattered stone, looking down into the dark.

"They're coming," she said. Her voice was Juniper's voice. It always had been; Ivo had simply refused to hear it. "All of them. He's coming last."

"Ash."

"He wants to watch." Wren turned, and in the starless light her face was half-solid, half-suggestion, like a figure seen through frosted glass. The closer they'd climbed to the crown's hunger, the more real she'd become — and the more afraid. "Ivo. When it starts, don't listen to him. He doesn't lie, exactly. That's what makes him dangerous. He tells you true things in the shape of traps."

Ivo's hands were shaking. She looked down at them — a charm-seller's hands, knot-scarred, ink-stained, ordinary — and thought of all the years she'd despised them for being small.

"Tell me how to unmake it," she said. "Plainly. Like I'm a customer who's slow."

Wren almost smiled. "You return what it took. Piece by piece. Every soul it swallowed, given back freely — freely, mind, or it's just theft moving the other way. And the piece it wants most—" her voice caught, "—the piece it's been starving for. Me."

"No."

"Ivo—"

"I said *no.* We find another way. There's always another way. Juniper found one for me, didn't she? Twelve years ago, when I deserved nothing—"

"And look what it cost." Wren's voice was gentle, which was worse than sharp. "Look what it cost *her.* You can't buy your way out of a debt by refusing to count it."

The mountain shuddered. Far below, light bloomed in the Underway's mouth — not torchlight, nothing so honest. A cold silver radiance, crown-colored, climbing.

Ivo wrapped the soul tighter against her heart and began to work.

* * *

She did it the way Juniper had taught her, twenty years ago, in a cottage that smelled of rosemary and woodsmoke: small charms, humble charms, knots and patience. No grand working. No borrowed power. She laid the soul on the flat face of the largest ward-stone shard and began to build around it a cradle of returning — thread from her sleeve, salt from her pocket, a hairpin bent into a circle, each element bound with words so old they weren't words anymore, just shapes the mouth remembered.

The cradle took the soul's pulse and answered it. *Here,* said the soul. *Here,* said the stones. The broken ward-runes began, very faintly, to glow — a color Ivo had never seen in them before because she had never seen them whole. Starlight-color. Remembered color.

And the sky noticed.

She felt it happen — felt the darkness above lean down like a great animal scenting blood, felt the crown's attention swing toward the mountaintop with the slow inevitability of a door swinging shut. The pressure redoubled. The silver light below brightened.

Then he stepped out of it.

Corvane Ash came up the last stretch of the mountain path unhurried, as though he were strolling the colonnade of his own courthouse. His robes were still magistrate-black, but they moved wrong now, hemmed in shadow that poured off him like smoke off wet coal. Above his brow, where no crown should sit, the Crown of Night burned — a circlet of absolute dark, edged in the stolen light of ten thousand devoured stars.

He stopped ten paces away. Behind him, filling the path from summit to treeline, stood the Court — hundreds of them, thousands, the soul-drained in their glittering rags, watching with those empty, shining eyes.

"Ivo Thackeray." Ash's voice hadn't changed. That was the horror of it. It was still the voice from the schoolroom, from the courtroom, patient and reasonable and warm as a hand on your shoulder right before it closed. "You've brought it home after all. I told them you would. Twelve years ago, when they wanted to hunt you down — I said, let her run. Let her carry it. A seed grows best left alone."

"You talk too much," Ivo said. Her voice only shook a little. "You always did."

He smiled. "And you've learned nothing. Look at you. Kneeling in the dirt with thread and salt. Do you know what I could have taught you, if you'd stayed? What I could give you *now?*" He spread his hands, and the shadow pooled in his palms like liquid. "The crown is not a thing, girl. It's an appetite. And appetites can be *fed.*"

"I'm not hungry," Ivo said. "That's the difference between us."

She snapped the first knot.

* * *

It went the way no duel in any story goes — no thunderbolts, no circling, no speeches between blows. It went fast and ugly and close.

Ash struck first, of course. He was never a man to wait. The shadow leapt from his hands like a thrown net, and Ivo rolled behind the ward-stone shard and felt it hit where she'd been: the stone didn't shatter, it *forgot itself*, crumbling into gravel that had never been part of anything.

Crown-magic. Not destruction — unmaking. Taking things back to before they were.

Her counter-charm was a luck-knot. The same one she'd sold to a dockworker a lifetime ago, three weeks ago, in Greyharrow. She flung it and the knot unraveled midair into a snarl of mischance — Ash's next strike went wide, his foot skidded on scree, and for one heartbeat the most powerful man under the starless sky stumbled like a drunkard.

"That's it?" he laughed, recovering. "Thread and superstition?"

"It's held me together for twelve years," Ivo said, and threw a second knot, and a third.

They were small things, her charms. A binding that caught his ankle for two seconds. A quieting-charm that stole the sound of his next word so the command died shapeless in his throat. A warmth-charm pressed into a stone and hurled, which did nothing at all except make him flinch, because he had forgotten, somewhere in the long dark of his rising, what warmth was.

And every small thing cost her. She could feel her own magic draining with each working, a well lowering, and she knew he could feel it too — knew he was letting her spend herself, patient as winter, waiting for the moment she stood empty-handed.

"You feel it," Ash said, walking forward through her knots like a man wading through shallow water. "That little emptiness. That's what power *is*, Ivo. Spending. Everyone who ever held it learned that lesson eventually. The crown learned it first of all — do you know why it ate the stars?" He gestured upward, and the darkness above seemed to lean closer. "Because it was *starving*. It took and took and the taking hollowed it, and now it cannot stop. That is the lesson, child. Power is a wound that eats its owner. I merely chose to be eaten last."

"Then we learned different lessons," Ivo said.

Her hands were nearly empty. One knot left in her sleeve, and the cradle at her feet glowing brighter, the soul pulsing faster — *here, here, here* — and Wren standing at the edge of the stone with her half-made hands open at her sides, waiting.

Ash raised both palms, and the shadow gathered in him like a wave gathering, all the Court's hunger funneling up through his body, and Ivo understood with perfect clarity that the next thing he threw would not miss.

So she stopped defending.

She reached down — not for her last knot, not for any charm at all — and laid both bare hands on the cradle, on the returning-work, on the oldest craft she knew, and she did the one thing no holder of power ever does.

She gave it away.

* * *

Not spent. *Given.* Freely, the way Wren had said, the way the unmaking demanded — every scrap of magic left in her, the well itself, the knack, the knack's mother-tongue, the thing that had lived in her since before Juniper found her thieving on the docks — she opened her hands and poured it out. Into the cradle. Into the broken ward-stones. Into the ground of Serle, which had been robbed of its wards, its stars, its sleep.

It didn't hurt. That surprised her most. It felt like setting down a pack carried so long she'd forgotten it was heavy. It felt like the exhale at the end of a held breath twelve years long.

Ash's blow broke over her like surf over a rock already underwater — and found nothing to take. Crown-magic feeds on power, on appetite, on the bright burning thing in a witch's chest, and Ivo Thackeray stood in the ruin of it with nothing left to burn, and the shadow passed through her and scattered like smoke through an open window.

"What—" For the first time in twenty years, Corvane Ash sounded uncertain. "What have you done?"

"Nothing," Ivo said. Her voice came out strange and light and clean. "That's rather the point."

The cradle blazed. The ward-stone shards drank her gift and remembered themselves, rune by rune, and light ran along the broken lines of the old wards like dawn running along a horizon — and above them, impossibly, the darkness *flinched*.

The crown was starving. Ash had said it himself. And here was a feast offered freely, given without taking, the one meal an appetite cannot digest — because you cannot consume a gift. You can only be changed by it.

The Court behind Ash made a sound. Thousands of them, all at once — not a scream, not a moan. A sigh. The sound of the hollow remembering fullness. Some of them swayed. Some of them fell to their knees. And the silver light of the crown guttered, and guttered again, like a lamp running dry.

"No," Ash said. He clutched at the air above his head, at the circlet of dark, and the crown clung to him and screamed — actually screamed, a thin starved keening that came from everywhere. "It chose *you*. It waited for *you*. You were meant to—"

"I know." Ivo got to her feet, empty-handed, ordinary, lighter than she had ever been. "That's why it ends tonight. You built a cage out of wanting, Corvane. I'm just not going to want anymore."

He came at her then with his bare hands, which was somehow the saddest thing of all — the great broker of souls, reduced to fists — and she didn't fight him. She simply stepped aside, and the ground where he lunged gave way, because the mountain had taken her side now, and Ash went down hard among the ward-stone shards, and the crown's keening rose to a pitch that cracked stones up and down the slope.

*Ivo.* Wren's voice, inside her ears and outside them. *Now. While it's weak. Give it back — all of it —*

Ivo turned to the cradle.

Juniper's soul pulsed against the cloth, bright now, fed on given light. And beside it stood Wren — more solid than Ivo had ever seen her, solid enough that the starless wind moved her hair, solid enough that her eyes, Juniper's eyes, were wet.

"Ivo." Wren smiled, and it was the smile from the cottage, from the mornings, from before. "Thank you. For carrying me. Both times."

"Wren—"

"Don't make it heavy." She stepped toward the cradle. "Juniper gave a piece of herself to keep you safe. I was that piece. And now I get to choose what I am — and I choose to go *home.*"

She reached out her half-real hands and lifted the soul from the cloth, and it fit against her chest the way a key fits a lock, the way a word fits a sentence, the way a daughter fits a doorway she thought she'd lost —

— and the crown shrieked, and the sky split, and the first star in twelve years kindled above Mount Serle like an eye opening.

Ash was screaming something. The Court was falling like puppets with cut strings. None of it mattered. Ivo dropped to her knees beside Wren, beside the brightening, blending, becoming shape of her, and held whatever parts of her she could still hold, and Wren — Juniper — the piece and the whole — whispered with the last of the separate voice:

"*Here,*" she said. "*I'm here.*"

And the night filled with falling light.
