## 20. The Stars Return

The crown did not shatter all at once.

It came apart the way a knot comes apart — patiently, thread by thread, each strand remembering that it had once been something else. Ivo knelt on the broken ward-stone at the summit of Mount Serle with her hands full of night, and she gave it back.

That was the whole of the working. That was everything her master had ever taught her, distilled to its last and smallest truth: *magic is not taken, Ivo-girl. It is given. The taking is only what happens when someone forgets.*

She had forgotten for twelve years. She would not forget now.

Piece by piece, the crown of night unmade itself through her fingers. A fragment of stolen sky here — a fisherman's soul from Greyharrow harbor, dim and salt-crusted, released upward like a bubble rising through black water. A fragment there — a child's laughter, a grandmother's patience, a soldier's courage worn smooth by centuries in the dark. Each one passed through Ivo's chest on its way home, and each one took a little of her with it. Not her blood. Not her breath. Her magic — the deep cold well of it she had carried since she was six years old and first tied a knot that held.

She felt it going the way one feels warmth leaving a house in winter: gradually, then all at once.

Wren stood beside her at the edge of the ward-stone, more solid than Ivo had ever seen her, lit from within by a light that was not starlight because there were still no stars. The crown's hunger reached for her across the mountain's shoulder — Ivo could feel it, a vast blind mouth opening beneath them, the Hollow Court rising through the rock like floodwater through a cellar floor. It wanted Wren back. Wren was the piece that had never been paid.

"No," Ivo said, to the mountain, to the Court, to the hunger itself. "This one is mine to give."

* * *

Below them, Ash climbed.

He did not climb the way men climb — hand over hand, foot over foot, grunting against the grade. He rose the way smoke rises, lifted on a column of borrowed souls, his magistrate's grey robes streaming behind him like a banner of ash. The crown's power poured off him in waves, and where it touched the stone, the stone went glassy and dark.

"You cannot," he called up, and his voice was every voice that had ever told Ivo she was nothing without power. Her father's voice. Her own voice, twelve years ago, in Juniper's cottage, reaching for the forbidden shelf. "The crown cannot be broken, girl. I told you. It can only be *worn*. Come down. Take your place. You were always meant for this — do you think I let you run? Do you think twelve years of hiding was anything but ripening?"

Ivo looked at him over her shoulder, and what she felt was not fear. It was pity, vast and cold as the space between stars.

"You taught me that power is taken," she said. "You never once asked where it comes from."

She turned back to the sky and gave away another piece.

Ash screamed — not with his mouth but with the crown, a sound like a thousand souls being asked to hold their breath forever. He hurled the Court's hunger at her, and it broke over the ward-stone like a wave over a sea-stack, and Ivo held, because she was not holding alone. Every soul she freed passed through her and left a little of its strength behind, the way a guest leaves warmth in a chair. The humble arithmetic of charms. Knots and patience. Juniper's whole grammar, spoken fluently at last.

Tam was somewhere below, in the dark of the Underway, his wrists probably bound, his father's ledger probably confiscated — and still his sabotage held. The soul-ledgers burned crooked in the vaults beneath her feet; she could feel the Court's accounting failing, debts coming due with no record to collect them against. He had bought her this window with his freedom. She would not waste it.

"Give it back," Ash snarled, closer now, close enough that she could smell the crown on him — cold iron and old snow and the inside of a locked room. "Give me what's mine—"

"It was never yours." Ivo's voice cracked on it, and she found she didn't care. "None of it was ever yours. That's the lesson, Corvane. That's the whole lesson. You just never learned to read."

She opened her hands.

The last piece of the crown — the heart of it, the piece that had been Juniper's soul before Juniper sold it, the piece that had lived twelve years folded into a luck-knot in Ivo's pocket, wearing the shape of a girl called Wren — rose between her palms like a coal lifted from a fire.

Wren looked at it. Then at Ivo.

"I remember the river," Wren said. Her voice was steady, and it was Juniper's voice, and it was entirely her own. "The day you ran. I remember you crying so hard you couldn't tie the boat-line, and I tied it for you. I've been tying your knots for twelve years, Ivo Thackeray. Time you learned to do it yourself."

"Wren—"

"Don't." The ghost smiled, and it was the saddest thing Ivo had ever seen, and the most alive. "Don't make it a goodbye. It's a *returning*. There's a difference. My master taught me that." A pause. "Both of my masters."

Then Wren stepped into the light.

There was no sound. That was the terrible part — Ivo had expected thunder, had braced for the mountain to crack open, and instead the returning was silent as snowfall. The fragment of soul settled into the greater soul the way water settles into water, and for one heartbeat the light above the ward-stone held the shape of a woman — tall, grey-streaked, hands scarred from forty years of hedge-craft — and then the shape let go of itself, and the crown of night let go of everything.

It shattered.

Not loudly. Like ice leaving a spring pond. Like a knot slipping loose after years of tension. The Crown of Night came apart into a thousand thousand fragments of returned sky, and they went *up*, all of them, streaming past Ivo's face with a wind that smelled of rain on hot stone, and the mountain drank the darkness out of itself, and the sky—

The sky remembered.

The first star came back above the eastern ridge, small and astonished. Then three more. Then a handful scattered like spilled salt. Then the whole slow tide of them, constellation by constellation, the Weaver and the Wolf and the Lantern-Bearer and all the patient names the ward-keepers had once stitched into the border stones — the heavens reigniting like a city lighting its lamps at dusk, until the black dome overhead was so thick with stars it seemed it must be heavy, seemed it might come down low enough to touch.

Ivo fell to her knees on the ward-stone and wept.

She wept for the stars, which she had not known she was grieving until they returned. She wept for Tam, bleeding somewhere in the dark below. She wept for Wren, who had been a ghost and a stranger and a friend and had been, all along, the truest part of the woman Ivo had betrayed. And she wept because her hands were empty — truly empty, for the first time since childhood. The well was dry. Whatever she had been, she was no longer it. The last charm-seller of Serle had spent herself entirely, and the sky was full of stars, and neither fact could be undone.

Behind her, boots on stone. Slow ones. Human ones.

Ash stood at the edge of the summit, grey-faced, ordinary, the crown gone from him like a fever breaking. He looked smaller than she had ever seen him — a tired man in ruined robes, sixty years old, alone. His mouth worked. No power came out of it.

"The souls," he whispered. "They're — I can feel them, they're all—"

And then he looked down at his hands, and Ivo watched him understand what was climbing the mountain toward him, and she turned away. That reckoning was not hers to give. It had been owed a long time, by other creditors.

* * *

Juniper woke in her arms.

One moment Ivo was alone on the summit, kneeling in the starlight with her empty hands, and the next there was weight against her — solid, breathing, smelling of woodsmoke and rue and the particular soap Juniper had made from goat's milk and nettle ash, the soap Ivo had not smelled in twelve years and would have known blindfolded in a crowd of thousands.

"Oof," said Juniper. "Someone catch my — ah. Hello, trouble."

Ivo could not speak. Her throat had closed around twelve years.

Juniper sat up in her arms — older, greyer, her face lined in ways Ivo's memory had refused to record, but her eyes the same sharp hazel, and looking at Ivo now with an expression that contained, impossibly, everything. The sale. The vault. The years on the shelf, dim and aware. The fragment that had slipped free and followed her runaway apprentice north, wearing borrowed flesh, learning to love her twice.

"You kept my knot," Juniper said. Her voice was rough. "Twelve years in your pocket. You absolute sentimental fool."

"You sold yourself," Ivo managed. "For me. You let me believe I'd — that I'd betrayed you, that you'd been taken because of what I did, and the whole time—"

"The whole time you were carrying me around like a lucky coin." Juniper laughed, and it broke in the middle, and became something else. "Ivo. Look at me. I chose it. That's the part you have to hear. I saw what Ash would do to you if I didn't pay, and I paid, and I would pay again, and hating yourself for it for twelve years was the worst interest charge imaginable. Do you hear me? The debt was mine. The guilt was never yours to keep."

"But Wren—"

"Wren was me." Juniper's face did something complicated — grief and pride braided tight together, exactly as the beat required, exactly as the night required. "Wren was the part of me that loved you best. Of course she stayed with you. Of course she grew. Souls are not coins, Ivo-girl. They're seeds. Give one a pocket and twelve years and someone to tend it—" Her voice failed. She pressed her scarred hand flat against Ivo's sternum, over her heart, the way she used to when checking a pupil's grounding before a working. "She knew your name before I did. In the end, I think she knew it better."

"She said it wasn't a goodbye."

"No." Juniper looked up. The stars wheeled above them, thick and burning, brighter over Serle than anywhere else in the world, as if the sky were leaning close to see. "It wasn't. Nothing given is ever gone. That's the first lesson. I taught you that when you were nine and you thought I was talking about charms." She exhaled, long and shaking, a woman re-entering her body after a very long errand. "I have been on a shelf, Ivo. In the dark. Aware. And even from there, I could feel you coming. Every mile. Every stubborn, guilty, unforgiving mile. You never once considered giving up. It was the most infuriating thing I have ever experienced, and I once watched you attempt to hex a tax collector."

Ivo laughed — a wet, wrecked sound — and pulled her master against her, and held on while the mountain cooled around them and the stars turned overhead like a wheel finally allowed to spin again.

Far below, faintly, the Hollow Court was exhaling its last. The glittering hungry halls were going dark hall by hall, the soul-drained drifting free like leaves off a stripped tree, and somewhere in that dark a man who had taught a generation that power is simply taken was learning, at last and thoroughly, what comes due.

But that was below. Here was the summit, and the stars, and the two of them.

"I can't work anymore," Ivo said into Juniper's shoulder. "It's all gone. Everything I gave — it went with the crown. I'm just — a woman on a mountain."

Juniper pulled back and studied her, this way and that, like appraising a knot for hidden strength. Then she smiled, and it was the old smile, the cottage smile, the one that had preceded every disaster and every lesson of Ivo's childhood.

"Mm," she said. "Well. I happen to know a shop in Greyharrow with a vacant lease. Terrible location. Wonderful landlady." She got to her feet, joints cracking, twelve years complaining in every one of them, and offered Ivo her hand. "Knots and patience, Ivo-girl. That's all a charm ever was. Come along. We're a long way from the road, and I have been on a shelf for twelve years, and the first thing I intend to do with my freedom is complain about the walk."

Ivo took her hand, and stood, and turned once to look back at the peak — at the broken ward-stone, already gathering frost, already becoming just a stone.

Below them, torchlight was climbing the mountain road. Many torches, moving fast, and ahead of them — limping, half-carried, unmistakably alive — a figure with his father's ledger under one arm, waving both hands over his head and shouting something about wardens, and warrants, and a magistrate who had better be brought to account before dawn.

Juniper sighed. "Is that the ward-keeper's boy?"

"That's Tam."

"He looks terrible."

"He's had quite a week."

"Well." Juniper started down the slope, pulling Ivo after her by the hand, into the starlight and the long descent toward whatever came next. "So have we all. Come on, trouble. Let's go see what the world wants to charge us for it."
