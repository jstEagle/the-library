## 21. Ash's Reckoning

The mountain was still breathing when Tam Vey climbed back down into it.

Not the way a mountain breathes — no wind through the passes, no slow exchange of night air and day. This was something older and hungrier, the long exhale of the Hollow Court dying beneath Serle's roots. It came up through the stone in waves, warm and foul, like the breath of a beast that had gorged itself for years and was now, at last, being made to give it all back.

Tam felt it against his face as he descended the Underway with a lantern in one hand and his father's ledger in the other. The book had not left his grip since the summit. His knuckles were white around it. He had carried it up the mountain to break the Court's ledgers, and he had carried it down again because — because he did not yet know what else to do with it. A man does not simply set down the thing that has ruined him. He has to learn how.

The passage walls wept. That was new. All the way down from the ward-stone chamber, water beaded on the black rock and ran in thin bright threads, pooling in the wheel-ruts of the old mine road. For twelve years the Underway had been dry as bone; the Court drank everything, even damp. Now the mountain was wringing itself out, and every drop that fell sounded, in the dark, like a small bell.

Behind him, somewhere far above, Ivo Thackeray was holding a dying witch in her arms while the sky remembered how to be a sky. Tam tried not to think about that. He had work below, and work was the only shape his grief could take without cutting him.

* * *

He found the first of them at the second landing, where the market stairs widened into the vaulted hall the Court had called the Exchange.

The lantern showed him a figure standing in the middle of the floor, very still, facing the great iron door of Ash's counting-house. Not standing — *waiting*. The posture of someone who has been kept waiting a long time and has decided to stop minding.

"Hello," Tam said, because his father had raised him to speak to people, even strange ones, even dead ones.

The figure turned. It was a woman, or had been. Her skin had the grey translucence of paper held too long against a window, and where her eyes should have been there was only a soft dark shine, like the inside of a mussel shell. She wore the clothes of a dockworker — Greyharrow cut, salt-stiff canvas, a luck-knot frayed at the wrist. Tam recognized the knot's pattern. Ivo's work. He'd seen a hundred of them in her stall at the twilight market.

"You're free," he said. "Do you understand? The crown is broken. You can go."

The woman looked at him. Or rather, she looked through him, at the iron door behind him, and Tam understood with a cold settling in his stomach that freedom was not what she wanted. Not yet. There was an order to these things. There were debts, and debts came due in sequence, and some debts could not be paid until they were *collected*.

"She's waiting for him," said a voice at Tam's shoulder, and he nearly dropped the lantern.

A man stood by the wall — another grey figure, this one in a warden's coat, its brass buttons gone dull as lead. "They're all waiting for him. We heard it fall. We felt it." The warden touched his own chest, where a heartbeat should have been. "Twelve years I stood in his queue with my soul folded up small in my pocket, and he told me it was fair price. Fair price." The word came out of him like a splinter.

"He's in there?" Tam asked.

"He never left," the warden said. "Men like him don't run. Running means admitting what you are."

Tam looked down at the ledger under his arm. His father's neat hand filled its pages: ward-stone by ward-stone, payment by payment, and beside each entry, in a column his father had thought hidden, the names of the souls pledged as surety. Tam had read those names by lantern-light on the road north. Some of them were in this room. He knew it the way you know a face in a crowd you've been dreading.

"I have to open the door," he said.

"We know," said the dockworker woman, and her voice was gentle, which was somehow worse. "We've been patient. We can be patient a little longer. But not forever, boy. Not forever."

* * *

The counting-house door was sealed with a charm Tam recognized from his father's ledgers — a ward of Ash's own design, sold to the Court at a markup, because Corvane Ash had never met a lock he wouldn't sell twice. Tam's father had built the counter-charm into the ward-keeper's ring, and Tam wore that ring now, had worn it since the night the stars went out, turning it and turning it like a man worrying a wound.

It opened the door the way a key opens a chest that turns out to hold something alive.

The room beyond was small. That was the first shock. Tam had imagined vaults, cathedrals of gold and glass, the grandeur of theft. Instead: a clerk's room. A desk. An abacus with beads of black glass. Shelves of ledgers — hundreds of them, each spine labeled in Ash's precise hand — and in the center of the desk, a single lamp burning low.

Corvane Ash sat behind the desk with his hands flat on its surface, the way a magistrate sits when court is in session and the verdict is already written.

He looked smaller than Tam remembered from the market. Smaller, and older, and — this was the strangest thing — *relieved*.

"You're the ward-keeper's son," Ash said. His voice had lost its music. In the twilight market it had rung like a struck coin; now it was just a voice, dry and tired, a man's voice at the end of a very long day. "Vey. Your father had beautiful penmanship. Did you know that? I used to read his reports just for the pleasure of it. Every stroke considered. Every number honest — except the ones that mattered."

"My father is dead," Tam said. "Or gone. Or whatever the Court leaves when it takes its payment."

"Gone," Ash agreed. "I brokered him myself. He signed on the same night you were born, did you know? A ward-keeper's wage doesn't cover a wet-nurse and a fever-charm and coal through winter. He sold a piece of himself to keep you fed, and then he spent twelve years selling pieces of everyone else so he'd never have to look at what he'd become." Ash smiled, and it was a terrible smile, thin as a knife's edge and just as clean. "You want to hate me for it. Everyone wants to hate me. But I was only ever the middleman, boy. The hunger was theirs. The hunger was always theirs."

"The crown is broken," Tam said. "Your power's gone. I watched it go."

"I know what I watched." For a moment something moved behind Ash's face — fear, maybe, or its exhausted cousin, acceptance. "Do you know what I was before all this? A hedge-lawyer in a river town. I took bribes. Small ones. Then larger ones. And one day a client couldn't pay in coin, and offered me something better, and I learned the lesson your witch learned, and your father learned, and every soul in this mountain learned: *power is simply taken.*" He spread his hands. "I took. That's all. No one ever stopped me. Do you understand what that does to a man? To be permitted, always, to be permitted?"

The lamp flickered. And in the flicker, Tam saw them.

They stood in the corners of the little room, in the shadows between the shelves, dozens of them, hundreds — grey figures with mussel-shell eyes, dockworkers and wardens and farmers and children, pressed close together like commuters in rain. They had come down through the mountain's waking veins, patient as water finding its level. None of them spoke. None of them needed to. The room had grown cold, and the cold had a shape, and the shape was *owed*.

Ash saw them too. Tam watched him see them — watched the magistrate's composure hold for three heartbeats, four, five, and then come apart like wet paper.

"They can't," Ash whispered. "The crown is broken. Without it, they're nothing. They're *nobody*, they can't—"

"They waited twelve years," Tam said. His voice surprised him — steady, quiet, his father's courtroom voice, though his father had never once used it in a courtroom. "Some of them longer. My father taught me that wards don't fail all at once. They fail slowly, and then all at once. I think people do too."

Ash looked at Tam then, truly looked, and for one naked moment the magistrate who had taught half the borderlands that power was taken seemed about to ask the ward-keeper's son for mercy.

Tam did not give it. But he did not look away either, which was its own kind of answer.

"They won't hurt you," Tam said. "That's the worst part, isn't it? You've seen what they are. They're just going to stand there. They're going to stand there until you understand exactly what you sold, and then they're going to collect it, and it's going to feel like fairness, because you taught everyone in this mountain that everything is a price."

The grey figures began to move. Not fast. Not violent. They crossed the little clerk's room the way frost crosses a window, and Corvane Ash stood up from his desk, and then sat down again, and then stood, and his mouth was moving — pleading, bargaining, offering, the reflexes of a lifetime — but no sound came out, because there was no one left in the room willing to make a deal.

The last thing Tam saw, before he closed the door out of a courtesy he could not fully explain even to himself, was Ash reaching for the abacus — reaching, with a broker's dying instinct, to count what remained.

Tam shut the door. Set his palm flat against the iron. Behind it, very faintly, came a sound like a long-held breath finally let go.

When he opened it again, the room was empty. The ledgers remained, hundreds of them, every name accounted for. On the desk, neatly squared, lay Ash's signet ring and his black-glass abacus, and beside them a scrap of paper in a shaking hand:

*All accounts settled.*

* * *

Above ground, the world was busy forgiving itself.

Tam walked out of the Underway into morning — real morning, the first in twelve years, with a sun that came up honest over Greyharrow's smoke and a wind off the harbor that smelled of tar and fish and nothing else. People stood in the streets looking up. Some were crying. Some were laughing. A child near the fish-market pointed at the sky and demanded, at volume, to know what the bright things were called, and an old woman was telling her, one by one, working from memory, getting most of them wrong, and nobody minded.

The wardens' gallows on Customs Hill stood empty. Someone had already sawed it down overnight; the wood was being split for hearth-fires in the low town, and the smell of it cooking reached Tam across the water. Hedge-witches were coming out of their shuttered shops blinking, squinting, testing charms in doorways like men testing ice. Nobody stopped them. Nobody dared.

And everywhere, everywhere, the returned. They came up out of cellars and culverts and the mouths of old mines, grey figures growing color by the hour, remembering their own names in public like drunkards remembering the walk home. The city took them in with a stunned, embarrassed tenderness — blankets, soup, questions asked gently or not at all. Twelve years of stolen people, walking home.

Tam did his part. He stood at the harbor steps for three days with his father's ledger open on his knees, reading names, matching faces, pointing the way. It was the hardest work he had ever done, harder than the Underway, harder than the summit, because every third name in the book was followed by a small notation in his father's hand — *paid in full, keep quiet* — and every time Tam read one aloud, he buried his father a little deeper.

On the fourth day, Wren found him.

She sat down beside him on the harbor steps without asking, the way she had done everything since the summit — since she had stepped into the returning soul and burned away like fog in sun, since Juniper had woken whole in Ivo's arms and wept for a girl none of them would ever meet again. Tam had liked Wren. She had been sharp and strange and kind in the specific way of people who know they are borrowed.

"You're doing penance," Wren said. Except it wasn't Wren anymore, quite — the voice was Juniper's now, older, rougher, with the ghost-girl's cadence underneath like a thread of silver in grey wool. "Reading the names. Carrying the book."

"Somebody has to know what he did."

"Somebody has to know what *your father* did," Juniper said gently. "That's different, and you know it's different, and you're using the book to hide from the difference."

Tam looked out at the harbor. The water was full of light. It seemed obscene, how much light there was.

"He sold the wards," Tam said. "To protect me. And then he vanished the same night they broke, and I spent my whole life thinking he was a coward, and he was — he was a man paying installments. That's worse. Cowardice is simple."

"Yes," Juniper said. "It usually is." She was quiet a moment. "I sold my soul to buy a runaway apprentice twelve years of running. I've had two days to remember it. Ask me if it was simple."

"Was it?"

"No." She smiled, and it was Wren's smile, briefly, like a face under the surface of water. "But it was mine. That's the whole difference, Tam. Everything in that book was taken. What we gave — that's the only property we ever really owned." She stood, brushed off her skirts, and looked down at him with mussel-dark eyes that were learning, slowly, to be brown again. "Your father's last entry. Have you read it?"

"There's no last entry. It ends mid-page. Ward-stone nine, and then nothing."

"Look again. Tonight. By lamplight, not daylight." She patted his shoulder once, lightly, and went down the steps toward the fish-market, where Ivo was waiting with a cart and two mugs of something hot, and where a small shop with a narrow frontage stood empty, its rent already haggled, its sign not yet painted.

* * *

That night, alone in the ward-house that no longer belonged to anyone, Tam lit a lamp and opened the ledger to the final page.

Ward-stone nine. His father's hand, steady as ever. The payment recorded. The pledge noted. And then, below it, crammed into the margin in writing that had begun controlled and deteriorated into scratches, as if the pen had been fighting the man holding it:

*If you are reading this, Tam, then the wards are broken and I am gone, and you will hate me, and you should, a little. But listen. The stones can be rebuilt. Not from my designs — burn my designs. I wrote them for a buyer, not a border. Build them from the ledger instead. Every name in this book is a place the wall failed. Build the wards where the names are thickest, and build them so they cannot be bought, and put your own name in the foundation where mine should have been, and forgive yourself before you need to.*

*The Court taught me that everything is a price. Prove it wrong. That is the whole work. That is the whole work. That is*

The ink stopped there. The pen had torn the page.

Tam sat for a long time with the lamp burning and the harbor sounds coming through the window — water, rigging, the ordinary music of an unhaunted night. Above the desk, through the glass, the stars were out in their thousands, indifferent and generous, and one of them, low over Mount Serle, burned brighter than it had any right to.

In the morning, he would burn his father's designs. In the morning, he would walk the border with the ledger under his arm and mark the ground where the names lay thickest. In the morning, he would begin.

But tonight he dipped a pen, and beneath his father's broken sentence, in his own careful hand — every stroke considered, every number honest — he wrote the first line of a new ledger:

*Ward-stone one. Built on a debt forgiven. Paid for by no one. Owned by everyone.*

Outside, far to the north, a light was rising over Greyharrow's newest shop — a candle in a narrow window, and beneath it, faintly, the sound of two women laughing over the painting of a sign.
