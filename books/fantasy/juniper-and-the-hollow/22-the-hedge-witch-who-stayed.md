## 22. The Hedge-Witch Who Stayed

The shop on Candlemaker's Row had been a chandler's before the stars came back, and it still smelled faintly of tallow and old smoke no matter how many times Ivo scrubbed the floors. She had come to think of the smell as honest. Everything in Greyharrow smelled of something — the harbor of brine and tar, the tanneries of their particular misery, the wardens' garrison of oiled leather and fear. Tallow and smoke was at least a smell that told the truth about itself.

She hung the sign on a grey morning in the first week of Thawmonth, when the gutters ran loud with snowmelt and the gulls argued over the fish-market scraps. Juniper had painted it herself, in her careful, crooked hand: **THACKERAY & JUNIPER — CHARMS, KNOTS, SMALL KINDNESSES.** Beneath, smaller, almost an afterthought: *No souls bought or sold. We wouldn't know how.*

"That last line's going to start conversations," Ivo said, holding the ladder steady while Juniper stepped down.

"Good," said Juniper. "Conversations are free. Everything else we charge for."

Ivo laughed — really laughed, the kind that surprised her on its way out — and went inside to unpack the stock, which was not much. A shelf of luck-knots tied from harbor-rope. Bundles of feverfew and yarrow hung from the rafters to dry. Candles Juniper made herself, beeswax and a thread of wick, nothing more. A jar of buttons. A jar of salt. A jar of patience, which was empty, because patience could not be stocked, only practiced, and they were both still practicing.

That was the whole inventory. That was the point.

* * *

Her magic did not come back. She had known it would not, even as Wren stepped into the returning soul on the summit of Serle, even as the crown came apart in her hands like a knot finally untied, even as the stars reignited one by one above the mountain like lamps being lit in a house long dark. Magic was not a thing you spent and recovered, like coin. It was a thing you were, and she had given what she was away, gladly, and what remained was a woman of forty-odd years with strong hands, a head full of small craft, and a scar across her palm where the crown's last ember had kissed her on its way to nothing.

Some mornings she reached for it anyway. Habit, mostly. She would wake before dawn, as she had for twelve years on the road, and lie in the narrow bed above the shop listening to the harbor bells, and some old hollow place in her chest would reach out the way a tongue finds the gap where a tooth used to be. And there would be nothing there. Just the gap. Just the quiet.

But the quiet was different now. Before, the quiet had been the sound of running. Now it was only the sound of Greyharrow waking up: the fish-carts on the cobbles, the gulls, Mrs. Pellweather two doors down beating a rug with the fury of a woman who had never once lost an argument. Ivo lay in it and let it hold her, and got up, and made tea, and went downstairs to open the shop.

The first customer came before the sign had been up an hour.

* * *

He was a dockworker, broad as a door, with rope-burned hands and the wary look of a man who had heard what happened to people who dealt with witches. He stood in the doorway a long time without coming in, working his cap in his fists.

"You're the one," he said at last. "From the mountain. The one the broadsheets talk about."

"I'm the one who sells knots," Ivo said. "The mountain's mostly talked about."

"They say you broke the crown." He said it the way men said things they didn't quite believe — carefully, testing the words for rot. "They say you gave your magic to do it. They say—" He stopped, embarrassed.

"They say a lot of things." Ivo set down the knot she was tying and looked at him properly. Under the wariness he was exhausted; under the exhaustion, frightened. She knew the shape of that. She had worn it for twelve years. "What do you need?"

"It's my daughter." The cap came apart in his hands entirely. "She took sick in the winter. Fever, then the coughing. The physickers did what they could. She's better — she's walking about — but she's scared. Scared of the dark now, since the night the stars went. Won't sleep unless there's a candle burning, and we can't afford to burn candles all night, and even then she wakes screaming." He looked at the shelf, at the little knots coiled like sleeping animals, and his voice dropped. "I don't want magic. I want something for her to hold."

Ivo got up and took down the softest cord she had — undyed wool, spun by a woman in the Weavers' Quarter who charged fair prices — and sat back down at the workbench where he could see her hands. She tied slowly, letting him watch every pass of the loop, every pull of the knot. Three loops for the three nights of fever survived. A fourth for the spring coming. A fifth left deliberately loose, unfinished.

"Why's that one loose?" he asked.

"So she can finish it herself," Ivo said. "When she's ready. A charm someone finishes themselves is worth ten someone else ties. Everyone knows that." Everyone did not know that. She had invented it just now, and it was true anyway, which was how the best charms worked.

She wrapped it in a scrap of linen and put it in his hands. "It's a knot," she said. "Wool and patience. When she holds it, tell her: this is five nights you already beat, and the sixth is hers to tie. That's all it is. That's all it needs to be."

He turned it over. He looked disappointed, she thought, for exactly one breath — and then something in his face eased, the way a man eases when he sets down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying. Because a charm that promised miracles was a debt, and a debt to the uncanny was a hook in the flesh. But wool and patience asked nothing back.

"How much?" he said.

"Two coppers."

He paid four and refused the change, and went out into the Thawmonth light holding the little bundle like it might fly away, and Ivo watched him go and found that her eyes were wet, and did not particularly mind.

* * *

Juniper found her like that at closing time, sitting behind the counter with the day's ledger open and unmarked, looking at nothing.

"Twelve years," Ivo said, when she felt her old master's shadow fall across the counter. "I crossed half the world selling charms to keep ahead of what I'd done. Luck-knots in Vell. Ward-tokens in the Salt Marches. I charmed a riverboat pilot's dice once so he'd win enough to feed his crew through a dead season, and I told myself it was kindness, and maybe it was, but I never once — not once — sold anything that was only what it was. There was always a little theft in it. A little borrowed power. A little of me reaching into the world and taking."

"And now?"

"Now I sell wool and patience." She laughed, scrubbing at her eyes with the heel of her hand. "And it's worth more. Isn't that obscene? All those years I thought power was the thing. Ash taught me power was the thing. Take it, hold it, spend it. And the truest charm I ever made was a knot a dockworker's daughter has to finish herself."

Juniper came around the counter and sat beside her on the stool, the way she used to sit beside her in the cottage kitchen a lifetime ago, shoulder to shoulder, close enough that Ivo could smell the beeswax on her hands. Twelve years older than the woman Ivo had betrayed, and yet somehow younger too — restored, whole, carrying everything and bowed by none of it.

"Do you regret it?" Juniper asked. "Giving it up. Some part of me has wondered, watching you these weeks. Whether you wake up hungry."

Ivo considered it honestly, because Juniper deserved honesty and had earned it twice over — once by selling her soul to buy Ivo's escape, and once by taking it back and standing in the doorway of the shop on the first morning and saying, simply, *I'm home*, as if twelve years were a mislaid afternoon.

"Sometimes I reach for it," she said. "Like a tooth that's gone. But hungry — no. Do you know what I felt, up on the summit, when the crown broke? Not loss. Relief. Like setting down a pack I'd forgotten I was wearing. All that power, and it was heavy, Junie. Every ounce of it was weight. Ash never understood that. He thought weight was worth. He thought the crown chose him because he was strong." She shook her head. "It chose him because he was hollow. It nearly chose me for the same reason."

"But it didn't."

"No." Ivo looked at her hands — the scarred palm, the ink-stained fingers, the knuckles that ached now when the weather turned, ordinary hands, mortal hands. "Because you kept a piece of me safe all those years without either of us knowing it. Whatever Wren was — whatever piece of you stayed with me — it must have caught a piece of me too. Held it somewhere the crown couldn't reach." She smiled, small and crooked. "You were always better at knots than I was."

Juniper huffed a laugh and bumped her shoulder against Ivo's, and they sat together in the cooling shop while the light went amber on the jars and the harbor bells rang the evening hour, and neither of them said anything else for a long time, because there are conversations that only work in silence.

* * *

Spring deepened. The shop found its rhythm.

Mornings, Juniper made candles and Ivo tied knots, and they argued cheerfully about whose craft was superior, a dispute neither wished to win. Afternoons brought customers: sailors wanting fair-wind tokens that were honestly just braided cord and good wishes; mothers buying feverfew; children pressing noses to the glass to stare at the jar labeled PATIENCE, which remained empty, to their perpetual outrage. Word spread the way word does in harbor towns — slowly, then all at once — until there were days Ivo barely sat down, and nights she fell asleep mid-sentence over the ledger.

Tam Vey came down from the mountain twice that spring. The first time he stood in the shop doorway with his father's ledger under his arm and a new badge on his coat — apprentice ward-keeper, provisional, the ink barely dry on whatever grudging arrangement the rebuilt Guild had forced itself to accept — and said, "My father's ledgers are going in the new ward-house archive. Open shelf. Where anyone can read what he did and why." His jaw worked. "As a warning. Not a blueprint."

"Good," Ivo said, and meant it, and saw that he needed to hear it meant.

The second time he came for luck-knots, three of them, for the crew raising the new ward-stones along the northern passes. He paid full price and counted it out coin by coin, and at the door he paused and said, awkwardly, "The wards are singing again. Properly, I mean. You can hear them at night if you stand on the peak. My father built them to sing. I'd forgotten that was the sound they made." Then he left quickly, before anyone could make him feel anything further, which was very like Tam.

Wren they did not speak of often, but they did not avoid speaking of her either. There was a chair by the shop window that everyone somehow understood was hers. On the anniversary of the summit — a date the broadsheets called the Night of Returning and Ivo privately called the night she lost a ghost and gained a whole — Juniper set a cup of tea at that place, untouched, and Ivo tied a sixth loop into a length of cord and left it loose, and they let the evening be what it was.

"She'd have liked the shop," Juniper said once, quietly, dusting the sill.

"She'd have complained about the rent," Ivo said, "and then stayed anyway."

"Yes," Juniper said. "Exactly that."

* * *

Summer came, and with it a letter.

It arrived on a Tuesday, carried by a courier in livery Ivo did not recognize — deep green, silver trim, a sigil of a seven-pointed star. The courier waited in the street while Ivo read it standing behind the counter, Juniper's hand warm on her shoulder.

The seal was the new Crown's — the restored court at Vell, such as it was, half the old nobility disgraced and the other half pretending they had always loved the stars. The letter was brief and written in a fine, careful hand:

*Madam Thackeray —*

*The Court extends its gratitude, insufficient though any gratitude must be, for the restoration of the heavens. It is proposed that a position be created befitting your service: Royal Charmwright, with estates, stipend, and authority over all hedge-craft within the realm. The Court believes your talents should not go unhoused.*

*I am instructed to await your reply.*

Ivo read it twice. Behind her, Juniper read it over her shoulder, and made a small sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

"Royal Charmwright," Juniper said. "Estates. Authority." Her voice was dry as August. "Power, freely offered, with a house attached."

"Mm." Ivo looked past the letter, out the shop window, down Candlemaker's Row. Mrs. Pellweather was beating her rug again. Two boys were racing hoops toward the harbor. The light came down gold between the rooftops and lay across the counter, across the jars, across the shelf of little knots waiting for whoever needed them next. Somewhere out there a dockworker's daughter was sleeping with five finished loops in her fist and a sixth waiting for her courage.

Twelve years ago, a letter like this would have been the dream entire. Power, housed, sanctioned, safe. The cage with gilt bars that Ash had promised her in the dark beneath Serle, dressed at last in respectable clothes.

"Fetch me a pen," Ivo said.

Juniver fetched it — Juniper fetched it, old habits of the road still tripping her tongue — and Ivo wrote her reply on the back of the courier's own letter, in her plain round hand, while the shop bell chimed and a customer came in dripping from a sudden squall, asking after a fair-wind token.

*To His Majesty's Court: With respect and thanks, no. I keep a shop. The shop keeps me. If the Court should ever need wool, patience, or small kindnesses, we are on Candlemaker's Row, and we charge fair prices.*

She signed it, folded it, and handed it back to the courier, who blinked at her as though she had declined the moon.

Then she rolled up her sleeves, tied on her apron, and turned to the rain-soaked sailor waiting at the counter, and asked him what he needed — and began, with strong ordinary hands, to tie.
