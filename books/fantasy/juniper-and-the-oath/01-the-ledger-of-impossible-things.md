## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The whalebone had been soaking in brine and ash for three days, and now it was ready to speak.

Jude Villalobos turned it in the lamplight, a curved slab the length of his forearm, gray-white as an overcast sky. To anyone else it would have been a curiosity — a scrimshaw blank, a paperweight for a merchant with too much coin and too little sense. To Jude it was a door, and behind the door a drowned man waited with his last words still caught between his teeth.

He set the bone on the leather apron spread across his workbench, chose a graver with a fine hooked point, and began.

The first cut was always the hardest. It had to be true — not beautiful, not clever, *true* — because bone remembered what was done to it, and a false stroke would close the door forever. Jude held his breath the way his mother had taught him to hold a needle, steady, no tremor, and drew the graver along the bone's grain. The sound it made was very small, a whisper of parting, like snow sliding from a roof.

And then, faint as breath on a winter pane, the voice came.

*—cold, it's so cold, why won't they—*

Jude did not flinch. He had learned, years ago, that flinching made the voice retreat, the way a bird goes quiet when a cat moves. He kept his hand moving, cutting the second line of the token, a spiral that opened like a fern uncurling. The voice grew clearer, the way a face grows clearer as you carve away everything that is not the face.

*—the hold flooded first. Marlow went under with the cargo. I told them the ballast was wrong, I told them, I told—*

The drowned sailor had been dead for eleven years. His ship, the *Kindly Fortune*, had gone down off the Teeth with a hold full of smuggled indigo, and the Gilded Tooth had been hired — quietly, expensively — to learn whether the cargo had been sabotaged or merely unlucky. Insurance, of a kind. The guild traded in impossible things, and impossible things required proof, and proof, in the Gilded Tooth's ledgers, was very often bone.

Jude carved the spiral's third turn. The voice broke apart and reassembled, closer now, as though the sailor were standing at his shoulder instead of lying in forty fathoms of green water with the indigo bleeding out of the broken hold like a bruise.

*—I told them the ballast was wrong. I told them. Someone moved it. Someone moved it in the night and I saw the rope marks, I saw—*

There it was. Jude's graver paused above the bone, hovering, and for a moment the workshop was utterly silent except for the tick of the stove and the sea-sound of the city beyond the shutters — the Gilded Tooth stood on Sable Row, close enough to the harbor that the gulls argued on its roof slates every morning.

Someone moved the ballast. Rope marks. The drowned man had been a witness to murder, and he had been waiting eleven years in the cold and the dark for someone to ask.

*That's enough,* Jude thought at the bone, gently, the way one gentles a horse. *I have what I need. You can rest.*

But he did not stop carving yet. There was a final line, always — a small closed loop at the spiral's heart, the seal that shut the door. He cut it with two strokes, precise as a surgeon's knot, and the voice thinned, and faded, and was gone. The workshop settled around him like a house at night. The whalebone lay still on the leather, warm under his fingertips in a way bone had no right to be, holding its dead man's testimony in its grain until someone with the gift — or someone willing to pay for the gift — chose to open it again.

Jude sat back and flexed his fingers. They ached. They always ached, after.

On the wall above the bench hung his tools, forty-one of them, each graver and gouge and rasp wrapped at the handle in red-dyed cord. On the shelf beside them sat the ledger — not the guild's great ledger, the gilded one in Corvane's office with its brass clasps and its columns of debts and favors, but *his* ledger, the quiet one, bound in gray calfskin. He opened it to the current page and dipped his pen.

*Token 447,* he wrote. *Whalebone, one piece, spiral-cut, sealed. Subject: Bosun Ellery Vance, d. 11 years past, wreck of the Kindly Fortune. Contents: testimony regarding ballast tampering, rope marks observed, names withheld pending—*

He stopped. Pending. Pending what?

Pending the client's decision, of course. Pending Corvane's decision. The Gilded Tooth did not deliver truth the way a baker delivered bread, warm and to anyone who asked. Truth was a commodity, and commodities were weighed, and sometimes a truth was worth more unspoken than spoken, and it was not Jude's place — it had never been his place — to ask what became of a testimony once it left his hands.

He finished the entry, blotted it, and closed the ledger. Then he wrapped the whalebone token in oilcloth, tied it with plain twine, and set it in the delivery tray with the others: a rib-token from a saint's finger-bone that whispered the location of a buried strongbox; a knuckle-token cut from a hanged man that could recite, in a voice like dry leaves, the exact wording of a will now conveniently lost. Impossible things. The Gilded Tooth's stock-in-trade.

The lamp guttered. Outside, Sable Row was going dark, the gaslamps being lit one by one down toward the harbor, and the gulls had gone to wherever gulls went when the wind turned. Jude trimmed the wick and let himself, for just a moment, sit in the quiet and feel the shape of his own solitude.

It fit him the way an old coat fits — badly, but familiarly.

No one in the city knew what he could do. That was the arrangement, unspoken but absolute. Corvane had found him in a workhouse when he was nine years old, a skinny boy who flinched from the chapel's relic-box because the saint inside it *wouldn't stop talking*, and Corvane had looked at him with those pale, patient eyes and said, *You'll do,* and had taken him home to Sable Row and fed him and taught him and never, not once in twenty years, asked him to demonstrate his gift in front of another living soul.

Jude had told himself for twenty years that this was kindness. Corvane protecting him. Corvane understanding that a gift like Jude's, known, would make him a treasure or a target, and that the Gilded Tooth's back rooms were the only place in the city where a bone-speaker could be both useful and safe.

Lately — and he could not have said when it began, only that it had, the way rot begins inside a beam — Jude had noticed that the arrangement suited Corvane very well too. A gift that no one knew about was a gift that no one could bid against. A carver who trusted no one with his secret was a carver who could never leave.

*You're tired,* Jude told himself, standing, rolling the ache out of his shoulders. *Tired men see rot in clean beams.*

He hung the apron on its hook. He banked the stove. And that was when the knock came at the workroom door — three raps, unhurried, the particular rhythm of a man who had never in his life needed to hurry through a door.

"Jude." Corvane's voice, smooth as poured wax. "You're wanted in my office. There's an appraisal."

* * *

Corvane's office sat at the heart of the Gilded Tooth like the heart in a body, and everything in it was designed to make visitors feel like blood — pumped through, used, sent on their way. The carpet was deep enough to swallow footsteps. The shelves held the guild's public face: curiosities, relics, a stuffed basilisk-ling that had turned out, on appraisal, to be a monitor lizard with a lie attached. And behind the desk, in a chair that might as well have been a throne with better upholstery, sat Master Corvane of the Gilded Tooth, sixty years old and smooth as a river stone, turning a signet ring around his finger.

"You've finished the *Fortune* token." It was not a question. Corvane never asked questions he already knew the answers to; it was, he said, a waste of breath.

"This morning." Jude set the wrapped token on the desk, along with the ledger page he'd copied out fair. "Ballast was tampered with. Rope marks on the strakes. The bosun saw it done — he doesn't say by whom. He died before he could name anyone."

Corvane read the page. He read everything twice; Jude had watched him do it for twenty years and had never decided whether it was caution or theater. "And the names he does say?"

"Marlow, the cargo-master. The ship's boy, Tally. He calls for his wife at the end." Jude kept his voice level. "Her name is Sera Vance. She lives on Tanner's Walk. She's been petitioning the harbormaster for a death-inquiry for nine years."

"Mm." Corvane set the page down and aligned its edge with the desk's edge, a small precise movement, the way he did everything. "The client will be pleased about the tampering. The rest—" he flicked two fingers, dismissive, "—is sentiment. We'll deliver the finding and the token. The widow is not our concern."

"She's been asking the water for eleven years," Jude said, before he could stop himself. "She just didn't know the water could answer."

Something moved behind Corvane's pale eyes — not anger, never anger; Corvane's displeasure was subtler than that, a change in temperature. "And that," he said softly, "is exactly the kind of sentence you must never say in front of anyone but me. Sentiment, Jude. It's how hearers get caught." He smiled, and the smile was warm the way a lamp is warm, light without heat. "Which brings me to your appraisal. A routine one. A reliquary, come in through the Salt Gate this morning, provenance questionable, contents questionable-er. The client wants to know if the bones inside are what the seller claims."

"What does the seller claim?"

"A saint's relics. Third-tier saint, minor house, nothing that should interest anyone." Corvane rose, and his shadow climbed the shelves behind him, long and unhurried. "But the crate was sealed with wax I don't recognize, and the man who brought it paid the gate-fee in old coin. Pre-Founding coin, Jude. Three centuries old, at least." He said it lightly, the way a man mentions weather, but Jude had learned to hear the weight under Corvane's lightness the way he heard the voices under the bone. "So you'll open it, and you'll read it, and you'll tell *me* what's inside before you tell the ledger. Understood?"

"Understood."

"Good boy." Corvane's hand landed briefly on Jude's shoulder, familiar as a yoke. "The crate's in the vault room. Take your time. Take *care*." And then, at the door, almost as an afterthought, in a tone so casual it was clearly not casual at all: "And Jude — if the bones say anything about kings, you'll come to me first. Before the ledger. Before anyone."

The vault room was down two flights, behind a door of black iron, and the crate waited on the trestle table where they'd left it, small and plain, its wax seal dark as a dried wound.

Jude stood over it a long moment, his hands steady, his heart not.

Then he picked up his knife and broke the seal.
