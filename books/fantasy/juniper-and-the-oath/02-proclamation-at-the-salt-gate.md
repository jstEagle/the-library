## 2. Proclamation at the Salt Gate

The proclamation went up on the Salt Gate before the gulls had finished their morning argument, and by the time Jude reached the fish-market the whole city had already read it aloud in a hundred different voices.

He heard it first as a change in the crowd's texture — that low animal hum of people pressed too close together, sharpened with something new. Fear has a sound. He had learned it young, the way other children learned birdsong: the wet catch of breath, the small silence a body makes when it decides not to speak. The market was full of it now, threaded through the ordinary noise of haggling and gutting and the slap of brine on stone.

The parchment was nailed to the gate's salt-bleached timbers at the height of a tall man's reach, and it was written large enough that even the illiterate could follow along as a crier spelled it out. Jude did not need the crier. He had read the words before they were spoken, standing on his toes in the crush of bodies, and he read them again as though repetition might alter them:

*By order of His Majesty's Inquisition: let it be known that there walk among us persons of the hearing kind, who traffic with the dead through carved bone and like instruments. Such commerce is an abomination against crown and covenant. Any person knowing of such a one shall report to the King's hunters, upon pain of complicity. A bounty of forty crowns is offered for each such person delivered alive, twenty for proof of destruction.*

Forty crowns. Jude did the arithmetic without meaning to — it was a habit of the trade — and found that forty crowns was eleven months of his lodging, his coal, his lamp-oil and his tea. Forty crowns was what half the harbor slums earned in a year of honest misery.

And everyone in this crowd knew someone who whispered to bone. That was the thing about Ashfall. You could not spit here without hitting a charm-seller or a knuckle-bone reader or some old wife who kept her grandmother's finger-joint in a locket and swore it told her when rain was coming. The hearing kind were not strangers. They were neighbors. They were the woman who sold you mussels and the boy who swept your step.

Which meant the proclamation was not a warning. It was a purchase order.

Jude pulled his collar up and moved through the market with his eyes down, doing the thing he had done all his life without ever naming it: making himself dull. A man who walks like furniture is not remembered. He bought nothing. Buying meant stopping, and stopping meant being seen, and being seen this morning felt like standing in water during lightning.

He was nearly clear of the stalls when the screaming started.

* * *

It came from the direction of Tanner's Row, and it was not the sharp theatrical shriek of a cutpurse caught. It was long and ragged and human, and it went on and on, and the crowd flowed toward it the way crowds do — hungry for spectacle, ashamed of the hunger, pretending both.

Jude should have walked the other way. He told himself so even as his feet carried him forward, because the voice was familiar, and familiarity is a hook no sensible man resists.

They had Maren Cobb against the wall of the smokehouse.

She was seventy if she was a day, a widow who shucked oysters on the tide-flats and had once, when Jude was nine and feverish, sat up all night with him because his landlady was drunk and someone had to. She was tiny. She looked smaller against the two hunters holding her arms, one on each side, their grey-and-white livery the only clean things in the whole street. Her basket lay overturned, oysters rolling into the gutter, and she was still clutching — absurdly, desperately — the little carved whelk-shell she wore on a cord around her neck. Her husband's last gift. Everyone on Tanner's Row knew what it was. Everyone had pretended for thirty years not to know.

"Let go of me, I'm nobody, I'm nobody—" she was saying, over and over, in the flat reasonable tone of a person trying to convince the world rather than herself.

"You're nobody," agreed the third hunter, the one doing the talking. He was young, with a soft face and hard eyes, and he held a pair of iron pincers the way a surgeon holds instruments. "That's why we're being gentle. Nobody doesn't need fingers."

The crowd made its small collective sound — that intake, that nothing — and did nothing.

Jude stood among them. He was taller than most; he could see everything. He saw Maren's eyes go from face to face in the crowd, searching, and he saw the moment she understood what she would find there, which was forty crowns' worth of averted gazes and not one friend. He saw her find him. For half a heartbeat her eyes met his over the shoulders of the mob, and there was no recognition in them at all — just the general appeal of the drowning to anyone on any shore — and somehow that was worse. She didn't know him. He knew her. He knew the exact pitch of the humming she did while she worked, and he knew that the whelk-shell held her husband saying *mind the tide, love, mind the tide*, and he knew that in ten minutes those pincers would have it off her neck, and that whatever the shell said after that would be said to men in grey and white.

He could have stepped forward. The thought arrived whole, unasked-for, the way the worst thoughts do: *step forward, say her name, take her place.* And beneath it, quieter and colder, the arithmetic again: *and then who keeps the ledgers? Who keeps the ledgers?*

He hated himself with a completeness that surprised him. He had thought himself past surprises of that kind.

Maren Cobb was dragged down Tanner's Row toward the Salt Gate, past the proclamation itself, her bare feet leaving wet prints on the stones, and the crowd closed behind her like water behind a keel, and Jude stood in the middle of it holding a basket he had never filled, and did nothing, and did nothing, and did nothing.

* * *

Corvane was burning tokens when Jude got back to the Gilded Tooth.

He smelled it before he saw it — that particular stink, bone-fat and resin, sweetish and wrong, like meat left too near a candle. It came drifting up the back stair from the counting room, and Jude climbed toward it with his stomach tightening, because he knew that smell, and he knew only one reason it would be in the guildhouse.

The counting room's shutters were closed against the day. Corvane had built a fire in the great hearth, which had not been lit in Jude's memory — the guildmaster was a miser with heat as with everything else — and he was feeding it from the third shelf of the appraisal cabinet. The shelf where the personal pieces lived. Not inventory. Not stock. The tokens people had brought in over thirty years because they had nowhere else to put them, because the Gilded Tooth was the only house in Ashfall that would not laugh at a widow's whelk-shell.

Corvane fed another handful to the flames. A carved raven's skull. A child's finger-bone scored with a mother's name. Things that had been somebody's whole grief, going up like tallow.

"They'll come with lists," Corvane said, without turning. He always knew Jude's tread; he had taught it to him, in a way, corrected it a thousand times across a thousand floors. "Inquisitors work from lists. Every token in this room is a name on a list waiting to be copied out. Burn the tokens, burn the names."

"That's Maren Cobb's shelf," Jude said. His voice came out strange and level. "Some of those aren't ours to burn."

"Nothing is anyone's to keep anymore." Corvane wiped his hands on his apron — the gesture of a tradesman, of the man who had found a starving boy in the ash-market and given him soup and a knife and a trade — and turned. His face was composed, but Jude had spent twenty years learning to read faces the way he read bone, and under the composure there was something moving, quick and dark, that might have been fear or might have been calculation wearing fear's clothes. With Corvane it had always been difficult to tell the difference. Jude was beginning to suspect there was no difference. "You were seen at the Salt Gate."

"I buy bread in the mornings. I'm allowed streets."

"You were seen *watching*. You watch the way you listen, boy — with your whole spine. Someone will remember it." Corvane came closer. Up close he smelled of smoke and camphor and the faint metallic tang of coin counted often. "From today, you don't hear anything. You understand me? Not a whisper. Not a word. No carving, no appraising of anything organic, nothing that has ever been part of anything living. You are a clerk. You are a clerk who has been a clerk since the day you arrived, and your hands are good for figures and nothing else."

"And the ledgers?" Jude asked.

It was the wrong question. He knew it was the wrong question the moment it left his mouth, because Corvane went very still, the way water goes still over something deep.

"What about them?"

"Thirty years of entries." Jude heard his own voice gathering speed, gathering heat, and could not stop it. "Every token we ever took in, described, dated, priced. *Whalebone token, drowned sailor, recovered speech, sold to a buyer in Vell.* *Finger-bone, grandmother's voice, retained for client.* I wrote half of those entries myself. My hand is on every page. If the tokens are evidence, then the ledgers are—"

"The ledgers," Corvane said quietly, "are the property of the Gilded Tooth, and will remain exactly where they have always been."

"Then you're keeping evidence. You're keeping *my* handwriting as evidence, and burning a widow's husband instead, and calling it prudence." The anger was fully awake in him now, and it was enormous, and it had been asleep for years, and he marveled at its size the way one marvels at a scar that turns out to have been a wound. "You're burning grief and filing guilt, and you want me to stand in this room and be a clerk."

"I want you to be *alive*." For a moment the mask slipped entirely, and what was underneath was older than the guildmaster, older than the guild — something tired and frightened and terribly patient. "Boy, there are things in this house older than the charter on your desk. There are arrangements older than the crown that's hunting you. If you keep your head down — if you are *dull*, if you are *furniture* — you will live through this winter, and I swear to you on the founding oath itself that what comes after will make sense of why."

The founding oath. Jude's blood went cold and then hot. Three hundred years the guild had traded in impossible things, and its charter began with a clause no one read aloud anymore, a clause about obligations owed downward, into the earth, to a king under a hill—

But Corvane was already turning back to the fire, and the moment closed like a door.

"Third shelf burns tonight," the guildmaster said. "The ledgers stay. Now go and copy out the quarter's duties, and touch nothing that has ever been alive."

Jude went. At the door he stopped, because the last thing on the third shelf was a small carved whelk-shell, brought in that very morning by a neighbor's grandson who could not bear to keep it and could not bear to burn it, and Corvane's hand was closing around it, and Jude found he could not watch.

He climbed to his room above the counting floor and sat down at his desk, where the ledgers lived in their locked case, thirty years of impossible things in his own careful hand. Evidence. He had always known that, somewhere. He had simply never been forced to hold the knowledge up to the light.

Below him, the fire crackled. Above him, the gulls wheeled and cried over the Salt Gate where the parchment hung, and forty crowns glittered in the heads of every neighbor he had ever not saved.

And under the floorboards, under the foundations, under three centuries of silence, something that had been waiting a very long time seemed — for the first time in his life — to be listening back.
