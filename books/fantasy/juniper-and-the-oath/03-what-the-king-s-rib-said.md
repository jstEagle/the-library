## 3. What the King's Rib Said

The reliquary arrived on a Tuesday, in the belly of a smuggler's sloop that smelled of bilgewater and rotting kelp, wrapped in oilcloth and lies.

Jude knew it was lies because everything the Gilded Tooth bought was. That was rather the point of the guild — impossible things, mislabeled as ordinary ones, so that the ordinary world would go on believing itself complete. A saint's fingerbone that was actually a murderer's knuckle. A dragon's egg that was a geode full of salt. A love philtre distilled from rain and regret. Jude had appraised them all, carving his little tokens, listening to what the dead had left behind in their bones like coins left in the pockets of drowned men.

He worked in the back room of the countinghouse, where the light came down through a single skylight furred with salt-spray, and where no one bothered him unless Corvane wanted something. The room smelled of beeswax and bone-dust and the faint mineral coldness of old stone. It was, Jude had long ago decided, the only room in Ashfall he trusted. Things could be heard here. Things could be *known*.

The reliquary sat on his workbench under the skylight, and it was beautiful, which was the first thing wrong with it.

It was a casket of blackened silver, no longer than a forearm, worked over with figures in relief: kings crowned and kneeling, armies rendered so fine their spears were needle-thin, and above them all a sun split by a sword. The craftsmanship was three centuries dead at least — older than the guild, older than the city's second wall, older than any dynasty currently claiming the throne. Whoever had made it had loved their king with a ferocity that still radiated off the metal like heat off a forge.

And whoever had smuggled it had lied about it beautifully. The manifest said *ecclesiastical goods, minor, one crate*. The crate contained candlesticks, a chipped processional cross, and this.

Jude put on his lens-cloth gloves and lifted the casket into the light.

* * *

There were rules to appraisal. Corvane had taught him most of them; he had taught himself the rest, in the years when his hands were small and the voices came too easily. Never listen through your bare skin if you can help it — always carve, always mediate, always give the dead a shape to pour themselves into, because an unshaped voice will pour into *you*. Never listen twice to the same bone in the same day. And never, ever listen to anything royal without telling Corvane first, because royal bones carried royal weight, and royal weight crushed.

But the reliquary was sealed, and sealed things had to be catalogued before they were opened, and cataloguing meant touching, and touching — even gloved, even careful — meant *listening*, a little, the way you might hear music through a wall.

So when Jude's fingers closed around the casket to turn it toward the light, he expected the usual murmur. The ambient hum of devotion. Three hundred years of prayers soaked into silver, saying nothing much, meaning nothing much.

What he got instead was silence.

Not emptiness — silence. The difference mattered. Emptiness was a bone with nothing in it, a room with no one home. This was a room with someone in it who had stopped breathing to hear whether he could hear them.

Jude set the casket down very slowly.

"Hello," he said, which was foolish, because he didn't speak aloud to relics, and also because the dead didn't need him to speak. But the silence held, attentive, patient, and Jude felt the hair rise along his forearms the way it did before a storm came off the harbor.

He should have gone for Corvane. He knew that even then. Rule the last, rule the oldest: never listen alone to anything that listens back.

Instead he reached for his knife.

* * *

The seal broke with a sound like a held breath released. Inside, on a bed of rotted velvet gone the color of dried blood, lay a single rib-bone, curved like a question mark, yellowed to the shade of old ivory. It was not large. It was not impressive. It looked like something pulled from a midden, except that the moment the lid came open, the whole back room went cold — not draft-cold but *deep*-cold, the cold of cellars and crypts and places the sun had given up on.

Jude did not touch it with his hands. He had learned that much wisdom. He took up a blank token of whalebone instead — a disc the size of a coin, uncarved, waiting — and held it over the rib while he worked the knife.

Carving was listening. That was the truth of his gift that no witch-hunter's proclamation would ever understand: he did not pull words out of the dead. He made a door, and the dead walked through it if they wished. The knife moved, and the whalebone opened, and Jude braced himself for whatever voice had been waiting three centuries in that silver box.

The voice came all at once, and it was mid-word.

"—and I swear it upon my own ribs, which shall be kept against that day—"

A man's voice. Deep, worn, furious in the way that only exhausted men are furious, the fury banked down to coals. Not a ghost's whispering. Not the fragmentary last-words Jude usually caught, the gasps and gurgles and final petty thoughts of the recently dead. This was a *speech*. A formal instrument. He was hearing the middle of an oath, sworn with all the terrible ceremony of a court, and the voice spoke as though it had been speaking continuously for three hundred years and had merely paused to let Jude catch up.

"—that no war shall wake, no blade be drawn in the old quarrel, until one of the hearing kind speaks my name freely."

The knife stopped.

Jude's hands had gone still without his permission, the way a man's legs stop on a cliff edge. In the whalebone token, the voice waited — not faded, not finished. *Waiting.* He could feel it the way you feel a held note in music, the tension of a chord unresolved, three centuries long.

"Until one of the hearing kind speaks my name freely."

He said it aloud without meaning to, tasting each word. The room seemed to lean in.

Hearing kind. The hearing kind. The words the crown's new proclamations used, painted fresh on the Salt Gate not two weeks past, words that had gotten Old Marta dragged from her loom with her wrists bound in iron. Words that had made Corvane burn a shelf of tokens and tell Jude to be stone, be deaf, be nothing.

But those words hadn't come from the crown. They couldn't have. They were *old*. They were written into this oath, carved into this moment, three hundred years before anyone thought to hunt anyone. The crown wasn't hunting the hearing kind despite the oath.

The crown was hunting them *because of it.*

Jude looked down at the rib, and the rib seemed to look back, and for the first time in his life he understood that a bone could be patient.

* * *

He did not carve again that day. He sat with the open reliquary under the skylight while the light crawled across the floorboards and died, and he thought, and the thinking felt less like thinking than like falling.

Because here was the thing he could not stop seeing: the founding charter of the Gilded Tooth hung framed in Corvane's office, behind glass, illuminated in gold leaf. Jude had dusted it a thousand times. He knew its clauses the way other men knew psalms. *The Guild shall hold in trust what cannot be owned. The Guild shall keep faith with the silenced. The Guild shall endure until the debt is paid.*

Until the debt is paid.

He had always read that as poetry. Guilds loved poetry; it dressed up bookkeeping. But an oath was not poetry. An oath was a mechanism. He was holding one in his hands — he could feel its gears, its springs, its terrible patience. *No war shall wake until one of the hearing kind speaks my name freely.* An oath needed a condition, and a condition needed a keeper, and a keeper needed—

Needed a bone-speaker. Needed someone who could hear the dead, who could know the name, who could one day stand in some appointed place and speak it freely and end the thing or renew it or break it. Needed someone found young, raised carefully, taught to carve and to listen and to trust no one, kept close like a lamp kept filled against a night the household knew was coming.

Needed, say, a boy bought off a plague-cart thirty years ago by a guildmaster with kind eyes and meticulous ledgers.

Jude stood up. His knees cracked. The room was dark now except for the reliquary, which seemed to hold its own small weather, and the rib within it lay curved and yellowed and quiet, and Jude hated it with a sudden completeness that frightened him — hated it the way you hate a door you've walked through and cannot walk back through.

Every kindness refracted differently now. The extra coin in his purse after hard months. The way Corvane had never let him take contracts outside the city. The way the guildmaster's lessons had always circled back, gently, inevitably, to the same questions: *what do you hear, boy? What does it say? Say it plainly, plainly now.* He had thought it was care. Care and curiosity together, the way a father is curious about a son.

But fathers did not keep their sons the way the Tooth kept assets. Fathers did not raise a tool and call the raising love.

"No," Jude said to the empty room, and his voice sounded strange to him, scraped raw. "I don't believe it. I won't."

The rib said nothing. It didn't need to. It had been waiting three hundred years; it could wait out one carver's denial too.

* * *

He wrapped the reliquary exactly as protocol demanded — oilcloth, twine, wax seal bearing his personal mark — and wrote the entry in the ledger in his own neat hand: *Reliquary, silver, provenance unknown. Contents: one human rib, male, elderly. No residual voice detected.*

The lie sat on the page looking exactly like the truth. That was the worst part, how easy it was. Thirty years of honest ledgers, and the first false line cost him nothing but the decision.

Then he blew out the lamp, locked the back room, and carried the ledger itself under his arm through the countinghouse corridors, past the clerks bent over their columns, past the brass scales and the smell of ink, to the office at the end where the founding charter glowed behind its glass like something holy.

Corvane was there, as he always was at this hour, trimming a pen by candlelight. He looked up, and his face did what it always did when Jude entered — warmed, settled, the face of a man whose family has come home.

"Late entry?" Corvane asked pleasantly. "Nothing troublesome, I hope."

Jude laid the ledger on the desk. He looked at the charter on the wall — *until the debt is paid* — and then at the man who had bought him, fed him, taught him, and lied to him for thirty years, and who might, for all Jude knew, be lying still.

"One rib," Jude said. "Old. Silent." He met Corvane's eyes and did not blink. "Though it occurs to me, master — how old does a bone have to be before it stops being silent?"

Something crossed Corvane's face then, quick as a fish under ice — there and smoothed away so fast Jude might have imagined it, except that he had spent his whole life reading faces the way other people read books, and he had not imagined it.

It was fear.

"Why do you ask?" said Corvane, very softly.

"Because," Jude said, "I think the Gilded Tooth was founded to wait for one. And I think you've known that since before you knew my name."

The candle between them guttered. Neither man moved to trim the wick, and in the failing light the charter's gold letters seemed to darken, clause by clause, like a sky going over before a storm.
