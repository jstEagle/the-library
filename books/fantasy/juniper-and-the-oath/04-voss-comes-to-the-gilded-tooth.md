## 4. Voss Comes to the Gilded Tooth

The knock came at dawn, and it was wrong.

Jude knew the sound of the Gilded Tooth's door the way he knew his own heartbeat — the fat merchant's double rap, the courier's impatient scratch, Corvane's own key turning in the lock like a promise kept. This was none of those. This was three measured blows, evenly spaced, struck by someone who had never once in her life needed to ask permission to enter anywhere.

He was already at his workbench, a half-finished heron of gull-bone under his knife, when Tam the door-boy came skidding down the stairs with his face gone the color of tallow.

"Tax assessors," Tam whispered. "Crown men. Four of them. And a woman."

Jude set down the knife. His hands were steady — they always were; bone taught you that — but something behind his ribs had begun to swing like a censer.

The ledgers. Three hundred years of ledgers, and every impossible thing in them written fair in his own hand: *one drowned sailor's last words, sold to a widow in Candle Row; one tooth of the buried king, appraised and returned to vault; one hearing, rendered gratis.* He had argued once, years ago, that the guild should keep two sets of books. Corvane had laughed and said the Gilded Tooth hid nothing, that honesty was the best disguise a fence could wear. Jude had believed him, because believing him was easier than the alternative.

Now the honesty sat on its shelves like kindling.

* * *

Inquisitor Serane Voss climbed the guild's stairs slowly, because slow looking was thorough looking.

She had been walking through this city for eleven days reading it the way other people read confessions. The Salt Gate proclamation had flushed out forty-one of the hearing kind so far — fishwives who could taste a drowned man's name on the wind, a bell-ringer who heard his dead mother in the bronze, children, always children, dragged weeping from attics while their neighbors studied their own shoes. Forty-one. And every one of them small. A gift that mattered would not live in an attic.

It would live somewhere with money. Money bought privacy, and privacy was where the dangerous ones grew.

So she climbed the stairs of the Gilded Tooth — guild of curiosities, dealer in impossible things, tax delinquent by exactly the amount she had manufactured for the pretext — and she let her gloved hand trail along the banister, counting doors, listening to the building breathe.

Corvane met her in the ledger room, all silver beard and merchant's warmth, bowing just deep enough to flatter without groveling. "Inquisitor. An honor, though I confess the honor would be greater if it weren't wearing the crown's tax seal."

"The crown has many faces, Master Corvane." She unwound her gloves finger by finger. "Today it wears the one that wants its due."

Behind him, at a long desk beneath a window of bottle-green glass, sat a young man with ink-stained fingers and a stillness she recognized. She had seen that stillness before — in soldiers who had held a line, in priests who had heard something in the dark. It was the stillness of a person listening.

She looked at the young man a moment too long, and watched him look back at the ledger as though it were the most fascinating object ever bound in calfskin.

*Too late,* she thought. *I've already seen you.*

* * *

Jude kept his eyes on the columns and made himself breathe like a man doing arithmetic.

Voss walked the room the way a tide walks a beach — unhurried, total, leaving nothing unexamined. He heard her boots stop at the shelf of whalebone tokens. Heard her lift one, turn it over. Heard the small dry sound of her thumbnail testing the carving.

"This is fine work," she said. "Who carves for you?"

"Our bone-carver," Corvane said smoothly. "Jude. Come here, boy, show the Inquisitor your craft."

And there it was — the trap laid open and honest, because Corvane's honesty was always a disguise. Refuse, and he was hiding something. Come forward, and he stood close enough for her to study.

Jude rose and crossed the floor and put out his hand with the tokens in it, letting her take them rather than offering, the way a craftsman does who is proud but not eager. Her fingers were cold even through the leather. Her eyes were gray as slate weather and did not blink enough.

"You carve freehand," she said. "No patterns?"

"Some patterns. The good pieces don't take them."

"Why not?"

"Bone has grain," Jude said. "Like wood. You can't argue with it. You follow what's there or you fight it, and if you fight it, it cracks." He heard his own voice and marveled at it — calm, bored, a tradesman's voice. He had never lied in his life. Every word he'd ever spoken had been true because truth was cheaper than the effort of anything else. Now he understood that lying was not saying false things. Lying was standing in front of a woman who could end you and talking about grain.

"And what do you make these for?" She turned a token — the drowned sailor's piece, he realized, the one he should have filed away. "Tokens. Keepsakes. Mourning pieces?"

"Mourning mostly," Jude said. "People like to hold something when they've lost someone. Bone remembers shape. That's all it is."

"That's all it is," Voss repeated softly.

And then, because the god of liars was cruel, the token spoke.

Not aloud. Not for her. For him — the way it always did, the dead man's last breath rising up through the carved grain into Jude's palm: *cold, so cold, tell Marta the nets—* And Jude, exhausted, terrified, holding the thing against his skin while an Inquisitor watched, felt his face do something. A flicker. A listener's flicker.

He saw her see it.

For one heartbeat the room was very quiet, and Jude understood that he was going to die in this green-lit room among the ledgers, and that the strangest part was the arithmetic still open on his desk, waiting for a hand that would never finish it.

"Master Corvane," Voss said, without moving her gaze from Jude's face, "your carver looks unwell."

"He works too many hours," Corvane said. "Genius keeps bad habits."

"Hm." She set the token down on the desk, precisely aligned with its edge, and Jude's heart began again. "Then perhaps he should rest. After the audit."

* * *

Serane Voss finished the audit in four hours and found the guild owing the crown one hundred and forty crowns, which was nine more than existed, which was the point. Pretexts were like hooks: you didn't need the fish to bite hard, only to touch the line.

But she was not thinking about taxes. She was thinking about grain.

*You follow what's there or you fight it.* It was a true answer. She distrusted true answers given quickly; liars rehearsed, but so did the innocent, and the innocent rehearsed worse. What she kept returning to was the flicker. She had interviewed two hundred hearers since the proclamation went up, and every one of them, in the moment the gift touched them, wore that same expression — a slight loosening around the mouth, as if someone else had briefly borrowed the face. The carver had worn it for less than a heartbeat, holding a mourning token, in a room full of witnesses, with everything to lose.

Either he was the finest actor she had ever seen, or he was the most disciplined hearer she had ever hunted.

Discipline meant training. Training meant someone had found him young.

She signed the assessment notice on Corvane's desk and said, pleasantly, "One more matter. My warrant covers persons as well as property. I'll want to speak with your staff. Individually."

Corvane's smile did not move, but she watched the cost of keeping it still arrive somewhere behind his eyes. "Of course."

She took them one at a time in the entry hall. Door-boys, clerks, a cook who cried about nothing. Last, a junior carver — a girl of maybe sixteen, Wren, ink on her fingers and bone dust in her braids, who answered questions about wages and apprenticeship papers in a voice that shook only slightly.

"What do you carve?" Voss asked her.

"Whatever Master Jude gives me to finish. He does the difficult pieces."

"Is he difficult?"

Wren blinked. "He's — kind. He fixed my first attempt instead of throwing it out. Said bone doesn't like being punished." She smiled, quick and helpless. "He says it likes being listened to."

Silence, then. Voss heard it the way she heard everything: completely.

"Thank you," she said. "You'll come with me. Routine verification of apprenticeship records. You'll be returned within the day."

Behind her, she heard Corvane begin a sentence about lawyers and charters, and heard the sentence die, because warrants did not negotiate. The girl looked past her, up the stairs, toward the ledger room — one look, quick as a bird — and then gathered her shawl and went quietly, the way the hunted go when they have learned that quiet is the only coin hunters accept.

* * *

From the top of the stairs, Jude watched the door close on Wren.

He had done everything right. That was the obscene part. He had lied well — better than he'd known he could — and the lie had worked, and because it had worked, the axe had fallen one arm's length away, on a girl who fixed his rough edges and laughed at his terrible jokes and said *bone likes being listened to* because she'd heard him say it and thought it was only craft.

Corvane appeared beside him, watching the street swallow the Inquisitor's party. "You did well," the old man murmured.

"She took Wren."

"She took the least valuable thing in the building." Corvane's voice was gentle, which was how Jude knew to be afraid of it. "That is what audits are for, Jude. They take a little, so they leave convinced there wasn't more to take. By tomorrow the girl will be released with an apology and a crown's coin for her trouble."

Jude said nothing. He was listening — he couldn't stop listening — to the house around him, to the settling timbers, to the hundred small deaths of bone in the walls, and underneath it all to the memory of Wren's one look up the stairs. Not a look of fear. A look of *don't worry about me*, which was worse, which was unbearable, which was a debt being signed in his name.

"She'll talk," he said finally. "Under questioning. About me. She won't mean to, but she talks when she's nervous, she told Voss about the tokens, she—"

"Then we have until tomorrow," Corvane said. "Which is more time than most men get." He put a hand on Jude's shoulder, warm and heavy, the same hand that had fed him, clothed him, taught him the knife. "Go finish your appraisal, boy. The reliquary won't wait, and neither will our buyer."

Jude went. But at the workbench, with the smuggled reliquary sitting wrapped in oilcloth like a sleeping thing, he did not reach for it at once. He sat with his hands flat on the wood and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that he had survived the morning for one reason only: someone kinder than he was had paid his price.

And somewhere across the city, in a room with no windows, Wren was about to learn what the crown paid for answers.

The reliquary waited. Jude unwrapped the oilcloth, because there was nothing else left to do — and the moment his bare skin touched the bone inside, a voice older than any drowned sailor rolled up through his palms like a bell struck underwater, mid-oath, mid-word, saying: *until one of the hearing kind speaks my name freely.*

He had heard the dead beg, curse, and weep.

He had never heard one of them make a bargain.
