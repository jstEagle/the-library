## 5. Corvane's Confession

The Gilded Tooth had never been quiet at night. Jude had grown up believing that a guild house breathed the way a ship did — timbers settling, coin shifting in its coffers, the low murmur of impossible things dreaming in their crates. Tonight it was silent in the way a held breath is silent, and he moved through its corridors knowing the silence was not empty but attentive.

He had taken the founding charter from the iron cabinet on the third landing. He had not planned to. His hands had simply done it, the way his hands always did when bone was near — reaching, hungry to listen.

Now he sat in Corvane's study with the charter unrolled across the desk between them, and the candle burned between them like a small yellow witness, and Corvane watched him with an expression Jude had never seen on that face before.

It was patience. But not the patient kindness of a man waiting for a boy to finish a sentence. This was the patience of a fisherman watching a line go taut.

"You've read it," Corvane said. Not a question.

"Twice." Jude's voice came out steadier than he felt. "The third clause. *And the oath shall be kept by one of the hearing kind, who shall tend the king's bones and keep his name unspoken, until such time as one of the hearing kind speaks his name freely.*" He looked up. "You knew what I was before you ever called me your nephew."

Corvane poured two glasses of plum brandy without asking whether Jude wanted one. He had done that for twenty years — poured without asking, decided without consulting, arranged the world around Jude the way a man arranges furniture. Jude had thought it was care. He was beginning to understand it had been maintenance.

"Sit down, Jude."

"I'm standing."

Something flickered across the guildmaster's face — hurt, maybe, or a very good imitation of it. That was the cruelest part, Jude realized. He could no longer tell the difference.

* * *

Jude had been seven years old when Corvane found him, though found was a generous word. The story, told a hundred times at a hundred suppers, was this: a fever-swept orphan in the tide-flats of Saltgate, carving little animals from chicken bones to sell for bread, and Corvane — young then, ambitious, already gathering the first ledgers of the Gilded Tooth — had seen the boy's work and recognized genius. Had lifted him out of the mud. Had given him a bed above the counting room, an apprenticeship, a name.

*A name.* Jude turned the word over now and heard it differently. Not a gift. An inscription. The way you label a crate.

"I remember the day you came," Corvane said quietly. "You don't, I think. You were half-dead with fever. You'd carved a gull from a knucklebone, and when I picked it up—" He stopped. Swirled the brandy in his glass. "When I picked it up, I heard a gull. Crying over water. A sound from somewhere I have never been." His eyes came up, sharp and old. "Do you know what that is, to a man who trades in impossible things? It is inventory. It is the rarest stock in the world, sitting in the mud, selling trinkets for bread."

"A gull," Jude said. "I was seven. I was crying for my mother, and I didn't know how, so I put it in the bone instead."

"Yes." Corvane set down his glass. "And I bought you."

The word landed like a hook through the cheek. *Bought.* Not rescued. Not adopted. There had been papers — Jude remembered signing something at twelve, his first contract, ink still strange under his fingernails. He had assumed it was apprenticeship papers. He understood now, with a slow cold turning in his stomach, that he had probably signed a bill of sale.

"From whom?"

"The woman who ran the foundling row. Two silver marks and a promise of bread for the other children." Corvane said it flatly, without flinching, because flinching was for men who had something left to protect. "I have never lied to you about money, Jude. I won't start tonight."

"And the oath." Jude pressed his palms flat against the charter, against the faded ink of a king's name he still would not let himself read aloud. "Three hundred years. The war frozen. Every generation, the guild keeps a hearer. Keeps him close. Keeps him *fed*." He laughed, and hated the sound of it. "All those suppers. You fattened me like a calf."

"I educated you."

"You *stocked* me."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The candle guttered and recovered. Somewhere below, the harbor slapped against the pilings, indifferent.

"It's true," Corvane said at last, and Jude felt something in his chest tear along a seam he hadn't known was there — not surprise, because some part of him had known since the reliquary, since the rib-bone sang, but the confirmation, the terrible ordinary weight of it said aloud. "Every word of it. The founder, Aldous Merrin, made the oath himself. He was the dead king's quartermaster, did you know that? Before the war. Before the perjury. He helped bury the truth and then spent the rest of his life trying to bury the war too, and the only way to do both was the oath. And the oath needed a keeper. One of the hearing kind, every generation, raised inside the guild, kept ignorant enough to be safe and skilled enough to be useful."

"Kept ignorant," Jude repeated. "Is that what my childhood was? Ignorance management?"

"It was protection." For the first time Corvane's voice roughened. "Do you know what happens to the hearing kind outside these walls? What happened last week at the Salt Gate? What Voss's hunters are doing right now in the tide-flats? I kept you behind ledgers and appraisals because ledgers are boring, Jude. Boredom is armor. No inquisitor burns a man for filing receipts."

"No," Jude said softly. "They burn the neighbor instead. They take the junior carver. They burn a shelf of tokens while I stand there and watch, and you tell me to hide, and I hide, because you taught me that hiding was love."

He saw it land. He was glad. He was ashamed of being glad.

* * *

The worst of it was not the buying. Jude sat with the brandy untouched in front of him and made himself inventory the whole of it, the way he'd inventory a smuggled reliquary — item by item, unsentimental, honest.

The winter he was nine and sick with lung-rot, Corvane had carried him personally to the physiker in Candle Lane and paid a fee that would have stocked the shop for a year. Jude had always believed that was love. Now he saw the ledger entry beneath it: a bone-speaker is irreplaceable; a bone-speaker must live. The kindness had been real. It had simply been *accounted for.*

The year he was fourteen and carved his first whalebone token — the drowned sailor, the one still filed under Impossible Acquisitions, Lot 1 — Corvane had wept. Actually wept, standing in the workroom with the token cupped in his big scarred hands, listening to a dead man's last words come out of a boy's carving. Jude had believed then that the tears were wonder. Perhaps they had been. But they had also been relief, the way a merchant weeps when a risky cargo comes safe to harbor. *My investment lives. My tool works.*

Even his loneliness had been designed. Jude had trusted no one with the gift — not the other apprentices, not the neighbors, no one — and he had thought that was his own flaw, his own secret shame, a failure of nerve. But who had taught him that secrecy? Who had praised him for discretion, rewarded him for silence, frowned at every friendship that got too close? Twenty years of careful cultivation, and the crop had come in exactly as planted: one man, alone, bound to the guildmaster by gratitude so deep it had passed for family.

"You never once asked me to renew anything," Jude said. "Not until now. Why wait? If I'm the instrument, why leave me in the case for twenty years?"

"Because the oath doesn't need renewal yet." Corvane leaned forward, and the candlelight made his face into something older than the man Jude had loved. "It needs renewal at the solstice, or it needs breaking, and either way it needs a speaker. But more than that—" he hesitated, and Jude watched, fascinated and horrified, as the guildmaster chose honesty like a man choosing which finger to cut off, "—more than that, I wanted you to have a life first. Whatever I am, whatever I did, I did not want you to be nothing but the oath. I wanted you to be a man who happened to be an instrument. Not the other way round."

"That's not mercy," Jude said. "That's aging the wine."

Corvane closed his eyes. When he opened them, all the warmth had been packed away, folded and stored like winter clothes in summer. What remained was the guildmaster. The trader. The man who had never lied about money.

"The witch-hunters are closing, Jude. Voss took Tam Weller yesterday. She'll have him talking within the week, and Tam has seen your workroom. She'll come back here with writs, and she will find the charter, and she will find *you*, and the crown will not use you to renew the oath. They will use you to break it — loudly, publicly, in a way that unfreezes three centuries of war and hands them a holy cause to march behind." He stood, and his shadow fell across the charter, across the king's buried name. "I need you renewed and gone before the solstice. Out of the city. Across the sea if it comes to that. Alive, and silent, and free."

"Free," Jude echoed. The word tasted like the mud of Saltgate. "You mean sold again. To whom this time? Or do I get to see the ledger entry?"

For one heartbeat — Jude would remember this heartbeat all his life — Corvane looked as if he might answer honestly. As if the old warmth might win, the man who had carried a fevered child through the rain to Candle Lane, the man who had wept over a gull carved from a knucklebone.

Then the door of the study opened without a knock, and the junior clerk stood there gray-faced, dripping harbor water, words tumbling out of him in pieces:

"They've got the tide-flats, master. All of them. Inquisitor Voss — she's taken the drowned choir. Every hearer under the flats. And she's asking—" the clerk swallowed, "—she's asking after you by description, Jude. *A carver of the hearing kind.* She knows you exist."

Jude rose slowly. The charter lay open between them, the dead king's name sleeping in its ink, and the candle flame bent toward the door as if leaning to listen.

"They're alive?" Jude asked. "The choir. Voss took them alive."

"For questioning," the clerk whispered.

Jude looked at Corvane. Corvane looked back, and Jude saw the calculation begin behind his eyes — the weighing, the pricing, the old familiar arithmetic of the Gilded Tooth — and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like peace, that whatever happened next, it would happen because he moved first.

He was already reaching for the charter when he said: "Then I know where I'm going."
