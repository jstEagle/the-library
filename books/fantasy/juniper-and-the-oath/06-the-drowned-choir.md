## 6. The Drowned Choir

Jude ran the way he had learned to run as a boy in Corvane's house: silently, with his breath held shallow and his mind already three streets ahead of his feet.

The rain helped. It came down in grey sheets off the harbor, drumming the cobbles of Saltcutter Row until every sound in the city drowned beneath it — the hoofbeats behind him, the shout that had gone up when he slipped out the guild's kitchen door, even his own heartbeat, which he could no longer trust to be only his own. For three days the bone he had touched in the reliquary had been speaking inside him, a king's voice wound through his sleep like wire: *until one of the hearing kind speaks my name freely.* Three days of knowing what he was worth, and to whom.

He cut left at the tannery, where the stink of lime and rotting hides was thick enough to gag a bloodhound, and dropped down the slick stone stairs that were not on any map Corvane owned. The stairs ended at the tide-flats.

At low water, the harbor slums revealed their second city: mudflats stretching black and gleaming toward the channel, stitched with the ribs of wrecked ships, the pilings of piers that had burned a generation ago, the humped backs of hulls the sea had taken and half returned. The mudlarks worked this ground by day, gleaning coins and copper nails from the silt. But the flats held other gleaners too. Everyone in Saltcutter knew the stories and no one said them aloud: that when the tide went far enough out, there were lights under the old breakwater, and voices, and people who had gone into the mud rather than into Voss's wagons.

Jude's boots sank to the ankle. The cold climbed through the leather at once, honest cold, the first honest thing he had felt since the king's rib.

*They will look for you above the waterline,* he told himself. *No one hunts below it.*

The breakwater loomed ahead, a spine of black stone furred with bladderwrack, and beneath its seaward arch the darkness was not empty. He saw the light first — a lamp burning low and green behind horn panes smeared with salt — and then the shapes around it: figures seated on crates and coiled rope, still as herons, and among them children, and among the children an old woman mending a net with fingers that never stopped moving, as if stillness itself were dangerous.

A man rose from the group before Jude was within twenty paces. He was broad and weathered, missing two fingers on his right hand, and he carried a boathook the way a soldier carries a spear.

"That's far enough," the man said. "State your business or state your name, and if you lie about either we'll know."

"You'll know," Jude repeated. His voice came out cracked. "You're hearers."

The word moved through the shelter under the arch like wind through wheat. Faces turned. A child stopped chewing. The old woman's needle paused for the first time.

"We're nothing," the man said carefully. "We're mudlarks. We're widows. We're nobody."

"I'm a bone-carver," Jude said. "I keep ledgers for the Gilded Tooth. And three days ago I heard a dead king say my whole life was built on top of a lock, and I've been running ever since." He pulled the whalebone token from his coat — the drowned sailor's last words, carved in his own hand, the first thing they would find if Voss caught him — and held it up so the green lamplight caught the grain. "I can prove what I am. I just don't know anymore whose side proof puts me on."

Silence, except the sea working at the stones.

Then the old woman spoke. "Let him in, Brannoc. He's soaked to the marrow and shaking like a struck bell. You don't shake like that unless you've heard something true and terrible."

Brannoc lowered the boathook slowly. "Come," he said. "And walk where I walk. The mud has opinions about strangers."

* * *

Under the breakwater the air smelled of tar, kelp, and lamp oil, and it was warmer than it had any right to be — heat rising through the stone from some old collapsed flue, the sea's own furnace. They gave him a blanket that smelled of smoke and someone else's life, and a tin cup of broth made from gull and kelp that he drank because refusing kindness seemed suddenly obscene.

There were eleven of them. Brannoc, who had been a wherryman until the day he heard his drowned brother singing in the mooring ropes and answered aloud, in front of witnesses. Sefa, a midwife whose gift lived in her palms and who had felt a dead woman's gratitude pass through them during a birth that should have killed them both. The old woman was Maren; she had been hearing the dead for sixty years and hiding it for fifty-nine, and she looked at Jude the way one jeweler looks at another's work.

"Corvane's boy," she said, when he gave his name. "Oh, don't flinch. We've known about you for years. The Gilded Tooth buys quiet children with careful hands, and everyone below the waterline knows why, even if the children don't."

"He told me I was family."

"Did he?" Maren's needle resumed its work. "Or did he tell you things that were *like* family, the way a cage is like a nest?"

Jude set down the cup. "Then tell me what you know. All of it. Because all I have is a king's voice saying an oath, and a charter in Corvane's vault older than the guild, and a feeling like the floor of the world is thinner than anyone admits."

Maren looked at him for a long moment, and then at Brannoc, and Brannoc shrugged — *he's here now* — and the old woman folded her mending at last.

"Then listen," she said, "because you're the first of the hearing kind to come to us freely in thirty years, and the dead have been trying to give you this message since before you were born."

She began, as all their histories began, with the war — the War of the Two Courts, the Fratricide, the three centuries of frozen enmity that every schoolchild learned: how King Aldric the Drowned had died at the mouth of this very harbor, betrayed and slain, and how his death had so enraged the dead of both nations that the living courts, terrified, had sworn the Great Truce — sealed, the priests taught, by the king's own dying forgiveness.

"That last part is the lie," Maren said. "We know because our dead told us. That's the thing your Inquisitors have never understood, may their gods keep them ignorant: the dead are terrible liars but excellent witnesses. They remember. And the ones who died in that war — the ones still standing in the mud where they fell, because no one ever laid them properly to rest — they remember what actually happened."

"The king didn't forgive," Brannoc said quietly. He had taken up a whetstone and a knife and was working the blade without looking at it, the way men do when words cost them. "My grandfather's grandfather was there. A common soldier of the harbor levy. When Aldric fell, the king's voice went into the water and everything with ears in this harbor heard it. Not forgiveness. Accusation. He named the men who arranged his death, and they weren't enemies. They were his own councilors, and the envoys of the court that signed the truce a week later. The war wasn't started by his death, carver. The war was started by the perjury that covered it up. Both courts swore a false peace on a murdered man's grave, and the dead refused to be silent about it, and so the living did the only thing left to them."

"They buried the truth alive," Sefa said, "and put a lock on the grave. An oath. *Until one of the hearing kind speaks my name freely.* Not a curse. A condition. Someone wanted a door left open — someone on the inside of the conspiracy, maybe, someone whose conscience rotted slower than the rest. The oath doesn't keep the peace. It keeps the *silence*. The peace is just what silence looks like from above the waterline."

Jude sat very still. Around him the lamp hissed and the sea gnawed the breakwater, and somewhere beneath the mud — he felt it now, unmistakably, the way you feel a tooth you've lost — hundreds of voices waited, patient as sediment.

"Three hundred years," he said. "Every treaty, every trade law, every border — all of it standing on a lie that one sentence could topple. And the guild—"

"—was built to guard the lock," Maren finished. "Impossible things, yes? That's what the Gilded Tooth trades in. Do you understand now what the most impossible thing in its vaults is? A bone-speaker. Someone who could renew the oath when it thins, generation after generation, and never be told why. Corvane didn't raise a son, Jude. He raised a key. And keys get used, and then they get kept somewhere dark so they can't turn themselves."

The blanket had stopped warming him. Or perhaps he had stopped noticing. "If I renew it, the lie holds. If I break it—" He thought of the reliquary, the rib warm under his thumb, the voice like a blade being drawn. "The war comes back. Thirty years of hunters burning hearers because the crown is terrified of exactly this moment."

"No," Maren said, and her voice changed — softened, and sharpened, both at once. "That's what they *told* you the choice was. Two doors, both dreadful, so you'll stand paralyzed between them until they come collect you. But the dead have been whispering under this mud for three centuries, child, and they've had time to think. They don't want the war back. They want the *witnessing*. There's a difference, and it's the width of the world. A war is what happens when the truth stays buried and festers. What they want is to stand up in front of both courts — the living ones, the guilty ones — and say what was done to them, and be heard. All they've ever needed is a voice loud enough and a room full enough."

"And the price of that voice," Brannoc said, not unkindly, "is usually the hearer who lends it."

The lamp guttered. Outside, the tide had turned; Jude could feel it in his bones before he heard it, a long slow exhale of the sea beginning to take its ground back. Somewhere above them, in the city of the living, Voss's wagons would be rolling, and Corvane would be composing whatever gentle lie he'd tell the hunters about his missing carver, and the solstice was coming whether any of them willed it or not.

But here — Jude looked around the low green light at the mudlark and the midwife and the old woman with her endless net, at the children asleep against the warm stone, at the eleven hunted people who had heard his confession and neither flinched nor reached for a wagon — here, for the first time since the whalebone token had first spoken to him at nine years old, he was not alone with it.

Belonging, he thought, was not something you were given. It was something you were *admitted to*, the way the sea admits the river: by arriving at the same place from a long way off.

"Rest," Maren said, reading him as easily as he read bone. "Sleep while the water's high. Because at dawn the tide goes out, and the choir under the flats has been waiting thirty years to meet you properly — and Jude, they have something to show you. Something they've saved. The king's true name. The one that was struck from every record, the one that isn't in Corvane's charter, the one that will make the oath shatter like ice in spring."

She smiled, and it was not entirely a comforting smile.

"They only ask one thing in return. That when you speak it — you speak it *loudly*, and in front of everyone who lied."
