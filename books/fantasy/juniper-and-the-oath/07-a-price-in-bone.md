## 7. A Price in Bone

Serane Voss had learned long ago that the tide kept secrets the way a miser kept coin: grudgingly, and only until someone pried.

She stood on the mudflats below the harbor slums as dawn came up gray and salt-stained, her boots sinking into silt that smelled of rot and old iron, and watched the water withdraw from the drowned quarter like a hand pulling back from a wound. Beneath the pilings of the collapsed fish-market, where no honest person had reason to go and every dishonest one had every reason, the outcasts were waking.

Her hunters moved through the fog in pairs, quiet as gulls. She had given them one instruction, repeated three times the night before: *no blades unless they are used first.* The crown wanted the hearing kind alive now. That was new. That was wrong. She had not yet let herself think about why it was wrong, because thinking about it felt like standing at the edge of a frozen pond and hearing the ice speak.

"There," murmured Brannic, her second, nodding toward a gap between two leaning pilings where lamplight bled amber into the mist. "Singing again."

It was singing, of a kind. Not with throats — she had understood that much by the third night of listening from the rooftops above the flats. The sound came from somewhere behind the air itself, a low braided murmur like wind through a ship's rigging, like a hundred voices remembering the same song at slightly different moments. The locals called them the drowned choir. The bounty notices called them *the hearing kind, unregistered practitioners of forbidden arts.* The notices did not mention that most of them were children when they'd been driven from their homes, or that the oldest among them looked to be perhaps forty, or that they had hidden under the city for years without harming so much as a fisherman's nets.

Voss had burned hearers before. She had stood in village squares with her seal-hot iron and read the charges aloud, and she had believed every word, because believing was the load-bearing wall of her life and she had never once tested it.

"Take them gently," she said. "All of them."

Brannic glanced at her. He had served under her for six years and knew that gentleness was not in her vocabulary, and that its sudden appearance meant something had shifted beneath the floorboards of her certainty. He said nothing. He was a smart man. Smart men survived her.

They went down into the drowned quarter in a crescent, twelve hunters through the fog, and the choir's song faltered as the first lanterns found them — huddled figures rising from nests of rope and driftwood, blinking, some of them clutching small carved things to their chests. Bone tokens. Dozens of them, strung on cords, tucked into belts, cradled like infants. Each one, Voss knew, held a dead voice. Each one was evidence. Each one was also, she was beginning to suspect, something else entirely: a kindness. A way of letting the silenced finish saying what they'd been killed for saying.

An old woman stepped forward to meet them, gray-haired, barefoot in the muck, with the calm of someone who had been expecting this visit for years and had already made her peace with it.

"You're the Voss woman," she said. "The one who took the cooper's boy off Tanner's Row."

"I am Inquisitor Serane Voss of the Crown's Commission," Voss said, and heard how the words sat in her mouth like coins from a foreign country. "You'll come quietly. All of you. No one needs to be hurt."

"No one ever needs to be hurt," the old woman said. "That's what makes it possible, isn't it? People keep doing it anyway." She looked past Voss, at the fog, at the city stacked above them in tiers of slate and smoke. "You've come for a bone-speaker. We don't have one. We're just listeners. The speaker is the one they want, and he isn't ours."

*He.* The word landed in Voss's chest and stayed there, cold as a swallowed stone. She had not told anyone outside the Commission that the sealed addendum to her orders specified a single priority target — *one of the hearing kind capable of speech with the sovereign dead, to be delivered alive and unmarked to the Palace Undercroft before solstice.* She had assumed the choir would scatter and deny. Instead the old woman was handing her the shape of the truth like a fishmonger handing over the day's catch.

"You know who I'm looking for," Voss said.

"We know what you're looking *for him for*," the old woman said. "And so will you, soon enough. Read your orders, Inquisitor. All of them. Even the parts written in the hand that isn't yours to question." She extended her wrists. "Well? We haven't got all morning, and neither have you, though you don't know that yet either."

They took thirty-one of the drowned choir that morning. It was, by the standards of the hunt, a bloodless triumph. Voss signed the manifest herself, in her precise clerk's hand, and hated the handwriting while she did it.

* * *

The Commission's floating prison was a converted grain hulk moored off the customs pier, and Voss walked its wet corridors that evening with the prisoners' tokens confiscated in a locked chest under her arm, feeling like a woman carrying stolen letters she hadn't yet worked up the courage to open.

In the wardroom, a courier waited for her — a palace man, silk at his collar, rain still beaded on his shoulders. He handed over a document heavy with wax and heavier with implication.

"From the Lord Auditor's office," he said. "Regarding your promotion. Congratulations are premature but anticipated."

She broke the seal alone.

The first page was what she expected: commendation, elevation to High Inquisitor, a stipend, a house in the Hill Quarter with a view of the harbor she had spent eleven years keeping safe. Her name rendered in gold leaf. She read it twice and felt nothing, which frightened her more than fear would have.

The second page was different. Different paper — older, cream-colored, soft at the edges. Different hand — not the Lord Auditor's crisp bureaucratic script but something older, formal, written by a person accustomed to signing deaths. And it bore a seal she had seen exactly once before, pressed into the cover of a book in her training years that novices were permitted to look at but never to borrow: the triple-crown sigil of the Palace Undercroft, the archive beneath the archive, where documents went when the truth they contained became inconvenient.

*Addendum to Commission Orders, by authority of the Office of the Undercroft:*

*The prisoner known as the bone-speaker is to be delivered alive, unharmed, and unaware of his destination. Upon arrival he is to be presented to the crypt of the Founding King, there to perform the Rite of Renewal as prescribed in the charter of the Gilded Tooth, witnessed by officers of the crown.*

*Should the bone-speaker refuse, or prove incapable, he is to be retained in the Undercroft indefinitely. His gift is a state instrument. Instruments are maintained; they are not freed.*

*Note well: the oath currently in force has held the cessation of hostilities for three hundred years. Its lapse coincides with the death of the last speaker. The crown does not seek to end the hearing kind. The crown seeks to own the only one whose voice can hold back the war.*

*No copy of this addendum exists outside this page. Burn after reading.*

Voss read it four times.

Then she sat down on the wardroom bench, which creaked under her like a confession, and stared at the lantern flame until her eyes watered.

Three hundred years. Every schoolchild in the realm could recite the catechism: the wicked king died, his enemies rejoiced, and the wise founders of the guild swore peace upon his grave, and the war froze like a river in winter, and the crown had kept the vigil ever since. She had recited it herself at seven years old, hand on heart, in a schoolroom that smelled of chalk and tallow. She had built her whole life on top of that story the way the drowned quarter was built on top of the mudflats — level by level, never asking what the ground was made of.

But the addendum didn't say *peace*. It said *cessation of hostilities*, which was a clerk's way of saying the war hadn't ended at all. It had been *held*. Frozen mid-scream, mid-charge, mid-grief, by an oath renewed generation after generation by a chain of bone-speakers bought, bred, or captured — and the last link in that chain had died, and the crown was not hunting the hearing kind to destroy them.

It was hunting them to find a replacement lock.

And the old woman on the flats had known. Had looked at Voss with pity, of all the terrible expressions available to a prisoner, and told her to read her orders.

"Inquisitor?" Brannic stood in the doorway, rain dripping off him. "The prisoners are secured. The, ah. The cooper's boy's mother came to the pier. We turned her away, per protocol." He hesitated. "She asked if her son was warm enough. I didn't know what to tell her."

"Tell her yes," Voss said, and heard the lie come out of her smooth as oil, and understood suddenly, viscerally, what it cost the people who lied to her professionally — the smugglers, the hearers, the guildmasters with their ledgers of impossible things. Lying was a tax levied on everyone the truth endangered.

She looked down at the addendum. *Burn after reading.*

Instead, she folded it along its original creases and slid it inside her breastplate, against her heart, where it lay like a splinter working its way toward something vital.

"Brannic. The interrogation schedule for tomorrow — cancel the first session."

"The boy? The cooper's boy?" He frowned. "The Undercroft wants transcripts by week's end—"

"The Undercroft," Voss said slowly, testing each word like a step on rotten decking, "wants a great many things, and has been very careful about which ones it writes down. Cancel the session. And send word to the Gilded Tooth — a routine follow-up on their tax audit. Nothing alarming. I want to know if a carver named Jude Villalobos is still sleeping under Corvane's roof."

Brannic's eyebrows rose a fraction. "You think the bone-speaker's there?"

"I think," Voss said, rising, taking her cloak from its hook, feeling the paper shift against her sternum with every breath, "that I have spent eleven years hunting liars, and I have just discovered I work for the best of them. Which means everyone I've caught is worth looking at again — starting with the ones the guildmaster is most anxious to convince me are harmless."

Outside, the fog had thickened until the harbor lamps hung in the dark like drowned stars. Somewhere beneath the tide-flats, in cells that smelled of grain dust and mildew, thirty-one listeners sat in silence with their confiscated voices locked in a chest, waiting.

And somewhere across the black water, in a guildhouse full of carved bone and older lies, a young man who could hear the dead was running out of time — and Serane Voss, High Inquisitor designate, hunter of the hearing kind, servant of the lie, was about to become the first witch-hunter in three centuries to ask the dead king's pardon.

She pulled up her hood and walked into the fog.
