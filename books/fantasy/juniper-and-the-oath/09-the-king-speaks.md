## 9. The King Speaks

The crypt beneath the Gilded Tooth had no roof the sun could reach, but Jude felt dawn anyway — a pressure behind his eyes, a thinning of the dark, as if three hundred years of held breath were finally being let out through every crack in the stone.

The oath was broken. He had carved the name — *Aldric Vane, called the Buried King* — into the old rib-bone with a burin shaking so badly he'd nearly ruined the letters, and then he had spoken it aloud, freely, before Corvane's white face and the assembled guild and the king's own seated bones. The sound the oath made when it shattered was not loud. It was the opposite of loud: a silence so total it rang, like the moment after a bell is struck, stretched out forever.

And now the war was waking.

He could feel it in his teeth. The frozen thing beneath the city — the war that had been held still for three centuries like a fly in amber — was flexing its wings.

"Jude." Corvane's voice came from somewhere above him, small and strange, stripped of all its ledger-room authority. "What have you done."

"What you raised me to do," Jude said. He did not look up from the bone in his hands. "Just not the way you wanted."

The crypt floor was weeping. He could hear it — actual water, black and mineral-sweet, seeping up between flagstones that had been dry since the founding. And under the water, under the stone, under everything: voices. Thousands of them. The dead of both armies, the dead of the burning years, rising out of their long compulsion like divers surfacing, gasping.

They were saying his name. *Bone-speaker. Bone-speaker.* Not with gratitude. With hunger. Three hundred years of silenced testimony, and every ghost in the ground wanted a mouth.

Jude pressed the rib-bone to his sternum and stood.

"I need the courts," he said. "Both of them. Before nightfall."

* * *

Inquisitor Serane Voss had spent eleven years learning that certainty was a weapon, and she had sharpened hers daily. Now it lay broken at her feet on the tide-flats, along with her sealed orders, which she had read twice by lantern light and once more by memory in the dark:

*Deliver the bone-speaker alive to the Lord Chancellor's faction. The gift must not be permitted to speak publicly. Terminate all other hearing kind.*

Not *capture*. *Terminate.* She had read the word four times before understanding settled into her stomach like swallowed lead. The crown was not hunting the gift because it was dangerous. The crown was hunting it because it was *true*, and truth, unmanaged, was the one thing the Chancellor's faction could not survive.

She had let them take the drowned choir anyway. Eleven hearers, bound and gagged with waxed cord, loaded onto a wagon like salted fish, while she stood in the mud holding orders that told her exactly what would happen to them at the Chancellery. She had told herself she would find a way to intervene. She had watched the wagon wheels cut ruts in the wet sand and known she was lying to herself.

Then the ground had shuddered.

It was not an earthquake. Voss had been in earthquakes; they shook buildings and rattled teeth and passed. This did not pass. This rose — a long, low groan out of the bedrock of the city, out of the hills, out of the harbor itself, where the water went suddenly glass-still and then began, impossibly, to recede. Every hunter in her company stopped moving. One of the bound hearers began to laugh through her gag, high and hysterical and joyful.

"The oath," the woman said when they tore the gag free. "Someone broke the oath. Oh, saints, someone finally—"

Voss left her hunters standing in the draining mud and ran for the Gilded Tooth.

* * *

By noon, half the city knew something had happened, though no two of them agreed what. Bells rang without ringers. Dogs howled in perfect unison and then fell silent all together, as if listening. In the Salt Gate square, the great bronze bell that had not sounded since the founding tolled once, by itself, and the note it struck was lower than any bell should go — a note people felt in their bowels rather than heard.

Corvane got the courts there first. That, Jude had to grant him. Whatever else the man was — buyer of children, keeper of oaths, betrayer of everything soft in himself in service of a peace built on graves — he understood power, and he understood that whatever came next would be decided in front of witnesses or not decided at all. By midday the Salt Gate square held the Lord Chancellor's faction in their grey silk, the Old Court loyalists in their mothballed ermine, the guildmasters, the harbor clergy, and a crowd of common folk who had climbed onto rooftops and cranes and each other's shoulders because when the dead start walking, everyone wants to see.

Jude walked out onto the gate's broad stone landing with the king's rib against his chest and his hearing going out of him like a tide.

That was the cost. He had known it since the crypt, since the moment the oath shattered and the gift poured out of him into the open air like light from a cracked lamp. Every word he pulled from the dead now cost him a piece of the sense itself. He had perhaps hours. Perhaps fewer. He intended to spend all of them.

"Bone-speaker." The Lord Chancellor's voice carried beautifully; it was trained to. Lord Hesperin Mault was a narrow man with a narrow face and the patience of a man who had waited thirty years for this day. "You have committed treason against the peace of three centuries. Surrender the relic and yourself, and the court may be merciful."

"The peace," Jude said, and his voice cracked on the second word, because the dead were already pressing in against his ears, thousands deep, patient and terrible. "Whose peace? Built on whose silence?"

He lifted the rib-bone high.

"You want to know what started the war? Not his death." He pointed down, through the stone, through the earth, to the vault where Aldric Vane's bones sat in their lead-lined coffin. "His death was the *end* of the beginning. You know what started it? A signature. A perjury. Two courts swearing before witnesses that the king had renounced his claim freely — when in truth he never signed anything, and the men who swore it had already divided his harbors between them."

Murmuring, spreading like fire in dry grass. Mault's face did not change, which was itself an answer.

"Lies," Mault said pleasantly. "The ravings of a criminal gift."

"Then you won't mind a witness."

Jude set the rib-bone on the stone at his feet, knelt, and put both hands flat against the gate's ancient rock. He reached down with the last of his hearing, down past the roots of the city, and found the voice he had first heard in a smuggler's reliquary — dry, formal, furious, waiting three hundred years for exactly this.

*Aldric Vane,* he called. *King in the earth. They are gathered. Speak your name freely — I have spoken it for you. Now speak your truth.*

And the dead king rose.

* * *

Serane Voss arrived at the square in time to see the stones of the Salt Gate begin to glow.

Not burn — glow, a pale gold light rising out of the rock like heat off summer pavement, and out of that light a figure assembled itself, joint by joint, the way frost forms on glass. A tall man in armor three centuries out of fashion, translucent as smoke over a candle, standing where Jude knelt. The crowd's screaming lasted ten seconds and then stopped all at once, because the dead king raised one hand and the silence he commanded was absolute.

Voss had spent her life hunting the hearing kind. She had told herself it was heresy she fought, corruption, the foul traffic of souls. Standing in the crowd with her hunters around her and her sealed orders folded against her heart, she finally admitted what she had known since the tide-flats: she had been sent to murder witnesses.

The dead king spoke. His voice was not sound so much as weather — it moved through the square, through bodies, through bone.

*"I never renounced my claim. I never signed the Accord of Ashes. I was dying of poison when the chancellors of that age came to my deathbed and asked me to bless their division of my kingdom, and I refused, and they swore before the assembled courts that I had consented. Here are their names."*

And he gave them. Names that meant nothing to the crowd and everything to six families in it — names still carved on chancellery doors, still printed in law books, still worn as rings by men in grey silk. Voss watched Lord Hesperin Mault go the color of tallow.

*"My war was never about conquest. It was about this lie. And your peace — your beautiful frozen peace — is that lie, preserved in ice, renewed every generation by a child bought and bound to keep it."*

Every head in the square turned toward Corvane.

The guildmaster did not flinch. He stood on the guild's platform with his hands folded, an old man facing the sum of his life, and when he spoke his voice was steady. "I kept the peace. Whatever it cost. Ask any soul in this square whether they'd rather have had three hundred years of war instead. I'll answer for it. I always intended to answer for it."

"And the children?" Jude's voice, hoarse, from the stones. "The ones you bought. The ones you burned tokens in front of. Was that the peace too?"

Corvane looked at him for a long moment. "Yes," he said simply. "Everything was the peace. That is what an oath is, boy. I thought you'd learned that by now."

*"Enough,"* said the dead king, and even the wind obeyed. *"I do not ask for my war back. I ask for the truth entered into the record of the living. Let the courts judge the perjury. Let the heirs of the signatories answer. Let the hearing kind be freed from hunt and hiding alike. Grant me this, and I will lie down again — and take my armies' anger with me."*

Silence. Then Lord Mault, smooth as ever: "The court will consider these—"

"No," said Serane Voss.

She stepped out of the crowd, and behind her, one by one, her hunters stepped out too — twenty-two of them, crossbows slung, faces grim, forming a line between the Chancellor's faction and the glowing stones. Voss drew the sealed orders from her coat and held them up.

"Inquisitor's warrant, countersigned by the Chancellery. *Terminate all other hearing kind.* Read it aloud, someone. Read it to the square. These are the men who froze the truth for three hundred years, and these—" she turned the paper so the crowd could see the seal, "—are the orders they gave me last week. While your peace still held. Ask yourselves what they planned for the rest of you."

Mault's composure broke at last, just at the edges, like ice underfoot.

* * *

Jude heard almost none of what followed.

The gift was leaving him in sheets now, like snow sliding off a roof. The dead king's voice thinned to a whisper, then to a pressure, then to nothing at all — and with it went the drowned sailor, the whalebone tokens, the choir of the tide-flats, the whole vast murmuring country of the dead that had been Jude's only constant companion since childhood. He knelt on the warm stone and listened to it go, and found, to his surprise, that he was weeping — not from grief alone but from a grief so tangled with relief he couldn't pull the threads apart.

The last thing he heard clearly was the king himself, faint as a draft under a door:

*"Well done, bone-speaker. Rest now. You have earned what I never could."*

Then silence. True silence, total, ordinary, human silence — the first of his life.

When he looked up, the light was gone from the stones, the square was in uproar, Voss's hunters were marching the Chancellor's faction toward the courts under arrest, and Corvane was gone from the platform — slipped away in the chaos, or perhaps simply finished, an instrument setting itself down.

Jude got slowly to his feet, deaf to the roaring crowd, deaf to everything, and put his hand flat one last time on the stone where the king had stood.

Nothing. Warm stone, cooling.

He smiled, and it hurt, and he began the long walk down out of the square to find out what a man built after the end of his gift — and whether the guildmaster, somewhere in the fleeing crowds, had left the ledgers where Jude could reach them. Because the ledgers were evidence now. The ledgers were everything.

And someone would come for them before nightfall.
