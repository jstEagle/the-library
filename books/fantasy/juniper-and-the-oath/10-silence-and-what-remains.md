## 10. Silence, and What Remains

The first winter after the oath broke, Jude learned to sleep.

It sounds like nothing — a small domestic victory, the kind no chronicler bothers to record — but for a man who had spent thirty years falling asleep to the murmur of the dead, it was a country he had never visited. The silence came in layers, the way the tide goes out. First the great voices vanished: the buried king, whose testimony had ended three centuries of frozen war and very nearly started a new one in the doing. Then the middling dead, the merchants and murderers and drowned sailors whose last words Jude had carried in whalebone and ivory for half his life. And at the end, so gradually he could not name the day it finished, the whispering itself — that faint tide-sound beneath every bone he touched, the gift that had been his loneliness and his livelihood and his secret, all three at once.

He kept one token on the shelf above his workbench. A rib of ash wood, carved in the shape of a bone but never of bone, because he could not bear to hold real bone anymore and hear nothing. It was not grief exactly. It was more like the ache in a hand that has held an oar for years and finally lets go: the ghost of the weight, the water still moving where the blade had been.

On quiet mornings he would take it down anyway, close his eyes, and press it to his ear as if it were a shell.

Nothing. Blessed, terrible nothing.

* * *

The Gilded Tooth reopened in spring, though it was no longer quite the Gilded Tooth. Corvane's ledgers — all forty-one volumes of impossible things, each entry a price paid for something that should never have been sold — were carried up from the vaults and set out on long tables in the guild hall's main room, open to any page, under glass, with lamps burning beside them day and night. Jude had argued for burning them. Voss had argued for locking them in the crown archive, where sealed orders went to be forgotten. In the end they compromised on light, which satisfied neither of them and was probably correct.

The school took the rest of the building. There were benches now where the appraisal tables had stood, and chalkboards where the ledger of debts had hung, and children — some of them hearing-kind, most of them not — learning the history that had been carved out of the chronicles like rot cut from a beam. They learned about the war that hadn't started with a king's death. They learned about the perjury of two living courts, the oath sworn over a silenced grave, the three hundred years of peace that had been, in truth, a held breath. They learned that the Gilded Tooth had traded in impossible things, and that the most impossible thing it ever traded was a boy.

Jude taught the senior class himself, three mornings a week. He taught them carving — honest carving, joinery and inlay, the craft stripped of its sorcery — and he taught them listening, which was different now. The hearing-kind among them could still feel the dead the way you feel weather through a wall: pressure, presence, the sense of someone standing just behind the plaster. But none of them could make out words anymore. Whatever Jude had done in the crypt, when he gave the king back his voice before both assembled courts, he had spent the gift like a coin snapped in half. What remained in the world was echo without language. The dead were present, but they could not testify.

Which was, he supposed, the point. No one should have to buy silence again.

* * *

Voss came on the first of every month, punctual as rent.

She arrived without her hunters now, in plain grey wool instead of the crown's white-and-ash, and she always entered through the trade door rather than the front, as if she were still ashamed to be seen coming here — or perhaps as if she feared being seen enjoying it. She brought documents. She read aloud to the students from the depositions taken during the reckoning, her voice flat and precise, stripping the horror down to its bones so the children could examine the structure underneath. She was merciless about dates. She made them memorize the names of the drowned choir, all eleven, including the ones who hadn't survived the flats.

"You don't have to do this," Jude told her once, in the corridor outside the hall, watching her straighten her collar before going in. "No one appointed you."

"No one did." She smoothed a crease that wasn't there. "That's rather the difficulty, isn't it? The law failed. The courts perjured themselves, the guild profited, the crown hunted children to protect a lie. I spent nine years believing the law was the shape of justice." She looked at him then, and there was something in her face he had never seen during the hunt — not softness, nothing so easy, but the tired honesty of a woman who had audited her own soul and found discrepancies. "So now I keep the ledgers myself. Someone has to remember what was owed, even if it can never be repaid."

"The choir families received their settlements," Jude said. "The crown paid—"

"The crown paid what the crown decided the dead were worth." Her mouth thinned. "I've read enough ledgers to know a figure chosen for the payer's comfort when I see one. That's why I come. Not because I'm your patron." She said the word *patron* the way other people said *leech*. "Because every month I sit in that room and listen to what we did, so that I will never be able to say I didn't know."

She went in. Through the doorway Jude heard her begin: *"Deposition of Maren Oduya, tide-hearer, taken the fourth of Harvestmoon..."* and the room went still around her voice, thirty children leaning forward into the true history of their city.

He stood in the corridor a moment longer, in the smell of chalk and lamp oil, and let himself feel it — not happiness, nothing so clean, but the particular gravity of a thing built right. Peace earned, not granted. Paid for. He ran his thumb along the doorframe, along the grain of old oak, and heard only wood.

* * *

In autumn, a girl came to the trade door with a bundle wrapped in oilcloth.

She was perhaps fourteen, harbor-born, with salt-cracked hands and the wary posture of someone who had learned to flinch at white cloaks. She set the bundle on the counter and unwrapped it without a word: a whalebone token, old, the carving worn nearly smooth, worked in a style Jude recognized because he had invented it — the deep-cut spiral, the lip of bone left thin enough to carry a voice.

"Where did you get this?"

"My gran. She said—" The girl stopped, swallowed. "She said it used to sing. Before. She said if the quiet ever lifted, I was to bring it to the man who broke the king."

Jude turned the token over in his hands. His own early work, or something very like it; he could see the young carver's hesitation in the uncertain depth of the cuts. A drowned sailor's last words, maybe. A mother's name spoken into bone by someone who loved her. He pressed it to his ear, out of habit older than thought.

Silence. Of course. The same blessed, terrible nothing.

But the girl was watching him with an expression he knew from the inside — the look of someone who carries a sound no one else can hear and has begun to wonder if she is mad. And when she spoke again, her voice dropped low, the way his own had dropped for thirty years, the way you learn to speak when you are afraid of being overheard by the wrong living person:

"It's not gone for everyone." Her eyes were wet and fierce. "My gran said the king took the words away when he testified. Took them from everyone, everywhere, so no one could ever be bought or hunted for them again. But my gran also said—" she steadied herself against the counter, "—she said oaths break, and broken things mend crooked. She said there are places where the quiet is thin. She said she heard her sister, once, at the ebb of the lowest tide of the year, down past the old flats. Just one word. Just her name."

Jude set the token down very carefully, the way you set down something that might be a weapon.

The solstice ebb was six weeks away. The lowest tide in nineteen years, the almanacs agreed. Down past the old flats, where the drowned choir had hidden, where the truth had surfaced once already like a body refusing to stay sunk.

"Tell me your name," he said.

"Maren," the girl said. "Maren Oduya. My gran was Maren too."

Of course she was. The dead had always kept better ledgers than the living.

Jude looked past her, through the open trade door, toward the harbor — gray water, gray sky, and somewhere beneath it, thin as a hairline crack in glaze, a silence that might not be total after all. Behind him, Voss's flat voice recited deposition after deposition to a room full of children. Ahead of him, the tide was preparing to go out farther than it had gone in anyone's lifetime.

Some silences, he thought, are endings.

And some are held breath.

"We'll need warm clothes," he said, reaching for his coat. "And we leave before dawn."
