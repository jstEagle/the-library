## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Years

The widow's grief weighed about as much as a wet wool coat.

That was how I thought of memories — in weights and textures, the way a fishmonger thinks of his catch. Grief was heavy and damp and clung to everything it touched. Joy was lighter but slippery; it always tried to slide back to its owner mid-extraction, like a cat that didn't want to be held. Love was the worst. Love had hooks in it. You could pull it out clean if you were very good, and I was very good, but you'd carry the hook-marks on your fingers for days afterward, little stinging crescents that no salve would touch.

I sat across from Maren Ostrow in her kitchen while her daughter-in-law paced the yard outside, and I held the grief in both hands like something living, because in a way it was.

"Tell me again what you want gone," I said. "All of it, or just the worst of it?"

Maren's hands were folded on the table the way old women fold their hands when they've decided to be brave about something. Her knuckles were big as walnuts. She'd been a handsome woman once; grief had been eating her face for eleven months, and it had gotten through most of the soft parts.

"All of it," she said. Then, quickly: "Not him. Not remembering him. Just — the part where he isn't there anymore."

"That's not how it works." I kept my voice gentle. People always wanted surgery without scars. "The missing is attached to the man. I can take the sharp edge off. The mornings where you reach for him. The sound of the house at night. I can't leave you your husband and delete his dying. He comes as a whole or he goes as a whole."

She looked at me the way they all looked at me at that point in the conversation — like I was a priest who'd just told them their god took payment in teeth.

"And then I won't be sad?"

"You'll be sad," I said. "You'll just not know why. It'll sit in you like weather. Some people find that worse. Some people find it's the only mercy there is."

Outside, the daughter-in-law stopped pacing. Through the window I could see her watching us, arms crossed, jaw set. She was the one paying. That was usually how it went — the family couldn't stand watching the widowed one waste away, so they bought the cure whether the patient wanted it or not. I'd learned not to ask too hard whose grief it actually was. In the Salt Valley, everything belonged to whoever could pay for it, including the past.

"I want it gone," Maren said, quieter now. "All of it. Please."

* * *

Here is how theft works, in case you've never had anything taken from you, which would make you either very lucky or very young.

A memory isn't stored in the head the way coins are stored in a purse. It's more like salt dissolved in water. You can't grab it. But you can make the water want to give it up. There's a trick to it — a pressure, a pull, a kind of listening — and when you do it right, the memory condenses out of the person like frost forming on glass, and you catch it before it melts back in.

I caught Maren's grief the way I'd caught hundreds of others. Two fingers to her temple, my eyes closed, my breath slowed down to match hers until we were breathing the same air in the same rhythm, which is the door everything walks through. And then the weight came into my hands, invisible and enormous, and I drew it out slow, slow, slow, because yanking is how you tear someone.

It took the better part of an hour. When it was done, Maren sat blinking at her kitchen table like a woman waking from a long nap, and her face — I watched this happen, I always watch — her face smoothed over. Not happy. Emptied. The lines around her mouth were still there, carved by eleven months of mourning, but the thing that had made them was gone, and now they were just lines, archaeology without the civilization.

"Oh," she said. She touched her own cheek, found it dry, and seemed surprised. "Oh, it's raining? I thought I heard rain."

"It rained last night," I said.

"Did it." She smiled at me, polite, vacant, a hostess greeting a stranger. "Can I get you some tea?"

The daughter-in-law came in and paid me in salt-script — the valley's currency, stamped with the seal of the Saltworks Guild, good nowhere else in the world, which was rather the point. Forty marks. A fair price for a husband's death, extracted from the woman least able to pay it. I counted it twice, tucked it away, and did not look at Maren on my way out, because looking is how thieves get caught, and I have never once been caught.

* * *

People think the Salt Valley got its name from the white flats south of town, the pans where nothing grows and the horizon dissolves into glare. They're wrong, mostly. The name is older than the pans. The valley is called Salt because salt is dead ground — nothing takes root in it, nothing feeds on it, and nothing magical can live in it either. Magic needs purchase. Magic needs living soil, living minds, oaths and debts and hungers to fasten onto. The valley has none of those things. It is the one place in the world where a spell slides off you like rain off waxed canvas.

Which is why I stayed. Which is why every thief, exile, oath-breaker, and haunted soul within five hundred miles eventually washed up here, blinking in the flat white light, grateful and half-blind.

I was born somewhere else. I don't know where. That's not a figure of speech and it's not self-pity — it's inventory. I keep meticulous accounts. I could tell you the taste of a king's coronation feast, because I hold it. I could describe the inside of a temple that burned down two hundred years ago, because I hold that too. I have the last sunrise a drowned fisherman ever saw, the first snowfall of forty different childhoods, a soldier's entire war compressed down to three terrible minutes. I am the richest woman in the valley and possibly the world, if richness means the sheer tonnage of other people's lives stacked in your skull.

And my own? My own fits in a thimble.

I know my name is Astrid Okafor, because Corvin gave it to me. I know I came to the valley nineteen years ago, age six, carried in a smuggler's cart under sacking, because Corvin told me so. I know Corvin taught me the trade, fed me, housed me, forbade me — gently, but absolutely — from asking where I'd come from or who had left me with him. Everything else is borrowed. I live in a house built entirely out of other people's furniture.

Corvin says it's safer that way. Corvin says a lot of things in that voice of his, gravel poured over warm honey, and for nineteen years I have drunk it down without tasting what was wrong with it.

Mostly.

* * *

My cottage sits at the north edge of Hollowmere, the last house before the scrub gives up entirely and the salt pans begin. I chose it for the view, which is to say for the absence of one — nothing out there but white, white, white, all the way to the mountains that ring the valley like a cupped hand. Nothing to see means nothing to remember seeing. I liked the simplicity of that, once.

That evening I sat at my table doing what I do every evening after a job: sorting.

This is the part of the trade nobody pays for and everybody would pay anything to never learn about. Every stolen memory has to go somewhere. Keepers like me don't destroy what we take — destruction is possible but wasteful, and besides, some clients come back wanting their pain returned, having discovered that a self with holes in it leaks. So we store. In the old days, before the valley, keepers used salt-glass vials and vaults and all manner of craft I only half understand. Out here, magicless, we use the only vessel left.

We use ourselves.

I sat with Maren's grief laid open in my mind, examining it the way a jeweler examines a stone before setting it. Eleven months of a marriage ending by inches — coughing first, then the doctors, then the bed, then the silence. Underneath the grief, woven through it like thread through cloth, were forty years of ordinary love: arguments about money, jokes about his snoring, the way he'd hummed while sharpening knives. All of it soaked in the same brine. You cannot lift the dye out of the cloth. I'd warned her. She'd said please anyway.

I set it on the shelf in my head where I keep such things. Shelf after shelf after shelf. Somewhere in there, a girl's first kiss. Somewhere, a mother's lullaby in a language I don't speak but can sing perfectly. Somewhere, the exact color of the sky over a battlefield at dawn, which was pink, which nobody ever believes.

I lit a lamp. I ate bread and hard cheese standing up, the way I always eat, as if meals were transactions rather than occasions. I washed one plate, one cup, one knife. One of everything. That's the arithmetic of my life, and I do it without thinking anymore, which is maybe the loneliest thing about it — that loneliness stops hurting when it becomes bookkeeping.

From the shelf, unbidden, a memory surfaced: a client from two winters back, a young man who sold me his wedding day because he needed coin and had nothing else of value. I'd given him a fair price. I'd watched him walk away lighter and poorer and free, and I'd taken that day home and lived inside it that night, the way I sometimes do — the bride's nervous laughter, the smell of rosemary in her hair, the moment when everyone raised their cups and the light came through the wine and turned the whole room amber. For one hour I was a man who had been loved in front of witnesses.

Then I put it back on the shelf, because that's the rule, the only rule I've never broken: borrow, don't keep. A thief who lives in stolen houses ends up believing she owns them.

I blew out the lamp.

* * *

The storm came in off the pans around midnight, the way the bad ones do — no wind at first, just a pressure change you feel in your fillings, and then the white wall rolling up from the south like the world's largest tide deciding to come inland. Salt storms are the valley's one piece of theater. They bury fences, blind livestock, scour paint off doors. Old-timers say the valley throws them to remind everyone who's in charge.

I woke to the sound of my shutters banging and got up to latch them, and that's when I heard it.

Not the storm. Under the storm. A knocking — small, arrhythmic, more persistence than force. The kind of knocking made by someone who doesn't have the strength to knock properly but has decided, with the whole of whatever remains of them, to keep trying anyway.

I stood in my dark kitchen with the wind screaming against the walls and I did not move for a long moment, because in nineteen years of living alone at the edge of Hollowmere, no one had ever knocked on my door at night. Thieves don't get visitors. Thieves get customers, during daylight, by appointment, with payment in hand.

The knocking came again. Three small hits, then a pause, then three more. Stubborn. Almost polite.

I should have waited for morning. Any sensible resident of the valley would have — storms kill the foolish, and whoever was out there was either foolish or desperate, and desperate people are dangerous in direct proportion to their desperation. I knew all of this. I recited it to myself like a lesson.

Then I opened the door, because apparently nineteen years of competence hadn't cured me of being, at bottom, the kind of person who opens doors.

The child on my step was maybe seven years old, caked in dried mud and salt crust to the eyebrows, wearing clothes that had been fine ones once — I could tell by the stitching, the kind of work you don't find west of the mountains — and now hung off him like flags after a battle. His lips were cracked white. His eyes were the strangest part: not frightened, the way any sane child alone in a salt storm should be, but searching, urgent, fixed on my face like I was a page he was trying to read.

He opened his mouth, and what came out was not Hollowmere dialect, not lowland trader-speak, not the mountain tongues the caravans bring through. It was older than any of those. It sounded the way riverbeds look from above — winding, ancient, carved by something long gone.

And here is the thing I will spend the rest of this story trying to explain: I understood him.

Every word. Clear as my own thoughts. A language I had never learned, pouring out of a mud-caked child on my doorstep, and it landed in me like it had been waiting there all along, like a key sliding into a lock I didn't know my chest contained.

*"You're the keeper,"* he said. *"I brought him home. Please — I brought him home."*

Behind him, the storm rolled white and endless to the edge of the world, and for the first time in nineteen years, the magicless valley felt less like a sanctuary than like a wall — and something, somewhere beyond it, had just found the door.
