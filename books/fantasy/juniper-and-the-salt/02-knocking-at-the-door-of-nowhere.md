## 2. Knocking at the Door of Nowhere

The storm came in off the flats an hour past dusk, the way the bad ones always did — no wind at first, just a pressure change that made my teeth ache and the lamp flame stand up straight like a soldier hearing boots. Then the sky turned the color of old bone and began to shed.

Salt storms are not like rain. Rain falls; salt *arrives*. It comes down in fine white sheets that hiss against the shutters and pile on the windowsills like snow that wants to be inside. It gets into everything. Into the flour, the wool, the cracks between floorboards, the spaces between your thoughts if you let it. Old-timers in the valley say a night of heavy salt can scour a man's dreams clean out of him, and I half believe it, because I've woken after storms feeling strangely light, as though something small had been carried off in the white.

I was banking the fire when the knocking started.

Three knocks. Pause. Three more. Small knocks, low on the door, the height of a child's fist rather than a grown man's.

Nobody knocks on Astrid Okafor's door. That is not self-pity; it is bookkeeping. In eleven years of living at the edge of Brackish Row, where the cottages lean together like drunkards and the last streetlamp dies a hundred paces from my threshold, exactly four people have ever knocked: Corvin, twice; a tax assessor who left quickly; and a widow whose grief I'd lifted, coming back to ask for her mother's face too, which I would not give her. Grief is heavy, but it is *hers*, and some things you don't take twice.

So when the knocking came, I did what any sensible thief does with an unexpected sound at night. I put out the lamp, took up the knife I keep under the loose brick by the hearth, and stood very still in the dark, listening.

The knocking came again. Three, pause, three. Patient. Almost polite.

"Who's there?" I said, because silence invites worse things than answers do.

The reply came through the door in a language I did not know.

That is not remarkable, you understand. The Salt Valley collects strays the way a drain collects water — deserters, debtors, apostate hedge-witches, folk fleeing marriages or magics or both. On any given market day you can hear six tongues in the fish stalls alone. A child crying words I didn't recognize should have meant nothing to me.

Except that I understood him.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only honesty I have left. I did not *recognize* the language. I did not guess at it from tone, the way you can guess a beggar means *bread* regardless of what he actually says. I understood it the way you understand your own name — instantly, completely, without translation, without effort, without permission. The words went into my ear and arrived already unpacked somewhere behind my eyes, and I could not have told you how they got there, only that they had always been there, waiting, like furniture in a room I'd never entered.

*Please,* he said through the door. *The white is eating me.*

My hand went cold around the knife.

Because here is the thing about the Salt Valley, the thing every child here learns before they learn their letters: magic cannot follow you across the border. That is the entire point of us. The valley sits in a wound in the world's skin where the old powers don't reach — no charms, no curses, no scrying, no whispering things in the dark. People come here to be *ordinary*, deliberately, desperately ordinary, and the price is that nothing extraordinary may live here either. The wards on the borders aren't walls. They're absences. Magic doesn't bounce off them; it simply isn't, inside, the way sound isn't underwater.

And yet a child was standing in my doorway saying *the white is eating me* in a tongue older than any kingdom, older, I somehow knew, than the kingdoms themselves — and the knowing sat in my skull like a splinter I couldn't find the edge of.

*The white.* Every instinct I owned stood up and pointed at the storm.

I opened the door.

He was small. Six, maybe seven, though famine writes its own arithmetic on children and makes them hard to date. Mud to the eyebrows — actual mud, black river mud, which meant he'd crossed the fenlands, which meant he'd come from the north, from beyond the fens, from a very long way indeed for someone whose legs barely reached my hip. Salt crusted his hair white, so that at first glance he looked like an old man, a little grandfather, until he looked up at me and I saw his eyes were young and enormous and absolutely silent.

Not crying. Not trembling. Just looking at me with the terrible calm of someone who has already used up all his fear somewhere else and has none left for this.

"You're late," I said stupidly, because I didn't know what else to say to a mud-caked apparition, and then, before I could stop myself, in his own tongue — his own impossible, ancient, wrong-language tongue — "*Come in before the white takes the rest of you.*"

The words left my mouth and I heard them and felt the floor tilt.

He stepped past me into the cottage without hesitation, the way you enter a house you've been told about all your life, and stood dripping on my one good rug while I closed the door against the hissing white and leaned on it and tried to breathe.

*What are you?* I asked him, still in that language, because apparently my mouth had opinions now.

He shook his head. Opened his mouth. Closed it. And I saw it then, plain as a scar: he wasn't choosing silence. He *couldn't* speak — not that tongue, not any tongue, not aloud. Whatever voice had knocked and answered had come from somewhere that wasn't his throat.

A mute child who speaks. I have stolen memories from madmen and prophets and one archbishop, and I had never once heard of such a thing.

*

I got him warm first. That much I know how to do; Corvin drilled it into me years ago — *body before questions, Astrid, always body before questions, a frozen client tells you nothing worth coin.* I stripped the salt-crusted outer rags off him, which came away stiff as bark, wrapped him in the blanket I use for clients coming down off a lifting, and put broth in front of him. He ate the way children eat after long hunger: fast, then slow, then fast again, watching me between spoonfuls with those enormous quiet eyes.

While he ate, I watched him back, and my mind — trained, professional, mercenary — ran its inventory whether I wanted it to or not.

His hands were wrong. Not injured, not deformed. Wrong the way a nobleman's hands are wrong on a farm boy: too soft at the fingertips, uncalloused, kept. Someone had kept this child somewhere clean. His hair had been cut recently and badly, hacked short with a knife, which read as flight or disguise. And there, on the inside of his left wrist, half-hidden by the blanket: a mark. Not ink. Not brand. It looked almost like a scar in the shape of a word, except that the shape shifted when I blinked, and I decided very firmly not to blink at it again.

Marks that shift mean magic. Marks that mean magic should not exist inside the valley. My skin crawled in a way I hadn't let it crawl since I was small.

He finished the broth and set the bowl down with exaggerated care, the care of a child who has been taught that breaking things brings consequences, and folded his hands in his lap, and waited. Court manners. I'd seen them once, years ago, in a disgraced chamberlain whose memories I lifted for a jealous rival — the particular stillness of people raised to be looked at.

"Where is your family?" I asked, in Common, and he tilted his head, and I realized he didn't have it. Of course he didn't. So I asked again in the old tongue, hating myself a little more with each syllable that came out fluent: *Where is your family?*

Something moved behind his eyes — grief, vast and dammed up. He shook his head slowly. Then he pointed at himself, and then, with great deliberation, at his own temple, and made a gesture like pouring something out of a jug.

*I carry them,* he said, without words, in the tongue I shouldn't know. *They gave me what they could carry and sent me ahead.*

*Gave you what?*

He touched his temple again. Pressed his lips together. And then, because children are honest in ways that terrify adults, he reached across the table and touched two fingers, gently, to my forehead — the exact gesture, I realized with a lurch, that I use to begin a lifting, fingers to the brow, the oldest trick in my trade — and his eyes went wide and fixed, and he whispered, soundlessly, moving his mouth:

*You're one of ours.*

I pulled back so fast I knocked the bowl off the table. It didn't break. Nothing broke. The cottage was very quiet, and outside the storm hissed like the whole world being scoured.

"One of whose?" I demanded, in Common, in the old tongue, in both, my voice cracking on the second. "Whose are you? Who sent you? How do I know your language?"

He didn't answer. He was looking past me now, at the dark window, and his small face had gone tight with a new expression, one I recognized from clients on the table: the look of someone remembering something that is trying to kill them.

Then his eyes rolled back and he slid off the chair, and I caught him before he hit the floor.

*

I'll say this for myself: I didn't panic. Panic is a luxury for people with clean consciences and full attics. I carried him to the bed — my bed; he could have the blanket and I'd take the chair — and I checked his breathing and his pulse, both steady, both slow, the rhythm of deep sleep rather than collapse. Whatever had taken him, it wasn't fever. It was more like a tide going out.

I should have gone for the herb-woman on Brackish Row. I should have gone for anyone. Instead I sat on the edge of the bed with my knife across my knees and watched a strange child sleep in a salt storm, and I told myself it was caution. It was not entirely caution.

It was that when I'd caught him, his lips had been moving. Still talking, even unconscious. Still saying something, over and over, in that buried language, and I had bent close without thinking, the way you bend toward a whisper, and heard:

*Aldric. Aldric. Hold on. Aldric.*

A king's name. I knew it the way everyone knows it — Aldric the Unbroken, last true king of the old line, dead or vanished or deposed depending on which side of the mountains you learned your history, a name from ballads and border disputes, a name with dust on it. Nobody living wept over Aldric. Nobody living had cause to hold on to him.

But a mute child in river mud was holding on to him with everything he had, and the name came out of his sleeping mouth in the language that lived in my head without my invitation, and I sat in the dark and felt the first cold finger of real dread slide down my spine.

Because here is what I do, and I am good at it, and I have never once wondered why I'm good at it, because wondering is how you end up poor: I take memories out of heads and hold them. I can hold a widow's twenty years of grief in a glass vial without spilling a drop. I can carry a dying man's whole life across town and deliver it intact to his son. My own attic, as I've said, is nearly bare — but my *hands*, my hands are full of other people's yesterdays, always have been, since the first day Corvin put a vial in my fist and said *you're a natural, girl, like you were born to carry what isn't yours.*

*Like you were born to carry what isn't yours.*

I looked at the sleeping boy, and at my own hands, and for the first time in eleven years the sentence turned over and showed me its underside.

Outside, the storm screamed. Inside, the child murmured his dead king's name. And somewhere in the marrow of me, in a place I did not examine because examining it felt like standing on ice and hearing it speak, something answered the boy's call — something old, something patient, something that knew this language better than I did and was, even now, waking up to ask me why I had forgotten it.

Juniper — I didn't know his name yet; I wouldn't learn it until morning — slept on, and dreamed, and the dream leaked.

That is the only word for what happened next. It *leaked*, out of him and into the room, the way light leaks under a door. The fire guttered though there was no draft. The salt on the windowsill shivered. And for three heartbeats I saw — not with my eyes, my eyes were open and seeing my own miserable kitchen — but *behind* them: a crowned man, upright, drowning. Not in water. In white. Sinking through it slowly, endlessly, arms spread, mouth open on a scream that made no sound, while above him something vast wore his face like a mask and smiled with his smile and signed decrees with his hands.

Then it was gone. The fire steadied. The boy breathed.

I sat very still for a long time, knife across my knees, heart going like a smith's hammer, and did the arithmetic I had spent eleven years refusing to do.

Magic cannot cross into the valley. Everyone knows that. Everyone relies on that. It is the one law of my life, the wall I have built my whole self against.

And yet a child carrying a drowned king's ghost had walked through the fenlands and the wards and the white storm to my door, and the magic in him was leaking, and the borders of the sanctuary I trusted with my soul were starting — I could feel it, the way you feel weather in an old wound — to feel less like walls and more like a cage with something circling outside it, patient, hungry, and *already aware of my name.*

The boy stirred. His lips moved again.

This time, the word he shaped was not a king's name.

It was mine.
