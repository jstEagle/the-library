## 3. What the Valley Cannot Hold

The boy slept for two days.

Not the sleep of a child worn out from travel — I know that sleep, have seen it in the faces of runaways who wash up in the Salt Valley every spring, hollow-eyed and grateful. This was something else. He lay on my cot with his hands folded over his chest like a man laid out for burial, and he breathed so slowly I caught myself twice pressing two fingers under his jaw to make sure the tide was still coming in.

I worked anyway. Grief doesn't wait, and neither do clients. On the second morning I lifted a mason's fear of heights out of his skull while the boy breathed his slow tide-breath three feet away, and I noticed something I didn't want to notice: my hands were shaking. Not during the lift — during the lift I am always steady, that's the one thing I can count on about myself. After. When I set the little glass vial of the man's terror into its padded case and sealed it and handed it over and took the coin. My hands shook after.

I told myself it was the storm. The salt storm had passed in the night, but storms leave residue, a fine white dust on everything, and dust gets into the lungs and makes a body twitchy. That's what I told myself. I've been telling myself things in this valley for eleven years, and I've gotten very good at it. It's practically a trade skill.

The boy had a name. He'd given it to me before the language left him — or before I stopped being able to hear it, which is not the same thing but felt like it. *Juniper.* A plant name, a hedge name, a name you give a child you expect to be hardy. He'd said it in that dead tongue and then again, slower, pointing at his own chest with the grave patience of someone teaching a very stupid student, until I understood. Then he'd eaten three bowls of barley soup, drunk half a jug of well water, and lain down on my cot as if he'd been doing it all his life.

That should have been the first warning. Nobody sleeps that easily in a stranger's house unless they trust absolutely or are too far gone to be afraid. But I was tired, and he was small, and there is a part of me — a part I keep locked up tighter than any stolen memory — that has been waiting eleven years for someone small enough to need me.

So I let him sleep. And on the second night, he began to dream aloud.

* * *

I was mending a hem by candlelight when it started. A sound first, low in his throat, a word repeated over and over with the rhythm of waves against a breakwater. I set down the needle because my hands wanted to shake again and I wasn't going to let them.

He was speaking the dead tongue. But this time I could still understand it, and what I understood made the candle flame seem suddenly very far away.

*"White,"* he said. *"It's white, it's all white, Papa, the water is white—"*

Papa.

I crossed the room without deciding to. Kneeling beside the cot, I watched his face in the guttering light: a child's face, mud-scrubbed now, maybe seven years old, with a small crescent scar above his left eyebrow. His eyes moved beneath the lids in fast, darting flickers, chasing something. Chasing someone.

*"Hold your breath,"* he whispered. *"Papa, hold your breath, the white is coming—"*

And then the dream leaked.

I don't know how else to describe it. I steal memories for a living; I know what remembering looks like from the outside, the way a face goes soft and unfocused when it turns inward. This was not that. This was the dream pushing outward, spilling past the edges of him like water over a full cup, and the room filled with it — not images in the air, nothing so kind, but images behind my eyes, arriving without my permission the way a smell arrives, the way grief arrives.

A corridor of white stone. A throne. A man on the throne wearing a crown that did not sit on his head so much as grow from it, branches of pale crystal sunk into his scalp like roots into soil. He was smiling. That was the worst part, at first — the smile, wide and warm and wrong, a smile stretched over something screaming.

Then the dream turned, the way dreams turn, and I saw the same man somewhere else: underwater, or what passes for underwater in a place where the water is made of salt. Drowning in white. Not struggling. Looking at me — at whoever the dreamer was, at the boy, at *me* through the boy — with an expression I recognized instantly because I wear it myself most mornings in the mirror. It was the look of someone signaling calmly from a burning building. *I'm here. I'm still here. Don't come. But if you come, hurry.*

His lips moved. The dead tongue again, and the dream translated it straight into my chest without bothering to stop at my ears:

*Find the keeper's daughter.*

I came back to myself kneeling on the floorboards with the candle burned down to a stub and the boy still breathing his slow tide-breath, and outside the window the valley lay silent and silvered under the moon, and everything was exactly where I had left it.

Except that magic does not work in the Salt Valley. That is the whole point of the valley. That is why I am here.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I sat at my table with the ledger of my inventory open in front of me — forty-one vials, forty-one borrowed lives, each one tagged in my own cramped hand — and I tried to think like a professional instead of like a woman who had just been spoken to through a sleeping child by a drowning king.

Magic cannot cross the valley's borders. Every child raised here learns it before they learn their letters. The valley eats it, drains it, grinds it fine the way the salt wind grinds stone. Wards fail at the border stones. Curses unravel mid-flight and drift down as harmless glitter. Even the gods, if the old stories are true, go quiet and merely mortal when they step past the boundary markers. That's why the thieves and debtors and haunted come here. That's why *I* came here. In the valley, nothing can reach into your head without your permission.

So explain the dream.

Explain how a crowned man's voice traveled across whatever distance lies between here and wherever he is drowning, through a mute child's sleeping mouth, and landed behind my eyes like a coin dropped down a well. Magic cannot cross the border. Therefore either the voice was never magic, or —

Or the border isn't holding.

I got up and walked out into the cold before dawn, boots crunching on the salt crust, and climbed the low ridge behind my cottage where the boundary stones stand. There are nine of them within sight of my door, leaning gray teeth in a circle around the valley, older than any kingdom, older than any language except maybe the one the boy speaks. Shepherds chalk marks on them for luck. Children dare each other to touch them.

I put my palm flat against the nearest one.

Cold. Just cold, the way stone is cold, and under my hand the faint gritty pulse of salt crystals growing, which is the only heartbeat the valley has ever needed. For eleven years I have touched these stones in bad moments the way other people touch talismans, and for eleven years they have answered with the same dumb, blessed silence: *nothing here but rock, nothing reaches you here, you are nowhere and no one and safe.*

This time the silence had a texture.

I stood there a long time, listening to nothing, learning the shape of the nothing. And the nothing was — strained. Taut. Like the skin of a drum someone had quietly begun to press a finger against. Like a held breath. Like the pause between lightning and thunder, stretched thin and getting thinner.

And far off, at the edge of hearing, at the edge of the world, something circled. I never saw it. I never heard it. But I felt it the way a rabbit feels the hawk — not as a sound or a sight but as a change in the quality of the air, a pressure on the back of the neck, an ancient arithmetic running silently overhead: *how fast is the prey moving, how long until it tires, how long until—*

Wolves. That was the image that came, unbidden, and would not leave. Magic circling the valley's borders like wolves around a fire that is burning lower than the people inside it realize. Patient. Hungry. And drawn, I understood with a certainty that arrived fully formed and unwelcome, drawn to one specific scent. One small sleeping boy carrying one specific cargo.

I took my hand off the stone. My palm print stayed a moment in the frost, then faded.

Behind me, down in the cottage, a candle was still burning in a window for a child who called a drowning king *papa*. Ahead of me, the boundary stone stood leaning and cold and no longer quite empty, and beyond it the dark hills rolled away toward a capital city where, if the dream was true, a crown was eating a man alive.

Eleven years ago I chose this valley because nothing could follow me in. Nothing could find me, nothing could touch me, nothing could take from me what little I had left. I built my life inside that promise the way a hermit crab builds inside a shell, and I told myself the shell was a home.

Standing on the ridge with the wolves circling somewhere out past the edge of the lamplight, I finally understood what every animal that has ever hidden in a burrow understands at the end:

A sanctuary is just a cage with better lighting. And when the thing hunting you is patient enough, it doesn't need to get in.

It only needs to wait for you to starve, or come out.

The candle in my window flickered. Down the slope, the boy cried out once in his sleep — a single word in the dead tongue, and this time I heard it plainly, and this time it was not *white*, and it was not *papa*.

It was a name. My name. *Astrid.*

Spoken in a child's voice, in a language dead a thousand years, by a boy who had never heard it — and out beyond the boundary stones, in the dark hills, something turned toward the sound with the smooth, total attention of a wolf that has just heard the fire crackle lower.

I went down to the cottage, packed a bag, and started counting my coins.
