## 6. The Price of Leaving

The smuggler's den sat at the bottom of a dry cistern, which told Astrid everything she needed to know about Wren before she ever met him. Anyone who chose to live in a hole built for water, in the one valley where rain came down gray and mineral-heavy, was either desperate or very good at pretending not to be.

She left Juniper sleeping at the cottage with the door barred and a knife under his blanket — he'd watched her place it with those too-old eyes and nodded like a soldier accepting orders. Three hours' walk through the salt flats, hood up against the wind that scoured everything white. She'd memorized the route from a memory she'd lifted off a dead man years ago, a cartographer who'd spent his last decade mapping the valley's underworld because the surface bored him. She still carried his patience with her, somewhere behind her ribs. It was useful tonight.

The cistern's mouth gaped between two collapsed granaries. A rope ladder hung into the dark, frayed at every third rung, which was either carelessness or a filter. Astrid climbed down anyway.

The bottom smelled of tallow smoke and wet stone. Lanterns burned low along curved walls that had once held drinking water for a town that no longer existed, and now held crates, bedrolls, a cookfire, and six people who all stopped moving when her boots hit the floor.

One of them stood. He was younger than she expected — mid-thirties maybe, lean as a whip handle, with a face that had been broken at least twice and set by someone in a hurry. His hair was cropped close over a scalp scarred white, and when he smiled it was quick and crooked, like something he did with stolen goods.

"You're the thief," he said.

"I'm a broker."

"Sure." He waved her toward a crate near the fire. "Wren. Sit. You look like a woman who's been walking through weather, and I don't do business with people who are shivering. Bad for negotiation. Both parties get stupid."

Astrid sat. The fire was real heat, and she hated how much her body leaned into it. "You were recommended."

"By whom?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters enormously." He crouched across from her, forearms on knees, studying her the way she studied marks. "If someone recommended me, they know my routes. If nobody did, you found me yourself, which means you're either brilliant or a problem. I charge differently depending on which."

"A fence in Grayhollow. He said you cross the border line twice a season and come back un-hollowed."

Wren's smile didn't move but something behind it did. "Grayhollow. That's four days' walk north." He glanced at one of his crew, a broad woman mending a net by the wall. "Nobody walks four days through the flats to lie about a recommendation. All right, broker. What do you want?"

"Passage out of the valley. Two of us. Across the border line and however far it takes until we're clear."

The den went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when someone has said something genuinely foolish. One of the crew laughed, short and disbelieving, then stopped when Wren raised two fingers.

"Out," Wren said. "Of the valley."

"Yes."

"Across the line. Where magic lives."

"Yes."

He turned a small iron ring over in his fingers, around and around. "People leave the valley all the time. Merchants. Pilgrims. Fools. They go out, they come back, mostly fine. But there's leaving and there's *leaving*, and your face says the second kind. So let me ask what I ask everyone who sits where you're sitting." He looked up. "What are you carrying that magic wants?"

"Nothing."

"Everyone carries something."

"Then nothing it can find."

Wren laughed — a real laugh this time, sharp and delighted. "Oh, I like you. That's the correct answer even though it's obviously a lie. Most people say *I'm just visiting my sister* and their hands shake." He stood, paced to the fire, fed it a splinter of crate-wood. "Here's my price structure. Crossing the line costs double what staying inside does, because after the line my crew and I are worth less than our gear — anyone with a grudge and a hex can take us. So: passage for two is forty crowns, half now, half at the crossing point. Plus one more thing."

"Which is?"

"The truth about why. Not the real truth — I don't need names or crimes. Just enough that if whatever's hunting you comes asking, I know what I sold you into."

Astrid had prepared for this. She'd prepared three versions of this, layered like lockpicks in a sleeve. But sitting in the warm dark of a dead cistern, watching a stranger feed a fire, she found herself reaching past all three versions toward something honest, and that frightened her more than the price did.

"There's a child," she said. "He appeared at my door during the storm. He speaks a language that died before the kingdom had a name. And things are circling the valley borders looking for him. Looking for us, now."

Wren went still. Not dramatically — he was too good for dramatic — but the iron ring stopped turning in his fingers.

"That language," he said carefully. "You understand it."

It wasn't a question, and the fact that it wasn't a question meant someone had talked, or Wren knew more than a smuggler should. "How do you know that?"

"Because three weeks ago a man came through here heading the other way. Into the valley, which almost nobody does. Paid me in old coin — real old, the kind with faces no one mints anymore. He asked about the border lines. Where they're weakest. Where magic presses hardest." Wren crouched again, and his voice dropped. "And he said, before he left, that if a woman ever came looking for passage out — a thief, a broker, someone who deals in heads — I should tell her the salt remembers her name."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, wind dragged itself across the cistern mouth with a sound like a long exhale.

"What man?" Astrid heard her own voice go flat, controlled, the voice she used when her hands wanted to shake. "Describe him."

"Tall. Gray at the temples. Walked like his knees hated him. Kept touching his pockets like he was checking something was still there." Wren tilted his head. "Sound familiar?"

*Corvin.* Or someone sent by Corvin. Or someone Corvin feared. Her mentor, who had gone pale at a dead child's babble and lied to her face with twenty years of practiced calm. Her heart was doing arithmetic she didn't want finished.

"When did he cross?" she asked.

"He didn't. Not through here. But the coin he paid with — I took it to a buyer in the lowlands last month, and the buyer took one look and wouldn't touch it. Said money like that comes out of the palace vaults, and palace vaults have been sealed since the king stopped being seen in public." Wren's eyes were steady on hers. "So. Woman who deals in heads, carrying a child who speaks a dead tongue, running from something that already knows her name and pays in royal gold. You want to revise your answer about what you're carrying?"

Astrid thought about lying. It would be easy; it was always easy. But Wren had just handed her information for free — real information, dangerous information — and every instinct she owned said men like this kept ledgers, and free things went into them.

"A king," she said quietly. "I think I'm carrying a king."

The den held its breath. Then Wren exhaled through his teeth, long and slow, and looked at his crew, and they all suddenly became very interested in their work.

"Well," he said. "That explains the old coin."

"Does it change your price?"

"It changes everything except the price." He grinned, and there was something underneath the grin now, something tired and hard. "Forty crowns stands. Half now. We leave tomorrow at first light, before the storm cycle turns — there's a gap in the weather for maybe two days, and after that the flats eat travelers. We cross at the Shallows, where the line runs thin. Old road under the salt. You'll feel it when we pass — like walking through a cold curtain. After that, no promises. Magic finds what it wants to find, and whatever knows your name will know exactly where you've been the moment you step over."

"I understand."

"Do you? Because here's the part I need you to actually hear." Wren sat back on his heels. "I've crossed eleven times. Twelve, counting the trip coming home. Every time, the line feels different. Lately it feels *thin*. Like ice in late spring. Something on the other side is pressing against it, has been for months, and my crew thinks I'm paranoid, but I've been smuggling through that border since I was fifteen and I know what a healthy border feels like. This isn't one." He pointed a splinter of wood at her, ember-bright at the tip. "Whatever's circling your valley isn't waiting outside anymore. It's leaning on the door. And you're about to open it from the inside."

Astrid let that settle. She thought of Juniper's dreams leaking across the cottage floor, images of a crowned man drowning in white. She thought of the way the salt storms had thickened these past weeks, as if the sky itself were packing tighter. She thought of the border she'd trusted her whole life — the one invisible wall that made theft safe, that made her life possible, that she'd never once questioned because questioning it meant admitting she was a prisoner who'd decorated her cell.

Magic couldn't follow her here. That had been the whole point. That had been the whole *life*.

But something knew her name. And things that know your name don't stay outside walls forever.

"Half now," she said, and counted out twenty crowns from the pouch at her belt, setting them on the crate between them. "The rest at the Shallows. If you get us across."

"If I get you across." Wren swept the coins up without counting them, which was either trust or an insult, and she suspected with him it was both. "We move at first light. Bring the boy, bring food, bring nothing that matters more than your skin. And broker—" he paused, and for a moment the smuggler's polish dropped and she saw the man underneath, the fifteen-year-old who'd crossed his first border and felt the line hum, "—when we hit the Shallows and you feel that curtain go cold, whatever you're afraid of finding on the other side? Decide right now whether you'd rather face it or die wondering. Because the line doesn't give you time to choose. It just asks."

"I've spent my whole life taking other people's answers out of their heads," Astrid said. "I'm overdue to find one of my own."

Wren looked at her for a long moment. Then he laughed again, quieter this time, and shook his head.

"Gods help me, I think I actually believe you." He stood and offered his hand — calloused, scarred across the palm, steady. "Welcome aboard, broker. Try not to get my crew hollowed."

She took it. His grip was warm and certain, and it struck her, absurdly, that this was the first hand she'd shaken in years that wasn't trying to take something from her or sell her something. Just a hand. Just a bargain, plain and priced.

She was halfway up the rope ladder when his voice followed her out of the dark:

"Broker. The man with the gray temples — he left something else. Said only to give it to you if you paid full price without haggling."

Astrid paused, rungs biting her palms.

"You didn't haggle," Wren called up. "Come back down."

At the bottom, he pressed a small object into her hand: a disc of salt-glass, clouded, worn smooth at the edges as if it had been carried a long way in a pocket that checked on it often. Inside the glass, faint as frost, swirled a shape she recognized with a lurch that started in her stomach and ended somewhere behind her eyes.

A keyhole. The disc was shaped like a keyhole.

And etched around its rim, in a script older than any kingdom — the same script that lived in Juniper's mouth — ran a single word.

She couldn't read it. But she understood it, the way she'd understood the boy's voice in the storm, knowledge arriving from some locked room inside her that she'd never had a key to.

The word was *keeper*.

Above the cistern, the wind changed direction, and far away across the black flats, so faint it might have been imagination, something answered — a sound like a thousand voices saying one name at once.

Her name.

Astrid closed her fist around the glass until its edges bit, and climbed toward the light.
