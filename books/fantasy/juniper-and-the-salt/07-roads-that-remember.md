## 7. Roads That Remember

The smuggler's cart smelled of pickled fish and old lies, and Astrid hated it immediately.

Wren had named a price that was less coin than promise — *you'll owe me one theft, of my choosing, when I ask* — and she'd shaken on it before her fear could talk her out of it. Now they rattled north along the valley's rim road in a cart whose wheels had been muffled with wool so the salt patrols wouldn't hear them coming. Juniper sat between them on a sack of dried cod, small and silent, watching the world go by with the patience of someone who had seen worse things than weather.

He hadn't spoken since the cottage. Not in his dead tongue, not at all. But he'd taken Astrid's hand at the border without being asked, and he hadn't let go.

"Comfortable?" Wren asked, flicking the reins. He was younger than his reputation — maybe thirty, with a face like a shrug and eyes that catalogued everything and committed to nothing. "Because I want it on record that I don't do children. Or kings. Or whatever he is."

"He's a boy," Astrid said.

"Boys don't make my waystones itch." He nodded ahead, to where a squat pillar of grey stone rose from the roadside, taller than a man, its surface carved over with script worn nearly smooth. "You know what these are?"

"Markers," Astrid said. "Old road-stones. They predate the kingdom."

"They're older than that." Wren slowed the cart as they drew level with it. "My grandmother called them listening stones. Said the roads remember everyone who walks them, and every hundred miles or so, they like to say so."

"That's superstition."

"Probably." He grinned. "But we're carrying a boy who dreams a drowned king, so I've decided to be superstitious for the duration."

Astrid didn't answer. She was watching Juniper. The boy had gone rigid, his hand tightening around hers until the bones ached, and his eyes were fixed on the waystone with an expression she couldn't read — recognition, or hunger, or grief.

As the cart passed, the wind moved through the grass around the stone though there was no wind anywhere else, and Astrid heard — faintly, unmistakably — a whisper.

Not words. A sound. A woman's voice, humming three notes of a lullaby she almost knew.

Her skin went cold.

"You heard it too," Wren said quietly. All the flippancy had drained out of his voice. "I could see it land on you like a bird."

"It's nothing."

"Astrid." He said her name carefully, like testing ice. "I've run this road eleven years. The stones have never once said anything to me. What did it sing?"

She pulled her hand free of Juniper's grip and folded both hands in her lap where no one could see them shake. "Nothing. An old song."

But it wasn't nothing, and it wasn't old — or rather, it was old in the way a scar is old: something from before, from a time she had no access to, surfacing now along the road like a body rising in a flooded field. She had spent her whole life in the Salt Valley, where magic couldn't follow, where memory-thieves like her could work clean because nothing supernatural could interfere with the trade. She had never once wondered what she'd been running from, because Corvin had made sure she never learned to wonder. Questions about her childhood were forbidden. That was the first rule he'd ever taught her, before lockpicking, before the knife-work, before anything:

*Some doors stay shut because you opened them once already.*

She'd thought it was a thief's proverb. Sitting in a fish cart with a mute boy's warmth against her arm and a dead woman's lullaby still ringing in her ears, Astrid understood, with a vertigo that made the horizon tilt, that it might have been a warning.

* * *

They camped that night in the lee of a ruined tollhouse, and the second waystone found them.

This one stood alone in a fallow field, half-swallowed by blackberry bramble, and Juniper walked to it before anyone could stop him. He pressed both palms flat against the stone and closed his eyes, and Astrid felt the air change the way it changes before a storm — pressure dropping, some vast attention turning toward them through the ground itself.

"Wren," she said. "Get away from it."

"I'm not touching it. I'm standing a very reasonable distance—"

"*Away.*"

Wren went. Astrid didn't. She couldn't. Because Juniper had turned his head and was looking at her over his shoulder, and he was beckoning.

The whisper came again, and this time it was not a hum. It was a voice, low and cracked, speaking a language Astrid did not know and understood completely, the same horrible fluency she'd felt the night the boy arrived at her door.

*"Little keeper. Little keeper, born under the open sky. They carried you in across the white line with your name folded up in wax."*

"Stop," Astrid whispered.

*"Your mother sang you down the mountain road. Your mother's hands smelled of salt-glass and lamp oil. Your mother—"*

"**Stop.**"

She seized Juniper's shoulders and pulled him back from the stone, and the moment his skin broke contact the voice cut off like a snuffed candle. The field was just a field. The night was just night. Brambles, cold stars, the smell of cod and wet wool.

Juniper looked up at her. His eyes were enormous in the firelight, and in them she saw something that undid her more than the voice had: pity. The child pitied *her.*

"What did it say?" Wren had crept back, keeping a wary distance. "I saw your face. Something landed."

"It said—" Her throat worked. "It said I was born outside the valley."

The words came out of her sounding foreign, borrowed, like a stranger's confession. Born outside. Everyone in the Salt Valley was born outside, technically; people came there fleeing magic, debts, memories. But there was a difference between arriving and being *carried*, name folded in wax, across the white line where magic couldn't follow—

"—and it called me keeper."

Wren was quiet for a long moment. Then: "Keeper of what?"

"I don't know." And that was the truest, most terrible sentence she had ever spoken. Twenty-nine years of living, and she could count her own memories on two hands: Corvin's cottage, the apprenticeship, the trade, a thousand stolen pasts stacked in her head like contraband, and almost nothing that was actually *hers*. She had told herself for years that this was the occupational hazard of her work — that holding other people's lives had crowded out her own. She had even been proud of it, in a grim way. The emptiest vault is the hardest to crack.

Now she stood in a dark field listening to a stone call her *little keeper*, and the pride curdled into horror.

Her emptiness wasn't a hazard. It was architecture. Someone had built it.

Someone had emptied her.

* * *

They made the third stone by midmorning, and this time Astrid didn't fight it.

She told herself she was being tactical — that if the roads remembered her, the roads might also remember things worth knowing, things that would keep them alive on whatever road led to a king drowning in white. But tactics were a story she told while her hands shook. The truth was simpler and worse: she needed to know. Need had been growing in her all night like a toothache, and by dawn she would have traded every stolen memory in her skull for one true one.

The third waystone stood at a crossroads where four old tracks met, beside a leaning gibbet-post long rotted to a stub. Juniper reached it first. He didn't touch it this time. He simply stood before it, waiting, the way you wait outside a door for permission to enter.

Astrid dismounted the cart and stood beside him.

The whisper came like a tide.

*"The keeper's daughter. The last of the line. Your mother held the vault-keys beneath her tongue when she slept. Your father bargained with the white thing and lost more than he knew. You were six years old when the erasure came, and you laughed, because they told you it was a game — close your eyes, little one, count to ten, and when you open them you'll be somewhere safe—"*

Astrid's knees buckled. Wren caught her elbow, and she shook him off, and stayed upright through sheer spite.

Six years old. A game. Counting to ten.

She tried to reach for the memory behind the words and found nothing, of course — the stone spoke *about* her past, it could not give it back. It was like hearing a description of a house you'd lived in: the blue door, the crooked stair, the smell of bread — and knowing you would never walk through that door again. Except she had never known the house existed until this morning, which meant she wasn't grieving a loss.

She was grieving a theft. Her own.

"Corvin," she said aloud, and the name tasted like blood.

"The mentor?" Wren said. "The one who raised you?"

"The one who raised me." Her voice came out flat and strange. "The one who forbade questions. The one who taught me that some doors stay shut because—" She stopped. Pressed her palms against her eyes. "He knew. He's always known. Everything the stones are saying, he's known since before I could form the question."

"You don't know that."

"Don't I?" She dropped her hands. "Who else? Who else could empty a child and carry her across the valley line and raise her among thieves and never once let her ask why? That takes years, Wren. That takes a whole life spent lying. There's exactly one person who's had a whole life to spend."

Juniper tugged her sleeve. When she looked down, he pointed at the waystone, then at himself, then made a shape in the air with both hands — a crown, small and careful.

Then he pointed at her, and touched his own chest, over his heart.

*I'm carrying him,* Astrid realized. *And somehow the roads know I'm the one meant to carry him back.* The stones weren't haunting her. They were *guiding* her — an old network waking up, one pillar at a time, feeding her herself in pieces because pieces were all she could bear.

Or all someone had left her able to bear.

"We keep moving," she said. Her voice steadied as she said it, and she was grateful for that, at least. Some part of her still worked like a thief: calm hands, clear eyes, take the next step. "North, to the capital. We find the king. We find out what I am."

"And when we find your Corvin?" Wren asked gently. Too gently. "What then?"

Astrid looked at the crossroads, at the four pale tracks running out toward horizons she had never once, in twenty-nine years, wanted to see. She thought of Corvin's hands teaching hers to lift a memory clean, of his voice saying *some doors stay shut*, of every kindness he'd ever done her, reordering itself now into evidence.

"I'll ask him why," she said. "And then I'll decide whether the man who raised me is the man I owe my life to, or the man I owe a reckoning."

Behind them, unbidden, the waystone whispered one last fragment as they rolled north — soft as ash settling:

*"She counted to ten, little keeper. She never stopped counting."*

Astrid rode on and did not look back, and in her chest, where her missing childhood should have been, something knocked — patient, insistent, asking to be let in.
