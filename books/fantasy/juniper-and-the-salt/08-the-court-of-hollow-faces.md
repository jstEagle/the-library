## 8. The Court of Hollow Faces

The capital announced itself three days before we saw it.

Wren pointed it out from the ridge, where the road bent through a stand of salt-burned pines. "There," he said, and spat. "Vhalcrest."

I had expected spires, banners, the usual architecture of power. What I saw instead was a city wearing white like a shawl. Salt had crept up the outer walls in pale veins, climbing toward the rooftops as if the whole place were slowly being pulled down into the earth's own memory. The towers were still golden at their tips — gilded, proud — but everything below the third story had gone the color of bone.

Juniper stood on his toes against the cart rail and made a sound low in his throat. Not a word. A warning.

"I know," I said. "I see it."

He tugged my sleeve and pointed, not at the walls but at the road ahead of us: a column of travelers, carts and pilgrims and merchants, all flowing toward the gates. Ordinary enough. But every tenth person or so walked with a particular quality I recognized from somewhere I couldn't name yet — a smoothness, an economy, like water finding its level. They didn't jostle. They didn't hurry. They moved as if they had agreed, long ago, to want nothing.

"Oath-takers," Wren said quietly, following my gaze. He'd been to Vhalcrest twice since the king stopped being the king, though he hadn't used that phrasing. Nobody did. Officially, Aldric the Steady ruled as he always had, and the harvests were fine, and the roads were safe, and if you noticed that your neighbor had started smiling at nothing you kept it behind your teeth. "Half of them, anyway. The rest are just people."

"And how do you tell which is which?"

Wren's mouth twisted. "The hollow ones never bargain. They pay asking price. They thank you for cheating them." He flicked the reins. "Best way to spot one is to try to rob him. A man with all his oaths intact will fight you for his purse. A man who's given his word away—" he shrugged, "—he'll hand it over and smile while you do it."

* * *

We entered through the Tanners' Gate because Wren said the guards there were bored rather than loyal, and bored was safer. It was true. The two soldiers at the arch barely looked up from their dice, and I understood why within a hundred steps: there was nothing left in this city worth guarding anyone from. The streets were clean. Too clean. No beggars worked the corners — not because they'd been driven off, but because begging requires wanting, and wanting had been taxed out of half the population by something no assessor could name.

Juniper held my hand so tightly my fingers ached. He walked close, hip against my leg, and his eyes moved constantly — doorways, rooftops, the faces above us. Once he stopped dead in the middle of the street and stared at a woman selling bread, a round cheerful woman who was laughing at something her daughter said, and Juniper stared at her until she laughed again, and then he exhaled and pulled me onward.

It took me a moment to understand what he'd been doing. He was checking whether her laughter reached her eyes.

It had. She was one of the ones still full.

We lodged where Wren said to lodge, a rooming house above a dyer's shop in the Weft Quarter, run by a widow named Sefa who charged double and asked nothing, which in Vhalcrest was the honest rate. From our window we could see the palace district across the river — the Spire, the Hall of Oaths, the terraced gardens where, according to Wren, the court now held its audiences in absolute silence, because the king no longer spoke in public. He only listened. And everyone who came before him knelt and swore something — fealty, service, a daughter's hand, a debt forgiven — and rose again lighter than they'd arrived.

"Lighter," I said. "That's the word for it?"

"That's the word." Wren counted coins onto the table, paying Sefa through the floorboards' logic of exact change. "My cousin took the oath of trade guild last spring. Swore to uphold the standards of his craft before the throne. Came home that night and ate dinner and told his wife the meal was fine and she believed him because he said it the same way he'd say the rain was rain. Six months later she left him. Said living with him was like living with a well-dressed wall." He looked up. "He wasn't cruel. That's the thing people get wrong. The hollow ones aren't cruel. They're *absent*. Cruelty needs a self to be cruel with."

That night Juniper dreamed loudly. I sat beside his pallet with my palm hovering over his brow, not quite touching, reading the edges of it the way you'd warm your hands at someone else's fire without stealing: white, always white, and a crowned figure standing in the flood of it, and beneath the crown a face that was trying very hard to remember how to scream.

In the morning I went to work.

* * *

The plan was Wren's, refined by me, approved by Juniper with a single solemn nod. Memory-brokers were licensed in Vhalcrest — that much hadn't changed since Corvin's day — and a licensed broker could request audience at the Hall of Oaths under the pretext of offering services to the Crown. Every regime wants its records managed. Even, perhaps especially, a regime that was eating its subjects' minds one vow at a time.

The problem was the license. Mine had been issued in the Salt Valley, and the valley's licenses were honored here the way valley coin was honored: technically, grudgingly, and only after a fee. The fee went to the Guild of Records, whose hall stood on Oath Street directly across from the Hall itself, so that brokers could watch the throne's front door all day, the way moths watch a lamp.

I dressed the part. Good boots, plain dark wool, the flat leather case of a working broker slung across my back — inside it, instead of instruments, I'd packed Juniper's spare shirt and a brick wrapped in cloth, because a broker's case should have weight and a broker should never actually open it. I wore my hair pinned back the way Corvin taught me, severe and forgettable. *You are furniture,* he used to say during practice runs through market crowds. *Furniture with excellent hearing.*

The Guild clerk was a narrow man named Pellam with ink-stained cuffs and eyes that stayed exactly where I put them, which was the first good sign I'd had in days. Full. He was full. His attention snagged and slid and returned; he got impatient when I was slow; he sighed at my valley license like a man personally burdened by geography.

"The reciprocity fee is twelve crowns," he said.

"Fifteen," I countered, "if you expedite the audience petition today rather than next week."

Something flickered behind his face — greed wrestling procedure, and greed winning, as it does everywhere magic hasn't gotten to first. "The Hall sets its own schedule. I can submit today. Whether they grant—"

"They'll grant." I set fifteen crowns on the counter and let my voice drop into the register I used on clients who needed convincing that I was more expensive than I looked. "Because I'm not applying as a petitioner. I'm applying as a specialist. Tell them the broker from the Salt Valley has experience with oath-residue. With vows that don't release cleanly. Tell them I've seen what happens to a mind when an oath is taken back improperly." I leaned in slightly. "Tell them I know how to unmake a promise without killing the promiser."

Pellam's pen stopped moving.

For three full seconds the Guild hall was silent around us — clerks copying, a courier stamping wet ink, the ordinary machinery of paperwork — and in that silence I felt the building listen. Not Pellam. The building. Or whatever moved through it the way blood moves through a body, along channels I couldn't see but could suddenly, horribly, feel: threads running out from the Hall of Oaths into every quarter of the city, thin as spider silk, and at the far end of each thread a small tug, a small pull, the faintest sensation of being *tasted*.

Then it passed. Pellam blinked, wrote, stamped.

"Audience in four days," he said. "Morning bell. Don't be late. The king dislikes waiting."

"The king," I said carefully, "dislikes waiting?"

"He doesn't say so." Pellam handed me a receipt and a visitor's token, a small disc of white stone that was cold in my palm and stayed cold. "But the schedule never slips anymore. Everything happens exactly when it's meant to. People find it reassuring." He smiled, briefly, professionally. "I find it terrifying, but I'm told that's a character flaw."

* * *

Four days. Which meant four days of learning the city, and the city taught fast.

I learned that the Hollow — that was the street name for them, whispered, half-joking, the way you name a thing you're afraid will hear you — concentrated near the palace district and thinned toward the docks. I learned that the great guilds had all renewed their charters before the throne in the past year, every one of them, voluntarily, ceremonially, and that afterward their meetings ran shorter and their disputes vanished and their innovation died. I learned that the temples still held services, but the confessionals had been removed, because confession is a kind of unbinding and something in the palace preferred promises to stay made.

And I learned about the White Vigil.

They gathered at dusk on the far bank of the river, ordinary people in ordinary clothes, standing in silence facing the Spire. Not protesting — protesting requires anger, and anger had been drained along with everything else from too many of them. They just stood. Some held candles that wouldn't light, because wax remembers fire even when fire won't come. An old woman stood at the front holding a framed portrait of a young man in a guard's uniform, and she stood the way stones stand.

"They think he's still in there," Wren said, watching from the bridge with me, Juniper between us. "The king. Underneath. They come and stand so that if some part of him can still see out, he'll know somebody noticed."

"Do you think he can?"

Wren was quiet a moment. "I think if he couldn't, they'd have stopped coming by now. Hope's the one thing the Hollow can't take, because hope isn't an oath. You can't swear loyalty to a maybe." He glanced down at Juniper. "Speaking of maybes. The boy's been counting."

I looked. Juniper stood at the bridge rail, lips moving, pointing at figures across the water. Counting the vigil. When he finished he turned to me and held up both hands, once, twice, then folded one finger down.

Nineteen. Nineteen nights they'd stood. Or nineteen of something — with Juniper, numbers arrived sideways, and I'd learned not to press. But he held the number up with the gravity of a verdict, and then he did something worse. He pointed at the Spire, and then at me, and then pressed his fist flat against his own chest, over his heart, and held it there.

*What's in there knows you're coming.*

Or maybe: *what's in there is already reaching for you.*

Both readings sat in my stomach like swallowed stones.

* * *

On the third night, the case was almost opened.

A knock at our door past midnight — soft, precise, three knocks evenly spaced, the knock of someone paid by the hour. I put Juniper behind me in the corner and opened the door with my knife hand ready, and found a woman in palace livery, gray and silver, standing in the corridor with a lantern.

"You're the valley broker," she said. Not a question. Her face was pleasant and her eyes were wrong — not empty, exactly. *Rearranged.* Like furniture in a room where someone had been searching. "An advance audience. Tonight. His Majesty prefers to... interview specialists privately before formal presentation."

"His Majesty doesn't sleep?"

"His Majesty," she said, with the mild certainty of a woman reciting scripture, "is always awake."

Every instinct I owned voted to refuse. I thought about Wren asleep down the hall, about Juniper's cold little hand in mine, about the threads I'd felt in the Guild hall, tasting, reaching. Going tonight meant walking into the web on the spider's schedule instead of mine.

Going tomorrow meant it would come here instead.

"I'll need a moment to dress," I said.

The palace at night was worse than the palace could possibly be by day. By day there would be crowds, noise, witnesses, friction — all the things that keep a place honest. At night the corridors were lit at perfect intervals and patrolled by guards who turned at their marks in perfect synchrony, and the silence had texture, a pressure, like deep water. Our footsteps made no echo. I noticed that and made myself stop noticing, because noticing it fully might mean understanding it, and understanding it fully might mean screaming.

The woman led me through the Hall of Oaths itself — vast, pillared, its floor inlaid with a map of the kingdom in white stone — and I felt the oaths. That's the only word for it. Thousands of them, layered into the air like sediment, like rings in a tree, and every one of them humming faintly with that pulling taste I'd felt in the Guild hall. The room was a larder. The kingdom was a larder. Somewhere at the center of it all, something was eating, patiently, without malice, the way winter eats.

At the far end of the hall, doors of black wood opened onto a smaller chamber, lamplit, warm, almost domestic. A table. Two chairs. A decanter of wine, untouched, sweating gently.

The king sat with his back to the door, looking at the fire.

"Master broker," he said, without turning. His voice was Aldric's voice — I knew it from coins and proclamations, the steady baritone every subject grew up hearing read aloud at festivals. But it came out slightly wrong, slightly *averaged*, like a chorus of one. "Sit. Please."

I sat. He turned, and I made the mistake of meeting his eyes.

They were kind. That was the horror of it. They were the kindest eyes I had ever seen on a powerful man, warm and patient and full of a gentle amusement at the whole ridiculous pageant of human striving — and behind the kindness, far down, something moved the way a shape moves under ice. Something that had been drowning for years and had learned, finally, to stop struggling, because struggling only used up air.

The Salt looked at me through King Aldric's borrowed face, and it smiled, and the smile was genuine, and that was the worst discovery of the night: it wasn't pretending. It liked me. Hunger loves what feeds it.

"You carry such interesting emptiness," the king said softly. "Most people are full of themselves. You're full of other people. It's a rare discipline." He poured wine into one glass, then a second, though he didn't drink and I didn't touch mine. "I have a proposition. There are oaths in this kingdom that have gone... stale. Taken badly. Held wrongly. I need a specialist who can dissolve a promise cleanly — who can reach into a mind and take a vow back out without breaking the vessel." He slid a paper across the table. Terms. Generous terms. Absurdly generous. "And in exchange, I am prepared to offer you something few brokers ever find."

"My fee is coin," I said.

"Your fee," the king said, "is a question. Ask it. Any question. I will answer truly. That is my gift to specialists: one true answer, freely given." He leaned forward, and the firelight moved strangely in his eyes, and underneath the kindness the drowned thing pressed its face against the ice. "Everyone who comes to me wants to ask about the future. Power. Survival. But you — I think you want to ask about yourself. Who you were. Why a woman who steals memories for a living owns almost none of her own." The smile widened by a degree. "I could tell you your name. Your real one. The one before the valley. Wouldn't that be worth a little honest work?"

The chamber was very quiet. The fire ticked. Somewhere below us, faint as a heartbeat, I felt the threads pull — thousands of them, drinking.

And I understood, with total clarity, that it knew. It knew about Juniper. It knew why I'd really come. This offer was a hook baited with my own missing life, and the only question was whether I was the kind of fish who bit.

So I gave it the answer a memory-broker would give. The answer furniture would give.

"I'll review the stale oaths," I said, taking the paper, keeping my hands steady through sheer professional spite. "I'll need access to the oath-records. Names, dates, the wording of each vow. And I'll need four days to assess methodology before I commit."

"Of course." The king rose, gracious, satisfied, and walked me to the door himself. At the threshold he paused, and his voice dropped into something almost tender. "One courtesy, master broker, since we're colleagues. Whatever you've brought into my city — however well hidden — do keep it away from the Hall during audiences. Children disrupt the ceremony." A beat. "And children are such *rich* vessels. So many unlived years. So much unsworn time." He smiled his kind, bottomless smile. "It would be a shame if something happened to it."

The doors closed behind me. The gray-and-silver woman walked me back through the white-map hall, past the humming larder of oaths, out into the night air of Vhalcrest, where I finally let myself breathe.

I didn't go straight back to the Weft Quarter. I doubled through the tanneries, changed direction twice, watched my shadow in shop glass. Clean. Then home, where Wren sat up waiting with a knife across his knees and Juniper bolted upright at the sound of the latch.

I told them everything. The offer. The one true answer. The warning about rich vessels.

When I finished, Juniper climbed off his pallet, crossed the room, and took my face in both his small cold hands. He looked into my eyes the way he'd checked the bread-seller, hunting for hollowness, for absence, for the rearranged look. Whatever he found there must have satisfied him, because he nodded once — and then he spoke.

Not the dead tongue. For the first time since the storm, in words, cracked and rusted and unmistakably deliberate:

"One true answer," he whispered. "Lie to it."

And in the silence after, from the direction of the palace, so faint it might have been the wind off the river, I heard the threads begin to pull.
