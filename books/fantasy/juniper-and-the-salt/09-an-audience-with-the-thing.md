## 9. An Audience with the Thing

The chamberlain's smile did not reach his eyes. Nothing in the palace reached anyone's eyes; that was the first thing I understood about the capital, and the last thing I would be allowed to forget.

"His Majesty will receive the broker at the ninth bell," he said. "You will speak when spoken to. You will not look directly at the crown."

"And if I do?"

The smile held. The eyes stayed empty as two coins dropped down a well. "No one has done it twice."

He left me in a waiting room furnished for someone far grander than a memory-broker — velvet the color of dried blood, a fireplace big enough to roast an ox, windows of glass so thick the morning came through them gray and thick as porridge. Juniper sat beside me on a bench too high for his feet to touch the floor, swinging them, humming something in his dead language under his breath. Wren stood by the door with his arms crossed, wearing the borrowed livery of a merchant's guard like it itched.

"You don't have to do this," he said for the fourth time since dawn.

"I know." I smoothed the front of my coat. Broker's gray, good wool, the kind of garment that says *I am expensive and I am discreet*. "That's why I'm doing it."

"That's not logic, that's spite."

"Spite is a kind of logic. It's just arithmetic with grudges." I looked at Juniper. He had stopped humming. He was staring at the doors at the far end of the room — tall things, black oak, banded in iron gone green with age — and his small hands had gone still in his lap.

"What is it?" I asked him, though he couldn't answer. He never answered. Instead he lifted one hand and pressed it flat against his own chest, over his heart, then turned the hand over and looked at his palm as if expecting to find something written there.

I knew what he meant. I felt it too. Ever since we'd come inside the walls, some part of me had been leaning toward those doors like a plant toward light — or like a moth toward a lamp, which was the more honest comparison.

"They're going to taste you in there," Wren said quietly. "Whatever sits on that throne. You understand that? A memory-broker walks in carrying a head full of other people's lives, and the thing that eats oaths is going to want a bite."

"Then it'll get a mouthful of a widow's grief and a soldier's shame and forty years of a fisherman's mornings," I said. "Let it choke."

Wren didn't laugh. That frightened me more than the doors did.

* * *

They came for us at the ninth bell exactly, because whatever wore the king's face had never once been late and never once been early, and punctuality, I would learn, is the smallest and most terrible of its hungers. Two guards flanked us down corridors that all bent subtly toward the center of the palace, the way water bends toward a drain. The guards' boots made no sound on the stone. I checked twice. Their boots were ordinary leather. The stone simply declined to carry their weight, or their weight declined to press on the stone, and either way my skin crawled.

Juniper walked between Wren and me, holding one hand of each. His grip was small and dry and very tight.

The throne room had been beautiful once. You could see the bones of it — vaulted ceiling painted with constellations, pillars carved with the long genealogy of Aldric's line, every king and queen rendered in pale stone from Harrow the Founder down to the man who sat at the end of it now. But someone had whitewashed everything. Every color, every gilded edge, every painted star, drowned under coats and coats of white. The room wasn't decorated. It was embalmed.

And the courtiers — the court of hollow faces, I'd called them in my own head before I ever saw them, and I'd been crueler than I knew, because they weren't hollow. Hollow implies empty space. These people were full. Full to the brim with one thing only, the way a cup filled to the lip with salt water has no room for anything else. They stood in ranks along the walls in their fine clothes, and when the doors opened they all turned their heads at once, smoothly, together, like a field of flowers following the sun, and every face wore the same gentle, vacant, satisfied smile.

"Smile back," Wren breathed.

I smiled back. My face felt like a mask strapped on over a scream.

At the end of the room, on a throne that had also been whitewashed — or perhaps it had always been white, I couldn't tell anymore — sat King Aldric.

Except it wasn't. I'm a thief of twenty years' practice; I know a stolen thing when I see one, even when the theft is wearing the original's body and its crown and its tired, handsome face. What sat on the throne was wearing Aldric the way a coat is worn. And underneath —

Underneath, drowning.

I felt him before I understood him: a faint pressure behind my eyes, like a second heartbeat that wasn't mine, rhythmic and desperate, the way you can sometimes feel another person crying in a dark room without hearing a sound. *In there*, I thought. *He's in there. Alive. Watching through eyes he can't close.*

Juniper's hand crushed mine.

"Do not kneel yet," said the king, and his voice was warm and reasonable and wrong, wrong the way a portrait's eyes are wrong, following you. "First, let me look at our guest. Come forward, broker. Bring the child, if you like. I am fond of children."

Every instinct I owned voted no. I brought him anyway, because leaving him with Wren meant leaving him out of my sight in this place, and I would sooner have cut off my own hand.

Up close, the thing on the throne resolved into details that made it worse, not better. The king's face was well-made, fifty-ish, lined around the eyes with what might have been humor in better years. The crown sat on his brow like it had grown there — white gold, unadorned, and frosted, actually frosted, crystals of salt creeping up from the band into the metal like frost climbing a windowpane. His hands rested on the arms of the throne, and his fingernails were faintly crystalline at the tips, translucent, catching the light.

And his eyes. Everyone had warned me about the crown. No one had warned me about the eyes, which were the king's own gray, human, exhausted eyes — and behind them, deep down, at the bottom of them like a stone at the bottom of a well, something else looking out. Something patient. Something that had been hungry for a very, very long time and had learned that hunger goes down easier with manners.

"You are Astrid Okafor," it said.

Not a question. My name in its mouth sounded like a key trying a lock.

"Your Majesty honors me," I said, and bowed the precise degree a licensed broker bows — respectful, transactional, forgettable.

"Oh, I honor you indeed." The smile widened by a millimeter. "A woman who walks into my court carrying thirty-one lives in her skull that are not her own. Do you know how rare that is? How *nourishing* merely to stand near?"

There it was. The pressure behind my eyes sharpened into something almost physical — a tongue, vast and delicate, pressing against the inside of my thoughts the way you press your tongue against a sore tooth. Testing. Tasting.

I am not going to pretend I was brave about it. It was violation, pure and simple, but violation performed with such exquisite gentleness that screaming would have made *me* the mad one. It moved through my stolen inventory like a connoisseur through a cellar. The widow's grief — a pause, a sigh of appreciation, *oh, that's an old vintage*. The soldier's shame at the burning of Kelst. The fisherman's forty years of gray dawns. A baker's pride. A murderer's last afternoon, which I'd taken off him at the gallows' foot for a fee paid by his victim's family, and which the Salt lingered over longest of all, humming.

Then it reached the edges of me. The places where my own memories should have been.

It stopped.

The whole throne room stopped. The courtiers' smiles froze mid-curve. Even the torches seemed to lean in. For three heartbeats the only sound in the world was Juniper breathing beside me, fast and shallow.

"Well," said the Salt softly. "Well, well, well. A keeper's daughter."

My mouth went dry. "Your Majesty mistakes—"

"Do not insult us both." It rose from the throne — and the court sank with it, every hollow face dropping to its knees in one smooth wave, and Wren went down too, dragging Juniper, and I alone stayed standing because I suddenly could not remember how knees worked. "You have a hole in you, Astrid Okafor. A beautiful hole. Do you know what most people's holes look like, from in here? Ragged. Bleeding at the edges. Yours is clean. Surgical. Cut by a master and cauterized with love." It descended the three steps of the dais slowly, crown glittering, salt-crusted hem whispering on stone. "Someone took your childhood from you so carefully that the taking itself became a kind of protection. Do you know who does work like that?"

"No," I said.

"A keeper," it said. "Only a keeper. And keepers, little thief, are the only flavor I have never tasted."

It stopped an arm's length away. This close, I could smell it — not brine, exactly, but the memory of brine, the idea of the sea reduced to its driest essence. Its eyes held mine and I could not look away, and I realized distantly that I had broken the chamberlain's rule without noticing, that I was looking directly at the crown, and nothing happened, because rules made for courtiers were not made for whatever I was.

"I could give it back to you," it said.

The words went through me like a blade through warm butter.

"Every year of it. Your mother's face. Your father's voice. The house you were born in, the name you were born with, the reason your keeper thought you worth erasing. I have been alive a long time, Astrid Okafor, and I have eaten ten thousand oaths and ten thousand lives, and I remember *everything*. Everything that has ever passed beneath a crown's shadow is filed in me. Including—" it leaned closer, voice dropping to a confidant's murmur, "—including a certain night, long ago, when a memory-keeper carried a sleeping girl across the border of a magicless valley while the sky behind them burned white."

My heart was slamming. Juniper's hand found mine again without either of us deciding it.

"All you must give me," the Salt said, "is the boy."

There it was. There it always was. Every seduction in the history of the world comes down to *this small thing, this one small thing, no one will even miss it.*

"He's nothing," it continued, smooth as oil on water. "A vessel. A jar I filled and lost track of, and jars do not matter, only what was in them — and I have already poured that out elsewhere, or will again. Give me the boy and walk out of here whole. Whole, Astrid. Can you imagine it? You, who has spent her life holding everyone's past but her own. One word. One nod. And you will know your mother's voice. You will know why the valley was built. You will know *yourself*."

And here is the thing I will carry to whatever grave I get: for one full breath, I wanted it.

Not weakly. Not in some flickering moment of frailty I could dismiss later. I wanted it with my whole body, the way a drowning woman wants air. Twenty years of being a house that strangers lived in. Twenty years of stitching an identity out of other people's offcuts — a widow's patience here, a soldier's steadiness there, a fisherman's love of rain. One nod, and I would be a person instead of a ledger.

The Salt watched me want it. I could tell it was watching, savoring, the way I myself savored the moment before a mark realized what they'd lost. It was in no hurry. Hunger that has waited centuries knows how to wait minutes.

Juniper tugged my hand.

I looked down. He was staring up at me, mud-brown eyes wide, and he did not shake his head, did not plead, did not do any of the things a child in a story would do. He simply looked at me with complete, terrible trust — the trust of someone who had already decided that whatever I chose, he would go along, because I was the one who'd fed him soup in a storm and taught him to sleep behind a locked door.

And I thought: *that's a memory. That's mine. Nobody gave it to me, nobody sold it to me, nobody stole it for me. I made it myself, in a kitchen, with bad soup.*

Maybe that was enough to build on.

"No," I said.

The word came out steady. I was proud of that.

The Salt's expression did not change, which was somehow worse than anger. "No?"

"He's not a jar. And neither am I." I straightened my spine. "You want to know what I've learned, stealing other people's heads for a living? Memory isn't the past. It's the present, wearing costume. You're offering me a costume. I'll find the real thing myself."

Something flickered at the bottom of those gray eyes. Not rage. Interest. Which frightened me far more.

"There she is," it murmured. "The keeper's blood. Stubborn as a vault door." It stepped back, ascended the dais, settled onto the throne, and became again merely a tired king in a white room, and somehow that transformation was the most monstrous trick of all. "Then hear my terms, broker, since you decline my gift. You and the child will remain guests of the court. Guests are free to go wherever they wish within the palace. The gates, of course, are closed — there is sickness in the lowlands, you understand. Precautions." The smile returned, gentle as snowfall. "Take any rooms you like. Ask any questions you like. Speak to anyone you like." A pause, delicately measured. "Everyone here will tell you the truth. That is rather the problem with my court, Astrid Okafor. No one can lie to me, and so no one can lie to you."

Guards appeared at my elbows — or men shaped like guards; I was no longer confident of the distinction. Wren pulled Juniper close.

"One more thing," the Salt said as we were turned toward the doors. Its voice followed us, soft, almost kind. "Ask your questions quickly, thief. And when a man named Corvin arrives at my gate — and he will arrive, he has been walking toward this city for eleven days, old men walk slowly when they carry heavy secrets — do try to forgive him before he confesses. Confessions go down so much easier with forgiveness already given."

The doors closed behind us with a sound like a book shutting.

In the corridor, Wren exhaled shakily. "What did it say? At the end?"

I stared at the green-ironed doors. Somewhere beyond them, a drowned man was watching through borrowed eyes, and somewhere behind me, an old man I loved was limping toward a city that ate truth for breakfast, carrying the secret of everything I'd been.

"It said," I heard myself answer, "that I'm about to learn where I came from. And that I should decide now whether I'm going to survive knowing it."
