## 10. Corvin's Confession

The city of Vael Corren had a smell, and the smell was wrong.

Corvin had known it would be. He'd smelled it thirty years ago, when he'd walked out of it with a sleeping child in his arms and a lie in his mouth so large it had become the architecture of his life. But knowing a thing and smelling it again were different animals, and the animal in his chest was clawing.

Oaths. That was the smell. Ten thousand sworn loyalties fermenting in marble halls, and underneath them something drinking.

He came through the south gate on foot, because horses were expensive and because he wanted the city to have time to notice him. He was an old man now, gray to the collarbones, with a limp he'd earned falling down a stairwell in the Salt Valley carrying a girl who weighed nothing and a secret that weighed everything. He wore a broker's coat with the sigils burned off, which told anyone who knew how to read coats exactly one thing: *I used to matter, and I paid to stop.*

The gate guards didn't look at him. That was the first horror. In the old days — his days, the days before — gate guards looked at everyone, because looking was loyalty made visible. These two stared at the middle distance with the pleasant blankness of men whose minds had been skimmed like cream. Sworn and drained. He counted four more hollow faces between the gate and the fountain square before he stopped counting, because counting was a way of screaming.

*She's here,* he thought. *Astrid is here, in this city, walking past these empty-eyed people, and she doesn't know what they are because I never taught her what they are. I taught her locks and ledgers and how to lift a childhood out of a skull without leaving fingerprints. I taught her everything except why.*

Thirty years of reasons, and every one of them felt thinner than the last with each step into the capital.

* * *

Finding her was not difficult. Finding her had never been difficult; that was the curse of having built someone yourself. He went where an Astrid would go — not the great inns, she'd consider them traps, but the memory-brokers' row behind the chandlers' quarter, where the licensed thieves kept offices small enough to flee from. He asked a boy selling roasted chestnuts whether he'd seen a woman with salt-gray eyes asking after private rooms, and the boy pointed with his chin, and Corvin tipped him a copper for the economy of the gesture.

The innkeeper at the Drowned Bell took his coin and said, "Third floor, end room. She's got the boy with her. Quiet child." A pause, and then, with the faint frown of a man noticing something a beat too late: "Funny. Can't quite recall her face."

"You will," Corvin said gently. "It's a good face."

He climbed the stairs slowly, telling himself the slowness was the leg. It wasn't. Every stair was a year he could have told her. Every landing was a birthday, a fever, a night she'd cried in her sleep about a mother she couldn't remember having, while he sat outside her door with his head in his hands rehearsing a truth he never delivered.

At the top of the stairs he stopped, because the door was already open, and Astrid was standing in it with a knife held low and professional, and behind her — gods, behind her — was the boy.

Juniper. Aldric's son. The child he'd helped smuggle out of the palace the night the king drowned in his own body, the night the vault was sealed, the night Corvin had chosen which secret to keep and which life to bury.

"Hello, little thief," he said.

Her eyes did something complicated. Relief and rage fighting for the same ground, as they always had. "You followed us."

"I preceded you, technically. You're just faster." He looked at the knife until she put it away, which she did, which undid him more than any blade could have. "May I come in? What I have to say shouldn't be said in a hallway."

"It shouldn't be said at all," she said, but she stepped back, and he took the gift.

* * *

The room was small and clean and had one window facing away from the palace, which meant Astrid had chosen it, because Astrid faced away from things she intended to approach from behind.

The boy sat on the bed, watching Corvin with those old, old eyes. When their gazes met, the child spoke — three words in the dead tongue, soft as ash falling.

And Corvin, who had spent thirty years pretending he didn't know that language, answered him in it.

Astrid went very still. "You *understand* him."

"I always understood him." Corvin lowered himself onto the room's single chair, and his knee announced his age, and he let it, because he wanted her to see an old man and not a liar, though of course he was both. "That language isn't dead, Astrid. It's hidden. It's the tongue the memory-keepers used among themselves, back when there were memory-keepers, back when we had a whole college and a vault under the palace and a king worth serving." He closed his eyes. "I was the last of them. Royal Memory-Keeper, third rank, oath-sworn to House Aldric going back six generations. And you —"

"Astrid." Her voice was flat. She knew. Some part of her had known since the waystones whispered to her on the road; he could see it in the set of her shoulders, the way she braced against the truth like weather. "Say my name. All of it."

"Your name is Astrid Okafor Marchetti-Vale." He said it carefully, each piece placed like a stone over a grave. "Marchetti was your mother. Vale was your father's cover name, mine. Your true name — your keeper-name — is Astrid of the Ninth Vault, daughter of Sera Marchetti, Keeper of the Deep Records, and I am sorry, I am so sorry, that you are only hearing this in a rented room above a chandler's shop instead of in the vault where your mother died sealing it."

The silence lasted long enough for him to hear the city breathing outside, that vast slow inhalation of ten thousand drained minds.

"Erased," Astrid said finally. "You erased me."

"I saved you."

"You *erased* me!" She was across the room before he saw her move, twenty years of stolen skills in her body, her hand fisted in his coat, and he did not resist her because he had imagined this moment every day for three decades and in none of the imaginings had he deserved to resist. "There was a woman — there was a *mother* — and I have walked past her grave in my own head my entire life and never once wondered why the ground there was bare! You took —"

"I took a childhood that would have gotten you killed." His voice cracked and he let it crack; let her hear the cost of the lie in the voice that told it. "Listen to me. Listen, because I will only have the courage to say this once. The Salt hunts keepers' bloodlines. Not fears — *hunts*. It ate the other eight vaults in a single night, Astrid. Eight colleges of keepers, gone, every mind in them poured out and drunk, because keeper-minds are the only minds it cannot simply swallow. We hold memories too tightly. We're gristle in its throat. So it kills us instead, or worse — it wears us, makes us open the doors from inside. Your mother saw it coming. Your father — Aldric — he bargained with it, bought three days with a promise he didn't understand he was making, and in those three days your mother sealed the Ninth Vault with herself inside it, and I carried you out through the cisterns while the court sang oaths to a king who was already drowning."

His hands were shaking. He watched them shake with a strange detachment, as if they belonged to the young man he'd been.

"And then I brought you to the valley," he said, "because the valley is the one place its hunger can't reach, and I stole your childhood — I, the last royal keeper, turned my own art on my own ward — because a blank child is invisible, and a marked one is a beacon. Every year I meant to give it back. Every year I found a reason. And the reason was always the same reason: that as long as you remembered nothing, the Salt had nothing in you to follow home."

"So you made me a thief," she said. Her grip hadn't loosened. "You made me a thief of memories. You taught me to take from other people the very thing you took from me."

"Yes."

"That's either the cruelest joke ever told or —" her voice broke, and he saw the exact moment the rage found what was underneath it, which was grief, which was love, which was the whole terrible knot — "or you thought if I learned how it worked, someday I could undo it. Someday I could steal myself back."

Corvin laughed, or wept, or did whatever an old man does when both happen at once. "You were undoing locks before you could write your name. I never doubted you'd find the vault. I only prayed you'd find it after I was dead, because I'm a coward, little thief, and I always have been. The bravest thing I ever did was run, and I've been running downhill ever since."

* * *

The boy slid off the bed.

None of them had heard him move — he moved the way children move in dreams, soundlessly, importantly — and he stood between them with one small hand on each: Astrid's wrist, Corvin's knee. And he spoke in the hidden tongue, and this time he spoke at length, and Corvin translated because Astrid deserved every word even as each one landed like a stone.

"He says: *The white thing showed me your face, old one, in the dream where the king drowns. It knows a keeper lives. It has been waiting for you to come.*" Corvin swallowed. "He says: *It let you into the city.*"

Astrid's head came up. "What does that mean? Wren got us through the checkpoints — bribed —"

"Bribed guards don't forget faces," Corvin said quietly. "Hollow ones do. Think, Astrid. Think like a keeper. The Salt emptied this city's loyalty and drank it, and a creature that drinks ten thousand oaths a day knows the taste of every mind that crosses its threshold. You walked in wearing a broker's mask and it tasted the shape beneath. It let you stand in the throne room. It offered you your own past." He rose, knees protesting, and took her by the shoulders the way he had when she was seven and learning to pick her first lock. "It didn't offer you your childhood because it's generous. It offered because your childhood is the key to the Ninth Vault, and the vault is the one door in this kingdom it has never been able to open. Your mother sealed it from inside, with keeper's blood, and the Salt has been locked out of the deep records for thirty years, starving around the edges of them, and now the last keeper's daughter is standing in its parlor holding the key in her own head."

"Then it wants me to open it."

"It wants you to *want* to open it. There's a difference, and your life is the difference." His grip tightened. "I lied to you for thirty years to keep you out of this city, and I failed, and now I'm going to do the thing I should have done when you were twelve. I'm going to tell you the rest of it — all of it, the bargain your father made, the words your mother wrote on the vault door, what the unbinding requires — and then you're going to decide, because you're a keeper's daughter and deciding is the family trade."

Below the window, faintly, the bells of Vael Corren began to ring — not the hour, not the half-hour. The alarm pattern. Old, almost forgotten, a pattern Corvin had heard only once before, on the night eight vaults burned.

In the streets, people were stopping mid-stride. Turning. All of them together. All of them smiling.

"They know I'm here," Corvin said. "They've known since the gate."

* * *

Astrid was already moving — gathering the boy's coat, checking the window, doing the mathematics of escape that Corvin had taught her and that no teacher had ever been prouder or more ashamed. But Juniper did not move. The child stood in the center of the room, face tilted toward the ceiling, toward the palace on its hill, and his mouth opened, and what came out was not the hidden tongue.

It was a man's voice. Deep. Tired. Drowned.

"*Keeper.* One word, through the child's throat, and the bells stopped ringing as if the city itself had flinched. "*You brought me the last of the bloodline. How thoughtful. Come home, old friend. Bring the key. Bring the boy. I have kept your king warm for you.*"

Juniper swayed. Astrid caught him before he fell, and the child's eyes when they found Corvin's were his own again, young and terrified, and Corvin understood with perfect clarity that the choice he had run from for thirty years had finally caught up with all three of them in a rented room, and that running was over.

"The cisterns," he said. "Under the palace. I still remember the way your mother sealed. Astrid — little thief — we go down tonight, or we don't go at all."
