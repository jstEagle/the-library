## 11. Juniper Speaks

The words come back to me in the dark.

That is how I think of it — not that I learn them again, but that they return, like birds to a roof at evening. They had gone somewhere. They had been waiting. And now, with Astrid's hand warm on my brow and her breath held the way people hold it when they are afraid of what they might see, the words come home.

*Ilmen sathra. Ilmen sathra. Veth.*

*Light of mine. Light of mine. Come back.*

I know whose voice that is. It is the first voice I ever heard. It is a woman's voice, and it is singing over a cradle made of woven reeds, and there is salt on the wind outside and honey on her fingers when she touches my hair. I am very small. The world is warm. Then the world is not warm anymore, and I will not tell you about that part yet, because even inside my own head some doors are locked from the other side.

My name is not Juniper.

I have known that for a while, the way you know a stone is in your shoe. Astrid named me on the second night, when I could not tell her anything, because she said I smelled like the juniper smoke she burned to keep the moths from her wool, and because names are things you give when you cannot take them. I let her. A name given in kindness is not a small thing. But underneath the name she gave me, there is another one, older, heavier, and tonight — with the storm pressing against the shutters and the candle guttering and Astrid's fingers trembling against my skin — the old one rises up through me like water finding a crack.

*Aldren veth Corane.*

I say it out loud.

I do not mean to. My mouth has been mostly closed for as long as I can remember, which is not long, which is part of the problem, which is everything. Words in Astrid's language sit in my chest like swallowed river stones. But this word — these words — they are not stones. They are the river itself. They come up my throat and out of me in the old tongue, the tongue of the First Keep, the tongue they say died with the old queens, and the candle flame stands up straight as a soldier, and every hair on my arms lifts, and somewhere far away — farther than the storm, farther than the valley, farther than the world's skin — something enormous turns its attention toward us like an eye opening.

Astrid makes a sound I have never heard a grown person make. Small. Like a hinge.

"Say that again," she whispers. In her language. I understand her language. I have understood it since the night I arrived on her doorstep caked in mud, and I did not understand why I understood it, and neither did she, and we both agreed without saying so not to look directly at that particular strangeness. "Juniper. Say it again. Slowly."

"Aldren veth Corane," I say. My voice sounds strange to me. Rusty. Young. "It means—" I stop, because meaning is harder than speaking. Meaning requires the thing behind the word, and the thing behind the word is a man drowning in white, a man I dream about every night, a man who wears a crown of salt and screams where no one can hear him except me. "It means *the king's light returns.*"

The silence after that is so complete I can hear the salt storm grinding against the roof tiles, patient as teeth.

---

Let me tell you what I know, all of it, in order, the way Astrid taught me to lay out cards before a reading: oldest at the bottom, newest on top.

At the bottom: a tower room with a round window. A woman singing. Honey. Salt. A man with kind eyes and a tired crown who used to let me sit on his knee and hold the seal-ring while he worked, because he said a king's hands should learn early what they carry. He smelled of ink and cedar. He called me *ilmen*. Little light. That is the bottom card, and it is warm, and I would live inside it forever if the deck allowed.

Above that: fire. Not near us — beyond the mountains, in the lowlands, in the places where magic pools like rainwater. The servants whispering. The king — my father, though I did not have that word yet either, only the shape of it — standing at the round window for whole days, watching the horizon, and the crown sitting wrong on his head, tilted, like it was listening to something else.

Above that: the night everything changed. My father kneeling in front of me in the dark, holding my face in both hands, and his eyes were his own eyes but barely, like a lamp behind frosted glass, and he was talking fast and quiet in the old tongue, faster than I had ever heard anyone talk, and he pressed something against my forehead — his forehead to mine, skin to skin — and said, *Veth ilmen, cora sath. Ilmen veth, cora sath.* Which is a riddle and also an instruction and also goodbye: *The light goes into the vessel; the vessel carries the light; be small, be silent, be carried.*

And then a woman I did not know — not my mother, my mother was already gone by then, another door locked from the other side — wrapped me in a fish-smelling cloak and put me in the bottom of a cart under nets, and we traveled for a long time, and I was told not to make a sound, and I was very good at not making a sound. I had practice. The thing in the crown listened for voices. Especially small ones. Especially ones that spoke the old tongue, because the old tongue was the one language it could not wear like a coat.

Above that: the border of the valley. The moment we crossed it, the world went quiet in a way I had never felt — no hum, no whisper, no pressure behind my ears. Magicless ground. Dead ground, the priests would have said. Safe ground. The woman carrying me started crying, quietly, the way you cry when you have been holding it too long, and I remember thinking that the quiet must be very heavy if it made a grown person leak.

And then the top card, the newest one: the woman leaving me at a cottage door in a salt storm. Knocking. Running. Gone. And a door opening, and lamplight, and a face looking down at me — sharp-eyed, guarded, younger than her hands — and me trying to speak and nothing coming out, because the fragment my father pressed into me had gone deep and taken my words with it, or maybe I had simply decided, in the way children decide things absolutely, that if I opened my mouth the listening thing would find me.

So I stayed silent. For weeks. While Astrid fed me and scowled at me and taught me cards and pretended not to be gentle. While I dreamed every night of a crowned man drowning in white, and leaked those dreams into the room, and watched Astrid go pale and start asking questions she had forbidden herself to ask for twenty years.

And now the top card has turned over, and it says: *speak.*

---

"The king's light returns," Astrid repeats. She has not moved her hand from my brow. Her fingers are cold. Hers are always cold; she says memory-work drains the heat out of you, and I believe her, because her hands feel like someone who has carried too many lives that were not her own. "Juniper— Aldren—" She stops. Tries again. "Is that your name? Is that what you're called?"

"It's half of it." I sit up. The blanket falls away. The candle flame leans toward me like it wants to hear. "Aldren veth Corane. The rest—" I press my fist against my sternum, where the fragment lives. I can feel it tonight the way you feel a swallowed coin, solid and present and wrong-shaped. "The rest is in here. He put it in me. Before."

"Before what?"

"Before the Salt finished eating him."

Astrid closes her eyes. When she opens them, they are different — not softer exactly, but aimed, the way an archer's eyes change when the target finally steps clear of the trees. "Tell me everything," she says. "In order. Slowly. Don't leave anything out because you think it's small. In my trade, the small things are always the load-bearing walls."

So I tell her. All of it, the way I laid it out for you. The tower room. The honey. The tilted crown. The night of the riddle and the fish-smelling cloak. She listens without interrupting, which I learn later is rare for her, and her face does the thing faces do around fires — flickering between shapes, grief and fury and fear taking turns.

When I get to the part about the old tongue being invisible to the Salt, she goes very still.

"Say that again."

"It can't hear the old tongue properly. My father said—" I close my eyes and reach for the exact words, and they come, and they come in his voice, and for a moment I am four years old and safe and terrified all at once. "*Sathra mel ilmen vorath. The hunger wears every oath, every vow, every promise sworn aloud. But the First Tongue predates its hunger. Words spoken in it pass beneath its ear like coins beneath a blind man's feet.*"

"That's why you were smuggled out," she says slowly. "Not just because you're his son. Because you're his son *and* you speak it. You're a message that walks."

"I'm a key," I say, because that is the other thing he said, the last thing, right before the cart: *You are the key, ilmen. Keys are small. Keys are quiet. Keys are only useless until the lock appears.* "He said the lock would appear. He didn't say what it would look like."

Astrid looks at me for a long moment. Then she does something unexpected. She laughs — one short, cracked note, no humor in it at all.

"He should have said," she mutters. "Because I'm looking at it."

---

Here is what happens next, and I want to be honest about it, because honesty is the one inheritance my father gave me that nobody can steal.

Astrid asks me to try something. She has been touching my brow for nights now, reading the edges of my dreams the way she reads other people's memories — carefully, from the outside, never diving in. Tonight she wants to go deeper. She wants to follow the fragment down to where it connects to the drowned man, and see how far the thread runs.

"You don't have to," she says. "If it hurts, or if it feels like losing him, we stop. Immediately. I mean it, Aldren. Some things shouldn't be pried open just because we're curious."

But it isn't curiosity. I can see that. It's the same thing that made her take me in off the step instead of turning me out into the storm, though she'd deny it with her last coin. It's the thing under the competence, the loneliness shaped exactly like a door. She has spent her whole life holding other people's pasts and owning almost none of her own, and here, sleeping in her spare bed, is a boy made almost entirely of somebody else's mind — and some part of her recognizes the architecture.

"Do it," I say.

She wets her thumb in the candle-wax dish, presses it to my brow, and speaks a word in her craft-tongue — a soft word, a hinge-word — and the world folds.

I have been inside my own head my whole life, obviously. But I have never seen it from the outside. Astrid takes us both down through the layers like divers, and I watch my own mind drift past: the tower room, the honey, the fire, the cart, the border, the cottage. Rooms in a house. And below all the rooms, below the cellar, below the foundations, there is a door of white salt-crystal, and behind it, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, a man is singing.

He is singing in the old tongue. He has been singing since the night he pressed his forehead to mine. He has been singing for years, alone, in the dark of his own stolen body, and the song is the riddle, the instruction, the goodbye, over and over:

*Ilmen sathra. Ilmen sathra. Veth.*

*Light of mine. Light of mine. Come back.*

Except he doesn't mean himself. I understand that now, with Astrid beside me in the deep place, her presence steady as a handrail. He isn't calling himself back. He is calling *me* back — the piece of himself he hid inside me, the true king, the uncorroded mind, the father I actually knew. He hid the real Aldric in his son the way you hide a seed in winter soil, and the thing wearing his body and his crown has been searching for that seed ever since, and it has never found it, because it cannot hear the old tongue, and the seed sings only in the old tongue.

We surface. Astrid pulls her hand back like she's touched a stove.

"He's alive," she says. Her voice shakes. "Aldric — the real one — he's alive in there. All this time."

"Yes."

"And the thing on the throne—"

"Wears him. Like a coat." I hug my knees. The candle has burned down to a stub; the storm outside has changed pitch, lower, more deliberate, like something breathing through gritted teeth. "It eats oaths. Every noble who swears loyalty feeds it. Every regiment, every courtier, every kneeling servant. That's why everyone at court smiles with empty eyes. They're not loyal. They're *lunch.*"

"How do you know that?"

"I don't know how I know it." I touch my sternum again. "I just do. It's in the fragment. Pieces of what he saw while it ate him. He watched it happen for years, Astrid. He watched his friends go hollow one smile at a time, and he couldn't scream, because screaming is loud, and loud things get found."

Astrid stands. Paces. The floorboards know her weight and creak in familiar places. She stops at the window, where the storm has thinned enough to show the moonlight turning the valley white — not snow-white, salt-white, the whole basin glowing like a bowl of bone — and I watch her shoulders rise and fall three times, the way she does when she is converting fear into decisions.

"Then we go to him," she says.

I knew she would say it. I knew it the way I know the tune of my father's song. But hearing it still lands in my chest like a dropped stone.

"To the capital? To the Salt?" My voice comes out smaller than I want. "It's been looking for me. Since the night we crossed the border. It got louder every day we traveled. It knows a keeper's blood came out of the valley — it said so, in the dreams, it *talked* to me—"

"I know." She turns from the window. Moonlight cuts her face in half; the lit half is calm, the dark half I can't read. "Wren leaves in two days. We'll be on it."

"We?"

"You didn't think I'd found the lock and then misplaced the key, did you?" Something crosses her face then, quick as a fish under ice — grief, maybe, or its cousin — and is gone. "Besides. You'll need someone who can steal memories. What we're about to attempt is theft, Aldren. The biggest job of my life. We're going to steal a king back from a god."

I should be afraid. I am afraid — my hands won't stay still, and the fragment in my chest pulses like a second heart, and somewhere beyond the valley's edge the enormous attention keeps turning toward us, patient, starving, sure. But underneath the fear there is something else, something I haven't felt since the tower room with the round window and the honey and the tired kind eyes.

Hope. With a deadline attached, yes. Hope with teeth on all sides. But hope.

"One condition," I say, because I am my father's son, and kings' children learn to bargain young.

Astrid raises an eyebrow. "You're six."

"I'm old enough to know that adults say *we* and then decide things alone." I lift my chin the way he used to when the ambassadors got condescending. "When we find him — when we get the fragment back to where it belongs — you tell me first. Before you do anything. Whatever it costs. Promise. Swear it."

Her jaw tightens. I have learned enough about her to know that oaths sit badly in her mouth; she trades in other people's vows and keeps few of her own. But she looks at me — at the seed of her king, at the small quiet key, at whatever else she sees when she looks at me that she never explains — and she nods once.

"Sworn," she says. "On the trade."

Outside, the storm stops altogether. All at once, like a held breath. And in the ringing silence, from everywhere and nowhere, from beneath the salt and above the moon, something vast and hungry and *listening* begins — very softly, very mockingly — to hum the melody of my father's song.

It still cannot hear the words.

But it has heard the tune.
