## 12. Oaths Devoured

The bells started at dawn.

Not the cathedral bells — those had been silent since we arrived, their ropes cut, because a thing that eats oaths has no use for calls to prayer. These were the alarm bells on the outer walls, and they did not stop. They rang the way a man screams when he has forgotten every other word he knows.

I was awake before the first toll finished. So was Juniper, sitting bolt upright in the narrow bed of Wren's safehouse, his eyes open and fixed on nothing, his small hands pressed flat against the blanket as if holding down something that wanted to rise.

"It's louder," he said in the old tongue. His voice had been coming back to him in pieces since the throne room — a word here, a sentence there, each one costing him sleep. "It's eating faster."

"I know." I dressed in the dark. "Stay with Wren."

"He won't stay with me," Juniper said. "He'll go look. He always goes look."

He was right about that too. Wren was already in the stairwell, boots on, knife at his hip, looking like a man who had slept in his clothes because sleep had become a rumor. He caught my eye and jerked his chin toward the window.

"You want to see this," he said. "Or maybe you don't. But you should."

* * *

From the roof of the dye-house, we could see the eastern parade ground, where the Third Regiment mustered every morning for inspection. Two thousand men in the king's grey-and-gold. I had watched them drill two days ago, when we first came into the city — crisp lines, sharp turns, the mechanical pride of soldiers who believed in the crown on the wall behind them.

They were still drilling. That was the horror of it. The formations were perfect. The turns were crisp. But the sound had changed.

An army makes noise. Boots, breath, the low animal hum of two thousand bodies being alive together. The Third Regiment had gone quiet except for the boots. No commands — or rather, no voice giving them. They moved as if the orders were arriving somewhere inside the skull, direct from the palace, from the crowned thing that sat where a king should be.

"They went hollow overnight," Wren said. He said it flatly, the way he reported cargo weights and customs bribes. Flatness was how he kept from feeling things; I'd learned that much about him on the road. "My contact in the quartermaster's office says half of them stopped eating yesterday. Just stood at the mess tables until someone moved them along. This morning—" he gestured at the parade ground, at the silent wheeling ranks, "—this morning they don't need anyone to move them along."

Below us, the regiment turned as one body and faced the palace. Held. Turned back. Faced the palace again.

"Practicing," I said.

"What?"

"That's what it looks like when it practices." My mouth was dry. "Astrid— when I touched Juniper's dream, I saw it do this. It rehearses its feeding. It learns the shape of a mind before it takes it whole. Those men aren't hollow yet. They're being *fitted*."

Wren looked at me for a long moment. Then down at the parade ground. Then he spat over the edge of the roof, which was, I had come to understand, his version of prayer.

"How long?"

"A week. Maybe less. And it's not just the regiment." I pointed west, toward the merchant quarter, where the morning crowds should have been thickening around the fish stalls and the moneylenders' doors. The streets were moving, but wrong — people walking in straighter lines than people walk, faces aimed forward, no one stopping to argue about the price of herring. "It's spreading through the oaths. Every guild oath, every marriage vow, every sworn debt. Anyone who ever swore loyalty to the crown is a door, and it's been walking through doors all night."

Juniper tugged my sleeve. I hadn't heard him climb the roof stairs behind us. Of course he hadn't stayed with Wren. He never stayed anywhere.

"The bells," he said. "Why are the bells still ringing if everyone can hear them?"

"Because some people aren't hollow yet," Wren said. "And the ones who aren't are ringing the bells."

"And the ones who are?"

Wren didn't answer. Below us, the regiment turned to face the palace a third time, and this time they did not turn back.

* * *

We spent the day doing the thing I was worst at: waiting.

Corvin had not come back from the vault district. He'd gone before dawn to check whether the memory-vault beneath the old chancery still held — whether the Salt's hunger had reached down into the salt-glass shelves where my childhood sat sealed — and he'd promised to return by midday. Midday came. Then the afternoon bells. Then the light going copper over the rooftops, and no Corvin.

"He's careful," I told Juniper, who was drawing in the dust on the floorboards with a burnt stick. He drew spirals. He was always drawing spirals. I tried not to think about why spirals, why the coiled shape of a thing that winds inward and never stops.

"He's old," Juniper said, with the terrible diplomacy of children.

"He's *careful*."

"He's both." He didn't look up. "In the dreams, there was an old man with ink on his hands. The king trusted him. The Salt hated him most of everyone, because he could unmake what it made." He paused. "Is that Corvin?"

My chest did something complicated. "Yes."

"Then he'll come back. The Salt hates him, but it fears him more. Fear makes things slow."

Out of the mouths of eight-year-olds. I sat down beside him and let him draw a spiral on my palm with the burnt stick, and I pretended my hand wasn't shaking.

Wren came up from the cellar at dusk with three cups and a bottle of something that smelled like it could strip paint, and poured with the ceremony of a man who needed his hands busy. "News," he said. "All of it bad. Pick your flavor."

"Wren."

"Fine. One flavor, then. The garrison at the river gate swore new oaths tonight — renewal ceremonies, the whole pageant, banners and kneeling and the crown held high. Two hundred men." He drank. "One hundred and ninety of them walked out of the ceremony hollow. The other ten ran. The ten who ran are the only reason we know what happened, and two of those ten were found an hour later standing at the gate, asking to be readmitted. Asking *politely*. In unison."

Juniper had gone very still. "It's speeding up because it knows we're here."

"Yeah," Wren said, and didn't soften it, because he'd decided somewhere on the road that the boy got truth the same as adults, and I loved him a little for that even when the truth was a knife. "It knows. Astrid touched its king's mind. You spoke in the throne room and cracked its hold for eleven seconds. It's been counting the days since. Everything it does now, it does faster, because it wants us found before we find the vault."

"Then we go tonight," I said. "Now. We don't wait for Corvin."

"And walk into the vault district past two hundred hollowed guards?" Wren set down his cup. "The river gate is closed. The east postern is closed. The smugglers' culvert under the tannery — the one I've used nine times to move contraband under this city's nose — that's our way in and our way out, and it's still open, because nobody hollow thinks about drainage. But Astrid." He leaned forward. "If we use it to get in, we need it to get out. One way through. One chance. We burn that bridge the moment we cross it."

"Then we cross it once, and we don't come back this way."

"Where do we go, then? Out the far side of the vault into—" he waved a hand at the world beyond the walls, "—what? The valley? Three weeks' hard travel with a child, hunted by a god wearing a crown?"

"Yes," I said.

He stared at me. "That's your whole plan. *Yes.*"

"My plan is that every hour we wait, another hundred people stop being people. My plan is that Corvin is down there right now, alone, with whatever he went to find. My plan is that I am done letting the people I love stand between me and the thing that took everything from me twice." I heard my own voice come out level and cold and knew it was the voice of the thief, the professional, the woman who lifted griefs from widows' heads for coin. Good. That voice didn't shake. "You can wait here if you want. Take Juniper. Run for the coast. I release you from any bargain we made."

Wren looked at me for a long time. Then at Juniper. Then at his cup, which he drained and set down upside down — smuggler's sign, I would learn later, for *the route is spent*.

"You release me from nothing," he said. "I don't work on release. I work on contract, and my contract is a skinny little ghost of a boy who sings dead languages in his sleep, and I don't abandon cargo." He stood. "Culvert. Now, while the changeover watch is hollow and punctual. And Astrid — pray your old man's still breathing down there, because I'm not digging him out of a vault full of salt."

* * *

The culvert smelled exactly as bad as it sounded.

We went single file through the dark — Wren ahead with a shuttered lantern, Juniper's hand in mine, the ceiling low enough that Wren stooped and I ducked and Juniper walked upright, which felt like some kind of metaphor I was too tired to name. The slime underfoot sucked at our boots. Somewhere above us, through four feet of stone and sewer, the city hummed its new quiet hum, and I tried not to imagine what the humming was.

Halfway through, Juniper stopped.

"Astrid." His voice came out thin. "There's something behind us."

"There's rats behind us. There's always rats."

"Not rats." He turned. In the shuttered glow I saw his face, pale and certain. "It's in the water. It followed the water. Water swears oaths too — to run downhill, to reach the sea. Didn't you know? Everything that keeps a promise belongs to it a little."

I didn't know that. I wished very much that I still didn't.

Behind us, far back in the tunnel, the dark went white.

Not lamplight-white. Salt-white — the color of the storm on the night Juniper arrived at my door, the color of the drowned king's dream, the color of the thing itself. It came up the culvert without rushing. It didn't need to rush. It was in the water, and the water was everywhere, and it had all the time that oaths had ever taken to be spoken.

"Run," Wren said, and for once his voice wasn't flat at all.

We ran. Slime and stone and Juniper's hand crushing mine, the white light swelling behind us, and ahead — the grate. The culvert's iron mouth, rusted shut these twenty years, according to Wren's own maps. He hit it with his shoulder once, twice, and it groaned but held, and behind us the white light touched the water at our heels, and I felt it begin to *taste* —

It tasted my fear first. That's how it works; I understood that now, viscerally, the way you understand fire. It drinks the oath-shaped things, and fear is an oath — the body swearing to the heart that it will keep it alive. I felt my fear start to leave me, felt it drain down my arm toward the water like heat from a wound, and the worst part, the part I will never tell Juniper, is that it felt *relief*. To be less afraid. To be lighter. To be hollowed gently, kindly, one promise at a time—

Juniper sang.

One note. A single syllable of the old tongue, cracked and childish and absolutely clear, and the white light flinched. The water shuddered. The draining stopped as if a valve had slammed shut, and the light withdrew — slowly, reluctantly, the way a tongue leaves a broken tooth — back down the tunnel, back into the dark, taking its patience with it.

Silence. Dripping. My heart slamming.

Wren was staring at the boy. "What in the salted hells was that?"

"It doesn't like my language," Juniper said simply. "It ate all the people who spoke it. Words remember being eaten." He squeezed my hand. "Come on. The grate."

Wren hit it a third time and it gave — rust shearing, hinges screaming — and we spilled out into the night air of the vault district, into an alley that stank of tannin and old blood, under stars I was suddenly, stupidly grateful to see.

Wren leaned against the wall, breathing hard. Then he started laughing, quietly, horribly.

"You know what that was?" he said. "That was our exit. That culvert was the only way out of this district that isn't watched, and it's watched now. It's watched by *it*. We're in the vault quarter with a child, a knife, and no road home."

"We weren't going home."

"No," Wren agreed. "We weren't." He pushed off the wall, straightened his coat, and became once again the flat-voiced man who reported cargo weights. "The chancery's two streets north. If your Corvin's alive, he's there. And if the vault's open—" he met my eyes, "—then everything it wants is in one building, and so are we. So let's not be surprised by what comes next."

We went north. Behind us, deep in the earth, the water whispered its promises downhill, and I understood, finally, what the cost of every alliance was: that each one of them — Corvin's silence, Wren's route, Juniper's song, my stolen years — was a coin already spent, and the thing below had begun calling in its debts.

The chancery rose ahead of us against the stars, black and blind.

Its front doors stood open.

Every lamp inside was burning.
