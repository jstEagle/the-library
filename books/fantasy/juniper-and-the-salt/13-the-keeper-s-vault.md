## 13. The Keeper's Vault

The chancery was not empty.

That was the first thing I understood, standing in the doorway with Juniper's hand gone rigid in mine. The lamps burned on every desk, every shelf, every sill — dozens of them, oil-fed, freshly trimmed — and not one of them warmed the room. The light just sat there, yellow and obedient, like a dog that had been told to stay.

"They're lit for us," Corvin said quietly. "It wants us to see."

"It" being the Salt. I had stopped pretending I didn't know what he meant. Three days since Wren's sacrifice at the river ford, three days since we'd come down out of the hills with the boy's song still caught in our teeth, and the city had changed around us the way a face changes when someone you trusted starts smiling too much. The courtiers smiled. The guards smiled. A baker had smiled at me this morning while handing over bread that tasted of nothing at all, and behind his eyes there had been a whiteness like the inside of an eggshell.

But the chancery — the old records house, squat and blind against the stars — this was different. This was a message.

Juniper tugged my hand. He looked up at me and said something in the dead tongue, soft, and I felt the words land in me the way they always did now, translated by some door in my blood I hadn't known was open until him.

*The room remembers being full.*

"He says people used to work here," I murmured to Corvin. "Scribes. Keepers."

Corvin's jaw tightened. He hadn't slept properly since the ford. Neither had I, but he wore it worse, or wore it more honestly, which for Corvin amounted to the same thing. "Then let's not keep the room waiting."

We went in. Our boots left prints in a fine dusting of salt across the floorboards — salt that had no business being indoors, salt that crept up from the foundations now the way damp did in cheaper houses. It crunched underfoot all the way to the back wall, where a clerk's counter stood abandoned with a ledger still open on it, its last entry half-finished, the ink browned forty years ago.

*Inventory, final audit,* the heading read. *Vault access restricted to Keeper's blood.*

Corvin stopped so suddenly I walked into his back.

"You knew," I said. Not a question. I was past questions with him; they'd become a kind of ritual between us, me asking, him not answering, both of us keeping count.

"I suspected." He set his palm flat against the counter, below the ledger, where the wood was worn smooth in two shallow hollows — the shape, I realized, of hands braced there day after day, year after year. "I knew this building. I worked in it. What I didn't know — what I *swore* I didn't know — was whether anything was still down there."

"Whether *what* was still down there?"

He turned and looked at me, and in the lamp-light his face was older than I had ever allowed it to be. Seventy years of careful living, of teaching a stolen child how to lift memories without leaving scars, of never once saying her name because names were hooks and hooks were dangerous. All of it was in his face now, spent at last, like a purse turned out on a table.

"Your childhood, Astrid. Twenty years of it, sealed in glass. And the only key is standing right here asking me questions."

* * *

The way down was behind the counter, through a door I would have sworn wasn't there when we entered — a low door in the stone, unmarked, its hinges bright with use. That was the thing about the Salt's hospitality: it opened doors. It wanted us down there. Every step we took toward the vault was a step it had already counted.

I made Corvin go first anyway.

The stair spiraled into the earth, cut from the same white rock as everything else in this country, and the deeper we went the more the lamplight above fell away and the more the walls themselves gave off a faint grey shimmer, like moonlight trapped in bone. Juniper rode on my back, arms around my neck, his breath quick and warm against my ear. He'd refused to be left above with Wren. He'd refused it so violently — screaming in his dead language, clinging to my coat — that Wren had finally said, "Let the lad come, Astrid. Whatever's down there, he's got more right to it than any of us."

Wren himself stayed at the top of the stair with a knife and a lantern and instructions to drop the door shut if anything came in after us. He'd saluted with two fingers, grinning his crooked grin, and said, "If I don't see you again, tell your past self I said hello." Then, quieter, the grin gone: "Don't die down there. I've grown accustomed to owing you."

Now the stair ended, and the vault began.

It was round, and vast, and cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature. Shelves ran the curved walls floor to ceiling, and on the shelves, row upon row upon row, stood vessels of salt-glass — cloudy, milky panes blown into jars and orbs and long sealed tubes, each one faintly luminous, each one holding a swirl of something that moved when you weren't looking directly at it. Memories. Hundreds of them. Thousands. The confiscated, the surrendered, the extracted — a kingdom's worth of yesterdays, shelved and catalogued in a dead woman's handwriting.

And in the center of the room, alone on a pedestal of black stone, one vessel larger than all the rest.

A cask, waist-high, stoppered with lead. Inside it, swirling slow as cream in tea, was a light I recognized.

I had never seen it before in my life. That was the whole point. But I recognized it the way you recognize your own reflection in a window — instantly, wrongly, completely.

"That's me," I said. My voice came out very small in all that stone. "That's — those are mine."

"Yes." Corvin crossed to the shelves first, running his fingers along the labels, reading. His lips moved. "Your mother catalogued these. Her system. I only ever learned half of it." He pulled a small tube from a high shelf, blew dust from the label, and held it out to me with a hand that was not quite steady. "Read it."

I took it. The label was written in a fine, slanted script, ink faded but legible:

*Okafor, Adaeze. Keeper of the Royal Memory. Extraction: voluntary. Contents: the rising of the Salt, the first oath, the bargain of the well. Seal: her own hand. Note —* and here the writing pressed harder, the pen nearly tearing the paper, *— note: if anyone reads this, the Salt has won. Burn rather than open.*

Below it, smaller, almost an afterthought:

*For my daughter, when she is old enough to forgive me.*

The tube was warm in my hands. Or my hands were cold everywhere else, which came to the same thing.

"She knew," I whispered. "She knew it would end like this, and she sealed her own memories away so the Salt couldn't drink them, and she wrote a note to me like she was — like she was leaving milk money on the table—"

"Astrid."

"—like forgiveness was a *schedule*, like I could just arrive at it, like twenty years of my life being locked in a jar was a thing a daughter could simply be *old enough* for—"

"*Astrid.*" Corvin took my shoulders. Up close his eyes were wet, and he didn't hide it, and somehow that was worse than everything else. "Look at the cask."

I looked.

The light inside it had begun to move faster.

* * *

"There's a mechanism," Corvin said. He'd circled the pedestal twice, examining it the way he examined everything — patiently, thoroughly, like a man defusing something. "Lead stopper, wax seal, and underneath—" he crouched, gestured "—a lock. Not metal. Blood-lock. Keeper's craft. Your mother built this vault, and your mother keyed it, and unless I'm very much mistaken, it will open for exactly one person in the world."

"Me."

"You. Which means the choice is yours, and I want you to make it with your eyes open." He rose, and his voice dropped into the register he'd used when I was nine and learning to hold a stranger's grief without letting it stain me. Lesson-voice. Last-lesson voice. "Opening this will call the Salt. Not might. Will. We are standing in a cellar directly beneath its throne, and that cask holds twenty years of keeper's memory — the story of its first rising, the terms of the bargain your father struck, everything it has spent four decades trying to erase. The moment the seal breaks, it will feel that the way you'd feel a knife laid against your throat. It will come down that stair, and it will bring everything it owns with it, and everything it owns is currently wearing the royal guard."

Above us, faint through fathoms of stone, a bell began to ring.

Corvin closed his eyes. "There's the summons. Court's been alerted to intruders in the chancery. We have minutes, not hours."

"Then say the rest fast."

"The rest is this." He stepped close, and took both my hands, and held them the way he had when I was small and feverish — firmly, like something worth holding onto. "You can take yourself back. Everything they took — your mother's voice, your father's face, the valley before the valley, the reason you flinch at oaths, the reason you never leave, all of it — it's in that glass, and it's yours, and no one on this earth has the right to refuse it to you. Not even me. *Especially* not me." His grip tightened. "But understand what you'll be walking out of here carrying. Those memories aren't a gift, Astrid. They're a wound that healed wrong, and opening it will hurt more than anything the Salt has ever done to you. And you'll have to carry that wound while running from an immortal hunger with a child on your back. So I am asking you — not telling, *asking*, for the first time in twenty years — do you want it? Even knowing the price?"

Juniper slid down from my back. He stood between us, looking up at the cask, at the light turning inside it like a slow golden storm. Then he reached out and touched the glass, gently, the way you touch a sleeping animal.

The light stilled.

And through the dead tongue, in a voice that was half a boy and half something much older, he said: *"She waits in there. She has waited a long time. She is not afraid anymore. She is only tired of being a secret."*

My mother. Speaking through a child who carried a king's mind, about a woman thirty years dead, whose memories swirled six feet from my hands.

I laughed. It came out cracked and wet and it was the truest sound I'd made in years.

"All right," I said. "All right. Yes. Wake her up."

* * *

The blood-lock was simple, because keeper-craft was always simple — that was its whole philosophy. No keys, no passwords, nothing that could be stolen or tortured out of you. Just a shallow basin carved into the pedestal's side, shaped like an open hand.

I pressed my palm into it.

For a heartbeat, nothing. Then the basin drank — I felt it, an actual pulling, a thirst — and thin lines of red spread outward from my hand through channels in the stone, branching, branching again, tracing patterns I somehow knew without knowing: my mother's mark, the keeper's sigil, the same sign I'd seen burned into the waystones on the road north, the sign that had whispered fragments of my name to me in the dark.

The sigil completed itself. The whole room exhaled.

Every vessel on every shelf brightened at once — hundreds of imprisoned memories flickering like a city lighting at dusk — and the cask's lead stopper groaned, split, and fell away in two halves that rang on the stone floor like a struck bell.

The light rose out of it.

Not spilled — *rose*, deliberate, gathering itself into a column above the pedestal, and inside the light I saw shapes: a kitchen with herbs drying from the rafters. A man laughing with his sleeves rolled up. A woman's hands, ink-stained, turning the pages of a catalogue. A valley under stars. A well. A bargain. Fire, far off, and a crown, and a voice like grinding stone saying *give me the girl*—

The Salt screamed.

Not from the stairwell. From everywhere. From the walls themselves, from the salt dust on the floors, from the city overhead — a sound like a glacier calving, like ten thousand oaths breaking at once, and the lamplight in the vault turned white, and the shelves began to rattle, and somewhere far above us Wren started shouting.

Corvin put himself between me and the stair, knife out, old and straight-backed and absolutely calm.

"Take it quickly," he said. "However it comes, however it hurts — take all of it, and then we run."

The column of light bent toward me like a head bowing.

And my mother's voice — her actual voice, warm and tired and thirty years late — said, from everywhere and nowhere, from inside my own skull:

*"Oh, my love. There you are."*

The light came home.
