## 16. Siege of the Empty

The bells started at dawn, and they did not stop.

Astrid had heard bells in the capital before — coronation bells, market bells, the lazy tolling of saints' hours. These were none of those. These were alarm bells, rung by hands that no longer cared whether the rope burned them, and beneath their clangor she could hear something worse: footsteps. Hundreds of them. Thousands. The whole palace turning over in its sleep like a body deciding to stand.

"They know," Wren said from the doorway of the undercroft where they'd hidden through the night. He had a knife out and was cleaning it with a rag, though it was already clean. "Been knowing since the vault opened. Question's only how fast they come."

"Then we go now." Astrid pulled Juniper close with one hand and checked her satchel with the other. Inside: three vials of stolen memory, a coil of salt-wire, Corvin's key, and a dead woman's word for *keeper* written on a scrap of paper she'd been carrying since the waystones gave it back to her. "Corvin said the descent is behind the throne hall. Two flights down, then the old stair."

"Corvin says a lot of things." Wren spat. "Man shows up after twenty years wearing keeper's robes and expects us to follow him into a hole."

"He erased my childhood to keep me alive. I think he's earned the benefit of the doubt."

"I think men who erase things always have one more thing to erase." But Wren was already moving, knife away, because whatever else he was, he was fast when it mattered.

They went up through the servant passages, Juniper's small hand locked in Astrid's grip. The boy hadn't spoken since the vault — not in his old tongue, not at all — but he pressed against her side like a burr, and every few steps he looked up at her with Aldric's grey eyes, and she remembered what they were walking toward: a king buried alive inside his own body, waiting on a keeper's blood.

The first body stopped them cold.

It lay across the passage mouth, a guard in palace livery, and there was nothing wrong with him except everything. No wounds. No blood. Just a face gone smooth as an egg, eyes open and reflecting light without taking any in. Hollowed. One of the empty.

"Don't touch him," Corvin said quietly, behind them. She hadn't heard him arrive; twenty years of theft had taught him to move like regret. His robes were travel-stained, his hands steady. Only his eyes were wrong — wet, and fixed on her face as if memorizing it. "He can't be woken. What's left isn't sleeping. It's just... absent."

"How many?" Astrid asked.

"All of them, soon enough. The Salt fed deep last night. Every oath sworn in this palace is a door, and it went through them all." Corvin looked down the corridor toward the throne hall, where the bells still hammered. "We have perhaps an hour before it stops needing doors."

* * *

They almost made it.

The entrance to the old stair stood behind the dais of the throne hall itself — of course it did; kings had always liked their secrets close to their crowns — and getting there meant crossing forty yards of open marble beneath a ceiling painted with the kingdom's whole proud history. Astrid counted heads as they slipped along the wall: a dozen courtiers standing in the shadows, motionless, smiling. Smiling was the worst part. The Salt wore its people like festival masks, and it had taught them all the same smile.

Halfway across, the nearest courtier turned his head.

Not his body. Just his head, smooth and slow, like a weathervane finding wind. Then another turned. Then all of them, in unison, a sound like wet cloth tearing, and the smiles widened past what teeth could do.

"Run," Corvin said.

They ran. The courtiers came alive all at once — not charging, nothing so honest — *flowing*, converging with terrible patience, hands outstretched, and where those hands touched a fleeing maid near the doors, the girl simply sat down mid-stride and began to smile. Astrid dragged Juniper over a fallen bench, felt fingers graze her hair, cold as brine. Wren threw a lantern; it burst and bought them three seconds of beautiful firelight chaos.

Then they were at the dais, and Corvin had the flagstone up, and the dark of the old stair breathed out at them like a held sigh finally released.

"You first," Corvin said, and pushed Juniper gently toward the hole. "Both of you. Go."

Astrid looked at him and understood, in the way you understand a debt only when the collector arrives, what he intended.

"No."

"Yes." He smiled — his own smile, crooked and tired, the one she'd trusted since she was six years old and he'd taught her which memories weighed least. "Someone holds the stair. You know the arithmetic, Astrid. I've done it ten thousand times. It's the only sum I ever taught you honestly."

"The vault key—"

"Is yours now." He pressed it into her palm and folded her fingers over it, and his hands were shaking, and that frightened her more than the courtiers did. "I should have told you everything twenty years ago. I should have let you hate me while you still had a childhood to hate me from. Instead I stole your grief along with your name, and called it protection, and told myself love was just loyalty with better manners." His voice cracked clean through. "It wasn't. It was cowardice wearing your mother's coat."

Behind him, the courtiers had stopped. All of them. Standing in a perfect crescent at the edge of the firelight, smiling, waiting, as if they knew exactly how long this farewell would take and were content to let it finish.

"Corvin—" Her throat closed around his name.

"Astrid Okafor." He said it slowly, tasting it, the way he'd once tasted other people's memories before pouring them into salt-glass. "Keeper's daughter. Worst student I ever had. Best thief this valley ever made." He kissed her forehead, brief and rough, smelling of lamp oil and old paper. "Your mother sealed the vault with a song. Your father bargained away his own name to buy you time. And I lied to you every day of your life because the truth would have put you in that thing's mouth." He stepped back, toward the crescent of hollow faces. "So take the truth downstairs instead. Unbind him. Make my lying worth something."

"They'll kill you."

"No," he said, and drew himself up in his shabby keeper's robes, and for one moment he looked exactly like what he'd been: the last royal memory-keeper, standing in the house of the king he'd served. "They'll empty me. There's a difference, and it matters. Everything I am goes into the dark with my eyes open — and the Salt gets nothing it can use, because I've spent the last hour forgetting myself on purpose. Every step of our road together. Your face, child. I'm giving it back to the dark unremembered." He laughed, wet and short. "First honest theft of my career. I'm stealing *me* from *it*."

The courtiers moved.

Corvin turned to meet them with his arms spread wide, filling the stairwell mouth the way he'd filled every doorway of her childhood — too large, too loud, absolutely certain — and Astrid grabbed Juniper and dropped into the dark.

She caught one more glimpse from below: her mentor, silhouetted against the burning throne hall, holding the narrow stone throat of the stair against a tide of smiling emptiness. She heard him say something she couldn't make out. She chose to believe it was her name.

Then the flagstone boomed shut above them, and the sound that followed — thud, thud, thud, patient as surf — was Corvin's body keeping a promise.

Juniper tugged her sleeve. In the blackness his hand found hers and pressed it flat against his own chest, over his heart, and then — barely, barely — he hummed two notes of the old tongue. The song his mother had used. The thread.

Below them, far down in the roots of the palace, something vast turned over in its sleep, and Astrid felt the dark recognize her: *keeper's daughter, come home.*

She lit the lamp with hands that would not stop shaking, took the boy's hand again, and started down the stairs toward the drowned king — carrying a key, a song, and the last gift of a man who had loved her in the only language he'd ever learned fluently:

the lie.
