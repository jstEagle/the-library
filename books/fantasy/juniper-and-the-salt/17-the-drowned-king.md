## 17. The Drowned King

The stair went down farther than the palace had any right to be deep.

Astrid counted steps because counting was something her hands and feet could do while the rest of her screamed. Two hundred. Three hundred. The walls changed from dressed stone to raw salt-rock, glittering faintly, sweating brine that ran in slow silver threads and pooled on the treads. Juniper rode on her hip now — he was too big for it, really, all elbows and mud-colored hair, but neither of them suggested she put him down. His arms were locked around her neck. His breath came fast against her ear.

Behind them, above them, Corvin held the stair.

She could still hear him. That was the worst of it — not silence, but sound. The ring of his knife against hollowed steel. His voice, steady as a man reading a ledger: *Again. Again. Left side, girl, they always favor the left.* He had taught her to fight the way he'd taught her to steal, which was to say cruelly, thoroughly, with an affection so dry you could mistake it for contempt until the moment you couldn't.

Then the sounds stopped, and the dark behind her became just dark, and Astrid did not let herself think about what that meant because thinking about it would put her on the floor, and Juniper needed her walking.

*Later,* she told herself, the way she'd once told clients. *Grief keeps. It's the one thing nobody ever wants stolen.*

At the bottom of the stair was a door with no lock, no handle, no hinge. It was a slab of salt-glass taller than two men, and inside it, suspended like a fly in amber, hung a crown.

Not a metaphor. An actual crown, iron and black gold, drifting in the translucent stone, turning slowly, patiently, the way things turn when they have all the time in the world. The Salt's anchor. She felt it before she understood it — a pull at the base of her skull, gentle as a hook through soft flesh. Every oath sworn in the capital above was a thread running down through the rock into that crown, and every thread ended in a mouth that was always eating.

Juniper made a small sound. Not fear. Recognition.

"I know," Astrid said. Her voice came out rough. "I know, little fish."

He had stopped being *the boy* somewhere on the road and become *little fish*, because of the way he swam through other people's minds the way she moved through crowds — silently, without disturbing the water. He pressed his palm flat against the salt-glass, and where his skin touched, the surface clouded like breath on a winter window.

And the window opened.

Not physically. Nothing so kind. But Astrid felt the threshold give the way she felt a memory give when she gripped it right — a softening, a yielding, a door that had only been waiting for someone with the blood to knock. Keeper's blood. Her mother's word rose unbidden from the fragments Corvin had returned to her, a word in the dead tongue that meant both *keeper* and *key*, and Astrid stepped through the glass with Juniper's hand in hers, and the world went white.

* * *

Inside was the king's mind, and the king's mind was drowning.

There was no other word for it. They stood — did they stand? Astrid decided they stood, because the mind obliged her, shaping itself around her expectations like water around a dropped stone — on a shore of grey silt beneath a sky the color of old milk. The sea here was not water. It was memory, vast and slow-moving, and it rose in tides that never crested, swallowing the landscape inch by patient inch. Where it touched, things dissolved. A colonnade. A garden. A woman laughing, her face already gone smooth as an egg.

And in the middle distance, walking out of the flood with his crown still on his head, was a drowned man.

He was enormous and diminished at once — a king's silhouette worn down to a wick. Salt crusted his shoulders like frost. Each wave broke over him and each wave took something, and he let it, because there was nothing left in him to refuse with. His eyes, when they found Astrid, were the eyes of a man looking up through ice.

"You're late," Aldric said.

His voice was ruinous, barely a voice at all, more the memory of one. But underneath the ruin was something dry and dark and unexpectedly funny, and Astrid felt her throat tighten, because she knew that tone. She'd heard it in Corvin's voice for twenty years. Grief that has learned manners.

"Your son is here," she said.

Something moved across the drowned king's face. It might have been hope. Hope looks different on a starving face — thinner, more afraid.

"Juniper." Aldric knelt. It cost him visibly, the great joints grinding like a ship in weather. The boy stepped forward, and then stopped, and looked up at Astrid first — asking permission, or offering reassurance, she couldn't tell which, and it broke something loose in her chest either way. "You kept my tongue," Aldric said to him. "They tried to drown it out of you and you kept it."

Juniper answered in the dead language, three words, soft. Astrid understood them without understanding how: *I brought him.* Meaning the fragment. Meaning the piece of his father's true mind that loyal hands had smuggled out of the palace sewn into a child's skin before the Salt finished its meal.

"That fragment," Aldric said, and now he looked at Astrid, and his eyes were terrible and clear. "It's why I'm still me. A splinter of myself, floating. Enough to remember my daughter's name. Not enough to lift a finger." He raised one salt-crusted hand. "Do you know what I am, under this? Do you know what it did to take me?"

"It eats oaths," Astrid said. "Corvin told me. It feeds on loyalty."

"Loyalty." The king laughed, and the laugh was worse than weeping. "Yes. It found the richest table in the world. Every crown is made of oaths, girl. Every kingdom is a granary. It didn't conquer us. It sat down to dinner." He gestured at the rising tide. "This is what remains when it's finished with a mind. The silt. The flood takes the rest, year by year, oath by oath. And when I am entirely gone, it will wear my shape until the body fails, and then it will find another crown. There is always another crown."

"How do we stop it?"

"You don't stop a hunger. You break its *hold*." Aldric's gaze went past her, to the horizon of milk-white sky, where something vast turned over in the deep — the Salt itself, aware of them now, curious, patient. "My coronation oath. The first oath it ever drank from me. It's the anchor. Break the oath, break the binding, and everything it has swallowed through me comes back up. Every hollowed courtier. Every empty soldier. All of them, filled again."

"Then break it."

"With what?" And here the king smiled, and it was the saddest thing Astrid had seen in a life crowded with sad things. "An oath is a memory, keeper. The moment I swore it, the Salt swallowed the swearing — the words, the weight, the meaning. You cannot renounce what you no longer possess. To break the oath, the oath must be *returned*. Poured back into the breach where it was taken. Filled to overflowing, until the anchor chokes on it and lets go."

Astrid felt it coming the way you feel a stair miss its last step.

"And who pours?"

"A keeper," Aldric said gently. "Only a keeper can carry memory across the breach. Only a keeper can pour." He looked at her, and then — slowly, with the whole weight of a father's terror in it — he looked at Juniper.

"No," Astrid said.

"You haven't heard the price."

"I said no."

"The breach doesn't open for free." The tide lapped at their feet, grey and warm as bathwater. "Memory poured into it does not return. It isn't given. It's *consumed* — burned as fuel to force the oath back down the Salt's throat. Everything poured is gone forever. From everyone." He straightened, and the salt on his shoulders crackled. "All of mine is already eaten. The boy carries a fragment, not a life — it would burn too fast, too thin. It might choke the anchor. It might not. But a keeper—" his voice caught, actually caught, this ruined king, "—a keeper who has spent a lifetime carrying other people's pasts. A keeper who has just reclaimed her own. A keeper full to the brim with stolen years and buried childhood—"

"Don't say it."

"—would burn long enough. Would burn *bright* enough. Would fill the breach so completely the anchor would have no choice but to release."

The milk-white sky seemed to press closer. Somewhere in the deep, the Salt turned over again, and Astrid felt it taste the edge of her thoughts, the way it had tasted them in the throne room — that horrible, gentle attention, that infinite patience. *Come,* it seemed to say. *Or send the child. Either mouth will do.*

"All of it," Astrid said. Her voice sounded far away, like someone else's. "Every memory I have. Mine and everyone else's. Everything."

"Everything," Aldric agreed. "And what remains afterward is what you were before your mother first set a memory in your hands. Before language. Before names." He knelt again, bringing his ruined face level with hers, and this close she could see that his eyes were wet, and that the wetness was not seawater. "I have watched this thing eat a kingdom. I will not watch it eat a child. But I am not the one who decides, and I will not pretend to be. That is the one oath left to me — that I won't lie to you."

Juniper tugged her sleeve. She looked down. He was watching her with those old, old eyes, and he lifted his small hands and signed — no, spoke, in the dead tongue, and the words assembled themselves in her mind with terrible clarity:

*I remember you carrying me. I remember the storm. If it must be someone, it should be me. I am small. There is less of me to lose.*

"There isn't less of anyone," Astrid said, and her voice cracked clean through. "That's the whole point. That's the entire point of everything."

She looked at the king. She looked at the boy. She looked at the tide, still rising, still patient, already at their knees — and beyond it, at the shore of silt where the last unlabeled shapes of a drowned mind lay scattered like furniture in a flooded house.

Somewhere above them, a mentor was dead on a stair. Somewhere above that, a smuggler was spending himself like coin. Somewhere above that, a court of hollow faces was smiling with borrowed mouths. Everyone in this story had paid. Everyone had poured themselves out for everyone else, drop by drop, and called it love.

And now the ledger had come to her, and it wanted everything, and the only question left in the world was whose name was written on the line.

Juniper's hand found hers. Small. Warm. Trusting her with the whole of his weight, the way he had since the storm door, the way he had since the beginning.

Astrid closed her eyes.

When she opened them, she was smiling, and the smile frightened even her.

"Get behind me," she said, "both of you. And whatever happens — whatever you see me forget — don't let go of the song."

Because she had one thing the Salt had never tasted. One thread it couldn't cut, because it wasn't a memory at all. It was the dead language itself, living in her now, poured into her by a child's dreams and a king's need — and if memory was the fuel, then the song would be the wick.

She reached down into herself, into the vault Corvin had unsealed, into the twenty years of stolen griefs and first kisses and mothers' faces and wars, into her own mother's hands and her own erased childhood, and she gathered it all the way she had once gathered a widow's sorrow, gently, expertly, completely.

The tide rose. The Salt leaned in, hungry, certain, patient.

Astrid Okafor began to sing.
