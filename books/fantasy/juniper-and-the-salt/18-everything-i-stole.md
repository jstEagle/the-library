## 18. Everything I Stole

The drowned king's mind was a sea with no floor.

I stood in it — or whatever stood passes for standing when you are a thought inside another man's skull — and I watched Aldric drift past me like a shipwreck that had forgotten it was ever a ship. He was smaller than I'd imagined. Not the boy-sized ghost of Juniper's dreams, not the crowned colossus the Salt wore upstairs like a stolen coat. Just a tired man in the ruins of his own memory, holding onto a single image the way a drowning sailor holds a plank.

The image was a garden. A small boy, laughing, with dirt on his knees and a sprig of some flowering weed tucked behind his ear.

Juniper.

"No," I said aloud, and my voice made ripples in the white. "You don't get to keep him either."

Because that was the choice, laid out clean as a knife on a table. The breach in Aldric's mind — the wound the Salt had carved to climb inside him — could only be closed from this side. And closing it required filling it. A keeper's pour. Every memory, given freely, poured into the gap until the gap was no longer hungry and the walls knitted shut around what remained.

Corvin had told me how it worked, back when he still thought he'd have time to teach me everything. *A keeper can seal any breach,* he'd said, *but the breach takes its price in kind. It eats what you feed it. All of it.*

All of it. My whole self, or the child's.

Juniper was waiting above me. I could feel him through the thread — the thin silver singing-line of his dead language that had carried me down here, that had held the door of Aldric's skull open while the hollowed court hammered at the doors of the vault stair behind us. He was singing still. His voice came down through the white like light through water, wavering, exhausted, a nine-year-old boy who had been carrying a king in his chest since before he could talk, and who did not know yet what was being weighed against him in the dark.

*Pour the child in,* the breach whispered. It didn't speak in words so much as in hungers, but I understood it the way you understand cold. *Small things fill small spaces. The little one would fit. The little one would never know.*

"You first," I told it.

And then I turned to Aldric, drifting in his garden, and I said the thing I had come all this way to say.

"Your Majesty. I'm a thief."

He turned. His eyes were the color of the valley salt flats at dusk, and they were so sad that something in my chest cracked along an old seam.

"I've stolen memories for fifteen years," I said. "Griefs, first loves, the faces of the dead, the shame of men who couldn't live with what they'd done. I took them because people paid me, and then I kept them because taking was the only thing I knew how to do." I swallowed. "I'm going to give them back now. Not to their owners. To you. To the hole in you. And then I'm going to give you mine."

"Astrid." His voice was rust and riverbed. The king beneath the king. "Child, do you know what you're offering?"

"I know exactly what I'm offering." I laughed, and it came out wrong, wet. "That's the joke. I spent my whole life hoarding other people's yesterdays because I didn't have any of my own. Turns out I was a vault waiting for a breach."

Down the thread, Juniper's song faltered.

*No,* the song said, in the old tongue that my bones understood even though my mind had only just learned it. *No, no, keeper-mine, no.*

"Yes," I answered, in the same tongue, badly, my accent a cart in a rutted road. "Hush and hold the door."

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about memory: it has weight.

When Corvin taught me the lift — the delicate, surgical theft, sliding a widow's grief out of her skull like a splinter — he taught me that memories weigh almost nothing coming out. A gram of grief. An ounce of joy. You carry a hundred lifetimes in your head and your shoulders never ache.

But that is only true of other people's memories, held loosely, the way you hold a stranger's coat.

Your own memories are sewn into you.

I sat down in the white — actually sat, cross-legged, absurdly, in the drowning place of a mad king — and I opened myself the way I had never once opened myself in fifteen years of thieving. Not a lift. Not a skim. The whole vault, unbarred.

And I began to pour.

The widow's grief went first. I had carried it for six years, a black stone of a thing, a husband lost to fever with his hand half-raised in goodbye. I let it go and felt it fall through me like a coin down a well, and the breach caught it, tasted it, and shuddered. *More,* it said. It would always say more. That was the whole of the Salt, I understood suddenly — not malice, not empire, just appetite wearing a crown because crowns were where the oaths were thickest. Hunger, dressed as majesty.

"More," I agreed. "You want more? I'll show you more."

The smuggler Wren's memory of his mother's lullaby, bought off him in a card game he'd cheated at anyway. Gone. The taste of it left my mouth like smoke.

A lord's confession of a murder committed twenty years ago in an orchard. Gone. I had kept it as leverage and told myself that was prudence. It was just weight.

A bride's terror on her wedding morning. A soldier's last sight of home — poppies, a red roof, a dog running the fence line. A child's memory of being born, which should have been impossible to keep and yet I'd held it for a decade like a pearl too big for its setting. A traitor's face. A saint's doubt. The smell of bread in a village I'd robbed and never returned to.

Gone. Gone. Gone.

Each one fell into the breach and the breach grew quieter, the way a screaming animal goes quiet when it finally eats. The white around Aldric thickened, took on colors — poppy-red, wedding-white, orchard-green — because the breach was no longer empty, and empty places don't have colors.

And Aldric watched me with his rust-and-riverbed eyes and wept without moving, because he was a king and kings learn early to cry in place.

* * *

Somewhere above me, the doors were giving way.

I felt it through the thread — Wren's mind flickering like a lamp in wind, his thoughts arriving in fragments: *stair's gone, girl, whatever you're doing, the stair is GONE—* and then Corvin, older and steadier and burning down fast, holding the last landing alone against a hundred oath-drained courtiers who smiled as they climbed over each other's bodies.

Hold, old man, I thought. Hold one more minute. I'm spending your life's work right now and I refuse to waste it.

Because here was the arithmetic Corvin had never taught me, the equation every keeper learns alone or not at all: a memory given away freely does not leave a hole. A memory taken leaves a scar. But a memory *given* — given in love, in sacrifice, in the full knowledge of what it is — leaves something else. A shape. An absence shaped exactly like the thing that filled it, the way a mold remembers the statue.

I would lose everything. I might remain a person. Those were different fates, and I was betting my soul they were.

The stolen years ran out faster than I expected. Fifteen years of theft sounds like an ocean until you start pouring it, and then it's a bucket, a cup, a spoonful. The breach stirred again, restless, tasting the dregs.

And then there was nothing left between it and the two things I had saved for last.

Juniper's song wavered above me, thinner now. Through the thread I felt Corvin take a wound — felt it the way you feel a door slam in a house you grew up in — and get up anyway.

"All right," I said to the white. "All right. You wanted the keeper's bloodline. Here it is."

My own past. The reclaimed years, three days old in my head and older than kingdoms in my bones.

My mother's hands, quick and brown, sorting salt-glass vials by the color of the light they held. Her voice teaching me the old tongue as a game, *this word means keeper, this word means door, this word means the-one-who-stands-in-the-doorway-so-the-dark-cannot-pass.* Her face on the last day, calm as still water, sealing the vault with me on the outside of it and her name already leaving my mouth.

The Salt's first rising — I had been four, and I remembered it as weather, a white storm that ate the shouting men. My father's bargain, struck in a cellar: *take her memory, take her childhood, take her name if you must, but she will be nobody, and nobody is the one thing it cannot find.* Corvin younger than I'd ever known him, weeping as he worked the lift on a sleeping child, whispering *forgive me, forgive me, forgive me* — and I had heard those words my whole life without knowing they were addressed to me.

Every birthday I hadn't had. Every grave I hadn't visited. The whole buried country of Astrid Okafor, dug up at last, and I gave it away in armfuls.

It hurt worse than anything. It hurt like being unmade politely. Each memory tore free with roots attached, and I screamed somewhere far away in the white, and the breach drank my mother's laugh, my father's cowardice, Corvin's tears, the word for *keeper*, the word for *door* —

and I held back exactly one thing.

Not a memory. Smaller than that. The mold-shape, the absence-with-a-form, the place where Astrid had been. I cupped it in whatever I still was and I did not let the breach touch it, because a person needs a vessel even if the vessel is empty, and I intended — God help me, I intended — to walk out of this sea with a vessel.

"Last of it," I whispered. "Come and get it."

* * *

The breach rose up around me like a tide deciding.

And in that moment, with nothing left in me but the shape of myself and the sound of a child singing, I finally understood the last piece — the piece Corvin died not knowing, the piece the Salt had never learned in all its centuries of eating.

Oaths feed it. Theft feeds it. Taking and binding and owning — all of it is food, because all of it is grip, and grip is what hunger understands.

But a gift is not food. A gift is poison.

Everything I had poured in, I had poured freely. No price asked, no hold retained, no ownership claimed. Fifteen years of stolen lives and one reclaimed childhood, surrendered without a single thread of possession left attached — and the breach, built entirely of taking, had no teeth for it. The memories didn't dissolve into its hunger. They *filled* it. They packed the wound like earth packs a grave, dense and final, and the hunger found itself surrounded on every side by things it could not digest because they had already been let go.

You cannot devour what refuses to be possessed.

The breach screamed. The white shook. And down the silver thread, Juniper — clever, stubborn, impossible Juniper — felt the turn and changed his song. The old tongue shifted key, from holding to loosing, from *door* to *keeper*, and his voice came pouring down the thread into the dying breach like sunlight into a cellar:

*the-one-who-stands-in-the-doorway-so-the-dark-cannot-pass*

I sang it with him. Badly. Accent like a cart in a rutted road. Tears I no longer had memories to attach to, falling in a sea that was turning gold at the edges, green, poppy-red, wedding-white.

And the breach closed.

Not slowly. Not gently. It snapped shut around everything I had been like a book slamming, and the last thing I poured in — deliberately, choosing it, my final act as a person with a past — was the image of a small boy with dirt on his knees and a flower behind his ear, laughing in a garden, so that the king adrift in his own ruins would have something to swim toward.

Then the white took my name, my mother's name, my father's bargain, Corvin's forgiveness, fifteen years of other people's yesterdays, and the word for *door*, and the word for *keeper* —

and gave me back the silence where I used to be.

* * *

I woke on cold stone with someone shaking me, and the first thing I saw was a face I did not know.

He was young-ish, sharp-eyed, bleeding from the ear, grinning like a man who has outrun something enormous. Behind him a stairwell was choked with fallen courtiers, and among them, folded neatly against the wall with his hands in his lap and his eyes toward the ceiling, was an old man who was not breathing, whose face even I — knowing nothing, having nothing — somehow recognized as the face of someone who had loved me very much and paid for it.

I looked at the stranger. I looked at the old man. I looked at my own hands, which were a thief's hands, scarred and steady, and meant nothing to me at all.

"Well," said the stranger, and his voice cracked clean down the middle. "Well. Hello, Astrid."

The name landed in me like a coin dropped into an empty well. I waited for the splash of recognition, the echo of a life.

There was no splash. There was only the well, and the dark, and — far above us, through broken stone, through a palace waking from a hundred-year nightmare — a small voice singing in a language I no longer understood, though some part of me, deeper than memory, deeper than the theft, hummed along.
