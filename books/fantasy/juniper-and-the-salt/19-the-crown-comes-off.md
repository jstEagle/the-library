## 19. The Crown Comes Off

The first thing I know is the taste of salt.

Not the sea's salt. Not sweat. Something older — the taste of a word held too long in the mouth until it stops being a word and becomes a stone you cannot spit out. It sits on my tongue like a coin bitten by every thief in the kingdom.

The second thing I know is that I have been screaming.

I understand this not because I hear myself but because my throat is a ruin, because the sound has been going on somewhere beneath me, below the floor of me, in a cellar where they keep the things a king is not allowed to say. The scream has been mine for years. Perhaps decades. It has worn grooves into me like water wearing stone, and now, suddenly, it has stopped, and the silence where it lived is so vast I fall into it.

I open my eyes.

White. Everything white. No — not everything. A room resolving itself out of the white the way a face resolves out of fog: pillars, banners gone grey with dust, a dais, and light coming from nowhere and everywhere at once, the light of a place that has forgotten what windows are for.

And a girl.

She is kneeling in front of me, or she was kneeling; she is falling now, folding sideways like laundry slipping off a line, and there is blood at her nose and her eyes are open and empty as two coins left on a dead man's eyes. A boy catches her. Small, mud-brown, solemn as an owl. He says something in a language I have not heard since I was young enough to be carried, and the strange thing — the impossible thing, the thing that makes my heart lurch against ribs that feel borrowed — is that I understand him.

*Father,* the language says. Not to me. To the man drowning inside his own skull, wherever he is. *You can come up now.*

Up. The word lands in me like a hook through the gill.

Because I am underwater. I understand that too, all at once, the way a dreamer understands the dream is a dream only at the moment of waking. All this time — how long? — I have been under. Under the white, under the crown, under the thing that wears my face to court and smiles with my mouth and signs death warrants with my hand. I have been down in the deep of my own mind, in the cold dark where it keeps me, and I have watched through the surface above me as a king who is not me ruled my kingdom, fed on my people's oaths, grew fat and bright and hungry on their loyalty while I sank.

I remember drowning now. That is the cruelty of it — the memory comes back whole, in one piece, like a body pulled from a river. The night it took me. The vault beneath the palace. My own voice saying yes when I should have said no, because it had found the shape of my loneliness and poured itself into that shape, and what king alive would refuse a companion who agreed with everything he'd ever feared?

*I will carry your burdens,* it had said. And I, tired, so tired, thirty years old and already worn smooth by the office like a stair worn smooth by feet, had said: *Yes.*

An oath. Of course an oath. Everything in this kingdom runs on oaths the way mills run on water, and I gave it the largest one I owned. I swore my crown to it, and it climbed up through the oath like a serpent climbing a rope, and it has been wearing me ever since.

The girl falls. The boy holds her. And I —

I rise.

It is not graceful. Nothing about this is graceful. Rising through my own mind is like rising through frozen lake-water, through layers of cold that each try to close over my head, and each layer is a year I lost, and I pass them like floors of a burning building: the year the granaries failed and I signed the order I did not sign; the year my wife stopped meeting my eyes because the thing wearing me had begun to look at her the way a butcher looks at stock; the year a child was born and taken away before I could hold him, and I felt the arms that held him and they were not my arms —

Juniper.

My son.

He is standing three feet away from me, real, breathing, mud still dried in the creases of his knuckles, and he is seven years old, or eight, and he has my father's brow and my mother's stubborn chin, and he is holding a woman who gave everything — I can see it, see the blankness spreading behind her eyes like frost across glass, see the places where she used to be already smoothed over — and he is singing.

That is what pulls me the last distance. Not duty. Not rage, though the rage is there, vast and patient as a glacier. It is the song. His old tongue, the tongue of the keepers, the tongue my grandmother sang over cradles, and it moves through the white of this drowned place like a current, and I grab it, and it carries me.

I break the surface.

* * *

For a moment I am in both places at once, and I will not pretend otherwise, because a man should be honest in the last hour of his life even if he was never honest in any of the others.

I am in the vault-dark of my mind, gasping, alive. And I am also in the throne room, because the thing is still there, still wearing me, still seated on the throne with its hands — my hands — folded on the armrests, watching the girl collapse with an expression of mild interest, the way a farmer watches weather.

It feels me rise. I feel it feel me.

*No,* it says, and its voice is everywhere, is the walls, is the salt in the mortar between the stones. *You are done. You were done decades ago. Go back down.*

"I am the King of Cerenne," I say, and my voice comes out cracked, ruined, a hinge that hasn't turned in twenty years. "And you are a guest."

*A guest.* Something like laughter moves through the stone. *I am the house.*

"You are hunger," I say. "I know the difference now. Hunger wears whatever feeds it best. For me, it wore agreement. For my lords, it wore ambition. For my soldiers, it wore belonging." I take a step forward in the dark of myself, and marvel — marvel — that my legs obey me, that anything obeys me anymore. "But hunger has one weakness, Salt. It cannot survive the end of the meal."

*There is no end,* it says, and for the first time I hear something beneath the vastness of it. Something quick. Something almost small. *The oaths renew. Loyalty renews. Every generation kneels. I am patient as—*

"No," I say. "They don't kneel to you. They kneel to the crown. And the crown—" I look up, in the dark, at the shape I have been avoiding for twenty years, the anchor of iron and gold sitting on the head of the thing that wears me, the chain running from it down through the throne, through the foundations, into the earth, binding every hollowed face in this palace to my perjury. "The crown was never yours. It was sworn by me. And I am here now, and I unsay it."

Silence. Then the whole buried palace shudders, and the Salt understands, all at once, what I have understood for perhaps four seconds, and it moves to stop me with the speed of panic, and it is too late, because I have spent twenty years learning patience in the dark, and patience always beats panic.

*Wait,* it says. *Aldric. Wait. I can give you back the years. I can give you her — your wife, I kept her memories, I kept everything, you can have—*

"There it is," I say softly. "The truth of you at last. Not power. Comfort." And I think of the girl on the floor, the thief, the keeper's daughter, who told me once — whose voice reaches me even now, faint, through the thinning walls of my own mind — that the Salt had offered her the same currency. *It doesn't tempt with thrones,* she said. *It tempts with home.* "Every soul you ever ate, you ate by offering them back what they'd lost. You are not a king of anything. You are a pawnshop of grief."

*I kept them safe,* it says, and the voice cracks, actually cracks, and I hear the child-thing underneath, the hunger that started small once, that only ever wanted not to be empty. *They were going to be lost. Everyone is lost. I kept them—*

"I know," I say, and I mean it, and that is the worst mercy I have ever given. "But keeping is not the same as loving. Put them down."

Then I speak the oath.

* * *

I spoke it at nineteen, in this same hall, with the crown heavy on my head and my father's coffin still fresh behind me, and I spoke it well, because I was raised to speak well, and every word of it is still carved into the air of this room the way footprints are carved into snow. Oaths do not fade here. Oaths are the only things in Cerenne that never fade.

*I, Aldric of House Veyra, swear by crown and salt and stone to bear the burdens of my people until my last breath, to hold what is given me and yield nothing that is sworn—*

I say it backward.

Not the words reversed — that is story-book nonsense. I say it *backward*, which is to say: I unsay it. I stand in the deepest cellar of my own mind, in the place where my scream wore its grooves, and I take each clause of my coronation and I release it, deliberately, formally, with all the ceremony it was given in the first place, because an oath made in ceremony must be broken in ceremony or it does not break at all.

"To bear the burdens of my people" — released. Let them bear their own; they are stronger than I ever believed, and I have the evidence kneeling bloody-nosed on the flagstones upstairs.

"To hold what is given me" — released. I was never meant to hold a kingdom the way a fist holds a bird.

"And yield nothing that is sworn" — and here I stop, because this is the clause the Salt lives in, the load-bearing lie, the promise that let it climb the rope of my word into the seat of me. Yield nothing. Hold forever. Grip until the gripped thing dies.

"Yield everything," I say instead. "I yield everything."

The crown comes off.

I feel it happen in both worlds at once. In the throne room, my hands — the hands of the thing — rise against its will, trembling, fighting, and lift the iron-and-gold weight from its own head, and the moment the metal clears skin the chain of oaths running down through the palace begins to snap, link by link by link, and I hear them go, all across the city, all across the valley and the lowlands beyond: the lord in his manor blinking awake mid-sentence; the soldier on the wall dropping his spear and looking at his hands as though seeing them for the first time in years; the chambermaid, the tax-clerk, the bishop, the headsman, each of them surfacing gasping from a long dive they never chose to take.

In my mind, the white drains away like water finding a crack in a dam. And what is underneath the white is not glory. It is a small grey room. It is morning. It is ordinary light, the kind I have not seen in twenty years, falling through a window that does not exist onto a floor that does not exist, and I sit down in it, in the middle of my own recovered self, and I weep like a boy.

* * *

The Salt does not die. I want that recorded, whoever finds this, whoever remembers it after. It does not die. Starving things do not die quickly; they shrink, and retreat, and wait. I feel it go — feel it pour out of the walls and down through the foundations and away into the deep earth where it was born, diminished, furious, patient still, patient as geology. We have not killed it. We have only ended its tenancy.

But its tenant is leaving, and we both know it, and in the last moment before it goes it turns to me one final time, and what it says is not a threat.

*Was it worth it?* it asks. *Twenty years in the dark. Was the kingdom worth it?*

And I think about my answer, because I owe the truth to something, even to this.

"You were in the chair," I say. "Not me. So ask yourself whether *your* twenty years were worth it." I pause. "Mine haven't happened yet. I intend to make them count in whatever time I'm given. Which brings me to the matter of rent."

*Rent.*

"You wore my body. You ate my people. You kept my wife's memories in your vaults like jars of preserves." I stand, in the grey room, in the morning light. "So before you go: give them back. All of them. Every oath you swallowed, every memory you hoarded, every name you took. Pour it all back into the world on your way down. Consider it restitution."

It is silent a long moment. Then, faintly, from very far beneath the earth, I hear the sound of a vast door opening, and a wind that smells of rain and old paper rising through the roots of the palace, carrying things home.

*You are a strange king,* it says.

"I was a tired one," I say. "There's a difference. Goodbye."

* * *

When I come back to my body — my true body, the one that has been sitting unused in a sealed chamber beneath the throne for longer than I can bear to calculate — it takes three tries to open my eyes.

The first try, I see only white, and I panic, thinking it has kept me after all. The second try, the white resolves into ceiling stone, damp-stained, ordinary, beautiful. The third try, I turn my head, and there they are.

The girl — Astrid, the boy called her, Astrid — lies on the flags beside me, breathing, blank-eyed, emptied of everything she poured into me to pull me out. The boy kneels over her, and when he sees my eyes open he goes absolutely still, the way wild things go still, and then he crawls to me across the stones and puts his small hand flat on my chest, right over the heart, the way healers do, the way keepers do.

"Father," he says. Out loud. In the common tongue. His first word in it, I would swear; his accent is terrible; it is the finest thing I have ever heard.

"Hello," I manage. My voice is gravel and rust. "You've grown."

His face crumples and he climbs onto me, careful of my frailty, and holds on, and I hold him back with arms that weigh nothing and feel like they could carry kingdoms.

I do not have long. I know it the way you know weather coming. Whatever the Salt drained from this body over the years, it did not return with the rest; the girl bought my mind back, not my flesh, and the flesh has been dying politely for two decades, waiting for someone to inform it that the news could finally be delivered. There is no bitterness in the knowledge. There is, unexpectedly, relief — the specific relief of a man setting down a load he has carried so long his shoulders have forgotten their shape without it.

Wren appears — the smuggler, sharp-faced, wet-eyed and pretending not to be — and Corvin, old Corvin, who I thought dead these twenty years, who I now understand has been hiding in a salt valley raising my rescuer, and I laugh, which costs me a rib, and I tell him he owes me an apology and a drink, in whichever order the gods prefer.

But mostly there is Juniper. I hold my son, and I tell him the things that matter, quickly, because time is short and kings are practiced at brevity: that his mother loved him with her last act and it worked; that the crown is a tool and tools can be set down; that the song he sang in the dark reached further than any army I ever fielded. And then I ask him to sing it again, just once, here, in the damp and the dust, and he does, his small voice cracking on the high notes, and I close my eyes and listen to the language of my grandmother's cradle carry me out past the walls of myself.

The last thing I feel is his hand in mine, and the last thing I think is not of crowns or kingdoms or the ancient hunger sleeping in the deep earth, biding.

It is: *so this is what morning feels like.*

And then the tired man rests, at last, and the crown lies on the stone beside him, off, at last, and far above, through the broken doors of the buried chamber, the first true dawn in twenty years is coming up over a court full of people waking — and none of them yet knows what has woken with them, deep beneath the world, turning over in its sleep, beginning, slowly, to starve.
