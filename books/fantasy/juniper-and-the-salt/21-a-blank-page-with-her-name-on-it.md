## 21. A Blank Page with Her Name On It

The first thing I learned about myself was that I liked honey in my tea.

It seems a small thing to build a life on. But when you wake with nothing — no mother's face, no first snowfall, no name you can feel in your mouth like your own tooth — the small things are the load-bearing walls. I stood in Wren's kitchen on the third morning after the world ended, holding a cup of tea I didn't remember asking for, and Juniper pushed the honey pot across the table without looking up from his bread.

"You take three spoons," he said. "You always took three."

I took three. It was sweet in a way that felt like recognition, like a hand finding a hand in the dark. And I thought: *so this is what I am. Someone who takes three spoons of honey.* It wasn't much. But it was mine, and no one had stolen it, and no one had given it back to me out of pity. It had simply survived, some stubborn root of me buried too deep even for sacrifice to reach.

I am told I gave everything away. I don't remember the giving. What I have instead is an inventory, assembled piece by piece by the people who loved me, and I have decided — deliberately, the way you decide to plant a garden — that this counts.

* * *

Wren tells stories the way other men gamble: too loudly, with stakes he can't cover, and always certain the next hand will fix everything.

He came by every evening that first month, ostensibly to check the roof, which did not need checking, and stayed until the candles guttered, telling me who I had been.

"You were the best thief in the Salt Valley," he said, on the fourth evening, feet up on my table like he owned it. "Not the flashiest. The quietest. I once watched you lift a grief out of a woman's head so gently she thanked you for it. Thanked you. Paid you extra. Walked home lighter than she'd been in years." He shook his head. "I've seen surgeons with less delicacy."

"Was I good company?"

"Terrible." He grinned. "You never laughed at my jokes. You watched everyone like they owed you money. You had this look — like the whole valley was a lock and you were waiting for someone to hand you the key so you could leave." He paused, and his voice dropped into something almost gentle, which on Wren's face looked like weather gone wrong. "Then the boy showed up at your door and you handed him the key yourself."

"What was I afraid of?"

He considered that longer than he considered most things. "Leaving," he said finally. "And staying. Same fear, two doors."

I wrote it down. I write everything down now — I'll come to that. That night I added to the ledger: *Astrid Okafor. Best thief in the valley. Did not laugh at jokes. Afraid of both doors.*

It is a strange thing, being a stranger's character study of yourself. Stranger still when the stranger is right.

* * *

Juniper teaches me songs.

He says I knew them before — that his old tongue lived in me somewhere, that I sang one once, badly, walking through the salt flats, and he'd nearly cried hearing it come out of a mouth that couldn't possibly know it. I don't remember. But when he sings, something in my chest turns toward him like a plant toward light, and I've stopped needing the memory to trust the turning.

His language has no word for *orphan*, he told me. It has a word that means roughly *the one the river carried*, and it isn't sad. Rivers carry things to places, not away from them. He taught me the word on a Tuesday, sitting on my doorstep, and made me say it back until my accent stopped offending him.

"That's you now," he said. "The one the river carried."

"To where?"

He shrugged with the enormous dignity only children and kings can manage. "Rivers don't tell. That's why there's a word."

Some nights he sings the mourning songs for his father — not the king, he's careful to say, but the fragment, the small true Aldric who lived in him through all those white years and who went quiet when the crown came off. He grieves in two directions at once, the way only a child can: openly, and without end. I sit with him and hold whatever part of him wants holding, and I do not need memories of my own mother to know what this is. Love, it turns out, does not keep records. It just keeps.

But some nights he sings the other songs — the ones about keepers, about women who carry other people's years in jars of glass and salt — and when he sings those, he watches me sidelong, the way you watch a fire you're not sure is out.

"There's a verse about you," he said once. "In the oldest song. About the keeper who poured herself out."

"I don't want to hear it."

"You will," he said, serene as tide. "Someday you'll ask. You always ask. Wren says so."

I asked Wren later if I'd been predictable. He said I'd been the most predictable person he ever met, except about the things that mattered, where I'd been completely insane. I wrote that down too.

* * *

Corvin left letters.

That sentence deserves its own paragraph, because Corvin — whom I know only as a shape in other people's stories, a man who held a stair against a hollowed court and fell so a thief and a child could go on living — Corvin apparently anticipated everything, including this. Including me, sitting here with a blank where a life should be, trying to assemble myself from scraps.

There are eleven letters. They were found among his effects and delivered by a palace clerk who would not meet my eye, and they are addressed simply: *For Astrid, when she asks.*

The first one begins: *You will hate me, and you should, briefly, and then you should stop, because hatred requires a self that remembers why, and I spent thirty years making sure yours would be free to begin again.*

He writes like a man building a bridge across a gorge he knows he won't cross himself. Letter by letter he gives me back my childhood — not the memories themselves, he couldn't do that, but their outlines, their shadows thrown on the wall. My mother's hands smelled of salt-glass and rosemary. My father bargained with the dark so that I wouldn't have to. I laughed at exactly the same joke at age four that I refuse to laugh at now (Wren confirmed this; I'm told it involves a goat). I was born outside the valley, under an open sky, in a house where memory was kept like water is kept in a drought — carefully, and for everyone.

*I erased you,* he writes in the sixth letter, *because the Salt hunts keeper's blood, and a child it cannot find is a child it cannot eat. I told myself it was duty. It was love wearing duty's coat, and I knew it, and I let the lie stand because the truth would have cost you everything. I am not sorry. I am sorry that I am not sorry. Both are true. You taught me that, though you won't remember teaching it.*

I read that letter four times. Then I put it down and walked out to the garden and stood in the rain for an hour, feeling something move through me that had no name and no history and needed neither.

Grief, I've decided, doesn't require remembering. It only requires loving. And apparently I loved him enough that even empty, I ache.

* * *

Here is what I have built so far. I keep it in a journal — a real one, paper and ink, because I trust nothing written inside my own head anymore:

*Astrid Okafor. Takes three spoons of honey. Best quiet thief in the Salt Valley; retired. Does not laugh at goat jokes. Afraid of both doors. Carried by the river. Loved a stubborn old liar who died holding a stair. Loves a boy who sings. Gave away everything she had and woke up lighter, and the lightness, everyone agrees, looked like peace.*

It is not a life. It is the scaffolding of one. But here is the thing nobody warned me about, the thing I discover new every morning like a gift left on the step: a self built from other people's accounts is still a self. More than that — it is a *chosen* self. Every fact I keep, I keep on purpose. Every flaw I accept, I accept with my eyes open. The woman I was remembered herself into being, and her memory was taken, and what remains is something rarer: a person assembled entirely from evidence of love.

Wren says I was colder before. Juniper says I was sadder. Both say I was braver, and I've decided to grow back into that one, the way you grow back into a coat that belonged to someone you admire.

* * *

Yesterday a letter came from the capital. Aldric — tired, grateful, unrecognizably human in his handwriting — reports that the court is learning to be empty in a new way: not drained, but open. Waiting to be filled with things they choose. He asks, delicately, whether I remember anything of our time together beneath the palace. I do not. I wrote back and told him that as far as I'm concerned we met yesterday, and that I like him fine, and that he owes me a debt he can repay by governing well. Juniper added a postscript in the old tongue that made the king's clerk weep, or so the next letter claims.

And yesterday evening, standing at my gate watching the sun go down over the flats — the flats that used to be safe, that used to be *nothing*, that are now just land like any other land, humming faintly with the magic that spilled in when the sanctuary fell — I felt the strangest sensation. Not memory. Not quite longing. Something like a door standing open in a wall I hadn't known was there.

Juniper came to stand beside me. He didn't sing. He just said, "You're going to ask soon."

"Ask what?"

"Where the river goes." He looked out at the darkening flats, at the far horizon where the coast must be, though I've never seen it — or I have, and don't know it. "Wren's fixing the boat. He thinks I don't know. He's terrible at secrets."

I stood there with my blank page and my borrowed life and my three spoons of honey, and I felt the door swing wider.

Tonight I'll ask. Tonight I'll pack. And tomorrow — the river willing — I'll find out what the one the river carried was carried *for*.
