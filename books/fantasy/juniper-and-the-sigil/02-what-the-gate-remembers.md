## 2. What the Gate Remembers

Sebastian Delacroix had three rules for his daughter, and she had broken all of them before supper.

Rule the first: never open the door to strangers after dark. Rule the second: never touch what a stranger leaves behind. Rule the third—the one he'd made her recite like a catechism since she was small enough to sit on his boot—*whatever comes through the gate stays on your side of it.*

She had opened the door. She had taken the map. And now she was standing in the kitchen holding it up to the lamp as though it were a shopping list and not, as far as Sebastian could tell, the end of every careful thing he had built in twenty years.

"Juniper," he said, in the voice he used for ledgers that wouldn't balance.

"It's salt," she said, not looking up. "The whole edge of it. He was—Da, he *dissolved*. Into salt, right there on the step. I swept him into the gutter because I didn't know what else to do, and then I felt terrible about it, so I said a few words."

"You gave a funeral to a puddle of brine."

"I gave a *dignified* funeral to a puddle of brine." She finally looked at him, and her eyes were doing the thing they did when an account refused to close—narrowed, hungry, already halfway to the answer. "He knew my name, Da. He said 'give this to the gatekeeper's daughter' and then he came apart like a wet biscuit. That's not nothing. That's *addressed post*."

Sebastian crossed the kitchen and held out his hand. "Give it here."

For a moment—one long, lamp-flickering moment—she hesitated. His daughter, who had never once hesitated to hand over anything: keys, reports, the last honey roll. Then she put the map in his palm, and he understood that the hesitation had cost her something, and that frightened him more than the map did.

He looked down.

And the floor went out from under him.

He knew the handwriting. He would have known it upside down, in the dark, drowned, dead—which, he supposed, in a manner of speaking, was exactly where he was reading it. The looping ascenders. The way she crossed her t's with a little flourish like a bird taking off. Twenty years of not letting himself think about it, and here it was anyway, patient as water finding its level.

*Elowen.*

"Da?" Juniper's voice came from very far away. "You've gone the color of the ledger's bad pages."

"Where is the man who gave you this," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Dissolved. I told you. Into—" She stopped. Watched him. Juniper missed nothing; it was her gift and her curse and the reason the guild trusted her with their books. "You know whose writing that is."

"No."

"You do. You've gone all still, the way you go when Mother's name comes up, except you usually go still for half a second and this has been—" she squinted at the lamp, counting, because of course she was counting, "—eleven seconds."

"Juniper." He set the map face-down on the table, gently, the way you'd set down something sleeping. "This is guild business. This is *gate* business. It is not yours."

"I'm the one he gave it to."

"He was dying and confused and possibly made of salt. Dying men will hand their hats to a lamppost." He heard how it sounded even as he said it—too loud, too fast—and hated himself for it.

Her chin came up. She had Elowen's chin too. The whole girl was a letter he kept rereading. "You're lying to me."

"I am keeping you safe. There's a difference, and someday you'll thank me for it."

"Those are the same sentence in different clothes, Da, and I keep both sets of books."

Gods, but he loved her. That was the whole trouble. He loved her the way the sea loves a seawall—constantly, enormously, and with the full knowledge that one day something would come along to test the masonry.

"Sit down," he said.

She sat. He almost hadn't expected her to obey, and the surprise must have shown, because she said, "I'm sitting because my feet hurt, not because you asked."

Fair enough. He put the kettle on, because in the Delacroix household, catastrophe was traditionally accompanied by tea, and because it gave his hands something honest to do while his mouth decided what it could bear to say.

* * *

He told her almost none of it.

That was the plan, going in: almost none. A father's rationing. But she sat across the table with her hands folded like an auditor awaiting figures, and she was twenty years old and sharp as a paper cut, and the words came out of him the way blood comes out of you—not because you want them to, but because there's a hole.

"There is a covenant," he said. "Between our world and the one on the other side of the gate. Older than the guild, older than the town, older than any book the guild pretends it wrote first. It keeps the two realms apart. It keeps them *sealed*. If it fails, things come through. Things that were never meant to have edges."

"What kind of things?"

"The kind you don't sweep into the gutter and say words over." He poured the tea. Her eyes flicked to the map, face-down between them, and he moved his cup onto it, casually, a paperweight of domesticity. "The covenant isn't free. Nothing worth sealing ever is. It was paid for, once. A debt was laid down, and the covenant draws on that debt the way a bank draws on gold."

"What debt?"

"An old one."

"Whose?"

"A settled matter." He drank his tea too fast and scalded his tongue, which felt appropriate. "Twenty years ago I took a post guarding that gate, and I have guarded it every day since, and in twenty years I have never once left it unwatched, and I am telling you now, as your father and as the gatekeeper: burn that map, forget the salty gentleman, and tomorrow we will laugh about it over pie."

Juniper was quiet for a moment. Then, gently, in the tone she used with guild clerks who had fudged a column: "You said 'a settled matter.' Nobody says 'settled' about a debt unless it isn't. Settled debts get called *paid*."

He should have raised her to be worse with numbers.

"And you said twenty years," she went on, softer now, which was worse. "Da. Mother died twenty years ago."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere out in the dark, the sea did its endless arithmetic against the shore.

"Do not," Sebastian said, "finish that thought."

"I'm not finishing it. I'm just standing next to it." She reached across the table, and he watched her hand come toward the map like watching a tide come in—inevitable, unhurried, entirely indifferent to his wishes. He put his own hand flat on top of it first.

"Juniper Delacroix," he said, "if you follow whatever road that map describes, I will—" He stopped. What? Disown her? Lock her in her room like a heroine in a ballad? He was a gatekeeper and a widower and a man who had once been young enough to believe promises were load-bearing. There was nothing he could actually do, and they both knew it, and the knowing sat between them like a third person at the table.

"You'll what?" she asked, not cruelly. Just honestly. She got her honesty from somewhere too.

"I'll worry," he said finally, deflated. "Enormously. Inconsolably. I'll worry at you the way the tide worries at a cliff, and I will not apologize for a single grain of it."

Something in her face cracked then—not defiance, just grief, arriving early. "Why won't you tell me? Whatever it is you're carrying, you've been carrying it since I was a baby. You think I haven't seen it? You hold it wrong, Da. You hold it like a hot pan. Everyone can see."

And because he was tired, and because the handwriting on that map had knocked twenty years of masonry loose in his chest, he gave her one true thing. Just one. A father's ration.

"The covenant was sealed with a life," he said. "Someone's life, given so the gate would close and stay closed. That's the debt it draws on. That's all you need to know, and it's already more than you need to know, and if you repeat a word of it I will deny it with such conviction that even I'll believe me."

Juniper had gone very pale. "Whose life?"

"Settled matter."

"*Whose.*"

"Drink your tea."

She drank her tea. It was the only order she'd obeyed all night, and they both pretended it meant he'd won.

* * *

He waited until she went up to bed. He listened to her door close, counted to a hundred—she'd have counted his counting, had she known, and grinned about it—and then he took the map to the hearth.

He stood a while with it in his hands first. That was weakness, and he allowed it, the way you allow yourself one look back at a house you're leaving. The paper was soft with salt-damp. Her flourishes leapt off every line. In the margin, in that same bird-taking-flight script, a single word he refused to read aloud even in the privacy of his own skull: *come.*

"I'm sorry, love," he murmured. "But I buried you once, and I did it properly, and I'll not dig you up for a map."

He fed it to the fire. It caught, curled, went orange at the edges, and he watched until the last flourish was ash, and he felt the way a man feels when he pays off a debt by selling something he can't replace—relieved, ruined, and certain he'd gotten the better end of the bargain.

Then he went upstairs to check on his daughter, because he always did, because some vigils don't end.

Her bed was empty. Her window was open. On her pillow, weighted down with her good ink pen, lay a note in a neat accountant's hand:

*Gone to copy something important. Back before you wake. Don't wait up. —J.*

*P.S. You taught me to always keep a duplicate of anything that matters. You called it "the first law of ledgers." I copied it yesterday, Da. Before you ever saw it. While you were being suspicious at the kettle.*

*P.P.S. Whose life, Da?*

Sebastian stood in his daughter's empty room for eleven seconds—he counted, because of course he counted—and then he went downstairs, took his coat off its hook, and did the thing he had not done in twenty years.

He left the gate unwatched.
