## 4. The Guild of Borrowed Years

They caught her on the third morning, in the market town of Saltmere, because she made the mistake every ledger-keeper swears never to make: she trusted a round number.

Juniper had been paying for everything in exact coin—seven coppers for bread, three for cheese, one for the ferryman who didn't ask questions—and counting the change aloud like a woman with nothing to hide. But when the innkeeper at the Gull and Anchor quoted her eleven coppers for a room, she paid twelve without thinking, because twelve was tidier, and somewhere in the back of her mind a small voice said *you'll want the change anyway*, and that small voice was wrong. The innkeeper's daughter pocketed the extra copper and remembered the face above it. By dusk, Mr. Vane knew which room she slept in.

She woke to lamplight and the smell of expensive cologne worn over something older underneath, like roses painted on rot.

"Miss Delacroix," said a voice she already hated. "You really must stop overpaying people. It's how empires fall."

* * *

The guild house in Saltmere was not what she expected. She had grown up in the counting-house at Grayharbor, all ledgers and lamp oil and her father's silence, and she had imagined the guild's true seat as something similar—drier, colder, more honest about its arithmetic. Instead they put her in a sitting room with velvet chairs and a fire that smelled faintly of cinnamon, and served her tea in porcelain so thin she could see the shadow of her fingers through it.

"This is a kidnapping," she said.

"This is hospitality," said the woman who had poured the tea—a compact, silver-haired person with ink-stained cuffs and the unbothered air of someone who had outlived three husbands and two financial panics. "The kidnapping is optional. I'm Mistress Corvane. I keep the master ledger."

"You keep the—" Juniper sat up so fast the tea sloshed. "The master ledger is here?"

Mistress Corvane smiled the way a fisherman smiles at a trout that has just volunteered. "Everything is here, child. Every year ever borrowed, every name ever sold, every memory ever traded. You'd be astonished what people will give up to get through a hard winter." She set down the pot. "You've been keeping branch ledgers your whole life, I'm told. Your father taught you?"

"My father taught me that the guild are vultures."

"Mm. And yet he signed our books often enough." Corvane folded her hands. "You're tired, Miss Delacroix. You've been running for three days on salt pork and fear. Rest tonight. Tomorrow, if you're calm, I'll show you the master ledger myself. It's the least I can do for a colleague."

"I'm not your colleague."

"No," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "Not yet."

They locked the door anyway. It was, Juniper noted, a very nice lock.

* * *

Vane did not sleep that night either.

He stood in the guild's map room with the door shut and let himself, for exactly four minutes by his own watch, feel the thing he refused to call excitement. On the table before him lay a copy of the survey his agents had made of the stranger's death site: salt residue in a perfect human silhouette, a scatter of coins fused half-melted into the cobblestones, and—one detail his men had missed until he pointed it out—the ghost of an impression where a paper had lain, its fibers still holding the shape of coastlines.

A map drawn in blood does not simply dissolve, Vane knew. It transfers. Whatever the dying man had pressed into Juniper Delacroix's hands was not paper. It was a claim. And claims, unlike paper, could not be burned, only transferred—which meant it was still in her possession, in whatever form, and possession was merely a word for something that had not yet been properly invoiced.

He reviewed the file on Sebastian Delacroix again. Twenty years at the gate. No debts recorded. No trades recorded. No vices, no visits, no letters. A man who appeared in the guild's books only as a counterparty to a single transaction, dated twenty years past, the entry itself sealed under a privacy clause so old it predated the current administration.

Vane had read ten thousand sealed entries. He had never once wanted to open one this badly.

"The girl will trade," said Mistress Corvane, appearing beside him with the silent tread of her profession. "They always trade. Grief is the most liquid asset there is."

"Grief is volatile," Vane corrected. "It spikes and then it crashes. We should price it now, while she's at peak." He tapped the sealed entry. "And when she asks what her father signed away, we tell her the truth. All of it. Truth is the only currency we hold that she actually wants."

Corvane studied him. "You enjoy this."

"I enjoy markets," Vane said. "Markets are honest. Everything has a price; most people simply haven't read their own invoice." He turned back to the map of the coast, to the small marked point where the gate stood in its cliff, guarded by a man who had abandoned his post for the first time in twenty years to chase his daughter. "Tomorrow we find out what she'll pay."

* * *

The master ledger lived behind three doors and looked, disappointingly, like a ledger.

Juniper had imagined something grander—iron clasps, chains, maybe a whisper of menace rising off the pages. Instead it was a fat, cracked-leather volume on a reading stand, its spine mended twice, its pages soft as cloth from handling. It smelled like her father's counting-house. That was the cruelest part. Twenty years of her childhood had been spent breathing this exact smell while her father hunched over branch books, and she had thought she was learning bookkeeping. She had been learning, apparently, the family business.

Mistress Corvane left her alone with it. That alone should have warned her.

Juniper found the D's within the hour. Delacroix, Juniper—no entries, good, she was clean. Delacroix, Sebastian—and there her finger stopped.

There were pages of him. Not dozens. Hundreds. Small entries, decades of them, each one modest, each one precise:

*Borrowed against: six months of memory (the color of his wife's dress). Repaid: n/a. Rolled over.*

*Borrowed against: one year of hearing. Repaid in full, plus interest.*

*Borrowed against: the sound of his daughter laughing, aged four.*

She read that last one three times. Her father had sold the sound of her laughing. She tried to remember whether he had ever seemed not to hear it, and realized she couldn't—because if he'd sold it, he wouldn't have known what he was missing, would he? That was the trick of the guild's arithmetic. You could sell anything as long as you sold the missing of it too.

Then she found the sealed page.

It was thicker than the others, stitched at the margin with black thread, and the privacy clause across its header had faded to brown. But seals are for outsiders. Juniper had been breaking into her father's locked drawers since she was nine, and a ledger is a ledger, and thread is thread. She worked it loose with her hairpin, heart hammering, telling herself that this was auditing, that auditing was legal, that she was practically a professional.

The entry was short. Entries for lives always were; there wasn't much to itemize.

*Received: one life, whole and entire, of M— Delacroix [name redacted by prior agreement].*
*Purpose: payment of covenant debt; sealing of the gate at Grayharbor.*
*Tendered by: S. Delacroix.*
*Witnessed: the gate itself.*
*Terms: irrevocable. Non-transferable. The debt is paid so long as the gate holds.*

Below it, in her father's handwriting—the same careful, upright script he used for totals, the same script she had imitated in her own ledgers since she was seven—was his signature.

Juniper did not cry. She was surprised by that. She had expected, if this moment ever came, to come apart entirely. Instead she felt something worse: clarity. The numbers arranged themselves without her help. Her mother had not died. Her mother had been *spent*. And the man who had raised her to believe that the guild's trades were theft, that some things must never be priced, had walked into this room twenty years ago and priced the only thing that mattered—quietly, precisely, in his best handwriting—to save a gate that must never open.

To keep her safe. To keep everyone safe. Both things were true. That was the trap of it. There was no villain in the entry. There was only a father, a wife, and a number.

"There it is," said Mr. Vane, from the doorway.

* * *

He let her sit with it a long moment. This was technique, and he knew it was technique, and he did it anyway, because technique works regardless of whether its user is sincere, and Vane had long ago stopped requiring himself to be sincere.

"You hate us now," he said, settling into the chair across the stand. "I understand. Everyone hates us at this stage. Then, usually around year three, they realize we were simply the ones willing to write it down. Your father made a trade. We recorded it honestly. Which of us, exactly, is the monster?"

"He sold my mother."

"He *sealed a gate*," Vane said, "with the only currency that would serve. And the gate held for twenty years, and no one from the far side ate a single child of Grayharbor, and your father got to raise his daughter in peace. That is the finest transaction I have ever seen recorded, and I have seen them all." He leaned forward. "But the covenant is failing, Miss Delacroix. Debts don't stay paid when the collateral walks off. Something in the terms has broken, and the guild needs to know what, because if the gate opens, the market opens, and I intend to be standing on the right side of it."

"The map isn't for sale."

"Everything is for sale. You simply haven't heard your price yet." He produced, from his breast pocket, a slim folder, and laid it on the stand beside the open ledger with the tenderness of a man laying down a winning card. "So here is mine. The complete file on the covenant. The original terms. What broke. What the dying man was carrying and why. What your mother's trade actually purchased—not the summary, the full instrument, every clause." He watched her eyes move over the folder. "In exchange: the map. Or whatever form it currently takes. You give me that, and you walk out of here knowing everything. Full truth, freely given, no further obligation. I am told that's rare."

Juniper looked at the folder. Then at the ledger, at her father's signature drying brown on the page like an old wound.

Her whole life, her father had protected her with silence. Had decided, alone, what she could bear to know, and paid for the decision with pieces of himself she was only now finding in the margins of other people's books. And here was Vane offering the opposite: everything, all at once, priced fairly, no love in it anywhere.

Truth without love. Love without truth. Those were the only two offers anyone ever made, she thought, and both of them cost everything.

"I'll take the folder," she said.

Vane's smile thinned. "And the map?"

"And you'll take the map," Juniper said, "when I'm finished with it. That's the trade. The folder now, the map after. If you refuse, I burn the knowledge of its contents out of my own head—I know exactly which window to walk to, I've read your tariff schedule since I was ten, and memory-stripping is cheaper than you think." She stood, tucked the folder under her arm, and gave him the small, polite smile her father used on guild assessors: courteous, total, and absolutely closed. "You said everything has a price, Mr. Vane. So does patience. Consider this an installment plan."

For a moment she thought he would refuse. Then he laughed—a short, genuine, startled sound—and inclined his head like a man conceding a well-played hand.

"Installment plan," he repeated. "Your father's daughter after all." At the door he paused. "One courtesy, free of charge. The enforcers I recalled from the coast road this morning—Corvane thinks I recalled all of them. I recalled all but one. He's very good, and he doesn't report to me." His eyes flicked to the folder under her arm. "Read quickly, Miss Delacroix."

* * *

She read until the candles drowned in their own wax.

The covenant's terms were older than the guild, older than the town, written in a hand that hurt her eyes to look at directly. Most of it was architecture—thresholds, weights, balances—but buried in the fourth clause was a sentence she copied onto her cuff in pencil, because paper could be taken from her and skin, so far, had not been:

*The gate holds so long as the debt holds, and the debt holds so long as the payer's line endures.*

Her mother's life had bought twenty years. But the clause hadn't said twenty years. It had said *the payer's line*. And her father's line—her father's line was her.

Which meant the covenant hadn't failed. It had *matured*. It had come due the day she was born, or the day her mother's trade finally ran dry, and every mile she had run from Grayharbor she had been running along the inside of the terms themselves.

Outside her locked door, boots passed in the corridor. Only one set. Unhurried. Stopping, briefly, outside her room, then moving on.

Juniper blew out the candle, folded the map's memory tight against her ribs where no invoice could reach it yet, and began, quietly, to work the lock with her hairpin.

She had a gate to reach before the guild's one honest knife did—and, for the first time in her life, a question she intended to ask her father with the whole truth in her hands.
