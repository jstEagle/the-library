## 5. A Door in the Water

The map did not lead anywhere sensible.

Juniper had copied it three times before leaving the guild's reach—once on paper, once on the inside of her left boot's tongue, and once in her own memory, which she was beginning to suspect was no longer entirely her own to trust. The stranger's ghost had taken payment for its first two fragments of guidance: the name of the coastal village where she could lose Vane's riders, and then, later, the trick of reading the map's watermarks by moonlight. Each time, something small went missing from her. The smell of the counting-house's ink, gone. Her grandmother's laugh, thinned to a rumor of a laugh. She kept a running tally in a little notebook, because she was her father's daughter and even grief deserved proper bookkeeping.

*Paid: ink-smell. Paid: Grandmother Ada's laugh (partial). Paid: the taste of plums.*

She was beginning to understand that the ghost paid for nothing. It only took.

The coast road south was all gorse and wind and the long gray argument between sea and sky. She walked it for two days, sleeping in hay barns, paying a fishwife three coppers and one harmless memory—the color of a particular ribbon—to be pointed toward Saltmere, which appeared on no map but the dead stranger's. On the third morning the road simply gave up and became tide flats, and there, half a mile out, standing in the retreating water like a patient debtor, was a chapel.

Only its roof and the top arc of a window showed above the sea. The window glass had long since gone, and through the empty arch the water inside glowed a strange, deep green, as though the chapel held a piece of somewhere else and was embarrassed about it.

"That's not on any chart," said a voice behind her.

Juniper did not scream, which she counted among her life's small triumphs. She turned instead with the flat, unimpressed expression of a woman who has audited smugglers' invoices for eleven years and can smell a lie at forty paces.

The man on the rocks was youngish, sun-browned, with rope-scarred hands and the kind of face that looked like it apologized often and meant it about half the time. He had a boat pulled up behind him—a narrow, tar-black thing—and he was watching her with an expression caught between suspicion and hope, like a man who has found a purse on the road and is already worrying whose it is.

"Neither are you," Juniper said. "Saltmere isn't on any chart either."

"No." He said it carefully. "It isn't."

They regarded each other across six feet of barnacled rock, two people each pretending very hard not to recognize what the other was.

"You're here for the door," he said finally.

"I'm here for the *chapel*," Juniper said. "Doors imply I intend to go through. I'm a ledger-keeper. I only want to look at the entries."

Something flickered across his face—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile kept its coat. "A ledger-keeper. For the guild?"

"For anyone who pays honestly."

"And if the guild pays?"

"The guild doesn't pay honestly. That's rather their whole business model."

That did earn the smile, brief and crooked. He stuck out a hand scarred like old lace. "Wren."

"Juniper." She shook it. His grip was careful, the way sailors' grips were when they'd learned how easily small bones broke.

He looked past her at the chapel, and his face did something complicated. "You'll want to wait for the tide," he said. "Low water's in an hour. And you'll want me, whether you know it yet or not, because the path out there is mine—I laid the stones myself, and I'm the only person alive who knows which ones lie."

* * *

His brother's name was Finch, and Wren told her about him the way people tell stories they've told themselves so many times the telling has worn smooth.

"We ferried for the gate. Not through it—nobody goes *through*—but along it. There's a current that runs from the far side, thin as thread, and things come down it. Letters that answer questions you haven't asked yet. Seeds that grow into whatever the planter is loneliest for. Once, a violin that played the song your mother hummed while she died, and I don't mean a song *like* it, I mean—" He stopped. Started again. "Finch carried cargo. Small things. Harmless things, we thought. Then the guild offered him a contract with a clause he didn't read properly, because Finch read the way I swim—confidently and badly. The clause said the guild could collect on default in kind."

"In kind," Juniper repeated quietly. She knew the phrase. She had written it into a hundred contracts without once letting herself think about what *kind* meant.

"He defaulted. Owed them nine months of ferry work. They collected nine months of *him*. Not his time—they took the man himself, whole, and posted him through the gate as payment on some debt of theirs to the other realm." Wren's hands were very still on the tiller. "I've rowed out to this chapel every low tide for four years. Some days the water in there shows me things. It's never shown me my brother. But it's never once shown me nothing, either, and I've decided that means something."

The boat slid over stones only he could see. Up close, the drowned chapel was worse and better than it had looked from shore—worse because the walls were furred with pale growth that moved against the current, better because the green light inside was warm, almost candle-colored, and the silence had the quality of a held breath rather than a grave.

The ghost stood in the shallows beside the boat.

Juniper had stopped being startled by it somewhere around the second night. It wore the stranger's shape still—a lean, salt-gray figure—but lately, when the light came through it at certain angles, she thought she saw something else underneath, the way a watermark hides under paper. It raised one hand, and she felt the familiar cold tug behind her eyes, the sensation of a page turning in a book she hadn't agreed to lend.

*"Ask him nothing you aren't prepared to pay for,"* the ghost said, in a voice like wind through a keyhole. *"And offer him nothing. Love is collateral, daughter of the ledger. The covenant will claim whoever you let close. That is how debts work. That is how they have always worked."*

"I wasn't going to—" Juniper muttered, and then realized she was arguing aloud with empty air in front of a stranger, which was not the impression she'd hoped to make.

Wren glanced from her to the water and back. "You talk to it too, then."

"*Too?*"

"It's been following me since Saltmere." He said it lightly, but his knuckles were white on the oar. "I figured it was Finch. First stupid hopeful thought I've had in years." He laughed, short and unhappy. "It's not him. Whatever it is, it's older than him."

Juniper looked at the ghost, and the ghost looked back with an expression she couldn't read—which was, she was beginning to realize, exactly the expression she imagined her mother might wear, if her mother had been reduced to arithmetic and patience.

* * *

They waited out the tide in the chapel itself, because Wren said the roof kept off the worst of the weather and because, though neither of them said it, the green light made the waiting less lonely.

He had brought bread and hard cheese and a jar of pickled things that were aggressively edible. Juniper produced dried apricots and, after a moment's internal negotiation, shared them. They ate sitting on a fallen pew with their boots off, feet dangling toward water that glowed like a lamp under green glass, and it occurred to Juniper that this was the first meal in eleven years she had eaten with someone who wasn't charging her for the company.

"So," Wren said. "The map."

"How do you know about the map?"

"Because the map is why the guild's been crawling up the coast asking after a dark-haired girl who 'keeps accounts.' Because a man in a beautiful coat offered me fifty crowns to report any keeper who came asking about the chapel, and I said yes, because I'm a smuggler and fifty crowns is fifty crowns." He winced. "And then I watched you walk down onto my flats with your nose in a notebook, and I thought, oh no, she's *exactly* the type, and now I'm eating her apricots and confessing, so apparently I've gotten soft."

"What type?"

"Honest," he said, like an accusation.

Juniper looked at him for a long moment. The green light moved on his face. Somewhere in her chest, beneath the folded map-memory and the tally of lost things, something shifted its weight, and she noted it the way she noted everything: reluctantly, precisely, in a column she hadn't opened in years.

"Mr. Vane," she said. "Elegant. Smiles like a contract's fine print."

"That's him."

"He'll be here by tomorrow. Maybe tonight. He wants the gate open and owned." She hugged her knees. "And I need it closed and free, and I need it done before he arrives, and I have maybe half a plan and a ghost who keeps invoicing me for my own childhood."

Wren was quiet. Then: "My brother used to say the best plans are the ones with room in them for someone else to row."

"That's not a plan, that's a passenger policy."

"Best kind there is." He nudged the jar of pickles toward her. "So. You need someone who knows the water, knows the stones, knows what the gate does to people who touch it wrong. And I need—" He stopped. Looked at the green light. "Four years I've needed to know if anything of Finch is still on the other side. If the gate closes clean, I'll never know. If it stays open, the guild owns everything that comes through, forever, and I'll wish I didn't know." His jaw worked. "Either way I lose. So I may as well lose next to somebody honest."

Juniper reached for her notebook—the tally, the cold accounting of everything the ghost had taken—and found, to her alarm, that what she wanted to write in it just then had nothing to do with losses.

The ghost drifted up through the chapel floor, translucent against the green, and its voice came like frost forming.

*"You are doing it already,"* it said sadly. *"Already you are writing him into your margins. Child, listen to me. Everything you love becomes collateral. I loved, and look what the debt made of me. Close your columns. Keep your ledgers sealed. It is the only mercy this covenant leaves anyone."*

And underneath the stranger's borrowed voice, faint as a watermark, Juniper heard—or thought she heard, or desperately heard—something warmer. A woman's voice, tired and fierce, saying the same words in the opposite spirit: *not as a warning, but as a mother's apology for the world.*

"Wren," Juniper said slowly, "when the gate takes its payments—does it ever leave change?"

He blinked. "Change?"

"A remainder. Something given back. Guilds always round in their own favor; covenants might too." She was flipping pages in her mind now, old contracts, old clauses, the stranger's bloodstained map and the handwriting on it that her father had recognized. "Everything I've been told assumes the debt is simple. Debts are never simple. Someone always skims."

Outside, the tide had turned. The water around the chapel began its slow climb, patient as a collector, and far up the coast road—though neither of them could see it yet—a black carriage rolled toward Saltmere at an unhurried pace, carrying a man in a beautiful coat and a ledger full of other people's prices.

Wren picked up his oars. "Well?" he said. "Where are we rowing?"

Juniper looked at the green light, at the ghost, at the man who had confessed to treachery over apricots, and made the first entry in a column she suspected would cost her everything.

"Through," she said.
