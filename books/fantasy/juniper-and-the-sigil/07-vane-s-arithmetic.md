## 7. Vane's Arithmetic

Mr. Vane had always believed that the world was a ledger that had never been properly audited.

He stood now on the salt flats before the gate, his good boots ruined in the first ten steps and his opinion of coastal geography permanently lowered, and watched his people work. Twelve of them, handpicked, each one carrying a lantern of guild glass that burned without oil. They had reached the gate a full day ahead of the girl, which was not luck. Luck was what incompetent men called arithmetic they hadn't bothered to do.

The gate itself was less impressive than its reputation. A seam in the air, roughly man-shaped, standing alone on a shelf of black rock where the tide came to die. It did not gleam or thunder. It simply *was*, the way a locked door is, and Vane found he respected it for that. Doors that boasted were doors that had already been opened.

"Sir," said Pell, his junior clerk, consulting a brass instrument that clicked and whirred. "Residual flow is negligible. Whatever's holding it shut is holding it *well*."

"Of course it is," Vane said. "Sebastian Delacroix is a meticulous man. Twenty years at a post with no visitors, no wages worth mentioning, and no relief. That isn't devotion, Pell. That's amortization." He drew off one glove, finger by finger, and touched the cold air near the seam. It hummed against his skin like a struck wire. "Someone paid an enormous sum to close this thing, and someone has been paying interest ever since. The question—the only question—is whether the debt can be restructured."

"And if it can't?"

"Then we hold it open ourselves." He said it the way other men commented on the weather. "Look at this place. A covenant between realms, sealed and forgotten, and every year the guild trades memories at three hundred percent markup because supply is artificially constrained. Do you know what happens when the gate opens? Supply becomes infinite. Years, names, griefs—harvested fresh from the far side instead of scraped from desperate donors here." He smiled at the seam as though it were a colleague. "Covenants are just markets that haven't been priced yet. Someone made a deal in the dark and called it sacred so no one would check the books."

Pell shifted. "The old stories say the far realm takes what it's owed. If we breach it without paying—"

"The stories were written by the first family of gatekeepers, who had a monopoly to protect." Vane replaced his glove. "I've read their ledgers, Pell. All of them. Sacred duties have margins. Miracles have overhead. Now—" he turned, coat flaring with theatrical precision he'd practiced in front of mirrors since he was nineteen, "—set the anchor stakes. When the girl arrives, I want her to see that the future has already been built without her."

* * *

The stakes went in at dusk, six of them, driven into the rock in a ring around the gate. Guild glass sang faintly between them, a sound like wet fingers on crystal, and the seam in the air began—very slowly—to widen. Not open. Widen. A door does not need to be broken if you can persuade the frame.

Vane sat on a folding stool—he had brought a folding stool across forty miles of tidal flats, because dignity was also a form of leverage—and reviewed the day's figures by lantern light. The girl would come. She had copied the map; she had fled the counting-house; she had, according to the enforcer who'd lost her at Salt and Ash Road, acquired a smuggler with a fast boat and a face like an apology. Sentiment slowed people down but rarely stopped them. He gave her until tomorrow's low tide.

What he had not budgeted for was the father.

Vane heard him before he saw him—one set of footsteps on the flats, unhurried, heavy, the walk of a man who had carried everything he owned on his back for twenty years and saw no reason to start hurrying now. Sebastian Delacroix came up out of the dark like something the tide had decided to return, gray-bearded, salt-crusted, with a gatekeeper's iron ring still on his hand and an expression that had been sanded down to two features: tiredness and refusal.

"You left your post," Vane said, genuinely interested. "Twenty years. I confess I didn't think you had it in you."

"I didn't leave it." Sebastian stopped at the edge of the lantern light. Behind him, far off across the water, the distant gate-warden's beacon—the one Vane had assumed was decorative—went dark. "I closed it. Properly, the way my grandfather taught me. There's nothing there to guard anymore except the memory of guarding it." His eyes went past Vane to the seam, to the widening dark within it, and something moved through his face like weather. "You've been picking at it."

"Negotiating," Vane corrected. "There's a difference. One is vandalism. The other is commerce."

"There's no difference to the thing being opened." Sebastian walked forward—not toward Vane, Vane noted with professional displeasure, but around him, placing himself squarely between the gate and the approach from the flats. Between the gate and the road his daughter would take. "Step away from my gate, Mr. Vane."

"*Your* gate." Vane rose from the stool, folding it with a snap. "Forgive me, gatekeeper, but let us do sums, since neither of us can help it. You sealed this covenant twenty years ago with a payment you did not make yourself. You signed away your wife's life and then spent two decades pretending the signature was duty rather than theft. Your daughter has learned the entry exists. She is coming here to pay a debt you've been dodging since before she could walk. And you propose to stand in front of the door and call it yours?" He spread his hands. "I am not the villain in your arithmetic, Delacroix. I'm just the auditor who arrived."

For a long moment Sebastian said nothing. The lanterns hissed. Somewhere out on the flats, water ticked against stone, patient as bookkeeping.

"You're right about the debt," Sebastian said at last, and Vane, who had prepared rebuttals to denial, rage, and tears, found himself briefly without notes. "Every word. I traded her life because the alternative was both of theirs, and I told myself for twenty years that choosing was the same as earning." He rolled his shoulders, and Vane saw that under the salt and gray the man was built like the doorframes he'd guarded—nothing ornamental, all load-bearing. "But here's where your ledger's wrong, auditor. You think a debt unpaid is a debt unowned. It isn't. I've been paying interest this whole time. Every night I stood at that gate instead of sleeping, every birthday I spent watching her through a window instead of holding her—that was the interest. Compounding. Daily." He looked at the seam, and his voice dropped to something almost gentle. "And tomorrow my daughter pays the principal. So yes. I abandoned my post. Because my post was never the gate."

Vane studied him with the particular fascination of a man watching an asset behave irrationally. "You understand what she'll lose. The rite takes everything inherited. Her memories of you will be the first item collected. She'll look at you tomorrow and see a stranger."

"I know."

"She may thank you for it. There's a mercy in forgetting a man who sold her mother."

"I know that too." Sebastian's jaw worked once. "Knowing doesn't move me. That's the part you'll never put in a column, Vane. Love doesn't audit well. It pays out on terms nobody sane would sign."

"How touching," Vane said, and meant none of it, and was disturbed to find a small part of him meaning some of it. He gestured, and his twelve clerks stepped out of the dark with their lanterns, forming a line between Sebastian and the stakes. "Nevertheless. The guild holds the anchor points. The girl will arrive, and she will find that the market has already moved. Grief makes people reckless; desperation makes them sign. Either way, I get what I came for."

"You're assuming she'll bargain with you."

"I'm assuming everyone bargains. It's the one universal law I've never seen broken."

Sebastian almost smiled. It was a terrible sight, like a cliff deciding to be friendly. "Then you've never met my wife," he said, "or my daughter. We Delacroix keep our accounts in a currency you don't trade in."

The seam behind them chose that moment to sigh—a long, low exhalation, like a house settling—and the dark inside it deepened by a visible degree. Pell's instrument began clicking faster, faster, and then produced a single clear chime that every person on the rock felt in their teeth.

"Sir," Pell said thinly. "Flow's reversing. Something on the far side is... pushing."

Vane looked at the gate. The gate, insofar as a seam could be said to do anything, looked back.

And out on the black water, right on schedule, a small boat's lamp came bobbing toward the shore—two silhouettes aboard, one rowing, one standing at the bow with her hair whipping in the wind, and beside her, faint as breath on glass, a third shape that glowed.

"Right on time," Vane murmured, and reached into his coat for the contract he'd had drafted in triplicate. "Let's see what the daughter inherited besides the key."

Sebastian stepped forward to stand beside him at the waterline, shoulder to shoulder with the enemy, both of them watching the boat come in.

"One warning, auditor," the gatekeeper said quietly. "Whatever you've written in those papers—she won't read it. She'll read *you*. And she was raised on ledgers. She'll find the lie in you before you finish your opening sentence."

Vane smiled his very best smile, the one that had closed nine hostile acquisitions and two marriages.

"Then we shall see," he said, "which of us is better at arithmetic."

Behind them, the gate sighed again—and this time, distinctly, something on the far side knocked.
