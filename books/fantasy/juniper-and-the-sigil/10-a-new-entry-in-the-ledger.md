## 10. A New Entry in the Ledger

The counting-house smelled different in spring. Juniper had noticed it the first warm morning after the thaw—less ink and dust, more salt air through the open shutters, the faint green smell of the harbor coming up the hill. She had opened every window against tradition. Old Master Corvane would have fainted. Old Master Corvane was also dead, disgraced, and posthumously revealed to have been skimming years off the top of pensioners' trades for a decade, so Juniper did not feel especially bound by his opinions on ventilation.

She sat at her father's old desk now—not the one she'd kept as a clerk, but the wide oak one at the head of the room, with the brass lamp and the drawer that stuck—and she kept the ledgers honestly.

That was the whole revolution, really. No banners, no proclamations. Just a guild that traded fairly: memories bought at their true weight, years sold back at no markup, names returned free of charge to anyone who could prove they'd been taken in bad faith. It turned out that when you stopped cheating people, they came to you willingly, and business was better than it had ever been under Vane's arithmetic.

Vane himself was gone. Pulled through the gate as collateral, along with everything he'd tried to hold open. Juniper had written the entry herself:

*One covenant, reforged. One debt, paid in full. Payment received: all memories held by J. Delacroix regarding S. Delacroix (father), per terms of inheritance.*

*Balance remaining: love, unaccounted for.*

She still didn't know what she'd meant by that last line. It had come out of her pen at three in the morning, and she'd decided it was true enough to keep.

* * *

Wren came by on Thursdays. He always came on Thursdays, because Thursday was when she balanced the week's trades, and he had learned—patiently, with the stubbornness of a man who had once smuggled contraband past an armed guild blockade—that Juniper Delacroix could not be interrupted mid-column.

He brought things. A loaf from the baker who overcharged everyone except Wren. A jar of honey. Once, a small carved boat he claimed he'd made himself, which was a lie, because Wren could barely carve cheese, but she'd let him keep the lie because it cost him nothing and gave him something to be proud of.

"Good morning," he said today, setting down the loaf. "You look like a woman who found an error."

"I found two errors." She didn't look up. "Both mine. Don't gloat."

"I would never." He leaned against the desk, which she permitted, which was itself a kind of progress. "There's a festival on the pier Saturday. Lanterns on the water. The whole town turns out."

"Mm."

"There'll be dancing."

"Mm."

"There'll be food you don't have to cook yourself."

That got her to look up. "What kind of food?"

"Juniper." He said her name gently, the way you'd set something fragile down. "I know you don't remember me asking before. I know that every time I ask, it's the first time for you, and I'm sorry for that, and I'm going to keep doing it anyway. Saturday. The lanterns. Will you come with me?"

And there it was again—the strange vertigo of it. This man with salt-bleached hair and a scar through one eyebrow, looking at her like she was a shore he'd been sailing toward for months. She felt nothing she could name. But her hands, resting on the ledger, had gone quiet in a way they didn't usually go quiet, as if some part of her below the arithmetic recognized him and had settled, like a ship finding its mooring.

"Yes," she said. "All right. Saturday."

His whole face changed—the careful composure cracking into something young and unguarded—and Juniper thought, not for the first time: *I must have been very foolish about this man once.* She wished she knew how foolish. She suspected, from the way he smiled, extremely.

* * *

It was the stuck drawer that started it.

The bottom-left drawer of her father's desk had jammed since the day she'd inherited it, and she'd simply worked around it, the way you work around any grief too heavy to move. But on a slow Tuesday in late spring, with the ledger balanced and the light going gold across the floor, she knelt down with a flat iron and a bar of wax and decided she was done being managed by furniture.

The drawer shrieked, resisted, and gave.

Inside: dust, a dead moth, and a single ledger folio, thin, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp. Her mother's handwriting on the spine—or what she understood to be her mother's handwriting, though she had no memory of ever learning it. The letters looked like her own, which unsettled her more than anything else in the room.

The label read: *Delacroix, J. — deposit, one memory. Held in trust. Do not spend without cause.*

Her heart went strange and slow. She sat down on the floor with her back against the desk, the way she used to as a child—she supposed; she couldn't actually remember—and unwrapped the cloth.

A memory-folio. She knew the craft intimately; she appraised dozens a week. This one was small, carefully made, its edges sealed with wax pressed by a thumb she somehow knew had been chipped on the left side. Inside, in that same familiar-unfamiliar hand:

*One afternoon. All three of us. Kept so she could choose.*

Below it, smaller:

*I'm sorry, little bird. I couldn't leave you nothing. But I couldn't leave you everything either—you'd have spent your life inside one afternoon instead of living past it. So here is the rule I set: read it once if you need to know you were loved. Then put it back. Love isn't a thing you spend. It's a thing you carry.*

Juniper sat on the floor of the counting-house for a long time.

Outside, the harbor bells rang the hour. Somewhere below, Wren was probably fixing a boat, or lying to someone charmingly about having built it. Her father—she knew the facts, the way she knew the entries: Sebastian Delacroix, gatekeeper, carried her home from the gate with her hollow as a swept hearth, wept over her, stayed near, visited on Sundays, sat in her counting-house drinking tea with a daughter who was polite to him the way you're polite to a kind stranger. He never once asked her to remember. He only ever said, "You look like your mother," and left it there, and it broke her a little every time, though she couldn't have said why.

She knew she had been loved. That was the terrible, generous shape of it: the evidence was everywhere. In the way Wren looked at her. In her father's Sunday visits. In this folio, wrapped against the damp by a woman who had died so a gate could close, and who had spent her one act of haunting making sure her daughter got to choose.

But knowing wasn't the same as remembering. And God, she wanted. She wanted it the way you want a limb you've lost—an ache shaped exactly like a missing thing.

*Read it once if you need to know you were loved.*

She needed.

Juniper climbed into her chair, laid the folio open beneath the brass lamp, and did what she had done ten thousand times with other people's memories: she read.

* * *

An afternoon. Late summer, by the light. A garden behind a small stone house, bees drunk in the lavender, laundry on the line snapping in the wind.

A woman with dark hair and a chipped thumbnail kneeling in the dirt, showing a small girl how to tell a weed from a seedling—"patience, little bird, patience, everything worth keeping looks like nothing at first"—and the girl, four maybe five, solemn as a magistrate, patting the soil with hands far too big for her.

And a man at the clothesline, pinning up shirts badly on purpose to make the girl laugh, and the woman scolding him without heat—"Sebastian, honestly, the wind will take them"—and him saying, "Then the wind will owe me a shirt," and the girl laughing so hard she fell over in the dirt, and the woman laughing too, helplessly, one hand pressed to her mouth, and then—

Then the woman looked directly out of the memory. Directly at her. As if she'd known, all those years ago, exactly where her daughter would be standing.

*"There you are,"* she said, in a voice like warm bread. *"Look at you. Look how well you turned out. We were so ordinary that day, my love. It was the best day of my life precisely because it was nothing special. That's the secret nobody in the guild will ever write down: the impossible things are cheap. It's the ordinary ones that cost everything. Go be ordinary. Go fall in love with a boy who lies about carving boats. Go have Sunday tea with your father and let it stop breaking you. You don't need this afternoon. You never did. You just needed to know it happened."*

She smiled, and the smile was Juniper's own smile, and the memory ended.

* * *

Juniper closed the folio with shaking hands.

She was crying. She noted it distantly, the way she noted errors in a column—not alarmed, just observed. She cried for a while, alone in the gold light, and it hurt, and it healed, both at once, the way a bone sets.

Then she dried her face, wrapped the folio back in its oilcloth, and did not file it in the archive.

Instead she wrote a new entry in the master ledger, in her own hand, in her mother's hand, in the hand they shared:

*Deposit received and reviewed. Item: one ordinary afternoon, value beyond appraisal. Disposition: retained, not spent. To be carried forward indefinitely. Holder's note: I choose this. Not because I was told to. Because it's mine.*

She rewrapped it, and rather than hide it in a stuck drawer where grief could guard it, she placed it in the open cabinet behind her desk—the one any clerk could reach, the one marked *Held in Trust*—where it would sit in plain sight, honest as everything else in the house now.

Then she blew out the lamp, locked up, and walked down toward the harbor, where the evening was turning gold and a man with salt-bleached hair was waiting on the pier, pretending he hadn't been waiting long.

"Saturday," she called to him. "Lanterns. Dancing. Don't be late."

"I'm never late," Wren said, grinning. "I'm fashionably nautical."

"That's not a word."

"It is where I'm from."

They walked home together along the water, and above them the first stars came out, steady and patient, like entries in a ledger that went on forever—and somewhere between the lamplight and the tide line, Juniper reached over and took his hand, not because she remembered why, but because some things, once chosen, need no reason to keep being true.
