## 1. The Date That Was Written

There are three rules to surviving the temple archive, and I had broken all of them before noon.

Rule one: never read what you're only meant to copy. Rule two: never linger past the last bell. Rule three — and this is the one that matters — never, under any circumstances, touch the sealed shelf.

The sealed shelf is the one at the back of the sub-crypt, behind the iron grate, where the books that embarrassed somebody important go to be forgotten. I'd catalogued it a hundred times without opening so much as a clasp. *Inventory, not inquiry,* I told myself every morning, because August Thornbury is very good at telling herself things she has no intention of obeying.

But my debt to the temple is eleven years old and growing interest like mold, and the head archivist had promised that if I finished the sealed inventory by week's end, he'd speak to the debtors' court on my behalf. Freedom, dangled on a string. So there I was, past the last bell, copying spines by tallow-light with ink-stained fingers and a candle burning dangerously low.

The book in question sat at the end of the row, bound in grey leather gone soft as old skin, its clasp stamped with the sigil of the Covenant — the interlocked rings of city and undercity that they put on coins nobody uses anymore. No title. No catalogue number. Just a date burned into the cover in a hand that trembled slightly:

*This night.*

Which is not how you date a book. Books get years. Centuries, even. This one got *tonight*, the way you'd date a threat.

I should have set it down. I want that on the record, somewhere, in whatever ledger the gods keep: I knew better. But rule one was already broken, and curiosity is a debt collector too, and it always finds you.

The clasp opened at my touch. Not forced — *opened*, the way a door opens for someone expected.

The first hundred pages were the usual prophetic drivel. The Spire stands, the Spire watches, the Spire shall stand while the covenant holds. Verse after verse of it, the same hymn they'd drilled into us at temple school, except here the margins were full of corrections in different hands across different centuries — little annotations like *this already happened* and *he lied about this part* and, once, in furious red ink, *the High Canon knows.*

Then the pages changed.

The handwriting shifted — still old, still careful, but urgent now, pressing hard enough to tear the paper in places. And the words stopped being about kings and canons and started being about a girl.

*On this night, when the bells ring the ninth hour and the archive burns, the Thornbury girl will find this book. August. Daughter of Maren. She will carry it below.*

My name. My mother's name. Written down before I was born, or near enough — the ink had gone brown with age, the paper soft as bread. I read it three times. Then a fourth, because the first three felt like the kind of mistake you make when you've been breathing tallow fumes too long.

*August.* In a century-old book. Along with today's date, which I confirmed against my own inventory sheet with hands that would not stay steady.

People don't feature in prophecies. People feature in debts, in ledgers, in the temple's long arithmetic of who owes whom. Prophecies are for saints and disasters, and I had spent twenty-six years being aggressively neither.

Below that entry, more text, fresher-looking, as though the page couldn't quite decide what it wanted to say:

*She will hesitate. She will run. Whether she reaches the flooded quarter before the enforcers—*

And then the sentence simply stopped, mid-word, the ink still wet.

I touched it. It smudged.

A prophecy, rewriting itself. Around me. Right now. Like a quill somewhere scratching out my next move and waiting to see what I did before committing it to the page.

That was when the bells rang the ninth hour, and the archive doors above me boomed open, and boots came down the crypt stairs — too many boots, moving in the cadence enforcers use when they've come to burn something and don't much care what else catches.

*The archive burns.* The book had known. The book had been sitting behind an iron grate for a hundred years, waiting for tonight specifically, and I was standing in it.

I did the arithmetic of a cornered animal. One stairway, occupied. One coal chute, too narrow — probably. One window, barred. And one book that had just made me the most dangerous object in the temple's possession, because whatever Vess and his enforcers were coming to destroy, I was now carrying it in my arms like a baby.

I took the coal chute. "Probably" turned out to be generous; I lost a boot, most of my dignity, and a good deal of skin, and landed in the ash-heap behind the temple kitchens smelling like a chimney sweep's funeral. Above me, orange light bloomed in the archive windows. They hadn't even waited to search the crypt. Whatever the book was, it mattered more than catching me — which meant they didn't know yet that the book had grown legs.

Small mercies. I clutched it tighter and ran.

* * *

The undercity doesn't appear on any map the temple prints, which is precisely why it works. You reach it through the gutters and cellar-doors of the river district, down stairs that officially lead nowhere, into a warren of lamplight and steam and commerce of every legality. I'd sold charms down here for years — small ones, harmless ones, luck-knots and fever-charms, nothing that would interest an enforcer. I knew the faces. I did not know the face currently blocking my way out of the steam-tunnels, perched on a crate like he owned it, holding my missing boot.

"Fen," I said. "Give it back."

"Thornbury." He turned the boot over, examining the sole. "You know, most people leave the temple through the door. With permission. Wearing both shoes."

"Most people aren't being chased."

"Nobody's chasing you. I watched the whole street. Enforcers went in, enforcers are still in, and the building's on fire, which frankly looks bad for them." He hopped down, and I noticed — because noticing things is how Fen stays alive — that his eyes kept flicking to the bundle under my arm. "So. What did you steal?"

"I didn't steal anything. It stole itself. It—" I stopped, because explaining *the prophecy opened for me and then started writing my biography in real time* sounded insane even to me, and I was the one holding the evidence. "It's a book. It has my name in it. Written a hundred years ago."

Fen's expression did something complicated. Pickpockets have excellent poker faces, but underneath theirs runs a current of pure superstition — you don't survive the streets without believing in luck, and luck is just prophecy with worse manners.

"Show me," he said.

I showed him. He read fast, lips moving, and I watched the color leave his face at exactly the line I'd known he'd hit. *She will carry it below.* His eyes came up to mine.

"This says tonight. This says *you*. Tonight, you." He said it the way you'd say *the wall is falling, the water is rising* — flat, factual, terrified. "August. The enforcers weren't burning a book. They were burning a *date*. And you're standing in it."

"I noticed," I said, wryly, because terror makes me flippant and someone in this partnership had to be calm, even if it was a performance. "Which is why I'm going to the flooded quarter, and why you're going to show me the way, and why you're going to do it quickly."

"Why would I do that?"

"Because your name is probably in here too, and neither of us wants to find out what happens to the people who refuse."

He looked at the book. Looked at the smoke still rising from the temple hill. Looked at me — really looked, the way he must look at marks, weighing what I was worth and what I'd cost him.

"The flooded quarter's keeper-shrine," he said finally. "There's a woman down there they defrocked years ago. A healer. If anyone knows what a covenant-shard looks like, it's her." He tossed me my boot. "But I'm charging you. Double rates. Hazard pay."

"You're charging a woman fleeing for her life."

"I'm charging the woman from the prophecy. That's premium clientele." He was already walking, quick and light, deeper into the dark. "Keep up, Thornbury. The book says tonight, and I'd rather not find out what happens to the guide who was late."

I followed. And here is the thing I haven't told anyone, the thing I'll deny later if anyone asks: as we descended, with the temple burning above and my whole careful, cramped, indebted life burning with it, I did not feel trapped.

I felt *chosen*. For the first time in twenty-six years, some vast old book had looked down the length of a century and picked *me* — not my debt, not my mother's shame, not the girl who copies other people's words for other people's purposes. Me.

The feeling was terrifying. It was also, I am ashamed to report, magnificent.

Behind us, faint through the grates, the ninth bell finished ringing. Somewhere ahead, in the flooded quarter, a disgraced healer was about to have a very bad night.

And somewhere between the pages, a wet line of ink was drying into a sentence I hadn't written yet.
