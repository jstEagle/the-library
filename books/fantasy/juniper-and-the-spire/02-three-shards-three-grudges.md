## 2. Three Shards, Three Grudges

*August*

The flooded quarter smelled like a drowned church. Old incense and river rot, all of it soaked into the bones of buildings the temple had abandoned when the water table turned against them, as if piety could be revoked by damp.

Fen walked ahead of me along a catwalk that had once been someone's balcony, testing each plank with a toe before committing his weight. He hadn't spoken in an hour, which I was learning was his way of speaking.

"You could just tell me where we're going," I said.

"You could just fall off this walkway," he said, "and save everyone some time."

"Charming."

"I'm not charming. I'm employed." He glanced back at me, and in the grey light his face had the particular blankness of someone doing arithmetic they didn't like. "The healer's name is Maren Ostrow. She used to mend broken covenant-oaths for the temple — literally sew them back together when they frayed. Then she patched up a smuggler instead of turning him in, and they struck her name from the rolls and let her keep her hands, which the temple considers generosity."

"And she holds a shard."

"She holds *something*. Everyone down here holds something." He stopped at a door half-swallowed by moss and knocked in a rhythm — three, pause, two — like a sentence with a limp. "Just remember: she'll ask you why you're here. And 'a book told me to' is going to go over about as well as you'd expect."

The door opened on a chain. Through the gap: one eye, brown and unimpressed, set in a face weathered like a dock piling.

"You brought a charm-seller," the eye said to Fen.

"She brought herself," Fen said. "I just carried the book."

Maren Ostrow looked at me for a long moment. Then she shut the door, and my heart did something humiliating, and then the chain rattled and the door opened all the way.

Inside, the room was dry. That was the first miracle. The second was the table covered in needlework — not embroidery, but *sutures*, lengths of silver thread stitched through scraps of parchment, each scrap humming faintly against my teeth the way a struck bell hums after the sound stops.

"You can feel them," Maren said. It wasn't a question.

"The charms. Yes. Always could."

"Mm." She sat, and gestured at nothing in particular, which I took to mean *sit somewhere*. "Fen says you want the shards. The three pieces of the old pact. Do you know what they are?"

"Fragments of the covenant between the city and the under-realm. The book says—"

"The book." Her mouth twisted. "Books are how the temple keeps its lies straight. Sit down, girl, and answer me honestly, because I will know if you don't; I've spent thirty years listening to what people say underneath their words. Why are you here?"

And there it was. The question I'd been dodging since I ran through the archive doors with a burning prophecy under my arm.

I could have lied. I'm good at lying; it's most of retail. But her hands were still, folded over those humming scraps, and her eye — the other was lost to something she didn't explain — waited on me with terrible patience.

"I want my debt cleared," I said. "I've owed the temple since I was fourteen. They own my labor until I'm forty, and every charm I've ever sold illegally has been to buy back hours of my own life. If the covenant reforges, the debt rolls dissolve. That's why I'm here. Not justice. Not prophecy. Arithmetic."

Silence. Somewhere below us, water moved through a street that used to be a street.

Then Maren laughed — short, rusty, surprised out of her. "Honest arithmetic. Well." She drew a scrap of parchment from her sleeve, silver-threaded, and held it out. "The temple taught me that oaths are just debts with better posture. You'll do."

The shard was warm. When my fingers closed around it, something climbed my arm like heat up a chimney, and for one vertiginous moment I could hear the whole flooded quarter breathing — every hidden thing, every smuggled charm, every oath whispered over contraband candles. It felt *magnificent*.

I wanted more of it immediately, and that frightened me more than the enforcers had.

* * *

*Fen*

Here's what nobody tells you about running messages for people who don't officially exist: eventually you learn the whole city's secret grammar. Which doors knock in code. Which priests take bribes. Which cells in the temple undercroft hold prisoners whose names have been scraped off the registers.

The stone-singer was in cell nine.

August didn't like the plan. August, I was learning, had opinions the way rivers have water — constantly, and mostly downhill. She stood in the shadow of the undercroft stair with her arms crossed, holding two shards now, because the feral child in the tannery district had handed hers over the day before with no test at all, just a look, like she'd been waiting years for someone to collect it. That left the hard one.

"He won't come quietly," August said.

"He can't come any other way. He's chained to the wall by an oath-collar. Temple iron. It'll kill him if he leaves the cell radius."

"So we break the collar."

"With what? Your inventory of love-charms?" I pulled the lockpicks from my boot. "We get him out by getting the collar's key, and the key lives on Canon-Sergeant Ellery's belt, and Ellery drinks in the Gilded Cusp every night until the bells, because grief is predictable and I exploit it professionally."

She stared at me. "You've done this before."

"I've done worse before. This is a step up, morally."

The Gilded Cusp smelled of tallow and bad decisions. Ellery sat in his corner like furniture, and I lifted the key off him so smoothly he never stopped staring into his cup, and if my hand shook afterward it was only because August was watching me with an expression I couldn't file anywhere, and I didn't like expressions I couldn't file.

Cell nine was cold enough to see your breath. The stone-singer sat against the far wall, a big man gone grey, chains of iron script looped around his throat. His lips were moving. Always moving. Singing to the stones so quietly even the stones could barely hear.

"We're getting you out," August said.

He didn't stop singing. But the wall behind him shivered, and a crack ran up it, neat as handwriting, and in the old tongue — which I don't speak, but understood anyway, the way you understand thunder — he said:

"*Why.*"

August knelt. Close enough to touch the collar. "Because the Spire falls tonight, and the covenant dies with it, unless the three shards reforge it. Because the temple broke the pact on purpose. Because—" she stopped. Started again, quieter. "Because I want my debt cleared more than I want justice, and I'm tired of pretending otherwise, and maybe that's exactly the kind of person a broken covenant needs. Someone who knows what she's owed."

The singing stopped.

The stone-singer looked at her for a long time. Then he laughed, low, and the laugh cracked the floor, gently, the way frost cracks a window.

"Truth," he rumbled. "At last, truth in a temple of anything else." He tilted his head toward the collar. "Turn the key, little debtor. And when we reach the Spire — sing with me. All of you. The pact remembers voices, not names."

* * *

*August*

Three shards in my pocket, weighing less than coins and more than mountains. A pickpocket who'd stolen a key for me and pretended it cost him nothing. A healer stitching her suture-threads into my coat hem "for luck," though we both knew it was warding. A child trailing us through the tannery rows, silent, speaking only in the old tongue that made the air taste of rain.

Found family, the prophecy would probably call it, if prophecies did anything so kind. What it actually felt like was friction — everyone rubbing against everyone else's grievances until we sparked.

But as dusk came down and the Spire rose black against the last light, I felt the shards answering each other through the fabric of my pocket, humming harmonies, and power pooled in me like wine in a cup, and gods forgive me, it felt *good*.

"August," Fen said suddenly, grabbing my arm. He was looking at the Spire. At its crown, where a light had begun to burn that no honest tower should carry. "The bells. Listen."

I listened. The evening bells were ringing — but wrong. Backwards. Counting down.

"They're not marking the hour," Fen said. "They're marking *him*. Vess is starting early."

The Spire began, very softly, to sing.
