## 3. What the High Canon Erased

The three shards fit together the way grudges do — reluctantly, and with a lot of grinding.

We'd gathered them in the flooded quarter's drowned chapel, which sounds more romantic than it was. It smelled of low tide and old incense, and the healer, Maren Ostwick, had spent most of the evening pretending she wasn't crying while she stitched a gash on Fen's arm that he swore didn't hurt. The stone-singer, Brannoc, sat with his manacled wrists in his lap like they were a gift someone had given him and he hadn't decided whether to be insulted. And the child — Wren, though she'd only answered to that name after I'd said it wrong four times in the old tongue until I got it right — crouched on the altar with the third shard glowing faintly against her sternum like a swallowed star.

Three shards. Three people the temple had chewed up and spat out, all of them now looking at me as if I were the one holding the map.

Which, to be fair, I was. The prophecy book had stopped rewriting itself somewhere around midnight, its pages settling into a final configuration that ended with a single line: *The ink is not yet dry.*

I'd been trying not to think about what that meant.

"They'll come at dawn," Brannoc said. His voice was exactly what you'd expect from a man who sang to stones for a living: gravel and patience. "Vess doesn't send enforcers for this. He comes himself."

"Good," I said.

It came out steadier than I felt. Fen glanced at me from under his hood — he'd been glancing at me all night, cataloguing my lies the way he used to catalogue pockets — but he didn't call me on it. That was the thing about pickpockets. They knew when you were palming something.

Maren tied off her stitch and looked up. "August. Whatever you're planning—"

"I'm not planning anything. That's the problem." I turned the book over in my hands. Its cover was warm, which books should not be. "Every time I decide something, it decides back."

"That's what power feels like," Wren said, in the old tongue, and then translated herself into the common speech with the flat precision of someone reciting a lesson: "That is what power feels like before it eats you."

Cheerful child.

* * *

High Canon Vess arrived an hour before dawn, alone, which was somehow worse than an army.

He walked into the drowned chapel through water that should have soaked him to the knee and didn't, because of course it didn't. The covenant's magic still bent around him like a river around a stone — magic he was supposed to be *distributing*, by the terms of a pact written before either city had a name, and instead had been hoarding for thirty years behind a smile made of candlelight and patience.

"August Thornbury." He said my name the way you'd read a label. "You have your mother's habit of being where you shouldn't."

"You knew my mother?"

Something moved behind his eyes. Not guilt. Regret, maybe, if regret can be a kind of bookkeeping. "I knew everyone, once. That was the cost of keeping two worlds alive." He gestured, and the chapel's water went still as glass. "Give me the book and the shards, and walk away. You'll live. They'll live. The Spire will stand another century, and no one will ever know how close it came."

"And the covenant?"

"The covenant," Vess said gently, "is already broken. Has been since the night your mother found my ledgers. Everything since has been maintenance."

The word landed in my chest like a dropped coin. "*Maintenance.*"

"She came to me with proof. She was going to tell the conclave, the undercity elders, everyone." He folded his hands. "So I took the only thing she couldn't testify with. Her memories of it. All of them, really — it seemed kinder to take the whole cloth than leave her with holes. She woke up believing she'd never known me at all. Believing—" and here his voice did something almost tender, which made my skin crawl worse than any threat could, "—believing she'd never known *you* were part of why she'd stayed."

I heard myself ask the question even though every instinct screamed not to. "Part of why she—"

"Left you with the temple fosterage? August. Who do you think signed the order?"

The chapel was very quiet. Water dripped somewhere, counting seconds. Fen had gone rigid beside me; Brannoc's hands had closed into fists around his chains; Wren watched me with those old-tongue eyes that saw too much.

And I thought about my mother — the woman I'd spent twenty years building out of almost nothing. A face in one surviving portrait. A charm-seller's ledger with her handwriting in the margins. A single memory, mine, of her laughing in a kitchen while rain came down: the smell of rosemary, the warmth of her hand, the sound she made when she laughed, which I had replayed so many times I was afraid I'd wear it out.

"She's alive," I said. "Isn't she. You kept her memories. Which means you kept *her*."

Vess smiled the way a banker smiles. "She lives comfortably in the temple infirmary. Content. Empty of everything that hurt her — including, I'm afraid, the memory of you. But memories can be returned, August. Every one of them. Hers. Yours. A mother who knows your name again, who remembers the kitchen and the rain." He extended one hand, palm up, patient as stone. "Walk away. Give me the shards. And tomorrow morning I will put your mother back together and give her to you whole."

There it was. The offer I'd been walking toward my entire life without knowing it.

My mother's laugh. Her actual laugh, not the worn recording of it. Twenty years of her, handed back like a lost parcel.

Fen shifted beside me. "August," he said quietly. "Don't."

"Don't worry," I told him. "I'm not going to take it."

"No," Vess agreed, and there was no smile left at all. "You're going to refuse, because you're your mother's daughter, and then I'm going to erase you too, and the Spire will fall on schedule, and I will harvest what remains. It was never truly an offer, child. It was a courtesy."

Above us, through the chapel's broken roof, the sky had begun to change — not dawn's grey but something older, a bruised violet light bleeding down from the Spire's distant crown. The whole tower groaned, a sound I felt in my teeth, in the flooded floor, in the shards against three separate hearts.

Wren stood up on the altar. In the old tongue she said something short and absolute, and Brannoc translated, his voice rising like a landslide:

"She says: the pact remembers its keepers."

Maren pressed her shard into my hand. It burned cold. Then Brannoc's, still warm from his chest. Then Wren's, which hummed like a struck bell.

Three shards in my palms, and the prophecy book open at my feet, its last line pulsing softly: *the ink is not yet dry.*

"You built your fortune on a broken promise," I said to Vess, and my voice didn't shake, which surprised us both. "So here's mine. I'm going to reforge the covenant tonight. And I'm not going to use your ledgers, or your hoard, or your stolen years. I'm going to use something you can't confiscate, because you've never had one."

"Which is?"

"A memory worth losing."

The Spire groaned again, louder, and this time the sound didn't stop — it deepened, spread, became a long descending note like the tower clearing its throat to speak. Cracks of violet light raced down its flanks, visible even from here, miles away, beautiful and terrible as a struck match.

Dawn broke over a falling Spire, and we ran.
