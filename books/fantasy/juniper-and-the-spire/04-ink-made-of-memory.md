## 4. Ink Made of Memory

**August**

The Spire's heart was not what the hymns promised.

The hymns said light. The hymns said a throne of dawn and a choir built into the architecture itself, singing the covenant awake every morning. What was actually there was a room like the inside of a bell that had never been rung — round, cold, and hung with chains that weren't holding anything anymore. In the center stood a pedestal of black glass, and on it, a quill.

Not a golden quill. Not a relic. A quill made of bone, worn smooth by centuries of hands, and beside it an empty book whose pages breathed.

"That's it?" Fen said, from somewhere behind my left shoulder. "We climbed nine hundred stairs and fought through a corridor of very committed fanatics for *stationery*?"

"It's the oldest object in either realm," said Maren the healer, her voice still rough from the flooded quarter's damp. "Older than the temple. Older than the city. It doesn't take ink, August. It takes something truer."

I knew. I had known since the third shard settled into my palm in the undercity, since the feral child — Wren, we'd finally learned to call her — pressed her small hand over mine and spoke the old tongue directly into my bones: *the pact is written in what you cannot buy back.*

"I know," I said again, out loud this time, because saying things out loud is how I lie to myself least successfully.

Vess's voice came down from everywhere at once, the way it always did, as if the Spire itself were his mouth. "August Thornbury." He sounded almost kind. That was the worst part; he always sounded kind right before he took something. "You've assembled the shards. You've done the prophecy's work better than I could have hoped. And now you stand before the binding with nothing left to lose but your own mind."

"Careful," I called up to the dark where his voice lived. "That almost sounds like respect."

"It is arithmetic. Your mother stood where you stand. She chose poorly."

My mother. The word still opened a hole in me shaped exactly like her — a woman I knew only as a smell of dried lavender and a single memory of hands braiding my hair too tight, because everything else had been scraped out of her, and then out of me secondhand, by a man who hoarded magic the way misers hoard coins.

"She chose *exactly* right," I said. "She found out what you did. You just made sure no one would ever believe her."

The chains around us began to hum. Above, through the Spire's throat, I could hear stone grinding against stone — the fall beginning on schedule, because prophecies are punctual even when high canons are not.

Fen stepped up beside me. So did Maren. So did Corvin the stone-singer, freed three days ago from a cell he'd been singing to out of spite, and Wren, who looked at the breathing book the way other children looked at sweets.

"So which is it?" Fen asked quietly. "Which memories?"

I had thought about this on the stairs, all nine hundred of them. I had made lists. I am a charm-seller; I make lists.

Everything except one.

* * *

**Fen**

Here's the thing nobody tells you about being a pickpocket: you learn to read hands. Not faces — faces lie professionally. Hands tell you what a person is about to do three seconds before they know it themselves.

So when August rolled up her sleeve and set her bare wrist against the black glass, I already knew what she'd decided, and I hated it, and I said, "There has to be another way," knowing there wasn't, because I'd watched Maren check and Corvin check and even Wren shake her head with those ancient eyes.

"There isn't," August said. "But thanks for the dramatic objection. Really rounds out the scene."

"You're insufferable."

"I'm *chosen*, apparently. It's different."

The quill lifted off its pedestal on its own. Bone-white, patient, hungry. It hovered at her wrist like a mosquito with a sense of occasion.

"The covenant needs ink," Maren murmured. "Living ink. Given, not taken. Vess never understood that — he tried to *take* magic for thirty years, and look what it got him. A tower that's eating itself."

Corvin began to sing, low, the stone-song that had kept him sane in his cell, and the walls of the chamber stopped their grinding. Wren put both hands flat against the floor and the old tongue poured out of her like water finding its level.

And August said, "Take the childhood. Take my mother."

"No," I said. Stupidly. Uselessly.

"Fen." She looked at me, and her hands were steady, which broke me worse than shaking would have. "It's the one thing Vess could never steal, because she gave it to me freely. Freely given, freely spent. That's the whole rule, isn't it? The book's been trying to tell us since chapter one."

She didn't wait for my answer. Writers never do.

The quill touched her wrist and wrote.

* * *

**Maren**

I have healed the dying. I have held the dying. Neither prepared me for watching a living woman be unmade gently, one summer afternoon at a time.

Light came out of her — not blood, light — and each thread of it was a memory leaving: a kitchen with a crooked door. A woman humming off-key. Lavender. Rain on a tin roof. A birthday with a cake that collapsed and was eaten anyway, laughing. I saw them leave her because the binding showed them to all of us as they passed, and I understood then why the old covenant demanded this price. You cannot bind two realms together with anything less than proof that the binder values the bond above herself.

August swayed. Fen caught her. Of course Fen caught her; that boy has been catching things since before he could walk, mostly things that didn't belong to him.

"Is it working?" she whispered. Her voice had gone strange — lighter, emptier, a room after the furniture's been sold.

The book on the pedestal turned its own pages, filling with script that glowed like embers, and the chains rose from the floor, and the whole Spire shuddered — not falling now, *choosing* — and far above us we heard Vess scream.

* * *

**Corvin**

I sang the Spire steady while the new covenant wrote itself through her, and I will tell you honestly, stone-singer to whoever reads this: I have sung to granite for forty years and granite has never once surprised me. August surprised me.

Because when the last thread of her childhood left her — the braiding of hair, the lavender, the woman's voice saying *my little juniper berry* — August did not collapse into grief. She laughed. A small, cracked, absolutely furious laugh, and she said:

"He wanted my memories. He can't have them. They're spoken for."

And the book flared white.

* * *

**Wren**

*(translated from the old tongue, badly, by Corvin, who apologizes)*

The false shepherd stood in the falling light and the light unmade him. Not killed — unmade, which is different, which is what happens when a pact remembers who broke it. Every promise he ever swallowed came back out of him. Every stolen year went home. He became the shape of a man made entirely of debts, and then the debts were paid elsewhere, and then there was no Vess at all, only his robes folding down onto the floor like a puppet whose string everyone finally agreed to stop pretending existed.

The child says also: the Spire did not fall. The Spire *knelt*. There is a difference, and the difference is her.

* * *

**August**

Afterward, we sat on the nine hundred stairs because none of us had the strength for anything grander, and the city below was lit gold with a covenant renewed, and I felt — fine. That's the terrible part. I felt fine.

"What was she like?" Fen asked carefully. "Your mother. If you want to say."

I opened my mouth and found the hole where the answer should have been. Clean-edged. Precise. A wound shaped like a whole childhood.

"I don't know," I said. "I know I loved her. I know it cost me everything I had of her. But the *her* part—" I shook my head. "It's gone. I spent it."

Maren took my hand. Corvin started humming something soft. Wren climbed into my lap without asking, the way children do when they've decided you're theirs.

"But here's the thing," I said, and my voice steadied. "Everyone else remembers. You four. The book. Maybe the whole city, now — it's written into the covenant, isn't it? My mother's in the walls of both realms forever. I just can't visit her there myself."

"That's the worst bargain I've ever heard," Fen said.

"It's the best one I've ever made."

Wren tugged my sleeve and said something in the old tongue, and Corvin translated, smiling: *"She says the juniper tree remembers its berries even after they fall."*

I looked out over the city — our city, both cities, stitched back together with the only ink I had — and I thought: I don't remember what I saved.

But everyone I love does.

And that, as bargains go, will do.
