## 1. The Charm-Seller's Quiet

The bell above the door of Thackeray's Charms & Curios had a crack in it, so instead of ringing it made a sound like a small animal being stepped on. Casper Thackeray had meant to fix it for eleven years. She'd grown fond of it, in the way one grows fond of a limp — it was honest about the state of things.

"Morning," said the morning, in the person of Old Marta, who came in every third day to buy protection from something new.

"Morning, Marta."

Marta shuffled to the counter, peered at the display of charms — little knotted cords, tin medallions, jars of river-stones that Casper had personally collected while wading and swearing — and announced her trouble for the week. "The neighbor's cat is hexing my hens."

"The neighbor's cat."

"It has *eyes*, Casper. You've seen them."

Casper had seen the cat. It was orange and stupid and once fell into Marta's rain barrel. "I have just the thing." She took down a cord with three knots in it. "Hang it over the coop door. If the cat means your hens harm, it will forget which way the coop is."

This was, technically, true. The charm did nothing at all, but cats have short memories and poor opinions of chickens, and so far the magic had held perfectly. Marta paid her two coppers, pressed one of them into Casper's palm for luck — an old woman's superstition, giving money away to keep it coming back — and shuffled out, stepping on the bell's feelings as she went.

Casper stood alone in her shop and let out a breath she'd been holding for twenty years.

That was the shape of it, really. Twenty years of breaths, held lightly, released carefully, never once gasped. She was very good at it now. She could hold a breath through a ward-inspection, through a soldier's bored questions, through the moment each dawn when the ember of her real magic woke in her chest like a banked coal and asked, hopefully, *today?* And every day she answered it the same way: *not today.*

She opened her right hand under the counter, the way she did a hundred times a day, a habit worn smooth as a prayer bead. The palm was empty. It had been empty for twenty years. But there was a warmth there anyway, faint as sunlight through wool, and if she let herself — she didn't, usually, but some mornings, before the customers came — she could feel it settle into the lines of her hand like water finding a channel.

Veth's fire. Or what was left of it. A battle-priest's share of a god's own flame, folded down small enough to hide in a body, hidden in a city that would burn her for it.

Grayhollow had laws about people like her. Not cruel laws, not on paper. Mages were required to register, and registered mages were required to wear a gray band on their sleeve, and gray-banded mages were required to live inside the low quarter where the drains backed up when it rained, and everyone knew what happened to gray-banded mages who were noticed doing anything interesting. Interesting was a word that got used at tribunals. *Interesting* was how you ended up explaining yourself to men with ledgers and no sense of humor.

So Casper Thackeray sold charms that didn't work, to people whose problems didn't need working charms, and she was invisible, and invisible was safe, and safe was —

Safe was a room you got used to. That was all.

* * *

The morning trade came and went the way it always did: a fisherman wanting fair winds (knot-charm, four coppers, worked because he was a good sailor anyway), a young man wanting a love token that wouldn't embarrass him (a pressed juniper sprig in glass, six coppers, worked because the girl he liked already liked him and only needed a sign), a nervous mother wanting a fever-charm for her daughter (this one Casper charged nothing for, and felt the old guilt bite down hard, because a fever-charm that did nothing was a lie told to a frightened woman, and Casper had sworn off lies that hurt).

She gave the mother boiled willow bark and instructions, actual medicine, actual care, and watched her go with the specific ache of a woman who could, with a word and a touch, have burned the fever out of the child entirely — and hadn't, and wouldn't, and hated herself in the small quiet way that had become as routine as unlocking the door.

"You could help people," said the coal in her chest, in the voice it always used, which was no voice at all, just a pressure behind the sternum, a warmth in the palm, a patience that had lasted twenty years and showed no sign of cracking.

"I could get us both killed," Casper answered, silently, in the voice she always used, which was tired.

"That's not what I asked."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

This was the whole of their conversation, most days. It had been the whole of their conversation for twenty years, and somewhere in there Casper had stopped thinking of it as talking to herself and started thinking of it as talking to *him* — Veth, the silent god, the god who had never spoken a word in his existence and had needed no words, whose priests had fought with fire in their hands and silence in their mouths, and who had gone quiet one night two decades ago and never come back.

The order said he had died. The order had scattered before anyone could ask dying gods for details. Casper had done the arithmetic herself, alone, in the years after: gods did not die quietly. Gods died loudly, with earthquakes and weeping statues and the sky going strange. Whatever had happened to Veth had happened without a single sign, which meant either he had died so completely that even his death was erased, or he had been taken so thoroughly that there was nothing left to make signs with.

She had decided, somewhere around year nine, that it didn't matter. Dead or taken, he wasn't answering, and a priestess of a silent god in a city that hated priests was just a woman with a dangerous warmth in her hand and nowhere to put it.

The bell made its wounded noise. Casper looked up with her shopkeeper's face already on.

* * *

The afternoon brought Denny Cobb, who was twelve, who came in every Saturday, and who was the closest thing Casper had to a problem she couldn't solve with two coppers and a straight face.

"Afternoon, Mistress Thackeray." He said it formally, because he was saving up formality the way other boys saved up marbles. "I've got four coppers and a question."

"Four coppers buys the question free."

He leaned on the counter, which she pretended not to mind, because he was a good boy and his elbows were clean. "My da says magic isn't real. My gran says it used to be. My gran also says the king is a lizard wearing another lizard, so I take her views with salt. Which one's right? Da or Gran?"

"About the king?"

"About magic."

Casper considered the shelf behind him, the rows of harmless knots and stones and bottled nothing, twenty years of her life arranged neatly by price. "Magic's like weather," she said. "Some places have more of it than others. Some years it doesn't come at all. People who say it isn't real have just lived somewhere dry."

Denny absorbed this with the frown of a boy building a cathedral out of the sentence. "Have *you* ever seen it?"

And there it was. The question, arriving on schedule, the way it arrived every few months from some child or drunk or dreamer, the question she had answered wrong exactly zero times in twenty years, because answering it right even once would be the last interesting thing she ever did.

"No," she said pleasantly. "Can't say I have."

"But you *sell* it."

"I sell hope with knots in it. Hope's real. Knots are cheap." She slid a bent nail across the counter. "Here. Hang this over your door and your da will come home from the tavern earlier than your gran expects. Works every time."

"Why?"

"Because your gran expects him at midnight and I gave the nail to *you*, and you'll stand in the doorway looking hopeful, and no man alive walks past that." Four coppers. Denny grinned, paid, and left, stepping on the bell.

Casper watched him go and thought, not for the first time, that this was the cruelest joke of her hiding: she was *good* at it. She had built a whole warm little life out of almost-magic, out of psychology and willow bark and knowing which neighbors were hexed by cats, and the life fit her like a glove, and gloves keep your hands warm and stop them from doing anything else.

Half-lived. That was the word for it, though she never said it aloud. A life half-lived, in a city half-awake, serving a god half-gone, keeping a fire half-alive. Everything halved. Everything careful. Twenty years of careful, stacked up like the jars on her shelves, and not one jar of it worth opening.

* * *

Near dusk, a man came in whom Casper had never seen before, and Casper prided herself on having seen everyone. Grayhollow was a city of ten thousand souls and she knew perhaps four hundred of them by name and all of them by gait; strangers were rare and therefore interesting, and interesting, as established, was dangerous.

He was ordinary in the aggressive way of a man trying to be ordinary. Brown coat, brown boots, brown hair going politely gray. He browsed the shelves without touching anything, which no one did — everyone touched everything, it was a shop of talismans, touching was the point — and then he came to the counter and set down a coin that was slightly wrong.

Not counterfeit. Wrong. The king's face on it was King Aldric's, correct profile, correct crown, but the metal was too pale, and when Casper tilted it to the light there was a smell off it, faint, like a snuffed candle. Like the moment after a flame dies, when the wick still remembers burning.

"We don't take foreign coin," she said, though Grayhollow minted its own and this was nearly it.

"It's not foreign." He smiled. It was a well-made smile, fitted to his face like the coin's face was fitted to the king's. "It's new."

"New mint's still warm, then. This one's cold."

Something moved behind his eyes — not suspicion, nothing so useful as suspicion. A kind of *attention*, flat and total, the way a fish looks at a hook. "You have a good eye," he said. "For a charm-seller."

"I have a good eye for bad coin. Everyone in trade does."

"Mm." He picked up a knot-charm from the counter, turned it over once, and set it down — and Casper's skin crawled, because the knot was wrong now. She had tied that knot herself that morning, three loops, and she knew her own hands' work the way she knew her own teeth, and the knot on the counter had four loops. As if the cord had been *edited*. As if someone had reached into the morning and quietly removed a loop, and the world had accepted the correction without complaint.

She did not look at it twice. Looking twice was interesting. She rang up his purchase — he bought nothing, and paid for buying nothing with the pale coin, which she dropped into the reject jar rather than the till, and he watched her do it and smiled again, that fitted smile — and he left, and the bell made its small hurt sound, and Casper stood very still for a long moment.

Then she picked up the knot-charm and untied it loop by loop, and there were three loops, and then four again, and then she couldn't tell anymore, and she threw it in the stove and watched it burn with a heat that was, for one unguarded heartbeat, warmer than the stove deserved.

Her palm flared. Just once. Just enough that she had to close her fist and press it against her apron and breathe, slowly, through her nose, the twenty-year breath, the practiced breath.

*Not today,* she told the coal.

But the coal didn't answer, and that was new. Usually it argued. Usually it hoped at her, stubbornly, all evening. Tonight it was simply warm, and attentive, and quiet — and Casper Thackeray, who had spent twenty years learning the language of silences, recognized this one.

It was the silence of something waiting.

* * *

She closed the shop early, which she never did, telling herself it was the ache in her hip and knowing it wasn't. She counted the till, which came out right. She ate bread and hard cheese standing at her kitchen window, watching the street below, watching the light go long and amber and then thin.

Grayhollow sat in a bowl of hills, and from her window she could see the border wall away to the east, a gray seam on the horizon, crowned with the ward-towers that had stood since before her grandmother's grandmother. The towers carried the great wards — old magic, older than the kingdom, older than names, the lattice that kept out the wild dark beyond the hills. Every child in Grayhollow grew up on those towers the way children grow up on mountains: permanent, assumed, part of the shape of the sky.

Casper had grown up on them differently. Her order had helped raise three of those towers, back when the world had gods in it and her god had walked it in silence, laying wards with fire instead of mortar. She had stood on one once, at nineteen, with Veth's warmth in her hand and her whole life ahead of her, and she had thought: *I am standing on something that will outlast me.* She had been right, in the way that mattered least.

The sun went down. The first stars came out over the eastern hills, above the wall, above the towers — bright, familiar, wrongly numbered, though she couldn't have said why *wrongly*, only that some old instinct in her counted them every night and every night the count came back true, and tonight, for the first time in twenty years, she found herself counting at all.

She finished the bread. She washed the plate. She banked her fire. She did all the small closing rituals of a careful woman, and through all of it the coal in her chest stayed warm and awake and silent, and her right palm stayed open at her side, though she kept telling it to close.

Outside, the lamplighter made his rounds. Inside, Casper blew out her candle and stood in the dark, listening to her city settle — cart wheels, a dog, Marta's hens complaining about the cat, the thousand small sounds of ten thousand people who believed tomorrow would arrive on schedule.

Far to the east, past the wall, past the hills, the last light faded.

And the first star went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded. *Out* — pinched, like a wick between wet fingers, there and then not, leaving a hole in the sky shaped exactly like itself.

Casper's counting instinct, the old one, the priestess one, woke all the way up for the first time in twenty years and began, very calmly, to scream.

The second star went out. Then the third. Then a handful at once, a scatter of small darknesses blooming across the east like frost spreading over a window, and beneath them, far off, so faint she might have imagined it, came a sound she knew from her old life, a sound she had heard exactly once before and prayed never to hear again:

Wards breaking. A long, splintering crack rolling across the hills, like bone, like ice, like the world clearing its throat to forget something enormous.

In her dark kitchen, Casper Thackeray opened her right hand all the way, and the ember of a dead god rose in her palm like a sunrise deciding whether to happen.

"*Oh,*" she said, to no one, to him, to whatever was listening. "*No.*"
