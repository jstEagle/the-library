## 2. Wards Like Broken Teeth

The sound came at dusk, and it came from everywhere.

Casper was in her kitchen with a cup of barley tea going cold beside her, doing what she did most evenings: nothing, carefully. It was a skill she had practiced for twenty years, the art of being no one in particular, and she had gotten very good at it. She sat in the one chair that didn't creak. She kept her breathing even. She thought about charms.

Specifically, she thought about the batch of luck-charms she'd have to finish by market day—little knotted cords dyed the blue-gray of Veth's old vestments, because people trusted that color even if they'd forgotten why. They were mostly string and mostly lies. A charm of Casper's making might get you through a bad week. It would not get you through anything worth writing down.

She was reaching for the tea when the crack came.

It rolled across Grayhollow like thunder that had learned to think. It started somewhere far to the east—the border, the hills, the long gray line of standing stones that had held since before anyone's grandmother—and it traveled through the ground, up through the legs of her chair, into her teeth. It sounded exactly like what it was: something enormous snapping. Bone. Ice. The hinge of a door that had been shut for a thousand years.

Casper's cup cracked in her hand. Not dropped—*cracked*, split clean along its glaze, and the tea went steaming across the table, and she stared at it because staring at small comprehensible things was how she survived large incomprehensible ones.

Then the stars began to go out.

She saw it through the window over her sink, the one she never opened because opening windows invited questions. First the bright one low in the west—the sailors called it the Lantern, though half of them had forgotten why—winked out like a candle pinched between wet fingers. Then another. Then two more, close together, as if someone were walking along the sky snuffing them in order.

Not clouds. Not occlusion. Casper had spent twenty years teaching herself to notice when magic was lying to her, and this wasn't lying. The stars weren't hidden. They were *leaving*. Going out the way a memory goes out—not darkened, but *unmade*, so that you couldn't even say afterward where they had been.

"Oh," she said again, to the same empty room. "No, no, no."

And then her right hand woke up.

*

There is no polite way to describe what twenty years of buried magic feels like when it stops being buried. Casper had spent two decades holding it down the way you hold a lid on a boiling pot—with weight, with habit, with the constant low-grade effort of a woman who has decided that the alternative is death. She had gotten so used to the pressure that she'd stopped feeling it, the way you stop feeling your own shoes.

The shoes came off all at once.

It went through her like a struck bell. Her whole body rang. The ember—that warm coal of Veth's presence she'd carried in her palm since the night everything ended, the thing she fed a drop of attention every morning the way you feed a fire you're not supposed to have—rose up her arm, filled her chest, poured out through her skin until she could feel the whole street. Every house. Every pipe and hearthstone. The woman three doors down burning onions. The cat on the roof opposite, whose name was Bristle and who was thinking about a pigeon. The wards—or where the wards had been—a great absence running along the edge of the world like a pulled tooth.

She could feel the hole where they'd been. That was the worst part. Not the breaking; the *shape* of the breaking, still ringing, still singing its last note out into the dark.

"Stop," Casper whispered, gripping the ruined table with both hands. "Stop it. Sit *down*."

Her magic had never been good at obedience. That had been the joke, once—Veth's priests were famously the quietest order in the world, and his gifts were famously the loudest. Silence outside, weather inside. She had spent twenty years being weatherless. And now every ward she'd ever woven into her own skin, every knot and seal and little lie of *I am only a charm-seller, I am only tired, I am only a woman with string*, was unraveling like wet thread, and underneath it all there was light, and the light had her name on it.

Somewhere down the street, a dog began to howl. Then another. Then, absurdly, Bristle the cat, yowling at the darkening sky like a small furry prophet.

Casper got her hands flat on the table and breathed, and did the oldest exercise in her order's book: *name five things*. The table. The cracked cup. The smell of burnt onion. The cold coming through the window glass. The sound of boots—

Boots.

Many boots. In the street below her window, moving fast, in the organized rhythm of soldiers who had been given orders and were enjoying them.

*

Grayhollow had laws about magic the way a scarred dog has opinions about hands. The city sat a hard day's ride from the border, close enough to remember what the wards were for, and sometime in the last century—Casper had never bothered to learn exactly when, because learning things made you interesting—the magistrates had decided that the safest mage was an absent one. There were forms. There were registries, for the few licensed hedge-witches whose work was deemed harmless. There were sweeps.

Casper had watched sweeps from behind her shuttered window twice in twenty years. They were not subtle. Soldiers went door to door with iron rods wrapped in prayer-cloth, and the rods hummed near active magic, and the humming was followed by questions, and the questions were followed by carts.

She blew out her lamp, which was pointless but felt decisive, and stood very still in her kitchen while the boots multiplied outside.

Through the gap in her shutters she could see torchlight moving up Thistle Row. She could see neighbors gathering on their stoops in nightclothes, pointing up at the wounded sky, asking each other questions nobody could answer. Old Marta from the dye shop was out in the street in her apron, arms crossed, glaring at the missing stars as though they owed her money. Someone's child was crying. Somewhere a bell had started ringing—the temple bell, the big one, rung perhaps four times a century—and it didn't stop, just went on and on, bronze voice cracking with repetition, saying the only word bells know: *now, now, now.*

A soldier hammered on a door across the lane. Voices. The rod-hum, faint, like a trapped insect. Then silence, and the boots moved on.

They were checking everyone. Of course they were. The wards had just died and the stars were going out and the first instinct of frightened men with authority was to find someone to blame, and the oldest someone-to-blame in Grayhollow's ledger was a mage.

Casper looked down at her right hand.

It was glowing. Faintly, gutteringly, the way a banked coal glows when the wind finds it—but glowing, unmistakably, threads of gold rising off her knuckles like heat-shimmer, and every iron rod within a mile would be screaming about it.

"*Down,*" she told it, through her teeth, pressing her palm against her apron. "Twenty years. Twenty years you've been quiet. You can manage one more night."

The magic did not agree. The magic, in fact, seemed delighted. It surged up her arm again, and this time it went past her—out through the walls, out into the street, and Casper felt it touch the broken line of the border wards and *reach toward them*, the way a hand reaches toward a wound, and she understood with sick clarity what it wanted to do.

It wanted to fix them.

It wanted her to stand in the middle of Thistle Row in front of forty soldiers and a hundred terrified neighbors and knit the sky back together with her bare hands, and some traitorous part of her—the part she'd been starving for twenty years—wanted that too, wanted it so badly her eyes stung.

"No," she said aloud, and her voice cracked in the middle of the word. "You don't get to want things. I don't get to want things. That was the deal."

The deal, of course, had been made alone, in the dark, twenty years ago, by a younger woman kneeling in the ruins of the only home she'd ever loved, promising a silent god that she would keep his last ember safe and never, ever use it, because using it would make her visible, and visible things got taken. It had seemed like wisdom at the time. Tonight, with the stars dying overhead and her own power howling in her ears like a locked dog, it seemed a lot more like fear wearing wisdom's coat.

The boots reached her building.

Three hard knocks on the door downstairs, and the muffled voice of the landlady, Mrs. Pell, who took Casper's rent and asked no questions and was about to earn every copper of it. Then the stairs. Then the knock on *her* door, and a voice, young and scared and trying to sound official:

"Open in the name of the magistrate! Ward inspection!"

Casper pressed her back against the kitchen wall, out of the sightline of the door, and did the fastest arithmetic of her life. The glow was fading—her years of practice hadn't entirely rotted away; she could smother it, given ten seconds and cooperation from her own bones. The room was dark. Charm-sellers were registered, tolerated, boring. If she opened the door as a tired woman with string, if her hands stayed quiet, if the soldier's rod hummed only the way it hummed near everyone's little household superstitions—

If. If. If.

She thought, wildly, of Veth. Not of the god, exactly—of the man-shaped presence he'd worn in her memories, the patient weight of him at her shoulder during twenty years of ordination, the way he'd once said, in the voice that was less a voice than a settling of snow: *Small honest things, Casper. The world runs on them.* He had believed that. She had built a whole life on the wreckage of believing it too.

The stars went out. Another one—she felt it go, actually felt it, a cold pluck somewhere behind her sternum, like a harp string cut—and the child crying in the street cried louder, and Mrs. Pell's footsteps retreated down the stairs, and the knock came again, harder.

"Last warning!"

Casper Thackeray looked at her right hand, which had gone dark and obedient and utterly untrustworthy, and she thought: *one more night.* One more night of being small. One more morning of string and lies. And then, tomorrow, or the day after, she would figure out what in the name of everything holy had snapped the wards, and why the sky was forgetting itself, and whether the ember in her palm was a relic or a message.

Because it couldn't be a coincidence. Nothing that size was ever a coincidence. The wards had held for a thousand years, and the stars had burned for longer, and both had broken on the same dusk, and Casper's sleeping magic had woken on the same hour, and somewhere under all that fear a cold clear voice she recognized as her own said: *this is the beginning of something, and beginnings do not wait for the brave. They come for whoever's nearest.*

She crossed the kitchen, composed her face into the mild blankness of a woman interrupted over her tea, and opened the door.

The soldier was nineteen at most, torchlight jumping on his cheekbones, iron rod held out before him like a candle he didn't trust. Behind him, the sky hung black and starless where stars should have been, and the whole street was looking up at it, and the bell went on saying *now, now, now.*

"Name and trade," the boy said.

"Casper Thackeray. Charms." She held up the knotted cord from the table, the blue-gray one, and let him see her hands, which trembled—which was true, and useful, and read as fear of soldiers rather than fear of the sky. "Luck knots. Nothing that hums."

He passed the rod slowly around her doorway. It stayed quiet. It stayed quiet because she made it stay quiet, sweating, smiling her tired smile, holding the lid down with both invisible hands while the pot screamed underneath.

The boy lowered the rod. Relief loosened his shoulders, and for a moment he was just a kid standing in a doorway on the worst night of his life, and he glanced up at the empty sky and then, unexpectedly, at her.

"They say the border's gone," he said. Quietly. Off the record. "The whole line. Stones just—fell over. And there's riders coming up the coast road. Gray ones. Nobody can say what they look like, and my sergeant says that's the worst part, that nobody can *say*."

Something went cold in Casper's chest, colder than the missing stars.

"Riders," she repeated.

"Gray ones." He shifted, embarrassed, official again. "Stay indoors, mistress. Curfew till dawn." He turned, boots receding down the stairs, and called back over his shoulder: "Lock your door. Whatever's coming, it comes from outside."

Casper closed the door and leaned against it and listened to the bell, and the boots, and the child, and the enormous ringing silence where the wards used to sing.

*Riders nobody can describe.* She knew that shape of wrongness. She had felt it once before, twenty years ago, on the worst night of her life, in the moment before everything ended—something approaching that could not be remembered properly, something that erased the words you tried to hold it with. She had told herself she'd imagined it. She had gotten very good at imagining things.

In her right palm, without her permission, the ember flared—brief, fierce, joyful, like a dog hearing its master's step on the stair.

And far away, down the coast road, through the toothless dark where the border used to be, Casper felt—for the first time in twenty years, with her whole waking skin—something *coming*. Many-footed. Patient. Hungry in a way that had nothing to do with food.

It was looking for something. It was following something small and bright and desperate.

And it was three days out at most.

"*Oh no,*" Casper said, for the third time that night, and this time she meant it most of all.
