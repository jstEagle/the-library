## 3. The Girl Who Carried Fire

Juniper had learned three things about running in the last nine days.

One: never stop moving, even to sleep. Two: never trust a road that looks too smooth, because roads that forget themselves also forget the people on them. Three: if you must cry, do it while walking, because crying while standing still is how you die.

She was very good at crying while walking now. She could do it without sobbing, without even hiccupping, just letting the tears slide down her face and dry in the cold wind off the hills, like the weather was handling her grief for her so she could keep her hands on the egg.

The egg hung in its lantern at her hip, swinging with her stride. It was no bigger than a goose's egg, pale as a coal gone cold, and when she held it up to the light she could see something inside it turning slowly, like a moth in a jar. Warmth pulsed out of it in slow heartbeats. Once, nine days ago and a whole world away, that warmth had been enough to heat a whole hatchery. Now Juniper cupped her hands around the lantern and pretended she couldn't feel the pulses getting farther apart.

"You're fine," she told it, for the hundredth time that day. "We're both fine. Everything is fine."

Everything was so thoroughly not fine that the word had started to feel like a joke she was telling to keep herself company.

Behind her, somewhere past the hills, the borderlands were gone. Not burned, not flooded — *gone*, the way a word goes when you can't remember it. She'd looked back once, from the ridge above Millbrook, and seen the valley where she'd been born, and it had been there, and it hadn't. The houses stood. The smoke rose. But when she tried to think of her mother's face, she found a smooth gray place in her mind where the face should have been, like a portrait someone had sanded down. She could remember that she had a mother. She could remember that the mother had smelled like woodsmoke and had hands that were rough from dragon-feed. But the face itself, the voice, the name — sanded away.

The riders had done that. The gray ones on the gray horses, the ones with no faces at all, just smooth blankness where a face should go, like eggs before they're written on.

Juniper ran faster when she thought about them, so she thought about them a lot.

* * *

She reached Grayhollow on the eleventh day, at dusk, when the city was lighting its lamps and the smell of bread and coal-smoke came over the walls like a welcome she had no right to.

The gate guards didn't even look at her. That was the first good thing in nine days — a pair of bored soldiers in dented helms, waving through a cart of turnips, a peddler with a mule, a girl in a patched coat with soot on her chin and a lantern at her hip. One of them glanced at her and she braced herself, hand going to the lantern strap, heart hammering out a little war-drum rhythm. *Be nobody. Be nobody. You're a nobody from nowhere, going nowhere.*

The guard said, "Mind the puddle, miss," and went back to picking his teeth.

Juniper could have kissed him. She didn't, mostly because her legs had stopped taking orders about four miles back and kissing anyone would have meant stopping.

Inside the walls, Grayhollow was loud and warm and alive in a way that made her chest hurt. People arguing about fish prices. Children chasing a dog with a stolen sausage. A woman leaning out a window shouting a name — *Tam! Tam, if you've broken that window again* — and somewhere a boy's laughter. All of it ordinary. All of it still *whole*, these people carrying their memories around like loose coins, spending them without a thought, not knowing what it was to reach for your own mother's face and find nothing there.

Juniper walked until the streets narrowed and the lamplight thinned, because crowds were good for losing riders in but bad for staying alive in, and her grandmother — no. Someone. Someone had told her that. Someone with a voice like gravel and honey. The memory had a hole in it where the someone should be, and she'd stopped poking at it because poking made her cry, and she had a schedule of crying and it was not now.

The lantern gave a faint, stuttering warmth against her hip.

"I know," she murmured. "I know. Soon."

She'd promised it warmth and a hearth and a hatchery full of cousins, back when the promise had been a real thing a real keeper could make. Now the promise had shrunk to: *a chimney, any chimney, a candle, a lit window, please, anything.*

She found one. A crooked little shop wedged between a cobbler's and a house that leaned like a drunk, its window glowing amber, a hand-painted sign that read THACKERAY'S CHARMS in letters that had been confident once and were now mostly peeling. In the window, on a shelf of dusty velvet, sat an array of little charms — knotted cords, painted stones, a dried salamander that Juniper strongly suspected had never been magical even when it was alive.

She stood in the street looking at it, swaying on her feet.

It wasn't the charms. It was the light. It was the fact that the window was lit, which meant a hearth, which meant warmth, which meant the ember might last one more night. It was also, she admitted, the fact that her side had been bleeding through her coat for two days and the world had gone soft and swimmy at the edges, and soft swimmy edges were how people died in ditches.

"Right," she said to the lantern. "We're going in. Be charming. Or — be an egg. Just be an egg, that's fine."

The door stuck. She shouldered it, and it gave all at once, and Juniper — who had planned to be mysterious, and weary, and dignified — fell directly into a shelf of painted stones and lay there for a moment among the clatter, looking up at a low ceiling crossed with drying herbs, thinking: *this is how I die, killed by a charm shop, and nobody will even write it down.*

* * *

The woman who came out of the back room was perhaps forty, with gray-shot brown hair pinned up in a style that had given up on elegance and settled for containment, and eyes the color of a storm deciding whether to bother. She looked at Juniper. She looked at the scattered stones. She looked at the blood soaking through Juniper's coat, which Juniper only then realized was visible, which explained a great deal about the swimminess.

"Out," the woman said.

"Please." Juniper tried to get up. Her side had opinions about this, loudly. She ended up half-sitting among the stones, one hand pressed to her ribs, the other still wrapped around the lantern strap. "I'll pay. I don't — I don't have much, but I'll pay, I just need — "

"You need a healer, not a charm shop."

"I need a warm room for one night. That's all. One night, and I'm gone by dawn, and you'll never see me again." She heard how it sounded. She heard the wheeze in it, the desperation. She made herself look the woman in the eyes anyway, because her grandmother — *someone* — had said that people believe you when you can hold their gaze, and Juniper had gotten very good, these nine days, at holding gazes while lying with every inch of her body except her eyes.

The woman's eyes narrowed. "You're hurt."

"It's nothing."

"It's *soaking through your coat*."

"It's been doing that for two days," Juniper said, and then, because the swimminess was getting worse and her filter was going with it, added, "it's actually much better than it was. Yesterday it was soaking through faster."

The woman stared at her. And then, to Juniper's complete surprise, she made a sound that was almost a laugh, short and unwilling, like it had escaped custody.

"Gods help me," the woman muttered. "Sit still before you fall down."

She disappeared into the back and came back with a basin, clean linen, and a bottle of something that smelled like it could strip paint. She knelt on the floorboards beside Juniper without a word about dignity or the mess, and cut the coat away from the wound with a small sharp knife, and Juniper hissed and gripped the lantern strap and did not scream, because screaming was loud and loud was dangerous.

The wound was a slice across her ribs, long and shallow-but-not-shallow-enough, from a rider's blade on the first night, when the hatchery burned. It had been closing. It had been *trying* to close, fed by the ember's warmth pressed against it every night. But the ember was dimmer now, and the wound had gone angry and red, and the woman looked at it with an expression Juniper couldn't read.

"This is a blade wound," the woman said. "Clean edge. Recent enough. Who cut you?"

"Bandits," Juniper said.

"Bandits," the woman repeated, in the tone of someone filing a lie in a drawer for later.

"Big ones. Very committed."

The woman cleaned the wound. It hurt so much that Juniper bit her own sleeve and saw white sparks, and through the white sparks she noticed things about the shop she hadn't noticed before. The herbs hanging from the ceiling weren't decorative — they were arranged, deliberately, in patterns she half-recognized from the hatchery's old wards. The painted stones on the shelf weren't painted at all; the color came from *inside* them, faint and banked, like coals under ash. And the woman's hands, wrapping the linen, moved with a sureness that no charm-seller's hands should have, a sureness that Juniper had only ever seen on keepers handling fire.

Juniper looked up at her, and the woman looked down at her, and for one long moment neither of them said anything, and the lantern against Juniper's hip gave a slow, warm pulse, like a heart noticing a friend.

"You're not a charm-seller," Juniper said.

"You're not a bandit victim," the woman said back.

They regarded each other. Outside, the street had gone quiet. Too quiet, Juniper realized — the fish-arguments and the sausage-dog and the shouting woman had all stopped at once, the way birds stop before a storm.

"I'm Juniper," she said, because it seemed suddenly important, before the quiet arrived, to have said her name to somebody. To have been *somebody* to somebody, even for a moment, even a lying somebody.

The woman hesitated. Juniper watched her decide — watched it happen, actually watched the decision move through her face like weather — and then the woman said, "Thackeray. Casper Thackeray," in the voice of someone handing over something she'd sworn never to hand over.

And that was when the horses came.

* * *

Juniper heard them first with her skin, before her ears caught up — a coldness rolling down the street like water finding a drain. The lantern flared against her hip, hot as a stove-lid, and the ember inside it turned over in its sleep and *whined*, a thin high note she'd never heard it make, and every hair on her body stood up.

"No," she whispered. "No, no, no —"

Casper Thackeray had gone very still, kneeling on the floor with bloody linen in her hands. "What is it? What's out there?"

"Riders." Juniper was already moving, dragging herself up the shelf, white pain flashing through her ribs. "Gray riders. They followed me from the border. They can't — they can't find the egg, do you understand, they *can't*, they'll — "

The lamp in the window guttered. Not blew out — *guttered*, the flame leaning away from the glass as if trying to hide. Down the street, the amber light of the other windows began to go out one by one, not all at once but in a line, like footsteps, like something walking up the street and drinking the light as it came.

And the sounds went with it. Juniper pressed her eye to the gap in the shutter and saw the street she'd walked an hour ago — and saw the *remembering* of it draining away. The cobbler's sign was fading. The puddle the guard had warned her about was still there, but she could no longer have said what street it was on. The shouting woman's voice had stopped mid-word somewhere in the city's memory, and would never finish.

Through the window, at the far end of the street, the riders came. Gray horses, gray cloaks, and where faces should have been — nothing. Smooth. Blank. Unwritten.

One of them turned its head — there was no head, but it turned anyway — toward the charm shop's window.

"They're here," Juniper said. Her voice came out very small. She hated it. She was sixteen years old and the last keeper of the last ember of dragon-fire and her voice came out small as a child's. "They're here, they found me, I'm sorry, I led them right to you —"

"Get away from the window." Casper was up, and there was something in her face that hadn't been there before — not fear, exactly, though fear was in it, but underneath the fear something older and harder, like bedrock under soil. "The back door. Is it —"

"It's a wall," Juniper said. "I checked. It's a wall."

"Of course it's a wall." Casper pressed her back against the counter, and her hand — Juniper saw it, would remember seeing it for the rest of her life — her hand closed into a fist, and the fist began, very faintly, to glow. Not the painted-stone glow, not the banked-coal glow. A white, fierce, *awake* light, leaking between her fingers like something that had been held down for twenty years and had just heard the door open.

"You *are* a charm-seller," Juniper breathed.

"I'm going to be a dead charm-seller in about ten seconds if you don't tell me what those things are and what they want."

"They want the egg." Juniper pulled the lantern up and held it against her chest with both arms, the way she'd held it through the burning hatchery, through the river, through nine days of running, and felt it pulse against her heart like a small furious second heartbeat. "They want the last dragon-fire in the world. And they want me to forget I ever had it."

The shop door rattled. Once. Twice. The handle turned, slowly, like something that had forgotten how handles worked and was relearning.

Casper Thackeray looked at the door. She looked at the glowing fist. She looked at Juniper — at the bleeding, exhausted, terrified girl holding a lantern like it was the last warm thing in the world, which, as far as Juniper knew, it was.

"Behind the counter," Casper said. "Now. And whatever you hear — whatever you *see* —"

The door burst inward off its hinges, and gray poured through it like smoke given weight, and the light in Casper's fist rose to fill the whole shop.

Juniper didn't hide. She was too busy watching, with her heart in her throat and the ember blazing against her chest, as the quiet charm-seller of Grayhollow raised her burning hand against the dark — and spoke a word that Juniper's bones knew, though her sanded-down mind had lost it, a word that meant *let there be light, and let it remember*.
