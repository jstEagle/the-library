## 4. What the Riders Wanted

The word came out of Casper like a tooth she'd been clenching for twenty years.

*Lux memora.*

Light bloomed in the shop — not the polite amber of her counter-lamps but something older, whiter, a light with opinions. It poured across the floorboards and struck the gray things that had come through her door, and they *screamed*, though they had no mouths to scream with.

There were two of them. She'd expected more. They were shaped like men the way a glove is shaped like a hand — the right silhouette, worn by nothing. Their faces were smooth as river stones, their armor gray as ash, their horses — no. She looked again and understood with a lurch that there had never been horses. The lower halves of them simply trailed off into smoke, like figures in an unfinished sketch.

"Behind the counter," Casper said again, because repetition was all she had. "Don't touch them."

Juniper was already behind the counter, crouched low, the lantern-egg hugged to her chest. The girl's eyes were enormous. "What are they?"

"I was about to ask them the same thing."

One of the riders moved. Not walked — *poured*, the way spilled ink finds the cracks in a table. It flowed toward the shelf where Casper kept her love-charms, the ones made of string and pigeon bone and honest fraud, and where its trailing edge brushed the wood, the wood simply stopped being interesting. That was the only way Casper could describe it. The grain didn't vanish; the *history* did. A shelf that had been cut, sanded, sold, and dusted every week for two decades became, in the space of a heartbeat, just planks. Meaningless ones. Her eyes slid off them like rain off waxed canvas.

"Oh," Casper said softly. "Oh, you're not soldiers."

The second rider turned its blank face toward her voice.

"You're *forgetting*. Given legs."

She should have run. Twenty years of discipline said run: out the back, down Tanner's Row, become nobody again before anybody looked twice. But the light in her fist was warm, and it knew her, and beneath the terror there was something so shameful she could barely look at it directly.

It felt *good*. To speak the old words. To be, for one whole breath, what she had been made to be.

The first rider lunged — poured itself at Juniper, at the egg, at the ember-smell of it — and Casper moved without deciding to.

Her free hand drew the sign of Veth in the air: circle, then line, then the small crossbar at the base that meant *a door held shut against the dark*. Her mouth spoke the syllables her body had never forgotten even as her mind pretended they were gone. And the light left her hand like a thrown spear.

It caught the rider mid-flow and unmade it from the edges inward. There was no blood. There wasn't even ash. The thing simply came apart into a fine gray dust that hung in the lamplight for a moment — and then, horribly, began to *drift toward her*, toward the light, as if trying to remember what it had been.

Casper flinched back. The dust touched the hem of her apron and the apron forgot it had ever been dyed blue.

"Veth's quiet bones," she whispered, which was either a prayer or a swear, and after twenty years she honestly couldn't tell anymore.

The second rider hesitated. If a faceless thing can hesitate. It drifted sideways, putting the counter between itself and her light, and its smooth head tilted — and Casper felt, distinctly, the sensation of being *read*. Of something going through her the way Hale used to go through the archive ledgers, licking his thumb, looking for one name among thousands.

It found what it was looking for. She watched it find it.

The rider's blank face split into something that was almost a smile, if a smile could be carved rather than felt.

"*Thackeray,*" it said. Its voice was the sound of a page tearing. "*Veth's blade. The one who ran.*"

The shop went very still. Behind the counter, Juniper made a small sound.

"I don't know what you're talking about," Casper said, in the flat merchant's voice she'd polished for two decades, and the lie tasted exactly as thin as it was.

The rider drifted closer. Where it passed, the memory went out of things. Her father's chair by the stove became a stool. The crack in the third floorboard — put there the winter she'd dropped an entire crate of candle-wax, laughing until she cried — became just a crack. The shop was being sanded down around her, smoothed into a room that had never belonged to anyone, and she understood, with a cold clarity that felt almost like calm, that this was how it would take her. Not with claws. With *erosion*. It would wear away everything she'd built here until there was nothing left worth defending, and then it would drift through the empty shape of her life and out the other side.

Unless she burned it first.

"Casper," Juniper whispered. "Your hand."

Casper looked down. The light in her palm had changed. It wasn't the white spear-light anymore. It had gone gold at the center, deep and steady, the color of the last coal in a hearth at midnight, and it was pulsing — once per heartbeat, her heartbeat — like something answering a call it had been waiting twenty years to hear.

She thought about the word *ran*.

She thought about a border shrine, twenty years ago, and a god who had needed her, and a young woman whose hands had shaken too badly to hold a sword.

"No," Casper told the rider conversationally. "I didn't run. I hid. There's a difference. Running means you've stopped fighting." She raised her burning hand. "I'm told I'm very slow to learn lessons. But I do eventually learn them."

The rider poured itself at her.

She met it with everything — not the careful measured trickle of power she'd allowed herself over two decades, not the charm-seller's thimbleful, but the whole drowned well of her, hauled up bucket by bucket in a single motion. The words came faster than thought, in the old liturgical cadence, call and response, and some buried part of her supplied the responses without being asked, the way a tongue finds its own language in sleep.

*Who stands at the door?*

*I stand.*

*Whose light is carried?*

*Mine, and His, and mine again.*

The gold light hit the rider like dawn hitting fog. It shrieked — that tearing-page voice rising to a pitch that set her teeth humming — and came apart, and this time the gray dust didn't drift toward her. It hung in the air, confused, purposeless, and then settled on the floorboards as ordinary dirt, because forgetting without a will behind it is just dust.

Silence. Real silence, the kind Grayhollow hadn't heard since the wards broke.

Casper stood in the middle of her ruined shop with her hand still raised, breathing like a bellows, feeling twenty years of careful smallness crumble off her like the apron's dye.

"Well," she said. "That's that, then."

* * *

Juniper emerged from behind the counter the way a hedgehog emerges from under a leaf pile: slowly, in stages, ready to retreat at any moment. She surveyed the shop — the doorless doorway, the gray dust, the shelves that had gone strangely bland, the charm-seller standing in the wreckage with light still guttering off her fingers.

"You killed them," Juniper said.

"Yes."

"With a word."

"Several words, technically. There was a whole liturgy. You missed most of it." Casper flexed her fingers. The light was fading now, sinking back under her skin, and she felt its departure like a door closing on a warm room. "Are you hurt?"

"Me? No. Are *you*?" The girl's eyes were doing something complicated — fear and awe wrestling for the same real estate. "They knew your name. They called you—"

"Don't." Sharper than she meant. Casper softened it with a breath. "Later. Maybe. Right now I need to think, and thinking is hard enough without—" She gestured vaguely at her own past.

But Juniper wasn't listening anymore. She was staring at the counter. At the egg.

"Casper. *Casper.* It's moving."

The lantern-egg — dull glass all evening, the ember inside barely a spark — had begun to glow. Not steadily. In pulses. Once per heartbeat.

*Her* heartbeat.

"Oh no," Casper said, with the particular dread of a woman recognizing a signature on a contract. "No, no. It's been doing that since the riders came. Since I—" She looked at her own palm. "Since I used the light. Oh, you absolute *egg*, don't you dare—"

A hairline crack opened along the shell with a sound like ice on a pond in spring.

Juniper made a noise halfway between delight and horror. "It's hatching! It's hatching *now*? Here?" She spun in a circle, taking in the shattered door, the dust of two dead horrors, the night full of extinguished stars beyond the windows. "It picked the worst possible—"

Another crack. A third. Then a piece of shell fell away like a petal, and through the gap peered an eye.

It was a good eye. Enormous, liquid-dark, ringed in gold, and it fixed on Casper Thackeray with the unwavering attention of a creature that has decided something important and will not be taking questions.

"What's it doing?" Juniper whispered.

"It's looking at me."

"Why is it looking at *you*? I carried it three hundred miles! I slept in ditches! I fought off a *weasel!*"

"I don't know!" Which was a lie, and they both knew it, because they both knew about the pulse matching her pulse, the ember answering her light. Dragon-fire recognized priest-fire. Some old kinship, older than either of them. "Maybe it likes my shop."

The egg split open like a flower given permission, and out of it climbed the smallest dragon Casper had ever seen or imagined. It was moth-sized — genuinely moth-sized, its wings folded damp and trembling along its back, its body no longer than her thumb. It was the color of the space between stars, shot through with veins of gold, and it stood on the counter on wobbly legs, blinked its enormous eye at her, and sneezed a puff of sparks that singed a hole in a love-charm.

"Charming," Casper said.

The dragon took two steps, teetered, and launched itself at her face.

She had a half-second to consider dodging. She didn't, partly because there wasn't time and mostly because some traitorous instinct said *catch it*, and so she caught it — cupped it against her chest with both hands, feeling its heartbeat hammering against hers, fast-fast-slow, fast-fast-slow, until gradually the two rhythms found each other and settled into one.

The warmth spread up her arms. Not burning. *Filling.* Like drinking hot tea on a cold day, if the tea were alive and loved you.

"Oh," Juniper breathed. "Oh, it's imprinting. On *you*. That's — the keepers always said the fire chooses, but I thought — I carried it, I *fed* it—" Her voice cracked, and she turned away quickly, scrubbing at her eyes with her sleeve. "Sorry. Sorry, that's stupid, it doesn't matter who it—"

"Juniper." Casper shifted the dragon to one arm — it immediately burrowed into her elbow like a cat, radiating smugness — and reached out with her free hand. She hesitated, then rested it on the girl's shoulder. "It matters. Of course it matters. But listen to me." She waited until Juniper looked up. "This isn't rejection. This is a job offer. Whatever your people were, whatever the egg was for — it seems the fire wants us both. You carried it here. I'll carry it forward. That's not a slight. That's a relay."

Juniper sniffed. "You're very good at speeches for someone who pretends to sell fake charms."

"I practice in the mirror." Casper looked down at the dragon. It yawned, showing a mouth full of needle teeth and a throat that glowed like a banked furnace. "Hello, then. What do I call you?"

The dragon bit her thumb. Gently, as far as dragons go, which is to say it broke the skin.

"Wonderful. We'll workshop it."

* * *

They worked by candlelight, because the lamps kept guttering whenever the dragon flew past — it had discovered flight and was drunk on it, looping drunken figure-eights around the rafters, shedding sparks like a festival pinwheel. Juniper chased it with a soup ladle. Casper sat amid the ruins of her life and took inventory.

Two riders dead. Two names spoken aloud in her shop — hers, and her god's title. A door off its hinges that every neighbor on Tanner's Row had certainly heard break. A hatchling dragon doing laps of her ceiling like the world's smallest, most incendiary comet.

And somewhere out there, whoever sent the riders now knew exactly where she was.

"They'll send more," she said.

Juniper stopped chasing. The dragon landed on her head, which she bore with the resignation of the long-suffering. "More riders?"

"Mm. Those two were scouts. Or collectors. Whatever they collect." Casper turned her palm up and studied it. The skin was unmarked, but she could still feel the words in it, the way a hand remembers a rope. "Twenty years, I told myself the price of hiding was worth it. Keep the head down, sell the fake charms, stay small, and the world leaves you alone. And it worked. It *worked*, Juniper. Do you know how rare that is? A person with something dangerous inside them, living a whole quiet life, untouched?" She laughed, and it came out wrong. "Except it didn't work, did it. Because the world didn't leave me alone. The world just saved me for later. All that hiding bought me nothing but interest on the debt."

The dragon fluttered down from Juniper's head and landed on Casper's knee. It looked up at her with that gold-ringed eye, and it did not blink, and it did not look away, and there was something in its gaze that she recognized from a lifetime ago — from a shrine, from a god who had also watched her without blinking, waiting for her to stop being afraid.

"You're judging me," she told it. "You're the size of a moth and you're judging me."

It chirped. It sounded agreeable. It judged her anyway.

"Casper." Juniper had gone still, hugging the empty eggshell to her chest like a broken bowl. "The riders. What they said. About you running." Her voice was very careful, the way voices get when someone has decided to be brave. "I don't need to know tonight. But whatever it is — whatever you did, or didn't do — I ran too. From the borderlands. From the riders. I left people behind because I was carrying this and I couldn't fight and I couldn't save anyone, I could only *run*, and I've hated myself for six days straight." She lifted her chin. "So. Whatever it is. I'm not going to be the one who throws stones."

Casper looked at her for a long moment. Sixteen years old, sanded-down mind, three hundred miles in her shoes, offering absolution to a stranger because she knew how much it cost to go without.

"The capital," Casper said at last. "You're going to Corvenna. That's why you came to a charm-seller — you wanted protection for the road."

"I wanted *anything*," Juniper admitted. "You were the fourth shop I tried. The others were closed. Or empty. Or—" She swallowed. "Or the people in them had forgotten how shops worked."

The dragon kneaded Casper's knee with tiny talons, punctuating the sentence.

"And now you've seen what I am," Casper said. "Seen it burn things to dust. Seen it kill. And you're still sitting in my ruined shop asking me to walk you into the city where the thing that ate my god wears a crown."

Juniper blinked. "Your *what*?"

Ah. Well. That was going to come out eventually.

Casper rose, joints cracking, dragon riding her forearm like a falconer's perch, and looked out through the doorless doorway at the starless sky. Somewhere south, past roads that were forgetting themselves, Corvenna glittered with stolen light. Somewhere in that glitter, a king sat on a throne with an ancient hunger coiled behind his eyes, wearing her god's death like a hat.

Twenty years of invisibility, ended in one night. She ought to feel naked. Instead — and this was the shameful part, the part she'd examine later, or never — she felt *lighter*. Terrified and lighter, the way you feel stepping off a ledge you've been standing on the edge of for two decades.

"Pack light," she said. "We leave before dawn, while the soldiers are still sweeping the east quarter. And Juniper?"

"Yeah?"

"If anything else comes through that door tonight, stand behind me. I'm done pretending the counter helps."

From the rafters, where it had crept back up to dive-bomb the candles again, the little dragon sneezed sparks in what Casper chose to interpret as agreement.

Outside, far off across the dark rooftops of Grayhollow, something screamed — long, tearing, many-throated. More riders. More than two. And they were calling her name.
