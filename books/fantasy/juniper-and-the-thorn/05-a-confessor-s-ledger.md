## 5. A Confessor's Ledger

The Archive of Saint Vellum had been, in Casper's girlhood, a place of hushed reverence: forty thousand volumes under vaulted ceilings, the air thick with beeswax and old ink, confessors moving between the shelves like patient ghosts. Now it was a place where the roof leaked into a bucket that someone had labeled, with grim municipal humor, *THE SKY*.

She found Brother Hale exactly where she'd expected to find him—not among the books, but beneath them, sprawled in a reading alcove on the third floor with an empty bottle of plum brandy cradled against his chest like an infant he was too tired to put down.

He looked terrible. He had always looked terrible, even twenty years ago, when he'd been the youngest confessor ever assigned to the Grayhollow chapter—red-nosed, rumpled, with the permanent expression of a man who has read too many endings. Age had only deepened the craft of it.

"Hale," Casper said quietly.

One eye opened. It took a long moment to focus, and when it did, it went wide and sober all at once, which for Hale constituted a minor miracle.

"Thackeray," he breathed. "Veth's teeth. They said you were dead."

"They say that about everyone who leaves the order."

"No, I mean *dead* dead. There was a ledger entry." He sat up with the care of a man negotiating with his own spine. "Chapter Nine, Dispositions. 'Sister Casper Thackeray, lost to apostasy, presumed deceased.' I wrote it myself. I gave you a very dignified death. You should be flattered."

"I'm flattered."

"You're not. You have your arms crossed. That's your disapproving posture." He squinted at her, and something behind the drunkenness sharpened—the old archivist, the man who could find any lie in any text because he'd catalogued most of them personally. "Twenty years, Casper. Why now? Why come out of your nice quiet grave?"

Casper glanced down the aisle. The Archive was nearly empty these days; the city outside still rang with panic from the night the stars died, soldiers at every corner, priests of the new civic liturgy burning incense over nothing. But empty wasn't safe. Nothing was safe anymore.

"Can we talk somewhere without ears?" she asked.

"Everywhere has ears now." But he hauled himself up, tucked the bottle into his robe like a scholar shelving a misfiled volume, and led her up a narrow stair to the map room—a domed chamber where the great celestial charts hung in rotting splendor. The star maps. Someone had taken charcoal to them, crossing out constellation after constellation, and beside each crossed-out star, in Hale's cramped hand, was written a date and a single word: *gone*.

Casper stopped in the doorway. "How many?"

"Forty-one." Hale's voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man holding something heavy by a fraying rope. "As of this morning. Forty-one stars, extinguished in nine days. And before you ask—no, it isn't clouds, it isn't eclipse, it isn't the wards failing. The wards failing is a symptom, not the sickness. I've been drinking about it for a week and I've reached some conclusions I would give a great deal to be wrong about."

"Tell me anyway."

He walked to the nearest chart and touched one of the blacked-out circles almost tenderly, the way you'd touch a gravestone.

"When I was a novice," he said, "old Confessor Merrin told me a thing he swore me to secrecy over, and I have kept that secret for thirty years, mostly by drowning it. He said the order didn't begin as a church. It began as a *watch*. Before Veth was a god people prayed to, he was a god people *guarded*—or rather, they guarded what was inside him. Do you know what a god actually is, Casper? Underneath the hymns?"

"A very large story," Casper said slowly. "A memory so old and so loved it became... load-bearing."

Hale blinked at her. "That's better than Merrin managed, and he had a decade to work on it. Yes. A god is the world's remembering of itself, given a face. And Veth was the oldest face we had. The god of silence, of things kept, of memory held safe in the dark. Every secret ever confessed to our order—he held them. Not recorded them. *Held* them. That was the whole theology, underneath everything: we didn't forgive sins, we carried them so no one else had to."

"And something ate him."

"Something ate him." Hale lowered himself onto a stool, and for once his hands were steady. "Merrin called it the Thorn. He said it was older than Veth—older than naming. A hunger that doesn't eat flesh or light. It eats *remembering*. It doesn't kill a thing; it makes the world forget the thing was ever there, and then the thing comes apart at the seams, because nothing can hold its shape without being remembered. And it couldn't take Veth from outside. So it did it from inside."

"Inside what?"

"Inside a king." Hale met her eyes. "Thirty years ago, a young prince came to our chapter house in Corvenna for confession. Aldric. He was frightened of something—something he'd found, or that had found him. Merrin heard his confession. And Merrin made a choice—I don't know if it was cowardice or mercy or both—but he didn't hold what the boy confessed. He let it go. He let it *in*. And the Thorn has been growing inside Aldric ever since, slow as ivy, eating the god from within. Veth didn't die, Casper. Gods don't die while anyone remembers them. He was *swallowed*. Dissolved. Every prayer since has been falling into that thing's mouth."

The room seemed to tilt. Twenty years of silence, of hollow charms and averted eyes, of praying into a dark that never answered—and here was the answer, ugly and enormous, sitting in a leaky archive drinking plum brandy.

"The stars," she said. Her voice came out strange. "The stars going out—"

"Each one is a memory the world loses forever." Hale gestured at the defaced charts. "I worked it out from the dates. Cross-reference them with what's been happening—the village of Marrowdale, three days after the second star died. Nobody there remembers their own founding. The well is dry because nobody remembers where the water was. The fourth star, the fifth—whole songs gone from every throat that ever sang them. The Thorn is finishing its meal, Casper. It ate the god. Now it's eating the pantry."

Casper turned away, gripping the edge of a table, and let herself feel it—not the tidy grief of the last twenty years but the raw, furious kind, the kind with edges. She had spent two decades believing she had abandoned a dead god. That her silence had been mourning. That her guilt was a stone she carried for a grave.

It wasn't a grave. It was a war that had never ended, and she had spent it hiding in a shop selling charms that barely worked.

"There's more," Hale said gently. "There's always more; that's the trouble with archives. The order knew the Thorn might finish someday. We kept contingencies. One of them walked out of our keeping twenty years ago wearing your face."

She looked back at him. "What are you talking about?"

"Merrin chose you, Casper. Of all of us. He trained you differently and never said why—you must have felt it, the way your magic ran deeper than the others', the way he pushed you toward the old rites nobody used. You were the contingency. The blade kept sharp in case the god needed cutting free." He smiled, and it was a broken, fond, terrible smile. "And then the day the Thorn first struck, you ran. I don't blame you. Merrin never blamed you. Fear is honest; courage is just fear that showed up anyway. But the blade left the armory, and the war went on without it."

Every word landed like a stone dropped into still water. She thought of the drowned shrine, of the memories she kept locked behind her own private wards, of the moment she had fled and never named aloud, not even to herself.

"The riders," she said instead, because the other road was too wide to walk yet. "Gray riders. Faceless. They came for a girl in my shop—a girl carrying dragon-fire, the last ember of it. What are they?"

"The Thorn's hands. Made of solidified forgetting—they unmake what they touch. If it wants the ember badly enough to spend riders on it..." Hale leaned forward, suddenly urgent, the drunkard shell cracking open entirely. "Casper. Dragon-fire is the one fire that burns without consuming. It's the only flame that could sever the Thorn from its host without killing the king—or the world he's woven through. The keepers bred dragons for it, back when there were keepers. If a girl walked into your shop carrying the last living ember, then either the gods are kind—"

"—or the Thorn is afraid," Casper finished.

They looked at each other across the ruined star charts, two survivors of an order that had forgotten itself, and something settled in Casper's chest that hadn't settled in twenty years. Not peace. Purpose has more edges than peace. But adjacent.

"Grief is a debt," Hale said quietly. "You've been paying interest on yours for twenty years. Time to pay the principal."

Casper almost laughed. "You've gotten eloquent in your cups."

"I've always been eloquent. The cups just gave me an excuse not to use it." He heaved himself upright, swayed, steadied himself on the chart table, and looked at the crossed-out stars with an expression she recognized from mirrors: a man deciding to stop running. "The rite you'd need—the Unbinding—it's in the sealed collection. Sub-vault three. I'm the only one left with the key, and my hands shake too much for fine work, but my memory's still sound, drunk as it looks. Give me until morning and I'll have the whole rite laid out for you. Every word, every cost."

"And the cost will be terrible," Casper said. It wasn't a question.

"It's always terrible. That's how you know it matters." He fished the bottle from his robe, considered it, and set it down unopened on the table—a small surrender, and the largest she'd ever seen him make. "Go home to your girl and your egg, Thackeray. Sleep, if sleeping's still a thing we do. Come back at dawn."

Casper paused at the door, looking back at him standing alone among the murdered constellations, small and rumpled and suddenly, unmistakably, a confessor again.

"Hale. The ledger entry. 'Lost to apostasy, presumed deceased.'"

"What of it?"

"Amend it," she said. "*Returned.*"

His grin followed her down the stairs like a lamp.

* * *

She was three streets from the Archive when she saw the posters going up on the temple doors, fresh paste gleaming in the lamplight, soldiers smoothing the corners with gauntleted hands. She stopped. She read. The words seemed to rearrange themselves as she read them, refusing to cohere, and she had to read them twice:

*By decree of His Majesty King Aldric, Beloved of the Realm: Let it be known that the realm's troubles arise from hoarded magic. All practitioners of the old arts shall present themselves for the king's cleansing, upon pain of—*

The rest blurred. Not metaphorically. The letters actually slid, like water finding its level, and Casper understood with a cold drop in her stomach that she was looking at the Thorn's handwriting—decree as digestion, law as forgetting. Present yourself for cleansing meant *come be eaten*. The king wasn't hunting mages out of fear.

He was setting the table.

And somewhere in the palace above the city, behind eyes that had once belonged to a frightened young prince, the Thorn had already tasted a god. It knew what priest-magic felt like. It had felt hers burn two riders to ash in her own shop.

Casper pulled her hood up and moved, unhurried, the way invisible people move, and thought: *he knows I exist now.*

Behind her, high in the Archive's dome, a man with shaking hands was opening a sub-vault that hadn't been opened in thirty years, and above the rooftops of Grayhollow, the sky sat forty-one stars short of true, and the forty-second was beginning, very faintly, to flicker.
