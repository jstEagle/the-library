## 6. The Road That Forgets Itself

The road out of Grayhollow smelled like wet wool and old rain, and Juniper decided within the first mile that she hated it.

Not the smell—she'd slept in worse. She hated the way the road *behaved*. It should have gone somewhere. Roads were supposed to be honest things: you put one foot in front of the other and they carried you toward a place with a name. But this road kept doing something wrong at the edges of her eyes, something like a river forgetting which way was downhill. Twice she looked back at Grayhollow's walls and could not have said how far they'd walked. Once she looked forward and the road seemed to have been *expecting* them, curving up to meet her feet like a dog that had learned to beg.

"You're staring at it," Casper said. She walked like a woman who had spent twenty years practicing how to be unremarkable, hood up, hands in pockets, somehow managing to take up less space than a person legally could. "Staring makes it worse."

"Does it?"

"It makes everything worse. Staring, hoping, singing, asking questions." Casper ticked these off on her fingers. "I had a whole list. I'll write it down for you."

"You're joking."

"I'm mostly joking. The list is real, though."

Behind them, riding in the crook of Casper's arm inside its lantern like a coal that had opinions, the dragon sneezed. A thread of smoke curled up through the glass and hung there, smelling faintly of cinnamon and thunderstorms. Juniper had named him Wick on their second night together, over Casper's objections that naming things made you responsible for them, and Juniper's counter-objection that she had been responsible for him since before he hatched, so the name changed nothing except how nice it felt to say it.

"Wick," she said now, softly, and felt the ember answer—a small warm pulse against her ribs, like a second heartbeat that had agreed to keep time with hers. That was the thing nobody had warned her about, back when there had been anybodys to do the warning: carrying the last fire in the world wasn't like carrying a torch. It was like carrying someone. He dreamed when she dreamed. He flared when she was afraid. And lately, whenever they passed one of the empty places, he pressed against the lantern glass and *whined*, low and terrible, like a dog outside a door where someone was crying.

They passed the first empty village by midmorning.

It wasn't ruined. That was the worst part. Ruins made sense; ruins told you what had happened. This village stood whole—thatched roofs, a well with a bucket still on its rope, laundry strung between two houses, stiff with frost and forgotten. A cradle sat under one window. A hoe leaned against a fence as if its owner meant to come back after lunch.

Nobody came back after lunch. Nobody came back after anything. The doors stood open on cold hearths, and when Juniper called out—she couldn't help it, some animal part of her insisted on trying—the word *hello* fell into the street and lay there like a dropped coin.

"No names," Casper murmured. "Don't say your name here. Don't say his." She nodded at the lantern. "Names are handles. Things without memories of their own will grab onto whatever handles they can find."

"What things?"

Casper didn't answer, which was an answer. Juniper had learned that about her in three days of travel: the charm-seller answered maybe half of all questions out loud, and the other half got answered later, usually at night, usually right when Juniper had stopped expecting it, delivered flat and terrible while Casper stared into the fire like it owed her money.

They filled their waterskins at the well. Brother Hale, walking behind them with the rolling gait of a man whose knees had filed formal complaints years ago, read the village signpost and went pale under his wine-flushed cheeks.

"This is Harrowmere," he said. "Or—it was. The sign says—" He squinted. "The sign says nothing. There's paint where letters used to be. Just... smooth wood."

"The Thorn doesn't burn villages," Casper said quietly. "It *edits* them."

Juniper touched the signpost. Under her fingers the wood was warm, which it had no business being in the frost, and for half a heartbeat she thought she heard something underneath the silence—not sound exactly, more like the shape of a sound, the dent a bell leaves in the air after it stops ringing. Someone had rung that bell every morning for two hundred years. Now the ringing was gone and only the dent remained.

Wick whined against the glass.

* * *

They found the child four miles on, at a crossroads shrine so old the god-statue on it had weathered faceless even before the stars went out—which, Hale observed darkly, was either very good luck or extremely bad timing.

She was perhaps seven, sitting on the shrine steps with her knees drawn up, wearing a coat too big for her and an expression too old for her face. When she saw them she didn't run. That was the first wrong thing. Children who had been alone long enough stopped running toward people; they just watched, the way birds watch a scarecrow, deciding.

"Hallo," Juniper said, keeping her voice light, keeping her hands visible. Old habit from the borderlands, where you showed strangers your palms so they'd know you weren't holding steel. "Are you lost?"

"I don't know," the girl said. It wasn't a child's sulk. It was a genuine, considered answer, delivered with the weary honesty of someone reporting the weather. "I was going somewhere. I think my mam is there. Or she was. I can't remember her face." She frowned, working at it like a knot. "I can't remember if I can't remember. Does that make sense?"

Juniper's chest did something painful. "What's your name?"

The girl opened her mouth. Closed it. Her chin started to wobble, and she fought it down with a ferocity that broke Juniper's heart in a completely different way. "I almost had it this morning," she whispered. "It was right *there*."

Behind Juniper, Wick flared so hot the lantern glass chimed.

Later—much later, when she understood what she was—Juniper would learn that the ember had never once acted without her leave. But that afternoon on the shrine steps, it didn't feel like a decision. It felt like a door blowing open in a storm. Heat poured up her arm, through her palm, out of her fingers before she knew she'd reached for the girl—and the fire that left her wasn't flame. It was *warmth*, golden and specific, and it moved through the space between them like a memory looking for its owner.

The girl gasped.

"Mam sings it wrong," she blurted, laughing and crying at once. "She always sings it wrong, she forgets the middle verse, she goes 'la-la' and pats my head like I won't notice—" The words tumbled out of her, a lullaby spilling free, and she sang six bars of it, off-key, perfect, *hers*. "That's mine. That's my mam's song. I have a mam. I have—" Her face crumpled. "I don't remember where."

"East," Hale said gently. "There's a town east. Millbrook. Or it was." He crouched to the girl's level, and Juniper saw him do something she hadn't seen him do sober: press his old signet ring into her small hand. "You show them that. Someone there will know it. Someone has to."

The girl ran east. Juniper watched until the road took her, and then she became aware of two things at once.

The first was that Casper was staring at her with an expression Juniper had no name for—something between awe and alarm, the way you might look at a match struck in a powder store.

The second was the cold.

It hit her all at once, a hollowness spreading out from her sternum, and she staggered. The lantern's glow had dimmed. Not dimmed like a lamp turned down—dimmed like a star seen through smoke. Where Wick had been a bright coal he was now a dull orange pebble, and the pulse she felt against her ribs had gone thin and fluttery.

"Juniper." Casper caught her elbow. "How much did that cost?"

"I don't—" Juniper blinked. The world swayed. "It didn't cost anything. It's just fire. Fire's just—"

"Nothing is just fire." Casper's grip tightened. "Especially not the last of it. Sit down before you fall down, and then tell me honestly: what did it feel like?"

Juniper sat on the shrine steps, in the exact spot the girl had warmed, and tried to find words for it. "Like... giving blood, but from somewhere else. Like I paid with a coin I didn't know I had in my pocket." She looked at the lantern. Wick pressed against the glass toward her, and his little flame bent toward her hand the way flowers bend toward sun, and she felt him *asking*—not for himself, she realized. Asking on behalf of the world. Every empty house they passed, every blank signpost, every person wandering a road that had eaten their names: he felt all of it, all the time, and he wanted to fix it, and he would spend himself down to nothing doing it if she let him.

"He's going to want to do that again," Juniper said quietly. "At the next village. And the next. He's going to want to give everyone back their mothers' songs."

"Yes," Casper said.

"And each time costs us."

"Yes."

"So we ration miracles." Juniper laughed, and it came out bitterer than she intended. "I fled the borderlands with the last fire in the world, and my big plan was *don't get caught*, and now I have to decide who remembers their own children."

Casper sat down beside her. For a long moment neither of them spoke. Then, unexpectedly, the charm-seller reached over and straightened Juniper's collar—a small, fussy, motherly gesture that looked absurd coming from a woman built entirely out of locked doors.

"My rule used to be *never be noticed*," Casper said. "Twenty years. Never heal anyone who might talk. Never bless anything bigger than a wart. You know why?" She didn't wait for an answer. "Because every miracle spends something, and I told myself the something was me, and I was a coward about paying." She looked at the lantern, at the dimmed ember, at Juniper. "You're braver than me at sixteen. But bravery without arithmetic gets people killed. So we do the arithmetic. Together. Small fires for the worst hurts. We can't relight the world, but we can leave lit candles behind us, and maybe—" her jaw tightened— "maybe enough candles make a road."

"A road that remembers itself," Juniper said.

"That would be a first."

Hale, who had been pretending to study the faceless statue, cleared his throat. "There's a village ahead. Ashford, if the maps in my head still work, which is a wager I'd no longer take." He hesitated. "There'll be people. Hungry ones, confused ones. You can't fix them all."

"I know," Juniper said.

"You'll want to anyway."

"I know *that* too."

* * *

Ashford had lost less than Harrowmere and it was somehow worse for it. The people were still there—but they were *thin*, the way a word gets thin when you repeat it too many times without meaning it. They remembered how to farm and bake and bury their dead. They remembered each other's faces, mostly. What they'd lost was smaller and stranger: the blacksmith couldn't recall why he'd chosen his trade. The innkeeper had a daughter she loved fiercely and could not remember birthing. An old man sat by the well every day trying to reconstruct his wife's name from fragments—*it started soft, it ended like a bird taking off*—and getting it wrong in a new way each evening, and apologizing to her each morning.

"They're calling it the Quiet," the innkeeper told them, setting down bowls of stew Juniper suspected the woman couldn't quite afford. "Started with the stars. Folk say the king's done something wicked in Corvenna. Folk say a lot of things." Her eyes flicked to the lantern at Juniper's hip, and lingered, hungry. "Folk say some travelers carry remedies."

"Don't," Casper said, mild as milk.

"I wasn't asking for me."

"You were asking for everyone, which is worse."

But that night, in the inn's common room, Juniper did the arithmetic Casper had taught her, and it came out ugly. Forty people. One ember, already guttering. She lay awake listening to Wick's pulse against her ribs—slower tonight, she was nearly sure, though she kept telling herself she was imagining it—and did the math of a god: how much fire does it take to give a blacksmith back his reason? How much for a name? Could she spend him a thimble at a time and stretch one dragon across a dying country, or was every candle she lit a page torn out of the only book left?

Wick stirred. Through the lantern glass, his tiny eye found hers, and she would have sworn on anything holy and several things that weren't that he winked.

*Greedy*, she thought at him. *You'd give it all away tomorrow.*

He flared, just slightly. Warm. Unrepentant.

"Yeah," she whispered. "Me too."

* * *

They left Ashford at dawn, and the road forgot itself again.

This time Juniper felt it happen. They crested a hill with the village behind them and the world ahead, and halfway down the far slope the road simply... lost confidence. It forked where no fork had been. The ruts pointed three directions at once. A milestone stood at the crossing, and Hale wiped the moss from it with his sleeve and went quiet in a way that made Juniper's stomach drop.

"Well?" Casper said.

"There's no inscription," Hale said. "There's a place where an inscription should be. And—" he turned the stone toward the light, and his voice went thin— "there's a chisel mark. Fresh. Like someone *removed* the words recently. Deliberately."

Wind moved along the road. Somewhere ahead, past the fork, past the hills, Juniper heard—or thought she heard—the faintest sound, carried on air that had gone suddenly cold: hoofbeats. Many of them. Moving in unison, with none of the clatter real hooves make, as if the horses had forgotten how to be loud.

Casper's hand closed around her shoulder. "Off the road," the charm-seller breathed. "Now."

In the lantern, Wick's flame stood perfectly straight and burned white.
