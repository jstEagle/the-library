## 7. Salt and Old Vows

The road to the coast had forgotten it was a road.

Casper noticed it first as a softness underfoot—cart ruts that trailed off into grass mid-stride, as though the earth itself had lost interest halfway through the thought. Milestones stood like shy children, their inscriptions worn to blank ovals of stone. Once, an hour past noon, they passed a signpost pointing confidently in four directions at once.

"That one's just wrong," Juniper said, squinting at it. "Even before all this, that would have been wrong."

"Or it's honest now," Casper said. "Most signs only pretend to know where you're going."

The lantern swung from Juniper's hand, and inside it the little dragon—no bigger than a moth, wings like scraps of burnt paper—dozed in a spiral around the ember's glow. Casper still hadn't settled on a name for it. She'd offered six. The dragon had ignored five and bitten her finger over the sixth, which she chose to interpret as a review rather than a preference.

They smelled the sea before they saw it: salt and rot and something older underneath, the smell of a thing that had been drowning for a very long time.

The shrine sat in a tidal basin a half-mile from the gray lip of the water—a ring of standing stones, half-swallowed by brine marsh, each one carved with the closed-eye sigil of Veth. At low tide the stones emerged like drowned men coming up for air. At high tide, Casper knew without knowing how she knew, the water covered them entirely, and the god's symbol slept beneath the sea with its eyes shut.

She had never been here before. She was certain of that.

And yet her feet found the path between the stones without hesitation, skirting the deep silt pools exactly, turning left at the third stone as if following instructions whispered years ago.

"Casper?" Juniper's voice came small behind her. "You've gone strange."

"I'm always strange."

"You've gone *stranger*. You're walking like someone who lives here."

Casper stopped. The tide was out; the basin lay open, glistening, ribbed with sand and the pale husks of crabs. In the center of the stone circle, a shallow pool of seawater had gathered in a depression worn smooth as a bowl—an offering basin, some part of her supplied, cut for that purpose, filled twice daily by the faithful tide.

Her palm ached. Not the old ache of buried magic, which she'd lived with so long it had become a kind of companion, but a new ache, sharp and specific, like a splinter working toward the surface.

"I need a moment," she said. "Alone."

Juniper looked at her the way sixteen-year-olds look at adults who are clearly lying about being fine—which is to say, with devastating accuracy. But she only nodded and retreated to the circle's edge, sitting down on a fallen stone with the lantern in her lap, close enough to hear if Casper called out. The girl had a gift for that, Casper was learning. For giving space without giving up on you.

Casper walked to the basin alone.

* * *

The water in the bowl was clear as glass and cold as confession.

She knelt—her knees finding the worn grooves in the stone, grooves shaped, she realized with a lurch, by other kneeling people across centuries—and looked down into the pool, and the pool looked back.

Not with her reflection.

With memory.

It came up through her like floodwater through a cellar, twenty years of dammed things breaking all at once, and Casper had time to think, absurdly, *I built that wall so well*—and then she was no longer in the tidal basin at all.

* * *

She was young. She was thirty and strong and certain, and she stood in the great chapel of Corvenna in her battle-priest's grays, the closed eye stitched over her heart, and King Aldric was kneeling at the altar, and he was screaming.

No—that wasn't right. He was trying to scream. What came out of him was quieter than screaming, worse than screaming: a sound like a page being torn out of a book, over and over, the sound of a man forgetting his own daughter's face while his daughter held his hand and begged him to say her name.

And Veth—

Veth was there. Of course Veth was there. Not as men imagined gods, vast and thunderous, but the way he actually was: quiet, present, a pressure like the moment before snowfall. Casper felt him all through the chapel, all through herself, pouring through her consecrated bones like cold clean water. He was *afraid*. Gods could be afraid. She hadn't known that until that night. He was afraid, and he was reaching for her—not with words, because Veth had never used words, but with meaning, pressing it into her the way he always did:

*My blade. My chosen. Stand.*

The Thorn came out of the king's mouth.

That was how she remembered it now, with the terrible clarity of recovered truth: a darkness unfolding from between Aldric's lips like smoke learning to have shape, like a black flower blooming in reverse, petals of un-light spreading wide. It had no eyes and it looked at her anyway. It had no voice and it said her name anyway—not aloud, not in sound, but in the space where sound should be, and what it said was:

*I know what you fear. I am patient. I can wait inside your fear forever.*

And Casper Thackeray—chosen blade of the silent god, sworn sword of the closed eye, the woman who had trained for twenty years for exactly this moment—

ran.

Not strategically. Not to rally the order, not to guard the queen, not to carry warning to the border temples. She ran the way animals run, blind and graceless, shoving past Brother Hale in the chapel door, out into the Corvenna night, through streets that blurred with tears she didn't remember shedding until later, until the chapel was far behind her and Veth's presence tore away from her back like skin.

He didn't call after her.

That was the worst of it. That was the wound she'd spent two decades salting: he didn't call after her, because Veth understood free will better than any god ever had, and he let her choose, and she chose the dark street and the running and the small surviving life, and behind her the light went out of the world's largest temple one candle at a time.

The last thing she felt of him, before she walled her magic away and buried the memory deeper than the wall, was not anger.

It was forgiveness.

He forgave her while she abandoned him. He forgave her *in advance*, the way you love someone, and that forgiveness had been heavier than any punishment could ever be, and she had carried it for twenty years without letting herself look at it even once.

* * *

Casper came back to herself on her hands and knees in the salt mud, gasping, her face wet, the tide pool in the offering bowl rippling gently as though pleased with itself.

"Casper!" Juniper was beside her instantly, lantern raised, the little dragon awake and buzzing against the glass like an angry bee. "What happened? Was it the riders? I didn't hear—"

"Memory," Casper rasped. Her throat was raw. Had she been crying out? "The shrine keeps them. Or—" she laughed, and it came out cracked and awful, "—or it kept mine. All these years. Waiting for me to come kneel in the right groove like a good little priestess."

Juniper crouched next to her, careful of the mud, and did not touch her, and did not leave. "Was it bad?"

"It was true." Casper sat back on her heels. Salt stung the scrapes on her palms. "There's a difference."

She told her. Not all of it—she couldn't, not yet, some parts were still too hot to hold—but the shape of it. The chapel. The king. The Thorn unfolding. And the running.

When she finished, Juniper was quiet for a long moment. Out past the stones, the sea made its slow gray argument with the shore.

"You were scared," Juniper said finally.

"I was a coward."

"Those aren't the same thing." The girl's chin came up, stubborn. "I ran too. From the riders, from the borderlands, from everything. Does that make me a coward?"

"You're sixteen, and you carried the last fire in the world through the dark for days. That makes you brave."

"And you were one person facing a thing that eats gods, and you carried yourself through the dark for twenty years. Maybe that makes you brave too. Maybe both things are true." Juniper shrugged, awkward and fierce. "Tam says—said—bravery isn't not-running. It's what you do after."

Casper looked at her. The ember-light caught the girl's face and turned it gold at the edges, and Casper felt the old familiar grief shift inside her, the way a broken bone shifts when the weather changes—from dead weight into something that could become a lever.

"He forgave me," she said quietly. "At the end. While I was still running. He forgave me, and I've spent twenty years refusing to accept it, because accepting it would mean admitting there was something to forgive."

"Is that so terrible?"

"It's the most human thing I've ever done," Casper said. "That's what's terrible about it."

* * *

The tide began to come in while they still sat among the stones, and they watched the water climb the god's carvings one closed eye at a time, patient as everything else in this broken country.

Casper turned her palm up. Beneath the skin, her magic stirred—not the panicked wildfire of the night the wards fell, but something steadier. A banked thing. A blade remembering it had once been sharpened.

"I keep waiting," she said, mostly to herself, "to feel forgiven. Like it should arrive someday, the way rain arrives. And then this morning it occurred to me, kneeling in the mud like a fool, that maybe it already arrived. Twenty years ago. And I just—" her hand closed, "—never opened the door."

"So open it," Juniper said simply.

"It's not that simple."

"Why not?"

Because self-forgiveness was a country she had no map for. Because guilt had been the load-bearing wall of her whole hidden life, and she didn't know what would stand if she took it out. Because Veth was gone—or eaten, or dissolved, or whatever horror Hale's ledger implied—and there was no one left to release her, and releasing herself felt like theft.

But the Thorn wore a king's crown. The stars went out one by one, and every one of them was a memory dying. And somewhere ahead, in a palace grown over with black brambles, the thing she had run from was waiting, patient as it had promised to be, still speaking in the space where sound should be.

Casper opened her hand.

"I can't forgive myself yet," she told the girl honestly. "Maybe when it's done. Maybe never. But I can stop hiding from what I am." She looked down at her palm, at the faint silver light gathering there like dawn under skin. "I was his blade. I set the blade down. I'm picking it up again."

Juniper smiled—the first full smile Casper had seen from her since the ford, since Tam, since the funeral of small things they'd performed with river water and silence. "Good," she said. "Because I can't do this alone, and the dragon likes you better than me, which is frankly insulting."

The dragon, hearing itself discussed, buzzed imperiously against the lantern glass.

They rose. Behind them the sea swallowed the last of the standing stones, and the closed eyes of Veth went under without protest, keeping their long watch beneath the water. Casper bowed to them—low, formal, the bow of a soldier to a fallen commander—and did not weep, and counted that as progress.

They climbed out of the basin onto the coast road, and the wind came off the water carrying salt and the smell of morning.

It was Juniper who saw it first, and her grip on Casper's sleeve said everything before her voice did.

Down the coast road, perhaps a mile distant, a line of figures had appeared where no figures had been. They did not walk so much as arrive, over and over, like gaps in the world moving. Gray. Faceless. And they were not alone—at their head rode something larger, something that cast a shadow the wrong color, and it had stopped, and it was looking directly at them.

"They found us," Juniper whispered.

"No," Casper said, and felt the blade in her soul come fully out of its sheath for the first time in twenty years. "We found each other. There's a difference."

She rolled up her sleeves.

"Stay behind me this time, Juniper. Both of you." The little dragon flared bright as a struck match. "I'm done running."
