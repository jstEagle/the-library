## 8. The Mercenary Company of Last Resort

The caravan found them on the third morning out of Grayhollow, or perhaps they found the caravan—it was hard to say who was doing the finding, since the caravan itself seemed only half-sure it existed.

Juniper saw it first: a straggle of wagons and mules and one extremely offended ox coming up the road through the gray mist, flanked by a dozen armed figures in mismatched armor. The dragon—Ember, though she was still small enough to ride in Juniper's hood and mostly used it as a napping hammock—lifted her head and chirped a warning.

"They're not riders," Juniper said. "Riders don't argue about breakfast."

And indeed, as the group drew closer, she could hear them clearly.

"—all I'm saying is that somebody in this company hired us, and I'd like to know who."

"We've been over this, Doss."

"And we'll go over it again until somebody remembers! You don't guard a caravan for free. Somebody's paying. Or *was* paying. Or—" Doss, a big man with a crossbow slung across his back like an afterthought, threw his hands up. "Does anyone remember what we're even guarding them *to*?"

"Salt," said a woman near the front. "I think. There's salt in the wagons. Probably."

"That's not a destination, Petra, that's a condiment."

Casper had gone very still beside Juniper, in the way she did when she was listening with more than her ears. "Mercenaries," she murmured. "Look at their eyes, girl. They've been touched by the forgetting. Not erased—just... frayed. They know they have a past. They can't reach it."

The leader of the mercenaries rode forward—a broad-shouldered woman with iron-gray hair cropped short and a scar that started at her left eyebrow and wandered down her cheek like it was looking for somewhere better to be. She reined in a few paces away and looked them over with the flat, unhurried assessment of someone who had decided long ago that surprises were for other people.

"You three," she said. "Traveling light. Girl's wounded." Her gaze flicked to the bandage on Juniper's arm, then to Casper, then—lingering—to the shape of Ember's tail poking out of Juniper's hood before dismissing it as probably a scarf. "You looking for protection or trouble?"

"Protection," Casper said smoothly. "We're headed to Corvenna."

A low murmur went through the mercenary line. The scarred woman didn't move.

"So are we," she said. "Or we were. Or we will be. Honestly, some days I'm not sure which tense we're living in." She spat to the side of the road, a gesture so practiced it was nearly ceremonial. "Sergeant Tamwell. Most people call me Tam. This is the Free Company of the Broken Oath."

"We never broke any oaths," Doss protested from behind her. "We just can't find them."

"The name's aspirational," Tam said, without turning around. "Fifty coppers a day each, and you walk when the wagons walk. We eat at dusk, we argue at dawn, and nobody asks anybody about their past, because none of us can answer and it makes the evenings depressing." She paused. "That last part's a company rule. I think. It feels like something I would have made up."

Casper glanced sideways at Juniper, and something passed between them—the silent negotiation of two people who had been making decisions together for exactly four days and were still figuring out the grammar of it. Then Casper nodded. "Done."

Tam held out a hand, and Casper shook it. Juniper noticed that Tam's grip lingered a half-second too long, her brow creasing faintly, like a word sitting on the tip of her tongue.

"You remind me of someone," Tam said slowly. "Can't think who. Can't even think if it's someone I liked."

"Most people can't," Casper said quietly. "It's all right."

* * *

The caravan, it turned out, was a floating village of people who had all lost different pieces of themselves. The wagon-master, a round cheerful man named Odo, remembered every song he'd ever heard but not a single face from before the stars went out—including, he suspected sadly, his own mother's. A pair of twin brothers had forgotten they were twins and simply believed they were very similar friends. A young clerk named Pellis kept a ledger of everything anyone told him about himself, because otherwise, he said, "I'd wake up a stranger every morning, and honestly the ledger version of me is much more organized than I ever was."

Juniper fit herself into the rhythm of the road the way water fits into a cup. She hauled water. She helped mend a wagon wheel. She learned that the ox was named Duchess and hated everyone equally, which Juniper privately respected.

And she watched the mercenaries.

They were good, in the way that broken things can be good—careful, watchful, kind to each other in the gruff shorthand of people who trusted each other with their lives but couldn't quite remember why. They trained each morning in the frost. They stood watches through the night. And every night around the fire, someone would try, once, to reach back into the fog where their pasts should be, and come back empty-handed, and the others would pour them another cup of tea and change the subject.

It was on the fifth night that Juniper sat down next to Tam.

The sergeant was sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening, turning it against the whetstone in long slow strokes, watching the fire. Up close, Juniper could see that the scar on her cheek was old—not rider-work, just life—and that her hands were steady but her eyes weren't.

"My da taught me to sharpen knives," Juniper offered. "He said you should always do it slow, because a rushed edge is a lie you tell yourself."

Tam snorted. "Your da sounds smarter than anyone in this company." She paused. "Do you remember him? Your da?"

"Some." It wasn't entirely a lie. Juniper remembered hands, flour-dusted. A voice humming. Whether those things belonged to a father or a grandfather or a stranger whose memory she'd borrowed, she couldn't say. Her own past was a locked room, and she'd stopped rattling the handle years ago. "Not enough."

"Mm." Tam tested the blade against her thumb, satisfied. "You know what the worst part is? It's not losing it. It's knowing there was something worth losing. Like having a house burn down and only being left with the shape of the doorway." She looked into the fire. "I know I loved somebody. I can feel the hole where it was. A person-shaped hole. I just can't get the person back into it."

Juniper's chest did something complicated. Ember stirred in her hood, warm against her neck, and the warmth spread down her arm and into her palm—that familiar prickle, the ember-glow she'd been carrying since the borderlands, the thing the gray riders wanted badly enough to kill for.

She hadn't meant to use it here. She'd promised herself she'd save it, hoard it, spend nothing on the road. Every flicker dimmed the flame; every gift cost the egg-ember a little of its light, and the light was the only weapon in the world that mattered anymore. Casper had said so. Hale had said so, back in Grayhollow, before the road took them.

But Tam was looking at the fire with a person-shaped hole in her chest, and Juniper was sixteen, and she had never once in her life managed to see a hurt thing and leave it alone.

"Give me your hand," Juniper said.

Tam raised an eyebrow. "Why?"

"Because I want to show you something. And because if it works, you'll want to sit down, and you're already sitting down, so really this is the most efficient possible arrangement."

That startled a laugh out of the sergeant—a real one, rough and surprised—and she held out her hand, callused palm up, the way you'd offer trust to a bird you weren't sure wouldn't bite.

Juniper took it. Ember crawled down out of the hood and settled onto their joined hands, tiny and molten, wings folded like a smug little paper lantern. Her glow brightened.

"Think about the hole," Juniper said softly. "Don't chase the memory. Just... hold still, and let the fire look."

The glow pulsed once. Twice. Warmth ran up Tam's arm, and Juniper felt it happen—the ember reaching into the frayed places, finding the torn threads of a memory and drawing them tight, weaving, mending—

Tam went rigid. Her breath caught. Her eyes went wide and wet all at once, and her hand clamped around Juniper's with a grip that would have bruised anyone who wasn't holding a piece of the last dragon-fire in the world.

"A garden," Tam whispered. "*There's a garden.* Yellow flowers—I planted—no, *she* planted them, I just kept digging them up by accident and she laughed at me—" A sound came out of her that was half laugh and half sob. "Her name. Oh gods, her name is *Maren*. Maren. She grew tomatoes that tasted like punishment and she made me eat them anyway and I *did*, every summer, I ate every single one because she—" Tam pressed her free hand over her mouth. Tears slid down through the groove of the scar. "She's real. She was real. I have a wife. I *had*—"

"She might still be alive," Juniper said gently. "The forgetting takes memories. It doesn't take people. Not yet."

Tam stared at her. At Ember. At the small golden light already fading, dimming, spending itself, and Juniper felt the cost settle into her bones like cold—the glow a shade weaker, Ember's chirp a note lower. Worth it. Always worth it. But a cost.

"What are you?" Tam asked, and there was no suspicion in it, only awe.

"Tired," Juniper said, and yawned enormously, which was true and also a convenient escape route.

* * *

By morning, the whole company knew.

Not the truth—never the truth—but the shape of it. That the Voss girl, the quiet one traveling with the charm-witch, could give you back pieces of yourself. Odo appeared at her bedroll at dawn with the hopeful expression of a man about to ask for a second helping. Pellis arrived with his ledger and a list. Even Duchess the ox regarded Juniper with what she chose to interpret as dawning respect rather than hunger.

"I can't fix everything," Juniper told them, gathered around the dying fire while the wagons loaded. "It costs something. Real something. So—one memory each. One. And you don't get to pick which one. The fire picks. Apparently it has opinions."

"It's a fire with editorial standards," Doss said solemnly. "Respect it."

One by one, over the following days, they came to her. Petra got back the face of the brother she'd buried, and cried for an hour and then laughed at his terrible jokes for another hour, retelling them to no one. One of the almost-twins recovered the knowledge that he was older by eleven minutes and wielded it with insufferable majesty. Pellis wrote a new page in his ledger, in careful letters: *I was kind. Someone remembered me being kind. Start there.*

And Tam—Tam started sitting next to Juniper at every meal, telling her about Maren. All of it. Pouring the recovered memory out like water from a jug, again and again, not because she feared losing it but because saying it made it heavier, more real, more *hers*. Maren who argued with merchants on principle. Maren who saved the yellow seeds from the garden in a tin and moved them to three different houses over the years. Maren who had kissed her under a bad roof during a storm and said, *well, now you're stuck with me.*

"You'll meet her someday," Juniper said. "If she's alive. When this is over."

"If," Tam said. But she said it smiling, and she'd started wearing a yellow ribbon braided into her sword-belt strap, bought off a peddler for an outrageous price, and nobody in the company mentioned it, because soldiers understand some things without discussing them.

Casper watched all of it from a slight distance, arms crossed, expression unreadable. On the sixth evening she sat down beside Juniper at the fire and handed her a cup of tea without being asked.

"You're spending too much," she said. No anger in it. Just fact.

"I know."

"The ember is the only thing in the world that can cut him loose. Every flicker you give away—"

"—is a flicker that goes to someone who needed it." Juniper wrapped both hands around the cup. "I know the math, Casper. I do. But you should have seen Pellis's face. You should have seen *Tam's* face. If the world ends anyway, I'd rather it end having given away what it could."

Casper was quiet for a long moment. The fire cracked. Somewhere behind them, Doss was losing an argument about salt to a man who couldn't remember why they had any.

"When I was young," Casper said at last, "my order taught us that mercy was a resource. Ration it. Spend it where it buys the most." She looked into the flames. "I rationed for twenty years, girl. I'm the stingiest woman alive. And I'm telling you—" her voice caught, just slightly, on something old and unhealed, "—don't learn it from me."

Juniper leaned her head briefly against the older woman's shoulder. Casper allowed it for exactly three seconds, which from Casper was practically an embrace.

* * *

They crossed into the hill country on the ninth day, where the road wound between ridges dark with pine, and the caravan slowed to a crawl, and Tam doubled the watches without being asked.

"Something's wrong," she said that evening, crouched by the fire next to Juniper, cleaning her blade with the unhurried economy of long habit. "Birds went quiet at noon. Didn't come back. And the mist in the valleys—" she jerked her chin toward the gray seeping between the hills, thicker than it had any right to be, moving against the wind. "That's not weather. I've soldiered in—somewhere. Enough somewheres. Weather doesn't *wait*."

Juniper's hand drifted up to her hood. Ember was awake, fully awake for the first time in days, her little body taut as a drawn bowstring, a thin thread of smoke rising from her nostrils.

The gray riders had found them once before, at the edge of the borderlands, and Juniper had run until her feet bled. She'd hoped distance was enough. She'd hoped wrong.

"How many days to Corvenna?" she asked.

"Four. Five, with the wagons." Tam's eyes swept the darkening ridgeline. "There's a river ahead. The ford at Marrow Bridge—or what used to be Marrow Bridge. Water's high this time of year." She paused, and her voice dropped into something quieter. "Whatever's coming, girl—you and your grandmother get behind us when it does. Company rule."

"You don't even know what we are."

"Don't need to." Tam sheathed her knife and stood, rolling her shoulders. "You gave me back my wife's name. Whatever you're carrying that those things want—" she tapped the yellow ribbon on her belt, once, like a salute, "—it's worth holding the line for."

Far upstream, in the gathering dark, something screamed. It was not a bird, and it was not a person, and it was not anything that had ever been alive.

Every lantern in the caravan went out at once, all together, as if the night had drawn a breath and blown.

Tam's hand went to her sword. "Wake the company," she said.
