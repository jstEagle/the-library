## 9. Riders at the Ford

The river at Marrow's Ford was the color of old pewter and about as welcoming, and Sergeant Tam Vess stood in the shallows of it with his boots off, his trousers rolled to the knee, and an expression of profound personal betrayal on his weathered face.

"It's cold," he announced, as though this were news the world had been withholding from him specifically.

"You're standing in a river," Casper said. "In autumn. In the mountains."

"I'm *scouting* in a river. There's a difference. Scouting is supposed to be dignified." He took another step and made a sound like a man being ambushed by his own ankles. "The ford's passable. Wagon-axle deep at the middle, gravel bottom, current's lazy as a Sunday. But I don't like it."

"You don't like anything."

"I liked that pie in Hallowmere," Tam said. "Before we all forgot who baked it or what was in it. I've been chasing the memory of that pie for three days now. It had... berries? Possibly. It might have been a soup." He shook his head, and for a moment the humor drained out of him, leaving something older and wearier behind. "That's what gets me, priest. Not the forgetting itself. The reaching after it. Like a tongue worrying a missing tooth."

Casper knew exactly what he meant. She'd spent twenty years worrying a tooth she couldn't even name — the absence where her god had been, smooth-edged and aching.

"Riders?" she asked, because the alternative was standing in a cold river talking about pies until she cried.

"Maybe. Maybe nothing." Tam squinted upstream, where the far bank rose into pine shadow. "No dust. No birdsong from the treeline, though, and there should be birdsong. Birds don't sing when something gray is walking." He looked at her sideways. "You've seen them up close, haven't you? The riders. Back in Grayhollow."

Casper kept her face still. It was a skill she'd practiced for two decades; she could hold a expression like a wall holds mortar.

"Juniper talks in her sleep," Tam said. "And you talk in yours too, apparently, which is how I know you're not just a charm-seller. Nobody dreams about *liturgy* unless they mean it."

"The caravan hired you to guard wagons," Casper said.

"The caravan hired me to guard wagons, yes, although none of us can remember signing the contract, which I feel is legally significant." He waded back to shore, grimacing as he pulled his boots on over wet feet. "Here's my thinking, priest. Whatever those riders are, they want your girl. And whatever your girl is carrying, it's worth killing for. Which means when they come — and they'll come at the ford, because if I were them, that's where I'd come — I need to know what you can do. So I don't waste men positioning around a weapon that isn't there."

"And if the weapon isn't there?"

Tam laced his boot with the unhurried competence of a man who had done ten thousand small hard things and expected to do ten thousand more. "Then I'll be very polite about it while we all die," he said. "But I don't think it isn't there. I think you've been sitting on it so long you've forgotten its shape. And I think—" he straightened, and met her eyes, and there was no mockery left in him at all— "I think you're going to have to remember fast."

* * *

They crossed at midmorning, when the light came slantwise through the pines and turned the river into hammered tin.

It went wrong almost immediately, the way things did. The lead wagon reached the middle of the ford, wheels churning silver spray, and Juniper rode on the bench beside the driver with the lantern cradled against her chest under her coat — Casper could see the faint warmth of it through the wool, a glow like a coal remembered rather than seen. The little dragon rode on Casper's shoulder, moth-light and curious, its tail curled around her collar like a question mark. It had taken to her with the uncomplicated devotion of small creatures, and she had found, to her private horror, that she was devoted back.

The birds stopped singing all at once.

Not gradually. All at once, the way a room goes quiet when a door opens. The pine shadow on the far bank seemed to deepen without moving, the way ink spreads through water, and then they were there — six of them, seven, eight — gray riders on gray horses, emerging from the treeline without sound, without dust, without any concession to the fact that the world insisted things must arrive somehow.

They had no faces. Casper had known that, had killed two of them in her shop and known it, but knowing it in memory and seeing it again across forty yards of shallow water were different countries. Where features should have been there was only smooth blankness, like a page someone had scraped clean, and looking at them made her eyes water and her mind slide sideways, grasping for details that refused to stick. She couldn't say afterward whether they wore armor or robes. She couldn't say how many there were. Her count changed every time she tried.

"They're here!" someone shouted, uselessly, and then the caravan dissolved into the particular chaos of ordinary people meeting extraordinary violence: horses screaming, a wagoner hauling his team around in a spray of gravel, the drovers scattering, Tam's voice cutting through it all like a whip-crack.

"Form on the wagons! Shields front! *Move*, you beautiful idiots, move!"

The riders came down into the water.

That was the worst part, Casper thought later. They didn't charge. They simply entered the river and kept coming, unhurried, inevitable, and where their hooves touched the water the river *forgot itself* — the current stuttered, the ripples smoothed flat, a whole lane of the ford going glassy and dead as though the water had never learned to move. Fish floated up belly-white in their wake. The reeds along the bank grayed and sagged like old paper.

A rider reached the lead wagon. The wagoner swung a whip at it, and where the lash touched, the leather didn't break — it *unmade*. The whip became a length of nothing, then less than nothing, and the wagoner stared at his own empty hand with an expression of dawning, animal horror, and Juniper screamed his name — Brenn, his name was Brenn, Casper would remember that much if it killed her — and the rider reached for him.

Casper moved.

She didn't decide to move. Twenty years of hiding sat on her like a stone, and underneath the stone something older woke and shoved it aside, and she was running out into the ford before her mind caught up, splashing through water that forgot her footprints behind her, the little dragon clinging to her shoulder with its claws pricking through her coat.

"*Veth.*"

The name came out of her like an arrow leaving a bow. She hadn't spoken it aloud in twenty years. It tasted of incense and cold chapels and a hand on her head in blessing, and the moment it left her mouth the world answered.

Light came up out of the river.

Not firelight, not sunlight — something whiter and older, the light of a god whose whole nature had been *witnessing*, who had watched over every small remembered thing, and it poured up around Casper in a column that turned the gray water to blazing silver. The nearest rider recoiled. For the first time, the blankness of its face seemed to express something — a kind of terrible recognition, the way vermin recognize flame.

"You will not take one more thing," Casper said, and her voice came out doubled, hers and something vaster speaking through the same throat. "*I remember you.*"

She raised her hand, palm out, and spoke the words of the Rite of Witness — the first prayer every battle-priest learned, the one that named a thing fully so it could not fade, could not dissolve, could not be unmade. Naming was Veth's oldest power. Naming was the opposite of the Thorn.

The rider's true name came to her the way such things always had: not heard but *known*, rising in her like water in a well. It was a long name. It was a hungry name. It was the name of a thing that had once been a servant of the dark between stars before the Thorn had folded it into its own appetite, and when Casper spoke it aloud the sound alone made two horses bolt and made Tam, thirty feet away, drop to one knee in the water as though the word had weight.

The rider burned.

There was no other word for it. White light closed over it like a fist, and the gray thing shrieked — a sound like a book burning, like a name being called for the last time — and came apart into ash that the river carried away. The water it had frozen remembered how to move. Somewhere a bird started singing again, tentatively, as though apologizing for the interruption.

For one heartbeat, one shining heartbeat, Casper Thackeray stood in a river with her god's light pouring off her like rain off a roof, and she felt *whole*. Twenty years of smallness burned away in an instant. This was what she had been. This was what she had buried alive in a cellar of charms and false modesty, and it had waited for her, patient as a seed, and oh, the *relief* of it—

The second rider nearly took her head off.

She ducked — instinct older than theology — and its blade passed through the space where her neck had been, and where the blade touched the surface of the river behind her, a swath of water simply ceased to have ever existed. Casper came up swinging. The second prayer was older, harsher, the Rite of Unmaking's Answer, and she put it through the rider's blank face like a nail through paper. It burned. A third came behind it, and she burned that too, and a fourth, and somewhere in the middle of it she was aware of Tam bellowing orders, of Juniper standing up on the wagon bench with the lantern held high, of the little dragon on her shoulder spreading wings it shouldn't have had yet and adding its tiny thread of gold to her white blaze—

And then it was over.

Eight riders. Eight names spoken. Eight piles of ash dissolving downstream. The pine bank stood empty and ordinary, birdsong returning in ragged pieces, and the caravan stood in the shallows staring at her the way people stare at a storm that has passed through without killing them.

Casper stood in the water, breathing hard, the light guttering out of her like a lamp running dry, and understood with cold clarity that she had just done the one thing she had sworn never to do.

She had been noticed.

* * *

Tam found her sitting on a rock at the river's edge, wringing out her coat hem, watching the little dragon chase minnows in the shallows with the doomed optimism of the very young.

"Well," Tam said. He sat down beside her without asking, which was very Tam. "That was a thing I saw."

"Yes."

"With the light. And the shouting of names. And the — was that fourth one screaming in *verse*?"

"Liturgical verse. The Rite has a meter. It helps with the pronunciation."

"Naturally." He was quiet a moment. Downstream, the wagoners were righting the lead wagon and arguing loudly about whether Brenn had actually been touched or had merely fallen, and whether falling counted, and what exactly they'd all been doing before the attack anyway. "You're crying," Tam observed.

"I'm not."

"There's a distinct lack of argument in your voice, though, which worries me more." He pulled a flask from his coat and offered it. She took it. It was terrible. It helped anyway. "How many people saw that?"

"All of them."

"And riders report to someone. Everything reports to someone, these days." Tam looked out at the water, and his jaw worked. "Corvenna will hear about a woman at Marrow's Ford who burned eight of the king's hunters with a dead god's fire. If they weren't already certain, they are now. You've painted a sign on all of us, priest. On the girl most of all."

"I know."

"Do you?" He turned to look at her, and there was no anger in it, which somehow made it worse. "Because I need you to understand what you've bought. There's no more traveling quiet. No more fords and ferries and keeping our heads down. From here it's a race, and everyone in it knows we're running."

"I know," Casper said again, and found that the fear underneath the words was real but not, anymore, the largest thing in her. Underneath the fear was something that had been waiting twenty years to be used, and it was awake now, and it was *glad*. "I'd make the same choice again. Ask me tomorrow and I'll probably wish I'd let them take me instead. But today — today I'd burn them all again."

Tam nodded slowly. "Good answer," he said. "Honest ones usually hurt." Then he stood, and rolled his shoulders, and became Sergeant Vess again, all business, and Casper might have believed the transition complete if she hadn't seen him glance once, quickly, at the treeline — checking, the way soldiers check, counting exits.

He never got to use them.

* * *

It happened at the edge of camp, in the blue hour when the fires were lit and the smell of bad stew made everything briefly, blessedly normal.

The rider had not been among the eight. That was the horror of it, the thing Casper replayed for years afterward: there had been a ninth, hidden, patient, and it had not come for Juniper or the lantern or even for Casper herself. It had simply walked out of the dusk between the wagons, unnoticed, unremarkable, the way grief walks into a house, and it had put its hand on Tam Vess's shoulder as he bent over the cookfire.

He didn't scream. That was the second horror. He made a small sound, a surprised one, like a man remembering something, and when Casper turned at the dragon's sudden shriek she saw the gray shape already withdrawing, already dissolving back into the dark, and Tam still standing, still upright, one hand pressed to his shoulder where the fabric of his coat was graying outward like frost spreading on glass.

"TAM!"

He looked at her. His eyes were still his eyes. That was the third horror, the one that broke something in her that she suspected would never quite set right: the forgetting took the edges first, the memories, the history, and left the person standing inside it, watching themselves go.

"Priest," he said. His voice was steady. "Priest, I can't — there was a pie. There was a pie, and I can't find it. Help me look?"

He swayed. Casper caught him, and Juniper was there, white-faced, the lantern held up uselessly, its ember too dim now to do anything but glow like a dying coal. "Can you fix it?" Juniper whispered. "Can you fix him, please, please—"

Casper laid her hands over the gray spread and spoke every naming she knew, every rite, every prayer, and the light rose in her thin and flickering and slid off the forgetting like rain off oilcloth. Some things, once unwritten, stayed unwritten. She had known that since Grayhollow. She had hoped, desperately, stupidly, to be wrong.

Tam sank down against a wagon wheel, and the men gathered, and he looked at each of their faces in turn with the fierce attention of a man memorizing — or trying to, or failing to, or refusing to stop trying regardless.

"Sergeant," said the youngest drover, weeping openly, "what do we do?"

Tam smiled. It was a good smile. It was, Casper thought, the exact smile of a man who had finally remembered the pie.

"Hold the line," he said. "Hold the *line*, you beautiful idiots. Get the priest and the girl to Corvenna. Don't you dare forget that I told you." His gaze found Casper's, and held it, and in it was no fear at all, only the weary, stubborn clarity of a man spending his last coin on something worth buying. "You remember for all of us. That's the job now. That's the whole bloody job."

His hand slipped from his shoulder. The gray had reached his heart.

What was left was a man-shaped quiet, peaceful as snowfall, wearing Tam's coat. The little dragon landed beside it and made a sound Casper had never heard from it before, low and mourning, and Juniper knelt in the dirt and did not cry, which frightened Casper more than tears would have.

Far to the south, past the pines, past the hills, a star that had survived the first extinguishing flickered — and went out.

And in the silence after, Casper heard the hoofbeats. Many of them. Coming up the north road, unhurried, inevitable, flying no banner she could see — but she knew, the way she now knew rider-names, the way she knew the taste of her own guilt: these were not the Thorn's riders.

These wore the crown.

They crested the hill above the ford as the camp fires guttered, a long column of the king's own cavalry, lances black against the last of the light, and at their head a herald raised a scroll sealed in gray wax and called out, in a voice like a door closing:

"Casper Thackeray. By order of King Aldric, you are summoned to Corvenna."
