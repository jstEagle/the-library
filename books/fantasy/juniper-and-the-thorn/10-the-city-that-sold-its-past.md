## 10. The City That Sold Its Past

The road to Corvenna was paved with good intentions and then, apparently, with actual gold, because Juniper had never seen a highway so wide or so shamelessly ornamented. Lantern-posts every fifty paces, each one holding a little glass star that glowed without fire. Statues of kings she didn't recognize lining the verges like well-dressed scarecrows.

"That one's King Aldric," Hale said, gesturing at a marble man on a horse with an expression of profound boredom. "Or it's supposed to be. The face is wrong."

"The face is wrong," Juniper repeated.

"Chiseled over. Someone paid to have his jaw softened. Look at the hands too — those aren't a king's hands, they're a scholar's." Hale shifted on the cart bench, wincing where the rider-touch had blackened his forearm up past the elbow. The wound had stopped spreading, mostly, but it had a smell like cold ash and old paper, and Casper changed the dressing twice a day without ever meeting his eyes while she did it.

Juniper looked at the statue's hands and felt the lantern in her lap grow suddenly heavy. Inside it, curled no bigger than her thumb, the dragon slept against the ember, its wings folded like a moth's, its scales the color of a banked fire. It had eaten three crumbs of charred bread that morning and pronounced them acceptable by belching a puff of smoke shaped vaguely like a heart. She'd named it Pip, partly for the sound it made when it sneezed, and partly because naming things made them harder to lose, and she was done losing things.

"You're doing the thing," Casper said from the driver's bench. She didn't turn around. Her braid was coming loose again; Juniper kept meaning to tell her and kept not telling her, because watching Casper discover it herself was one of the few reliable pleasures left on this road.

"What thing?"

"The clutching thing. You've been hugging that lantern like it owes you money since the last milestone."

"It might owe me money," Juniper said. "It's very mysterious."

Casper snorted. But her eyes went to the horizon, where Corvenna sat on its hill like a coal wrapped in silk, and her mouth did the flat thing it did instead of frowning, and Juniper hugged the lantern a little tighter anyway.

Because here was the thing Juniper hadn't said out loud yet, not even to Pip, not even in the dark of their shared bedrolls: the ember was dimmer. Not dying — she would have known, she thought, she *hoped* she would have known — but dimmer, the way a hearth is dimmer in the morning than at midnight. Every time she gave a piece of it away, a lullaby here, a grandmother's face there, it came back smaller. And they were walking toward the largest city in the world, full of people who had forgotten everything, and she could already feel it pulling at her like a hundred small hands.

*Not yet,* she told the ember. *We're not there yet.*

The ember, being an ember, said nothing, which was the most Veth-like thing about it, according to Casper, who got a strange look on her face whenever she talked about her god, as if she were describing an old lover to a room full of strangers.

* * *

They came through the Salt Gate at midmorning, and Juniper forgot how to breathe.

She had seen Grayhollow, which had seemed enormous once. She had seen border towns burning, which had seemed endless. Corvenna was neither. It was a city built by people who had run out of modesty — towers stacked on towers, bridges strung between rooftops like cobwebs spun by ambitious spiders, and above it all, the palace, glowing.

That was the part that stopped her. The palace didn't catch light. It *made* it, a soft white radiance pouring off its spires like heat off a forge, except it wasn't warm. Juniper knew warmth. Warmth lived in her lap and sneezed smoke-hearts. This was light the way a stolen purse is money — technically true, deeply wrong.

"Starlight," Hale said quietly. "Every star that's gone out since the Thorn woke. It's been drinking them."

"How many are left?" Juniper asked.

Hale was silent long enough that she turned to look at him. His face had gone the gray of old dishwater. "I don't know," he said. "I used to know all their names. I used to teach them to novices. There were—" He stopped. His brow furrowed, genuinely furrowed, a man reaching into his own pocket and finding it sewn shut. "There were some," he finished, and Juniper's stomach dropped through the cart floor and kept going.

Even Hale's forgetting had forgetting now.

* * *

The market district smelled like bread.

That should have been comforting. Bread is the smell of safety, of ordinary mornings, of Casper grumbling over a camp stove while Pip tried to dive-bomb the skillet. But the bread smell in Corvenna was wrong the way the palace light was wrong — too sweet, too uniform, rising from a hundred identical stalls where vendors ladled steaming loaves out of cauldrons and handed them across counters with the bright, hollow cheer of people reciting lines.

"Memory for bread!" called a woman with a red scarf and a smile that reached her mouth and stopped there. "Warm loaf, fair trade! Small memories for small loaves, big ones for the festival batch!"

Juniper drifted closer, Casper a half-step behind her, close enough that their shoulders touched — Casper did that more lately, Juniper noticed. Standing where she could be grabbed if grabbing became necessary.

At the stall, a young man stood counting coins he clearly didn't have. "What'll a first kiss get me?"

Red Scarf beamed. "A loaf and a honey roll! First kisses are premium stock, love, especially if you remember the weather."

"It was raining," the young man said. Something flickered across his face — not sadness exactly, more like a man checking a pocket one last time before handing it over. "It was raining and she laughed with her whole body. Is that — will I keep the laughing part?"

"You'll keep knowing you were happy," Red Scarf said gently. "That's the best part anyway."

And the terrible thing, the thing that made Juniper's chest hurt in a new shape, was that the young man nodded like this was reasonable. Like *knowing you were happy* was a fair wage for having been happy.

He closed his eyes. Red Scarf held out a small copper bell, and it rang once, thin and clear, and the young man swayed, and when he opened his eyes they were smooth as pond water. He took the bread. He bit into it right there, standing up, and said, "Ooh, that's good," with genuine delight, and walked away lighter.

Juniper watched him go until the crowd swallowed him.

"They queue up," she said. Her voice came out strange. "They *queue*, Casper. There's a line."

"There's always a line," said a voice beside them, "when the alternative is hunger."

He was old, the speaker, sitting on an overturned crate with a blanket over his knees and a half-eaten loaf balanced on top, and his eyes were the opposite of the young man's — crammed so full of remembering they looked ready to burst. "Name's Odo. You two are country folk. I can tell. You flinch."

"We flinch," Casper agreed, "because your city sells souls for carbs."

"Bread isn't the sin." Odo tore off a crust and chewed it with the deliberation of a man who had argued this exact point many times and won none of them. "Bread is just what they pay us in. The palace takes the memories. Palace needs 'em for something — nobody knows what, nobody asks, asking gets you a visit from the gray gentlemen." He nodded at Juniper's lantern. "You want to hide that, girl. Palace men pay bounty for unregistered flame. Call it 'unlicensed illumination.' Fine is a memory. Can't pay, they take it off you at the door."

Juniper's arms closed around the lantern. "It's a moth," she said. "It's a decorative moth."

"Moths don't make your shadow dance," Odo said, not unkindly. "Yours is doing a little jig right now."

Juniper looked down. Sure enough, the lantern's glow threw her shadow on the cobbles, and the shadow's edges flickered with wing-shapes, and Pip, sensing attention, chose that moment to yawn, which produced a sound like a teakettle falling in love.

Casper stepped smoothly in front of the lantern. "Thank you for the civic advice," she said, in the voice Juniper privately called her *market voice*, the one that could sell a broken charm to a bishop. "We're leaving."

"Everyone's leaving," Odo said. "Nobody leaves. That's the trick of Corvenna." He took another bite of bread and looked up at the glowing palace with an expression Juniper couldn't read until she realized it was longing. "My wife's up there somewhere. In a jar or a bell or whatever they keep them in. Forty years of her. I sold the small stuff to eat, year by year, thinking I'd stop. Then one day I couldn't remember why I'd wanted to stop." He smiled at them, and it was the saddest smile Juniper had ever seen, because it was completely sincere. "Enjoy your moth."

* * *

They found lodging above a dye-shop in the Tanners' Quarter, chosen by Casper specifically because the streets around it were too narrow for gray riders to ride abreast, a fact she delivered with the grim satisfaction of a woman who had spent twenty years thinking about escape routes and finally gotten to use the hobby professionally.

The room was small and smelled of vinegar. Pip approved immediately, claimed the windowsill as sovereign territory, and fell asleep in a patch of sun, one wing draped over the lantern like a child guarding a secret.

Juniper sat on the bed and took the lantern into her lap and looked — really looked — at the ember inside.

It was smaller. She had been telling herself it wasn't. She had been very busy, and roads were long, and there had been riders and funerals and a mercenary sergeant's grave under a willow tree that Juniper still couldn't pass without her throat closing. But there was no busy in this room. There was only vinegar and late light and the truth.

The ember that had filled the lantern in the borderlands now sat in it like a pebble in a bowl. Still alive. Still warm. But a hand's worth of fire where there had been an arm's worth, and every gift she'd given — the lullaby, the miller's daughter's mother's face, poor Tam's name spoken whole over his grave — each one had cost, and the bill was coming due.

"Casper," she said.

Casper was at the window, doing her nightly survey of the street below with the focus of a general and the posture of a woman pretending she wasn't worried. "Mm."

"If it runs out—" Juniper stopped. Started again. "When we get to the king. If there's not enough left to cut him free."

Casper came away from the window. She sat down on the bed beside Juniper, which she almost never did, and looked at the lantern for a long moment.

"Then we find more fire," she said.

"Fire isn't lying around, Casper. It's not turnips."

"No," Casper said. "But I've been thinking. About what the Thorn eats. Memories. Stars. All the same thing, Hale says — a star goes out, the world loses something, and the palace hoards the loss in jars." Her jaw set. "Which means the palace is sitting on the biggest pile of stolen fire in the world. We don't need the ember to be big enough. We need it to be *bright* enough to light the fuse. The rest is already in the building."

Juniper stared at her. "You want to rob the palace."

"I want to *liberate* the palace's inventory," Casper said with dignity. "Robbery implies we're taking something they deserve to keep."

Despite everything — the dimming ember, Odo's smile, the young man walking away lighter with his bread — Juniper felt her mouth twitch. "That's robbery with better vocabulary."

"All crime is," Casper said. "Ask any priest."

Then the humor drained out of her face, slowly, like water out of a cracked bowl, and she looked at the window, toward the hill, toward the glow.

"But Juniper. Before any of that. Tomorrow we find out what the palace wants with us."

Juniper went cold. "Wants with *us*?"

Casper reached into her coat and produced a letter, heavy cream paper, sealed with wax the color of dried blood. It had been delivered an hour ago by a servant whose eyes were smooth as pond water, who had knocked, bowed, and departed without a single word, and who had left behind him a silence that still hadn't quite lifted from the room.

It was addressed, in beautiful looping script, to:

**The Charm-Seller of Grayhollow, the Fire-Carrying Girl, and the Moth.**

"He knows," Juniper whispered. Pip woke, sensing the change in the air, and pressed himself against the lantern glass, and the ember inside flared — just slightly, just for a heartbeat — as if it had heard its own name read aloud by the thing that had eaten its god.

"He knows," Casper confirmed. "And he's invited us to court." She turned the letter over. On the back, beneath the seal, four words in that same lovely hand:

***Come hungry. Bring dessert.***

Juniper clutched the lantern and, for the first time since the borderlands burned, felt something other than dread coil up in her chest — hot, sharp, and unmistakably angry.

"Good," she said. "So do we."
