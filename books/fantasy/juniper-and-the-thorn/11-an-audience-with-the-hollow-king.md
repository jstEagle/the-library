## 11. An Audience With the Hollow King

The summons came at dawn, delivered by a boy in palace livery who looked like he'd rather be delivering anything else—a fish, perhaps, or news of a death in the family.

"Casper Thackeray," he read from a scroll, wincing at the pronunciation. "Charm-seller of Grayhollow Street. You are commanded to attend His Majesty King Aldric at the capital of Corvenna, there to answer questions pertaining to... um." He squinted. "Pertaining to matters of the crown."

"Which matters?" Casper asked.

"I'm not told which matters." The boy held out the scroll like it might bite him. "I'm just told you come, or soldiers come instead. They said—" he lowered his voice, glancing up and down the empty street— "they said to tell you the king already knows your name. All three of them."

*All three of them.* Her name, her order-name, and the name Veth had whispered over her the night she was anointed blade. Casper took the scroll with a steady hand because her hand had learned steadiness as a survival skill, and smiled at the boy because smiling was cheaper than screaming.

"Tell whoever sent you that I'll come."

"They didn't send anyone," the boy said, backing away. "The scroll was on my pillow when I woke up. It's been happening all week. People just wake up with scrolls." He hesitated. "Is it going to be all right? My mum says the stars are coming back wrong. She says they're too *neat* now."

Casper looked up. The morning sky was pale and thin, and the few stars still clinging to its edge did look neat—arranged, like beads on a string someone had strung with great care and no love.

"Go home," she said gently. "Lock your door. Eat breakfast with your mum."

He went. Casper stood in her doorway for a long moment, then turned back into the shop where Juniper sat on the counter swinging her legs, the lantern-dragon egg cradled against her chest, the moth-sized dragon itself asleep in her hair like a bizarre ornament.

"Well?" Juniper said.

"I've been summoned to court."

"To *court*? Like, the actual palace? Where the king is?" Juniper's eyes went wide, then narrow. "You're not going."

"I'm absolutely going."

"That's the stupidest thing I've ever heard, and I once watched a man try to milk a rooster."

"Juniper—"

"No, listen. The riders hunt us. The stars are dying. The king is—" she dropped her voice even though the shop was empty, "—the thing Hale described. And you want to walk through the front door?"

"The scroll knows my order-name," Casper said quietly. "It knows the name Veth gave me. If they know that much, hiding buys nothing. If I run, they hunt, and when they find me they find *you*, and I would rather face whatever's on that throne than spend the rest of my life looking over my shoulder while you carry that egg." She tapped the lantern lightly with one finger. "Besides. There's something I need to see."

"What?"

"Whether it remembers me." She heard how that sounded and winced. "Whether *I* remember me, rather. Twenty years is long enough to forget what you're afraid of. I'd like to check."

Juniper slid off the counter. For a moment she looked every one of her sixteen years—young and scared and stubborn about both. Then she lifted her chin in a way that made her look exactly like the dragon-keepers must have looked, ten generations deep, standing between the world and the dark.

"Then I'm coming with you."

"You are not."

"I'm the one carrying the last fire in the world. You think I'm staying here with a note that says *gone to see the king, back by supper*?" She crossed her arms. "Hale can watch the shop. He owes you. He told me so himself, twice, once while sober."

Casper opened her mouth. Closed it. Somewhere in the last weeks she had apparently become a person who lost arguments to teenagers, and she couldn't pinpoint when it had happened, only that it kept happening.

"Hale stays outside the city walls," she said finally. "If we don't come back by the third bell after dusk, he takes you east and never looks back."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I'll tell him embarrassing stories about you until he agrees."

"He doesn't have any leverage over me."

"He will. I have *years* of material."

Juniper tried very hard not to smile and failed, which Casper counted as a small victory, and small victories were the only kind she'd been collecting lately.

* * *

Corvenna had been beautiful once. Casper knew this the way you know things about a person who has died—that they had been young, that they had laughed, that the evidence is still there under everything else. The bones of beauty remained: bridges of white stone, avenues wide enough for processions, fountains carved with heroes whose faces had gone smooth as eggs. But the color had drained out of the city the way color drains from a face. People moved through the streets quickly and quietly, and in the market squares they traded—not coins, Casper noticed, but small glass vials stoppered with wax.

"What are those?" Juniper whispered.

Hale, walking between them with his hood up and his hands shaking only moderately, answered without moving his lips, an old confessor's trick. "Memories. A summer afternoon goes for a loaf and a bit of cheese. Your mother's face fetches enough to eat for a month." He spat, delicately, into the gutter. "The palace buys them by the barrel. That's the glow you'll see on the towers tonight. That's what starlight is now. Other people's yesterdays, burned for light."

Juniper's hand found the lantern and gripped it hard enough that the little dragon inside chirped in protest.

They were stopped at the palace gates by guards whose armor was gray in a way armor shouldn't be—not weathered, not dull, but *unremembered*, as though the concept of shine had been subtracted from it. One guard looked at Casper's scroll, then at her face, and something flickered behind his eyes. Recognition, maybe. Or the absence of it.

"The charm-seller," he said. "And guests." He stepped aside. "His Majesty will see you in the Star Hall. Do not touch anything that glows."

"That seems like advice more places should give," Hale muttered.

The Star Hall had been a throne room once. Now the throne stood at the far end beneath a ceiling that had been replaced entirely with glass, and beyond the glass there should have been sky. Instead there were stars—hundreds of them, thousands, hanging in a darkness that no honest night ever held, each one burning steady and cold and *arranged*. Constellations that had never existed. Patterns with no meaning except possession.

And on the throne sat King Aldric.

Casper had seen him once, twenty years ago, from the back of a crowd—a broad-shouldered man with a laugh people said could be heard three streets away. The man on the throne was still broad-shouldered. That was nearly all that remained. He sat very straight, the way furniture sits, and his face had the terrible smoothness of a face that had forgotten how to make expressions and was working from descriptions. His crown was black iron worked into the shape of thorn branches, and the thorns had grown—she could see it, the slow living creep of them—down past his ears, along his jaw, tiny black buds swelling at his temples like the promise of some awful spring.

"Kneel," said a herald who may or may not have been alive.

Casper knelt. Beside her, Juniper knelt a half-second late, and Hale knelt a full second late with the practiced grace of a man who had knelt before worse.

"Rise," said the king.

His voice was Aldric's voice. That was the worst part. It was warm, it was human, it was a voice built for telling jokes across a tavern table—and it came out of a mouth that did not move quite right, syllables arriving a heartbeat after the lips shaped them, like a puppet operated by someone remembering sound secondhand.

"Casper Thackeray." The king rose from the throne, and the thorns on his crown creaked softly, like a ship in a calm sea. "Once Casper of the Silent Order. Once the Blade Anointed. Once—" and here the mouth curved into something that wanted to be a smile, "—once beloved."

Behind her, she heard Juniper's breath catch. Behind the king's eyes, something *moved*. Not a reflection. Not a trick of the starlight. Something vast turning over in a shallow grave, aware of the light for the first time in twenty years.

"You know me," Casper said.

"I remember you." The king descended the dais steps slowly, thorns whispering against marble. "That is my whole nature, little blade. I am the thing that remembers when nothing else will. I remember the first word ever spoken, and the last prayer of the last believer of every dead god, and I remember a girl in a chapel of roots who swore herself to a silent god and then ran when he needed her loudest." He stopped three paces away. Close enough that she could smell him—not rot, worse, *absence*, the smell of a room where something used to be. "Do you know what I remember most about you?"

"No," Casper said. "But I suspect you'll tell me."

"Your fear. It was delicious. Fear always is—it's memory at its most concentrated. Every fear is a memory of a wound that hasn't happened yet." The king tilted his head, and the starlight above caught the black buds at his temples. "I have thought of you often across these twenty quiet years. You hid so well. Such a small life. Charms that barely worked. A shop that smelled of dust and dishonesty. I admired it, in a way. A masterpiece of forgetting, performed by someone who remembered everything."

"Why am I here?"

"Because the game has changed." The king spread his hands, and Casper saw that his palms had thorns too, fine as needles. "Your wards fell because I finished unmaking the border between the world and myself. Your stars die because memories die, and I am *hungry*, and the world has been hoarding. But you—" the voice softened, and it was obscene how well the softness fit it, "—you are sitting on a feast I cannot reach. Veth poured himself into you, blade-anointed. His rites, his years, his name for you, the sound of his voice in your skull on the night you were consecrated. It's all still in there, buried under twenty years of dust. I can't dig it out. But *you* could. And I—" the mouth smiled again, wider this time, and Casper saw that the teeth behind the smile were very slightly wrong, "—I am prepared to trade."

The hall went very quiet. Even the stars seemed to lean in.

"Give me the girl," the king said. "The keeper-child and her ember. She walks to me willingly—or you simply step aside when I send for her, I'm flexible—and in exchange, I give you back every memory of your god. Whole. Undamaged. Every rite, every year, every hour you knelt in the dark and felt him listening. You will be his blade again, truly, completely. You will have him back." The voice dropped to a murmur meant only for her, intimate as a lover's. "You have spent twenty years mourning a god you can barely remember. I am offering you the grief's end. I am offering you *him*."

And here was the thing Casper would never tell anyone, later, not even Juniper, not even in the ledger of her own honesty: for three full heartbeats, she considered it.

Three heartbeats. In the first, she saw the chapel of roots, candle-smoke, the warmth of a presence that had no voice but spoke anyway. In the second, she saw her own hands at nineteen, steady with purpose instead of practice. In the third, she saw nothing at all—just the ache, the enormous hollow ache of a life half-lived, and the promise of it being filled.

Then she heard a small sound beside her. Juniper, shifting her weight, the lantern clinking softly against her belt, the dragon inside making a sleepy, trusting chirp.

The third heartbeat ended.

"No," Casper said.

The king's smile did not falter, which was somehow worse than if it had. "No?"

"No. You're offering me a ghost and asking me to pay with the only living thing left worth paying for. Veth wouldn't take that price. I know it, even if I can't remember knowing it—I know it the way you know a lullaby's shape without the words. Whatever he was, he wasn't a god who bought children." She lifted her chin. "And neither am I."

For a moment, the hall was perfectly still.

Then the king sighed, and the sigh came out wrong—too deep, too many voices braided into one, a sound like a forest settling in winter—and the thorns at his temples bloomed. Black flowers opened all at once, and the starlight overhead dimmed, and every guard in the hall turned their smooth faces toward Casper in perfect unison.

"A pity," the king said, and now the warm tavern-voice was gone entirely, and what remained was older than kingdoms. "I did so admire your hiding. Shall we see what's underneath it?"

The guards drew their blades, and the blades were gray, and Casper understood with cold clarity that she had perhaps four seconds and no plan whatsoever.

She got three of them wrong.

The first guard she sidestepped, catching his wrist and using his own momentum to put him into the second guard, which bought her a moment. The moment ended when a third guard's gauntleted fist caught her across the cheekbone and the world went white and sideways. She hit the marble. Above her the false stars wheeled. Hands seized her arms, her shoulders, and the gray metal was *cold* in a way that reached past skin, past muscle, down toward the place where memories lived, and she felt it begin to pull—

A crash. Glass. Heat.

Juniper had thrown the lantern. Not the egg—the lantern, the empty housing, hurled at the nearest brazier, and the oil inside went up in a bloom of orange that sent the guards staggering back with something like instinct, something like remembered fear of fire. And in the smoke and shouting, a hand closed around Casper's collar and hauled her upright, and it was Hale, red-faced and swearing with the fluency of forty years in the archives, dragging her toward a servant's door she hadn't seen.

"The girl?" Casper gasped.

"Right behind us, and *faster than both of us*, move—"

They ran. Through kitchens where cooks stared with blank, hungry eyes. Through corridors where the wallpaper showed hunting scenes with the animals' faces worn away. Behind them, unhurried, patient as winter, came the sound of the king's voice echoing off stone:

"You cannot hide in a world I am eating, little blade. Everything you love is already on my table."

Then a side door, then an alley, then rain—real rain, blessedly ordinary—and the three of them huddled under a bridge while palace horns sounded overhead, and Casper pressed her bleeding face against cold stone and laughed until it turned into something else.

"You came back for me," she said to Hale.

"Don't make it strange." He was shaking, badly, and his left hand had gone gray at the fingertips where a guard had gripped him. He tucked it into his sleeve. "We need to move. The undercity. There's a place—old, older than the palace, older than the order. If anywhere in this city still remembers how to hide, it's there."

"The chapel?" Juniper asked quietly.

Hale looked at her for a long moment. "How do you know that word?"

"I don't," Juniper said. "It knows me."

Above them, through the gaps in the bridge, the false stars burned in their neat, arranged patterns—and Casper, watching them, felt the first true thing she'd felt in twenty years settle into her chest like a coal: not hope, exactly. Closer to a vow.

They moved, and the dark of the undercity took them in.
