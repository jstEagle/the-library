## 12. Beneath the Chapel of Roots

The undercity of Corvenna smelled like wet stone and old candles and, faintly, like the inside of a very old shoe.

"That's the river," Hale had said, wheezing as he led them down a stair that seemed to have been designed by someone who hated stairs. "River smell. Mostly."

"Mostly," Juniper repeated, holding the lantern high. The dragon—Lantern, because naming him anything grander felt like tempting fate—rode in his eggshell on a cord around her neck, dim as a coal wrapped in ash. He hadn't flared since the palace purge. He hadn't done much of anything except breathe, slow and small, like something trying to remember how.

Behind her, Casper walked in silence. That wasn't unusual—Casper could out-silence a statue—but this silence had a different weight. Since the shrine, since the rite, Casper had been a stranger wearing a familiar face, and Juniper kept catching herself about to say things like *remember when* and then remembering that she didn't. Neither of them did. Not together.

So she narrated instead. She'd been doing it for days, telling Casper their own story like a bard who'd only heard it secondhand: *You found me bleeding in your shop. You killed two gray men with light that came out of your hands like it was angry at them. You complain about my cooking but you always finish it.* Casper never said stop. Sometimes, when Juniper got to the good parts, the corner of her mouth did something almost like smiling, the way a person smiles at a story they don't know is theirs yet.

"Here," Hale said, and stopped so suddenly Juniper walked into his back, which was like walking into a damp mattress that had opinions.

They stood before a wall. Just a wall—cracked plaster, water stains shaped like countries that no longer existed. Juniper looked at Casper. Casper looked at Hale. Hale looked pleased with himself, which for a dying man was either admirable or alarming.

"It's a wall," Juniper said.

"It's a door," Hale said. "It's been a door longer than it's been a wall. Put your hand flat on it and ask nicely."

"Ask nicely," Juniper echoed.

"You're a keeper's daughter. It'll know."

Her hand went to the lantern at her throat before her brain caught up. *Keeper's daughter.* The words still didn't fit right, like shoes borrowed from someone with different feet. She pressed her palm against the plaster. It was cold, then less cold, then—there, deep underneath, faint as a heartbeat through three blankets—something answered.

*Oh,* said the wall, in the way walls say things, which is not in words at all. *Oh, one of yours.*

The plaster softened like candle wax. It didn't melt so much as *step aside*, folding open around an archway no wider than a coffin, and warm air breathed out at them, smelling of honey and dust and something older than both.

"Well," said Casper, in the tone of a woman who had spent twenty years selling charms that didn't work and had just discovered the building she'd been standing next to her whole life was magic. "That's embarrassing."

* * *

The chapel beneath Corvenna was not large, but it was *deep*, in the way that a well is deep or a grief is deep. Roots had come down through the ceiling centuries ago—not choking thorns, nothing like the black brambles above, but pale, patient roots, silver-grey, hanging like the strings of an instrument nobody played anymore. They filled the space without crowding it. Candles burned in niches along the walls, hundreds of them, none recently lit, all somehow still burning.

Juniper counted the pews out of habit. Eleven. Each one carved from a single piece of wood, each one worn smooth in the middle by generations of sitting.

At the far end stood no altar. Instead there was a tree—or the ghost of one—a trunk of woven roots rising into the dark overhead, and set into its heart, a hollow. A cradle-shaped hollow, exactly lantern-sized, exactly ember-colored, and empty.

Hale lowered himself onto the nearest pew with the groan of a man whose body had filed a formal complaint. "Sit," he said. "Both of you. This is going to take a while, and I intend to be dramatic about it."

"You're dying," Casper said. It wasn't unkind. It was just true, the way Casper said true things.

"I'm dying *slowly*," Hale corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference is I get to talk." He gestured at the root-tree. "You wanted to know what you are, girl. So. Lesson one."

Juniper sat. The pew was cold and then warm, like the wall had been. Lantern shifted against her collarbone, and for the first time in days he turned—not much, just a quarter-turn—toward the empty hollow in the tree. Toward home, maybe. Or toward the shape of home, remembered by someone too small to remember anything.

"The order you knew me from," Hale began, "the Silent Brothers of Veth—we weren't priests. Not first. We were *wardens*. Do you know what we were wardens of?"

"A god," Casper said quietly.

"A god's *worst day*," Hale said. "Everyone thinks Veth was the god of silence. Of quiet, of secrets, of held tongues. Nonsense. Veth was the god of memory. Every remembered thing, every name kept, every story told twice so it doesn't die—that was his breath. And everything forgotten—" Hale tapped his own temple, where the rider's touch had left a grey bloom spreading like frost. "Everything forgotten went somewhere. It had to go somewhere. Nothing simply stops existing; it gets *put away*. And Veth put it away, in the dark under the world, behind a door, and he set a jailer over that door."

Juniper's mouth had gone dry. "The Thorn."

"The Thorn is what forgetting looks like when you let it pile up long enough to want things," Hale said. "It isn't evil, exactly. Evil wants. The Thorn just *empties*. It's hunger without a stomach. And a thousand years ago, it learned that if it couldn't get past the door, it could grow a key from the inside." He looked at Casper. "You know this part better than I do. You were there for the end of it."

"I was there for the beginning of the end," Casper said. Her voice was flat, careful, a woman carrying something breakable across ice. "I don't remember the rest. Tell it."

Hale nodded slowly. "Veth fought it. Of course he did. But you can't fight forgetting with memory alone—the more he reached for his power, the more of himself the Thorn ate. So he did the last thing a god can do. He broke off a piece of his own fire—the part of him that remembers *light*, the first thing anyone ever remembered—and he gave it away. Gave it to mortals. Swore them to keep it hidden, keep it fed, keep it *sharp*. Because fire is the one thing the Thorn cannot digest. Burned memories leave ash, and ash remembers."

"The dragons," Juniper whispered.

"The dragons," Hale agreed. "Ember-keepers. Dragon-fire is Veth's own flame, banked small enough to hide in an egg. Your people didn't worship him, girl. They *imprisoned for him*. They were his jailers' jailers—the lock inside the lock. The chapel up top, the one the city forgot? Built over this one. This one built over the door itself." He pointed, with a shaking finger, at the root-tree. At the hollow. "And the bloodline that kept the last ember—the last living piece of the god's first memory—is your bloodline. Has been for nine hundred years. Your mother sat in that pew. Her mother before her. They died keeping it, and the ember passed to you the way it passes in your family: badly, secretly, and far too young."

Juniper looked down at the lantern. At the tiny, guttering spark inside the shell, breathing its slow small breaths.

She thought about the orphanage at Marrowfen, where the matron had called her *that Voss girl* as though Voss were a disease. She thought about how she'd always known things she shouldn't—how to calm a frightened animal with a hum she'd never been taught, how fire behaved around her like a dog that recognized its owner. She thought about the night the border fell, and the gray riders coming, and how they hadn't been chasing her.

They'd been chasing *it*. And she'd run straight into the arms of the one person in Grayhollow who could burn them.

"Nobody told me," she said. Her voice cracked in the middle, and she hated it, and she kept going anyway. "Nine hundred years. My whole family. Nobody thought to write it down somewhere I'd find it?"

"They wrote it down everywhere," Hale said gently. "In songs you thought were nonsense. In the lullaby your mother sang—did you ever wonder why every verse ends with *keep the light till morning*? In the way your people buried their dead facing east, toward the dawn, because dawn is just the world remembering the sun. You were told, Juniper. You were told constantly. You just weren't told in words."

And that was the moment it landed—not the history, not the theology, but the lullaby. Four notes, over and over, the one thing she had left of her mother, the thing she'd hummed to the egg every night without knowing why. *Keep the light till morning. Keep the light till morning.*

She was crying before she decided to, which was embarrassing, and she cried the way she did everything, thoroughly and without much dignity, and Casper—stranger-Casper, nameless-Casper—moved down the pew without hesitation and put an arm around her shoulders like it was the most obvious thing in the world. Like some parts of a person survive even when the memories don't.

"There," Casper said awkwardly, patting her shoulder twice, the way you'd pat a horse. It should have been funny. It was funny. Juniper laughed in the middle of sobbing, which made her snort, which made it worse, and Hale watched them both with an expression of profound scholarly satisfaction, like a man watching his life's thesis defend itself.

* * *

Later—after the crying, after Hale had produced a flask of something that tasted like a barn fire and insisted they drink it "for the nerves"—Juniper stood before the hollow in the root-tree.

"Do I put him in?" she asked. "Is that what this is? Does he go back?"

"No," Hale said sharply, and then coughed, and then continued in the voice of a man who had spent forty years being interrupted mid-sentence and refused to allow death to do it to him now. "No. The hollow is where the ember was *first* set. It's not a bed. It's a whetstone. When the time comes—if the time comes—the ember goes into that hollow and the chapel does what it was built to do. It sharpens. It turns a little light into a blade." His eyes, sunken and yellowed as they were, fixed on hers. "But hear me, keeper. A whetstone takes something away to make an edge. Whatever you sharpen that flame against, you will not get back. Understand?"

"No," Juniper said honestly.

"Good," Hale said. "Only fools understand sacrifices in advance." He settled back against the pew. "Now. The rite of Unbinding. It'll take me the rest of the night to teach it to her—" he nodded at Casper "—and I haven't got many nights left, so pay attention, both of you, because I'm only saying it wrong once."

He began to speak the old words, low and halting, and Casper leaned in beside him, and Juniper watched the two of them bent together over the last secret of a dead order—one of them dying of forgetting, the other missing everything worth remembering—and something settled in her chest. Not peace. Peace was too soft a word. More like a keel. Like she'd been a boat drifting sideways her whole life and had finally, finally been given something heavy to hold in her belly.

She was a jailer's daughter. The lock inside the lock. The last page of a nine-hundred-year ledger, and the ledger wasn't finished.

Lantern stirred against her skin. And then—so faint she might have imagined it—he warmed. One degree. Two. A thread of gold showed through the ash-grey of his shell, thin as a hair, steady as a pulse.

Above them, muffled by thirty feet of stone and earth, Corvenna rang its midnight bells. All except one tower. The tower that had gone silent weeks ago, when its bell-ringer traded the memory of his trade for bread.

Juniper looked up at the roots, at the patient silver strings of the dead god's instrument, and made a promise she intended to keep in words, out loud, so the ash would remember:

"We're going to cut him free," she said. "Whoever's left of him. And then we're going to teach the world its own name again. Starting with the bells."

From the darkness of the stairwell behind them came a sound that did not belong in a holy place: a wet, dragging footstep, and the soft grey whisper of something that erased as it walked.

Hale stopped mid-word. Casper's hand found her knife.

"They found the door," Hale murmured. "Of course they found the door. I *told* the order the wards needed renewing. Nobody listens to archivists."

Juniper turned to face the dark, one hand curled protectively around the lantern, the ember's new-gold thread pulsing against her palm like a small heart deciding, against all odds, to keep beating.

"Stay behind me," Casper said.

"No," said Juniper, and was surprised at how easily it came. "This one's mine."
