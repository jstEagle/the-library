## 13. Hale's Confession

Brother Hale was dying in the back room of a candlemaker's shop, and he was complaining about it.

"You'd think," he said, propped against a sack of tallow ends with a blanket over his knees that someone—Juniper, probably—had tucked there without asking, "that after forty years of service to the silent god, I might be granted a dignified death. A bed. A last meal. Perhaps a choir."

"You left the order before I was born," Casper said. She was grinding something in a mortar that she had no intention of giving him, because the something was flour and honey and hope, and hope was not medicine. "You don't get a choir. You get me."

"Ah," Hale said. "The punishment continues even unto death." He smiled when he said it, which was how she knew the pain had gotten bad. Hale only joked when he was losing an argument with his own body.

The rider-touch wounds ran up his left arm like frost on a windowpane—gray veins branching from gray blotches, skin gone the color of old ash. Where the riders' fingers had closed around his wrist at the ford, trying to pull him off Tam's body, they had begun to erase him. Not kill. Erase. The difference mattered, and the difference was the whole reason Casper's hands were shaking as she worked.

She had seen what rider-touch did to objects. A door they brushed became a doorway to nowhere. A name carved on a bridge simply wasn't, and neither was the bridge's reason for being named. On flesh, it worked slower. It took the man first, then the memory of the man, then the space where the man had stood, and by the end even grief couldn't find purchase because grief needed a shape to hold.

Hale had perhaps two days. He knew it. He had known it since the ford, apparently, because he had spent the entire journey to Corvenna's undercity drinking from a flask and cataloguing his sins aloud like a merchant taking inventory.

Casper had assumed the inventory would be short. Brother Hale, last survivor of the Order of the Quiet Vigil, drunk archivist of a dead faith, keeper of a ledger nobody read. What sins could a man like that have? Drinking? Neglecting his filing?

She should have known better. Nobody survived twenty years of hiding the way she had without learning one truth above all others: the quiet ones were never quiet inside.

* * *

The candlemaker's cellar had been Juniper's find—a chapel of roots, the first place Veth had ever been worshipped, older than Grayhollow, older than the crown. Tree roots grew through the ceiling in pale braids, and the walls were hung with votive ribbons so old their prayers had worn down to single syllables. It smelled of wax and loam and, faintly, of the particular stillness that Casper had spent twenty years pretending she didn't miss.

The tiny dragon—Moth, because Juniper had named her Moth and Casper had lost the argument in under four seconds—slept in the crook of Hale's elbow, rising and falling with his breath. She had taken to him immediately. Dragons, Juniper said, liked people who were mostly done pretending.

Juniper herself sat on the stairs with the lantern-egg cradled in her lap, pretending very hard not to listen, which meant she was listening to every word. Casper let her. There were things in this room the girl deserved to hear, and things she didn't, and Casper no longer trusted herself to sort them.

"Hale." She set down the mortar. "Tell me about the Unbinding."

"Straight to business. You always were." He fumbled at his collar and drew out a cord, and on the cord hung a small iron key, green with age. "Under the third altar stone. There's a box. In the box is everything the Vigil ever learned about the Thorn—the rites, the warnings, the thing itself. The rite of Unbinding is the last page. It's written in Old Vethic, which you'll remember how to read the moment you see it, because you wrote half of it."

"I wrote—" Casper stopped. Her mouth had gone dry. "I've never seen Old Vethic in my life."

"No," Hale agreed gently. "You haven't. That's rather the point, isn't it?"

The silence in the root-chapel got very large. Even the votive ribbons seemed to stop moving.

"What did you do?" Casper asked. And then, because some part of her had known for twenty years and had simply kept the knowledge in the same locked room as everything else: "What did *we* do?"

Hale looked at his gray-spotted hand for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed—drunk-archivist gone, and underneath him someone else entirely, someone with a spine like a plumb line and a voice built for stone halls.

"On the night the Thorn first struck," he said, "I opened the king's gate."

* * *

He told it plainly, which was the worst way to hear it. No performance, no pleading. Just a man setting out facts on a table like a physician laying out instruments.

Thirty years ago, King Aldric had been young and frightened, and the kingdom had been at war with something it couldn't name—plagues that ate names, villages that woke up not knowing their own children, stars flickering at the edges of the sky like candles in a draft. The Vigil had been called to court. The Vigil had been asked to fix it.

"And we couldn't," Hale said. "We prayed and we fasted and we bled ourselves white over the altars, and Veth said nothing. Veth had been saying nothing for years, if you recall. The god of silence, silent. We told ourselves it was doctrine. We told ourselves silence was his language." His mouth twisted. "It wasn't. He was already wounded. Something had gotten its teeth into him at the world's edge, and he was holding it off with both hands, and he hadn't the breath left to tell us."

"How do you know that now?"

"Because I found the records after. Because I spent thirty years reading everything we burned." He laughed, short and terrible. "We burned so much, Casper. Do you know what a frightened church does with knowledge it can't use? It makes a bonfire of it and calls the smoke holy."

"The gate," Casper said. "You said you opened the king's gate."

"Aldric dreamed of a voice. Every night, the same voice, promising to save the kingdom if only the dreamer would let it in. The court physicians called it madness. The court mages called it possession and wanted to burn him, and Aldric was young enough to be terrified of his own protectors." Hale's eyes came up, gray and steady. "I was the Vigil's confessor. He confessed to me. And in the confessional, I told him how to open the door."

The words landed in Casper's chest like stones dropped down a well. She counted the seconds until they hit bottom.

"Why," she said. It wasn't really a question. But she needed to hear him say it, needed it more than she had ever needed anything, because if there was a reason—if there was even a bad reason—then the world still had rules.

"Because I believed it was Veth," Hale said simply. "A god who has gone silent, who comes to his faithful in dreams, who asks to be let closer—who wouldn't open the gate? I thought I was carrying out the will of the Quiet One. I thought the silence was a test, and here was the answer, and all I had to do was turn the key." He shook his head slowly. "It wasn't Veth. It was the thing that had been eating Veth, wearing his voice the way it wears Aldric's face now. I opened the king's gate, and I invited the Thorn into the last fortress between it and the world's memory. And it walked in, and it settled in behind the king's eyes, and it began to eat."

* * *

Juniper made a small sound on the stairs. Casper didn't look at her. If she looked at her, she would have to decide what her face was doing, and she didn't have a face for this. Twenty years of practicing invisibility, and there was no expression in her repertoire for *the man across from me helped unmake the world.*

"You didn't know," she said finally. It came out flat.

"No. I didn't know. Does that help?"

"It helps you sleep. Or it would, if you slept."

"I don't," Hale agreed. "I drink instead, which is sleep for cowards." He shifted against the tallow sack, and the gray marks crawled a little further up his arm, and Casper watched him refuse to look at them. "But here is the part you actually need, so attend. After the gate opened, the Vigil figured it out within a year. Too late, of course. The Thorn was rooted. Pulling it out would kill the king and take half the world's memory with it, like tearing ivy off a crumbling wall. So we did what churches do. We hid. We wrote everything down, sealed it, scattered it, and swore oaths of forgetting—actual oaths, binding ones, the kind that take the memory with them. Each of us carried one piece of the truth and forgot the rest, so that no torturer, no rider, no hungry god could ever gather it all from one skull."

"And yours was the gate."

"Mine was the gate." He touched his temple. "I forgot for thirty years, Casper. I woke every morning knowing only that I had failed at something enormous, the way you wake knowing you've forgotten an appointment. The guilt without the crime. I thought it was just age." His voice cracked, just slightly, on the absurdity. "Then the wards fell, and the riders came, and my piece of the oath broke open like an egg, and I remembered everything at once, and I vomited into a gutter in front of a fishmonger. Very dignified. The choir I mentioned."

Casper's hands had stopped shaking. That was the strange part. Somewhere in the last hour they had gone perfectly, terribly still, the way they went still before she did magic—the old magic, the priest-magic, the kind that cost.

"The rite," she said. "Unbinding. Tell me what it costs."

"Ah." Hale looked at her with something horribly like tenderness. "There it is. You always asked the price first. That's why Veth chose you, I think. Everyone else asked what the miracle could do. You asked what it would take out of you."

"*Hale.*"

"The rite requires a blade that remembers the god," he said. "Not a sword. A person. Someone Veth marked, someone who carries his memory in their bones—even buried, even burned, even sold. The Thorn grew inside Veth like a taproot in a tree. To sever it, you have to know the shape of the tree. You have to hold the whole of him in your mind while you cut—every prayer, every silence, every year—and the Thorn will fight you with exactly those memories. It will show you him alive. It will offer him back to you, whole, mid-laugh, the way he was before. And you have to keep cutting anyway, with your hands full of everything you loved, and at the end—" he drew a slow breath— "at the end, the rite burns the memories as fuel. All of them. The blade walks away empty. That's the price. That's the whole price, and it's everything."

The cellar was very quiet. Above them, faintly, the candle shop's floorboards creaked with the ordinary commerce of a city pretending nothing was wrong.

"That's why I forgot him," Casper said. It wasn't a question either. "Twenty years ago. My memories of Veth—they weren't stolen. They were the oath. I'm the blade, and somebody put me in a sheath."

"You are," Hale said. "And yes. And I'm sorry. I voted for it, for what that's worth. I argued you were too young to carry it awake." He managed a ghost of his usual dryness. "The order disagreed about many things, but we were unanimous that you were the best of us, and unanimous, as usual, about doing the cruelest possible thing to the best of us."

* * *

He taught her the rite that night, or the shape of it, because the true words lived under the altar stone and would come back to her hands like a language she'd spoken as a child. He made her recite the structure until she could do it blind: the naming of the tree, the finding of the root, the cut, the burning. He corrected her twice. He was a good teacher. He had always been a good teacher; it was the living he'd been bad at.

Near dawn, Juniper brought him water he didn't want and broth he couldn't keep down, and Moth woke and crawled up onto his chest and lay there with her wings folded, radiating a heat like a sun-warmed stone. Hale scratched her under the chin with his unmarked hand.

"There's a girl upstairs in the candle shop," he said to Casper, quietly, under the sound of Juniper's footsteps. "Sixteen years old, carrying the last fire in the world in a lantern, and she trusts you completely. I've watched you with her. You pretend you're reluctant. You're the least reluctant person I've ever met."

"She needs training, not sentiment."

"She needs you to live through this, and so do I need it, vicariously, being as I won't." He said it lightly, and it landed like an anvil. "So hear my last instruction, former novice, and don't argue, arguing is my job. When the Thorn offers you him—and it will, it knows exactly what you are—you will want to kneel. Kneeling will feel like coming home. And you must stand there, in the middle of everything you've lost, and cut anyway. Can you do that?"

Casper thought about lying to him. She genuinely considered it—one kindness, at the end, cheap and warm as the honey-flour in the mortar. Then she thought about what he'd said about cowardice and sleep, and she gave him the truth instead, because it was the only currency he'd ever respected.

"I don't know," she said.

"Good," Hale said. "'I don't know' is the only honest answer anyone's ever given that question. 'Yes' is pride, and pride dies at the first offer. 'I don't know' gets up every morning and tries again." He closed his eyes. "That'll do. That'll do nicely."

* * *

He died at noon, in the root-chapel, with the gray having reached his shoulder and begun to soften the outline of his face, so that looking at him was like looking at a sketch of Hale rather than Hale himself. He held on long enough to do one last thing, and of course it was the most infuriating thing available to him.

He insisted on absolution.

"I'm the last confessor of the Vigil," he said, gray-lipped, gripping Casper's wrist with the hand that still entirely existed. "And I am dying with an unconfessed sin on my soul, and I will not meet whatever's on the other side of this with the books unbalanced. So. Kneel down here, and hear it properly, and give me absolution, because I am not letting you carry my guilt out of this room along with everything else."

"Hale, I'm not a priest anymore. I'm barely a charm-seller."

"You're the last priest of Veth whether you like it or not, and I'm past caring about doctrine. Kneel, Thackeray, before I die out of spite."

So Casper knelt on the root-chapel floor, in the smell of wax and old prayers, and took his hands, and he confessed—he confessed the gate, and the pride, and the thirty years of drinking instead of remembering, and the specific and unforgivable sin of having voted to bury the truth in a girl too young to vote against it. His voice got smaller as the gray climbed. By the end he was whispering, and the words were less sentences than intentions, and Casper leaned close to catch them.

And she gave him absolution. She didn't know the form anymore—Veth had taken the words with her memories—but she knew the shape of it, the way you know the shape of a house you grew up in even after it burns. She put her hands on his head and she said, in plain Grayhollow speech, the truest thing she could find:

"You didn't know. You tried. You're forgiven—not because it was small, but because you spent your whole life paying for it, and the debt is paid, and you can put it down now. Put it down, old man. Go quietly. He was the god of quiet. He'll be glad of the company."

Hale smiled. It was the real smile, the archivist's smile, the one that meant he'd caught an error in the text and found it delightful.

"Mid-absolution," he murmured. "Dying mid-absolution. There's a joke in there. Give me a moment and I'll—"

He didn't get the moment. The gray reached his heart, and he went the way he had lived at the end: complaining slightly, trusting completely, unfinished.

Moth cried once—a thin, bright sound like a struck glass—and then pressed her small head against his cooling hand and stayed there.

* * *

Afterward, Casper sat on the chapel steps with the iron key in her fist and the whole of her purpose rearranged around a hole shaped like a grumpy old man. Juniper came and sat beside her without asking, which was the girl's particular genius—arriving without asking.

"He forgave you," Juniper said. "At the end. I heard it."

"He asked me for forgiveness. I gave it. Whether it took—" Casper turned the key over. Green iron, older than both of them, heavier than it looked. "Forgiveness is strange that way. You can hand it to someone too late for it to matter, and it matters anyway. Maybe especially then."

Juniper was quiet a moment. Then, in the careful voice she used when she was about to be braver than the adults: "The box under the altar. The rite. It burns your memories of him. All of them. That's what Hale said."

"That's what he said."

"So when it's done, you won't remember any of this. Me. The road. Any of it." The girl's hands tightened on the lantern. "You'll walk away from the throne room a stranger."

"Probably."

"And you're going to do it anyway."

Casper looked at her—at the soot on her jaw, the burn-scarred knuckles, the ember-light catching the underside of her chin—and felt the full weight of what Hale had called her least reluctant quality settle over her like a mantle.

"Yes," she said. "But not today. Today we dig up a box, and today you eat something, and today we figure out how to get into a palace grown over with thorns." She stood, and offered the girl her hand, and found that her voice had gone rough without permission. "Come on, Juniper. We've got a god to save and a king to free, and I intend to complain about both, extensively, in his honor."

They went down together into the dark under the altar stone, toward the last page of a dead order's handwriting—and above them, far away in the direction of the palace, the sky flickered once, like a star deciding whether tonight was the night it finally went out.
