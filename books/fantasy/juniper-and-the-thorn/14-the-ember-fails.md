## 14. The Ember Fails

The lantern went dark in Juniper's hands at the exact moment the bells stopped screaming.

She stood in the middle of Sable Street, in a crowd of maybe two hundred people who had been running from the palace purge—running until her fire had poured out of the egg-ember like a held breath finally released, washing over them, turning them invisible to the gray riders sweeping the avenue. Two hundred people who now stood blinking in the sudden ordinary lamplight, wondering why they'd been afraid.

The dragon inside the lantern was so small she could barely see him. A thread of gold no longer than her thumb, curled tight around the cold glass, his light guttering like a candle in a doorway.

"No," Juniper whispered. "No, no, wake up. I'm sorry. Wake up."

He didn't. His little chest rose and fell, rose and fell, each breath slower than the last, and the glow beneath his scales had gone the color of old ash.

Around her, the crowd was dispersing into doorways and alleys, murmuring thanks they didn't fully understand. An old woman pressed a heel of bread into Juniper's hands and said, "For whatever you did, dear," and then she was gone too, and Juniper was alone on Sable Street with a dying ember and the taste of copper in her mouth.

She had spent it. All of it. Every scrap of fire the keepers had bled and starved and died to protect, and she had poured it out in one great reckless wave because there were people running and riders behind them and she hadn't been able to *not*.

And now the one thing that could cut the Thorn free of the king—the whole reason for the road, the ford, Tam's body left face-down in the shallows, Hale's confession still wet on Casper's ears—was a cold coal in a cracked lantern.

Juniper sat down right there in the street, in the mud between the cobbles, and held the lantern against her chest, and did not cry, because crying felt like it would take something else she couldn't afford to lose.

*You've doomed everything,* said the voice in her head that sounded like every keeper elder she'd never met. *You had one task. One. Keep it burning until the rite. And you spent it on strangers.*

They hadn't felt like strangers. That was the terrible part. When the riders came down the avenue with their faces like smudged chalk and the crowd broke apart screaming, Juniper hadn't thought about the rite or the plan or Casper's careful voice saying *we spend nothing we don't have to spend*. She'd thought about a boy near the front, maybe ten years old, who had turned around to look at the riders instead of running, frozen the way small things freeze when the big thing is coming.

So she'd opened the lantern.

It had been the easiest thing she'd ever done. That was what shamed her most—not recklessness, but how *natural* it had felt, like the fire had been waiting all along to be given away.

The dragon stirred, just barely, and made a sound like a kettle deciding against boiling.

"I know," Juniper told him. "I'm sorry. Sleep. Just—don't go out. Please don't go out."

She got up. Her legs shook. Somewhere across the city, the palace glowed with its stolen starlight, and the last stars above Corvenna hung by threads, and somewhere in it all Casper was waiting for her with the rite of Unbinding folded up in her coat like a letter from a dead man.

Juniper looked at the lantern. Looked at the palace. Looked at the alley.

She ran down the alley.

* * *

She ran for a long time. Corvenna was built like a maze designed by someone who hated mazes, all crooked lanes and staircases that went nowhere, and Juniper knew cities the way she knew forests—from the ground up, from the hiding places—and she found herself a good one: an abandoned dyeworks under the aqueduct, all cold vats and dried pigment stains like old bruises on the walls. Blue and rust and a deep violet nobody used anymore.

She wedged herself into the corner of the largest vat, where the curve of it hid her from the doorway, and set the lantern on her knees.

"Here's the thing," she said to the dragon, because talking to him was easier than thinking. "Here's the thing. If you're gone, then the rite can't happen. And if the rite can't happen, then the Thorn eats the king's whole mind, and then it eats everything else, and everyone I've met since Grayhollow will forget they ever met me, which—" her voice wobbled, "—which is fine, actually, that's normal, people always forget me eventually, that's just how it goes."

The dragon breathed once. Slowly.

"But you're not supposed to be gone. You're the last one. You're the *last ember*, do you understand? There were hundreds of you once. Whole mountains of you. And the keepers kept you, generation after generation, and I'm the end of the line, and I *spent* you on—" she gestured helplessly at the wall, at the city, at everything, "—on a Tuesday. On a random awful Tuesday in Corvenna. They would have wanted me to save you for the king. For Veth. For the world. Not for a boy who froze in the street."

But he had frozen. And she had burned. And if she lived to be a hundred years old she knew she would do it again, and that was the problem, wasn't it? That was the whole rotten problem. She wasn't a keeper. Keepers were supposed to hold the line. She was just a girl who couldn't watch people get hurt.

Her own past stayed blank behind her eyes, as it always did—the orphan road, the nameless years before the borderlands, the reason a sixteen-year-old ended up alone carrying the world's last fire. Even her gift worked backwards: she could give other people their memories, hand them back their mothers' lullabies and their dead brothers' faces, and hers stayed an empty room with the furniture missing.

"Everyone gets to remember something," she told the vat ceiling bitterly. "Even the city. Even the *bread*. They trade memories here for loaves, did you know that? At least they get to choose. I don't even get a choice. I just get the hole."

The dragon didn't answer. His light had gone from ash to almost nothing.

Footsteps, then. Careful ones, on the dyeworks stairs—not the heavy tread of soldiers, not the wrongness of gray riders, whose steps sounded like pages turning. These were boots worn soft, placed deliberately, the walk of someone who had learned to move quietly through places she wasn't welcome.

Juniper knew those footsteps better than she knew anything else about the last few months.

She pulled her knees up higher, as if the vat's curve could hide her from Casper Thackeray, which it could not, because Casper had spent twenty years finding hidden things in Grayhollow and had once located Juniper in a hayloft during a hailstorm purely by listening to her breathing.

The footsteps stopped at the edge of the vat.

"You know," Casper said, "I've been robbed twice, stabbed once, and threatened by a mercenary captain with a crossbow this evening, and none of it was as exhausting as tracking you through this city. You hide well. You hide *annoyingly* well."

"I'm not coming back," Juniper said to her knees.

"Mm." Casper climbed up and sat on the rim of the vat, which groaned but held, and settled her coat around her like a woman settling in for a long sermon. In the dim light her face was tired and lined and stubborn. "Well. Then I'll just sit here. It's a nice vat. Very atmospheric. Smells like someone's abandoned ambitions."

"Casper—"

"Hale gave me the rite before he died. Did I tell you? Folded it into my coat like contraband. Half of it's in Old Liturgical, which I haven't read in twenty years, so honestly, you running away might be a mercy to us both." She paused. "I also brought food. Which you're not getting, because you're not coming back."

Juniper's stomach betrayed her with a sound like a small landslide.

"There it is," Casper said. "The famous keeper resolve."

"Stop it." Juniper's voice cracked. "Stop being funny. I ruined it. Do you understand? I *ruined it*. The ember's nearly dead. I spent it on—I don't even know how many people, two hundred, three hundred—it doesn't matter, because without the fire the rite is just words, and the words are just words, and the world ends because I couldn't stand to watch one horrible Tuesday happen to some strangers."

She was crying now, finally, furiously, the way she never let herself cry on the road.

"And the worst part," she said, "the actual worst part, is that I'd do it again. Tomorrow. I'd spend you again, and again, and again, until there was nothing, because I can't *not*. And a real keeper would have held the lantern shut. A real keeper would have watched them die and kept the fire for the god. So it shouldn't have been me. I should never have been the one carrying it. I'm the wrong person. I've been the wrong person my whole life, I just didn't know it until now."

Silence. The aqueduct dripped somewhere, patient as a clock.

Then Casper said, quietly, "Do you want to hear about the worst thing I ever did?"

Juniper wiped her nose. "...What?"

"The worst thing I ever did. It's a short story. Well—no, it's a long story, but the ending's short." Casper leaned back against the vat's rim. "Twenty-two years ago, I was the chosen blade of a god. Actual god, actual blade, actual chosen. Veth saw everything the world forgot and kept it safe, and I was his sword-arm, and I loved him the way you love a sunrise—you don't question it, you just arrange your whole life around it happening again tomorrow.

"And then the Thorn struck him for the first time. Inside King Aldric. I was there. I *felt* it—felt my god scream through every ward in the world—and do you know what I did?"

Juniper shook her head.

"I ran." Casper's voice didn't waver at all, which somehow made it worse. "Not tactically. Not strategically. I dropped my blade in a temple courtyard and I ran, because I was afraid, and I hid my magic under floorboards and sold fake charms to gullible farmers for twenty years and told myself I was being *careful*, being *wise*, when really I was just a coward with excellent bookkeeping. My god was being eaten alive and I filed myself under 'safe.'"

"That's not—" Juniper started.

"It is, though. That's the whole story. And here's the part I need you to hear, so listen." Casper leaned forward, and in the dark her eyes caught what little light the dragon had left. "I have spent twenty-two years wishing I had been the kind of person who runs *toward* the screaming. I would trade every year I hid for one moment of having been brave and stupid and wrong. You—" she pointed at Juniper, at the lantern, at the whole miserable vat, "—you spent the last ember of a dead god's fire to keep a frightened child from being erased. That is the single most keeper-like thing anyone has done in a generation. The elders would have clutched their ledgers and fainted, and then, privately, in the ledger where no one could see, they'd have written it down as the finest entry in the book."

"They needed the fire for the rite."

"Yes. And now we need a new plan, because plans are like charms—most of them barely work, and the ones that do work are never the ones you expected." Casper reached out and took the lantern gently from Juniper's hands, holding it up to the faint light. The dragon lay coiled at the bottom, thin as wire. "Look at him. He's not dead. He's *resting*. There's a difference, and I've had twenty years to learn the difference, because I spent two decades pretending a banked fire was a dead one."

Juniper stared at her. "You think he'll come back?"

"I think fire is the least honest thing in the world and the most forgiving. It goes out and it comes back and it never holds a grudge about it." Casper handed the lantern back. "But that's not why I came."

"Then why?"

"Because when I was sixteen, and afraid, and certain I had ruined everything, there was no one who came and sat on the edge of my vat and told me the truth." Casper's voice softened, just slightly, the way it only ever did for Juniper and the dragon and no one else alive. "There was just silence, and a god going quiet, and a girl deciding the silence meant she deserved it. So. Here I am. Sitting on your vat. Telling you the truth: you are not the wrong person. You are the *only* person. The elders chose the girl who couldn't watch people burn, because the ember was never meant to be hoarded, Juniper. It was meant to be *given*. That's what fire is for. They just hoped it wouldn't cost this much."

Juniper hugged the lantern to her chest. The dragon shifted, minutely, and for one heartbeat—a single, impossible heartbeat—his scales flickered gold.

"He moved," she breathed.

"He heard you," Casper said. "Fire listens. Now." She stood, joints cracking like green wood, and held out a hand and a wrapped bundle that smelled, gloriously, of Corvennan meat pies. "Come and eat, and then come and hear the new plan. Because while you were hiding in a dye vat feeling sorry for yourself—which, for the record, you did beautifully, very thorough—I found out something about the palace."

Juniper took the pie. "What?"

Casper smiled, and it was not entirely a comforting smile.

"The Thorn knows we're coming. It's known since the ford. And tonight, for the first time, it's *afraid*—because the last star above Corvenna isn't dying anymore, little keeper. Tonight, it started to grow."
