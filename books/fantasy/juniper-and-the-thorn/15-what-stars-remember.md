## 15. What Stars Remember

The shrine beneath the hill had no name anymore. That was the first thing Casper noticed when they climbed down into it—the absence where a name should have been, like a tooth-socket in the tongue. The walls were old Vethic stone, gray as ash, carved once with the god's silent alphabet, but the letters had been eaten hollow. She ran her fingers along them and felt nothing but smoothness, the way you'd feel a scar where a tattoo used to be.

"Well," she said, because someone had to say something, and twenty years of selling charms to nervous housewives had made her the sort of person who filled silence the way other people filled cellars with pickles. "It's cozy."

"It's a hole," said Juniper.

"It's a hole with excellent acoustics." Casper's voice bounced back at her off the domed ceiling, sounding braver than she felt. "That's practically architecture."

Juniper didn't laugh. She hadn't laughed properly in two days—not since the ember had guttered in her lantern down to something the size of a dying firefly, not since the crowd in Corvenna's lower market had scattered screaming from the palace purge and Juniper had spent nearly everything she had left to hold the panic back, to give three hundred strangers one more hour of remembering who they were. The dragon—moth-small, iridescent, currently asleep in the crook of Casper's elbow like a warm coin—had dimmed with it. Its scales, which had once thrown light like spilled gold, now looked like the inside of an oyster shell: faint, pearly, apologetic.

The ember was failing. And without it, there was no blade to cut the Thorn free of King Aldric, no fire bright enough to burn in that throne room of thorns. There was only Casper, Juniper, a very small sleeping dragon, and a hole in a hill that had forgotten its own name.

Casper set the lantern on the altar. The altar was a slab of black stone, and someone—long ago, before the eating—had carved into its edge the words SHE WHO REMEMBERS PAYS. She'd read it twice before understanding it was not decoration. It was a price tag.

"Brother Hale's book says the rite is called the Tithe of Stars," she said. "It's forbidden. It was forbidden even when the order was alive, which tells you how forbidden things work—they're always the ones that function."

"Function how?" Juniper's voice was flat. Tired. Sixteen years old and carrying the weight of the last living ember of dragon-fire, and she still managed to sound like a girl being asked to do arithmetic at dinner.

"You feed it," Casper said. "Memories. Real ones. The ember eats what we eat—it lives on meaning. That's why it's been starving. We've been feeding it road dust and fear." She turned the lantern so the ember caught the light, that pitiful spark. "You can't feed it someone else's past. It has to be given. Freely. That's the whole theology of it, apparently. The stars themselves are memories that chose to burn."

Juniper stared at her. Then at the lantern. Then at her, again, with the particular expression of a girl doing math she doesn't like.

"No," she said.

"Juniper—"

"No. Absolutely not. You don't get to—" Her voice cracked and she hated it, Casper could see her hating it. "You don't get to do the noble thing. I've met your noble thing. Your noble thing is lying to soldiers about ward-sigils and overcharging for love charms. You are *not* allowed to be a martyr, you're a *shopkeeper*."

"I am," Casper agreed, "a shopkeeper with a very specific inventory." She sat down on the altar's step, careful of the dragon, and patted the stone beside her. After a moment, sulking, Juniper sat too. Their shoulders touched. In the last weeks they had developed the habit of touching shoulders the way other people held hands—casually, constantly, as if confirming the other was still solid.

"Tell me what you'd lose," Juniper said finally. "Exactly. Not the poetry version."

"The poetry version is shorter." Casper looked up at the eaten letters on the dome. "My name. All of it—Thackeray, Wren, the whole ledger. Twenty years of Grayhollow. Every customer, every charm, every night I spent pretending my hands weren't burning." She paused. "And you."

Juniper went very still.

"The face-knowledge," Casper said quietly. "How I know you. Why I trust you. The whole shape of you in my head. That goes too. It has to—you're the largest thing in there worth eating."

The silence in the shrine was total. Somewhere above them, wind moved through dead grass, making a sound like pages turning.

"So you'd walk out of here," Juniper said, "and look at me like a stranger."

"Worse. I'd look at you like a stranger and follow you anyway, because apparently some part of me will still know the mission even if it doesn't know your birthday." Casper smiled. "Which is the fourteenth of Firstbloom, by the way. You told me on the road to Saltmere while eating an apple entirely wrong, core-first, like a small feral animal. I intend to remember that specifically, out of spite, for as long as the eating allows."

"Stop it."

"You chewed the seeds. Who chews the seeds?"

"*Stop*—" But Juniper was laughing now, or something adjacent to laughing, wet and furious, and she grabbed Casper's sleeve with both hands like a girl holding onto a door in a storm. "There has to be another way. There's always another way. You're the one who says that. You say it constantly. You said it about the ford, you said it about the tax collector in Marrow's Cross—"

"And then at the ford I burned half a river, and the tax collector turned out to be a rider wearing a man's skin, and both times the other way was worse." Casper covered one of Juniper's hands with her own. Her palm, she noted, had stopped trembling somewhere around the drowned shrine, and hadn't started again. Whatever else the Thorn had taken from her over the years, it had apparently left this: a terrible, settled calm, arriving exactly when needed, like a debt collector with good timing. "Juniper. Listen to me. I spent twenty years hiding. Do you know what I was actually hiding from? Not the soldiers. Not the pyres. I was hiding from the arithmetic. From knowing, every single day, exactly what my silence cost. I did the sums at the drowned shrine and I'm done pretending I can't count."

"That's not fair."

"No," Casper agreed. "It isn't. Almost nothing about this is fair. The world lost its name; you lost your childhood; a god got eaten by his own king. Fairness left the country some time ago and didn't leave a forwarding address." She lifted the lantern between them. The ember pulsed—weakly, hopefully, like a dog hearing the word *walk*. "But this part can be simple. This part is just a transaction. I've run a shop for twenty years. I know how to make change."

* * *

She prepared the way she prepared everything: badly at first, then precisely.

Hale's book gave the rite in the old silent tongue, which Casper could still read because the order had burned it into her before it burned anything else. She chalked the circle on the black altar—chalk, of all things, the humblest magic there was, the magic of schoolrooms and shop floors—and set the lantern at its heart. The dragon woke, stretched wings the size of her thumbnail, and regarded her with eyes like drops of molten glass.

"Don't look at me like that," she told it. "You're the beneficiary."

It chirped. It had learned, in its brief and moth-like life, exactly which of her tones meant *treat* and which meant *trouble*, and it had correctly identified neither.

Juniper stood outside the chalk line with her arms crossed and her jaw set, and she had not stopped crying, though she'd gotten quiet about it, which was somehow much worse. "Say it again," she demanded. "What you'll remember. After."

"After? A mission. A city. A king with thorns behind his eyes. A girl with a lantern." Casper tested each word like a stair. "I won't know why I trust you. But I will trust you. I've built it deep enough that the roots survive the fire. That's the trick of it, little keeper—that's the trick of *everything*. What matters most grows downward."

"That's not a real saying."

"I just invented it. Give it time."

Juniper wiped her face with the heel of her hand, leaving a streak of road-dirt that made her look approximately six years old. "If you come out of that circle and you don't know me," she said, "I'm going to tell you everything. All of it. The apple. The ford. Tam. The way you snore—"

"I do not snore."

"—like a cart with a broken axle, and you talk in your sleep about *inventory*, actual inventory, 'the blue candles are miscounted,' it's the most depressing thing I've ever heard—"

"All right, all right—"

"—and I'm going to make you learn me all over again, and it's going to take *forever*, and you're going to deserve it."

Casper looked at her—at this fierce, orphaned, seed-chewing child the world had handed her at the exact moment the world ended—and found, to her complete lack of surprise, that she was ready. That was the secret shame and secret grace of it. She had been ready since the wards broke. Since the stars went out. Since a bleeding girl collapsed through her door and made hiding impossible.

"Then that's my after," she said. "Being learned. Most people never get anyone willing to do the work." She stepped into the circle. "Light it."

* * *

The rite was not dramatic. That was the second thing she would have told anyone, if there had been anyone left to tell, and if she had retained the telling.

There was no thunder. No choir of vanished gods. The ember simply opened—like a mouth, like a page, like a door in a house you thought you knew—and began, gently, terribly, to *read* her.

The first thing it took was small, almost polite: the smell of Grayhollow's morning markets, wet wool and frying onions. She felt it lift out of her the way a splinter leaves, and the ember brightened by a hair. Then the taste of the cheap wine she bought from Ollin's cellar. Then Ollin himself, his bald head, his terrible jokes about priests. Gone. Brighter.

"Oh," she said aloud, understanding. "It's hungry-hungry. Not polite-hungry."

She fed it deliberately after that, because the alternative was letting it graze, and a grazing flame takes the strangest things. She fed it her shop—the counter worn smooth by twenty years of elbows, the drawer of mismatched buttons she'd never once used, the sign that read THACKERAY'S CHARMS in paint she'd mixed herself. She fed it the faces of customers whose names she'd never known but whose worries she'd carried: the widow afraid of her own attic, the boy who wanted courage for the army, the woman who came every week to buy protection from a husband already dead. Each memory went out of her like a coal passed hand to hand, and the ember drank, and grew, and the light in the dead shrine strengthened until the eaten letters on the walls threw shadows again.

Her twenties went. Her thirties. The long gray middle of her life, the hiding years, the ache of a life half-lived—all of it poured out, and strange to say, it hurt least of all. Those years had been a coat worn too long anyway. The ember took them and glowed like a small sun, and the dragon crept to the edge of the chalk and pressed itself against the warmth, scales kindling, growing—not vast, not yet, but *less apologetic*, which was something.

Then the ember reached for the deeper shelves, and Casper gripped the altar and let it.

Veth. The silent god. His presence like standing inside a held breath. The ordination, the vows, the years of being his blade and his ear. The drowning shrine. The moment of the Thorn's first strike, and her running—she let it take that too, the cowardice, the fear, the whole shameful architecture of her guilt, because a blade does not need its rust. The flame roared. The shrine lit white. For one impossible moment Casper saw the dome overhead not as eaten stone but as a sky, and understood suddenly, completely, what the stars had always been: not fires, but *rememberings*, millions of them, a world refusing to forget itself.

And then it reached for Juniper.

Not the mission-Juniper, not the lantern or the bloodline or the plan. It reached for the *girl*: the apple eaten core-first, the fourteenth of Firstbloom, the snoring accusation, the shoulder against hers on a hundred cold miles, the exact weight of her head when she fell asleep against Casper's arm on watch. The best things. The load-bearing things.

Casper held them for one breath. Two.

Then she thought: *what matters most grows downward,* and she opened her hands, all of her hands, every hidden hand she had ever kept, and let the flame have everything.

* * *

When it was done, the ember hung in the lantern like a captive star, and the dragon had grown to the size of a cat, wings folded, watching her with ancient patient eyes. The chalk circle was scorched black. The air smelled of ozone and, oddly, of bread.

A girl stood outside the circle. Young. Dark-haired. Tear-streaked, dirt-streaked, gripping a lantern with white knuckles, looking at Casper with an expression of such naked hope and terror that Casper's chest ached in a way she couldn't source.

Casper knew her the way you know a load-bearing wall: not by its story, but by what would fall without it.

"I know you," Casper said slowly, testing the words and finding them true in a way that bypassed her mind entirely. "I don't know *how* I know you. But I'd follow you into a burning building. I think I may literally be planning to."

The girl made a sound that was half sob, half laugh, and crossed the ruined circle in three strides and hugged her hard enough to crack ribs, and the cat-sized dragon draped itself across both their shoulders like a warm scarf, and Casper held on.

"My name," she said into the girl's hair. "Do I have one? Did I have one?"

"Casper," the girl said thickly. "Casper Thackeray. And you snore, and you miscount candles, and you're going to learn me all over again and it's going to take forever."

"Then we'd best start." Casper pulled back, wiped the girl's face with her thumb, briskly, the way you'd straighten a shop sign. "Name first. Juniper, yes? It suits you. Now—tell me about this king we're going to rob of his thorns. And start from the beginning." She lifted the lantern, and its new starlight fell across the eaten walls of the dead shrine, and for the first time in twenty years Casper Thackeray—whoever she was now—walked toward the dark without hiding anything at all.

Above them, far away, one dead star flickered—hesitated—and remembered how to burn.
