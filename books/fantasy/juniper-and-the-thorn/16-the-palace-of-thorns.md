## 16. The Palace of Thorns

The palace gates of Corvenna had once been gilded. Juniper knew this because half the gold still clung to them, flaking like old paint, and the other half had been swallowed by brambles so black they looked cut from midnight itself. The thorns were the size of her forearm and curved like hooks, and where they pierced the stone, the stone wept a fine gray dust that smelled — she couldn't explain how she knew this — like forgetting.

"Right," said the woman beside her, in Casper's voice, with Casper's face, looking at everything else with a stranger's eyes. "And you're certain I agreed to this."

"You wrote it down," Juniper said. She patted the satchel at her hip, where a folded page lived among the charms and dried bread crusts. "In your own hand. *If I forget who you are, let me tell you about the ford, and if that doesn't work, feed me soup.*"

"That does sound like me." The stranger-that-was-Casper studied the gates. Her hands, scarred and ink-stained and familiar as Juniper's own heartbeat until three days ago, flexed once at her sides. "I don't remember writing it. I don't remember much of anything past—" She stopped. She'd been stopping there for three days.

"The shrine," Juniper finished gently. "You stopped at the shrine."

"I stopped at the shrine," Casper agreed, and something in her jaw set, some stubbornness that had survived even when the memories holding it hadn't. "Well. Let's go meet whatever's eating my god."

She started forward, and Juniper grabbed her sleeve.

"Wait. You have to let me do the talking. Not because you can't talk — you talk fine, you're very good at talking, honestly sometimes too good — but because the palace listens." She nodded up at the brambles crowning the gate. They hadn't moved yet. That was something. "It grows toward sound. Toward *remembered* sound, Hale's notes said. If you walk through narrating your own life, it'll drink every word before it reaches your ears."

Casper looked at her for a long moment. Even without twenty years of shared road behind her eyes, she managed an expression Juniper recognized: the one that meant *you're being ridiculous, child, but go on.*

"And what will you be doing while I'm not talking?"

"Telling you who you are." Juniper lifted the lantern. Inside it, the ember burned low and stubborn, a coal wrapped in a dragon no bigger than her two cupped hands — except that the dragon was no longer quite that small. Three days ago he'd been moth-sized. Last night he'd stretched wings across the width of her palm, membrane-thin and glowing faintly at the edges, like paper held before a flame. He'd done it while staring at the palace, and Juniper had chosen not to think too hard about why. "That was the deal. You gave your memories to light him back up. So I carry them for you. All of them. Every step."

Something moved behind Casper's borrowed eyes — not memory, exactly, but the shape where memory should sit, the way a bed stays warm after its sleeper rises.

"All right," Casper said quietly. "Tell me who I am, then."

Juniper took a breath, pushed open the gate, and began.

* * *

The first corridor had been a servants' passage once. Now it was a throat of thorns, arched overhead so tightly that the torchlight from their own little lantern barely reached the walls. Brambles creaked somewhere above them, slow as glaciers, patient as rot.

"Your name is Casper Thackeray," Juniper said, keeping her voice low and steady, a lamp carried against wind. "You were a battle-priest of Veth, which means you fought for the quiet god, the one nobody remembers anymore because — well. Because of what's at the end of this hallway, basically. You sold charms in Grayhollow for twenty years and they barely worked, and you felt terrible about it, and you kept doing it anyway, because the alternative was being noticed, and your one rule was *never be noticed.*"

"A sensible rule," Casper murmured. "I find I respect my past self."

"You broke it for me." Juniper ducked under a thorn as long as a sickle. It passed close enough to tug at her hair, and she pretended it hadn't. "Riders came into your shop chasing me. Gray ones, faceless. You killed two of them with magic you'd sworn off, and then you complained about it the entire way out of the city, but you never once suggested going back."

"Complained how?"

"Lots of muttering about 'twenty years of discipline.' You called the egg a 'fire hazard' for a week. Then it hatched and you named it Cinders and fed it bacon off your own plate when you thought I was asleep."

"I did not name a dragon Cinders."

"He answers to it. Watch." Juniper raised her wrist, where the small dragon clung, drowsing in a spiral of his own tail. "Cinders."

One amber eye opened. The tiny head turned toward Casper with an expression of such naked adoration that the stranger-with-her-face actually stepped back and bumped a bramble, which hissed faintly where it touched her cloak.

"Oh, don't look at me like that," Casper told the dragon sternly. "I don't know you."

"He knows *you,*" Juniper said. "That's rather the point of all this."

They walked on. Behind them, softly, a bramble uncurled and followed.

* * *

The second corridor opened into what had been a gallery — portraits still hung along the walls, though the thorns had grown through most of the canvases, bursting out of painted chests and throats like the pictures themselves had been eaten from inside. In the gaps between paintings, the wall showed older plaster, and on the older plaster, someone had scratched words. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Names, mostly, and fragments: *my daughter's laugh was—* and *we used to keep bees in—* and one, repeated over and over in a shaking hand, *I HAD A SISTER I HAD A SISTER I HAD A*

Juniper made herself look away.

"This part," she said, and found her voice had gone thin. She cleared her throat and tried again. "This part I like telling. It's the ford."

"The ford?"

"The whole caravan nearly died at the river crossing. Riders ambushed us at dawn. And you — you stood up on the bank in front of forty mercenaries who'd forgotten their own contracts, and you did magic like I'd never seen. Like nothing in any story I've ever heard. Light came off you like heat off a forge, and you spoke a word I didn't understand, and the riders just — came apart. Like ash in rain." Juniper smiled despite herself, despite the scratched walls, despite everything. "Tam always said you looked like a hearth fire that had decided to take up arms."

"Tam?"

Her smile faltered. "He held the line so the rest of us could live. He didn't." She touched the scarred sergeant's iron pin, still fastened to her collar where she'd sewn it. "You told me afterward that grief is just love with nowhere to put itself. You said it like you'd learned it the hard way, more than once."

Casper had gone still. Not confused-still — something else. She pressed her palm flat against the wall, beside the scratches, careful to avoid the thorns threading between them.

"I don't remember saying it," she said slowly. "But it sounds true. It sounds like the sort of thing that costs something to learn." Her fingers curled, and she took her hand back. "Keep talking, Juniper. Whatever you do, keep talking."

So Juniper talked. About the drowned shrine, and the memory Casper had relived there — she told only the shape of it, not the wound of it, because some things belonged to their owner even when the owner couldn't claim them. About Brother Hale, drunk and brilliant, dying in a cellar with absolution on his lips. About the chapel of roots beneath the city, where Juniper had learned that her bloodline had been jailers, that the ember in her lantern was the blade meant to cut a god free, that her whole orphan life had been a key carried by people who'd forgotten they were a lock.

"And here's the funny part," Juniper said, because the corridor had grown too quiet and quiet corridors in this place grew teeth. "You spent your memories to relight the ember. Your name, your twenty years, your — me. You knew that's what it would cost. You did it anyway, and when I found you afterward, sitting in the ash of the shrine, you asked me who I was. And do you know what you said when I told you?"

"No," said Casper. "But I suspect I'm about to."

"You said, '*Good. Someone ought to know me, even if it isn't me.*'" Juniper's voice cracked on the last word, and she laughed at herself, wetly, wiping her face with her sleeve. "Honestly. Who says things like that? You're insufferable. You were insufferable before, too, you just hid it better."

Ahead of them, the corridor ended at a door of black iron, and the door was breathing.

Not metaphorically. It swelled and shrank, slow, in time with something vast below or beyond, and the brambles around its frame pulsed faintly with a light that wasn't light — a gray glow, starless, hungry. The last star, Juniper thought. Somewhere above all this stone, the last star in the sky was guttering, and the Thorn was drinking it down, and when it finished there would be nothing left anywhere that remembered being anything.

Cinders stirred on her wrist. He unfolded his wings — both of them now, full and trembling, wide as her forearm — and the membranes caught the gray glow and threw it back changed: warm. Gold-edged. For one heartbeat the whole corridor remembered sunlight.

The brambles recoiled, hissing, from the door.

"Well," said Casper, staring at the dragon with an expression that needed no memories behind it at all. "Hello, Cinders."

The dragon preened.

* * *

Beyond the iron door lay the great stair, and the great stair lay inside the throne room's approach, and the approach had become a garden of the Thorn. Black branches wove a cathedral ceiling sixty feet overhead. Between the pillars, the stolen starlight hung in jars — actual jars, glass and brass, glowing cold white — and each jar hummed a different fragment of sound: a lullaby, a market-day shout, a name called across a field. The world's memory, bottled and shelved like preserves.

Juniper walked with the lantern high and her voice steady, and she was deep into the story of the palace purge — *and you found me after, when I ran, and you didn't scold me, you told me about your own cowardice instead, you said fear is honest but it makes a terrible master* — when Casper suddenly stopped walking.

"What?" Juniper whispered.

"My hands are shaking," Casper said. She held them out, turned them over, examined them like a scholar reading a text. "Not from fear. From — recognition. My body knows this place even if I don't." Her gaze drifted up to the jars, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Twenty years ago I stood in a hall like this one. I can feel the shape of it. I knelt here. Or — I was supposed to kneel. And I ran instead."

Juniper's chest tightened. "That's the shrine memory. The one you wouldn't let me tell."

"Then I suppose I've just told myself." Casper exhaled, long and slow, and squared her shoulders, and Juniper watched a stranger assemble a soldier out of spare parts and sheer will. "Whatever I did back then, I have a chance to do differently. That's more than most people get. Come on."

They climbed. The humming jars grew louder, and beneath them, deeper, came another sound: a voice, or many voices braided into one, speaking in the tone of a king giving a beloved and terrible decree. Aldric's voice. The Thorn's voice. Both.

At the top of the stair, the last doors waited — taller than three men, grown entirely of thorn, and through their woven gaps bled a light that was going out even as Juniper watched. Dimming. Failing. The last star, dying in real time.

Above them, impossibly, through however many floors of stone, the sky must have been going dark forever.

Cinders left her wrist.

He rose off her arm in a spiral, wings beating, growing as he climbed — broader, longer, his glow swelling from coal to furnace — until he hovered before the thorn doors larger than an eagle, larger than anything a lantern had any right to hold, and he breathed out.

Fire, real fire, the first fire the world had seen since the stars began to die, washed over the doors. The thorns didn't burn so much as *scream*, curling away from the flame like creatures, and the doors split down the middle and fell outward into the throne room beyond with a crash that shook dust from the vaulted dark.

Warm air rolled over them, smelling of ash and old incense and, underneath it all, faintly, unbearably, of home.

Casper drew a breath. "Juniper." She said the name carefully, like testing whether it would hold her weight. "Whoever I was — thank you for carrying me this far."

"You're welcome," Juniper managed. "Now come meet yourself."

They stepped through together, the small-no-longer dragon blazing above them, into a throne room woven of thorns and dying light — and there, at the center, crowned and waiting, sat the thing that had eaten a god, and it turned its borrowed face toward Casper, and it smiled, and it said:

"*There* you are. I kept your seat warm."
