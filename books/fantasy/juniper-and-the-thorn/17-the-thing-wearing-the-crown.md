## 17. The Thing Wearing the Crown

The throne room doors opened the way a mouth opens—slowly, wetly, with too much intent behind it.

Casper had expected guards. She had expected wards, traps, at minimum some dramatic staircase. What she got was a corridor of black brambles that had eaten the palace from the inside out, thorned vines thick as her wrist winding up pillars of white marble, and a floor that remembered being beautiful under a century of dust and forgetting. Somewhere above, through a ceiling she couldn't see for the growth, the last star was dying. She could feel it going out the way you feel a candle gutter in another room—a small coldness spreading through the walls.

Beside her, Juniper shifted the lantern higher. The ember inside had come back roaring after the shrine—roaring, but not kindly. It burned now like a held breath, like something that knew what it was for.

"You're doing the thing," Juniper whispered.

"What thing."

"The thing where your jaw goes all square and you look like you're about to arm-wrestle a mountain."

"That's just my face."

"It's a bad face for sneaking," Juniper said. "We're supposed to be sneaking."

"We are past sneaking, Juniper. The brambles have opinions about us. I can hear them growing."

It was true. The thorns moved—not fast, not obviously, but when Casper wasn't looking directly at a vine, it crept closer to the path, narrowing it, herding them. Toward the throne. Everything in this palace herded toward the throne.

The tiny dragon rode on Casper's shoulder, grown since Grayhollow to the size of a well-fed cat, its scales the color of banked coals. It had refused to stay in the lantern. It had also, twice today, tried to eat a thornvine and spat it out with tremendous offense, which Casper chose to interpret as tactical reconnaissance.

"Names," she said quietly. They'd agreed on this in the corridors, Juniper narrating their whole journey so that the woman walking beside her—who had fed away her own memories of the girl—would know who she was traveling with. "Say my name again."

"Casper Thackeray," Juniper said. "You sold charms that didn't work in Grayhollow for twenty years, except they did work, you just pretended they didn't because you were hiding. You snore. You pretend you don't snore. You once threatened a mercenary sergeant with a soup ladle and he believed you."

"I did threaten him with a ladle."

"And you're the bravest coward I ever met," Juniper said, "which is apparently a real thing, because here you are."

"Flatterer." But the words landed somewhere under her ribs where the missing years ached, and she was grateful for them. A stranger's face needed a stranger's history handed to her by someone who loved it. That was the arrangement. That was the lamp carried for someone else.

The corridor ended.

The throne room of Corvenna had been, once, a marvel—she could feel the ghost of it, vaulted glass and starlight chandeliers, a room built to hold wonder. Now the thorns owned it. They climbed the dais in black coils, they hung from the vaults like the roots of an inverted forest, and every one of them pulsed faintly, dimly, with stolen light. Starlight, harvested. Memory, rendered visible and worn as decoration.

And on the throne sat King Aldric.

Or nearly. He sat very straight, hands on the armrests, a man-shaped thing in a crown, and he was wrong in the way a portrait is wrong when someone has painted over the eyes. His skin was too smooth. His stillness was too complete. And behind his face, behind his eyes, something looked out at Casper with an attention so vast and so patient that her knees went soft and her mouth went dry and twenty years of discipline screamed *down, down, kneel*—

She did not kneel. She gripped the strap of her satchel and made her legs be legs.

"Well," said the Thorn, in Aldric's voice, which was a warm voice, a voice built for coronations and pardons. "The little priest. Veth's last blade. I wondered which of you would come first—the girl with the fire, or the woman with the hole where her faith used to be."

"It's not a hole," Casper said. "It's a savings account."

Something flickered across the king's face. It might have been amusement. It might have been hunger deciding how to season itself.

Juniper stepped half in front of her, lantern raised, and the ember flared, and every thorn in the room leaned *away* from the light like grass bending from wind. Good girl. Casper put a hand on the dragon's spine—warm as a hearthstone—and felt it rumble.

"You came all this way," the Thorn said, "through roads that forgot themselves, through a city that sold its own childhood for bread, past the corpses of my riders—and for what? To perform a rite written by a dead order in a language nobody living speaks?" Aldric's head tilted. The motion was fluid, wrong, like a puppet learning grace. "I have watched you, Casper Thackeray. Do you know what I am? I am the thing that remembers *everything*. Every prayer Veth ever swallowed. Every name the stars carried before I put them out. I am the only archive left."

"Brother Hale kept a ledger," Casper said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved.

"Hale. Yes." Something like fondness. "He wrote beautifully. Did he tell you? Before the end?"

Casper said nothing. Grief was a door; the Thorn was standing at it, knocking politely.

"He told you enough," the Thorn said softly. "Enough to make you dangerous. So let us skip the part where we posture." Aldric rose from the throne. The movement took too long, joints unfolding like a spider waking, and yet the face remained kind, sorrowful, kingly. "Let me show you what I actually have."

* * *

The vision came without permission.

One moment Casper stood on a floor of dying light. The next she stood in a shrine she had not entered in twenty-five years—the chapel at Vethmar, whitewashed, smelling of cedar smoke and rain, and *he* was there.

Veth. Not the silent god of the iconography, not the veiled figure with the closed mouth. Him. Tall and ordinary-looking, sleeves rolled, mending a broken shutter with the patient competence of a man who believed holiness lived in hinges. He turned when she came in, and his whole face changed, and he said—

"There you are. I was starting to think you'd found better work."

His voice. Twenty years of silence and she had convinced herself she'd forgotten it, and she hadn't forgotten a syllable. It went through her like a blade finding the gap in armor.

"You left," he said, setting down the hammer, crossing the chapel, taking her hands in his warm human hands. No accusation in it. Only fact, and underneath the fact, something worse: forgiveness. "At the ford, when the first shadow came off the king's shoulders and I called your name. You ran. I know why. Fear isn't a sin, Casper. I never taught that. Fear is just love with nowhere to go."

"Don't," she whispered.

"But you came back," he said, smiling, and his eyes were brown and tired and alive, *alive*, and around them the chapel filled with morning light that no longer existed anywhere in the world. "You walked through a country eating itself to reach me. That's the ending I remember for us. That's the one I kept."

Her knees were going. She could feel them going. Every year of Grayhollow, every hollow charm, every night she'd lain awake listening for a god who never answered—all of it rearranged itself around this moment, this impossible mercy, and some deep animal part of her wanted nothing else in creation except to stay in this warm chapel with this warm man and let the world outside finish forgetting itself.

He touched her cheek. "Kneel," he said gently. "Not to me. With me. We'll pray together, like old times. And then you can rest. You've carried it long enough, haven't you? You can put it down."

Her knee touched the floor.

* * *

"—CASPER!"

Juniper's voice cracked across the vision like a whip, and with it came heat—a wall of it, the ember roaring somewhere impossibly close, and the chapel burned away like fog off a morning field.

Casper was on one knee on the throne room floor, and the Thorn stood over her with Aldric's hand extended, almost touching her hair, and its smile was no longer kind. Behind it, Juniper had both arms wrapped around the lantern, which blazed white-gold, and the little dragon had planted itself between Casper and the king with its wings half-spread and its teeth bared at something ten times its size, which was either bravery or terrible judgment, and knowing dragons, probably both.

"That wasn't him," Juniper said fiercely. "That wasn't him, Casper, look at me—Casper Thackeray, charm-seller, ladle-threatener, terrible liar, you told me once you'd rather die honest than live comfortable, so *look at me*—"

Casper looked at her.

A stranger's face. A girl she had no memories of, only stories about—stories Juniper had told her on the road, over campfires, in the drowned shrine, patiently, lovingly, building a Casper Thackeray out of secondhand timber. And the girl's eyes were wet and furious and absolutely certain, and the heat of the ember washed over Casper's back like a hand between her shoulder blades.

The vision had been perfect. That was how she knew it was false. The real Veth had never once asked her to rest. He'd asked her to get up. Every time. That was the whole religion, start to finish: *get up.*

She stood.

"No," Casper said.

The word came out small. She said it again, louder, and the dragon roared with her, a sound far too big for its body.

"No. He would never ask me to kneel while the world starved. He spent himself on the world—that was the entire point of him. You don't get to wear his face and offer me a nap." She pulled the satchel around, felt the rite-pages inside, Hale's careful dying handwriting. "And you don't get to keep the king."

The Thorn regarded her for a long moment. When it spoke again, the warmth was gone from Aldric's voice, and what remained was older than crowns, dry as ash, vast as the space between stars.

"Twice now," it said. "Twice you have refused me. Do you know how few things refuse me twice? The last was a dragon, and I drank her mountains dry." It spread Aldric's arms, and the throne room answered—every thorn in every wall uncoiling at once, the ceiling disappearing beneath a writhing canopy of black, the harvested starlight pulsing faster, brighter, hungry. "So be it. I offered you memory. I will take the rest instead. Your name. Your road. The girl's face. The little beast's warmth. I will unmake you in the order you loved things, priest, so you can watch each one go."

"Big talk," said Casper, "from a man wearing borrowed knees."

The dragon laughed. There was no other word for the sound—a crackling, delighted chitter—and then it leapt from her shoulder, wings snapping open, and it was growing even as it flew, cat to hound to pony, coal-red scales shedding sparks, and it hit the nearest wall of thorns like a falling star.

The battle began the way storms begin: all at once, everywhere, and much too loud.

Thorns came off the walls in whips and lances. Casper tore open her satchel, grabbed the first page of the rite—and couldn't read it, couldn't read anything, because a vine the width of her leg came down where her head had been and she rolled, came up with her palm burning, and spoke the first words of power she'd spoken publicly since Grayhollow. White fire sheeted off her hand and cut the vine in half. It screamed. All of them screamed, one voice through a thousand mouths, and the dying star overhead flickered like a pulse quickening.

"JUNIPER!" she shouted over the roar. "The rite needs a circle—I need ninety seconds at the dais—"

"Ninety seconds!" Juniper was already moving, low and fast, dragging the lantern with her, laying a line of ember-fire along the floor that the thorns recoiled from like living things from a hot iron. "Ninety seconds, she says, like the scary tree has a schedule—"

"IT DOES NOW!" Casper bellowed, and threw fire at the thing wearing the crown.

Aldric caught it. Caught it, in one smooth royal hand, and closed his fist, and the flame went out like a thought suppressed. The Thorn smiled with the king's handsome mouth.

"Oh, priest," it said. "I ate a god. What exactly were you planning to do?"

Casper backed toward the dais, palm smoking, heart hammering, the dragon wheeling overhead trailing sparks, Juniper shouting encouragement and insults in roughly equal measure—and under everything, beneath the rage and the terror and the ache of a face she'd been shown that wasn't real, one thought settled into her bones with terrible calm:

*Ninety seconds. Hold the line for ninety seconds.*

The thorns came down like nightfall, and Casper Thackeray, bravest coward in a forgotten world, ran straight into them.
