## 18. Unbinding

**Casper**

The rite of Unbinding required three things, according to Hale's dying scrawl: a blade of true fire, a circle of salt and old vows, and a soul willing to be opened like a letter.

Casper had the first two. The third was proving difficult.

She knelt at the center of the ruined chapel beneath the palace, in the place where Veth had first been worshipped back when worship was still legal and quiet. Juniper had chalked the circle around her with salt from the drowned shrine, hands shaking so badly that the line wobbled near the north point.

"Sorry," Juniper said. "Is it—does it have to be perfect?"

"It has to be *sincere*," Casper said. "The salt doesn't care about straight lines. It cares about whether you mean it."

"I mean it," Juniper said, with the ferocious certainty of sixteen. "I mean it so much it's embarrassing."

"Good. Embarrassment is practically liturgy."

Above them, through the chapel's broken ceiling, the last star hung over Corvenna like a coin about to drop down a well. The Thorn knew they were here. The whole palace of brambles knew; Casper could feel it pressing against the chapel walls the way a thumb presses against a beetle, curious, patient, certain of the outcome.

The tiny dragon sat on her shoulder. It had grown since Grayhollow — big as a cat now, big as a dog, its scales the color of banked coals — and when she reached up to scratch its jaw it made a sound like a kettle deciding to be brave.

"I need you to understand something," Casper said to it, and then, because it felt ridiculous talking to a dragon and not to the girl, she turned to Juniper too. "Both of you. When I start the rite, I'm going to be... open. Everything I am will be visible from the outside. The Thorn will try to take things. Memories. Names. It will say things designed to make me stop."

"Like what?" Juniper asked.

"Like offering me my god back."

Juniper went still. The lantern in her hands — the cold lantern, the one that had held the ember before the ember came to live in the dragon — caught what little light there was.

"And if you stop?"

"If I stop, we lose. And the world forgets its own name by morning." Casper rolled her shoulders. Twenty years of hiding magic had left her with the posture of someone perpetually apologizing for furniture. "So whatever I say, whatever I look like I'm doing — you hold the fire between me and it. That's your whole job. Don't listen to me. Don't save me. Just burn."

"That's a terrible job," Juniper said.

"It's the only one on offer."

The dragon butted its head against her cheek, once, hard enough to sting. She chose to interpret this as agreement.

She closed her eyes. She found the place inside herself where the buried power lived — not buried anymore, not since the ford, not since she'd burned riders out of the air in front of forty witnesses — and instead of reaching for it, she did the harder thing. She let it reach for her.

The rite began the way all of Veth's rites began: with silence.

Not the absence of sound. Silence as an act. Silence as a door held open.

"*Veth who is quiet,*" she breathed, in the old tongue, "*I unclose myself.*"

And she was unclosed.

It was like being flayed inside-out, except the skin being removed was made of memory. She felt every year of herself laid bare to the dark: the charm shop with its crooked sign, the taste of bad tea, the twenty years of small cowardices and smaller kindnesses, the shrine, the fear, the running. All of it hanging in the air around her like laundry, available to anything with the appetite to take it.

The Thorn took a breath.

She heard it the way you hear a house settle — vast, structural, wrong.

***There you are,*** it said, in a voice like King Aldric's voice played backward through water. ***Little blade. Little runaway.***

Casper held the silence. Held the rite. Her lips kept moving through words she'd half-forgotten, and the words were the only thing keeping her open in the right way — open like a door, not open like a wound.

***I could give him back to you.*** The voice slid closer. ***He's still in here, you know. In the parts of him I haven't digested. He remembers you. He remembers the ford at Serel, the rain, the vow you made with his name in your mouth. Would you like to hear it? I kept it. I keep everything. That's all remembering is — keeping.***

"Don't listen," Juniper whispered — to herself, or to the dragon, or maybe to Casper, which was against the rules, which Casper loved her for anyway.

The fire came up.

It rose off the dragon's back like heat off summer stone, and then it wasn't rising, it was *pouring*, a wall of gold-and-green flame between Casper's kneeling body and the dark, and the chapel that had been cold for a century suddenly remembered warmth.

The Thorn screamed. Not loudly. The worst thing about it was how quietly it screamed, like a man biting down on leather.

Casper kept chanting.

* * *

**Juniper**

Holding the fire was the hardest thing Juniper had ever done, and she had once walked eleven days on a broken ankle carrying the last hope of the world in a lantern, so the bar was high.

The dragon — Ash, she'd named it on the road, because Tam would have laughed at anything grander — stood over Casper like a roof. Its wings had come in properly two days ago, and now they spread across the whole chapel ceiling, shedding embers that fell upward, which Juniper had decided not to think about too hard because thinking about it made her feel dizzy.

And the Thorn pushed.

It came against the fire like a tide against a seawall. Every wave was made of forgetting. Where it touched the flames, Juniper lost things — small things first. The name of the innkeeper at the Company camp. The color of Tam's scarf. Then bigger things. Her mother's face, already gone, already spent. The word for the feeling of being safe.

Each loss hurt like a tooth coming out.

"You're spending yourself," Casper had told her once, about the ember. "Everything costs. That's not cruelty, that's just arithmetic."

Well. Juniper could do arithmetic.

"Ash," she said, and her voice cracked down the middle. "Whatever happens — don't stop burning. Even if I forget why. Even if I forget *you*. The fire remembers. Fire always remembers what it's for."

The dragon turned its head and looked at her with eyes like twin sunrises, and made a sound that was absolutely, unmistakably, *yes.*

Then the Thorn changed tactics, and Juniper learned what despair actually sounded like.

***Girl,*** it said, directly into her skull, warm as a hand on her hair. ***You are the last keeper of a dead order, guarding an egg that hatched into a dying light. Look at her.*** It gestured, somehow, at Casper — kneeling, bleeding from the nose, mouth moving through the old words. ***Look at what she's spending. Do you know what she gave up to reignite me? Her name. Her years. Her knowledge of your face. She burned the memory of you to save the world, and when it's done, she will look at you the way strangers look at each other in markets. Is that a victory? Is that worth your arithmetic?***

Juniper's grip on the lantern handle went white.

"She knows," Juniper said. "She told me herself. She said—" and here her voice did something humiliating, wobbling like a child's, "—she said love isn't the memory. Love is what carries the memory. Like a lamp. You can hand a lamp to a stranger. It's still light."

***Sentimental.***

"Yeah," Juniper said, and fed another armful of herself into the fire — the smell of the borderlands, the song the egg used to hum, the exact blue of the sky the morning everything ended. "We're sentimental. It's our whole religion. Come and get some."

The Thorn came and got some.

* * *

**Casper**

Pain, Casper discovered, had layers, like an onion built by someone with a grudge.

The first layer was the rite itself — the old words tearing channels through her that hadn't been used in twenty years, reopening every scar she'd earned as Veth's chosen blade. The second layer was the Thorn's fingers in her memories, plucking at them, testing which ones would tear. The third layer, the deepest, was stranger: it was *grief*, arriving early, grieving in advance for everything she was losing while she was still losing it.

The Thorn pulled.

She felt the first memory go — the charm shop, gone, just *gone*, and with it the crooked sign and the smell of dust and the particular creak of the third floorboard. She felt it leave the way you feel a river take a stone from under your foot.

She kept chanting.

It took the tea. It took the bad tea specifically, which felt personal. It took the face of the baker on Mire Street, the name of her landlady, the entire city of Grayhollow dissolving into gray static, and Casper chanted through it with tears running freely down her face because the words didn't require her to mean them anymore, thank the quiet god, the words held themselves.

***Stop,*** the Thorn said, and now there was something new in the voice. Something almost human. Almost pleading. ***Blade of Veth. Chosen one. Listen to me. I am not your enemy. I am his *continuation*. When I ate him, I became everything he was — every prayer he ever answered, every silence he ever kept. Kill me and you kill the last of him. Spare me, kneel to me, and I will pour him back into you, whole, every memory, every moment, every—***

For one terrible second, Casper saw it.

Veth. Alive. Standing in the rain at the ford at Serel the way he had stood twenty years ago, hood back, that dry half-smile he'd worn like armor, saying *you're late, little blade* — and she was on her feet, or her heart was, her knees stayed down but her heart stood up and took two steps toward the vision before—

Before the fire flared green.

Before a voice — young, cracked, furious, *herself* — shouted through the dark: "**CASPER! Your name is Casper Thackeray! You sell charms that barely work and you overcharge tourists and you talk to your teapot! You're a coward and you're kind and you're MY—**" the voice broke, reassembled, "**—you're mine, and you promised me arithmetic, so do the sum! Him back, world gone. Or him gone, world back. ADD IT UP!**"

Casper laughed.

It was an awful sound, wet and ragged, punched out of her mid-rite, and it did more damage to the Thorn than any word of power ever had. Because the vision flickered. Because the thing wearing Aldric's crown had offered her a god and gotten, in return, a middle-aged woman laughing through her nosebleed about a teapot.

***What is funny,*** the Thorn demanded.

"Nothing," Casper said, and her voice came out steady, steadier than it had been in twenty years. "Everything. You're offering me a god who never existed. My god drank bad tea. My god complained about his knees. My god forgave me before I finished apologizing, which was infuriating, and if he's really dissolved into the memories of the world, then he's in *this* — in her, in the fire, in the stupid joke that just saved my life. You don't have him. You never had him. You ate a throne and called it a god."

She planted both palms flat on the salt circle.

"*Veth who is quiet,*" she said, in the old tongue, loud now, no longer a whisper, "*I unclose myself, and I do not close again. Take what you need. I am the blade. Cut.*"

The rite turned inside out.

* * *

**Juniper**

Later, Juniper would struggle to describe what happened next, mostly because she was crying too hard to see most of it.

The fire stopped being hers.

That was the only way she could put it. One moment Ash was burning above Casper, holding the wall, and the next the fire *knew what it was for* — the way Casper had said, the way fire always remembers — and it poured down into the salt circle, into Casper, into the channels the rite had torn open, and Casper lit up from the inside like a paper lantern, like a window at dusk, like every hearth in every home the world had forgotten.

The Thorn shrieked and threw everything it had.

Juniper felt it take the rest of her childhood. Felt it take Tam — the scarred sergeant's laugh, gone, the weight of her hand on Juniper's head, gone, the promise Juniper had made at the ford, gone, gone, gone. She fed the fire faster to pay for it, spending herself down to the wick, and Ash grew to meet the demand — bigger, bigger, wings spanning the chapel, scales blazing white-gold, a dragon the size of a horse now, the size of a cathedral, roaring in a voice that was somehow still the small kettle-sound from a Grayhollow shelf.

"HOLD THE RITE!" Juniper screamed at Casper, though Casper couldn't hear her, though Casper was beyond hearing, bent double in a circle of white fire with the whole Thorn of the world clawing at the doors of her.

And Casper held.

Through the taking of her name — she saw it happen, saw Casper's lips form the word *Casper* and then falter, confused, and then *keep going anyway*, mouthing sounds without meanings, holding the rite on pure stubbornness with nothing left inside her but the rite itself.

Through the taking of her years. Through the taking of her face-knowledge, her history, her griefs, her guilts, her twenty years of hiding — all of it ripped away and devoured, and the woman in the fire did not stop, did not curl up, did not bargain, just *endured*, the way a mountain endures weather, the way a lamp endures the wind, the way —

The way, Juniper realized, with a sob that came from somewhere below her ribs, a keeper holds an ember.

"Oh," she whispered. "Oh, that's what you were. That's what you've been the whole time."

The Thorn must have felt it too — the shift, the moment the rite stopped being something happening *to* Casper and became something she simply *was*. Because it stopped pulling memories and started pulling everything, all at once, a final drowning grab, and the chapel groaned, and the last star through the broken ceiling guttered like a candle in a doorway—

—and in the center of the fire, Casper Thackeray, who no longer knew her own name, lifted her head.

Her eyes were full of light. Her mouth was full of old words. And where her heart should have been, visible through her glowing chest like a coal through ash, something was opening — a seam, a wound, a door — and through it Juniper could see, impossibly, faintly, the shape of the thing the rite had been digging toward all along.

A wound in the world. A split beginning, thin as a hair, right down the middle of the dark.

Inside it, something moved.

Something quiet.

"CASPER!" Juniper screamed, and Ash roared, and the fire went up like a second dawn, and the seam in the world tore wider, wide enough to see through, wide enough for the Thorn to scream in a voice that was suddenly, horribly, *two voices* — the monster's and, beneath it, muffled, faint, unmistakable:

King Aldric's.

Still alive. Still in there. Still waiting.

The rite had found him.

Now it had to cut.
