## 19. The God Who Was Eaten

The rite had a shape.

Casper had not known that until now. She had imagined Unbinding as a shout, a breaking, a hammer-blow of light — something like the old battle-priest hymns, all trumpets and wrath. But it was a shape: a circle drawn in ember-light and held open by will, like a door propped with a chair. The Thorn was inside King Aldric the way a root is inside soil, and you could not tear out a root by shouting at it. You had to open the ground.

So she opened the ground.

The circle burned beneath her feet in the ruined throne room of Corvenna, gold-white and trembling, and at its center Aldric hung in a cage of thorns that were also his own bones, his mouth open in a soundless scream. Juniper stood behind her with both arms raised, and the dragon — no longer moth-sized, no longer sparrow-sized, but vast as a cathedral bell and bright as one — coiled above them both, wings spread against the dark, burning back the brambles that came crawling down the walls like the room itself had decided to eat them.

"Hold!" Juniper shouted, which was a strange thing for a sixteen-year-old to shout at a former battle-priest, and yet Casper found it steadying. There was something about being ordered around by a girl who smelled of woodsmoke and stubbornness that made the will hold firm.

"I'm holding," Casper said through her teeth.

"You're crying!"

"That's also holding!"

The Thorn noticed what she was doing, of course. It had always noticed. It turned Aldric's head toward her — slowly, the way a ship turns, something enormous pretending to be small — and when it spoke, it used the king's voice, and under the king's voice it used another voice, older, quieter, one she had spent twenty years trying not to hear in her dreams.

*Casper.*

"No," she said.

*You always talked too much during rites. Veth said so. He said, that girl prays like she's arguing.*

"Shut up." Her hands shook. The circle wavered. Above her, the dragon roared and a wall of brambles caught fire and the heat washed over her like a hand pressing her shoulders back into place. She widened the circle. She held the door open.

The Thorn screamed then — not with Aldric's voice but with its own, a sound like a library burning, like every word in every book catching at once — and it *tore*. It reached into her the way it reached into everything, into memories, and it began pulling them out and eating them as fast as it could, because if it could not stop the rite it would stop the woman performing it.

It took her mother's kitchen. It took the smell of bread. It took the name of her first teacher and the color of the sky over Grayhollow on the morning she'd arrived with nothing but a satchel and a lie. Each loss was a small cold amputation, and Casper gasped at each one, and held.

*Juniper,* she thought, *if I forget your face, keep talking. Keep talking until I know you again.*

And Juniper, who could not have heard the thought, started talking anyway. "Your turn's coming, you ugly weed," she was telling the Thorn. "You know what I did to your riders? You know what I did to your brambles in the east corridor? I've been narrating this whole palace to a woman who doesn't remember me, and I did it *cheerfully*, so don't think you're scary—"

The circle closed around the king.

And the world split.

* * *

Later, Casper would try to describe it and fail, because there were no words left in any language for what happened next, or if there were, they had been eaten along with the smell of bread.

The Thorn came out of Aldric.

It came out the way a splinter comes out of a palm — slowly, horribly, dragging half the flesh's history with it — except the splinter was the size of a winter and made of every forgotten thing. It poured out of the king's mouth and eyes and the seams of him, black and glistening, thorned with the shapes of extinguished stars, and it filled the throne room from floor to rafters, and it *hated*, and its hatred had weight enough to bend the air.

Aldric collapsed. A thin man in a thin crown, empty as a dropped robe. Juniper dove for him, dragged him to the edge of the circle, out of the fire-lines. Good girl. Good, brave, impossible girl.

And in the wound where the Thorn had been — in the space it left behind, torn open between the world and whatever is underneath the world — Casper looked, and saw him.

Veth.

Not standing. Not sitting. Not anything so simple. He was *dissolved* — poured out through everything, the way salt dissolves through water and stops being visible without stopping being present. She saw him in the memory of a lullaby Juniper had restored in a village whose name had come back to it. She saw him in Tam's scarred hands, remembered now by the mercenaries who had buried him. She saw him in the taste of bread, somewhere, in some kitchen, in some life she could no longer recall. Every star that had gone out had not died. It had fallen *into* things. Into names and songs and the way an old woman's hands folded flour. The god had been eaten, yes — but eaten the way seed is eaten by soil.

He was everywhere he had ever been loved.

"Oh," Casper said. Her knees went. The circle held her up; it seemed to know she needed holding. "Oh, you *idiot*. You beautiful, patient idiot. You let it happen."

The presence that was not a voice and yet answered anyway stirred through her, warm as a hearth, sad as a grave.

*I could not fight it and remain myself,* the world-memory said, in the manner of rain saying something, in the manner of light. *If I had fought, I would have become it. So I scattered. I became small. I became them.* And the warmth turned toward Juniper, toward the lantern at her belt where the last ember slept, toward the dragon overhead. *Toward her line. The jailers. The keepers. I put myself where it could never finish swallowing me.*

"You could have told me." Her voice cracked down the middle. "Twenty years, you could have — one dream. One sign. I thought you'd *abandoned* me. I thought I'd abandoned *you*."

*You were afraid,* the warmth said, without judgment, which was somehow worse. *I knew. I chose a blade who could be afraid. Fear remembers what courage forgets.*

"Don't you dare be kind to me right now," Casper wept, laughing, furious, holding the circle with hands that shook like leaves. "Don't you *dare*."

But there was no time for the conversation twenty years owed them, because the Thorn gathered itself across the throne room and understood, in its ancient hungering way, exactly what she was seeing in the wound. And it laughed.

*You cannot kill me,* it said, wearing a hundred dead voices at once. *Look at what holds me apart from him. Look at what the rite burns as fuel.* It gestured with a limb like a winter branch, and Casper followed the gesture and saw it, and stopped breathing.

The rite needed fuel. All magic did; that was the first law she'd ever learned, before she'd learned her own name. The Unbinding drew on the nearest holy thing. And the nearest holy thing — the only holy thing left in the world, pooled in the wound, soaked through the circle, feeding the fire that held the Thorn out of the king —

Was Veth.

To kill the Thorn, she had to burn what remained of her god. All of it. Every dissolved star, every lullaby, every remembered handful. The ember and the remnants together, fed into one flame, and the flame would take the Thorn with it, and the world would go on with nothing holy left in it at all. No god. Not hidden, not scattered, not sleeping. Gone.

*Choose,* said the Thorn, delighted. *You have always been good at choosing wrong.*

* * *

The strange thing, Casper thought later — insofar as there was a later, insofar as there was a Casper to do the thinking — was how quiet it was inside her decision. She had expected war in her chest. She had expected the oldest temptation to make one more offer, and it did, briefly, sweetly: the Thorn showed her Veth whole, alive, seated on a stone bench in a garden she had lost the name of, patting the space beside him, waiting for her the way he had waited every evening of her service while she finished arguing with the other priests. Come sit, that vision said. Burn the girl instead. Have him back. Have yourself back.

She nearly knelt. Some honest part of her would always know she nearly knelt.

Then Juniper's voice cut through — not a battle cry, not a prayer, just the girl, talking, the way she talked, steady and furious and sixteen: "Whatever it's showing you, it's lying. It always lies. That's the whole thing about it. That's its entire personality."

And the tiny warmth at Casper's wrist — the ember's heat, the dragon's attention, the whole small congregation of everyone who had walked beside her since the wards broke — pressed against her like a forehead pressed to hers.

Casper stood up inside the circle.

"Yes," she said aloud, to the wound, to the warmth, to the god dissolved through every lullaby in the world. "Yes. I know what you'd say. You'd say the same thing you said when I took my vows. *A blade exists for what it protects, not for what it keeps.*"

The warmth flared — assent, or love, or both; they had never been different things with him.

"So burn," she whispered. "Burn, and take it with you. And I'll — I'll remember. Whatever's left of me will remember. That's the rite now. That's my office. I'll be the one who remembers you."

She brought her hands together, and the circle narrowed, and the ember rose from Juniper's lantern — the girl cried out, reached for it, then understood, and lowered her hand, and wept without making a child's noise about it, and Casper loved her for that, loved her with the last whole piece of herself.

The flame that rose was not gold and it was not white. It was every color a thing can be remembered in. It took the remnants of Veth into itself gently, the way night takes a candle, the way soil takes a seed, and the god did not resist, and the god did not cry out, and the last thing the warmth gave her — deliberately, pressed into her palm like a coin for a ferryman — was not a memory at all.

It was permission.

She struck.

The flame went through the Thorn like dawn through fog. For one instant the throne room was full of light, and in the light every eaten star blazed once, every lost name rang once, every forgotten lullaby sang one note — the whole scattered congregation of the god, called home and sent onward in the same breath — and the Thorn, the ancient hunger, the thing that had worn a king and unmade a sky, had time to understand exactly what was happening to it.

Then it burned, root and crown, hunger and hate, all the way down.

The scream it made was heard, though no ear heard it, in every place a memory had ever been kept.

And Casper Thackeray fell to her knees in the ashes of her god, weeping, empty-handed, holding nothing — and through her tears she saw, high above the broken ceiling, past the smoke, past the thorns crumbling to gray dust—

A star.

One star. Small and new and trembling, kindling in the black where no star had burned in a year.

Then a second. Then a third — and far away, faint as a bell across water, she heard a city full of people look up and begin, all at once, to remember their own names.
