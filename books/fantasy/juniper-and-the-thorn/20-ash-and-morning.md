## 20. Ash and Morning

Juniper had always imagined the end of the world would be loud.

It was not. The Thorn died screaming, yes—that part was loud enough to crack her teeth and fill her ears with a sound like a forest being unmade—but after the scream there was silence, and the silence was worse and better than anything she had ever heard. It was the silence of a held breath finally let go.

She was on her knees in the wreckage of the throne room, and Casper was beside her, and neither of them was moving, because moving felt like it might undo whatever had just been done.

The last thing Juniper had seen clearly was the dragon—her dragon, the moth-sized scrap of ember that had hatched in a charm-shop and grown vast enough to blot out the dark—wrapped around the Thorn like a fist of white fire while Casper spoke the rite of Unbinding through blood and tears. Then the light had swallowed everything, and now the light was gone, and they were kneeling in ash that drifted down like gray snow.

"Casper," Juniper said. Her voice came out as a croak. "Casper, are you—"

"I'm here." A pause. "I think. Mostly."

"Mostly?"

"My hands are missing. No—" A rustle. "No, there they are. False alarm."

Juniper laughed, or made a sound adjacent to laughing, and then she was crying instead, which was also adjacent. She reached out in the dark until her fingers found Casper's sleeve, and she held on, and Casper—stranger-with-Casper's-face, the woman who had burned away twenty years of herself to feed the flame—held on back.

That was when the first star came home.

It happened above them, through the shattered roof of the palace, where the sky had been a black wound for so long that Juniper had stopped thinking of it as sky at all. A point of light kindled, small and gold, and Juniper felt it land somewhere behind her ribs like a coin dropped into an empty well.

"Oh," she breathed.

Another star. Then another. They did not come all at once; they came one by one, each arrival accompanied by a feeling Juniper had no word for—a memory returning to the world, settling into place like a bird coming home to roost. And with each one, things around them *remembered*.

The throne room remembered its windows, and glass knitted itself back together with a sound like chimes. The banners remembered their colors, bleeding from gray back to crimson and silver. Somewhere below, Juniper heard a city remembering itself: bells that had forgotten how to ring finding their voices again, dogs barking at nothing and everything, a thousand people crying out at once as names returned to them—their mothers' names, their children's names, the names of streets they'd walked all their lives.

A soldier stumbled through the broken doors, young, gray-faced with exhaustion, staring up at the stars with his mouth open. He looked at Juniper and Casper and said, in a wondering voice, "My name is Pell. My name is *Pell*." As if he were tasting it. As if he'd been without it for years rather than hours.

"Pell," Casper said gravely. "Good name. Solid. Keep hold of it."

He blinked at them, saluted someone who wasn't there, and ran back out shouting.

* * *

They found King Aldric where the throne had been.

The throne itself was gone—burned to a lattice of black thorns that crumbled even as they watched, sifting into ash—and the king lay among the ruins like a coat someone had taken off. That was the only way Juniper could describe it. For months the Thorn had worn Aldric's body like armor, and now the wearing was over, and what remained looked *unworn*. Small. An old man in a fine robe gone gray at the seams, breathing shallowly, his eyes open and fixed on the returning stars.

His eyes were his own. That was the first thing Juniper noticed, and the relief of it nearly buckled her knees. Whatever had lived behind them was gone.

"Aldric," Casper said, kneeling. "Can you hear me?"

"I hear..." His voice was a ruin, but it was a human ruin. "I hear stars. I haven't heard stars in—" His face crumpled. "How long? How long has it been?"

"Twenty years," Casper said quietly. "Give or take."

"Twenty years." He said it the way a man says a sentence at his own trial. Then his gaze found Casper's face, and something moved through it—recognition, horror, grief. "You're one of Veth's. I remember... I remember praying. Before. I prayed every night, and something answered, and then something else answered instead, and I was so *tired*, and it promised me it would carry the weight if I just—" He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "Did I do this? Was it me?"

"No," Casper said, and Juniper heard the lie in it, gently told. "It wore you. That's not the same as being it."

"It feels the same."

"It never does," Casper said. "That's why it works."

Aldric wept then, silently, the way old men weep, as though he'd had practice and hated every hour of it. When he could speak again, he said: "There will be those who want me to answer for this. They'll be right to."

"There will be those who want a lot of things," Casper said. "Right now I want you to drink some water and stop lying on thorns."

"They're my thorns," Aldric said, with the ghost of something that might once have been royal humor. "I grew them."

"You grew *terrible* thorns. No wonder your kingdom fell apart."

And that—gods help her—was when Juniper started laughing, high and unhinged and helpless, because the world had just ended and un-ended in a single night and the freed king of Corvenna was making jokes about his own brambles, and if she didn't laugh she was going to come apart entirely. Casper glanced at her with one eyebrow raised, and Aldric stared at her as though she might be a second, smaller Thorn, and Juniper laughed until she wheezed, until it turned back into crying, until it settled into something that could pass for breathing.

* * *

The abdication happened three days later, in the great hall, before a crowd of citizens who had spent those days remembering who they were and discovering, with varying degrees of distress, what had been done in their names.

Juniper stood at the edge of the crowd with the cold lantern in her arms and watched Aldric set the crown of Corvenna on the steps of the dais—not handing it to anyone, just setting it down, the way you put down something heavy you've carried too far. He was thinner than he'd been in the throne room. Remembering, Juniper was learning, cost more than forgetting ever had.

"People of Corvenna," he said, and his voice cracked on the second word, and nobody laughed. "I cannot give you back what was taken. Some of it is gone past any finding. What I can give you is the truth: I opened the door. I was tired, and I was afraid, and something in the dark offered to carry what I could not, and I let it in. Whatever comes next must be built by hands that did not make that bargain. So I am stepping aside. Not because I am asked." He looked out over them, and his eyes were clear and terrible. "Because I am not fit. May whoever follows be better than I was. Which should not be difficult. A turnip would manage it."

Someone in the crowd snorted. Someone else actually laughed, and the laugh spread, ragged and disbelieving, and Juniper thought: *that* is what healing sounds like. Not solemnity. Not speeches. People remembering that laughter was allowed.

Afterward, in the corridor, Aldric passed close to where Juniper stood. He stopped. Up close he smelled of ash and soap, like a man trying very hard to be clean.

"You're the keeper," he said. "The one with the fire."

"I was," Juniper said. Her arms tightened around the lantern.

Aldric looked at the lantern—at the cold glass, the dead wick, the little drift of ash inside that no longer moved—and something in his face folded. "I'm sorry," he said. "For all of it. But especially—" He gestured, helpless, at the lantern. "Whatever that was. Whoever it was to you."

"She wasn't a whoever," Juniper said. "She was a *she*."

"Yes," Aldric said softly. "I know exactly what that means." And he bowed to her—an actual bow, deep, from a man who had been king for thirty years—and walked away down the corridor, alone, carrying his memories like stones.

* * *

The mourning lasted nine days, because Juniper counted, and because nine days was how long she sat in the undercity chapel of roots with the lantern in her lap and refused, politely but absolutely, to leave.

Hale was buried there, in the chapel where Veth had first been worshipped, under a slab that read only *HE HELPED AND HE WAS SORRY*, which Casper had insisted upon and which Juniper privately thought was the most honest epitaph in the history of stonework. Tam's name went on the wall nearby, along with the names of the mercenaries who had fallen at the ford, restored now by travelers' accounts since none of the company could remember them. The chapel smelled of candle smoke and wet stone and old roots, and Juniper sat beneath the largest root of all—the one that had once, supposedly, been the god's own arm reaching down into the earth—and held the lantern and did not eat enough and did not sleep much and talked to the ash inside the glass.

"You were the size of a moth," she told it. "Did you know that? You were the size of a moth and you bit Casper's thumb and you slept in my hood and you were terrified of rain. Terrified of *rain*. And then you got big. You got so big. You burned back the dark, and I don't—I don't even know if you knew what you were doing. I don't know if you chose it or if you just grew toward the biggest thing that needed burning and that was that."

The ash did not answer. It never did. It just sat there, a spoonful of gray in the bottom of the lantern, and every morning Juniper woke hoping it had shifted overnight, and every morning it hadn't.

On the fourth day Casper brought her bread and sat down against the root without asking permission, which was very Casper.

"You know," Casper said, "when I lost my memories, someone carried them for me. Narrated them. Walked me through corridors telling me who I was." She nodded at Juniper. "It was the single most annoying act of love I have ever received. I resented every minute of it."

"You cried twice."

"I was *grateful* twice. The rest was resentment." Casper broke the bread and handed half over. "Eat. Grief runs better on bread."

"She's not gone," Juniper said, and hated how small her voice was. "Everyone keeps saying it like she's gone, and she's not *gone*, she's—she's in the stars now, isn't she? Like him. Like Veth. They burned together, they're *everywhere* now, so technically—"

"Technically," Casper agreed.

"But she's not *here*." Juniper's throat closed. "She doesn't sit on my shoulder. She doesn't steal my breakfast. She's everywhere and that means she's nowhere and everyone keeps saying I should be happy because the stars are back and I *am* happy, I'm so happy I can't stand up most days, and I want her *back*."

Casper was quiet for a while. Outside, rain had begun—one of the first true rains since the wards fell, drumming on the chapel roof, and Juniper thought absurdly: *you were scared of this. You'd have hidden in my hood.*

"When I burned my memories," Casper said at last, "I knew what I was losing. Names, years, faces. What I didn't expect was how much space they'd leave behind. Twenty years of emptiness, sitting in me like a furnished room with the furniture gone." She turned the bread over in her fingers. "But here's the thing about empty rooms, Juniper. They don't stay empty. Something always moves in. Dust. Light. Mice, if you're unlucky. New memories, if you're lucky. The room doesn't care what fills it. It just waits."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to be true." Casper stood, joints cracking, and Juniper realized with a start that she still didn't know how old Casper was—had never known, would maybe never know, the woman having eaten her own birthday along with everything else. "Nine days is a good mourning. Ten is wallowing. Come find me tomorrow, or don't. Either way, I'm opening a school eventually and you're going to teach the fire lessons, because I can barely light a candle anymore and it's humiliating."

She left. Juniper stayed. The rain kept on.

* * *

On the ninth day, the ash stirred.

Juniper almost missed it. She had fallen asleep against the root, the lantern tipped sideways in her lap, dreaming of a road that forgot itself and a child singing a lullaby she'd lost and found again. She woke because the chapel was warm.

Not candle-warm. Not body-warm. Warm the way a hearth is warm, the way a forge is warm, warm from the *inside of the air*, and Juniper's heart slammed once against her ribs and went still.

The lantern lay in her lap. Inside the glass, the spoonful of ash was moving.

Not drifting. Not stirring the way dust stirs. Moving *with purpose*, gathering toward the center of the lantern floor, drawing inward and upward, building itself into a shape Juniper knew down to her bones: a small coiled body, a wedge of head, two wings folded like closed books. The ash took the shape and held it, and then the shape began to glow—faintly, uncertainly, the way embers glow when you think they're dead and breathe on them anyway—and then, with a sound like a struck match the size of a cathedral, the ash caught fire.

The little dragon opened her eyes.

They were new eyes. Juniper saw that immediately, and her heart broke and mended in the same breath. The old eyes had been ember-orange, fierce and knowing, ancient beyond her size. These were pale gold, wide, blinking, bewildered—the eyes of something seeing the world for the *first time*. This was not her dragon come back. This was not the same creature at all.

This was what came next.

"Oh," Juniper whispered. "Oh, hello."

The dragon looked at her. Considered her, with the grave attention of the very young. Then she sneezed, and a spark shot across the chapel and set fire to a memorial wreath, and Juniper lunged to pat it out, laughing and sobbing in the same motion, and by the time she'd smothered the flames the dragon had climbed halfway up her arm with the clumsy determination of a kitten, hooked her claws into Juniper's sleeve, and bitten—gently, experimentally—her thumb.

"Ow! Yes. Hello. We've met. Sort of. You wouldn't remember." Juniper cupped the tiny warm weight in both hands and lifted her up into the candlelight, and the dragon's wings buzzed once, twice, and she lifted off—wobbled—crashed into Juniper's chin—and tried again, and this time flew a full wobbling circle around the chapel, scattering ash and candle flames and nine days of grief like a broom through cobwebs.

Juniper stood in the middle of it all, tears streaming, laughing, and understood at last what Casper had meant about empty rooms.

Something had moved in.

The dragon landed on the great root overhead, perched among the god's stone fingers, and looked down at Juniper with her brand-new golden eyes, and outside the chapel the dawn was coming up gray and then rose and then gold, and the first bell of the morning rang out over a city that knew its own name.

Juniper wiped her face. Picked up the lantern—empty now, truly empty, and lighter than it had ever been—and tucked it under her arm, because keepers keep their tools even after the tool's work is done.

"Right," she said to the dragon, and to the chapel, and to the god in the roots and the stars coming home and the whole enormous grieving blooming world. "Let's go find Casper. She's going to pretend she isn't crying, and I want to watch."

The dragon chirped—a bright, brand-new sound, the first note of a song no one had heard yet—and leapt from the root onto Juniper's shoulder, and together they climbed the stairs out of the undercity, into the morning.
