## 21. A City Learning Its Name

The notice went up on the Warden's post on a Tuesday, in the rain, and half of Grayhollow came out to read it as though it might bite.

*By order of the Council of Nine, the Edicts of Silence are hereby repealed. The practice of charm-craft, hedge-healing, weather-sense, and kindred arts is no longer a crime within the city walls.*

Below that, in smaller letters, someone had added by hand: *About time.*

Casper Thackeray stood in the crowd under her umbrella and listened to the city murmur around her like a kettle coming to boil. An old woman beside her crossed herself three times, then seemed to remember that the god she'd been crossing herself toward had been eaten, dissolved, scattered into the grain of the world like salt into bread, and settled for patting her own chest instead.

"Twenty years," the old woman said to no one in particular. "I knew a woman on Tallow Lane who could set bones with a word. They took her away when I was a girl. I always wondered where they *took* her."

Casper did not say: they took her to the warden's cellar, and then to the border forts, and then into the ground. She did not say: I sold charms two streets from where they dragged her past my window, and I kept my head down, and I told myself it was wisdom.

She said, "Perhaps she got somewhere kinder."

The old woman squinted at her. "You're the charm-seller off Candlemaker's Row. The one who saved the city."

"I sold charms," Casper said. "Mostly they didn't work."

"That's not what my grandson says. He says you burned a hundred gray men out of the sky at the ford and the river boiled and—"

"The river did not boil." It had steamed, briefly, which was different, and she'd apologized to the fish afterward out of old habit. Veth had liked fish. Or rather — she caught herself, as she caught herself a dozen times a day — Veth had *liked* fish, according to Brother Hale's ledger, according to Juniper's careful notes, according to the shape of a memory she had fed into an ember like logs into a stove. She remembered having loved him the way you remember a room you grew up in after the house has burned: the dimensions of it, the warmth of it, but not the color of the curtains.

"Anyway," the old woman finished, satisfied, "you should be proud."

Casper watched the rain bead on the fresh ink of the notice until the words began to run, and thought about how pride was a coin she'd never learned to spend.

* * *

The school opened three weeks later in the front room of what had been her shop, because the back room still smelled faintly of gray rider ash no matter how many times Juniper scrubbed it, and because Casper refused on principle to teach children anything in a room that smelled of fear.

She'd hung a sign herself, lettered carefully: *THACKERAY & KEEPER — SMALL HONEST CHARMS, AND INSTRUCTION THEREIN.* Juniper had added, underneath, in her round, stubborn hand: *NO MIRACLES. WE MEAN IT.*

"It'll put people off," Casper had said.

"It'll keep out the ones who'd ask for miracles," Juniper said. "Which is most of them, this month."

She'd been right, as she was increasingly often, in the way of sixteen-year-olds who have carried fire across a broken country and earned the right to be smug about it. The first morning, eleven students crowded into the room meant for six: a baker's daughter with flour in her eyebrows; twin brothers who had been born deaf and wanted to learn the charm-speech that worked through the hands; a widow of perhaps seventy who sat in the very back and took notes like she was being examined; and a boy named Petch who had already, before the first bell, set his own sleeve on fire twice.

"How," Casper said, standing over him while he beat at his arm, "did you manage that. We haven't started."

"It just happened," Petch said, in the wounded tone of the genuinely innocent.

"Nothing *just happens* in this room. That is the entire curriculum." She pinched out the flame, checked his sleeve — scorched, salvageable — and looked at him properly. He was maybe ten, gap-toothed, with the particular restless brightness she had learned, over one long autumn of teaching, to recognize as either trouble or talent, and usually both. "Where did you feel it start?"

"My fingers? They got all... fizzy."

"Fizzy," Casper repeated gravely, and heard Juniper snort from the supply shelf. "Good. Fizzy is honest. Fire that comes without asking is just your own power showing up early, like a guest who arrives before the bread is done. Our first lesson is learning to bake the bread. Sit down, and do not touch anything."

He touched something within four minutes. But it was only the chalk, and the chalk, to be fair, had been enchanted against theft by Casper herself, so arguably he was the victim.

The morning went on. She taught them the warming-charm, the smallest honest magic she knew — cup your hands, think of something warm, speak the name of the warmth aloud. Not a spell, exactly. More like introducing yourself to the world and asking politely. The baker's daughter warmed her palms thinking of her mother's oven. One twin warmed his thinking of summer; the other, thinking of his brother, which made Casper turn away briefly to adjust the chalk tray.

The widow in the back raised her hand. "Mine won't take. My hands stay cold."

"What did you think of?"

"My husband." Her chin lifted, daring anyone to make something sentimental of it. "Forty-one years dead."

Casper came and sat beside her, which surprised the class, because Madam Thackeray did not sit, Madam Thackeray stood like a fencepost and radiated disapproval at sloppy knotwork. "Try again," she said quietly. "But don't think of him gone. Think of him mending a chair. Something small. Something he was *doing*."

The widow's mouth worked. Then her hands, folded in her lap, flushed pink to the wrists, and she stared at them as if they belonged to a stranger, and then she laughed — a rusty sound, a gate opening after years — and several students jumped.

"There," Casper said. "That's the whole art, really. The world remembers things better than we do. Magic is mostly letting it remind you."

She felt Juniper watching her from the shelf, and did not look over, because if she looked over she would see the girl's expression — that fierce, tender, worried look — and the widow would see it too, and the moment would become A Moment, and Casper had built twenty years of survival on the principle that moments were best left unremarked.

But afterward, sweeping chalk dust while the students spilled out into the wet street, Juniper said anyway, "You're getting good at this."

"At teaching?"

"At the other thing. The part where they bring you their cold hands and you find the warm thing underneath." Juniper leaned on the broom handle, stealing it, the way she stole everything — Casper's chair, the last honey-bun, whole conversations. "Hale would've liked to see it."

"Hale would've critiqued my lesson structure and then fallen asleep in the corner."

"He would've," Juniper agreed, grinning, and then the grin gentled. "He'd have been proud too. He'd never admit it. He'd write it down in the ledger and file it under *complaints*."

Casper smiled despite herself. Hale's ledger sat on its stand in the corner now, restored, expanded, guarded by a small dragon who had decided it was a nest. Speaking of whom—

A weight landed on her shoulder with the delicacy of a dropped brick. Ember — the dragon, moth-sized no longer but still absurdly small for a creature whose ancestor had burned back the dark at the end of the world — gripped her collar and peered at the chalk dust with vast golden interest.

"No," Casper said.

Ember sneezed a single spark onto the floorboards.

"*No.*"

Juniper scooped him up. "He wants to help sweep."

"He wants to commit arson. There's a difference, and it's the difference between my school and a smoking crater." But she scratched him under the chin anyway, because he made a sound like a kettle agreeing with you, and because she was weak, and everyone knew it.

* * *

The dreams had not stopped. Casper had not expected them to.

They were not nightmares. That was the strange mercy of it. She dreamed of a chapel full of quiet, of hands laid on a wound, of a voice that spoke in the space between heartbeats — and she woke each morning knowing, with total certainty, that she had been happy, and with equal certainty that she could not remember one detail of the happiness. It was like waking from a dream of sunlight into a bright room: the warmth lingered on the skin, and the source of it was gone.

Some mornings she lay there and inventoried what she'd paid. Her name, for a year — she'd had to relearn it, and even now it fit like borrowed boots. Twenty years of Grayhollow, every face, every small kindness, every cowardice. The color of Veth's eyes. Whether he had laughed. Hale's face — she knew Hale's story now, chapter and verse, from Juniper's telling and his ledger, but his face was a blank oval wearing a cassock, and she grieved a man she could not picture, which was grief with the volume turned all the way down and somehow worse for it.

What she had not lost was the feeling of the choice itself. That had stayed, seared clean. She had stood in a dead shrine with an ember dying in her hands and the world going dark, and she had chosen the world over her own past, and she would choose it again, and the certainty was the only thing she had left of him, and some days it was enough.

Some days it was a stone she carried in her chest and had learned to walk with, the way you learn to walk with a limp, until the limp becomes a way of moving and almost, almost becomes grace.

On those days, Juniper left tea outside her door without knocking, and Ember left a dead mouse, which was less comforting but sincerely meant.

* * *

In the sixth week, the Warden came to the school.

Casper saw him through the window first — the gray coat, the official stride — and her spine did the thing it had done for twenty years, the old cold drop, the body remembering laws the mind knew were repealed. She made herself keep chalking the day's lesson on the board. *WARMING. COOLING. THE HONEST KNOT. WHY WE ASK FIRST.*

Warden Brannock filled the doorway, ducking under the lintel. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with the tired eyes of someone who had spent decades enforcing edicts he'd privately hated, and since the fall of the Thorn he had aged ten years in the good way, like a man setting down a load.

"Madam Thackeray."

"Warden. You're scaring my students."

"They should be scared of nothing in this room, from what I hear." He glanced at the board, at the children — Petch was conspicuously holding his own hands flat on the desk, sweating with effort — and lowered his voice. "Council business. We're compiling a register. Voluntary. Names of practitioners, what arts they hold, nothing more. So that next time—" he stopped, and she watched him decide whether to finish the sentence, and finish it he did, like pulling a splinter, "—next time something comes out of the dark, we know who can help. And so that no one gets taken away in the night for a gift they never asked for. Never again. That's the whole of it."

Casper dried her hands on her apron. Twenty years ago — twenty years she did not remember, but knew by report, the way you know a war by its monuments — a Warden at her door had meant the cellar, the cart, the ground. This one stood shifting his weight like a man at a confessional, holding out a form.

"You want my name on a list," she said.

"If you'll give it."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I'll write *refused*, and that will be the end of it, and I'll still send my granddaughter here Tuesdays, because she's been lighting candles with her mind and her mother's at her wit's end."

Behind her, Juniper made a sound suspiciously like a snort of triumph.

Casper took the form. Read it. It was, as promised, nothing: a name, an art, a mark. At the bottom, in Brannock's blunt handwriting, someone had already entered the first line of the register — his own granddaughter, age nine, *candle-lighting, uncontrolled*.

She signed. *C. Thackeray — charm-craft, instruction, and one incident involving a river.* Let the historians puzzle over that.

When Brannock had gone, Juniper drifted over. "You hesitated."

"I weighed it."

"For three seconds."

"Some decisions deserve ceremony." Casper pinned the register copy to the wall by the door, next to the sign, next to Juniper's *NO MIRACLES*. Three documents, she reflected, that together constituted more honest government than Corvenna had managed in a century. "Besides. If I'm on a list, I'm visible. And I spent twenty years learning never to be noticed."

"And then you burned a hundred riders out of the sky."

"The river steamed."

"Casper."

She looked at the list. Eleven names now, hers at the top, the widow's beneath it — the widow had come in that morning and signed unprompted, *Marta Ellery, hearth-warming, forty years self-taught* — and Petch's, dictated solemnly while he held his singed sleeve. Small names. Honest ones. A city writing itself down so it couldn't be erased again.

"It's strange," she said. "Being seen. You'd think it would feel like losing something."

"Does it?"

She considered. "It feels like taking off tight boots."

* * *

The question came on a Thursday, near closing, from the last student out the door — the baker's daughter, Bree, who lingered the way thoughtful children do, packing her satchel with the slow precision of someone working up to something.

Casper let her work up to it. Waiting was half of teaching; Hale had taught her that, or rather his ledger had, in a marginal note in his crabbed hand: *Let them arrive. They always arrive.*

Bree arrived. "Madam Thackeray. Is the god gone?"

The room went very quiet. Ember lifted his head from the ledger-nest, gold eyes swiveling. In the doorway, unseen, Juniper stopped mid-step with a stack of slate-pencils and did not breathe.

It was the question under every question, the one the whole city carried like a stone in its shoe. The temples stood empty or repurposed — one was a granary now, one a very popular tavern called The Absent Friend — and people lit no candles to anyone, and yet on certain evenings, walking home, Casper saw strangers pause at thresholds and touch their doorposts, an old gesture, older than any creed, and she knew they were asking it too.

She could have given the safe answer. *We don't know.* She could have given the true-but-cruel one. *Yes.*

Instead she set down her chalk and sat on the edge of a student desk, which startled Bree more than any answer could have.

"When I was young," Casper said, "I served a silent god. He never spoke — that was his nature, not a punishment. He answered in other ways. Warmth where there shouldn't be warmth. A stillness in the middle of fear. The sense, sometimes, that you were being *held*, the way the ground holds you, without comment and without ever letting go." She turned her palm up, the old scar across it pale. "The Thorn ate him. Devoured him whole, we thought. But when the rite split them apart, I saw where he'd gone. He hadn't been destroyed, Bree. He'd been *dissolved*. Poured out into everything the Thorn tried to erase. Every memory that came home when the stars relit — that was him. Every small kindness the world kept doing even in the dark, out of pure habit — that was him too. He is not gone the way a lamp goes out. He's gone the way a candle goes into a room full of wicks."

Bree frowned, working it. "So... the world is..."

"The world is what's left of him." Casper said it simply, because it was simple; it had merely taken her a year and the loss of everything to say it without flinching. "Every time you warm your hands thinking of your mother's oven. Every time your brother thinks of you. That's not a metaphor. That's him, still answering, in the only voice he has left. We just have to do our half. He was always the quiet one."

Bree stood with this for a long moment. Then she nodded once, the way children nod when they have received something heavier than expected and decided to carry it anyway, and said, "My mum says the bread rises better lately. She thought it was the new flour."

"Could be the flour," Casper agreed gravely.

"Could be him."

"Could be."

Bree grinned suddenly, all gap and mischief, shouldered her satchel, and ran out into the evening light. In the doorway, Juniper finally exhaled and came in and set down the pencils and did not say anything, which from Juniper was a speech.

Casper began erasing the board. *WHY WE ASK FIRST* smeared under her hand and vanished, and she wrote nothing in its place, because tomorrow's lesson would need tomorrow's words.

"Anniversary's soon," Juniper said at last, to the chalk tray. "Of the shrine. Of — you know. What you gave up." She turned the tray a quarter-turn, aligning it with obsessive care. "I thought... there's a thing keepers used to do. It's in the ledger. When something terrible was beaten but not forgotten, they planted a thornbush over the place it happened. So the remembering would have roots. So nobody could pretend it hadn't grown there." She risked a glance up. "I thought we could plant one. Over the old shrine. You and me. If you wanted."

Casper stood with the dusty cloth in her hand and felt the stone in her chest shift — not lighter, never lighter, but settling into a shape she could build on. A bush of thorns, flowering over the grave of a god and a hunger both, tended by a woman who couldn't remember why she wept and a girl who remembered for her.

"All right," she said. "But I'm choosing where it goes. Somewhere with good light."

"There's a spot by the school."

"There's a spot by the school," Casper agreed, and reached for her coat, and outside the first star of the evening came up over Grayhollow like a lamp relit, steady and small and impossibly, stubbornly warm.
