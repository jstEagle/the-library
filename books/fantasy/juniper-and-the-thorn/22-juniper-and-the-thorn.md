## 22. Juniper and the Thorn

The shrine was still there.

That was the strange part, a year on. Juniper had expected the world to tidy itself after the Thorn died—to smooth over the crater of its passing, to fill in the pit beneath the Chapel of Roots with something useful, like a market square or a very nice garden. The world had other opinions. It left the wound open and let the grass grow around it, the way a body keeps a scar not because it enjoys remembering but because forgetting would be a lie.

So they came back on the anniversary, Juniper and Casper and a dragon named—after much argument—Biscuit, carrying a thornbush in a clay pot that Casper had bought from a woman in Grayhollow who swore it was cursed. Casper had paid extra for the curse. "Good marketing," she'd said. "Honest charms for honest prices, and one cursed bush for the road."

The pot sat between them at the edge of the old shrine, where the stones were blackened in a pattern nobody could explain and everybody privately understood. Morning light came down through the broken roof in long dusty columns. Somewhere above, Biscuit was doing lazy circles, his wings catching the sun so he flashed gold and then shadow and then gold again, like a coin someone kept flipping and never catching.

"You're sure about this," Casper said. It wasn't really a question. Casper's questions hadn't been questions since the day Juniper met her; they were doors she held open while you decided whether to walk through.

"He ate memories," Juniper said. She put her hand flat on the soil in the pot. It was good soil. She'd checked. "Everyone wants to pretend he never happened. Burn the records, pave the pit, tell children he was a story." She looked up. "But I *met* him. I watched him wear a king like a coat. If we erase him, we're just doing his work for him, aren't we? Forgetting, but politely."

Casper was quiet for a moment. A year ago, Juniper had learned, quiet was what Casper did instead of agreeing too fast. "When I was young," she said, "the order taught us that evil was a hole. An absence. You filled it with Veth and it went away." Her mouth did something complicated. "It turns out evil can be a *thing*. A hungry thing. And things that are real deserve graves."

"Or gardens," Juniper said.

"Or gardens," Casper agreed. "Gods, listen to me. A year ago I'd have called that heresy."

"What would you call it now?"

"Horticulture."

Juniper laughed, and the sound went up through the broken roof, and far overhead Biscuit wheeled once, sharply, the way he did when something pleased him—as if laughter were a smell he wanted to get closer to.

They dug together. That was the other thing a year had taught her: Casper Thackeray, former battle-priest of the silent god, killer of the unkillable, was extremely good at digging. She worked with the steady economy of someone who had spent twenty years pretending to be ordinary and had discovered, somewhere along the way, that she actually liked it. She got the hole exactly the right depth on the first try and then spent a full minute being annoyed that the sides weren't straighter.

"It doesn't have to be perfect," Juniper said.

"Everything has to be perfect. That's how you know you care."

"That's the saddest sentence I've ever heard from a woman holding a trowel."

"I contain multitudes," said Casper, and tamped the soil down around the thornbush's roots with more tenderness than she'd ever admit to.

The bush itself was nothing much to look at. Bare branches, gray-barked, mean little thorns along every inch of it. The woman in Grayhollow had sworn it grew wild on the borders, in places where the riders had passed—the places where forgetting had happened hardest—and that nothing else would take root there. Juniper had thought about that for a long time before choosing it. You didn't plant roses over a grave like this. You planted the thing that had survived it.

She pressed both palms against the soil when they were done. Old habit. Keeper's habit, though she hadn't known she was a keeper until the undercity chapel told her so, until the wall carvings showed her great-great-great-grandmothers holding lanterns up against the dark, generation after generation, an inheritance passed hand to hand like a cup of water carried across a desert.

*We remember,* the first page of the old ledger had said, in handwriting gone brown with age. *Not because it is pleasant. Because it is ours.*

The ember in her lantern was cold now. Had been cold since the morning ash stirred and something new hatched out of it—but she still carried the lantern everywhere, and she still touched it sometimes the way you touch a scar, to check that the past is still real.

"Juniper." Casper's voice had changed. Juniper looked up.

The thornbush was flowering.

Out-of-season, impossible, absurd—white blossoms opening along the black branches like small surprised faces, dozens of them, unfolding as they watched. The scent came off it sweet and sharp at once, honey and frost. Biscuit dropped out of the sky like a thrown stone, pulled up at the last second in a rush of wind that scattered petals, and landed on the topmost branch, which bent under his weight and sprang back, and he gripped it with his talons and spread his wings wide and made a sound deep in his chest that was half purr and half trumpet and entirely smug.

"Well," said Casper. "I suppose the curse was real after all."

"Biscuit," Juniper said, "did you do this?"

The dragon looked at her with one golden eye. He was the size of a large cat now, which everyone agreed was alarming, mostly because a year ago he'd fit in a teacup. He did not answer, obviously. Dragons didn't talk. But he did the thing he did sometimes—turned his head, deliberately, and looked at the cold lantern hanging at Juniper's hip.

And for half a heartbeat, just half, Juniper felt warmth.

Not fire. Not the old ember, which was gone, spent, burned up killing the Thorn along with everything else it loved. This was smaller. Newer. Like a coal buried under ash that somebody, somewhere, had breathed on.

"Oh," she said softly.

"Don't say 'oh' in that tone," Casper said. "That tone precedes quests."

"It's not a quest tone."

"It's absolutely a quest tone. I've heard your quest tone. It has a whole speech attached."

"It does *not*—" Juniper stopped. Because, honestly, it kind of did. She took a breath. "Maybe it's nothing. Maybe it's just—spring coming early. Maybe flowers don't care about calendars."

"Mm," said Casper, in the voice of a woman who had fought an ancient hunger wearing a king's face and was therefore entitled to skepticism about coincidences.

But she didn't push. That was the deal they'd built, the two of them, over a year of shared rooms and shared silences and one memorable incident involving a flooded cellar and a great deal of shouting: you let each other arrive at things in your own time. Casper had lost twenty years of her own memory feeding the ember back to life, and she was still finding the edges of that loss—a name she couldn't place, a street she didn't recognize, mornings when she woke reaching for someone the world insisted she'd never known. She never rushed Juniper toward hope. Hope, Casper said, was like bread dough. You couldn't hurry it or it came out dense.

Juniper knelt by the flowering bush instead and did the thing they had come here to do.

From her satchel she took the ledger.

It was new—not the ancient keeper's ledger, which rested now in the rebuilt chapel beneath Grayhollow, behind glass, where schoolchildren filed past it on field trips and whispered. This one Casper had bound herself, from leather and stubbornness, and the first hundred pages were blank. It had been blank for a year. Juniper had opened it a dozen times and closed it a dozen times, because the first page felt like it should be perfect, and Casper had told her repeatedly that perfection was a trap, and Juniper had told Casper that *she* should take her own advice, and they had reached a diplomatic stalemate.

Today the page didn't feel like a trap. Today it felt like a door.

She uncapped the ink bottle. Casper, tactfully, became very interested in the horizon.

*On the anniversary of the ending,* Juniper wrote, and her hand only shook a little, *we planted a thornbush over the place where the Thorn died.*

She paused. Looked at the words. They were true but they weren't the whole truth, and the whole truth was the entire point of the ledger.

*Not to honor it,* she wrote on. *Never that. But to remember it. Brother Hale used to say—well, Hale said a lot of things, most of them drunk, but this one turned out to be true—that a war isn't finished when the enemy dies. It's finished when you can say his name without flinching. So: the Thorn. There. Said it. Didn't flinch. Much.*

Behind her, Casper snorted. "You're putting Hale in the ledger?"

"Hale gets a page. Hale gets several pages. Hale helped the thing end the world and then helped us save it and died asking forgiveness, and if you think I'm letting the new keepers forget *that*, you haven't been paying attention."

"I pay excellent attention," Casper said. "It's my defining trait."

"It is absolutely not your defining trait."

"It's in the top three."

Juniper smiled and bent back over the page. The ink caught the morning light. Above her, Biscuit shifted on his branch, and petals drifted down and landed in her hair, and she let them stay.

*Here is what the new ledger will hold,* she wrote. *Names. All the names we can find. The villages that lost themselves on the road and found their way home. Tam, sergeant of the Company of Last Resort, who held a ford so strangers could live, and whose contract—this is true—we finally tracked down, and it turns out he was guarding a shipment of cheese, and he would have hated that, and we laughed, and then we cried, and that's what remembering is.*

*King Aldric, who abdicated and now teaches history in Corvenna, and who starts every class the same way: "I don't remember any of this. That's rather the point. Let's learn it together."*

*Casper Thackeray, who sold hollow charms for twenty years because she was afraid, and who was brave anyway, at the end, when it counted. Who tells her students the god is gone and means it, and who leaves a bowl of salt water on her windowsill every night, and won't say why, and I know why, and I will never write it here, because some memories belong to the person who paid for them.*

Juniper's pen slowed. The last lines were the hard ones, and she'd known they would be.

*And me. Juniper. Dragon-keeper, last of a line of jailers who turned out to be gardeners. I carried the last ember of dragon-fire across a country that was forgetting its own name, and I spent it, and it was enough, and it wasn't wasted, and some days I believe all three of those sentences.*

*The ember is cold. The lantern stays. That's the vow now—not to guard the fire, but to guard the memory of it. To write it down. To plant thornbushes over graves and watch what grows.*

*We remember. Not because it is pleasant. Because it is ours.*

She signed it. Her hand was steadier than she expected.

Then she sat back on her heels, and the silence of the ruined shrine settled around them, comfortable as an old coat, and Casper came and stood beside her and read the page upside down, which was a skill she refused to explain.

"Not bad," Casper said.

"You cried."

"I have dust in my eye."

"It's a ledger, Casper. There's no dust."

"There's *petals* everywhere, this shrine is a disgrace—" But her voice cracked on the word disgrace, and Juniper leaned her head against the older woman's shoulder, and they stood there a while, two keepers and a dragon, in a garden that used to be a wound.

It was Biscuit who broke it. He launched off the thornbush in a clatter of wings, circled twice, and then did something he had never done before: he landed on the cold lantern, hooked his talons over its handle, and picked it up.

"Biscuit," Juniper said carefully. "That's not a toy."

He flew a slow loop with the lantern swinging beneath him, and set it down in front of her, precisely, in the middle of the shrine's oldest stone—the stone where, a year ago, the last star had died and the rite had begun.

The lantern rocked once. Twice. Stilled.

And inside it, faint as breath on winter glass, something glowed.

Not gold, like the old ember. Not white, like the stars coming home. This light was green—new-leaf green, spring-morning green, the green of things that grow where nothing should. It pulsed once, softly, like a heartbeat learning its rhythm.

Juniper didn't move. Neither did Casper. Far away, in the town below the hill, a bell began to ring the hour, and the sound rolled up through the columns of light and out over the flowering thornbush and the blackened stones and the small impossible flame.

"Casper," Juniper whispered. "What do keepers do when the fire comes back different?"

Casper looked at the green light for a long moment. Then she crouched, opened the ledger to the second page, and held out the pen.

"I suppose," she said, "we start writing."
