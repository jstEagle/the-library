## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The last mile of Thornfell was always the worst, and Beatrix Okafor had made peace with that years ago. The forest didn't want her there. It never had. The trees leaned in like conspirators, the moss swallowed sound, and the veil itself pressed against her skin like a hand checking her pockets.

She moved by starlight, because that was how you moved through Thornfell. No lanterns — light drew the hollow-eyed things that lived between the trees. No roads — roads were where the ward-wardens waited with their ledgers and their chains. You navigated by the stars, the old ones, the ones the cartographers never bothered naming because they hung only over the veiled lands, bright as nailheads hammered into black velvet.

Beatrix knew them all. Knew that the Weaver's Loom hung low over the eastern ridge, that the Pale Sisters walked west to east across the sky in a slow procession that took a full night, that if you kept the Sisters at your left shoulder you'd come out of Thornfell exactly where the border stone stood half-buried in bracken, three miles from the smuggler's road and safety.

Three years ago she'd have prayed to Juniper for safe passage. Three years ago the god of thresholds and green places had still spoken through the mark on her wrist — a cool pulse, a sense of being watched over, the faint green shimmer that meant *you are seen, you are held*. Then one morning it had simply stopped. No warning. No fading. Just silence, the way a house goes silent when someone dies in another room.

She'd stopped praying after the first year. She hadn't stopped carrying the mark. That was the difference between faith and habit, she supposed, though she'd never bothered working out which was which.

Tonight the cargo was good — six vials of veilsap resin wrapped in oilcloth against her ribs, worth more than she'd make in a season of honest work, which was to say worth exactly what it cost to be here. Her client in Millbrook would pay on delivery, no questions, and Beatrix would drink something brown and cheap and sleep until noon for the first time in nine days.

The Pale Sisters were at her left shoulder. The ridge was ahead. All she had to do was walk.

She was fifty yards from the treeline when the stars went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded. *Out* — all of them at once, the named ones and the nameless, the Loom and the Sisters and every nailhead in the velvet, snuffed like candles pinched between wet fingers. The darkness that came down wasn't ordinary dark. It had weight. It settled on her shoulders and in her chest, and Beatrix Okafor, who had once stood in a burning barn and felt nothing worse than annoyance about her boots, felt fear crawl up her spine on cold little feet.

"Okay," she said aloud, because saying things aloud made them smaller. "Okay. That's weather. That's just weather."

It was not weather. Weather did not arrive with a sound like a held breath.

Then the wards broke.

She heard it before she saw it — a high crystalline ringing from the east, from the border, the sound of something vast and ancient failing all at once. And then the sky above the treeline lit green, a wall of light rising along the whole horizon, and the wards came down like glass. Not shattering outward but *inward*, collapsing sheet by sheet, and Beatrix felt each sheet go through her teeth, through the fillings in her teeth, through the bones of her inner ear. The air filled with glittering motes that weren't quite light and weren't quite dust, drifting down like snow that had forgotten which direction snow was supposed to fall.

The pressure against her skin — the veil's constant, familiar hand — vanished.

Thornfell's forest went quiet in a way forests should not be able to go quiet. No insects. No wind. Even her own breathing sounded borrowed.

And then her wrist screamed.

The mark — three years of silence, three years of a dead green line coiled around her forearm like a tattooed vine — ignited. Not pulsed. *Screamed*, a sensation with no honest name, pain and voice and grief fused into one white-hot frequency that dropped her to her knees in the bracken. She clawed at her sleeve, shoved it up, and the mark was blazing, green fire under her skin, the vine-shape writhing like something alive and drowning.

*Juniper.*

The god's name arrived in her head without language, without shape — just raw signal, a scream compressed into meaning: ***broken broken broken they are breaking it I am the door and they are breaking the door*** —

And then, worse, beneath the scream, fainter, fraying even as she grasped at it:

***— carry —***

The word cut off like a snapped thread. The blaze guttered down to embers, then to a dull ache, then to nothing at all. Silence again. But this time the silence had a texture, and the texture was *absence*, the absence of something that had been screaming at her a heartbeat ago and now was not there at all.

Beatrix knelt in the dead-dark bracken, shaking, her breath fogging in air that had gone cold enough to see it.

"Carry," she repeated. "Carry *what*?"

The forest did not answer. Nothing answered. That was the horror of it — the whole world had become the inside of her own skull, and her own skull was a bad place to be right now.

She got up because staying on her knees was how people died out here. Muscle memory took over: check the vials, check the knife, check the treeline. Her hands were steadier than she deserved. Nine years of smuggling bought you that much — your body learned to work while the rest of you was still screaming.

The border stone was ahead, or where the border stone should have been. She walked toward it through falling motes of dying ward-light, and when she reached the place she stopped so abruptly she nearly went over her own feet.

The stone was dead.

For as long as anyone could remember, the border stones had glowed — a soft amber luminescence, the visible face of the wards, the line between the human realm and the veiled lands drawn in light. Children were brought to them to be taught which side of the world they belonged on. Smugglers like Beatrix timed their runs by their pulse, amber to green, green to amber, the slow heartbeat of the boundary.

The stone was grey. Cold grey, dead grey, the grey of a hearthstone a hundred years after the last fire. She put her palm flat against it and felt nothing. No warmth, no hum, no heartbeat.

The border wasn't weakened. It wasn't breached. It was *gone*, and everything her instincts told her — everything nine years of Thornfell had carved into her — said that a border that doesn't exist is not freedom. A border that doesn't exist is an open wound, and things move into open wounds.

Behind her, deep in Thornfell, something howled. Long, and low, and wrong — too many harmonics for one throat, a sound like a choir learning to be a wolf. Then another, farther off, answering. Then a third, closer than either, and Beatrix decided she was done gathering information for the evening.

She ran.

The smuggler's road was a scar of packed earth through the heath, and she took it at a dead sprint, lungs burning, the vials thumping against her ribs like a second heart. Behind her the howling spread, joined, multiplied, the night filling up with voices that had been penned behind the wards since before her grandmother's grandmother and were now, for the first time in living memory, hearing open air.

Halfway to Millbrook she passed the first refugee — a warden, of all things, his ledger abandoned, his official coat torn at the shoulder, running the same direction she was with the same blind animal purpose. He didn't stop. Didn't look at her. Just screamed past into the dark, and that frightened her more than the howling, because ward-wardens did not run. Ward-wardens *were* the reason you ran.

The lights of Millbrook appeared as a smudge on the horizon, and around that smudge the sky was doing something impossible: rippling. Faint bands of green and violet sliding across the stars — except there were no stars anymore, only the ripple, the naked sky showing the bruised colors of whatever sat behind the world.

Her wrist ached in time with her footsteps. Dead again. Silent again. But she'd heard it. Three years of nothing, and tonight, on the night the world's oldest lock shattered, her god had screamed through her skin — *I am the door and they are breaking the door* — and then said one word to her, one word, before the thread snapped:

*Carry.*

Beatrix slowed at the edge of the heath, bent double, hands on knees, and made herself think. That was the discipline. Panic was a luxury; thinking was free. Who breaks a border? Not smugglers — a dead border killed their trade. Not Thornfell's exiles; the veil was the only roof they had. Someone had done this *on purpose*, with power enough to unmake what a thousand years of priests had built, and her silent god had been standing in the doorway when they came through.

And her god had called *her*. Out of every marked faithful in two realms — and there had been thousands, once — the scream had landed in her wrist like a thrown rope.

She straightened up. Millbrook's lamps glowed warm and ordinary ahead, and ordinary suddenly seemed like the most dangerous thing in the world, because ordinary people were about to find out what came through an open door.

There was exactly one group of people alive who might know what the mark meant, what had broken, and what she was supposed to carry. One crew. One family, if you could still call it that after what happened in Harrow's Ford — after the job that went sideways and the words said that couldn't be unsaid.

Halvard would be somewhere with paper and ink. The twins would be somewhere loud. And Ilsa — Ilsa, who used to serve the ward-priests, who knew the wards from the inside — Ilsa owed her an answer or a punch, and Beatrix would take whichever came first.

She turned off the smuggler's road toward the river, toward the old ferry crossing where her estranged crew drank when they wanted not to be found, and behind her the sky rippled green and violet over a world with no stars, no wards, and no rules left except the ones she carried in her own two hands.

The mark gave one faint flicker against her wrist — barely a whisper, barely a pulse — and went dark again.

But it pointed, the way a compass points. South, toward the river. Toward home, if home still existed.

Beatrix Okafor started walking.
