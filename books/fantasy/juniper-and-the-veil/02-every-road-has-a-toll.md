## 2. Every Road Has a Toll

The stars stayed dead for three days.

Beatrix Okafor learned this from the way people moved in the border town of Ashmill — heads tilted back, faces hungry and afraid, waiting for the sky to remember itself. It didn't. The moon hung up there like a coin dropped down a well, and around it nothing but black, black, black, so total it looked less like night than like a hole cut out of the world.

She kept her hood up and her eyes down. That was the trick to moving through a frightened town: be nobody. Beatrix had been nobody for most of her life, and she was very good at it.

Her covenant mark ached under her left sleeve — a slow, deep throb, like a bruise on the bone itself. Three years of silence from Juniper, and now the mark wouldn't stop whispering. Not words. Not yet. Just pressure, the way a river pushes against ice before the thaw.

*Not here,* she told it. *Not with half of Ashmill looking for someone to blame.*

And they were looking. Ward-wardens had flooded in from the garrison at the ford — grey-cloaked, spear-tipped, their badges freshly repainted with the Conclave's sunburst. She'd watched them drag a tinker out of his cart two streets over, demanding to know what he'd carried through Thornfell, who he'd paid, what he'd broken. Smugglers, the criers said. Smugglers and godless thieves had cracked the wards and let the veiled lands bleed through.

Beatrix had crossed Thornfell four nights ago with a pack full of uncut emberstone and a forged manifest. If the wards broke because of anything she'd done, she'd have known. Probably.

She bought bread, salt fish, a flask of lamp oil, and paid with Halvard's old scrip — risky, but the face on the note was good enough that the baker only squinted at it twice. Then she took the drainage road south, past the tanneries where the smell covered everything, and started walking toward the one place in the world she swore she'd never go back to.

* * *

The safehouse sat in a collapsed quarry outside Ashmill, half-swallowed by briar, its entrance hidden behind a curtain of ivy that Rook had spent an entire summer training to grow just so. Beatrix stood at the lip of the quarry for a long moment in the grey afternoon light, feeling like a woman about to knock on the door of a house she'd burned down herself.

Three years ago she'd walked out on them. Not quietly, either. There had been shouting. There had been Sefi's face, white with fury. There had been Halvard saying, in that terrible calm voice of his, *"When you need us again — and you will — don't expect the door to be open."*

She knocked anyway. Three short, two long. The old rhythm.

Nothing. Wind in the briars. Her mark throbbed.

Then, from somewhere above: "Wrong rhythm."

Her heart lurched. She knew the voice — Rook, older, rougher, but Rook. "It's not wrong," she called up. "You changed it."

A pause. Then a laugh, short and disbelieving, and a rope ladder unrolled down the rock face like the quarry was exhaling.

"Changed it after you left," Rook said, leaning over the edge. He was broader than she remembered, a scar splitting one eyebrow, but the grin was the same grin that had gotten them both jailed in Vell's Crossing when they were nineteen. "Figured anyone using the old one was either you or a warden who tortured it out of somebody. Wanted to know which."

"And if I'm the warden?"

"I'd be very disappointed in my interrogation resistance training." He waved her up. "Get off the road, Bea. Wardens swept the quarry rim yesterday. We've got maybe a day before they think to come back."

* * *

Inside, the safehouse smelled the same — lamp oil, woodsmoke, Halvard's ink. And there they all were, frozen mid-motion like figures in a tableau: Halvard at the table with a half-forged trade seal drying under his palm; Sefi by the hearth with an arrow nocked before Beatrix had even cleared the ladder; and a stranger, a tall grey-haired woman with ward-priest's ink still faded into her knuckles, standing near the back wall with the wary stillness of someone used to being the intruder.

Sefi lowered the bow one inch. One inch exactly.

"You've got nerve," she said.

"I've got nowhere else," Beatrix said. "Which is stronger."

"It isn't."

"No," Beatrix admitted. "But it's true."

Silence stretched. Halvard set down his pen with exaggerated care, the way he did when he wanted everyone to know he was choosing not to be angry. "Stars go out," he said, "wards shatter, and within three days you're scratching at our door. Either you're the luckiest woman alive or the wards broke because of you, and I genuinely can't decide which answer I'd prefer."

"Neither's true. I came out of Thornfell four nights before the collapse. Ask your own ledger — the run I never reported. Emberstone, uncut, twelve pounds of it, sold through the miller at Harrow Ford."

Halvard's jaw tightened, because he couldn't ask. The ledger was his memory, and she'd just recited his own bookkeeping back to him, which meant she'd read it, which meant — his eyes flicked to the loose floorboard by the hearth, then back, furious at himself for confirming it.

"You went through my floor."

"I went through your floor," Beatrix said, "because I needed to know if you'd sold me out. You hadn't. Congratulations. We're even for the betrayal I did commit, which was leaving without a word, and worse."

"That's not how even works—"

"How does it work, Halvard? Because I've had three years to figure it out and I've got nothing. So tell me the exchange rate for walking out on family, and I'll pay it, and then we can talk about whether any of us survives the month."

The words came out harder than she intended. Harder than she wanted. But something in the room shifted with them — Sefi's arrow dipping another inch, Rook exhaling through his nose, the stranger by the wall watching Beatrix with sharp, assessing eyes.

"The exchange rate," Halvard said finally, "is information. You want shelter? Start talking. What happened to the stars?"

So Beatrix talked. The crossing, the silence of the veil — Thornfell usually murmured, a low animal hum she'd felt in her teeth since childhood, and it had gone quiet as a grave — the sound like glass breaking everywhere at once, the mark waking up screaming. When she pushed back her sleeve and showed them the covenant mark, the stranger made a small sound and crossed the room before anyone could stop her.

"May I?" The woman's hands hovered over Beatrix's forearm without touching. Up close, Beatrix could see the faded latticework of priest's tattoos across her fingers, deliberately blurred with acid years ago. An exile. A defrocked ward-priest, hiding among smugglers.

"Who is she?" Beatrix asked.

"Ilsa," Rook said. "Pulled her out of a Conclave cell eight months back. She knows more about the wards than the wardens do." A beat. "She also hasn't stopped staring at your arm since you walked in, which I'm choosing to find reassuring."

Ilsa wasn't listening. Her fingertips had barely grazed the mark, and her breath had gone strange. "This isn't a brand," she whispered. "Everyone thinks the covenant marks are brands of service — the god's ownership, worn on the flesh. That's what the priesthood teaches. But this—" Her finger traced the outer ring of the design. "This is a keyhole. This is a lock."

"A lock to what?" Sefi asked.

"To him." Ilsa looked up, and her eyes were wet. "To Juniper. Child, do you understand what I'm telling you? Your god didn't leave. Your god didn't abandon the faithful. Juniper poured himself into the border wards — all of him, every scrap of divinity, anchored through marks like yours. Every faithful soul along the border was a pin holding the veil shut. He made himself the door and gave the keys to mortals."

The hearth popped. Nobody moved.

"Then why the silence?" Beatrix's voice came out flat. "Three years. If he was right here, holding the line, why did the mark go quiet? Why did the prayers stop landing?"

"Because a god spread thin across a hundred miles of veil has nothing left over," Ilsa said gently. "No voice. No presence. Nothing to give but the holding. Silence wasn't abandonment, Beatrix. Silence was the sacrifice already happening."

Beatrix looked down at her arm. The mark throbbed once, slow, like a heartbeat heard through a wall — and for the first time in three years, it didn't feel like absence. It felt like someone gripping her hand too tightly to speak.

"And now the wards are broken," Halvard said quietly. "Which means either the pins failed—"

"Pins don't fail all at once," Ilsa said. "All along the border, same hour, same night? No. Something pulled them out. Deliberately."

* * *

They worked through the evening by lamplight while rain started ticking on the quarry stones above. Rook cooked — he'd always cooked, badly, generously — and Sefi sharpened arrows and pretended not to listen, and Halvard drew maps of warden patrol routes from memory, and slowly, without anyone announcing it, the crew reassembled itself around the table like iron filings finding a magnet.

Beatrix caught Sefi watching her across the room.

"Don't," Sefi said, when Beatrix opened her mouth.

"I wasn't going to apologize."

"Good. It would've been insincere and we'd both have hated it." Sefi set down the arrow. "You left. It was cruel. I'm still angry. And you're staying tonight, because the wardens are hunting everyone with a covenant mark, and I am not letting them hang my sister in a market square out of spite." She picked the arrow back up. "That's not forgiveness. That's logistics."

"Sefi Okafor," Beatrix said, "has always been all heart."

"I will shoot you."

"There she is."

Something almost like a smile crossed Sefi's face and was killed immediately. But it had been there. Beatrix counted it, the way she counted everything worth keeping.

Near midnight, Rook climbed to the watch slit and went very still.

"Light," he said. "Quarry rim. Two lanterns, maybe three. Moving in a sweep pattern." He turned, and the humor was gone out of him entirely. "Wardens. They're not passing through. They're searching."

Halvard was already sweeping his forgeries into a satchel. "They found the scrip in Ashmill. Someone traced it."

"They'll have dogs," Sefi said, nocking the arrow she'd just finished. "And the briar screen won't stop a dog."

Ilsa stood, and there was something new in her face — the guilt from earlier hardened into something with edges. "There's a service tunnel under the east wall, from when this was a working quarry. It comes out in the fens." She hesitated. "And there's something else you all need to hear before we run, because if the wardens catch us, it dies with me. I know who broke the wards. I served under him."

The lantern light seemed to pull inward.

"His name is Corvin," Ilsa said. "And he didn't act alone. He acted on orders — orders from the Sunward Conclave itself."

Above them, boots crunched on quarry stone, and a dog began to bark.
