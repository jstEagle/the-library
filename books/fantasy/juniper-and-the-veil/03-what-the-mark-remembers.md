## 3. What the Mark Remembers

Ilsa had spent eleven years learning to read the language of wards, and four years pretending she'd forgotten it.

The safehouse was a collapsed tithe-barn outside Mirefen, all rotting beams and pigeon filth, and Beatrix Okafor sat on an upturned grain trough in the middle of it with her sleeve rolled to the shoulder, arm extended like a woman offering her wrist to a surgeon. The covenant mark caught the lamplight — a spiral of interlocking lines, fine as spider silk, wound from the heel of her palm to the crook of her elbow. It had been silver-grey for three years. Since the night Juniper went silent.

Since the night Ilsa walked away from the ward-priesthood and let herself believe the god had simply *stopped*.

"It's warm," Beatrix said. "It's been warm since the stars went out. Like a coal under the skin."

"Hold still."

Ilsa knelt and took the mercenary's forearm in both hands. Her fingers knew the old litany before her mind consented to it: *first the surface, then the structure, then the truth beneath.* She had traced a thousand marks like this in the cloister at Vael's Crossing. Branded faithful, all of them. Servants stamped with their god's ownership.

She began at the outermost line and followed it inward.

The first thing wrong was the depth. Service brands were shallow — ink and ash worked into the dermis, a signature, nothing more. This went deeper. The lines sank past the skin into something that wasn't quite flesh, and when Ilsa's fingertip crossed them she felt a resistance like touching the surface of water that refused to ripple.

"Does that hurt?"

"No," Beatrix said. "It feels like being *read*."

Ilsa almost laughed. Almost. Instead she kept tracing, line by line, and the shape of the thing assembled itself in her mind the way a ward assembled itself when you drew it correctly — not drawn but *revealed*, as if it had always been there under the paper.

Service brands were closed loops. A circle of ownership. You could read one blindfolded; every priest could, every child in the temple schools. This was not a loop.

This was a lock.

"I need the lamp closer," she said. "And I need you not to talk."

"Bossy," said Rook from the doorway, and Sefi elbowed him quiet.

Halvard dragged the lamp near without being asked. He'd been watching Ilsa with an expression she couldn't parse — suspicion, maybe, or the particular wariness of a man who forged documents for a living and recognized another kind of forgery when he saw one. Except this was the opposite of a forgery. This was the truest thing she'd ever touched.

Line by line, the mark gave up its grammar.

It was keyed. That was the second thing. A brand declared; a keyhole *awaited*. Every curve of the spiral was cut to receive something — a countersignature, a matching edge, a shape that existed somewhere else. Ilsa had seen keyhole-work exactly once, in the restricted archive beneath Vael's Crossing, in folios the ward-priests were forbidden to copy. Anchor-covenants. The oldest kind. The kind the Conclave had officially declared mythical two hundred years ago because the truth of them was too dangerous to teach.

An anchor-covenant didn't bind a servant to a god.

It bound a god to a place.

"Oh," Ilsa said. It came out very small.

"What?" Beatrix's voice was carefully flat, the voice of a woman who had learned that hope was the most expensive thing a smuggler could carry. "What does 'oh' mean? Ilsa. What does it mean?"

"You're not marked as hers." Ilsa's hands were shaking now, and she let them. "You're marked as *hers*. Juniper's. Not servant to god. God to — " She stopped. Started again. "Beatrix, do you know what the border wards are made of?"

"Stone and prayer and warden bureaucracy. That's what they told us."

"That's what they tell everyone." Ilsa sat back on her heels. The barn felt colder than it had a minute ago, though the lamp hadn't moved. "The wards are woven out of divine substance. Living divinity, spun thread by thread across nine hundred miles of border. That's why they've held for six centuries. That's why they can't be rebuilt once broken — you can't weave what no longer exists."

Sefi leaned forward. "So where did the divinity come from?"

"From a god who agreed to be unwoven." Ilsa heard her own voice go distant, lecture-flat, the way it always did when the alternative was screaming. "Slowly. Over centuries. An anchor-god pours itself into the wards at a rate the veil can bear. And the covenant mark — " she touched the spiral again, gently now, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal — "isn't a leash. It's the spool. The anchor-point where the last of the god is kept, so the pouring can be *finished* rather than merely stopped. So there's always a remainder. A seed. An ember."

The word left her mouth and hung there.

*Ember.*

Beatrix had used it herself, back on the road, half-delirious after the wards shattered: *it screamed through me like something burning down to nothing.* Ilsa hadn't understood. Now she understood, and understanding was worse.

"So she didn't leave us," Beatrix said. Each word placed like a foot on ice. "Three years of silence. Three years I thought — " Her jaw worked. "She was being poured out. All this time. Into the wards."

"And the silence wasn't abandonment," Ilsa said. "Silence was what was left over. You can't speak when you've given your voice away thread by thread. She kept only enough to hold the mark warm. Only enough to wait."

"Wait for what?"

"For someone to ask the right question." Ilsa looked up. "For the keyhole to be turned by someone who deserved to know what it opened."

* * *

They argued about it around a fire of damp pallet-wood, because that was what families did — even ragged, even estranged, even four years broken. Halvard said it was a trap. Rook said everything was a trap. Sefi, bandaged from the flight out of Mirefen, said nothing and watched Beatrix with the particular attention of a woman cataloguing injuries.

Ilsa barely heard them. She was copying the mark.

She'd found a stub of charcoal and the back of a torn waybill, and she was drawing the spiral from memory, line by line, and as she drew, the old archive-training woke in her like a scar reopening. Keyhole-covenants had countersignatures. The mark would answer to a specific working — a reading rite, uncorrupted, performed by someone who had *left* the priesthood cleanly. Not defected. Not fled. Released, or self-released, with the vows formally unwound.

Her stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

There were perhaps three people in the borderlands who fit that description. She was one of them. Which meant either extraordinary luck, or the fact that some part of her — the part that had walked out of Vael's Crossing with her vows unwound and her name struck from the rolls, the part that had spent four years drinking and hauling cargo and refusing to think about any of it — had been walking toward this barn the entire time.

"Ilsa." Beatrix crouched in front of her. Up close, the mercenary's eyes were bloodshot and steady. "You've gone somewhere. Come back and tell me where."

"The mark will answer a full reading," Ilsa said. "If it's done right. By the right person. It'll show what happened to the wards. Why they broke. Whether it was collapse or — "

"Or what?"

"Or murder."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the rafters, pigeons shifted.

"Do it," Beatrix said.

"If I do it wrong, it could burn the mark out. Burn *you* out. Whatever's left of her in there — I could snuff it." Ilsa's throat was tight. "I haven't done a reading in four years. I've spent four years making sure I couldn't."

"Then it's good you're doing it for someone instead of against them." Beatrix held out her arm. "I trusted the priests once. They told me my god had abandoned me and I should find work that didn't require faith. I'm not trusting them tonight. I'm trusting you."

*I don't deserve that,* Ilsa thought. *That's exactly why you're giving it to me.*

She took the arm.

* * *

The reading rite had no words anymore — the spoken liturgy had been stripped from the exiles' memories by decree, so what remained was structure, intention, the bare architecture of the working. Ilsa traced the outermost line of the spiral with one fingertip and *asked*. That was all the oldest rites ever were, the archivists whispered: asking, done honestly enough that the answer couldn't refuse.

The mark answered.

Light moved under Beatrix's skin like a fish under ice — silver, then gold, then a green so deep it was nearly black, juniper-green, the green of the shrine-candles Ilsa had tended for eleven years. And the mark showed her things.

Not images, exactly. More like memories that belonged to someone else, offered up through the keyhole with the desperate clarity of a dying woman dictating a will:

*Nine hundred miles of veil, and herself spread through all of it, thin as dawn mist, holding. Holding. Six hundred years of holding, and the ember at the center still warm, still waiting, still —*

*A knife of deliberate force, driven into the weave at the Graywater ford. Not decay. Not accident. Cut. Cut by hands that knew the pattern, cut from the inside, cut on orders —*

*And the scream. The scream that had gone through every marked faithful on the night the stars went out, except it hadn't been a scream of pain. It had been a scream of warning, the last full sentence a mostly-unraveled god could manage:*

*THEY DID THIS. FIND THE HANDS. THE EMBER MUST REACH THE HEART OR ALL OF IT FALLS.*

The light guttered out. Ilsa came back to herself on the barn floor with her cheek against the straw and Beatrix's hand fisted in her collar, holding her up, and everyone shouting her name.

"I'm here," she rasped. "I'm here."

"What did you see?" Beatrix's face was white, her arm cradled against her chest. The mark had gone dark — not dead-dark, Ilsa saw with relief, but banked, like a stove overnight. "Tell me."

Ilsa pushed herself upright. Her hands wouldn't stop trembling, so she pressed them flat against her knees and made herself say it plainly, the way she'd once reported to the archivists, the way she should have reported the doubts that made her leave, the way she'd never reported anything that mattered until tonight.

"The wards weren't worn down. They were cut. From inside the priesthood, at the Graywater ford, by someone who knew the weave intimately. On orders." She swallowed. "Juniper didn't fail us. Juniper was *betrayed* — has been being betrayed for six hundred years, slowly, and three nights ago someone finally finished the job. And the god's last strength went into a single message."

"Which is?" Halvard asked quietly.

Ilsa looked at Beatrix. At the dark, banked, patient spiral winding up her arm. At the woman who had carried a silent god for three years and never once stopped setting out the shrine-offering anyway.

"'The ember must reach the heart,'" Ilsa said, "or the veil falls, and both worlds fall with it. And I think — " her voice cracked, and she let it — "I think the ember is *her*. What's left. What's been waiting in your arm, Beatrix, this whole time, for someone to carry it home."

Silence in the barn. Pigeons shifting. Fire settling.

Then Beatrix Okafor laughed — one short, disbelieving bark, wet at the edges. "Three years I prayed to her to come back. And she was in my *arm* the whole time, waiting for me to stop praying and start walking."

"Not just walking," Ilsa said. Something was curdling in her chest, guilt and fury braided together into something with edges, something usable. Four years of running from Vael's Crossing, and it ended here, in a ruined barn, pointing her like a compass needle toward the people who had done this. "Someone gave those orders. Someone in the Conclave. And whoever they sent to cut the weave will have records, routes, a name. I know how the archives work. I know where the confession-paper goes."

Rook raised an eyebrow. "You want to break into a ward-priest archive?"

"No," Ilsa said, and stood, and felt four years of exile fall off her shoulders like a coat she no longer needed. "I want to find the man who cut the weave. Because if the Conclave ordered the wards broken, then someone swung the knife — and knife-swingers talk in their sleep."

Halvard was already pulling his ledger of contacts across the floor. "Graywater ford's two days east. Who do we know on that road?"

"One person," Ilsa said. "A disgraced priest named Corvin. And if anyone swung that knife — " she met Beatrix's eyes — "it was him."
