## 4. The Ward-Breaker's Confession

They tied him to a chair in the back room of a dead man's house, and Corvin Ashgrave thought: *this is fitting.*

The house belonged to a charcoal-burner who had died in the first hour of the collapse — one of the hundreds who had simply stopped, mid-breath, when the wards went down and the veil's pressure rolled across the lowlands like a tide. The man sat in his chair by the cold hearth still, because no one had come to bury him. His name had been Aldous. Corvin knew this because he had written the order that emptied this valley.

The rope bit into his wrists. Across the room, the big forger — Halvard — leaned against the doorframe with his arms crossed, watching him the way a man watches a rat he hasn't decided whether to drown. The twins stood by the window, Sefi pale and silent, Rook sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening. And in the corner, half in shadow, the exile Ilsa turned pages of a book she'd taken from his satchel, her lips moving as she read.

The mercenary, Okafor, crouched in front of him.

Up close, her covenant mark was visible above the collar of her shirt — a spiral of fine silver scarring, the old sigil of Juniper's faithful. Corvin had burned that sigil into a hundred arms and throats and foreheads over eleven years of service. He knew its geometry better than his own handwriting. He had never once wondered what it felt like from the inside.

"Name," she said.

"You know my name."

"I want to hear you say it."

He said it. It came out smaller than he intended.

"Corvin Ashgrave," Rook said from the window, not looking up from the knife. "Third chair of the Conclave's ward-liturgy. The youngest priest ever elevated. They used to print your face on the tithe-plates." A thin smile. "I forged a few of those plates, actually. You were very handsome on them."

"Rook," Sefi said quietly.

"What? He should know."

Okafor didn't smile. "You were at the border three nights ago. Wardens pulled your description off a dead sentry at the Thornfell gate. So before you give me a sermon, understand what I already know. Talk fast."

And there it was — the moment he had been running from since the stars went out. Not the wardens. Not the crew with their knives and their grief-sharpened patience. This. The telling.

Corvin looked at the dead man in his chair by the hearth, and began.

* * *

"I was born in Sunward," he said, "which means I was born believing the world was a lamp and we were its keepers. That's not doctrine, that's just — air. You breathe it. The Conclave keeps the light burning, the veiled lands are the dark pressing against the glass, and every prayer is a hand on the pane." He swallowed. "Eleven years in the liturgy, I never once questioned the glass."

"The wards," Ilsa said softly, without looking up. "You called them glass."

"Because that's what they told us they were. A barrier. A wall between what is holy and what is hungry." He laughed, and hated the sound of it. "I was the one who found the flaw, do you understand? In the anchor-lattice. Every ward has an anchor — a living thing bound into the working so the magic has something to hold onto. Ours was—" he nodded at Okafor's mark— "hers. Her god. Juniper poured itself into the border lattice three years ago, silently, without telling a single soul, and the Conclave's own diviners couldn't find where the power was coming from because they were looking for a mechanism and it was a *sacrifice.*"

"And you found it," Halvard said.

"I found it. And I reported it. And Archmagister Veyne thanked me, and gave me a team of twelve, and told me we were going to perform a *controlled translocation of the anchor-reserve.*" Corvin's voice cracked on the phrase. He'd rehearsed it so many times it had worn smooth as river stone, and now, saying it aloud to these people, it sounded exactly like what it was. "That was the language. Translocation. Reserve. Harvest-yield projections. I sat in a room with candles and incense and men who smelled of myrrh, and we drew up the rite that would pull a god out of a border like pulling a nail out of a beam, and nobody in that room said the word *steal.*"

"But you broke the wards," Okafor said. "All of them. At once. People died."

"People *died*," he agreed, and his voice rose, ugly with the self-loathing underneath it, "because the harvest required the lattice open. You can't drain a reservoir through a closed valve. The Conclave's arithmetic said the veil would hold for the duration — nine days, they calculated, nine days of open border while the extraction engines ran — and then the wards would be re-forged around the harvested reserve and everything would be *cleaner*, more efficient, the border under proper Conclave administration instead of resting on some dying god's back—"

"Nine days," Ilsa repeated. She had gone very still over her book.

"Yes."

She looked up at him then, and Corvin had faced the judgment of archmagisters and felt nothing like this. There was no fury in her face. Only a terrible, patient grief, like a woman reading a diagnosis she'd already guessed.

"Do you know what happens when the veil comes down?" she asked. "Not the border. The veil itself. The membrane between what has shape and what doesn't."

"The Conclave's models—"

"The Conclave's models were drawn by men who have never been closer to Thornfell than a map table." She closed the book. "The veil isn't a wall, priest. It's a skin. And your masters haven't built an engine to harvest its magic. They've built an engine to flay it. What bleeds out won't stop at the border. It will keep bleeding until both sides of the skin are the same thing — and the thing on the other side has no shape at all."

Silence. In the hearth, a log settled into ash with a sound like a sigh.

"That's not what they told us," Corvin said. It came out as a whisper. It was the truest sentence he had spoken in years.

"No," Ilsa said. "It wouldn't be."

* * *

Afterward they gave him water, which he hadn't expected, and left him alone with the dead man, which he had.

He studied Aldous's face. Ordinary. Charcoal dust in the creases of his knuckles even now, even dead. Corvin tried to remember whether the requisition orders for this valley had carried his signature or only his seal, and realized with a lurch that it mattered to him, that some cowardly clerk in his chest was still trying to file this horror under *not mine.*

It was yours. All of it. You held the stylus.

The door opened. Okafor came in alone and cut his ropes, and he flexed his hands and waited for whatever came next — the knife, the road, the warden's bounty.

Instead she dragged a second chair over and sat across from him, close, knees almost touching his.

"Three years," she said. "My god went silent three years ago. Do you know what that's like? Waking up every morning and reaching for it and finding nothing. I thought I'd done something. I thought I'd failed some test. I prayed myself hoarse in shrines that smelled like dust." Her jaw tightened. "It wasn't silence. It was it *holding the door shut with its body* while people like you decided how best to take it apart."

"I know."

"You don't know. But you're going to." She leaned forward. "Here's what happens now, Ashgrave. The wardens want you for murder. The Conclave wants you for treason — you ran, which means they've marked you, which means when the harvest goes wrong and the veil starts eating their engine, they'll need a traitor to blame, and you're already fitted for the part. Nobody in either realm is going to give you justice. So we're going to."

"We," he said.

"The crew votes tonight. I'm not the only voice." She stood. "But I'll tell you what I'm voting for, so you can sit here and decide if you can live under it. You're going to take us to the mountain's heart. Through every pass the Conclave seals, past every hunter they send, all the way to the place where your masters are flaying a god. And when we get there, you're going to stand in front of the archmagister who gave the order, and you're going to say everything you said in this room tonight, except this time you'll say it to his face, in front of witnesses, and you will not look away."

Corvin's throat worked. "They'll kill me."

"Probably," Okafor said, and there was no cruelty in it, only honesty, and somehow that was worse. "But you've already died once, three nights ago, when you broke the world. Everything since is just deciding what to be reborn as." She paused at the door. "For what it's worth — the god you helped gut? It screamed through my mark when the wards fell. First word it's spoken in three years. You know what it said?"

He shook his head.

"*Hold.*" She almost smiled. "Not save me. Not curse you. Just — hold. Like it still thought we were worth holding together." She opened the door. "Think about whether you're worth it too. The vote's at moonrise."

* * *

They voted at moonrise, in the yard, under a sky still wrong — the stars absent, the dark overhead flat and total, like a page torn out of the night.

Halvard voted yes, grudgingly, on the grounds that a former third chair of the ward-liturgy was worth more than a map. Rook voted no, eloquently, and then voted yes anyway because Sefi voted yes, and everyone understood without discussing it that Sefi's vote was really the dead of the valley voting through her. Ilsa voted yes before anyone asked her, and quoted something in Old Liturgical that made Corvin's skin crawl, a verse from the office of penitents: *let the breaker carry the burden to the breaking-place.*

Okafor voted last.

"Aye," she said. Then she looked at Corvin, tied again now — gently, but tied — and added: "One condition. He tells us the route. Tonight. Every sealed pass, every Conclave garrison, every safe road to the mountain's heart. Because if the veil dies in nine days, we've already lost two of them standing here talking."

Corvin wet his lips. Somewhere beyond the hills, faint as a heartbeat, he could feel the extraction engines beginning their work — a wrongness in the air, a slow suction, the world being drawn thin.

"There's one way," he said. "The Conclave sealed the ninety-nine known passes at dawn. But there's a hundredth road. Smugglers' lore — the Underthorn caverns. My masters don't know it exists because it isn't on any map the Conclave ever bought." He met Okafor's eyes. "It's also a three-day crawl through the dark under a mountain that's actively dying. Half the crews that ever tried it never came out."

Halvard grinned, sharp and joyless. "Those are our favorite odds."

"Then rest," Okafor said to the crew, and to Corvin: "Sleep, priest. Tomorrow you lead."

Corvin lay back against the post and watched the starless sky, and for the first time since the night the world broke, the shame in his chest did not feel like drowning.

It felt like ballast.
