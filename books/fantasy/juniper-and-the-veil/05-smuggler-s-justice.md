## 5. Smuggler's Justice

Rook counted the enemy before the first arrow flew. That was the trick of it — the trick nobody taught you, the trick you learned the hard way, bleeding in ditches with your sister's hand clamped over your mouth. Count first. Panic after. Panic after, if there's time.

Six shapes on the far bank, crouched among the reeds. Two more upstream where the alders thickened, bows already drawn. The river ran fast and gray between them, swollen with snowmelt, loud enough to cover a whisper and loud enough to cover a crossbow's ratchet.

"Eight," Rook breathed. "Two archers, upstream left."

Beatrix didn't look away from the water. "Halvard?"

"Crossing's dead," Halvard said. His voice was flat, the forger's voice, the voice he used when the thing in front of him was a problem to be solved and not a nightmare to be survived. "Ferry's gone. Rope's cut. They want us in the water."

"They want us in the water where we can't fight," Sefi said. She was grinning. Rook's twin was always grinning when the odds went bad, which was either courage or a sickness nobody had found a cure for. "So we don't cross. We go along."

"Along to where?" Ilsa asked.

"Away," Sefi said. "The word means what it means."

The first arrow came out of the alders and buried itself in the pack Corvin had been carrying, three inches from his spine. The disgraced priest went down in the mud with a sound like a kicked dog, and Rook felt something ugly and satisfying curl in her chest. Good. Lie there, priest. You dug this.

Then the reeds on the far bank moved, and the eight became eleven, and the counting stopped mattering, because the ambush wasn't a line — it was a mouth, and they were already inside it.

"Run the bank," Beatrix said, and that was all. No speech, no plan. Beatrix Okafor didn't waste words the way other people wasted coin. "Sefi, Rook, Corvin between you. Ilsa, Halvard, the packs. Go."

They went.

Rook ran. The riverbank was a churn of mud and stone, and the arrows came in pairs, one to make you flinch and one to kill you while you flinched. She heard one crack off a stone an arm's length from Halvard's head and heard Halvard swear with the fluency of a man who had forged a hundred official documents and considered profanity his true native tongue.

Corvin was slow. Of course he was slow. He was soft, priest-soft, hands that had never held anything heavier than a censer, and he was wheezing like a bellows inside of a minute, and Rook grabbed his collar and hauled him because that was the job, that was what Beatrix had said, and Beatrix's word was the only law Rook had ever trusted.

They made the bend where the cliffs pinched the river to a throat, and that was where the Conclave hunters showed them they weren't amateurs.

They'd anticipated the bend. Of course they had. Three of them came down the cliff path ahead — light armor, sun-gilt tabards, the white-and-gold that made Rook's teeth ache — and cut the bank off, and the arrows stopped, because arrows were for the running and now came the killing.

"Beatrix Okafor," the lead hunter called across the mud. A woman's voice, calm as a ledger. "Surrender the ember and the priest, and the rest of you walk. That is the Conclave's offer. It will not be repeated."

"The Conclave's offer," Beatrix said, "is that you think we're stupid."

"It's that we think you're tired."

Rook had to give her that. They were tired. Three days since the shrine, three days of the Underthorn's edge and cold camps and Beatrix going quiet in a way that none of them wanted to name, and now this.

And then Sefi laughed.

Rook knew that laugh. Every crew that ever ran Thornfell knew that laugh. It was the laugh that came right before Sefi did something that would either save everyone or kill them both, and there had never in twenty-six years of shared life been a reliable way to tell which.

Sefi threw Corvin at the hunters.

Not toward them — *at* them, a hundred and sixty pounds of terrified priest launched like a sack of wet wool, and the lead hunter flinched, and flinching was the whole game. Sefi came in behind him low and fast with her knives out, and Rook was already moving because that was the other half of the trick nobody taught you: when your twin does something insane, you don't ask questions, you *finish* it.

It almost worked. It should have worked. Sefi took the lead hunter's legs out and Rook put a second one down with a thrown hatchet and the third one was turning, panicking, and panicking men do stupid things, and this one did the stupidest thing possible.

He didn't aim at Sefi. He aimed at Corvin, scrambling in the mud, the ember's reluctant courier, the Conclave's prize.

Sefi saw it. Rook saw Sefi see it. There was time for exactly one thought, which was *no*, and then Sefi wasn't where she had been, because Sefi had thrown herself between the blade and the man she'd been mocking for a week.

The sound she made was small. That was the worst part. Rook had heard her sister scream through a broken arm, a burned hand, a collapsed mine shaft. This was small. A short, surprised breath, like someone had shown her a bill she hadn't expected.

Then the bank erupted — Beatrix, who had crossed the mud like a rumor, whose covenant mark was burning white through her sleeve — and the hunter who had stabbed Sefi learned what a mercenary with a dead god's fire in her arm could do. He didn't learn much. It was quick.

The other two ran. Rook let them. There was no room in her for anything but her sister.

* * *

Sefi lay in the lee of the cliffs with her head in Rook's lap, and the wound was bad.

Not the dramatic kind of bad, not the kind you could shout at. The blade had gone under her ribs, angled up, and there was blood in a place blood shouldn't be, and Ilsa — who had served the ward-priests and knew what bodies looked like when they were closing — went gray and quiet in a way that told Rook everything before Ilsa said a word.

"I can slow it," Ilsa said. "I can't stop it. Not here. She needs a real surgeon, a real altar, real—"

"She needs to not die," Rook said. Her voice came out strange. Level. She could hear herself from very far away, as if someone else were speaking. "So she's not going to die."

"Rook." Halvard crouched beside her. The old forger's face was wrecked, and he wasn't hiding it, which frightened Rook more than the blood. "Rook, listen to me."

"Where's the nearest—"

"Rook." Halvard took her hands. They were bloody. Everything was bloody. "The nearest real help is Vell's Crossing, two days back the way we came. Through Conclave lines. With a wounded woman. And behind us—" he nodded upstream, toward the mountain, toward the heart of the veil, "—is nine days of road and one ember and the end of the world."

Rook understood then, in the distant, ringing way you understand a verdict. She looked down at Sefi's face. Sefi was awake. Of course she was awake. Stubborn to the marrow, even now, even gray-lipped and cold.

"Hey," Sefi whispered. "Don't make that face."

"What face."

"The face where you're already deciding who to kill."

"I'm not deciding," Rook said. "I've decided."

Sefi's eyes slid past her, to where Corvin stood at the edge of the firelight, mud-caked, shaking, holding his own useless hands like they belonged to a stranger. The priest who had broken the wards. The priest whose orders had put the hunters on their trail. The priest Sefi had died stopping — no. The priest Sefi had *almost* died stopping, and Rook would not finish that sentence, not in her head, not anywhere.

"Corvin," Sefi said. "Come here, you useless—"

He came. He knelt. He was crying, Rook realized. Silently, the way priests cry, like they've had practice doing it where no one can see.

"I'm sorry," Corvin said. "I'm sorry. I never — I told myself it was duty. I told myself the Conclave knew things I didn't. And now you—" His voice broke. "Let them take me. Trade me. I'm the one they want. I'm the one who—"

"Shut up," Sefi said, without heat. "You're not listening. I didn't jump in front of you because you're worth it. I jumped in front of you because *we* decided you were, and we don't un-decide things." She coughed, and it was wet, and Rook's whole body clenched. "That's the difference between us and them, priest. They trade people. We don't."

Corvin made a sound that wasn't a word.

Rook looked up. Beatrix stood at the fire's edge, and her mark had gone dark again, and her face was the face Rook had followed into a hundred bad places — the face that meant the decision was already made and the only question was who said it out loud.

But Beatrix didn't say it. Beatrix looked around the fire, at each of them, and waited.

That was new. That was the new covenant, Rook thought, the one Juniper's silence had been building all along. Beatrix wasn't ordering them. She was asking.

"Vell's Crossing is two days back," Halvard said heavily. "Through their lines. If we split—"

"We don't split," Rook said.

"Then we lose the mountain," Ilsa said gently. "The nine days. Rook—"

"We don't split," Rook said again, and her voice cracked on it, and she let it crack, because her sister was bleeding into her lap and she was done being level. "And we don't trade him, and we don't leave him, because Sefi bled for him and I will burn the whole Conclave down before I let that be for nothing. So we don't split, and we don't go back, which means—"

"Which means," Halvard said slowly, "we take her *with* us."

Silence. The river ran. Somewhere upstream, the hunters were regrouping or retreating, and none of it mattered.

"She can't walk nine days," Ilsa said. "The wound will—"

"I can be carried," Sefi said. Her grin was back, white and terrible. "I've always wanted to be royalty. Carry me, peasants."

"It'll kill her," Ilsa whispered. "Movement, cold, infection — it could kill her."

"Staying could too," Rook said. "Everything could. That's not news."

She looked at Beatrix. Beatrix looked at the fire for a long moment, at the ember's faint warmth she alone could feel, at the road upstream that led to the mountain's heart and the end of everything, or the beginning, depending on how the next nine days went.

"Vote," Beatrix said. "All in favor of carrying her on. All of us, or none of us."

Five hands rose. Six, when Corvin raised his, trembling, and Sefi reached up with a bloodless hand and folded his fingers closed around hers and kept them there.

"Then it's settled," Beatrix said. "Halvard, build me a litter. Ilsa, everything you've got for the wound. Corvin — you carry her first watch. You carry her until I tell you to stop."

Corvin's head came up. "I—"

"You heard her," Rook said. She stood, and her legs were steady, and something had finished burning down in her chest and left clean ground behind, ground to build on. "You carry her. Every mile you carry her is a mile you owe her, and you're going to owe her for the rest of your life, priest, so you'd better hope she has a long one."

* * *

They moved at dusk, upstream, into the teeth of it.

Rook took the front, because Rook always took the front, and behind her she could hear the litter creaking, Ilsa's low instructions, Corvin's ragged breathing under the weight of the sister of his debt. Sefi, drifting in and out, murmured a smuggler's road-song, the one about ninety-nine roads and the one that's never mapped.

Beatrix fell into step beside Rook. For a while neither of them spoke. The mountain was a black shape ahead, and above it, where stars should have been, there was only the veil's slow bruise-colored shimmer.

"Rook," Beatrix said finally. "Back there. When you said *we don't split* — you knew it might cost us the mountain."

"I knew."

"And you said it anyway."

Rook watched the dark road. "Juniper poured itself out for people it never met," she said. "Seems like the least we can do is carry one of our own."

Beatrix was quiet a moment. Then: "The ember's getting heavier. She can feel it, I think. Feeding on something. I don't know what yet." Her hand drifted to her mark. "If it starts taking from her—"

"Then we walk faster."

Ahead, the river bent north, and the pass through the Underthorn yawned out of the cliffside like a throat — the suicidal smuggler's route, the one road the Conclave hadn't sealed because no one sane took it. Halvard had the forged papers. Ilsa had the herbs. Corvin had the way.

And Rook had a sister breathing shallow behind her, and a promise she'd carved into herself with that small, terrible sound in the mud: *we don't un-decide things.*

She stepped into the dark, and the crew followed her in.
