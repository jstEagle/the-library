## 6. The Hollow Shrine

The shrine had been beautiful once. You could still see the bones of it — a ring of white stone pillars climbing out of the hillside like ribs from shallow earth, each one carved with juniper branches that some patient hand had rendered leaf by leaf. Moss had taken the rest. Ivy strangled the altar. The mosaic floor, which must have shown the god's face in ten thousand tiles of green and silver, was cracked through the middle as if something enormous had stepped on it and kept walking.

Beatrix stood at the edge of it with her hood up and her breath fogging, and felt the mark on her forearm pull toward the center like a fish on a line.

"You feel it too," Ilsa said behind her.

"It feels *me*." She flexed her fingers. Under her sleeve, the covenant mark — three years dark, cold as river stone since the night the stars went out — was warm for the first time since she'd sworn it. Not burning. Warm the way a hearth is warm when you come in from snow. "It knows I'm here."

The others hung back at the tree line. Halvard had his crossbow half-raised at nothing, which was how he held a crossbow when he was frightened and didn't want anyone to know. Sefi's wound had been bound twice over, but she stood straight anyway, stubborn as her brother beside her. Rook hadn't spoken since the river crossing. Corvin knelt in the wet grass with his hands laced behind his head, because he'd asked them to keep him tied until they understood what he'd done, and no one had argued.

"Nine days," Ilsa said quietly. "That's what the ember told you."

"That's what it *screamed*." Beatrix pulled back her hood. "It wasn't words. It was arithmetic. Something counting down."

"Then stop standing in the rain and go count with it."

She almost laughed. Almost. Then she crossed the broken mosaic alone.

* * *

The altar was a slab of green-veined marble, hollowed in the center like a bowl, and in the bowl lay an ember the size of a sparrow's heart.

That was all. Three years of silence, a shattered border, a god poured out across two realms — and what remained fit in her cupped hands. It glowed a deep, exhausted amber, pulsing slow, and when she lifted it the warmth sank through her palms into her wrists and up along the old scar-tissue lines of the mark, and the mark lit.

Not the dull ember-glow of before. It blazed white-green, every line of it, the keyhole pattern Ilsa had traced glowing like a window thrown open in a dark house. And then the world folded.

* * *

She was standing in the wards.

Not near them. *In* them — or in their memory, their ghost, whatever a dying god could show a woman with mud on her boots. They stretched away from her in every direction: a lattice of light vast beyond sense, threads of green fire woven across the whole seam between worlds, each thread anchored to a point of living will. She understood, suddenly and completely, the way you understand things in dreams: every thread had been a prayer. Every knot a vow. Juniper had not built the veil the way masons build walls.

Juniper had *become* it. Thread by thread, year by century, the god had unpicked itself and woven its own substance into the border, trading divinity for duration, glory for endurance. That was why the prayers went unanswered. That was why the voice went silent. There had been nothing left over to speak with. A god can be a god or it can be a wall, and Juniper had chosen the wall, and had chosen it so long ago that the faithful had forgotten there had ever been a choice.

And threaded through it all, bright and small and impossibly familiar, ran one strand that hummed against her own blood. Her covenant. Her vow, spoken at nineteen with wine on her tongue and Halvard laughing at her solemnity: *I will carry what needs carrying.* She had thought it a figure of speech.

The lock turned both ways. Her oath had been one of the anchors. Her faithlessness these three years — not doubt, just absence, just a mercenary getting on with her life — had been one of the small loosening knots among thousands. Not enough to break anything. But enough that when the Conclave's ward-breakers came with Corvin's stolen rites and their ordered, methodical malice, the weave gave way where it was already worn thin.

She saw the night it broke. Saw it from inside the lattice: the threads snapping in sequence like harp strings cut by a careful hand, the light draining out of the seam between worlds, and through the widening gap the other side pressing in — Thornfell's wild country, hungry and unmoored, and behind it the deeper dark where the veil thinned to nothing at all.

Then the vision turned, as visions do, toward what was coming.

* * *

She saw the Conclave's harvest.

They had names for it — the Reclamation, the Great Gathering, terms printed in gold ink on documents Halvard would have wept over forging — but she saw it plain, without the gold ink. She saw the archmagister's engineers driving great iron staves into the wound in the world, siphoning what remained of the veiled lands' magic the way you tap a maple, except the tree was the border itself and the sap was the only thing holding two realities apart. She saw the first cisterns fill. She saw the Conclave's cities glow brighter than they had in a generation, and the priests smiling, and the ledgers balancing beautifully.

And she saw what happened next, because the ember showed her without mercy.

The veil did not thin gradually. It failed the way ice fails — silently, completely, all at once. Thornfell's magic flooding unchecked into a human realm that had forgotten how to hold it; the human realm's iron certainty bleeding backward into a wild country that would choke on it. Two worlds grinding against each other along a seam with nothing left to cushion it. She saw coastlines rewrite themselves. She saw forests arrive overnight in wheat country, and plowlands surface in the deep woods like drowned things rising. She saw people — thousands, tens of thousands — standing in fields that were suddenly two places at once, and most of them did not survive being two places at once.

The harvest wouldn't take years. It would take nine days after the last anchor failed. Nine days, and the ember was the last anchor, the final knot of the god still holding a finger's width of the seam closed, and it was fading, and it was here, in her hands, asking.

Not commanding. That was the thing that undid her. Juniper had spent everything it had and never once commanded her. The pulse of the ember against her palms said only: *this is true, this is coming, this is the way.* The mountain's heart, where the first thread had been tied. Carry me there. Tie the last knot. Or set me down and walk away, and let the worlds do what they'll do.

A god's last act was a request.

Beatrix opened her eyes on the ruined shrine, kneeling on broken mosaic, rain running down her face, the ember cupped against her sternum like something rescued from a fire.

* * *

They were all around her when she came back. She didn't remember them crossing the grass. Rook had a hand hovering near her shoulder, not quite touching, the way he touched everything — like he expected it to break or bite. Sefi sat on a fallen pillar, gray-faced, watching her with the flat attention of someone deciding whether to believe a miracle.

"Well?" Halvard said. "You've got the look. Ilsa gets the look when she's read something terrible. You've got the look."

"I saw the harvest." Her voice came out scraped. "What the Conclave's building. What happens after." She told them. All of it — the staves, the cisterns, the ice-failure of the veil, the fields that became two places at once. She watched Corvin's face while she spoke, and it was worse than watching the others', because he nodded. Small nods, helpless, a man hearing his own nightmare read back to him by a stranger.

"The archmagister knows," he said finally. "Some of them know. There are calculations. They believe the transition will be survivable if the flow is managed correctly. Managed." He laughed, and it was an awful sound. "I told myself the same thing. That's what orders are for. They let you borrow a belief you couldn't afford on your own."

"Nine days," Ilsa said. She had her journal out, writing fast, and Beatrix realized she'd been transcribing the vision as Beatrix spoke it — recording it, anchoring it, making it real and portable. "The mountain's heart is six days' hard travel if the passes were open. They're not open. So it's the Underthorn, or it's nothing."

"The Underthorn is a graveyard with a roof," Halvard said. "Half those caverns don't appear on any map made by a sober person."

"Then we'll need a map made by a drunk one," Rook said, and even Sefi huffed something that was almost a laugh.

Beatrix looked down at the ember. Up close, in ordinary light, it looked less like a god than like a coal someone had forgotten — dim, tired, guttering. But when she tilted it, deep inside, she caught a flicker of green. Juniper branches. The mosaic pattern, alive in miniature, turning slowly like a wheel.

Three years she'd prayed into a silence and called it abandonment. Three years she'd carried the mark like a debt she resented. And the whole time, the god she'd sworn to had been stretched thin across the entire border of the world, holding the door shut with its own body, unable to say so much as *hello.*

"I'm going," she said.

Nobody argued. That was the strange part. She'd braced for Halvard's objections, Rook's silences, Sefi's arithmetic of risk. Instead Ilsa closed her journal and said, "We'll need provisions by morning," and Halvard started listing what a person could buy in the village below with forged coin, and Rook simply nodded once, the way he did when a job stopped being a question.

Corvin rose from his knees. "The Underthorn has three entrances within reach. One of them is watched. I know which." He met her eyes, and there was no self-justification left in his face, only the raw place under it. "Let me be useful. It's the only penance I can afford."

"We'll vote on it," Beatrix said. "Like everything else."

She looked east, where the mountains stood black against a starless sky — a sky that should have been crowded with stars and wasn't, and wouldn't be again until someone tied the last knot. Nine days. Six days of road through the dark under the world, and no margin left for anything going wrong.

Behind her, faint as a heartbeat, the ember pulsed once against her chest — and far off in the hills, every juniper tree on the ridge shivered though there was no wind.

Something else had felt it wake.

Something that wasn't friendly.
